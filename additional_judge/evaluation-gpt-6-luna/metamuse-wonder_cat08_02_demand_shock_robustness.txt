1. **Stress scenarios**

The process needs policies that respond to changes in case mix and to disruptions at specific steps. I use five scenarios:

- **S1 — Peak mix shift:** Urgent and Complex-panel cases rise, approaching the peak-period mix of 29% and 20%. Total volume may also rise.
- **S2 — Assay degradation:** M2 becomes unreliable or an assay machine is unavailable; repeats increase, especially after night shifts and M2 use.
- **S3 — Validation absence:** A medical validator or the specialist reviewer is absent, with Complex-panel demand high.
- **S4 — Courier compression or delay:** Arrivals bunch at the 10:00 or 17:00 slots, or a delayed route delivers a large wave.
- **S5 — Intake defects:** Poor samples or metadata defects increase, driving more corrections, recollections, and repeat work. This can coincide with any of the other scenarios.

2. **Bottleneck-migration analysis**

- **S1:** Pre-analytical utilization rises from 74% to 96%, correction specialists from 62% to 91%, and the assay machines from 71% to 98%. At the same time, technical validation reaches 101% utilization and medical validation 93%. More Urgent work fragments machine schedules: the share bypassing batch wait falls from 74% to 58% at peak. Strict Urgent-first dispatch would ease Urgent waits but starve Routine and Complex-panel work, building WIP upstream. Reducing intake queues alone would push more cases into already congested preparation and assay queues.
- **S2:** Machine degradation first lengthens the assay queue, already at 18 hours median at peak. Repeat assay loops consume capacity twice; the peak DFG records 1,260 transitions to Repeat assay and 1,080 returns to Assay run. More assay output then reaches technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization. Sending all Routine work to faster but less reliable M2 could increase repeats and send work back to the machine bottleneck.
- **S3:** Medical validation has a 22-hour peak median queue; Hold for specialist review has a 36-hour queue, and the peak Hold-to-Medical edge takes 48 hours. Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist review. Expediting preparation or assays during a reviewer absence would create unvalidated WIP. Extra medical-validation hours help only if specialist review is also covered where needed.
- **S4:** Courier intake reaches 88% peak utilization and a 7.5-hour median queue. Compressed arrivals feed the 9.5-hour pre-analytical queue, then the 11-hour preparation queue and 18-hour assay queue. Smoothing deliveries can reduce the initial spike, but it will not create downstream capacity if machines or validators are already saturated.
- **S5:** Defects create correction and recollection work. Correction specialists have a 14-hour peak median queue, and recollection can add 60 hours from request to sample receipt. Earlier defect handling can prevent long loops, but if it increases clean flow without regard to downstream WIP, preparation and assay queues grow. Cross-training validators can ease correction queues but risks worsening technical validation, whose utilization is already 101% at peak.

3. **Robust redesign package**

- **Early defect triage:** Adapt I6 so poor samples are assessed within two hours, with clear acceptance criteria and a route for borderline cases. This can reduce correction and recollection loops across demand and courier scenarios. It depends on accurate triage and timely referrer feedback; poor criteria could cause avoidable recollections or cancellations.
- **Smooth courier arrivals:** Adapt I7 by negotiating pickup slots that reduce the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes. This steadies intake and pre-analytical workload, but offers little relief during a sustained machine outage.
- **Capped priority and batching:** Use a protected Urgent lane, but cap its share of preparation and assay capacity. Use small, frequent batches when urgent demand or queues rise; allow Routine batching only while Urgent waits remain within target. This preserves some setup efficiency without allowing I1-style starvation. It depends on reliable case labels and active queue monitoring.
- **Protect technical validation:** Cross-train two validators for accession correction (I4) only when correction queues exceed a trigger and technical validation has enough staffed capacity above a defined floor. This relieves a scarce correction pool, but cannot safely be a standing reassignment during peak validation load.
- **Validation-aware release planning:** Forecast Medical and specialist-review demand from the cases entering technical validation. Avoid accelerating Complex-panel assays when downstream review capacity is unavailable. This controls WIP but may defer work that could otherwise progress.
- **Gated registration automation:** Use I8 only for orders with complete, validated digital metadata, and audit defects against manual registration. This can help when clerk queues rise; it may expose pre-analytical or assay bottlenecks if defects pass through.

Avoid rigid M1/M2 dedication (I3), Routine release holds (I9), and uncapped Urgent-first dispatch (I1). Each can shift delay or rework to another case group or station.

4. **Scenario-specific policies**

| Policy | Trigger to activate | Action | Trigger to deactivate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1. Peak-mix protection** | Urgent + Complex share exceeds 35% for 3 days, or forecast Urgent p90 breaches 18h | Raise the protected Urgent capacity cap modestly; cap batch waits at 4h; defer non-urgent validator work | Mix below threshold for 5 days and Preparation and Assay queues below 8h |
| **P2. Assay contingency** | M2 outage exceeds 4h, Repeat assay rate exceeds 10% over 24h, or assay queue exceeds 12h median | Stop large Routine batches; run smaller batches; prioritize first-pass Urgent and Complex work on available reliable machines; require QC review before reruns; notify referrers about deferrable Routine work | Repeat rate below 8% for 48h and assay queue below 6h |
| **P3. Validation protection** | Medical queue exceeds 12h, Hold queue exceeds 10 cases, or specialist absence is announced | Add an evening medical-validation shift (I5); outsource suitable Complex-panel overflow (I10) only when the expected internal Hold delay exceeds the 12h transport delay; limit upstream expediting of cases that cannot be reviewed | Medical queue below 6h and Hold queue below 5 cases for 2 days |
| **P4. Courier surge protocol** | Any slot delivers over 130% of its planned average, or intake queue exceeds 4h | Open a temporary second intake point, prioritize defect triage, defer non-urgent clerical work, and cap registration overtime at 9 hours | Intake queue below 2h for two consecutive slots |
| **P5. Correction surge** | Correction queue exceeds 8h, or recollection exceeds 6% of receipts over 3 days | Use cross-trained validators only above the technical-validation staffing floor; give referrers targeted feedback on common defects | Correction queue below 4h for 2 days and technical validation remains above its staffing floor |

Do not use strict Urgent-first dispatch, rigid machine dedication, or Routine release holds as standing policies.

6. **Residual vulnerabilities**

- **Specialist dependency:** If the specialist and backup are both unavailable, Hold cases can still wait; partner capacity may not be equivalent or timely.
- **Technical-validation fragility:** Peak utilization is already 101%. Cross-training cannot safely relieve correction queues when technical validation is below its staffing floor.
- **New triage dependency:** Early defect triage introduces a failure mode: inaccurate rejection criteria can cause unnecessary recollections or cancellations. Triage decisions need ongoing review.
- **Priority-label gaming:** A protected Urgent cap can be undermined by inappropriate Urgent labels. Audit Urgent share by referrer.
- **Outsourcing complexity:** Partner transport adds 12 hours and creates sample custody and result-matching risks.
- **Machine common-mode risk:** The policies do not preserve throughput if both assay machines are unavailable.
- **Policy compliance:** Informal expediting can erode batch caps and priority limits, recreating queues elsewhere.

7. **Minimum monitoring dashboard**

**Leading indicators — review daily during peaks and use to trigger action**

- Arrivals by case type over 3 days: warning when Urgent + Complex exceeds 35%; critical above 40%.
- Arrivals by courier slot: warning above 115% of plan; activate P4 above 130%.
- Oldest and median queue age by case type at pre-analytical, correction, preparation, assay, technical validation, Medical validation, and specialist review: warning above 8h; critical above 12h at Assay or Medical, or above 8h at Correction.
- Repeat assay rate by machine and shift over 24 hours: warning above 8%; activate P2 above 10%.
- Recollection rate over 3 days: warning above 6%; critical above 8%.
- Medical and Hold queue counts, plus projected incoming Complex and Urgent work: activate P3 at Medical queue above 12h, Hold above 10 cases, or an announced specialist absence.
- Roster coverage for the next 7 days: flag any uncovered specialist or medical-validator shift.
- Urgent-label share by referrer: investigate unexplained growth of 3 percentage points per week.
- Registration overtime and auto-registration defects: flag shifts over 9 hours or auto-registration defect rates above manual rates.

**Lagging outcomes — review weekly to check whether relief is real**

- Median and p90 cycle time by case type, including Urgent performance against the 18h penalty threshold and Routine against the 72h target.
- Overall rework and repeat-after-failure rates, compared with the normal 7.5% and peak 11.8% baselines.
- Share of cases with any queue over 12h, compared with the normal 22% and peak 54% baselines.
- Cancellations after recollection, penalty payments, and partner-lab turnaround.

The daily indicators show bottleneck movement before cycle-time outcomes worsen; the weekly measures confirm whether the redesign improves delivery across case types.
