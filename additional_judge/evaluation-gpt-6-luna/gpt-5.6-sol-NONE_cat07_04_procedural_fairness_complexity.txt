# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates differ: Group A is at 78%, compared with 63% for B, 69% for C, and 58% for D. These gaps flag questions for review but do not establish unfairness, since case mix and routing differ.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After the preliminary controls, approval remains lower for B (−5.5 percentage points), C (−2.2 points), and D (−3.1 points) than for A. B’s gap is the largest; without uncertainty intervals or model diagnostics, we cannot judge its precision or cause.
- **Procedural burden:** B has more document requests and supervisor reviews after adjustment; C also has more supervisor reviews. Their support-related variants add steps, and both groups experience extra handovers. These are potential burdens even where routing serves a legitimate need.
- **Timeliness:** Raw median cycle times are longer for B and C. Adjusted cycle time remains higher for both (+2.6 and +4.1 days), while D’s difference is smaller (+0.9 days). Queues after language support, accommodation review, and supervisor review identify plausible sources, but do not establish how much of the disparity they explain.
- **Consistency of routing:** Fraud, language-support, and accommodation steps align with the stated policy triggers. Fairness depends on whether those triggers are applied consistently to applicants with the same relevant need and whether required steps are completed before decisions.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** The log records support contacts and requests, not whether assistance was timely, understandable, accessible, or respectful. A contact alone cannot establish that the underlying need was met.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** B and D have higher raw appeal rates than A; adjusted appeal probability is higher for B (+4.4 points) and D (+5.7 points). Rework is also higher for B and C. Appeals may reflect decision problems, barriers to understanding, or better access to appeal rights; these measures need interpretation alongside notice quality and appeal outcomes.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** B’s dependence on seven bilingual caseworkers and overloaded regional officers, and C’s dependence on five accommodation specialists, may create avoidable queues and concentrated handovers. Daily workload data cannot resolve how much delay reflects staffing, complexity, or individual assignment.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Estimate group differences in outcomes and process measures among cases with comparable observable needs. Match or stratify on risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior-case count, region, and month; also account for whether language support or accommodation is requested when comparing the relevant support pathways. Check overlap first, because some combinations may have few or no comparable cases. Report matched estimates, confidence intervals, and the cases excluded for lack of overlap.

Compare paths and variants both overall and within comparable need strata. For example, compare B and A on document requests among cases with similar documentation status, and compare C and A on supervisor review among accommodation-requesting cases. Examine whether triggered steps occur consistently, whether steps are skipped, and whether requests or reviews recur. A path difference may reflect legitimate routing, but a difference in completion time or burden among similarly situated cases may indicate an avoidable procedural disparity.

Compare resource assignment by region, group, need, and month. Measure case concentration, handovers per case, and time between assignments, including the generalist-to-specialist-to-generalist patterns reported for B and C. Account for worker and region effects where possible. Daily workload measurements limit conclusions about how much delay is caused by overload or assignment practices.

Decompose elapsed time into activity and waiting time where lifecycle data support that split. Compare distributions, including medians and upper percentiles, for relevant transitions within matched strata and variants. Check service-level performance for language-support and accommodation steps, which currently lack separate targets.

Treat risk and documentation variables cautiously. Risk scores may encode historical disparities, and documentation completeness may reflect language barriers or prior assistance. Re-estimate results with alternative specifications that omit or redefine these controls; compare findings with and without conditioning on them. A controlled disparity is evidence of an association after those adjustments, not proof of discrimination or proof that the controls are fair.

Finally, inspect appeals and rework alongside decision explanations, reversals, and support quality if those data can be collected. The present log cannot establish the accuracy or dignity of treatment. Distinguishing legitimate routing from discrimination requires policy-relevant comparisons, process audits, uncertainty estimates, and evidence about how support and decisions were delivered.

# 3. Evidence interpretation

**Supported by the evidence:** Raw outcomes and cycle times differ substantially by group. Some differences remain in the preliminary adjusted model, especially B’s lower approval probability and higher document-request, supervisor-review, and appeal probabilities. C retains longer adjusted cycle time and more supervisor review. B and C’s specialist pathways and handovers, together with reported queues, are plausible sources of additional procedural burden.

**Consistent with legitimate routing:** D’s more frequent fraud review is consistent with its higher measured risk share. B’s language-support contact and C’s accommodation review are consistent with the stated policy triggers. These facts justify examining the routing; they do not establish that every referral, delay, or handover is necessary or applied consistently.

**Not established:** The evidence does not show that group membership caused the adjusted gaps, that a particular worker acted with discriminatory intent, or that every remaining difference is unjustified. It also cannot determine whether support was effective, whether decisions were explained accessibly, or whether risk and documentation measures are unbiased. No confidence intervals, model diagnostics, or detailed resource histories are provided.

The strongest conclusion is that procedural fairness is uncertain, with material warning signs around B’s additional document requests and appeals and B and C’s support-related queues and handovers. Further matched comparisons and audits are needed to determine which burdens are necessary and which can be reduced.

# 4. Limitations and safeguards

- **Confounding and model choice:** The preliminary controls may omit relevant complexity and may also control for mechanisms through which barriers arise. Report sensitivity analyses rather than relying on one adjusted estimate.
- **Measurement:** Risk scores can reflect historical patterns, documentation completeness can be affected by language access, and outcomes omit explanation quality and dignity. Validate these measures and preserve a distinction between recorded need and actual need.
- **Uncertainty and overlap:** Provide confidence intervals and common-support diagnostics. Avoid strong conclusions for sparse strata or small resource groups.
- **Timing and workload:** Event timestamps and daily workload data may not identify actual waiting causes, service effort, or within-day capacity. Validate timestamp semantics and collect finer-grained assignment and workload data.
- **Privacy and use:** Limit access to identifiable case and worker data, report small groups carefully, and use results to improve procedures rather than label individual workers from noisy group comparisons.
