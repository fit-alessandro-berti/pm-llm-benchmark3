## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation

The raw fitness collapse in March is largely explained by a measurement change: `Validate Eligibility` was often logged as `Eligibility Screen`, while duplicated `Open Case` events also appeared. Mapping the renamed activity and removing the duplicate events raises fitness from 0.55 to 0.90 in March and leaves the mapped series around 0.86–0.91 thereafter. This strongly supports a logging-semantic contribution to the raw drop. It does not show that every case followed the intended process or that business behavior remained unchanged: fitness is relative to a particular model, label mapping, and event representation.

A mapping can restore apparent conformance when the underlying activity is equivalent, but that equivalence needs validation. If `Eligibility Screen` covers different work, or migration changed which events were captured, the mapping may hide meaningful distinctions. Duplicates can inflate event counts and distort directly-follows relations, variants, and trace conformance. Missing resource identifiers affect resource attribution and team-level performance analyses; because missingness is concentrated in migrated activities and is non-random, complete-case resource comparisons may be biased. They do not by themselves explain the operational-table cycle times or all case-level relation rates.

After May, the supplied normalized DFG rates and operational metrics both change: `Assess -> Request Info` rises from 12 to 24–29 occurrences per 100 cases, while rework and cycle time rise. The supplier disruption and changing case mix provide plausible operational mechanisms. Migration-related logging problems remain relevant, but the post-May patterns cannot be dismissed as logging drift alone.

## 9. Provisional attribution

The most confident attribution is the March raw-fitness fall to label and event-capture changes: mapping and duplicate removal recover most of the lost fitness, while the mapped series remains near its baseline. That is strong evidence of semantic drift in the log, not proof that all underlying work was unchanged.

From May onward, Supplier Z’s disruption plausibly contributed to rework: its share rises alongside normalized `Request Info` loops and rework variants. This is moderate-to-high confidence as a contribution, not a quantified causal share, because Supplier Z cases are non-random and already somewhat more complex. Staffing loss plausibly contributed to queue growth after June; the modest service-time increase alongside substantially higher reconstructed waiting supports a capacity mechanism, but the priority rule, demand, and non-random resource missingness complicate attribution.

The July priority rule may have redistributed delay between high-complexity and standard cases, but aggregates cannot show which group benefited. October–November’s high volume and complexity are consistent with renewal-season drift; persistent staffing constraints and backlog may amplify its effect. December’s improvement is compatible with overtime and the quality campaign, but lower load and complexity also provide a plausible explanation. The best current account is multiple interacting mechanisms with separate onsets. Case-level, stratified analyses and validated event semantics are needed to refine attribution.

## 10. Monitoring decision rules

1. If raw fitness drops while mapped fitness stays stable, investigate label and event changes, then validate the mapping against source records before concluding behavior is unchanged.
2. If supplier-adjusted incomplete-bundle and repeat-collection rates rise, investigate the affected supplier and compare matched cases by complexity and region.
3. If queue waiting rises while service time remains near baseline, review staffing, arrivals, backlog, and queue routing.
4. If high-complexity share or volume exceeds a seasonal capacity forecast, assess coverage and expected waits by priority group.
5. If Senior Assessment rates change within complexity strata without a corresponding policy change, audit routing and historical recording.
6. If missing resource IDs exceed validated limits, flag resource-level conclusions as unreliable and audit capture before team attribution.
7. If cycle time improves during an intervention as volume or complexity falls, compare against a case-mix-adjusted or matched baseline before crediting the intervention.
