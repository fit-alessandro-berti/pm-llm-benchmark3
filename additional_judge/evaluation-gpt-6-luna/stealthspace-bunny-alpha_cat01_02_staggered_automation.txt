The answer is thorough and appropriately cautious about causal identification, but several substantial arithmetic and consistency errors undermine its quantitative claims.

- The calendar-aligned comparison table contains incorrect calculations and labels. For example, Alpha’s median comparison is shown as “1.0% / +11.2% / 12.2 pp,” which mixes a percentage with percentage points and gives the wrong contrast. Using March versus April–May, Alpha’s median changes from 49.4 to 48.9 hours (−1.0%), while Delta/Epsilon’s equally weighted mean changes from 38.2 to 43.65 hours (+14.3%); the relative-change contrast is about −15.3 percentage points. Several other entries have similar sign or magnitude problems. The claimed initial-post untreated changes are also inconsistent across outcomes.

- The sensitivity analysis is materially wrong. Alpha’s March-to-August median change is −12.3%, while the controls’ is about +7.5%, yielding roughly an 19.8 percentage-point relative-change contrast—not 16.8. Beta’s corresponding contrast is roughly 12.6 points, not 7.5. This weakens the answer’s main quantitative evidence.

- The “treatment-intensity” table is mislabeled: it compares month 0 with August, but the entries are August minus month 0 with inconsistent signs and shorthand. For example, Alpha’s median falls from 48.3 to 43.3 hours, a −5.0-hour change, while the table reports 5.0. The table therefore obscures whether values represent improvement or deterioration.

- The proposed numerical scenario ranges are not convincingly derived. The median interval is presented as “12% to +8%” without a clear justification and appears inconsistent with the answer’s own contrasts. The escalation range of 0 to +12 pp is especially implausible for an availability-policy effect given the observed post-March changes are around 1–2 pp, with March’s fraud-desk change already affecting referrals. Calling these planning bounds does not make them informative bounds.

- There is a notable inconsistency in the conclusion: Section 7 reports favorable median contrasts for all adopters, while the claim ladder says Alpha and Beta had early median improvements. Their April–May mean medians actually rose from March (by 0.5 and 1.75 hours); the favorable contrasts arise only after comparison with controls.

The response otherwise follows the requested structure, defines a useful set of estimands, recognizes the limitations of branch-level aggregation and five clusters, and recommends a randomized embedded evaluation. Its code is detailed and appropriately labels regressions as descriptive. But the quantitative mistakes affect central evidence and the effect assessment, so the answer does not meet the requested standard of precision.
