## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

- **January–March:** Stable baseline: mature labels, low unknown activity and missingness, and consistent metrics. The model’s aggregate predictions roughly match breach rates.
- **April:** Treat this as a pipeline failure first. The diagnosis rename disrupted prediction prefixes, while duplicates and unknown activities increased. The sharp metric deterioration and overprediction are consistent with corrupted inputs. April is not reliable evidence of a new outcome relationship until features are rebuilt.
- **May:** Metrics recover after the mapping patch, but incomplete recomputation of the cached pre-diagnosis event count leaves residual uncertainty. Do not assume April’s features are repaired merely because the label mapping was patched.
- **June:** Remote routing changes the case mix: V2 rises to 52%, while V2’s matured breach rate falls to 0.09 and its diagnosis-to-remote-fix time remains stable. This is primarily a process and covariate shift. It can change aggregate metrics without implying deterioration within V2.
- **July:** Supplier delays begin to show in the DFG edge time and V3 breach rate. Labels are nearly mature, so the increase to 0.46 for V3 is meaningful early evidence of genuine parts-path deterioration.
- **August:** Supplier delays become severe; V3’s breach rate reaches 0.64. The overall breach rate rises while mean predicted risk stays near 0.19. The deterioration is concentrated in the parts path and is consistent with process drift causing a changed outcome relationship.
- **September:** V3 remains a major source of risk, while Partner Z adds a population shift and routes more cases through V3. Aggregate metrics combine supplier deterioration, changed variant mix, and slower Partner Z labels. Stratified, maturity-adjusted comparisons are needed to estimate each contribution.
- **October:** Do not interpret the observed 0.16 breach rate as improvement. Only 55% of labels are mature, the outcome API is backlogged, and longer-path cases are disproportionately delayed. Low AUROC and poor Brier/ECE are concerning but may also be distorted by selective label availability.

These months are not one homogeneous failure: April is dominated by pipeline corruption; June by routing and case-mix changes; August–September by real parts-path process deterioration alongside population shift and label delay; October by censoring that makes aggregate outcome metrics unreliable.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment:** Quarantine April predictions and features from training pending a clean rebuild. Mark October labels provisional and prevent them from driving retraining or lowering thresholds. For operational use, flag predictions from affected or poorly represented segments for review and avoid treating a score as reliable when required inputs or event prefixes are missing.

**Short-term recalibration:** After labels are mature enough and features are validated, assess calibration by variant and source. If ranking is still useful but risk levels shifted, recalibrate on a recent, mature, representative cohort. Avoid a global calibration curve if the V3 shift differs materially from V1 and V2.

**Feature repair:** Finish April backfill, normalize diagnosis aliases consistently, clean duplicate events with an audit trail, and validate queue-feature lineage. Add or assess rural distance, device age, and supplier-delay or parts-path signals where available at prediction time. Do not use information that occurs after the prediction point.

**Full retraining:** Consider retraining only after a fixed-vintage backtest confirms persistent degradation on clean data, including within-path evidence. July–September can inform analysis, but July is only 98% mature and August and September are less mature and increasingly affected by source/path selection. Use only mature labels or correct for censoring with a validated method. Exclude April until rebuilt and verified; exclude October until its labels mature. January–March provide a sound baseline but may not represent current processes.

A V3-specific recalibration is reasonable if V3’s conditional calibration shift is confirmed and there is enough mature data. A separate V3 model requires sufficient representative data and evidence that it improves stable out-of-time performance. Partner Z alone is too new and too confounded by V3 mix and delayed labels to justify its own model now.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

- **Predictive performance:** Track AUROC and other ranking metrics by month, variant, source, and mature cohort.
- **Calibration:** Track Brier, ECE, calibration curves, and mean predicted versus observed risk on fixed-vintage labels.
- **Label maturity:** Track maturity curves and delay distributions by path, source, and risk band; publish provisional and mature metrics separately.
- **Data quality:** Monitor unknown activities, duplicate rates, missing features, event ordering, diagnosis-prefix validity, and backfill lineage.
- **Process variants:** Track V1/V2/V3 and unmapped shares by both case-start and event-calendar month.
- **Performance DFG:** Track edge volumes and elapsed-time distributions, especially parts receipt and remote fix, by path and source.
- **Population:** Monitor feature distributions and source composition, including rural distance, device age, and Partner Z share; trigger stratified evaluation or reweighting checks.
- **Intervention and deployment:** Log model version, prediction volume, missing-input fallbacks, flags for low-confidence predictions, and any operational action taken from scores.

Alert dependencies should prevent immature or corrupted labels from creating false retraining signals. A label-maturity alarm should mark performance results provisional and defer model decisions; it should not itself request retraining. A data-quality alarm should route to pipeline repair and suppress interpretation of affected model metrics. A process-variant alarm should trigger stratified review. Escalate to recalibration or retraining review only when mature, clean, fixed-vintage evidence shows persistent degradation.

import pandas as pd
import pm4py

df = pd.read_csv("field_service_events.csv")
df["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["timestamp"], utc=True)
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df, case_id="case_id", activity_key="activity",
    timestamp_key="timestamp"
)

# Normalize aliases before deriving prediction-point features.
aliases = {
    "Initial Diagnosis Completed",
    "Diagnostic Assessment Completed",
}
df["concept:name"] = df["concept:name"].replace(
    {name: "DIAGNOSIS_COMPLETED" for name in aliases}
)

# Flag duplicates for audit; retain one identical case/activity/time event.
key = ["case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"]
df["duplicate_flag"] = df.duplicated(key, keep=False)
df = df.sort_values(key).drop_duplicates(key, keep="first")
df_clean = df[df["concept:name"] != "UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY"].copy()

def event_calendar_cohort(log, start, end):
    # Cases with events in the time window: operational activity view.
    return pm4py.filter_time_range(log, start, end, mode="events")

def case_start_cohort(log, start, end):
    # Cases whose first event is in the window: prediction cohort view.
    return pm4py.filter_time_range(log, start, end, mode="traces")

def summarize(log):
    dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
        log, granularity="1h"
    )
    return {
        "cases": log["case:concept:name"].nunique(),
        "dfg": dfg,
        "performance_dfg": perf_dfg,
        "variants": variants,
        "temporal_features": temporal,
    }

rows = []
for month in pd.period_range("2026-01", "2026-10", freq="M"):
    start, end = str(month.start_time), str(month.end_time)
    operational = event_calendar_cohort(df_clean, start, end)
    started = case_start_cohort(df_clean, start, end)
    rows.append({
        "month": str(month),
        "event_calendar": summarize(operational),
        "case_start": summarize(started),
    })

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

1. **Retraining on October’s immature labels:** The observed rate may be biased downward because longer-path cases are delayed. Training on it could make the model understate risk while V3 is experiencing severe supplier delays. Keep October out of training until a fixed-vintage maturity check supports its use.

2. **Calibrating around April’s pipeline error:** April’s corrupted prefixes and features could produce a calibration correction that compensates for bad inputs. Rebuild and validate the cohort before using it for calibration or training.

3. **Attributing August–September deterioration entirely to population shift:** V3 breach rates rose sharply and parts receipt times lengthened. A Partner Z adjustment alone could leave the broader supplier-driven V3 risk unaddressed.

4. **Treating Partner Z’s observed breach gap as a settled model disparity:** Partner Z has slower label maturity and more V3 cases, as well as different rural and device-age profiles. Compare matched, mature cohorts before changing thresholds, reweighting, or building a partner-specific model.

5. **Treating all drift as concept drift:** June’s routing change shifts variant mix while remote-fix times and V2 breach rates remain stable. Retraining on that aggregate change alone could obscure the difference between case-mix change and within-path deterioration.
