# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic

- **Handover pattern:** Local specialist loops fall; shared referrals are targeted and advice returns to local caseworkers with few repeat transfers.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Shared queues absorb added demand without sustained backlogs. Waits remain near or below the management estimates.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median and tail cycle times improve, especially for East. High-complexity cases do not suffer disproportionate delays.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Better specialist coverage and standardized decisions reduce rework and improve quality.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Queue ownership and local follow-through are explicit.
- **Fairness implications:** East improves without material deterioration for North, South, or customers needing regional or language accommodations.
- **Early warning signs:** Stable queue age and waits, few reassignments, and no widening of team or segment gaps.

## Central

- **Handover pattern:** Local loops decline, but more cases enter shared technical, legal/risk, and approval queues. Some advice returns generate additional transfers.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Median waits rise moderately; spikes occur during demand peaks and absences.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East improves, while North and South see mixed results. Overall median may improve while the 90th percentile worsens for complex cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standardization helps routine decisions, but reduced informal local advice increases rework in some complex or region-specific cases.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Responsibility is initially ambiguous between the shared queue and local caseworker.
- **Fairness implications:** Average performance masks uneven effects by team and customer segment.
- **Early warning signs:** Growing queue age, more reassignments, increasing waits for high-complexity cases, and divergent team-level outcomes.

## Adverse

- **Handover pattern:** Formal local-to-central transfers replace informal advice; cases bounce between queues and local teams.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Shared queues become bottlenecks, especially when technical staff also handle legal/risk triage and difficult quality checks.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Averages may remain stable initially, but tail times and SLA misses rise, especially for complex cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Poorer initial triage and lost regional knowledge increase rework and quality failures.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Local teams and shared services dispute ownership of delayed or incomplete cases.
- **Fairness implications:** North and South may lose expertise while East benefits; customers needing regional or language accommodation may face more transfers and delays.
- **Early warning signs:** Rapid growth in queue age, repeat referrals, missing-information rework, and worsening segment-specific outcomes.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** A stable average can conceal a worsening 90th or 95th percentile as some cases wait much longer in shared queues.
2. **High-complexity delays:** Fast routine cases can offset severe delays for complex cases; analyze waits, cycle time, and SLA misses by complexity and specialty.
3. **Local knowledge loss:** Reduced local expertise may increase rework or weaken regional decisions only after staff have handled fewer technical cases for some time.
4. **Fairness across teams and customers:** Improvement in East can mask deterioration in North or South, or for customer segments needing language or regional accommodation.
5. **Learning:** Routing hard cases away may reduce opportunities for local staff to build skills; monitor local resolution rates and staff feedback over time.
6. **Accountability:** Shared advice and approvals can leave ownership unclear, causing stalled cases or unresolved exceptions even if ordinary cases move quickly.
7. **Exception handling:** Informal local help may be replaced by a formal queue, making unusual cases more cumbersome.
8. **Resilience:** A shared service can concentrate expertise but also create a common point of failure during staff absence or demand spikes.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Pilot scope:** Start with a bounded East pilot for technical shared service, while retaining a named local technical liaison for early advice and regional context. Avoid moving legal, risk, difficult quality checks, and shared approvals all at once; phase those changes separately.
- **Comparison group:** Use North and South as comparison teams, while checking whether their case mix and trends are comparable. Compare pre/post changes rather than attributing every difference to the pilot.
- **Observation window:** Collect at least 8–12 weeks of baseline data and observe the pilot for 8–12 weeks, extending follow-up if outcome or rework data mature slowly.
- **Segmentation:** Report results by team, complexity, required specialty, and customer segment; also inspect priority and location where relevant.
- **Guardrails:** Track queue age and referral-to-start waits, 90th/95th percentile cycle time, SLA misses, repeat handovers, rework, outcomes, and customer satisfaction by segment. Set thresholds from baseline and service commitments before launch.
- **Staff feedback:** Collect weekly brief feedback from local caseworkers and shared specialists on ownership, access to advice, workload, and exception handling; review specific delayed or reworked cases.
- **Rollback or adjustment triggers:** Pause expansion if queue waits or tail cycle times breach agreed limits, rework or SLA misses rise materially for a segment, ownership problems persist, or staff report that urgent exceptions cannot be handled. Adjust routing, staffing, or local liaison capacity before resuming.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with a phased and bounded design.** The East team’s higher technical utilization and slower performance make it a reasonable place to test shared technical capacity, but the proposal bundles several structural changes whose effects would be difficult to distinguish if introduced together.

Expand only if the pilot shows acceptable queue waits and tail times, no material increase in rework or SLA misses, clear case ownership, and no sustained disadvantage for North, South, or customer segments. Management should not infer success from early average cycle-time improvements: backlog clearance, easier case mix, and gains in one team can mask longer waits for complex cases or worse outcomes elsewhere.
