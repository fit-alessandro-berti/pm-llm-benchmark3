The answer is unusually thorough and operationally thoughtful. It addresses stage eligibility, repeated alerts, calibration differences, uncertain effects, queue spillovers, fairness, feedback loops, and monitoring. It also shows its assumptions and makes the allocation conditional on them.

The main weakness is a mismatch between the stated allocation rule and the selected cases. It requires each case’s conservative direct benefit \(D_{\text{low}}\) to exceed estimated marginal queue harm. At the assumed €1,800 spillover debit per slot, I-02 fails that gate: its \(D_{\text{low}}\) is €950, while its midpoint score is €1,825. Yet I-02 is selected. The answer later discusses I-02’s sensitivity to congestion, but that does not resolve the inconsistency under the base assumption. The selection could be justified by a different uncertainty preference or portfolio-level comparison, but the rule would need to say so.

There are also smaller issues. It calls I-09’s central net score €1,150, but that is before the stated €1,800 queue debit; under the assumed base spillover, its marginal net is negative. The scenario table’s “direct breaches plausibly avoided” values are expected probability reductions, not observed breaches avoided, though it clarifies this in the surrounding text. The proposed one-in-four opportunity constraint is concrete but arbitrary and may be difficult to apply consistently. Finally, the specific cool-down, alert thresholds, and monitoring triggers are presented without evidence that these values are validated.

These flaws affect the central allocation decision, but the answer is explicit about uncertainty, exposes assumptions, and offers operational safeguards. The internal inconsistency prevents a higher grade.
