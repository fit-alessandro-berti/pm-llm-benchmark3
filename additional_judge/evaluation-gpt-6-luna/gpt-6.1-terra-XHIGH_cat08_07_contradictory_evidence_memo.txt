1. Executive memo

**Recommendation: adopt a limited hybrid (E).** Continue the simplified path only for verified simple, low-risk houses. Pause survey skipping for medium-risk or unclassified addresses and require a successful activation test before billing. Add permit checks where required, coordinator backup and shared case context. Do not scale Redesign R nationally yet.

The pilot shows a real trade-off. Median cycle time fell by 3.4 days, visible pre-complaint cost fell by EUR 23, and cases with three or more handovers dropped from 34% to 12%. But these gains coexist with worse p90 and p95 duration, first-time-right, complaints, second visits, manual activation, and estimated cost including rework. The gains are concentrated in simple houses; apartments and permit-sensitive old buildings show worse tails and quality. Faster typical cases do not offset evidence of harm in other segments.

The process-mining results support investigation, not a causal verdict. High fitness means the pilot model fits observed cases; low precision means it permits many behaviors. Frequent billing before successful activation and missing permit checks are alignment signals to verify against actual operations and event-log completeness. Sparse start timestamps, shifting regional mix, incomplete risk attributes, and immature downstream costs limit attribution.

Deploy the low-risk path in controlled stages, with risk eligibility verified and exceptions routed to a controlled process. In parallel, improve measurement and test survey skipping by risk stratum only where appropriate safeguards apply. Track quality, tail duration, complaints, complete downstream cost, and operational capacity against a concurrent comparator. Expand only when mature segment-level evidence meets pre-agreed guardrails.

3. Metric-conflict analysis

The median describes the middle case; it can improve while p90 and p95 worsen if a large share of straightforward cases gets faster while a smaller, riskier group accumulates long delays. The segment results fit that pattern: simple houses improved, while apartment and old-building tails deteriorated.

Lower handovers may reflect simpler ownership, but they do not establish better end-to-end outcomes. High coordinator utilization, increased second-visit workload, and stalled cases during coordinator absence suggest pressure points and weaker resilience. Fewer handovers can coexist with more rework.

Visible cost excludes much of the downstream handling. The lower pre-complaint cost and higher estimated inclusive cost therefore answer different questions. First-time-right, second visits, complaints, and manual activation all worsened, making the visible saving insufficient evidence of lower total cost.

High fitness measures how well the discovered model can replay observed cases; it does not show that the process is precise or desirable. Low precision and generalization, plus alignment deviations, point to varied or poorly captured behavior. They should prompt checks of event definitions, missing events, and actual controls—not be treated alone as proof of policy violations.

4. Decision options

| Option | Main benefit | Main drawback | When justified |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Scale R nationally | Extends the median-speed and visible-cost gains. | Exposes all segments to worse tail, quality, and service metrics with incomplete cost evidence. | Only after controlled evidence supports each segment and controls and capacity are adequate. |
| B. Roll back fully | Contains exposure to unresolved risks and restores the familiar path. | Gives up the apparent simple-house gains and may restore unnecessary waits and handovers. | If safeguards cannot be enforced or harm cannot be isolated. |
| C. Scale coordinator ownership only | Keeps clearer case ownership while suspending survey skipping and post-billing activation. | Coordinator overload, absence, and centralized knowledge remain concerns; ownership’s independent effect is unknown. | Where capacity, backup, and shared context are in place and evidence supports the ownership component. |
| D. Controlled regional experiment | Helps estimate effects with better measurement and comparison. | Delays some benefits and requires mature outcome windows and sufficient sample sizes. | For uncertain components and segments, with safeguards in place. |
| E. Limited hybrid | Preserves the strongest observed gains while containing exposure in higher-risk groups. | Risk misclassification and local capacity constraints could undermine the approach. | Best current choice if eligibility is verifiable, exceptions have a controlled path, and guardrails are monitored. |

5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by ability to change the near-term decision:

| Rank | Measurement or experiment | Decision value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M7: Monitor billing before activation and missing permit checks.** | Establishes the frequency and context of recorded deviations. Verify whether events are missing or checks occurred outside the log; confirmed control failures would support correction or pause. |
| 2 | **M6: Validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes.** | Shows whether risky addresses are wrongly classified as safe to skip. Poor or unstable performance would rule out skipping in affected groups. Include surveyed cases across risk levels to limit verification bias. |
| 3 | **M4: Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after billing.** | Tests whether savings survive complaint and revisit costs. Higher complete cost could block or narrow expansion. |
| 4 | **M3: Add structured reason codes for failures, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.** | Identifies failure modes that should shape targeted fixes and experiment design. |
| 5 | **M1: Randomize survey skipping by verified risk stratum.** | Estimates causal effects on speed, quality, tails, and cost; restrict to appropriate cases and preserve required controls. |
| 6 | **M5: Compare coordinator and shared-team ownership during coordinator absence.** | Tests whether backup and shared context reduce stalls without losing accountability. |
| 7 | **M2: Instrument start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation.** | Separates waiting from active work and helps locate queues; completion timestamps alone cannot do so. |
| 8 | **M8: Measure appointment speed and working-service experience separately.** | Shows whether faster appointments coincide with a worse experience of service after installation. |

Run the high-value control, rule-validation, cost, and reason-code work in parallel. For comparisons, use prospective assignment within region and verified risk strata, plan for tail and complaint outcomes, and follow cases through the relevant maturity windows. Report uncertainty and include in-flight cases so the longest cases are not excluded.

6. Final recommendation

Adopt **E**: continue the simplified path only for verified simple, low-risk houses in controlled stages. Pause survey skipping for medium-risk or unclassified addresses and routine post-billing activation checks. Verify permit handling, provide coordinator backup and shared case context, and monitor revisits and customer impact.

Start M7, M6, M4, and M3 now; prepare the safeguarded M1 experiment, and add M5, M2, and M8. Before testing, set segment-specific thresholds for tails, first-time-right, complaints, SLA breaches, complete cost, and staffing. Broader rollout should wait for mature, comparable evidence showing acceptable outcomes and verified controls.
