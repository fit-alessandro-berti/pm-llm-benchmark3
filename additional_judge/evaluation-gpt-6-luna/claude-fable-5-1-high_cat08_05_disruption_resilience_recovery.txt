**6/10** — The response is detailed and gives a useful recovery design, but its evaluation is weakened by treating several uncertain quantities and conclusions as established facts.

It correctly distinguishes resilience from concealed risk: prioritizing hospital-critical orders and deferring noncritical work can preserve essential supply; manual authorization, temporary release checks, and phone proof of delivery can be valid degraded controls when properly documented. Skipping authorization, relying on a stale inventory snapshot, and dispatching without a scan create material authorization, inventory, and traceability risks. The response also recognizes the pharmacy fill-rate and cancellation trade-offs, the unreliable-sync period, and the remediation tail, then proposes concrete controls, owners, and recovery measures.

The main weakness is overconfident inference. It presents estimates such as “~390 controlled” and “~640 cold-chain” gaps, “~1,500 excess” traceability exceptions, and “30% of backlog cancelled” as if directly supported, without showing the assumptions or calculations. It also makes firm judgments about what management should accept and when gaps become reportable without enough evidence about shipment-level records, applicable obligations, or recoverability. The strongest answer would label these as estimates or hypotheses, separate known event-log facts from inferred causes, and specify what evidence would resolve the uncertainty.
