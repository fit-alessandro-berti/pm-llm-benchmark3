## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

January–March provide a stable reference: metrics, label maturity, data quality, and route mix are broadly steady. V3 already has higher breach rates than V1 and V2, so changes in V3 share can move pooled metrics even without a model defect.

April is primarily a measurement and pipeline incident. The diagnosis rename disrupted prediction prefixes; duplicates and unknown activities also affected traces. The spike in predicted risk, poor AUROC and calibration, and 18% unmapped variants align with that incident. April should not be read as clean evidence of concept drift. May improves after the patch, but stale cached features and incomplete deduplication mean April records need replay before training or comparison.

June is a different pattern: the remote-routing policy moves V2 to 52%, while remote fix time remains stable and V2’s matured breach rate falls to 0.09. Pooled prevalence falls, but that alone does not imply the model failed. Evaluate performance at a fixed route mix and within variants.

July marks emerging supplier trouble: the parts-receipt median rises to 65 hours and V3’s matured breach rate to 0.46. August is more concerning: receipt time reaches 130 hours, V3 breach rate reaches 0.64, and pooled AUROC and calibration worsen. This is consistent with real process deterioration affecting V3, alongside a higher V3 share. Test whether the outcome relationship changed within comparable V3 cases.

September adds Partner Z and further V3 growth. Its 0.45 observed breach rate versus 0.27 elsewhere is not conclusive because Partner Z’s labels mature more slowly and its routes and customer profiles differ. The 82% overall maturity also limits pooled interpretation. Separate supplier and route effects from source mix using mature, comparable cases.

October is dominated by incomplete ascertainment: only 55% of labels are mature, delays are longest, and the backlog disproportionately affects long paths. Its low observed breach rate cannot establish improvement. Poor AUROC or calibration on these available labels may also be biased by which cases have reported outcomes. Treat October as provisional until fixed-vintage evaluation is possible.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment:** Mark October metrics provisional and prevent immature outcomes from being treated as non-breaches. Keep the production model and its versioned predictions available for comparison. Investigate supplier delays operationally; monitor V3 separately. Avoid using April’s reported features or predictions as a clean benchmark.

**Short-term recalibration:** Consider calibration only after validating clean, mature predictions and labels. June may be useful for a route-aware calibration check, but its policy-driven mix change requires fixed-mix evaluation. Do not calibrate on April’s corrupted prefixes, or on August–September without separating supplier deterioration from mix and population changes.

**Feature repair:** Reconstruct April and May lineage, normalize activity labels, deduplicate consistently, and recompute affected features from raw events. Add suitable prediction-time indicators for route, supplier exposure, and relevant service conditions only where they are available at diagnosis. Audit rural distance and device age for timing, quality, and missingness.

**Full retraining:** Decide after mature, repaired, chronological evaluation. If the conditional relationship changed within V3 after supplier deterioration, retrain or update with clean, representative, mature data and evaluate post-change performance. If the main issue is composition and calibration, preserve the model and use validated calibration or operational monitoring. Do not treat October as training-ready until outcomes mature.

January–March are the safest baseline training months. June may be usable with route and policy context. July–September require mature labels and explicit representation of the process and population changes. April is unsafe until replay repairs are complete; May is conditional on feature recomputation and lineage audit. October is unsafe for supervised training at this cutoff.

Variant-specific models are not yet justified by these aggregate tables. First establish within-variant failure on mature data and adequate sample size. A V3-specific model may be considered if conditional drift persists and prediction-time features support it; routing on a final realized path or future supplier delay would leak information.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

- **Predictive:** AUROC and other ranking measures by start month and fixed-vintage cohort, with sample counts and uncertainty.
- **Calibration:** Brier score, ECE, calibration curves, and observed-to-predicted risk by route, source, and month, only where labels meet a defined maturity standard.
- **Label maturity:** Maturity curves and delay distributions by route, source, and outcome; report unresolved-label share and changes in the available-label mix.
- **Data quality:** Activity-label coverage, unknown activity share, duplicate rates, feature missingness, prefix construction coverage, and raw-to-feature lineage checks.
- **Process variants:** Variant shares and unmapped cases, with explicit provisional status for incomplete traces.
- **Performance DFG:** Edge counts and elapsed-time distributions, especially Order Part → Part Received; include outstanding orders and censoring-aware time-to-receipt measures.
- **Population:** Source, rurality, device age, and route distributions; overlap checks and standardized comparisons for Partner Z.
- **Intervention and deployment:** Model and policy versions, thresholds, action exposure, and service outcomes, so changes in routing or intervention can be separated from model changes.

Alert dependencies should gate performance alarms on label maturity and data-quality validity. A label-delay alarm should trigger provisional status and fixed-vintage reporting, not automatic retraining. A pipeline alarm should trigger replay and feature repair before model diagnosis. Retraining review should require persistent degradation on mature, clean, comparable cohorts, especially within-variant evidence of changed conditional relationships.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from numbers import Integral

C, A, T = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"
raw = pd.read_csv("field_service_events.csv")
raw["_row"] = range(len(raw))
raw["raw_activity"] = raw["activity"]
raw["activity"] = raw["activity"].replace({
    "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed"
})
raw[T] = pd.to_datetime(raw["timestamp"], utc=True, errors="coerce")

# Prefer a stable event ID. Without one, remove only exact event duplicates;
# retain ambiguous same-case/activity/time rows for audited review.
if "event_id" in raw.columns:
    before = len(raw)
    raw = raw.drop_duplicates(["event_id"], keep="first")
    removed = before - len(raw)
else:
    keys = ["case_id", "activity", "timestamp"]
    before = len(raw)
    raw = raw.drop_duplicates(keys, keep="first")
    removed = before - len(raw)

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw, case_id="case_id", activity_key="activity", timestamp_key="timestamp"
).sort_values([C, T, "_row"], kind="stable")

def monthly_summaries(year, asof):
    asof = pd.Timestamp(asof)
    starts = df.loc[df[A].eq("Case Created")].groupby(C)[T].min()
    summaries = []

    def summarize(g):
        if g.empty:
            return {"cases": 0, "events": 0}
        dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(g)
        perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(g)
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(g)
        variant_counts = {
            str(v): int(n) if isinstance(n, Integral) else len(n)
            for v, n in variants.items()
        }

        edges = g[[C, A, T]].copy().sort_values([C, T], kind="stable")
        edges["next_activity"] = edges.groupby(C)[A].shift(-1)
        edges["next_time"] = edges.groupby(C)[T].shift(-1)
        edges["elapsed_s"] = (edges["next_time"] - edges[T]).dt.total_seconds()
        edge_medians = (
            edges.dropna(subset=["next_activity"])
            .groupby([A, "next_activity"])["elapsed_s"]
            .agg(count="size", median_seconds="median").reset_index()
        )
        return {
            "cases": int(g[C].nunique()), "events": len(g),
            "unknown_share": float(g[A].eq("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY").mean()),
            "variants": variant_counts, "dfg": dfg, "performance_dfg": perf_dfg,
            "edge_medians": edge_medians,
            "start_activities": start_activities, "end_activities": end_activities,
            "temporal_features": pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
                g, grouper_freq="MS"
            ),
        }

    for month in range(1, 13):
        lo = pd.Timestamp(year=year, month=month, day=1, tz="UTC")
        if lo > asof:
            break
        hi = lo + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)

        # Event-calendar cohort: events occurring within the month; paths are clipped.
        calendar = pm4py.filter_time_range(
            df, lo.isoformat(), hi.isoformat(), mode="events"
        )
        calendar = calendar.loc[(calendar[T] >= lo) & (calendar[T] < hi)]

        # Case-start cohort: all observed events through cutoff for cases started this month.
        case_ids = starts.index[(starts >= lo) & (starts < hi)]
        case_start = df.loc[df[C].isin(case_ids) & (df[T] <= asof)]

        for view, cohort in [("event_calendar", calendar), ("case_start", case_start)]:
            summaries.append({
                "month": lo.strftime("%Y-%m"), "view": view,
                "cutoff": asof.isoformat(), **summarize(cohort)
            })
    return summaries, {"removed_duplicate_rows": removed}
```

Event-calendar cohorts describe activity during a month but clip traces at its boundaries. Case-start cohorts follow cases begun that month through the cutoff and remain right-censored if unfinished. Neither should be treated as a completed variant distribution without checking closure and completeness. The sketch’s event missingness checks are not a substitute for joining diagnosis prefixes to the feature ledger. The PM4Py temporal output describes features; it does not itself establish drift.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

1. **Training on immature outcomes:** Treating pending labels as non-breaches or training only on quickly reported cases can teach the model that long V3 and Partner Z cases are safer. October’s apparent prevalence decrease is especially unreliable. Keep unresolved outcomes explicit and use fixed-vintage, maturity-aware comparisons.

2. **Calibrating around April’s pipeline incident:** Calibration could mask average overprediction while leaving incorrect prefixes, stale event counts, and missing scores. After repair, that adjustment may miscalibrate the model. Repair and replay first, then validate calibration on clean data.

3. **Attributing supplier deterioration to route mix alone:** More V3 cases raise aggregate risk, but V3’s matured breach rate also rises. Reweighting route shares alone could leave parts-exposed cases underpredicted and delay operational response. Track supplier waits, including outstanding orders, and test within-route outcomes.

4. **Treating Partner Z’s marginal gap as proof of model bias:** Its breach gap is confounded by route, rurality, device age, and slower label maturity. Compare mature cases within comparable profiles and routes, check overlap, and assess decision quality before considering a source-specific model or intervention.

5. **Retraining unnecessarily after June:** The V2 share rises while its breach rate and process time remain favorable. A changed mix can alter pooled metrics without a changed within-route relationship. Use fixed-mix and within-route evaluation before replacing the model.

6. **Leaking future information into a variant-specific model:** Choosing a model from the final realized route or using eventual parts delay would not be available at diagnosis. Enforce prediction-time feature availability and replay predictions chronologically.

7. **Mistaking incomplete traces or cleaned event counts for process improvement:** Calendar clipping, duplicate handling, and still-outstanding orders can bias variant and latency estimates. Keep cleaning audits, edge counts, separate cohorts, and censoring-aware waiting analyses.

8. **Ignoring intervention feedback:** Routing and supplier interventions can change both paths and outcomes. Record policy versions and exposure, and measure service outcomes alongside predictive metrics before interpreting later changes as model drift.
