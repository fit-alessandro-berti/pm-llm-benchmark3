The answer is detailed and decision-oriented, and it correctly rejects unconditional national scaling while recognizing that simple houses benefit and complex segments deteriorate. It also covers most requested process-mining evidence and ranks all eight measurement options.

However, it repeatedly presents unsupported inferences as established facts. It calls early billing “illegal” and claims regulatory exposure, stop-work orders, churn, and specific technical causes without evidence in the prompt. It invents thresholds, costs, timelines, staffing structures, and pilot findings—for example, a 3–4× truck-roll cost, “500 apartment cases,” an 8–12 week experiment, and claims that the EUR 207 estimate is substantially understated. It also misstates or overstates some figures: the 47% “failure spiral” combines apartments and old buildings without resolving possible overlap, and it says customer complaints rose by 60% when the supplied figures imply about 61% relative growth but do not establish the cause.

The evidence table does separate facts, interpretations, and uncertainties, but some entries cross those boundaries: the prompt does not say segment shares were extrapolated from weeks 3–6, nor that survey-skipping “directly fueled” the observed increases. The metric-conflict explanations likewise assert causal mechanisms and operational details not demonstrated by the data. Several alternatives are assessed using invented outcomes, such as rollback “restoring” FTR or precision and reducing utilization to baseline levels.

The recommendation is defensible in broad outline, but the memo should distinguish safeguards supported by the evidence from hypotheses to test, acknowledge pilot-region mix and incomplete attributes as limits on comparisons, and avoid prescribing hard numerical gates without a basis. Overall, it meets the requested structure and offers a plausible strategy, but its confident unsupported specificity substantially undermines its reliability.
