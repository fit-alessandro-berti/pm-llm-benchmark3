## 1. What is observed

Automation availability and actual automation use are different. Alpha and Beta had the system available from April; Gamma from July. Yet the automated fast path accounted for only 25–50% of their cases after launch, rising gradually from 4–6% before launch. The data therefore describe a branch-level offer of automation and a changing level of use, not universal case treatment.

Rollout was targeted: Alpha and Beta were selected for high volumes and poor historical cycle times, while Gamma followed a readiness pilot. Delta and Epsilon remained untreated through August. From April, routing sent about 10% more cases to Alpha and Beta, and about 5% more to Gamma after July. A fraud desk began serving all branches in March; automation routed somewhat more borderline cases there, but waiting times are unavailable. Staffing also changed, and a May product change increased high-complexity case shares in several branches.

Observed process measures move differently. Escalation rose in March across branches and increased further after adopters launched. Rework dipped in April for Alpha and Beta, then rose in May; Gamma’s rework fell after July. Cycle times worsened in May–June across adopters and nonadopters, then partly recovered. August medians were below January–March averages for adopters, while p90 values remained above those averages. CSAT is a monthly survey mean with response rates around 20–45%; response counts and respondent composition are unavailable. Branch-month summaries cannot reveal case-level path effects or sampling uncertainty.

Availability is a branch-level policy contrast; it includes local adaptations and any effect on routing. Actual use is a different treatment and requires stronger assumptions because adoption into the fast path is selective. Interference is plausible: branches share a fraud desk and routing system, so one branch’s rollout may affect others’ waiting times. “Automation” may also encompass different case eligibility, escalation, and learned work practices, making treatment versions nonuniform.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: cohort-specific event study / group-time ATT; report cohort and event-time effects, not a single TWFE coefficient | Availability from each branch’s actual launch month; dynamic effects by months since launch | Not-yet-treated Delta/Epsilon for Alpha/Beta; Delta/Epsilon for Gamma; avoid already-treated branches as controls | Branch and calendar-time baselines; pre-treatment outcomes; recorded arrivals/FTE, complexity, and routing indicators where available | Availability effect by adoption cohort and event time | Conditional parallel trends; no anticipation; no differential unmeasured shocks; limited spillovers; stable outcome definitions | Plot pre-trends; leave-one-control-out; event-time sensitivity; compare medians and p90; inspect overlap and sensitivity to May–June | Few branches, selected rollout, short post-period, and co-occurring shocks undermine assumptions |
| Matched-control difference-in-differences or synthetic control for each adopter/cohort | Launch date, with separate post periods | Weighted/matched Delta/Epsilon, selected on pre-period outcome levels and trends | Pre-period outcome trajectories, arrivals/FTE, complexity, and escalation | Adopter-specific availability effect | Good pre-fit; untreated potential outcomes track synthetic control; no differential shocks or spillovers | Pre-fit and placebo dates/branches; alternative donor weights; pre-period residuals | Only two untreated branches; matching cannot remove unobserved selection or post-launch shocks |
| Interrupted time series with controlled branches | April for Alpha/Beta and July for Gamma; allow immediate and slope changes | Nonadopting branches as concurrent controls | Calendar shocks and observed staffing, routing, and complexity changes | Immediate and gradual availability effects on branch outcome trajectory | Stable underlying trends; measured co-interventions; no coincident adopter-specific break | Pre-period trend and residual checks; alternative break dates; placebo outcomes and branches | Very short monthly series; May product change and branch-specific changes coincide with rollout |
| Case-level randomized phased rollout or randomized encouragement, prospectively | Randomize launch order or encouragement; record actual use and eligibility | Randomized later-launch branches or encouraged/nonencouraged cases | Baseline case risk and branch, prespecified | Availability ITT; encouragement-based use effect if instrument assumptions hold | Randomization integrity; for IV, exclusion and monotonicity; limited interference | Balance, compliance, attrition, contamination, queue spillovers | Not recoverable from supplied historical aggregate panel; IV exclusion may fail if encouragement changes workflow |

The cohort-specific event-study/ATT framework is preferable because adoption was staggered and effects plausibly vary with learning. It makes the timing and cohort comparisons visible, and avoids the opaque weighting that a conventional two-way fixed-effects coefficient can produce when already-treated branches serve as controls under heterogeneous effects. It also encourages reporting separate early and later effects rather than treating availability as a constant dose.

This remains a design proposal, not a source of credible precision from the current panel. There are only five branches, two never-treated branches, and three adopters with different launch dates. Pre-periods are short, and the treated branches were selected partly for poor historical performance. March fraud-desk changes, April routing, May case-mix changes, and staffing changes complicate conditional parallel trends. Adjustment for recorded variables cannot guarantee control of these differences; post-treatment measures may themselves be mechanisms. Report branch-level contrasts and sensitivity ranges, avoid conventional clustered p-values with five clusters, and treat event-study estimates as suggestive unless stronger data or design support becomes available.

## 5. Quantitative evidence and uncertainty

The raw changes are descriptive. From January–March to July–August, mean median cycle time fell by about 3.1 hours for Alpha, 0.8 for Beta, and 1.2 for Gamma; Delta fell 0.1 and Epsilon rose 0.3 hours. The pattern is not a controlled causal estimate: Alpha and Beta began from historically weaker performance, routing and staffing changed, and the May–June shock affected all branches.

A more focused arithmetic contrast compares May–June with July–August. Gamma’s median fell 9.2 hours; the average fall for Delta and Epsilon was 5.3 hours, giving a crude difference-in-differences of about −3.9 hours. Gamma’s rework fell 2.0 percentage points versus a 0.2-point fall in the two controls, a crude contrast of −1.8 points. Gamma’s p90 fell 13.7 hours versus 11.5 for controls, a crude contrast of −2.2 hours. These figures are consistent with a possible incremental improvement, but rely on one treated branch, two control branches, only two months in each comparison window, and no adjustment for Gamma-specific changes. In the same contrast, CSAT changed by −0.6 points for Gamma versus +0.1 for controls, which does not support a short-run satisfaction gain.

The April–May picture is mixed: Alpha and Beta rework fell in April, but their medians did not clearly improve; May–June medians and p90 worsened in adopters as well as controls. By August, adopter medians were below their January–March averages, but adopter p90 values remained roughly 3–12 hours above those averages. CSAT rose by August in all branches, so common recovery or survey composition may explain part of the rebound.

No single percent reduction is defensible. The panel does not identify the effect of availability separately from selection and concurrent changes, and it cannot identify the effect of actual use. Branch-month medians and p90s do not reveal case-level causal effects. The crude Gamma contrast is a useful descriptive signal, not a precise effect estimate; conventional standard errors would also be unreliable with only five branch clusters. A sensitivity range should not be presented as a confidence interval or as a validated causal bound.

## 6. Recommended rollout and learning policy

Do not communicate “automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a causal organization-wide claim from these data. A narrower, supportable statement is that automated fast-path use increased after launch; outcomes varied by branch and month; and Gamma’s post-June recovery was larger than that of the two nonadopters on median cycle time and rework in a short, unadjusted comparison. That pattern is compatible with benefit but does not establish it.

For expansion, use a randomized phased rollout across eligible branches if feasible, assigning launch order by lottery and preserving a meaningful later-start comparison group. If randomization is infeasible, use a prespecified matched or synthetic-control plan with a longer pre-period, transparent cohort-specific estimates, and no use of already-treated branches as controls. Keep recording availability and actual use separately; capture case-level eligibility, risk at registration, path and timestamps, specialist/fraud queue waits, rework loops, staffing, arrivals, routing, and survey response counts and composition.

Track median and p90 cycle time, rework, escalation, and customer outcomes together. Define operational guardrails for tail time, error/rework, and service quality before rollout, and pause or investigate if guardrails are breached. Evaluate early and mature periods separately to capture learning. This approach allows expansion to generate operational experience while preserving comparisons that can distinguish automation effects from demand, staffing, routing, and case-mix changes.
