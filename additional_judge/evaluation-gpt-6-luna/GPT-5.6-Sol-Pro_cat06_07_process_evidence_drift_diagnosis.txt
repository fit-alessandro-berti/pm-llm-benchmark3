## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

January through March provide a stable baseline: high AUROC, low calibration error, low unknown activity, and broadly stable route mix.

April is primarily a pipeline incident. The diagnosis rename disrupted prediction prefixes, while duplicates and unmapped activities increased. Risk rose well above the observed breach rate, and both discrimination and calibration worsened. The May patch improved the aggregates, but incomplete recomputation of April features means April remains unsafe for clean comparisons.

May is a partial recovery, with near-baseline breach rate and much better metrics. The residual gap could reflect incomplete backfill, remaining pipeline defects, or ordinary variation; it needs replay and lineage checks.

June is a composition and process-policy change. V2 usage rose from about a third to just over half, and V2 has a lower historical breach rate. The aggregate observed breach rate fell while within-variant rates stayed broadly similar. This pattern is consistent with mix change, not necessarily a newly failing model.

July marks emerging supplier-process deterioration: part-receipt time rose to 65 hours, and V3’s matured breach rate increased to 0.46. The global metrics also softened. This is evidence of a new operational problem and a possible conditional predictive shift, but the evidence does not yet isolate its effect on model quality.

August–September should not be collapsed into June’s route-mix change. The parts edge stretched further, and matured V3 breach rates rose sharply. At the same time, V3 became more common. September also introduced Partner Z, which has more rural cases and greater V3 usage. Thus operational deterioration within V3, changing mix, and a new population overlap. September’s aggregate metrics also use incomplete labels, so they cannot quantify these effects cleanly.

October’s apparent breach rate falls to 0.16 even as risk averages 0.23 and metrics remain poor. With only 55% label maturity and a backlog that disproportionately delays long paths, this is likely a censored snapshot, not evidence that the process recovered. October is not suitable for stable outcome-performance conclusions or ordinary supervised training.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

Immediately contain April-derived predictions: identify affected cases, reconstruct prefixes and features, and replay the frozen model. Keep October predictions provisional and expose label maturity alongside outcome metrics. Address the supplier delay through operations; an edge alarm alone should not trigger model retraining.

For short-term calibration, first validate corrected features and mature labels. Then test calibration by variant and source. A hierarchical or segment-specific calibrator may be appropriate if stable within-segment ranking remains but risk levels differ. June may need composition-aware reporting or calibration, while August–September may need a V3-specific response. Avoid a global adjustment that conceals those differences.

Repair feature pipelines before retraining. Reconsider missing or stale process, supplier, rural-distance, device-age, and route-intent features, but only if available at prediction time. A variant-specific model is justified only if the route is known at diagnosis time and there are enough mature examples; the realized final path must not leak future information.

January–March are the cleanest training baseline. May and June may be usable after lineage verification and with route-aware evaluation. Exclude April until recomputation is complete; treat July–September as potentially different process regimes and use only mature, audited labels for suitable analyses. Exclude October from ordinary supervised training until labels mature or censoring is addressed. Retrain when clean mature data show persistent within-stratum deterioration and validated features capture the changed regime.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

Track:

- Predictive quality: AUROC and Brier by start cohort, with sample counts and confidence intervals.
- Calibration: ECE, reliability plots, and calibration intercept/slope by variant and source.
- Label maturity: fraction labeled and delay curves by cohort, variant, and source.
- Data quality: unknown activities, duplicate rate, missingness, prefix validity, and feature lineage.
- Process variants: variant proportions, unmapped share, and route changes.
- Performance DFG: edge durations and counts, especially `Order Part -> Part Received`.
- Population: source, rurality, device age, overlap, and score/feature distributions.
- Intervention and deployment: score thresholds, actions, capacity, policy version, and outcomes after intervention.

Alert dependencies should distinguish symptoms from causes. A label-delay alert marks outcome metrics provisional and blocks training gates; it does not itself trigger retraining. A data-lineage alarm blocks affected evaluations until replay. Process-edge deterioration triggers operational investigation. Retraining requires clean lineage, sufficiently mature labels, and evidence of sustained within-stratum predictive deterioration.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

Training on October’s current labels could teach the model that unresolved long-path cases are low risk. Recalibrating around April’s pipeline defect could compensate for malformed inputs and become wrong after repair. Treating August–September as variant mix alone would miss the sharp V3 breach and supplier-edge deterioration. Treating June’s routing change as proof of model failure could prompt unnecessary retraining when within-variant rates remained broadly stable.

A blanket penalty for Partner Z could confound rurality, V3 routing, supplier conditions, and slower maturity. Using the realized final variant as an input would leak future information. A global recalibrator could mask opposite effects across V2 and V3. Finally, aggressive duplicate removal could erase legitimate repeated events and distort process evidence; preserve raw records and audit the rule.
