The response recognizes that default is observed only after activation and flags selection bias, but its cohort design and analysis repeatedly contradict that insight. The proposed target’s prediction time is 90 days *after* activation, so it cannot serve as an early warning based on the first few events. It also says the population is all applications while defining an activated-account outcome, leaving the estimand inconsistent.

The cohort flow does not account for all 48,000 applications. Its counts are miscategorized or duplicated: 18,900 non-default and 2,700 default are mixed under the wrong status labels; the 3,600 immature activated accounts are not the 3,600 missing feeds; and the table’s totals do not reconcile. Excluding omitted applications while later calling some censored or competing also conflicts. Rejected and withdrawn cases have outcomes undefined under activation, not ordinary right censoring. Transfer needs explicit treatment as competing or lost follow-up, with linkage uncertainty; permanent feed loss needs a missingness strategy. The sensitivity proposal to assign rejected cases a zero default risk repeats the central error the task warns against.

Prefix extraction is not properly specified: “last n events” is described as a time window, and elapsed time from Credit Review does not define a prediction landmark or prevent future-event inclusion. Discarding short cases risks selection bias. Features such as time to activation/default are post-prediction leakage; the audit’s rules for final limit, decision reason, documents, and risk score are insufficiently tied to an actual early prediction point. It also fails to address same-day future documents and extraction-time resource experience adequately.

The required leakage prevention and cohort accounting are absent.

The answer contains some useful cautions about policy and migration shifts, but the target semantics, cohort construction, censoring logic, and implementation have major flaws.
