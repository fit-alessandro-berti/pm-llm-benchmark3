# 1. Mechanism map

The change shifts work from local specialist loops to shared queues. It could reduce some transfers while adding queue waits and new ownership boundaries.

| Restructuring action | Mechanism | Observable process-mining effects |
|---|---|---|
| Merge local technical specialists | Fewer local caseworker–tech–caseworker loops, but more referrals into and returns from the Technical Shared Service (TSS). | Handover network: local tech edges may shrink while caseworker→TSS and TSS→caseworker edges grow. Track transfers per case and their wait-time distributions, not just total edges. Working-together networks may show less local pairing and more cross-team collaboration. |
| Route high-complexity and specialty cases centrally; combine technical, legal/risk, and difficult quality work | Work pools in shared queues. Competition among work types may create a bottleneck, especially for Legal/Risk, already at 91–93% utilization. | Handover waits and performance DFG times may rise at specialist and quality-check steps. Track waiting-time percentiles, queue depth if available, reassignment counts, and waits by complexity and specialty. |
| Raise specialist utilization | Higher utilization can use available capacity more effectively, but less spare capacity can increase waits and make absences costly. | Resource activity counts and service-time measures show workload; handover waits and cycle-time tails show whether capacity is becoming a constraint. Utilization alone does not establish improved throughput. |
| Standardize approvals and remove a local supervisor | Decisions may become more consistent, while responsibility for the case and decision may become less clear. | Compare approval variants and performance DFG edges. Watch reassignments, “clarify ownership” handover reasons, activity-resource similarity, and discovered role patterns. |
| Reduce local specialist availability and informal advice | More unnecessary referrals or weaker regional context could increase rework, particularly for complex and accommodation cases. | Track `local_expertise_flag`, rework flags, rework cases per activity, variants containing Rework, and outcomes by complexity and customer segment. |
| Reduce ad hoc cross-team cover | Fewer cross-team handovers may help routine cases; reliance on a smaller shared pool could weaken resilience when central staff are absent. | Track handover and waiting-time patterns during absences, reassignment counts, and changes in working-together and subcontracting networks. |
| Apply the same routing rules across teams and customers | Benefits and costs may differ: East may gain from relieving its overloaded specialist, while North/South lose embedded expertise. Regional and language needs may require extra coordination. | Compare p50/p90/p95 cycle times, SLA misses, rework, handovers, and outcomes by team, complexity, specialty, and customer segment. |

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic

- **Handover pattern:** Local technical loops decline. Referrals to TSS and returns through its queue rise as expected, while cross-team cover remains low.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** More work reaches specialists, but staffing and routing keep waits controlled. Legal/Risk demand does not materially exceed available capacity.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East’s median and tail improve. North and South remain near baseline; p90 and p95 do not worsen for complex cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standardized advice and quality checks reduce avoidable rework without losing useful local context. Outcomes remain stable or improve across segments.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Shared queues have clear service boundaries, and a named local case owner remains responsible for customer communication and follow-through.
- **Fairness implications:** East benefits without degrading North/South service. Language and regional accommodation cases have comparable waits and rework after controlling for complexity.
- **Early warning signs:** No sustained rise in specialist handover p90, reassignment counts, rework, or segment gaps; staff report timely advice and clear ownership.

## Central

- **Handover pattern:** Local tech loops fall, but central referrals and returns rise. The transfer count may look lower overall even as cases spend longer waiting at shared queues.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Median waits rise moderately at TSS, approval, and Legal/Risk. The tail grows most for high-complexity and specialty cases as utilization increases.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Simple cases and East may improve, keeping the overall average stable or better. High-complexity p90/p95 and some team-level SLA misses worsen.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standardization helps routine cases, while loss of local context raises rework for complex, regional, or accommodation cases.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Shared approval decisions are more consistent, but “who owns the next action?” becomes less clear. Reassignments and clarification handovers rise.
- **Fairness implications:** East may gain while North/South lose access to local expertise. Some customer segments experience longer waits or more transfers.
- **Early warning signs:** Rising p90 handover waits, more reassignment, new variants with repeated specialist or approval steps, widening team/segment gaps, and declining role-clarity feedback.

## Adverse

- **Handover pattern:** Local loops decline, but referrals, returns, and reassignment grow as work waits, is redirected, or needs clarification. Apparent reductions in cross-team handovers mask more central dependencies.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** TSS becomes a bottleneck while also handling Legal/Risk triage and difficult quality checks. Already highly utilized Legal/Risk capacity becomes saturated; absence at a central queue causes broad delays.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Simple cases may remain fast, concealing a pronounced increase in p90/p95, high-complexity SLA misses, and long-stalled cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework rises from missing local context, unclear ownership, or delayed quality checks. Standardization does not compensate for weaker exception handling.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases circulate among caseworkers, specialists, and approvers without clear ownership; responsibility for decisions and customer follow-up is disputed.
- **Fairness implications:** North/South, complex cases, and customers needing regional or language accommodation may face disproportionate waits and rework. East’s outcome is uncertain: its local overload may ease, but it remains exposed to the same central queue.
- **Early warning signs:** Growing queue waits and reassignment counts, sharp p95 increases, repeated approval/specialist variants, higher rework by segment, and staff reports of unavailable advice or unclear accountability.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** A stable mean can coexist with a large rise in p90/p95 waits or a small number of severely delayed cases. Track percentiles and SLA misses, not only averages.
2. **High-complexity cases:** Faster simple cases can offset slower complex cases in an overall average. Compare cycle-time distributions and rework within complexity and specialty strata.
3. **Local knowledge loss:** Removing embedded specialists may eliminate informal advice that prevents avoidable referrals. Rework or customer friction may appear later, and may be concentrated in regional or complex cases.
4. **Fairness across teams and customers:** East may improve while North/South worsen; accommodation customers may face more handovers. An overall average can conceal these disparities.
5. **Learning and skill atrophy:** Routing difficult cases away can reduce local staff’s exposure and future capability. Event logs can show changing resource–activity patterns, but staff feedback or separate capability assessments are also needed.
6. **Accountability and role clarity:** A shared approval may standardize decisions while obscuring who owns the case or customer follow-up. Reassignments and clarification handovers are signals, but do not fully establish accountability.
7. **Exception handling:** A standard queue may handle routine work efficiently but struggle with absence cover, unusual cases, language needs, or regional conventions. Exception variants and handover reasons should be examined separately.
8. **Resilience:** Fewer cross-team transfers may look efficient while increasing dependence on a central pool. Performance during absences or queue disruption is needed to assess whether the process can absorb shocks.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Scope:** Start with a limited TSS pilot for high-complexity technical cases in East, where E_Tech is already highly utilized and cycle times are longest. Keep local informal technical triage available. Do not broaden Legal/Risk routing while those specialists are near capacity unless capacity or explicit queue limits are added. Pilot shared approval separately for routine medium-value cases, with a named case owner. Defer centralizing difficult Quality Check until its queue capacity is demonstrated.
- **Comparison group:** Keep North and South on the current technical model initially. Compare East’s pre/post results with North/South changes over the same period, and compare East’s routed high-complexity cases with its other cases. Treat this as a monitored pilot, not a clean randomized experiment; record changes in case mix, staffing, and seasonality.
- **Observation window:** Use 8–12 weeks of baseline and 8–12 weeks of pilot, with weekly reporting and a stabilization period after each scope change. Follow outcome and rework measures long enough for those outcomes to mature.
- **Segmentation:** Report each metric by team, complexity, required specialty, customer segment, and priority. Include handover reason and local expertise participation where available.
- **Guardrails:** Monitor p50/p90/p95 cycle time; handover-wait percentiles at each shared queue; SLA misses; rework and outcomes; reassignment counts; specialist utilization; and gaps between teams and customer segments. Set numerical thresholds before launch, using baseline variation and SLA commitments. Do not let improved overall averages compensate for deterioration in a protected segment.
- **Staff feedback:** Run a short weekly pulse on access to informal advice, clarity of case ownership, ability to handle exceptions, and regional/language support. Hold monthly discussions with local caseworkers, specialists, approvers, and central queue staff; compare reported issues with event-log patterns.
- **Adjustment or rollback triggers:** Adjust routing or add capacity if queue waits rise persistently, particularly for high-complexity or Legal/Risk cases. Restore local triage or narrow eligibility if rework, reassignment, or segment gaps increase. Pause or roll back the affected component if high-complexity tail times or SLA misses breach pre-agreed limits, quality deteriorates, or central staff absence causes broad delays. Keep technical, approval, and quality-check changes separable so the failing component can be corrected.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with a narrower design.** East’s high specialist utilization and poorer cycle-time results make it a reasonable place to test whether shared technical capacity helps. The proposed central queues also create credible bottleneck and context-loss risks: Legal/Risk is already near capacity, North and South have valuable local expertise, and shared approval may blur ownership.

Start with high-complexity technical referrals in East while preserving local triage and named case ownership. Expand approval or Legal/Risk centralization only after separate evidence that capacity, tail waits, quality, and role clarity remain acceptable. Continue only if segmented p90/p95 times, SLA misses, rework, fairness gaps, and staff feedback stay within pre-agreed guardrails.

Management should not infer success from early averages. Simple cases, automation, or East’s improvement could mask longer tails for complex cases or worsening outcomes for North/South and accommodation customers. Early averages also cannot establish that learning, resilience, or accountability have been preserved.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

This sketch assumes before/after event data are available and that case-level attributes are consistent across each case. The SLA threshold and the meaning of `outcome` must be defined from the actual service rules. If attributes vary within a case, resolve them at case level before filtering.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TIME = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"
TEAM = "team"
COMPLEXITY = "case_complexity"
SPECIALTY = "required_specialty"
SEGMENT = "customer_segment"
REWORK = "rework_flag"
REASSIGN = "reassignment_count"

def prepare(raw):
    df = raw.copy()
    df[TIME] = pd.to_datetime(df[TIME], utc=True)
    return pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id=CASE,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TIME,
        resource_key=RES,
    )

before = prepare(raw_before)
after = prepare(raw_after)

def networks(log):
    return {
        "handover": pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log),
        "working_together": pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log),
        "subcontracting": pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log),
        "resource_similarity":
            pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log),
        "roles": pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log),
    }

def dfgs(log):
    dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    perf_dfg, perf_starts, perf_ends = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    return {
        "dfg": dfg,
        "starts": starts,
        "ends": ends,
        "performance_dfg": perf_dfg,
        "performance_starts": perf_starts,
        "performance_ends": perf_ends,
    }

def waiting_and_rework(log):
    with_waiting = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
    return {
        "service_time": pm4py.get_service_time(with_waiting),
        "rework_by_activity": pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log),
        "temporal_features": pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log),
    }

def case_table(log, sla_days):
    df = pd.DataFrame(log).sort_values([CASE, TIME])
    cases = df.groupby(CASE).agg(
        start=(TIME, "min"),
        end=(TIME, "max"),
        team=(TEAM, "first"),
        complexity=(COMPLEXITY, "first"),
        specialty=(SPECIALTY, "first"),
        segment=(SEGMENT, "first"),
        rework=(REWORK, "max"),
        reassignments=(REASSIGN, "max"),
    ).reset_index()

    cases["cycle_days"] = (
        (cases["end"] - cases["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    )
    cases["sla_miss"] = cases["cycle_days"] > sla_days
    return cases

def summarize_cases(log, sla_days):
    cases = case_table(log, sla_days)
    return cases.groupby(
        ["team", "complexity", "specialty", "segment"],
        dropna=False
    ).agg(
        cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
        p50_days=("cycle_days", "median"),
        p90_days=("cycle_days", lambda x: x.quantile(.90)),
        p95_days=("cycle_days", lambda x: x.quantile(.95)),
        sla_miss_rate=("sla_miss", "mean"),
        rework_rate=("rework", "mean"),
        mean_reassignments=("reassignments", "mean"),
    )

def filter_segment(log, attribute, values):
    # PM4Py filter for event attributes; use equivalent case-level filtering
    # if the attribute is not present consistently on all events in a case.
    return pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, attribute, values, positive=True
    )

def compare(before_log, after_log, sla_days):
    return {
        "networks": {
            "before": networks(before_log),
            "after": networks(after_log),
        },
        "dfgs": {
            "before": dfgs(before_log),
            "after": dfgs(after_log),
        },
        "waiting_rework": {
            "before": waiting_and_rework(before_log),
            "after": waiting_and_rework(after_log),
        },
        "case_summary": {
            "before": summarize_cases(before_log, sla_days),
            "after": summarize_cases(after_log, sla_days),
        },
    }

overall = compare(before, after, sla_days=7)

# Repeat the comparison on explicit strata; preserve the same strata in both periods.
strata = {
    "East": (TEAM, ["East"]),
    "North": (TEAM, ["North"]),
    "South": (TEAM, ["South"]),
    "high_complexity": (COMPLEXITY, ["high"]),
    "legal_risk_security": (SPECIALTY, ["legal", "risk", "security"]),
    "accommodation": (
        SEGMENT, ["language_accommodation", "regional_accommodation"]
    ),
}

segmented = {}
for name, (attribute, values) in strata.items():
    b = filter_segment(before, attribute, values)
    a = filter_segment(after, attribute, values)
    segmented[name] = compare(b, a, sla_days=7)

# Examine rework variants as a complement to the explicit rework flag.
variants_before = pm4py.get_variants(before)
variants_after = pm4py.get_variants(after)

rework_traces_before = [
    variant for variant in variants_before
    if any("Rework" in str(activity) for activity in variant)
]
rework_traces_after = [
    variant for variant in variants_after
    if any("Rework" in str(activity) for activity in variant)
]

before_rework_cases = pm4py.filter_variants(before, rework_traces_before)
after_rework_cases = pm4py.filter_variants(after, rework_traces_after)
```

Compare network edge weights and performance distributions on the same case strata. A network or discovered role change describes observed assignments; it does not by itself prove clearer accountability, fairer service, or improved learning.
