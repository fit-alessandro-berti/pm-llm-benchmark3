# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

I distinguish exploration, conformance enforcement, simulation, and stakeholder communication. Exploration can tolerate a permissive model that helps surface missing events, provided it is clearly labeled and never mistaken for policy. Enforcement needs reliable rejection of forbidden behavior and acceptance of required behavior; high fitness alone is not enough. Simulation needs defensible choices about optional paths and rework. Stakeholder communication needs readable, maintainable rules.

The process is only partly specified. In particular, the log lacks the sourcing attribute needed to make Supplier Review conditional and the affected-check attribute needed to determine what repeats after correction. Those gaps prevent a fully precise control-flow model. The probes are policy tests, not a complete specification.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

**Strongest case for S:** it is compact, fast in the reported runs, easy to explain at a high level, and tolerant of missing or unfamiliar events. That can help exploratory discovery where coverage is more valuable than policy fidelity.

**Strongest case against S:** it makes mandatory checks optional. In `X('Credit Check', tau)` and `X('Technical Review', tau)`, the silent branch allows either check to be skipped. `tau` also permits silent behavior, making it unclear which visible activities are genuinely required. The model is therefore unsafe for enforcement or policy simulation.

Concrete undesirable or ambiguous behavior it admits:

- `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` — skips both mandatory checks.
- `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — expedites before the mandatory checks.
- `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — can repeat a check without correction.
- `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Correct Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` — correction can be followed by no recheck.
- A trace with both `Manager Approval` and `Auto Approval` may be admitted because `X` commonly denotes exclusive choice, but its exact parsing semantics need confirmation; the model does not clearly document the intended P5 rule.
- A trace omitting Supplier Review may be admitted for an external order, because sourcing is unavailable to the model.

The last case reflects a genuine missing attribute. The skipped mandatory checks and misplaced Expedite Review are accidental semantic flaws, not justified noise tolerance. Noise tolerance should be handled through logging diagnostics or a separate exploratory view, not by making policy requirements optional.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

**Strongest case for C:** it encodes several concrete legal sequences, includes both mandatory checks, and avoids some of S’s obvious skip behavior. A finite variant catalog can be useful for audit discussion when the approved variants are known and deliberately maintained.

**Strongest case against C:** its branches enumerate selected sequences rather than expressing the policy relationships. It omits valid combinations such as B2’s Auto Approval with alternate check order and B5’s Supplier Review, Expedite Review, and Auto Approval combination. Its 0.744 holdout fitness is consistent with that brittleness, although the holdout’s policy status is unknown.

C’s correction branch repeats both checks after correction, even though policy says only affected checks should repeat. It cannot represent a correction affecting only Technical Review or only Credit Check. Its duplicate-label count of 33 is undefined, but branch enumeration can create repeated visible activity instances, complicating ownership and impact analysis. The informal comprehension score and simplicity score also suggest a maintenance burden.

High training precision may be an artifact of memorizing frequent training variants: C restricts behavior to a short list, and the reported precision was computed against positive traces only. It says nothing about a curated negative suite. C’s strictness may be appropriate as a review queue for unapproved behavior, but the current evidence does not establish that its listed sequences are complete policy.

# 6. Metric and Evidence Pathologies

The reported metrics are not enough to rank the models. Training and holdout fitness depend on split, preprocessing, alignment costs, and case composition, none of which are supplied. The 80/20 split is not reproducible without seeds and case IDs. Holdout degradation for C could reflect overfitting, legitimate new combinations, logging noise, or policy violations; F4 makes these alternatives concrete.

Positive-only alignment precision does not test whether forbidden traces are accepted. Token-based generalization is not a policy metric. Arc-degree simplicity is only one structural proxy. Median runtime hides tail latency. The duplicate-label count has no documented formula, and the stakeholder score has no sampling or task protocol.

The evaluation needs fixed preprocessing and alignment settings, reproducible splits, separate positive and curated negative suites, explicit probe outcomes, soundness checks, and runtime distributions. Report each metric separately with its definition and scope.

# 7. Third Model: Policy-Constrained Partial Order with Explicit Uncertainty

Use a compact model that expresses mandatory ordering constraints, approval choice, and controlled rework, while keeping unresolved attributes explicit. A readable policy sketch is:

```text
Register Dossier
→ (Credit Check || Technical Review)
→ [optional Expedite Review, only after both checks]
→ (Supplier Review optional while sourcing is unknown;
   required when sourcing = external)
→ XOR(Manager Approval, Auto Approval)
→ Release Order
```

Supplier Review and Expedite Review may occur in either order, provided Expedite Review follows both mandatory checks. The model should not encode Supplier Review as unconditionally optional in an enforcement system: until `sourcing` is available, its absence should raise a review flag for possible external orders.

Represent correction as an explicit loop to `Correct Dossier`, followed by a decision over the affected-check set:

```text
Correct Dossier
→ XOR(
    Credit Check,
    Technical Review,
    Credit Check → Technical Review
  )
```

The check subset must be supplied by the correction record. If it is absent, flag the case for review rather than silently accepting arbitrary repeats. Enforce P7 by disallowing repeated mandatory checks unless preceded by correction and named in the affected-check set.

This partial-order and guarded-choice design accepts both check orders and combinations of already allowed behavior without enumerating whole traces. A Petri net or declarative constraint model can implement it, with clear ownership for each rule. It should distinguish hard constraints (P1, P2, P4 ordering, P5, and P7) from conditional or unresolved rules (P3 and P6).

# 8. Validation Plan and Falsification Tests

Before using the third model for enforcement, test it against B1–B8 and a curated suite that includes:

- Negative cases for missing mandatory checks, early Expedite Review, both approvals, neither approval, release before approval, and repeated checks without correction.
- Positive combinations that vary check order, approval type, Supplier Review, and Expedite Review independently where policy allows.
- Rework cases for each affected-check subset, plus repeats that do not match the recorded subset.
- External and internal cases, including missing or contradictory sourcing data.
- Noisy logs with deliberately omitted events, scored separately from policy conformance.

Publish case IDs, split seed, preprocessing, alignment costs, model conversion, and tool versions. Report training and holdout fitness, positive precision, negative-suite acceptance, generalization, simplicity, duplicate-label definition, median and tail runtime, and soundness separately. Have compliance, operations, and IT raters perform concrete acceptance and change-impact tasks. A must-reject probe accepted by the model, or a must-accept probe rejected without a documented exception, should block enforcement use.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

Retire Model S as a control, compliance simulation, or audit artifact. It may remain available for exploration if it is clearly labeled as a permissive coverage map. F7 is a reasonable use-case limitation, not a waiver for using S as policy.

Do not treat Model C as the complete process model. Retain its sequences as a frequent-variant catalog and a source of positive examples. Its strictness can support a review queue for unseen behavior, but its 0.941 training precision does not establish compliance precision.

Use the third model as a candidate for communication and detective review, subject to validation. Do not enable hard blocking of Supplier Review or correction rechecks until sourcing and affected-check data are available. Before deployment, require reproducible evaluation settings, soundness, probe-suite results, a curated negative suite, and stakeholder review. Unseen combinations should enter a change-review process rather than be silently accepted or automatically labeled noncompliant.

# 11. Dissenting View

A conservative alternative is to use Model C as an allow-list and send every unseen trace to a change board. This may suit a process where unapproved combinations are unacceptable by default. But the current evidence does not show that C’s enumerated variants are complete or that its rejected holdout traces are violations. Its correction branch also conflicts with the stated affected-check policy.

The decision should change if compliance-labeled holdout cases show that traces accepted by the third model are mostly violations, or if policy requires an explicit allow-list. In that case, maintain C’s catalog through a formal variant approval workflow. Conversely, if sourcing and affected-check data remain unavailable, keep those rules in review status rather than treating either model’s behavior as settled policy.
