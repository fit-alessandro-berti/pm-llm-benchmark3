# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

I distinguish four uses:

- **Exploration:** expose plausible structure and combinations for discussion. A permissive model can help, but its omissions and assumptions must be visible.
- **Conformance enforcement:** decide whether a case may proceed. Mandatory checks and known forbidden behavior matter more than fitting noisy logs; no unverified assumption should silently become a pass.
- **Simulation:** generate possible cases and estimate outcomes. The model needs explicit assumptions and loop bounds; simulation results are only as sound as those assumptions.
- **Stakeholder communication:** help people understand and apply the process. Readability and correct judgments on policy examples matter, not just a high informal comprehension score.

For exploration, some fitness loss may be acceptable to avoid overfitting. For enforcement, neither aggregate fitness nor precision compensates for admitting forbidden traces or rejecting valid combinations. Comparisons here are provisional: the teams did not publish enough detail to reproduce them.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

**Strongest case for S:** It is compact and easy to discuss. The optional/parallel structure may tolerate missing or noisy observations and novel combinations. Its high reported holdout fitness and low runtime make it an attractive discovery baseline.

**Strongest case against S:** Its semantics contradict mandatory checks and ordering constraints. It treats all four reviews as optional, permits approval without checks, and permits expedite before checks. That is a policy flaw, not a noise-tolerance feature. It also treats Supplier Review as unconditionally optional without distinguishing external orders. Its apparent usability can normalize unsafe behavior.

Concrete undesirable or ambiguous accepted traces include:

1. `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` — skips both mandatory checks (B3).
2. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — expedite precedes checks (B4).
3. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — repeats a mandatory check without correction (P7).
4. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order` — same suspicious repeat, with the other check completed.
5. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Auto Approval, Release Order` — accepted, but S does not enforce any sourcing-dependent supplier requirement.
6. `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — accepted despite skipping mandatory checks.
7. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` — accepted while skipping checks and placing expedite too early.

The model’s tolerance for missing events may be useful in exploration, but it is not a defensible way to represent mandatory policy. Missing-event tolerance should be an explicit diagnostic or data-quality treatment, not an implicit permission to omit checks.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

**Strongest case for C:** It explicitly includes some common ordering variants, a supplier-review variant, an expedite variant, a correction variant, and one auto-approval variant. It is far more restrictive than S and could serve as a regression fixture for those observed traces.

**Strongest case against C:** Its eight enumerated traces are a catalog, not a compositional specification of the rules. It rejects combinations of behaviors that the stated policy permits: B2 uses the alternate check order with Auto Approval; B5 combines alternate order, Supplier Review, Expedite Review, and Auto Approval. This helps explain its holdout degradation and can turn legitimate novelty into apparent noncompliance.

The high training precision may be an artifact of enumerating frequent training variants. It says little about negative policy behavior because the auditor reports that precision used only observed positive traces. The model provides only one correction path, repeating both checks, although P6 allows only affected checks to repeat and the affected-check attribute is absent. It has no meaningful representation of the suspicious-repeat rule beyond which traces happen to be listed.

Branch enumeration also makes updates and impact analysis difficult: policy changes require editing many branches, and repeated labels can obscure which occurrence is owned or affected. The reported 33 duplicate labels cannot be evaluated without a definition, but F3’s maintainability concern is credible. Low simplicity, slow runtime, and poor informal comprehension reinforce that concern, although none alone proves the model is wrong.

# 6. Third Model: Policy-Factored, Attribute-Aware Hybrid

Use a factored control-flow model with small explicit choices and loops, plus a separate decision layer for information unavailable in the event log. Do not enumerate whole traces.

**Control-flow backbone:**

`Register Dossier → (Credit Check ∥ Technical Review) → [optional Expedite Review] → (Supplier Review may occur before or after Expedite Review) → exactly one of (Manager Approval, Auto Approval) → Release Order`

The mandatory checks may occur in either order, but both must finish before expedite and approval. Supplier Review is conditional on sourcing, so it cannot honestly be an unconditional optional pass in the control-flow model. The correction behavior is represented as a bounded correction/recheck loop driven by an explicit `affected_checks` decision: repeat Credit Check, Technical Review, or both, as applicable. A repeated mandatory check without a preceding correction is rejected or flagged.

This is a **hybrid**, not a single claim that the event sequence alone decides compliance:

- The control-flow checker evaluates ordering, required checks, approval exclusivity, correction sequence, and suspicious repeats.
- The sourcing decision uses a recorded `sourcing` attribute. Until it is present, Supplier Review compliance is **unverified**, not passed.
- The correction decision uses `affected_checks`. Until it is recorded, a correction/recheck trace is **conditional** where the dependency cannot be determined.
- Business-approved choices that remain unresolved are explicit assumptions, not inferred facts.

The backbone is safer than S because mandatory checks and ordering are enforced. It is more generalizable and maintainable than C because choices compose without enumerating every combination. Its limits are deliberate: P3 and P6 cannot be fully enforced from the current log.

# 7. Formal Specification Sketch

Define control-flow acceptance over visible events and classify attribute-dependent checks separately.

Let `sourcing ∈ {external, internal, missing}` and `affected_checks ⊆ {Credit Check, Technical Review, unknown}`. Assume for the provisional backbone that Supplier Review may occur before or after Expedite Review and that either approval type is eligible after both checks. These are assumptions requiring owner confirmation.

A control-flow-valid trace must satisfy:

1. It begins with exactly one `Register Dossier` and ends with exactly one `Release Order`.
2. It contains at least one Credit Check and one Technical Review before approval.
3. Credit Check and Technical Review may occur in either order.
4. Expedite Review occurs at most once, after both mandatory checks and before approval.
5. Supplier Review may occur at most once; its placement relative to Expedite Review is unrestricted, but it must be before approval.
6. Exactly one of Manager Approval and Auto Approval occurs, after the required checks and before release.
7. After a correction, only checks in the recorded `affected_checks` set may repeat; each affected check must be repeated as required by the owner-approved rule. If the set is missing, classify the trace as conditional.
8. A repeated mandatory check without a preceding Correct Dossier is suspicious and rejected for enforcement.
9. For external sourcing, Supplier Review is required. For internal sourcing, it is not. If sourcing is missing, the overall case is not a pass.

**Classification:**

- **Control-flow accept:** rules 1–6 and 8 hold; correction cases may be marked conditional if rule 7 cannot be evaluated.
- **Overall pass:** control-flow accept plus sourcing and affected-check facts sufficient to verify applicable conditions.
- **Reject:** a control-flow violation or a known attribute-dependent violation.
- **Conditional/unverified:** no known violation, but missing information prevents a compliance conclusion.

P6 does not specify whether every affected check must repeat or whether some may repeat optionally. That detail needs an owner decision; the sketch should not silently invent it.

# 8. Distinguishing Compliance from Discovery

Use the third model for discovery and policy review, with assumptions printed alongside the model. Do not treat high fitness, precision, or successful alignment as proof of compliance. Use the attribute-aware decision layer so missing sourcing and affected-check facts remain visible.

For enforcement, require the negative behavior suite to reject forbidden traces and the positive suite to accept valid combinations. Until sourcing is logged, do not mark an overall pass for cases where Supplier Review applicability is unknown. Until affected checks are recorded and the recheck rule is signed, keep correction cases conditional. Advisory findings can help expose data and policy gaps; they should not block releases as if the unresolved rules were settled.

# 9. Validation Plan

**Reproducibility.** Publish the PM4Py version, parsing/conversion code, preprocessing, split seed, case-ID manifest, alignment costs, and metric definitions. Re-run all three candidates on the same fixed train/holdout split and report results by relevant strata, including sourcing status, approval type, alternate check order, expedite presence, and correction/recheck behavior.

**Behavioral validation.** Turn B1–B8 into a versioned suite. Add probes for missing sourcing, both correction-only recheck options, both-check recheck, correction with no affected check repeated, multiple approvals, missing register/release, and supplier placement around expedite. Have the process owner resolve policy-dependent probes. Test the model’s language directly, including play-out, rather than inferring acceptance from fitness.

**Metrics.** Report mean and perfect-trace alignment fitness separately, precision, token-based generalization, simplicity, runtime median and tail percentile, and a clearly defined duplicate-label count. Add specificity over curated forbidden traces. Do not average these unlike measures into one score.

**Stakeholder validation.** Blind and randomize S, C, and the third model in a role-stratified review. Ask participants to classify positive and negative probes, measure accuracy against the signed policy, and separately record effort. The existing informal comprehension scores are not sufficient.

**Decision gate.** No model should enforce release until it accepts valid probes, rejects forbidden probes, passes implementation and soundness checks, and represents unresolved attributes as conditional rather than passing. Publish split verdicts for control flow, sourcing, and correction handling.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

Retire S and C as official process models; retain S as a permissive baseline and C’s listed variants as regression fixtures. Use the third model as a labeled hypothesis for exploration and communication after stakeholder validation. Use it for simulation only with explicit sourcing assumptions and loop bounds.

Do not use any of the three to block or allow release until the process owner signs the unresolved ordering, approval, and correction decisions; sourcing and affected-check attributes are captured; and the positive and negative probe suite passes. In the interim, run the third model in advisory mode and report unresolved cases as conditional or unverified. Keep enforcement decisions manual against the approved checklist until these conditions are met.

# 11. Dissenting View

The factored model risks turning every plausible combination into an allowed combination. The policy does not fully settle supplier placement, Auto Approval eligibility, or how correction relates to expedite. A closed catalog of approved variants may be safer for enforcement until the process owner signs the product space. Moreover, if the supplier attribute is absent, a model with optional Supplier Review may explain missing events for free and let data gaps persist.

This dissent changes the recommendation if the owner signs a closed variant matrix, if attribute-complete replay shows that unlisted combinations are not legitimate, or if operations treat conditional cases as passes. Conversely, a signed compositional policy plus recorded sourcing and affected-check attributes supports the factored model. Regardless, the current evidence does not justify using either submitted model for enforcement.
