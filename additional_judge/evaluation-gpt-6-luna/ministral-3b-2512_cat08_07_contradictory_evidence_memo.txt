## 1. Executive memo

Do not scale Redesign R nationally or roll it back wholesale. Use the hybrid option: continue the redesign for simple, low-risk houses, while suspending survey skipping and activation after billing for other segments. Require a successful activation test before billing starts. Keep permit checks for older apartment buildings. In parallel, run a controlled, risk-stratified experiment and improve measurement before expanding further.

The pilot shows a real trade-off. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, visible cost fell from €184 to €161, and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%. But p95 duration rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. Estimated cost including rework and complaints also rose, though it is incomplete. The gains are strongest for simple houses; apartments, older buildings, and business customers have worse tail times or quality outcomes.

These differences make a targeted approach more defensible than a broad rollout. The pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, and early segment data are incomplete, so the overall comparison does not establish that the redesign caused every deterioration. Still, frequent billing before successful activation, missing permit checks, and more failed visits are operational warning signals. High coordinator utilization and stalled cases during absences also raise concerns about resilience.

The process model’s high fitness does not resolve the uncertainty: its low precision means it allows many combinations of exceptions and skipped steps. Management should treat the observed paths and alignment deviations as signals to investigate, not proof that the redesign is reliable.

Prioritize risk-stratified validation of the building-risk rule, complete downstream cost measurement, and conformance monitoring for billing before activation and missing permits. Instrument start timestamps and collect reason codes so the team can locate delays and explain rework. Expand to higher-risk segments only after the rule performs well against field-survey outcomes, downstream costs and quality improve, and coordinator absence does not cause cases to stall. This preserves demonstrated gains where they appear safest while limiting exposure and buying decision-relevant evidence.

## 2. Evidence table

| Evidence item | Fact directly supported | Interpretation or plausible explanation | Uncertain assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median cycle time | Fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days. | Simplified paths may accelerate routine cases. | How much of the change reflects redesign versus region mix. |
| Tail duration | p90 rose from 31 to 38.5 days; p95 from 45 to 62 days. | Exceptions or rework may be concentrated in a subset of cases. | Whether the increase is causal or mainly due to pilot regions’ building mix. |
| First-time-right and rework | First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%; second visits rose from 11% to 18%. | Skipped surveys or unclear ownership may contribute. | Which change accounts for the deterioration. |
| Complaints | 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%; service complaints shifted toward “installed but not working” and “billing before activation.” | Faster billing or incomplete activation may damage customer experience. | Full complaint impact, since cost data omit downstream handling after week 4. |
| Handover count | Cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. | Coordinator ownership may improve continuity for routine cases. | Whether lower handovers compensate for reduced resilience. |
| Visible and estimated costs | Visible cost fell from €184 to €161; estimated cost including complaint/revisit handling rose from €198 to €207. | Savings may be offset by rework and complaints. | Complete lifetime cost; the estimate is incomplete. |
| Activation and billing | Manual activation rose from 7% to 15%; billing before successful activation is a frequent log deviation. | Relaxed activation controls may create billing disputes and rework. | The share of cases affected and resulting cost. |
| Segment outcomes | Simple low-risk houses improved in median time, p90, first-time-right, and complaints; older buildings and medium-risk apartments worsened on several measures. | A segment-specific rollout may retain benefits while limiting harm. | Whether segment differences persist with complete risk data. |
| Model quality | Pilot fitness is 0.94, precision 0.51, generalization 0.56. | The model accommodates many observed exception combinations and may not describe a reliable process. | How much reflects process variability versus event-log limitations. |
| Coordinator capacity | Utilization reached 93% in two regions; absences were associated with stalled cases. | Centralized ownership may create bottlenecks and fragility. | Whether staffing or shared-team changes would mitigate this. |

## 3. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks or limitations | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Scale nationally | Extends median-time, visible-cost, and handover gains. | Spreads worsening tails, rework, complaints, and activation-control concerns. | Not justified by current evidence. |
| B. Roll back | Restores the baseline process across segments. | Gives up observed gains for simple low-risk houses without evidence that rollback is needed there. | Too broad. |
| C. Scale coordinator ownership only | Preserves the handover reduction while suspending survey skipping and post-billing activation. | Coordinator overload and absence-related stalls may remain. | Plausible component to retain, with capacity and resilience safeguards. |
| D. Controlled regional experiment | Improves causal evidence and segment measurement. | Delays broader deployment and requires disciplined execution. | Necessary for decisions on higher-risk segments. |
| E. Hybrid rollout | Retains gains for simple houses and limits exposure in riskier segments while measurement improves. | Requires reliable segmentation, monitoring, and operational exceptions handling. | Recommended next step. |

## 5. Decision gates

- Continue Redesign R for simple, low-risk houses with monitoring.
- Suspend survey skipping and billing before successful activation outside that segment; retain permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings.
- Expand to apartments or older buildings only after risk-stratified evidence shows acceptable first-time-right, second-visit, complaint, and tail-duration outcomes.
- Broaden rollout only when downstream costs are measured fully and coordinator capacity and absence coverage are adequate.
