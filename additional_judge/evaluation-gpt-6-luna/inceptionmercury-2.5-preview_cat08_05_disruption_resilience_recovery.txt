**6.6/10**

The response follows the required seven-section format, uses the timeline and several key metrics, distinguishes resilience from concealed noncompliance, and proposes a concrete recovery process. It correctly identifies the rise in traceability and control gaps during T2, the lingering T4 audit workload, and the need for explicit prioritization and offline evidence capture.

Its main weakness is that some conclusions exceed the evidence or blur important distinctions. It says the hospital fast lane “bypassed system authorization” and implies it was paired with manual checks, but the DFG shows separate paths for the fast lane, manual authorization, and skipped authorization; it does not establish that fast-lane orders received manual checks. It also calls the 2,300 audit-remediation cases a queue, although the DFG shows 2,300 cases entering that step and 2,100 exiting to END; those are counts of transitions, not necessarily the queue’s remaining size. The claim that snapshot staleness caused cancellations and rework is plausible, but not demonstrated by the provided data.

The adaptation table is useful but oversimplifies some product risks. It treats ambient products as the scope for skipped authorization without evidence that every such case was ambient, and its proposed phone-proof limits do not address cold-chain temperature evidence. The recovery design omits recall-sensitive products from its minimum controls, and the prioritization rule—especially prioritizing controlled-substance backlogs—needs a clearer safety and urgency basis. Some thresholds, such as a 30-minute snapshot refresh, 80% hospital capacity, and fewer than 50 audit cases per week, are presented without justification. Finally, the response cites fitness scores but misses the separate percentage-fitting-traces figures for T3 and T4, and its management paragraph overstates that the T2 pharmacy fill-rate decline occurred specifically “to protect” hospital supply.

Overall, this is a strong, actionable answer with good temporal framing, but unsupported causal claims, a few metric interpretations, and gaps in product-specific controls prevent a higher grade.
