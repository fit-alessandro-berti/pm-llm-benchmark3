# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Use elapsed time from `Submit` as time zero and 72 hours as the SLA horizon. For terminal rows, observed duration is time to the stated terminal outcome. For open rows, age is right-censoring time at the observation cutoff; it does not establish what happened afterward.

Several useful estimands answer different operational questions:

- **Resolution by 72 hours:** probability of reaching `Resolve` by 72 hours. Treat withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as competing outcomes when estimating the real-world probability of resolution. This is useful for forecasting service success.
- **No internal terminal disposition by 72 hours:** probability that a case has not reached any observed terminal status by 72 hours. This matches an internal queue-management question, but a transfer counts as an internal disposition even though its external outcome is unknown.
- **Late resolution:** cumulative incidence of `Resolve` after 72 hours. This describes the observed late-resolution tail among cases still eligible for internal resolution; it differs from simply counting resolutions after 72 hours.
- **Cause-specific risk:** risk of a particular outcome such as withdrawal, rejection, or transfer before resolution. This can inform routing and governance.

A resolution at 76 hours is a known SLA miss and a late resolution. An open case aged 92 hours is a known case with no resolution by 72 hours, although its eventual outcome is unknown. A withdrawal at 27 hours is a competing outcome before the horizon: it is not a successful resolution, but it is not an observed resolution breach if “breach” means eventual resolution after 72 hours. A transfer at 68 hours is an internal observation stop; its contribution depends on whether transfer is treated as a competing disposition, censoring, or an operational failure.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| Observed status | Resolution endpoint | Competing-risk code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | Event at observed duration; classify as on-time if ≤72, late if >72 | Resolution | None | Retain continuous event time; report horizon-specific results |
| `withdrawn` | No resolution observed before withdrawal | Competing event | None for a resolution CIF | Treat as censoring for a hypothetical net-resolution analysis; or count as operational failure in a composite |
| `rejected` | No resolution observed before rejection | Competing event | None for a resolution CIF | Treat as censoring for a hypothetical net-resolution analysis; or count as operational failure in a composite |
| `transferred` | Internal resolution unobserved after transfer | Competing event in the internal-process estimand | None if transfer is an internal terminal disposition | (1) Competing event; (2) censor at transfer, assuming independent censoring; (3) failure in a composite endpoint, or impute later external resolution under a stated model |
| `open` | No event observed by age at cutoff | Censor at age | Right-censor at `duration_or_age_h` | Model informative censoring using available history; vary assumptions if censoring may depend on inactivity or case state |

Under transfer-as-competing, S08, S21, and S32 are competing events at 68, 26, and 96 hours, respectively. Under transfer-as-censoring, they leave the internal resolution risk set at those times. Under transfer-as-failure, they count as failures in a composite operational endpoint. The data do not reveal eventual external resolution, so none of these encodings identifies end-to-end resolution without additional assumptions or linked external outcomes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Restricting analysis to completed resolutions discards cases whose eventual outcomes are unknown and cases that ended through other dispositions. Among resolved cases, S03 resolved at 76 hours and S13 at 110 hours, so even the observed resolved subset contains SLA breaches. The share of resolved cases that were late is not the probability of breach for all submitted cases.

Open cases provide important information. S09 was still open at age 92 and therefore had no resolution by 72 hours; excluding it would hide a known horizon failure. S15 was open at age 35, so its eventual status at 72 hours is unknown. It is right-censored before the horizon, not known to have succeeded or failed. S24 was open at age 67 and is also censored before the horizon. By contrast, S09, S25, and S26 were open beyond 72 hours, so each is known to have remained unresolved at the SLA horizon.

Censoring may be informative. S11 was open at age 81 with a 55-hour last-event gap; S26 was open at age 104 with a 72-hour gap. These long gaps may signal stalled cases and may also affect whether a case remains observable. Standard independent-censoring assumptions are questionable if gaps or stage predict observation or resolution.

Withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are competing outcomes, not ordinary right-censoring when estimating real-world resolution probability. For example, S06 withdrew at 27 hours; treating it as censored implicitly allows it to resolve later in a hypothetical world. S08 transferred at 68 hours, and its external outcome is unknown. The distinction matters for both operational success and end-to-end service outcomes.

Cohorts differ in composition and policy exposure. Cohort A has 15 cases and B has 17; their priority and path distributions are not identical. Cohort B began after policy changes, but assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete. Differences between cohorts cannot therefore be interpreted as causal policy effects without stronger design and adjustment. For instance, the observed resolved B cases include S16 at 16 hours and S28 at 88 hours; these examples alone do not establish a cohort effect.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

Use **Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence** as the primary descriptive analysis for internal resolution, with withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as competing events and open cases right-censored at their observed ages. It directly estimates the observed process’s probability of resolution by 72 hours while respecting competing dispositions. With only 32 cases, estimates will be unstable; report counts, uncertainty intervals, and the event coding alongside each estimate.

Use a **multi-state model** as a secondary process analysis: cases move from submission through observed stages and then to resolution, withdrawal, rejection, or transfer. The supplied prefixes give ordering but not event times, so transition timing cannot be estimated reliably from these data alone. Treat the state model as a design for a timestamped follow-up analysis, and use this extract for descriptive path and state summaries.

**Kaplan–Meier** can estimate time to a composite terminal event or a net-resolution quantity under censoring assumptions. If competing outcomes are censored in a resolution-only KM, it estimates a hypothetical net risk and generally overstates real-world resolution probability. It should not be the primary operational estimate.

**Cause-specific hazards** can describe instantaneous transition rates to resolution or each competing disposition among cases still at risk. They are useful for studying stage-specific process changes, but require time-varying histories for activities such as escalation.

**Subdistribution hazards** can model covariate associations with a resolution cumulative incidence, but are difficult to interpret operationally and are not compelling with this small sample. Any model should be considered exploratory.

**Restricted mean time** can summarize time spent without resolution up to a chosen horizon, with explicit handling of competing events. It may be more interpretable than a hazard ratio for service planning, but requires a carefully defined endpoint.

**Landmarking** can support alerts at fixed elapsed times using only information available by each landmark. It avoids using future activity but sacrifices early cases and requires enough data for calibration.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Candidate features include current observed stage (`Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`), whether the path began with `AutoCheck`, number of `Review` visits, presence of `Rework`, path variant, elapsed time since the last event, and elapsed time since submission. For example, S07 and S27 show rework; S11 and S25 are open after escalation; S09 and S26 are open after review. These patterns motivate hypotheses, not estimated stage effects.

Priority, cohort, and indicators known at submission are baseline features. `Escalate`, repeated review, rework, and current stage are post-start, time-varying features. Treating “ever escalated” as a baseline predictor creates immortal-time bias: a case must remain in process long enough to escalate. It also risks leakage if the activity occurred after the prediction time. Use event timestamps and time-dependent covariates, or landmark predictions using only the prefix observed up to the landmark. The inactivity gap is also time-varying and may be informative about both risk and observation.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

1. **Primary internal competing-risk analysis:** count transfer, withdrawal, and rejection as competing terminal outcomes for the resolution CIF; right-censor opens at their ages. This estimates observed internal resolution under the recorded process.
2. **Transfer as censoring:** censor at transfer and estimate resolution under an independent-censoring assumption. Compare with the primary estimate; if transferred cases differ systematically, this can materially change inferred resolution probabilities.
3. **Transfer as operational failure:** define a composite failure of no successful internal resolution by 72 hours, with transfer counted as failure. This is useful for internal accountability but does not claim the external case was unresolved.
4. **Withdrawal/rejection alternatives:** compare competing-risk estimates with a net-resolution analysis that censors those exits, and with a composite operational-failure endpoint. The net analysis assumes those cases could otherwise have resolved and is not the observed process probability.
5. **Informative-censoring sensitivity:** stratify or adjust descriptively by current stage and last-event gap, and compare shorter-gap versus longer-gap open cases. With this sample, avoid claiming that adjustment removes informative censoring; present bounds or scenario results where feasible.
6. **Cohort-policy sensitivity:** report unadjusted cohort results, then stratify by priority and process history where cell sizes permit. Treat policy associations as descriptive because rollout was nonrandom and incomplete.

Qualitative conclusions can reverse when transfers are censored versus treated as failures, or when withdrawal and rejection are treated as competing outcomes versus censoring. Cohort comparisons can also reverse after priority or path stratification due to small, imbalanced groups. Long-gap open cases could represent stalled high-risk cases or simply delayed observation; the extract cannot distinguish these explanations.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Use the estimated probability of remaining unresolved at 72 hours to support queue monitoring, and use landmark predictions at earlier times to trigger review before the SLA deadline. Alerts should rely only on information available at alert time. A high inactivity gap or a case remaining in review can prompt a workload check or escalation review, but the observed associations should not be presented as causal effects of those actions.

For workload planning, combine projected unresolved volume with current queue size and staffing capacity. Track transfers separately so internal closure is not mistaken for end-to-end success. Governance dashboards should show resolution, competing dispositions, and censoring counts, with calibration checks by priority and cohort. Given the small sample, use wide uncertainty intervals and avoid operational thresholds until validated on a larger, time-stamped cohort. Check calibration and error rates by priority and cohort, and investigate differences before deploying alerts.

# Codes: 0=censored, 1=resolved, 2=withdrawn, 3=rejected,
# 4=transferred (competing), 5=transfer counted as operational failure.
def encode(status, transfer_mode):
    if status == "open":
        return 0
    if status == "resolved":
        return 1
    if status == "withdrawn":
        return 2
    if status == "rejected":
        return 3
    if status == "transferred":
        if transfer_mode == "competing":
            return 4
        if transfer_mode == "censor":
            return 0
        if transfer_mode == "failure":
            return 5
    raise ValueError(status)

# Build risk-set rows at each observed event time, preserving open cases.
# A censored case contributes to risk sets through its censoring time.
def event_table(data, transfer_mode):
    x = data.copy()
    x["event_code"] = x["status"].map(
        lambda s: encode(s, transfer_mode)
    )
    event_times = sorted(x.loc[x.event_code != 0, "time"].unique())
    out = []
    for t in event_times:
        at_risk = int((x.time >= t).sum())
        counts = x.loc[(x.time == t) & (x.event_code != 0), "event_code"].value_counts()
        out.append({
            "time": t, "at_risk": at_risk,
            "resolved": int(counts.get(1, 0)),
            "withdrawn": int(counts.get(2, 0)),
            "rejected": int(counts.get(3, 0)),
            "transferred": int(counts.get(4, 0)),
            "transfer_failure": int(counts.get(5, 0)),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(out)

# Two estimands: (a) resolution CIF by 72h with competing dispositions;
# (b) unresolved/no-resolution status known at 72h. For (b), cases censored
# before 72h are explicitly unknown, not silently classified as successes.
def aj_at_horizon(data, transfer_mode, horizon=72):
    x = data.copy()
    x["event_code"] = x["status"].map(lambda s: encode(s, transfer_mode))
    event_codes = sorted(set(x.event_code) - {0})
    cif = {code: 0.0 for code in event_codes}
    survival = 1.0
    for t in sorted(x.loc[x.event_code != 0, "time"].unique()):
        if t > horizon:
            break
        risk = int((x.time >= t).sum())
        d = x.loc[(x.time == t) & (x.event_code != 0), "event_code"].value_counts()
        d_all = int(d.sum())
        for code, count in d.items():
            cif[code] += survival * count / risk
        survival *= (1.0 - d_all / risk)
    # Directly observable 72h status and uncertainty due to early censoring.
    known_unresolved = int(((x.status == "open") & (x.time >= horizon)).sum())
    observed_resolution_by_72 = int(((x.status == "resolved") & (x.time <= horizon)).sum())
    unknown_at_72 = int(((x.status == "open") & (x.time < horizon)).sum())
    return {
        "cif_by_code": cif, "event_free_survival": survival,
        "observed_resolved_by_72_count": observed_resolution_by_72,
        "known_open_unresolved_at_72_count": known_unresolved,
        "unknown_open_before_72_count": unknown_at_72,
    }

# 9. Limitations and additional data

This extract has only 32 cases, no exact event timestamps, no external outcomes after transfer, and no randomized cohort assignment. Open cases have varying ages and inactivity gaps, so censoring may be informative. Prefixes provide activity order but not stage durations. More reliable process-aware survival analysis needs timestamped events, external transfer outcomes, a documented observation cutoff, rollout assignment and compliance data, workload and capacity measures, reasons for competing exits, and a larger follow-up sample.
