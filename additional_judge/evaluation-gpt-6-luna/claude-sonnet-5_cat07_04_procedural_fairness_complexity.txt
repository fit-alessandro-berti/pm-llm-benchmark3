# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity:** Approval rates range from 58% for Group D to 78% for Group A. These raw differences warrant attention but do not establish unfair treatment because the groups have different case mixes and routing.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After the preliminary controls, approval remains lower for Groups B, C, and D by 5.5, 2.2, and 3.1 percentage points, respectively. Adjusted appeal probability is higher by 4.4 points for B, 1.1 for C, and 5.7 for D. These are associations, not proof of discrimination or incorrect decisions.
- **Procedural burden:** Groups B and C more often encounter extra support or review steps. Those steps may be justified and rights-preserving, but they can still impose delay and coordination burdens.
- **Timeliness:** Median cycle time is higher for B and C than A, and the preliminary adjusted comparison still shows increases of 2.6 days for B and 4.1 days for C. Delays at support steps are a particular concern.
- **Consistency of routing:** Higher risk, accommodation requests, and language needs align with the stated routing policy. The evidence does not show whether similar cases within each group receive similar routing or whether the steps are applied consistently.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** The log records support contacts and requests, not whether support was timely, effective, accessible, or respectful.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Appeal and reopen/rework rates are higher for B, C, and D than A. Appeals can reflect difficult cases, but may also indicate unclear decisions or unequal access to explanations and review.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** B cases concentrate among seven bilingual workers and three overloaded regional officers; C cases among five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. This creates a plausible capacity-related source of unequal waiting and handovers.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Compare groups within common-support strata or with matching/weighting on region, month, channel, household size, prior case count, risk band, and documentation completeness. Include accommodation and language needs when estimating the effects of process routing, while separately examining whether those needs and their measurement are reliable. Report estimates with uncertainty and show raw and adjusted results.

Compare variant frequencies, transitions, and cycle times within matched strata. Separate expected policy paths—such as fraud review for high-risk cases—from additional steps, missing steps, and rework. Check whether the same policy triggers produce similar routes across groups.

Compare resource concentration, caseload, handovers, and waits by group and region. The daily workload measure limits conclusions about actual worker capacity, so resource comparisons should be treated as descriptive.

Decompose cycle time into time before and after each activity, especially language support, accommodation review, supervisor review, and generic stages. Distinguish service time from queue time where timestamps permit.

Run sensitivity analyses with and without risk score and documentation completeness, and with alternative definitions or proxies. These variables may encode historical disparities or language barriers, so an estimate “controlling” for them may mask part of the disparity.

Repeat comparisons by region and channel, and test whether group differences persist within each. Sparse subgroup samples should be reported with uncertainty rather than treated as evidence of no disparity.

Group B has a higher share of V2, which includes a missing-documents request (34% versus 24% for A), and V4, which includes language support and a documents request (24% versus 1% for A). Language needs make support routing legitimate under policy. Yet the 3.8-day median wait from language support to eligibility, the concentration of cases among seven bilingual workers, and extra handovers in two regions raise a procedural-burden concern. Group C has a much higher V5 share, which includes accommodation and supervisor review (31% versus 2% for A). Accommodation review is policy-required, but its 4.7-day median wait, the specialized queue, and handovers between general workers, specialists, and supervisors indicate a capacity and workflow concern. The adjusted cycle-time increase of 4.1 days is not explained by the listed controls alone, though unmeasured complexity may remain.

Group D has more V3 (28% versus 9% for A), consistent with its higher measured risk share and the policy’s fraud-review rule. Its higher V6 share (13% versus 4%) and adjusted appeal increase of 5.7 points warrant review, including whether risk adjustment is adequate and decisions are explained clearly.

Across groups, the supervisor-to-decision wait is longer for B and C than A. B also waits longer from application to verification and from completeness check to document request. These patterns suggest that burden is not confined to the necessary support steps. The summaries do not establish which differences are statistically reliable or whether routes were appropriate case by case.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

- Higher measured risk can legitimately send more cases to fraud review, increasing path length and cycle time.
- Language needs can require bilingual contact, while limited bilingual staffing creates queues and handovers.
- Accommodation needs can require specialized review; scarce specialists who also perform supervisor work can create delays or conflicts in scheduling.
- Incomplete documentation may appropriately trigger requests, but language barriers or inaccessible forms may contribute to incompleteness.
- Regional backlogs and overloaded officers can slow supervisor review and create group differences where applicants are unevenly distributed.
- Unclear or inaccessible decision explanations may contribute to appeals and rework.
- Risk scores or completeness assessments may reflect historical or measurement bias, creating unequal routing or decisions even when applied consistently.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Risk score and documentation completeness may be biased measures: risk can encode historical disparities, while completeness can reflect language access or form accessibility. Adjusting for them may therefore understate or distort disparities.

The log does not capture the severity or type of accommodation needed, unmet needs, applicants’ access to translation, or other circumstances that affect complexity. It also lacks a reliable measure of worker workload at the time each case was handled; daily-level workload cannot explain precise queue conditions.

Appeal flags do not show whether applicants could access appeals, whether the appeal was successful, or whether explanations were understandable and respectful. Outcomes alone do not establish whether eligibility decisions were correct. The preliminary adjusted differences also lack uncertainty intervals and details about model fit, overlap, and missing data.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Add language-support and accommodation capacity.** This may reduce specialist queues; it costs money, takes time to staff, and could move bottlenecks to later stages.
- **Set service-level targets for support steps.** This makes delays visible and accountable; targets could encourage rushed or lower-quality support unless quality is measured too.
- **Redesign routing to reduce handovers.** A consistent case owner or better coordination may reduce delays; cross-training and fewer handovers can weaken specialization or create new workload pressures.
- **Improve decision explanations and accessibility.** Clear, translated explanations may reduce avoidable appeals and rework; drafting and translation require time and staff capacity.
- **Monitor adjusted disparities over time.** Regular analysis can detect persistent differences; monitoring may provide false reassurance if biased covariates are treated as neutral.
- **Audit risk scores and documentation completeness.** This can identify measurement bias and improve routing; redesign may affect many cases and requires transparent validation.
- **Review regional and channel workflows.** Targeted changes can address local bottlenecks; interventions based on small subgroup counts may be unstable or displace delays elsewhere.
