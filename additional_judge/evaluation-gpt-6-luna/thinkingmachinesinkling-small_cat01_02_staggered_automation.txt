1. Availability is a branch-month indicator: Alpha and Beta had the system from April, Gamma from July, and Delta and Epsilon did not adopt by August. Availability does not imply that a case used automation. Actual automated-fast-path completion rose gradually among adopters, from about 4–6% before rollout to 32–50% by August.

Rollout was targeted: Alpha and Beta were selected partly for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma had a readiness pilot. Adoption timing is therefore related to branch characteristics and likely outcomes. At the same time, arrivals shifted toward adopting branches, staffing changed, case complexity rose in May, and a centralized fraud desk began serving all branches in March. Automated triage sent somewhat more borderline cases to that desk, but its waiting times are unavailable. Local work practices may also have changed.

The reported outcomes are branch-month summaries, not case-level records. They do not show path-specific times, queue waits, or which cases were automated. CSAT is a monthly survey mean with response rates around 20–45%, but response counts and respondent composition are unavailable. Inference is constrained by five branches and eight months of observations.

## 2. Causal estimands

Availability and use are different interventions: E1/E2 concern an offer of access, while E3 concerns actual use, which may be chosen based on case type, staff, or workload. Treatment versions also differ by branch readiness, learning, and routing rules. Interference is plausible because branches share a fraud desk; a branch’s outcome may depend on automation elsewhere.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: cohort-specific staggered event study with not-yet-treated controls** | Separate April cohort (Alpha, Beta) and July cohort (Gamma); event time relative to availability, with effects allowed to vary by cohort and event month | Not-yet-treated Delta and Epsilon; Gamma can serve as not-yet-treated control for April only | Branch and calendar time; pre-treatment outcomes and trends; arrivals, FTE, recorded complexity, and routing indicators where available; avoid conditioning on post-treatment mediators for total effect | Cohort- and horizon-specific availability effects on each branch-month outcome | Conditional parallel trends; no anticipation; no unmeasured concurrent branch-specific shocks; limited or modeled spillovers; stable outcome measurement | Plot cohort-specific pre-trends; placebo adoption dates; leave-one-branch-out estimates; compare control trajectories; sensitivity to April/May events and alternative control sets | Only two untreated branches, nonparallel trends and targeted adoption; common fraud-desk spillovers and unmeasured local changes |
| Matched-control difference-in-differences / synthetic control, separately by adopter or cohort | Adoption date for each branch; estimate branch-specific post-adoption gaps | Weighted combination of Delta/Epsilon and, for April cohort, pre-adoption Gamma | Pre-period outcome levels/slopes, volume, staffing, complexity, and observed routing | Branch-specific availability effect over observed post period | Weighted controls reproduce untreated potential outcomes; no contamination; adequate pre-period fit; no differential shocks | Pre-fit and pre-trend plots; placebo branches and dates; sensitivity to donor pool and weights | Very short pre-period and donor pool; adopters were selected for poor performance; Gamma is not an enduring control |
| Interrupted time series with concurrent controls | Monthly level/slope change at each rollout date, with separate intervention ramp or use intensity | Concurrent non-adopters and not-yet-treated branches to absorb common time shocks | Calendar shocks, arrivals, staffing, complexity, fraud-desk indicator; only measured covariates | Short-run level and trend change associated with availability | In absence of rollout, trends would continue; no coincident adopter-specific shocks; functional form adequate | Check pre-period fit; alternative break dates; level versus slope specifications; inspect residual autocorrelation | Only 3 pre-adoption months for Alpha/Beta, short series, and coincident routing/staffing/case-mix changes |
| Instrumental-variable estimate of use using availability or rollout timing as instrument | Availability/timing as an instrument for fast-path share | Variation in availability across branch-months | Branch, month, and pre-treatment covariates | Local effect of increased actual use among compliers | Relevance, exclusion, monotonicity, no interference, and instrument independence conditional on controls | First-stage strength; balance and pre-trends; sensitivity to exclusion violations | Exclusion is implausible: availability may change staffing, routing, training, or local practice beyond measured use |

The primary strategy should estimate cohort-specific effects of *availability* at explicit horizons, rather than pool all adopters into one coefficient. A modern staggered event study avoids the potentially misleading weighting of a conventional two-way fixed-effects model when effects differ by cohort or time since adoption. It also makes the available comparison periods visible: Delta and Epsilon are controls through August, and Gamma is a control only for the April cohort before July.

This remains a design for disciplined description and sensitivity analysis, not strong causal identification. There are only five branches, just two never-treated branches, and little pre-rollout history. The adopters were selected partly because of poor prior performance; April onward also brought routing shifts, while staffing and complexity changed at different times. The centralized fraud desk creates likely cross-branch interference. Pre-trend plots and placebo dates can expose some problems but cannot establish parallel trends with so few observations. Report cohort-specific point estimates and uncertainty cautiously, show raw trajectories, and make clear that no adjustment can recover unrecorded queue waits or local practice changes. Do not interpret use share as randomized dose.

## 5. Quantitative evidence and uncertainty

Descriptive contrasts help establish the scale and direction of changes, but are not treatment-effect estimates.

For Alpha and Beta, comparing January–March with April–June, median cycle time rose from 48.9 to 50.4 hours in Alpha and from 45.9 to 48.4 hours in Beta. P90 rose from 98.5 to 109.9 hours and from 92.9 to 103.0 hours, respectively. In July–August, the corresponding medians were 45.6 and 46.8 hours, and p90s 105.4 and 102.4 hours. Thus the early deterioration was followed by improvement in the later period, particularly for medians; p90 remained above pre-period levels.

Gamma’s January–June mean median was 42.3 hours and p90 85.8; July–August means were 37.2 and 84.9. Rework fell from 13.4% to 12.6%, while escalation rose from 8.8% to 10.7%. Its improvement coincides with adoption, but also follows the May complexity increase and June FTE loss, so timing alone does not identify a cause.

The untreated branches also worsened around May–June. Delta’s median rose from 36.5 hours in January–April to 42.6 in May–June, then fell to 39.3 in July–August. Epsilon rose from 40.3 to 48.0, then fell to 42.8. That pattern is consistent with common workload or case-mix pressure and makes a simple adopter pre/post comparison especially misleading.

CSAT rose in later adopter months, but the survey response rate varied and counts are unknown. No credible confidence interval for a causal effect follows from these branch-month summaries: there are only five branch clusters, and aggregate outcomes conceal case-level variation.

## 6. Why the requested single number is not defensible

“Automation reduces cycle time by X%” is not defensible from these data. Availability was not randomly assigned and was targeted to historically weak, high-volume branches. Actual use rose gradually and remained far below universal use. Outcomes also shifted with routing, staffing, complexity, the fraud desk, and local practice. The observed summaries lack the case-level data needed to separate fast-path performance from changes in which cases take that path, and the five-branch panel is too small to support reliable causal inference. A descriptive percent change could be calculated for a specified branch and period, but it would answer a before/after question, not the causal effect of automation.

## 7. Rollout policy for operational benefit and causal learning

Use a **randomized, capacity-constrained phased rollout** for Delta and Epsilon if operationally acceptable: randomly assign eligible teams, case queues, or time blocks to immediate versus delayed activation, then cross over the delayed units after a prespecified learning period. If randomization must be at branch level, the two branches alone are too few for dependable inference; use additional comparable branches or randomize rollout timing across many teams or queues. Keep routing, staffing plans, and eligibility rules as stable as feasible during the evaluation window, and record departures.

Track system availability and use separately. Capture case-level assignment, risk at registration, path transitions and timestamps, arrivals, staffing, routing, specialist and fraud-desk queue waits, rework loops, and survey invitations, responses, and response rates. Pre-specify intention-to-treat effects and outcomes at 0–2 and 3–5 months; report medians and tail quantiles alongside rework, escalation, and customer outcomes. Use randomized activation as the primary estimate; treat actual-use analyses as secondary unless the random assignment supports a clearly justified instrumental-variable analysis. Monitor safety and service-quality guardrails during expansion, and use the delayed units to estimate what would have happened without activation over the same calendar period.

## 8. Data needed to strengthen the evaluation

Collect case-level timestamps at registration, triage, specialist referral and acceptance, each information request or repeat review, resolution, and closure. Record assigned triage mode, actual automated/manual path, overrides, risk class at registration, referral reason, and subsequent pathway. This permits direct comparison of path-specific times and exposes whether cases are diverted into or out of automation.

Capture branch and shared fraud-desk queue lengths, arrival and service times, and routing source, including transfers between branches. Record daily or shift-level arrivals, productive staffing by role, absences, training, system uptime, and workflow changes. These data help distinguish automation from demand, capacity, and implementation effects.

For customer outcomes, store survey eligibility, invitation, response, response time, and available case characteristics for respondents and nonrespondents; report response rates and conduct sensitivity analyses for nonresponse. Extend the pre- and post-periods and add branches or randomized rollout units to support credible comparison and uncertainty estimation.
