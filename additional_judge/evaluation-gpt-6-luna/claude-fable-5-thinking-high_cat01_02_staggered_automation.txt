The answer is unusually thorough and follows the requested structure closely. It distinguishes availability from use, treats adoption as endogenous, discusses interference and changing treatment versions, separates median from p90 behavior, and recommends a rollout that could improve causal learning. The code is self-contained, uses the supplied data, and labels its regressions as descriptive.

Several errors and overclaims materially weaken the analysis:

- **The quantitative contrasts are not consistently reproducible from the stated windows.** For Alpha and Beta, the displayed DiD table appears to use the Jan–Mar pre-period and Apr–Aug post-period, but the claimed “leave-one-control-out” range and several Gamma values do not consistently match those windows. Gamma’s pre-window changes between sections, making comparisons difficult to audit.
- **The “intensity” analysis is not actually an intensity-model result.** It reports event-time DiDs at individual months and divides window DiDs by intensity above baseline. That ratio is not a dose–response estimate and is especially confounded by calendar time and learning. The supplied code does fit an intensity regression, but the answer does not report its output.
- **The primary event-study description overstates what its code estimates.** The code’s event-time regression uses common event-time indicators and all untreated and pre-treatment rows, rather than explicit cohort-by-event-time group-time contrasts. Its use of already-treated and pre-treatment observations as a pooled reference can reintroduce problems the prose says it avoids.
- **Some causal directions are asserted too confidently.** For example, the claim that additional routing volume and Alpha’s staffing loss bias against automation is plausible but not guaranteed; managers may respond to those pressures, and changing composition can move aggregate quantiles either way. Likewise, escalation is not necessarily a direct measure of specialist/fraud review congestion.
- **The rollout recommendation’s timing is internally inconsistent.** It proposes randomizing the first branch now, while also requiring three further clean pre-period months; it then describes January–August history as giving “11+ pre-months,” which is arithmetically wrong as of the stated data endpoint. It also suggests within-branch case randomization without resolving shared-queue interference or specifying eligibility and analysis details.

These flaws matter because the answer’s strongest conclusions—especially the proposed 4–9% median benefit and a positive escalation effect—depend on contrasts and assumptions that are not fully reconciled. Still, it appropriately rejects a universal “X%” claim, gives substantial uncertainty, and offers a credible evaluation-minded policy.
