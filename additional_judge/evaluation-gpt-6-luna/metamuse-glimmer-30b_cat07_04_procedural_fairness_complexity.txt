## 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity:** Approval rates vary from 78% for Group A to 58% for Group D. These raw differences identify gaps but do not establish unfair treatment because the groups differ in measured risk, documentation, channels, and support needs.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After adjustment, approval probability remains lower for Groups B (−5.5 percentage points), C (−2.2 points), and D (−3.1 points) relative to A. These are concerning residual associations, not causal estimates or proof of discrimination.
- **Procedural burden:** Groups B and C more often follow paths with extra support steps and handovers. Those steps may protect applicants’ rights, yet added transfers and waits can make the process more burdensome.
- **Timeliness:** Median cycle time is longer for B and C than A. Adjusted differences remain (+2.6 and +4.1 days, respectively), and support-step waits are substantial: 3.8 days after language support and 4.7 days after accommodation review. These figures show where delay occurs, but do not fully separate queueing from service time.
- **Consistency of routing:** Higher risk aligns with more fraud review for D, accommodation requests with accommodation review for C, and language needs with language support for B. These patterns fit stated policy. However, B’s adjusted increases in supervisor review (+8.0 points) and missing-document requests (+9.5 points) warrant investigation.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** The log records support contacts and accommodation reviews, not whether support was timely, effective, accessible, or respectful. The evidence cannot establish the quality of treatment.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Raw appeal rates are highest for D (16%) and B (13%); rework rates are highest for C (24%) and B (22%). Adjusted appeal probabilities remain higher for B (+4.4 points) and D (+5.7 points). These may reflect decision quality, explanation access, or case complexity; appeal outcomes and reasons are not given.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** B and C cases are concentrated among small specialist teams, with reported handovers between teams. That concentration can create unequal queues even when routing is justified. Daily workload data are too coarse to determine whether individual assignments or prioritization were fair.

## 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Estimate group differences within comparable strata and report both raw and adjusted results. Stratify or match on channel, household-size band, region, month, prior-case count, and carefully defined measures of case complexity. Compare results with and without risk score and documentation completeness: either may reflect historical disparities or barriers that are part of the process being assessed. Treat language-support need and accommodation request as eligibility for rights-preserving steps, not as reasons to discount the burden of those steps.

Compare paths and variants within those strata. For each group, measure path frequency, transition-specific waiting time, service time, cycle time, appeals, and rework. Compare cases on common paths, as well as support-required cases against similarly complex cases receiving the same support. Examine long-tail times and whether extra reviews or document requests follow stated rules.

Compare resource assignment by group, region, path, and case complexity. Assess caseloads, queue times, handovers, and outcomes for assigned workers or teams, accounting for workload and specialty. Daily workload records limit the precision of this comparison.

Report uncertainty intervals, sample sizes, and sensitivity to alternative control sets and definitions of complexity. Distinguish associations from causal effects: the available adjusted comparisons do not eliminate unobserved confounding or establish intent.

## 3. Evidence of legitimate versus concerning process differences

Several routing differences align with stated policy: Group D has a higher share of high-risk cases and more fraud-review paths; Group C more often requests accommodation and follows accommodation-review paths; Group B more often needs language support and follows language-support paths. These differences are consistent with legitimate routing.

There are also concerning residual differences. After adjustment, B has higher probabilities of supervisor review and missing-document requests, as well as longer cycle time and more appeals. C retains a substantial cycle-time difference. B’s and C’s support steps have notable waits and rely on small specialist teams; handovers add burden. These patterns suggest capacity and workflow problems that can burden applicants even when the support steps themselves are warranted.

The evidence does not show whether extra reviews were unnecessary, whether decisions were erroneous, or whether similarly situated applicants were treated differently by individual staff. It supports identifying risks and targets for audit, not concluding that intentional discrimination occurred.

## 4. Plausible mechanisms

- **Legitimate complexity:** Higher measured risk, incomplete documentation, and more prior cases may require additional review or document requests.
- **Support-team capacity:** Small bilingual and accommodation teams may create queues for necessary services, adding delay unrelated to applicants’ underlying eligibility.
- **Control measurement:** Documentation completeness may be affected by language access or form design. Risk scores may encode historical disparities. Adjusting for these factors can obscure part of the process disparity.
- **Regional workload and handovers:** Overloaded officers and transfers between generalists and specialists may add waiting, rework, and inconsistent handling.
- **Communication and decision quality:** Elevated appeals could reflect inaccessible explanations or procedural errors, but appeal reasons and outcomes are unavailable.

## 5. Limitations of available attributes

Risk score and documentation completeness are not necessarily neutral measures of case mix. They may reflect historical inequities, language barriers, or prior support quality. Other relevant factors—such as digital access, health literacy, and the details of applicants’ circumstances—are not observed.

Case outcome does not measure explanation quality, dignity, or whether support met applicants’ needs. The appeal flag does not report appeal reasons or results. Daily workload data hide queue conditions and prioritization within each day. Consequently, residual adjusted differences are not causal estimates, and raw parity comparisons cannot distinguish unfairness from legitimate case differences.

## 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- Add bilingual and accommodation-review capacity to reduce queues. This requires funding, recruitment, and training.
- Set timeliness targets for support steps, paired with quality measures so staff are not incentivized to rush or omit support.
- Reduce handovers through end-to-end assignment where feasible. This may constrain staffing flexibility and shift caseloads.
- Provide accessible, translated decision explanations and record communication accommodations. These require development and ongoing quality review.
- Monitor group differences in path, wait, service time, appeals, and rework within comparable strata. This adds reporting and governance work.
- Audit how risk scores and completeness measures are constructed and used. Changes may affect routing and eligibility decisions and require policy review.
