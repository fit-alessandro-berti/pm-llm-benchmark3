# 1. Stress scenarios

The scenarios below combine different case mixes and disruptions. They are plausible stress tests, not forecasts.

- **S1 — Peak mix shift:** Urgent and Complex-panel demand rises toward the peak mix (29% and 20%), while staffing and equipment remain available.
- **S2 — Assay degradation:** Peak mix coincides with an M2 failure-rate spike or an M1 outage. Repeat-after-failure work rises, particularly after night shifts.
- **S3 — Validation absence:** Medical-validator capacity falls, or the scheduled specialist absence coincides with a peak. Complex-panel and held cases accumulate.
- **S4 — Courier compression or delay:** Arrivals cluster around the 10:00 and 17:00 waves, or a delayed route delivers a late surge. Intake and pre-analytical queues rise together.
- **S5 — Defect and correction surge:** Poor samples or metadata errors increase during a demand wave, driving recollections and accession corrections.
- **S6 — Compound disruption:** Two or more of the above overlap, such as courier compression during an M1 outage and specialist absence.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The event log is flattened by order even though orders may contain multiple tubes and assays. DFG counts therefore indicate route frequency, not resource workload; the unbalanced paths should not be treated as a complete flow reconciliation. Queue and performance metrics give stronger evidence about where work waits.

- **S1:** Pre-analytical check is already at 96% utilization with a 9.5h peak median queue. Registration automation could move pressure there sooner. Corrections are also near capacity at 91%, with a 14h queue and a 16h correction-to-preparation edge. Farther downstream, Urgent bypasses batching less often during peak (58% versus 74% normally), while batch wait to assay run grows from 9h to 22h. Strict Urgent-first dispatch might reduce Urgent waits initially but fragment machine schedules and starve Routine and Complex work. It would then send a larger, more uneven flow to assay machines (98% peak utilization), technical validation (101%), and medical validation (93%). The 24h technical-to-medical and 5h medical-to-release peak edges show why preparation priority alone cannot ensure Urgent release within 18h.
- **S2:** Machine queues (18h peak median) and repeat loops are the first constraint: Assay run to Repeat assay grows from 3h to 8h and the repeat-to-assay edge from 10h to 26h. Repeats consume assay capacity twice and increase downstream technical-validation arrivals. When the assay backlog clears, technical validation at 101% peak utilization is likely to become the bottleneck, followed by medical validation. Dedicated M1 use for critical work can protect it if M1 is healthy, but routing all Routine work to the higher-failure M2 risks generating more repeats and moving the constraint back to the machines.
- **S3:** Medical validation has a 22h peak queue, despite 93% reported utilization; this is a strong warning that average utilization conceals arrival or roster variability. Technical validation to medical validation takes 24h at peak. Specialist holds are slower still: a 36h queue and a 48h hold-to-medical edge. Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to be held. Extra medical-validation hours may shift the queue to specialist review; cross-training technical validators for accession correction may shift it to technical validation, already at 101%.
- **S4:** The courier intake queue grows to 7.5h during peak; compressed arrivals then feed a pre-analytical queue of 9.5h at 96% utilization. Defects can send work back through recollection: the peak DFG has 520 recollection returns and 370 cancellations, while the recollection-to-receive edge takes 60h. Smoothing pickups can help intake, but a flatter arrival pattern may simply expose the pre-analytical constraint more consistently. Faster early triage may also overload that station if it means accepting more samples into an already saturated queue.
- **S5:** Errors increase work at both accession correction and pre-analytical check. Peak DFG shows 780 requests for recollection and 420 manual corrections from pre-analytical check; some corrected cases still enter recollection. Corrections can unblock preparation, but moving two technical validators to correction risks growing the technical queue. Early defect rejection can reduce downstream wasted work, while false rejection increases recollection loops and clerical explanation time. The DFG and edge times show that recollection is not a quick reset: its return-to-receive median is 60h in peak.
- **S6:** Multiple shocks can expose the next constraint faster than any single-station improvement resolves the backlog. For example, smoothing courier arrivals during an assay outage may shift the queue from intake to pre-analytical, then leave more cases waiting for scarce machines. Outsourcing Complex panels may ease local specialist review but adds 12h transport and traceability work, and does not automatically remove medical validation needs.

# 3. Robust redesign package

- **Protect contractual Urgent work with slack-based dispatch.** Prioritize Urgent cases by remaining SLA slack at preparation, assay, and validation handoffs, rather than assigning an unconditional Urgent-first rule at every station. Add an aging guard for Routine cases and a reserved path for already-started Complex panels. This depends on reliable timestamps, case labels, and estimates of remaining work. It can fail if work estimates are poor, priority flags are misused, or too many Urgent cases arrive to protect all of them.
- **Use early defect triage with feedback to sources.** Introduce I6 with clear defect codes, rapid physician notification, and review of rejection accuracy. This targets the recollection loops and correction work visible in the DFG. It depends on trained staff and actionable specimen-quality criteria; false rejection or a triage queue can worsen delays.
- **Gate registration automation.** Apply I8 only to orders with complete digital metadata, and monitor pre-analytical queue and error rates as it scales. Automation can free clerks for intake surges and recollection calls, but it may shift work downstream if metadata checks are weak.
- **Use flexible, capped batch rules.** Keep Routine batching where machine setup savings justify it, but do not double batch sizes universally. Use pull-based batch release with a maximum wait and explicit Urgent and started-Complex exceptions. This depends on knowing setup savings and case compatibility; frequent exceptions can erase gains, while large batches lengthen waits.
- **Build a cross-trained validation/correction pool with guarded assignments.** Cross-train as proposed, but assign people to accession correction only when technical-validation queue and arrival-to-completion ratio are below agreed limits. Use an evening medical-validation shift during sustained peak pressure. This depends on training, staffing availability, and validated scope of work; the shared pool cannot cover simultaneous surges at both stations.
- **Smooth courier arrivals where operationally feasible.** Shift pickup slots and tag urgent bags for prompt intake. Pair this with intake staffing and triage capacity, because smoothing alone can reveal the pre-analytical bottleneck.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Use activation and deactivation hysteresis to prevent frequent switching.

| Policy | Activate when | Actions | Deactivate when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P-A: Peak mix** | Urgent + Complex share exceeds 40% over 24h, or more than 5% of open Urgent cases are projected to miss their remaining slack | Activate slack-based dispatch and Routine aging guard; add evening medical validation if its queue exceeds 12h for two shifts | Combined share below 35% for 3 days and Urgent risk below 5% |
| **P-B: Assay degradation** | Machine outage exceeds 2h, repeat rate exceeds 12% over 24h, or assay queue exceeds 12h | Reserve reliable capacity for Urgent work and started Complex panels; cap Routine batches at 12; apply machine-specific QC and route repeats according to validated machine reliability | Machines available, repeat rate below 9%, and assay queue below 8h for 24h |
| **P-C: Validator/specialist absence** | Planned medical-validation and specialist capacity falls below 85% of plan, or specialist hold queue exceeds 24h | Stage Complex panels before the absence; use approved parallel review; consider outsourcing only when expected local wait exceeds partner transport plus processing time and traceability can be maintained | Capacity restored and hold queue below 12h |
| **P-D: Courier surge** | Any 2h arrival window exceeds 1.5 times its usual level, courier is over 60 minutes late, or intake queue exceeds 3h | Prioritize urgent bags at unload; temporarily move available clerical capacity to intake; pace intake into pre-analytical work to avoid creating an unmanaged downstream queue | Intake queue below 2h and arrivals stable for 2 days |
| **P-E: Defect surge** | Defect exits exceed 10% over 48h or correction queue exceeds 8h | Expand triage and source feedback; redeploy cross-trained staff only if technical-validation queue is below 8h; pause automation for the affected source if errors rise | Defect rate below 9% for 3 days and correction queue below 6h |
| **P-F: Compound disruption** | Any two of P-A through P-E are active | Duty manager reviews capacity and backlog each shift; communicate Routine risk; apply selective Complex overflow only where partner turnaround and traceability are acceptable | All active triggers clear for 2 consecutive days |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- Technical validation remains a major peak risk at 101% utilization. Improvements upstream may send it more work.
- The two accession specialists and recurring specialist absence remain fragile single points of failure.
- If M1 is unavailable, routing critical work to M2 may increase repeat risk; if M2 is restricted, total assay capacity falls.
- A sufficiently high Urgent share can overwhelm any priority policy and make contractual breaches unavoidable.
- Flattened order-level data obscures tube-level and assay-level workload. Validate thresholds against clean event data before treating them as precise.
- **Redesign-created vulnerability:** slack-based dispatch and exception rules add complexity. Incorrect labels, bad remaining-work estimates, or gaming of Urgent status can misorder work. False triage rejections and outsourcing traceability errors are additional risks.
