## 1. Target definition

The primary target is **default within 90 days after Account Activated**, among accounts that are activated and have an observable, mature outcome feed. The unit is an activated application at a prespecified early prefix landmark, such as the fifth event or 14 days after Application Started, whichever occurs first. Features must be available by that landmark.

The estimand is predictive risk in the selected population of activated accounts with mature observed labels, conditional on reaching activation and on the chosen prefix. It is not default risk for every application. Prediction time precedes activation, but inclusion in this target depends on a later event, so this is a conditional retrospective estimand rather than an unconditional forecast for all applicants.

A credible alternative is a multi-state target for all applications, with activation, rejection, withdrawal, transfer, default after activation, and remaining open represented as distinct transitions or outcomes. That answers a process-outcome question for the application population, not the conditional default-risk question. Rejected, withdrawn, and open cases must not be called non-defaults.

## 2. Cohort construction

Use one application-level record per case, with events ordered by timestamp and deterministic tie handling. Define a landmark before analysis and include only information available at that landmark. For the main binary analysis, include activated accounts only when the 90-day horizon has elapsed and the outcome feed has arrived by the extraction cutoff. Do not infer a negative label from a missing feed. Exclude applications that have not reached the landmark from that landmark’s risk set; analyze them separately in a multi-state analysis.

For transferred cases, attempt linkage where available and retain an explicit linkage status. A linked successor account should be deduplicated at the underlying application/person level when that identity can be established; unlinked transfers remain a distinct source of uncertainty.

## 3. Censoring and missing-label strategy

Ordinary right censoring applies when follow-up ends before the 90-day post-activation horizon. Delayed labels arise because the feed arrives 7–35 days after that horizon. These are different: a matured horizon with an unarrived feed is delayed or missing outcome data, not necessarily right censoring of the event process.

Activation creates selection: outcomes are defined only after exposure to credit. A model trained on activated accounts estimates risk for that selected population, not rejected or withdrawn applicants. Transfers may be competing events in the application process, or loss to follow-up after transfer if the objective is to follow the same underlying account. Permanently missing partner feeds are a missing-outcome mechanism whose dependence on risk may be informative.

Primary analysis: use the mature labeled activated cohort, report its composition and selection explicitly, and model time to default after activation with right censoring if event times are available. For the fixed 90-day binary endpoint, include only accounts whose horizon and feed are mature; treat missing feeds separately rather than as non-defaults. Compare observed-label rates by source, transfer linkage, calendar period, and available baseline features.

Sensitivity analyses should include inverse-probability-of-observation weighting under a missing-at-random assumption conditional on observed covariates, and bounds or scenario analyses for permanently missing feeds. A competing-risk/multi-state analysis can characterize rejection, withdrawal, and transfer from application start.

Maturity dates and observed feed delays are testable from timestamps. Observed differences in feed availability are partially testable. Missing-at-random assumptions and the outcomes that permanently missing feeds would have carried are fundamentally untestable without additional data or assumptions. Counterfactual default risk absent activation is also untestable from this export.

## 4. Prefix extraction

Generate prefixes at fixed event counts (for example, 3, 5, and 8 events) and elapsed-time landmarks (for example, 7 and 14 days after start). Sort events by timestamp, resolve ties consistently, and use only events at or before the landmark. Keep separate landmark cohorts so cases that have not yet accumulated the required events are not silently treated as equivalent.

For short cases, record that the landmark was not reached and retain their terminal outcome in the all-application analysis. Document loops should be represented by loop counts and time since prior review, rather than collapsed into an assumed single pass. Map renamed activities across the migration using a versioned alias table, preserve raw names, and flag ambiguous mappings. Identify and handle duplicates using stable event identifiers if available; otherwise use cautious, documented duplicate rules and compare results with and without deduplication.

## 5. Process-aware features

Static case attributes include channel, region, requested amount, and source system, provided they are captured at or before application start and do not change retrospectively.

Prefix-derived features include the observed activity sequence, counts and transitions, elapsed time since start and between events, time in current state, number of document-request/review loops, missing or repeated events, and whether the case has reached credit review by the landmark. Resource and queue features can include the event resource, event-time workload, queue delay, and resource history as known at that time. Calendar features can include start month, weekday, policy era, and migration era.

Historical reference features—such as prior activity frequencies, resource experience, queue baselines, and risk calibration—must be computed from data available before the prediction date, ideally within training folds and policy eras. Do not use experience measured at extraction time as if it were known at event time.

## 7. Validation design

Split by application start time, not randomly. A possible scheme is train on January 2024–June 2025, validate on July–September 2025, and test on October–December 2025, with each evaluation restricted to cases whose outcomes and delayed feeds matured by the extraction cutoff. Since later starts have less follow-up, report the eligible sample size and label-maturity rate for each window; do not compare immature cases as negatives.

The July 2025 policy change warrants separate calibration and performance reporting before and after the change, plus a sensitivity analysis with policy era interactions or retraining. The October migration warrants a separate test report and duplicate/name-mapping sensitivity analysis. Backtests should simulate what data and labels would have been available at each historical cutoff, including the feed delay.

For binary mature outcomes, report calibration plots, Brier score, log loss, discrimination (such as ROC AUC and precision-recall AUC), and threshold-specific operating measures. For time-to-event analyses, use censoring-aware metrics such as time-dependent Brier score and concordance, with appropriate censoring adjustment. Report subgroup performance and calibration by channel, region, source system, policy era, and transfer/linkage status where sample sizes permit.

A random split can mix policy regimes, migration artifacts, and near-duplicate or linked cases across partitions. It also ignores label maturity and can make performance look better than future deployment performance.

## 8. Limitations and decision-use boundary

The study can estimate predictive association for the observed, activated, labeled population under the historical policies and data capture. It cannot estimate default risk for rejected, withdrawn, or open applications merely by assigning them a negative label. It cannot establish the counterfactual default those applicants would have had if activated without assumptions about selection and lending policy.

Deployment could change the data-generating process in at least three ways:

1. Using predictions to alter acceptance thresholds changes who is activated and therefore the selected population.
2. Showing scores to reviewers can change review effort, decisions, and applicant treatment.
3. Policy changes, applicant behavior, partner routing, or system instrumentation can change feature and outcome distributions.

Partner feeds with permanently missing outcomes limit generalization, and partial transfer linkage prevents confidently following all transferred cases.

## 9. PM4Py-oriented Python implementation skeleton

The provided implementation skeleton does not meet the requested standard. Its event-log construction passes a DataFrame to `EventLog` in an unsupported manner, uses undefined names and functions, and applies an activity filter where a timestamp filter is needed. Its prefix-extraction call does not demonstrate the requested fixed-length prefixes, and the shown split code uses an invalid period-membership expression. It also does not correctly build case-level activation, terminal, censoring, and label-maturity information as of a configurable cutoff, nor does it implement point-in-time feature reconstruction. Therefore the design is conceptually stronger than the code, but the required PM4Py-oriented pipeline is not coherent enough to implement.
