**4.5/10**

The answer follows the required structure, gives both sides substantial treatment, covers all 15 cases, and proposes a layered architecture with a concrete POWL sketch and data controls. It also correctly distinguishes trace deviation from business misconduct in several important cases, especially SUP-706, SUP-707, SUP-711, and SUP-713.

The main weakness is its treatment of authority. It calls SUP-702 and SUP-704 “JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION” as if the pilot and directive clearly override CS-9. But PD-3 was not incorporated into the controlled standard, and the Compliance observer expressly disclaimed approval to weaken it. FD-7 permits a temporary emergency route, but the answer does not establish that its issuer had authority to suspend CS-9. The burden-of-proof section notices this dispute, then resolves it too confidently in favor of the exceptions. Its table even labels the steering committee the sign-off for PD-3 while acknowledging that PD-3 was not a CS-9 amendment. A more defensible judgment would distinguish a documented operational exception from a proven authorized policy exception.

The case judgments also overstate some findings. SUP-715 is treated as a definite COI violation, though the evidence says disclosure is required and none was recorded; it does not establish whether disclosure or independent review could still occur. SUP-712 is stronger, because CS-9 expressly requires different humans and the aliases resolve to one person. Conversely, describing SUP-704 as fully FD-7-compliant overlooks that the evidence says quality and approval occurred the next business day, but does not expressly say retrospective approval was obtained.

The revised POWL is not reliably executable as presented. In the standard route, placing the whole concurrent checks block before approval requires both checks before manual approval, a stricter ordering than M0’s explicit rule that both precede activation. The emergency branch omits the required `Register_Supplier` event if each branch is intended to represent the full onboarding route. The LOOP explanation also asserts 0..n repetitions without demonstrating that the proposed PM4Py operator semantics actually provide that behavior. These are material modeling issues in a task that explicitly asks for executable code and careful semantics.

Finally, the system-level claim that the six August cases and 14 clearly ineligible cases demonstrate systematic unauthorized use is plausible, but their overlap and case-level evidence are not given. The answer should avoid summing or implying distinct violations without resolving that uncertainty. Overall, this is a thorough and well-organized response, but its authority conclusions, some case classifications, and key model semantics keep it well short of a high score.
