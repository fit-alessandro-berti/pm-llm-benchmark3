The answer is unusually thorough and addresses most of the requested dimensions, but several core calculations and allocation decisions are inconsistent or unsupported enough to undermine the policy.

The most serious problem is the portfolio arithmetic. In the base scenario, “direct breaches plausibly avoided” for I-09 is listed as 0.24, even though the supplied range is 0.18–0.30 and the table elsewhere uses 0.24; for I-02 and I-04 it then mixes calibration-adjusted and unadjusted effects. The adverse direct-benefit range also appears understated: its stated variants do not consistently match the listed lower-bound effects and case values. More broadly, the table subtracts the selected cases’ false-alarm burdens in the “intervention cost” column, while labeling the column as intervention cost; this obscures the requested cost breakdown. Spillover is monetized using a generic untreated-case value, but the six exposed cases and their actual values are unknown, and the answer does not show how selected cases are excluded from that pool. The resulting net ranges and scenario-weighted expected net are therefore not reliably auditable.

The policy also introduces many precise parameters without evidence: a 90-minute post-diagnosis duration, €15k untreated-case value, segment probability shifts, proportional scaling of intervention effects by adjusted risk, halving sparse-segment lower bounds, and a 0.8 risk cap. These are presented as working assumptions, but some are used to make consequential choices rather than clearly separated as illustrative placeholders. In particular, observational calibration bias does not by itself justify scaling causal risk-reduction estimates proportionally, and a calibration correction to breach risk does not validate that transformation. The “minimum evidence change” policy is also muddled: persistence across refreshes can qualify an alert despite no new event, while other parts suppress unchanged repeats.

The case allocation has contradictions. I-02 is described as eligible and scheduled at 12:45, but its LFS is also listed as 12:45, leaving no operational slack for a 45-minute intervention plus the assumed post-diagnosis time. I-09’s stated LFS of 12:15 is not reconciled with starting immediately at 12:00. I-07’s LFS is said to have passed at 11:15, although the answer derives it using a 90-minute post-diagnosis placeholder; the task does not supply that minimum duration, so this is not a defensible hard exclusion. I-01’s tier and positive direct value also do not align cleanly with its stated net benefit after spillover. The portfolio’s conditional swap to I-06 is not reconciled with the scenario table, which evaluates only I-02.

There are good elements: I-03 is correctly excluded by stage; I-05’s repeated unchanged alert is distinguished from I-07’s new customer log; the answer treats intervention effects as uncertain, proposes a reserve, and gives concrete fairness, feedback-loop, and monitoring safeguards. However, some safeguards are over-specified or internally awkward, such as requiring randomized near-tie assignment while also calling the choice ethically clean without discussing the obligations of cases that may be denied intervention, and proposing effect estimation from selectively observed outcomes without fully resolving confounding and spillover interference.

Overall, the response follows the six-section format and is operationally ambitious, but the false precision and inconsistent feasibility and impact calculations are central flaws in a decision policy whose quality depends on them.
