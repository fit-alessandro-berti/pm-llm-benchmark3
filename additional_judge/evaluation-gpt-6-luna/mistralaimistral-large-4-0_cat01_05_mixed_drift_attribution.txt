## 2. Quantitative decomposition

The March raw-fitness decline is primarily a measurement artifact: raw fitness fell from about 0.92 to 0.55, a 37-point drop, while mapped fitness was 0.90. Mapping the new eligibility label and removing duplicate `Open Case` events therefore recover nearly all of the apparent loss. This does not establish that every other metric is unaffected by logging changes: missing resources rose from about 1% to 5–6%, which can bias resource-level comparisons.

The May supplier shift is substantial. Supplier Z’s share rose from 18% in April to 43% in May, while rework rose from 13% to 24% and the request-info loop rate doubled from 12 to 24 per 100 cases. By July, the loop reached 29 per 100. This supports a genuine behavioral change, though supplier mix and complexity are entangled.

Performance deterioration is visible in both median and tail cycle time: median rose from 31.2 h in March to 45.0 h in August, and p90 from 68 h to 110 h. The modest increase in Assess service time, about 4.8 to 5.4 hours, alongside much larger reconstructed pre-Assessment queue growth supports queueing/resource pressure as a major contributor. The four assessor departures are temporally aligned with the further June–August deterioration, but monthly aggregates cannot quantify their share.

October–November bring both a volume increase and a high-complexity share jump. Compared with September, volume rises from 670 to 760–790 and high-complexity share from 26% to 41–45%. That changes the population and plausibly strains capacity. December improves on several measures, but its coincidence with lower load, supplier recovery, overtime, and a document-quality campaign prevents a clean intervention effect estimate.

## 3. Process-mining interpretation

The raw DFG suggests a new `Eligibility Screen` path and `Open Case -> Register` relation in March. After label mapping and duplicate-event removal, normalized rates are nearly unchanged from January, supporting a logging/semantic explanation for the raw fitness collapse.

From May onward, the `Assess -> Request Info -> Collect Documents` loop rises materially. Its rate per 100 cases is more interpretable than raw edge counts, but it is still an aggregate edge rate, not a case-level probability or proof that every case follows the whole loop. A fuller analysis should measure case-level loop incidence, number of repetitions, time spent in each loop, and supplier-specific rates.

The October `Senior Assessment` increase is ambiguous. It could reflect more complex cases, the priority rule, improved recording of an historically inconsistent activity, or a combination. Conformance results against the old normative model should not treat this edge increase alone as proof of behavioral nonconformance.

## 4. Causal assessment

The strongest evidence supports March migration-related logging/semantic drift and May supplier-related behavioral rework. Staffing loss plausibly contributed to queueing from June onward, supported by the timing and the divergence between queue wait and Assess service time. Renewal season plausibly adds population and workload effects in October–November.

These causes overlap. Supplier Z’s cases were already more complex, staffing constraints could amplify rework delays, and the priority rule may improve waits for one group while worsening them for another. Month-level aggregates cannot identify independent causal contributions. December’s improvement is especially confounded; it cannot be attributed to overtime or the quality campaign alone.

## 5. Onset classification

- **March:** Abrupt measurement and logging shift aligned with migration; operational process change is not established by the raw fitness drop.
- **May:** Abrupt supplier-linked rise in rework, followed by continued worsening.
- **June–August:** Capacity-related deterioration likely accumulates after assessor departures, alongside supplier rework.
- **July:** Priority-rule change is an abrupt policy intervention with potentially opposing subgroup effects.
- **October:** Recurring seasonal onset in volume and complexity, with a potentially abrupt senior-assessment recording or routing change.
- **December:** Partial reversal during multiple simultaneous changes; causal onset of recovery is unresolved.

No formal change-point analysis has been applied, so these onset labels are descriptive rather than statistically confirmed.

## 6. Limits and missing evidence

The DFG fragment is incomplete and cannot establish full trace conformance, decision outcomes, or end-to-end routing. Monthly aggregation hides within-month timing and subgroup differences. Resource missingness is concentrated in migrated activities and is not obviously random, limiting resource-level inference. Variant normalization applies only to the supplied mapped-fitness series, so other operational metrics may not share the same corrections.

Useful next evidence includes case-level event logs with stable case, supplier, region, complexity, priority, and resource fields; queue and service times segmented by those fields; case-level loop measures; and prior-year seasonal baselines. A formal change-point analysis could test timing but would not by itself establish cause.

## 7. Monitoring design

1. Track raw and normalized fitness together, with explicit counts of label substitutions, duplicate technical events, and missing resource IDs. Alert on a raw-fitness drop above 10 percentage points when normalization recovers most of the gap; route it first to data-quality review.

2. Monitor case-level rework incidence, loop repetitions, and elapsed time in the request-info cycle by supplier, region, and complexity. Alert when supplier-specific loop rates rise against their own baseline and compare with non-Z cases before assigning supplier cause.

3. Track queue wait and service time separately, by assessor capacity and case segment. Alert when queue wait rises materially while service time remains near baseline, and report the staffing and workload context.

4. After priority-rule changes, report waiting-time distributions separately for high-complexity and standard cases, including p50 and p90. Alert on opposite-direction changes across groups even if aggregate cycle time is stable.

5. Compare renewal-season volume, complexity, rework, and queue measures against prior seasonal periods. Monitor Senior Assessment frequency alongside recording completeness and case complexity.

6. Evaluate interventions with a seasonal comparison and, where data allow, a controlled interrupted time series or difference-in-differences design. Do not attribute December improvement to the interventions without accounting for falling load and supplier recovery.

## 8. Provisional attribution

March is predominantly logging/semantic drift, with high confidence. May’s rework increase is plausibly supplier-driven, with high but not definitive confidence because supplier mix and complexity differ. June–August performance deterioration is consistent with capacity-driven queueing combined with rework, with moderate-to-high confidence. July’s priority rule plausibly redistributes waits, but the aggregate evidence cannot confirm subgroup effects. October–November show clear seasonal population and workload change; the senior-assessment jump may also include recording change. December is a mixed recovery, and the intervention contribution remains uncertain.
