# 4. PM4Py and event-log handling

No code is necessary to establish the diagnoses; the supplied fragments support a temporal-pattern review. PM4Py could calculate interval relations and batch labels, but its output would describe timestamp patterns only. It cannot decide whether equal starts represent shared setup, whether a common end is folder submission, or whether overlapping intervals belong to different people.

For a PM4Py log, preserve `queue_arrival`, lifecycle start and completion, `recorded_at`, source, precision, sequence number, and resource identity as separate attributes. Do not replace a missing or suspect timestamp with `recorded_at`. Mark uncertain or reconstructed values explicitly, and keep the original fields.

Recommended treatment by cluster:

- **G1:** Model as a capacity-four batch only after confirming fixture semantics. Keep the item arrival times and group start/end separate.
- **G2:** Preserve the group start as observed. If reconstructing serial starts, label the reconstruction as an assumption; otherwise use interval-censored item starts.
- **G3:** Do not use the shared completion as four individual completion times. Keep folder submission time separately and censor or reconstruct completions with explicit assumptions.
- **G4:** Preserve observed adjacency. Flag whether starts are scanner events or derived from prior completions; do not interpret cycle time as hands-on service without evidence.
- **G5:** Do not assign all overlaps to one worker. Use pool-level throughput and elapsed intervals while person identity remains unresolved.
- **G6:** Treat lifecycle start and completion as unavailable for service-time analysis. Retain 17:01 as insertion time and 17:00 as the recorded bulk-form value, not as proven execution times.
- **G7:** Do not treat rounded lifecycle values as point timestamps. Represent uncertainty intervals only when the rounding rule is established; otherwise mark durations and waits as uncertain.
- **G8:** Represent downtime and queue time separately once clocks can be aligned. The repair items appear sequential during the observed drain.

Sensitivity checks should vary time tolerance and compare results by source and cluster. A smaller tolerance may split near-equal records, but it cannot recover information lost to rounding or distinguish execution from bulk entry. Report how many labels change and avoid presenting one tolerance as proof of physical semantics.

# 5. Non-identifiability examples

1. **Identical starts and ends:** G1 could be a genuine four-item fixture batch; G6 could be a bulk form assigning one timestamp to many records; G7 could be quarter-hour rounding. All can yield a “simultaneous” temporal pattern, though the physical processes differ.
2. **Shared start, staggered ends:** G2 could be group setup followed by sequential work, genuine parallel inspection, or starts rounded to a common value while completions are live.
3. **Staggered starts, shared end:** G3 could represent reviews completed throughout the period and submitted together, real multitasking, multiple reviewers using one account, or a scheduled/common completion stamp.
4. **Adjacent intervals:** G4 and G8 could be serial work, scanner-derived boundaries, or fixed-cycle pacing. Adjacency alone does not prove uninterrupted hands-on effort.
5. **Overlapping intervals under one ID:** G5 could be several analysts on a shared account, one analyst multitasking, or lifecycle intervals that include pauses.
6. **Long wait then regular drain:** G8 could reflect capacity loss during the safety check, an intentional release policy, or clock offset between systems. The timestamps do not establish which caused the initial delay.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

Prioritize the following changes:

1. Capture execution starts and completions at the point of work; keep them nullable when unknown. Store form submission in a separate, system-generated `recorded_at` field.
2. Store timestamp precision and rounding rule per field, and retain unrounded values when available.
3. Synchronize source clocks and log clock offsets, especially between queue and safety systems.
4. Capture badge or session identity behind shared logins; define resource capacity and fixture positions.
5. Emit explicit group or batch identifiers and group lifecycle events for fixtures, group starts, folder submissions, and bulk closures.
6. Record per-item pickup/start events where group interfaces currently provide only one start, and mark derived timestamps with provenance.
7. Capture pause/resume transitions and downtime on the same clock as item events.
8. Retain sequence number and capture client event time as well as server insertion time.

# 7. Final adjudication

The strongest current conclusions are that G1 is consistent with a four-item fixture batch, G4 with a regular serial cycle, and G8 with a delayed queue followed by a serial drain. These are defensible working interpretations, not proof of all physical execution details.

G2 is best treated as a group-start event with uncertain item service intervals. G3’s shared completion is likely a folder submission timestamp and should not feed per-item service statistics. G5 supports pool-level elapsed-time and throughput reporting, but not worker-level utilization. G6 has no usable service duration and may show inflated apparent waiting alongside near-zero recorded service. G7’s lifecycle times are rounded and should not be used as exact points.

Until the underlying capture semantics are clarified, performance reporting should preserve uncertainty: use bounds or censored values where execution times are unavailable, distinguish batch and submission events from item work, and avoid resource-level utilization claims where identity or timing is unreliable.
