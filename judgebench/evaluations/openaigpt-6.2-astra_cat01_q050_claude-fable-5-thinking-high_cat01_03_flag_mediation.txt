6.3/10. The timestamp decomposition is the strongest part of the answer. The main deductions are for causal errors that change what the proposed interventions mean and what the pilot could establish.

- **P1 changes meaning.** Suppressing the *display* need not disable automated priority assignment or mandatory routing. The answer nevertheless predicts that flagged cases lose their lane and review demand falls. If only the interface changes, human judgment might change while both automated rules remain intact. E1, E2, and the policy comparison depend on resolving this distinction.
- **Policy effects are mislabeled as mediated effects.** P3 can change reviewer assignment, service, and the timing of arrivals at the senior queue, not just manual waiting. P2 can change reviewer behavior even for cases still reviewed. P4 combines changing criteria and adding capacity, two interventions with different consequences for who escalates. These are useful *total policy contrasts* once specified, but they do not isolate the claimed pathways without additional assumptions. E3 also asks for an unflagged review distribution conditional on exact score where there is no observed same-score support. Holding waiting fixed while changing review occurrence is undefined for a case that skips review.
- **The threshold argument contains a substantive error.** Perfect score separation in 20 cases does not prove a deterministic population rule. More importantly, if a sharp cutoff *is* confirmed, the absence of flagged cases below it and unflagged cases above it is precisely what a sharp regression-discontinuity design expects—not a reason it fails, as reversal condition 5 claims. The actual concerns are the unknown cutoff, sparse observations approaching it, and case mix: all three unflagged cases in the proposed band are High complexity, while both flagged cases are Medium.
- **The pilot cannot deliver two of its advertised conclusions as designed.** A 50/50 case-randomized comparison can estimate an assignment effect under a mixed policy. Blocking by time does not by itself identify what happens to shared queues under full P2 versus P0. Randomizing policy saturation across queue periods and accounting for backlog carryover would address that question. Likewise, auditing skipped intervention cases alone supplies no comparable control-arm error rate for the proposed two-percentage-point stopping rule. Reinstating mandatory review when the queue is overloaded could also increase demand at exactly the wrong time.
- **Several empirical claims exceed or contradict the records.** R17 is a High-complexity review handled by M1, contradicting “M2 handles all High-complexity reviews.” The flagged High-complexity median cycle is **24.1 hours**, not approximately 21. The 21.9-versus-8.8-hour comparison conditions on escalation; it does not show that “most flagged excess travels through” escalation. Among reviewed cases, escalation occurred in **6/10 versus 2/4**—weak evidence for a different escalation threshold. Reversal condition 1 also gets the direction wrong: greater hidden severity among *unflagged* cases would not explain why flagged cases took longer.
- **Some falsification tests do not discriminate between the stated explanations.** Equal queue lengths need not imply equal available staffing, workload ahead, or working hours. Triage service and residual-to-close can themselves change under the policy, making them questionable negative controls. And the sensitivity section supplies no bias model translating hidden risk into an hours-scale median difference.

The answer deserves substantial credit for separating waiting from service, preserving structural absences, reporting support counts, providing usable event-reshaping code, and distinguishing rejection from verified decision quality. Its descriptive conclusion—earlier triage alongside more downstream processing—is persuasive. Its stronger claims about *which mechanism accounts for the delay* and *what the pilot would identify* are not.
