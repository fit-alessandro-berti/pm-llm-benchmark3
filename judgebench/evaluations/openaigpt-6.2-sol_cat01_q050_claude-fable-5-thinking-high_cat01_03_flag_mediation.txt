- **The intervention framework is better than the estimands.** P1 suppresses display; it does not necessarily disable automated priority routing or review, yet the policy table assumes both change. P3 estimates a capacity-policy effect, not an isolated effect through review waiting: earlier review can change reviewer assignment, escalation timing, and downstream congestion. P4 conflates changing escalation criteria with adding senior capacity. The review-occurrence intervention also needs to specify how waiting, service, and escalation are generated for cases newly routed to review. A difference between escalated and non-escalated cases does not show that escalation carries “most” of the flagged excess.
- **Several identification claims are technically wrong or too strong.** A deterministic threshold is compatible with regression discontinuity; what is missing here is a verified cutoff and adequate observations near it, not both flag values at the same score. A standard E-value does not directly quantify confounding of a raw median difference in hours. Triage service and residual-to-close are not credible negative controls without ruling out causal paths through lane assignment, routing, and staffing. Likewise, comparing cases at equal observed queue lengths does not isolate prioritization from congestion or staffing.
- **The pilot cannot estimate what it promises.** A fixed 50/50 case-level assignment within each block does not, by itself, identify system spillovers relative to P0. Randomizing independent queues or treatment saturation would better address that question. Auditing only discretion-arm cases that skip review cannot support a stopping rule based on the difference in miss rates versus control. And reverting to mandatory review when the queue is already overloaded increases review demand relative to continuing the pilot; it may be a safety pause, but it is not a coherent capacity guardrail.

The queue-interference reversal condition is actually about batching or staffing, while the hidden-risk condition points in the wrong direction: greater hidden severity among *unflagged* cases does not explain longer completion times among *flagged* cases.

The answer deserves credit for separating stage occurrence from conditional duration, preserving absent stages as missing in the code, distinguishing elapsed time from causal effects in principle, and making decision quality part of the policy comparison. But the numerical discrepancies and design flaws undermine precisely the evidence the answer uses to recommend P2.
