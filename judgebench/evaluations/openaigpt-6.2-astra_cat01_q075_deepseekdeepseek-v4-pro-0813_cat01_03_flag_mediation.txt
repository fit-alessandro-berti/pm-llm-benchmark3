The main deductions concern the causal analysis rather than the arithmetic.

- **The intervention contrasts are not precise enough.** For P1, the answer identifies the key ambiguity—whether hiding the flag also disables automated routing—but never chooses a version. Staff inferring risk from a still-visible score is a possible response to display suppression, not a failure to isolate the display intervention. E3 then inherits the ambiguity by using the review distribution “under P1.” E4 substitutes unflagged cases’ waits without specifying a feasible scheduling rule. E5 calls a change in escalation policy an indirect effect without defining the parent policy contrast it mediates. The answer claims to use controlled direct effects but never specifies one with a mediator held fixed.
- **There is a concrete causal-ordering error.** Complexity is assigned at intake and can influence the flag. It cannot mediate an effect of that later flag, contrary to the warning that adjusting for complexity could block a flag-mediated path. The graph also lacks a path from hidden risk through human judgment to routing independent of the measured score and complexity, despite invoking that path in the prose. Asking for “no interference” to explain delay *through shared demand* rules out the mechanism being studied.
- **The observed gaps locate elapsed time, not necessarily queue congestion.** Without ready-for-review or queue-entry timestamps, triage completion to manual start can include handoff, off-shift, or eligibility delays. R20’s long elapsed time is not evidence that another flagged case delayed it. Calling manual review the “largest observed downstream driver” therefore goes beyond the otherwise careful descriptive analysis.
- **Several identification tests are overinterpreted.** The observed scores are completely separated by flag: unflagged scores end at 0.61 and flagged scores begin at 0.64. A threshold study needs a verified assignment rule and substantially more local data. Even a convincing null *local* effect would not show that the overall gap is mostly selection. Likewise, attenuation after adjusting for queue state does not establish interference: backlog measured after triage can itself be treatment-induced. The negative-control proposal names no concrete control, and “compliance-adjusted ITT” conflates an assignment effect with an effect of treatment received.
- **The pilot would not cleanly test P2 as written.** Cases and backlogs span half-day blocks, so control and intervention blocks can affect each other. The plan gives no carryover analysis. Protected manual slots also introduce a P3-like change unless provided identically in both arms. Eligibility ambiguously excludes cases subject to the very review rule being tested. Low discretionary uptake would still give informative evidence about the *policy’s* effectiveness; it would not make the randomized comparison uninformative.
- **One explicit code requirement is missed.** The small-subgroup warning never fires: both tested flag groups have exactly ten cases, while stage-specific supports as small as two go unchecked.

The answer explains **where the extra hours appear** substantially better than it defines **which intervention would remove them, for whom, and at what cost to other cases**. That distinction is central to the task.
