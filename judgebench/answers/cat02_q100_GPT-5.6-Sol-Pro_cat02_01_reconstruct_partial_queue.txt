# 1. Assumptions and notation

**A1 — Population completeness.** The table is treated as containing every case that contributes to S1 or S2 WIP during 08:00–14:00. In particular, there is no unlisted pre-08:00 backlog. If that assumption is false, the reconstructed queues and utilization are lower bounds for the real system.

**A2 — Central-clock reconstruction.** Reported timestamps are used as central estimates. Each reported operational timestamp (t) represents an actual time in approximately ([t-2.5,t+2.5]) minutes. Therefore, a duration computed from two rounded timestamps has the conservative coordinate-wise envelope

[
D_{\text{actual}}\in[\max(0,D_{\text{reported}}-5),,D_{\text{reported}}+5].
]

These are arithmetic outer bounds, not confidence intervals.

**A3 — Exact observation cutoff.** The analysis cutoff (T=14{:}00) is treated as exact. An elapsed duration from a rounded start or arrival to (T) therefore has a (\pm2.5)-minute rounding envelope rather than (\pm5).

**A4 — Tandem-station semantics.** S2 queue entry is the S1 completion event:

[
A_{i,2}=E_{i,1}.
]

There is no transportation delay between stations in the reconstructed WIP accounting.

**A5 — Resource feasibility.** Each listed resource is single-task and service is assumed non-preemptive. Missing starts must not precede queue entry, overlap the previous activity on the same resource, follow their own completion, or overlap the next known activity. Undocumented assistance is not assumed because a conflict-free reconstruction exists.

**A6 — Station-wide queue convention.** Queue length is calculated at station level, not as four separate resource-specific queues. The recorded resource constrains service feasibility, but not whether a waiting case counts as S1 or S2 queue WIP.

**A7 — Simultaneous-event convention.** Waiting intervals are half-open ([A,S)), and service intervals are half-open ([S,E)). At the same reported time, the conceptual order is completion, station arrival, and then service start. Reported queue snapshots are the state after processing the full timestamp batch. This prevents a zero-wait transfer or handoff from creating an artificial persistent queue.

**A8 — Missing-start scenarios.** Missing starts are not replaced by one supposedly known value. Three reported-clock scenarios are used:

* **Service-heavy:** earliest feasible start, hence maximum service and minimum waiting.
* **Midpoint:** midpoint of the feasible start interval.
* **Service-light:** latest feasible start, hence minimum service and maximum waiting.

The mathematical lower bound of zero service means “arbitrarily short given the timestamp resolution,” not that no work occurred.

**A9 — Status semantics.** `MISSING`, `OPEN`, and blank remain distinct:

* `MISSING`: activity completed, but its start is interval-censored.
* `OPEN`: start known, completion right-censored; elapsed service is counted, final service is unknown.
* Blank after station entry: waiting is right-censored at 14:00.
* Blank before station entry: the case has not reached that station.

**A10 — Capacity denominators.** S1 has (2\times360=720) available resource-minutes. S2 has

[
360+ (360-15)=705
]

available resource-minutes because C2 is unavailable for 15 minutes. Busy-time numerators include elapsed work on open activities.

**A11 — Rate intervals.** Subperiod counts use half-open intervals ([a,b)). Because timestamps near a subperiod boundary may shift by 2.5 minutes, a boundary count may change by one case; the directional conclusions are more reliable than the final decimal place of a rate.

**A12 — Little’s Law.** A full-station steady-state Little’s-Law estimate is not claimed: both queues change materially over the window, arrivals are strongly nonstationary, and eight cases are incomplete. The check is restricted to service subsystems during intervals in which the station queue stays nonempty and the resources operate in a quasi-saturated regime.

In the tables below, `d ± 5` denotes a reported duration of (d) minutes and its rounding envelope. For a missing start, `a–b; M=m` gives the reported-clock range and midpoint; a wider rounding-expanded envelope is stated separately.

# 2. Reconstruction method and formulas

For case (i) and station (j\in{1,2}), let:

* (A_{ij}): station arrival;
* (S_{ij}): service start;
* (E_{ij}): completion;
* (T=14{:}00): observation cutoff.

For a started activity,

[
W_{ij}=S_{ij}-A_{ij}.
]

For a case still waiting at (T), only elapsed waiting is known:

[
\widetilde W_{ij}(T)=T-A_{ij}, \qquad W_{ij}^{\text{final}}\geq \widetilde W_{ij}(T).
]

For a completed activity,

[
B_{ij}=E_{ij}-S_{ij}.
]

For an open activity,

[
\widetilde B_{ij}(T)=T-S_{ij}, \qquad B_{ij}^{\text{final}}\geq \widetilde B_{ij}(T).
]

Completed station sojourn is

[
V_{ij}=E_{ij}-A_{ij}=W_{ij}+B_{ij}.
]

For a case still in the station, elapsed station sojourn is (T-A_{ij}). For a completed two-station case,

[
C_i=E_{i2}-A_{i1}.
]

The total case sojourn is unaffected by an internal missing start.

For a missing start, the reported-clock feasible interval is

[
S_{ij}\in[L_{ij},U_{ij}],
]

where

[
L_{ij}=\max{A_{ij},\text{ previous completion on the same resource}},
]

and

[
U_{ij}=\min{E_{ij},\text{ next observed start on the same resource}}.
]

Queue length, number in service, and station WIP are

[
Q_j(t)=\sum_i\mathbf 1(A_{ij}\le t<S_{ij}),
]

with an unstarted case interpreted as (S_{ij}>T),

[
R_j(t)=\sum_i\mathbf 1(S_{ij}\le t<E_{ij}),
]

with an open completion interpreted as (E_{ij}>T), and

[
L_j(t)=Q_j(t)+R_j(t).
]

Time-average queue length and WIP are obtained by area under the corresponding step functions:

[
\overline Q_j=\frac{1}{360}\int_{08{:}00}^{14{:}00}Q_j(t),dt,
\qquad
\overline L_j=\frac{1}{360}\int_{08{:}00}^{14{:}00}L_j(t),dt.
]

Equivalently, (\overline Q_j) is total observed or elapsed waiting exposure divided by 360 minutes, while (\overline L_j) is total completed or elapsed station-sojourn exposure divided by 360.

For resource (r),

[
\text{Busy}*r=
\sum_i
\left|[S*{ij},\min(E_{ij},T))\cap[08{:}00,T)\right|,
]

and station utilization is

[
U_j=\frac{\sum_{r\in j}\text{Busy}_r}
{\text{available resource-minutes at }j}.
]

Subperiod arrival and departure rates are

[
\lambda^A_j(a,b)=\frac{N^A_j[a,b)}{b-a},
\qquad
\lambda^D_j(a,b)=\frac{N^D_j[a,b)}{b-a}.
]

For the restricted service-subsystem Little check,

[
\overline R_j \approx \lambda^D_j,\overline B_j,
]

where (\overline R_j) is mean concurrent service occupancy and (\overline B_j) is the mean service time of departures in the selected quasi-saturated interval. A small residual is expected from boundary-crossing activities and the short finite window.

# 5. Bottleneck and temporal interpretation

**Evidence for an early S1 bottleneck.** From 08:00 to 10:00, S1 receives 13 cases but completes only six. Its external arrival rate is 6.5/h against a departure rate of 3.0/h, and its WIP grows by seven. By 10:00, S1 has six waiting cases and seven total WIP, whereas S2 has two waiting and four WIP. S1 waits rise from 5 minutes for P01/P02 to 50–75 minutes around P07/P08. The early system congestion is therefore generated primarily before calibration.

**Evidence for a transition around 10:00–11:10.** S1 arrivals and departures balance at five each over this subperiod, but this balance occurs with an already large queue rather than an empty system. S2 receives five transfers and completes four, so downstream WIP begins to accumulate. C2’s outage occurs inside this transition, reducing available S2 capacity while two new S2 arrivals occur.

**Evidence for a late S2 bottleneck.** After 11:10, external arrivals to S1 fall to 1.4/h while S1 completes 3.2/h. Consequently, S1 WIP falls by five and reaches only two cases at 14:00. S2, however, continues receiving the delayed S1 output at 3.2/h while completing 2.5/h. Its WIP rises by two, reaches seven at 13:45, and remains six at the cutoff. At 14:00, S2 contains two open calibrations and four waiting cases, compared with one open inspection and one waiting case at S1.

**Structural capacity evidence.** The nonmissing completed-service medians are approximately 30 minutes at S1 and 40 minutes at S2. With two resources, these correspond to rough nominal capacities near 4/h and 3/h, respectively, before allowing for handoffs and variation. During the C2 outage, S2 temporarily has only one available resource. This makes S2 the lower-capacity station under sustained flow even though S1 experiences the first backlog because of the concentrated external arrival burst.

**Full-window utilization does not identify one static bottleneck.** The imputation-based utilization ranges overlap substantially: 74–84% for S1 and 73–81% for S2. Utilization averages away the sequence in which congestion moves through the tandem system. Queue trajectories, rate imbalances, and cutoff WIP are more diagnostic here than choosing the station with the slightly larger midpoint utilization.

**Maintenance interpretation.** C2 finishes P06 at 10:50, is unavailable from 10:55 through 11:10, and starts P08 at 11:15. Thus the observed C2 gap is 25 minutes, of which 15 minutes are known unavailable capacity. P08 had already entered the S2 queue at 10:20. The outage therefore plausibly delayed the C2 chain, but only the 15 resource-minutes of lost capacity are directly identified. The remaining pre- and post-outage gaps could reflect normal handoff, rounding, dispatching, or delayed logging.

A no-maintenance counterfactual cannot be reconstructed exactly because the dispatch rule and expected handoff gap are not supplied. The outage is also insufficient by itself to explain the late S2 backlog: S2 waits were already 20–25 minutes for P06/P07, and the queue continues growing long after C2 returns.

The most defensible bottleneck conclusion is therefore **time-varying**:

[
\boxed{\text{S1 is the dominant early queueing bottleneck; S2 becomes the dominant late-flow bottleneck.}}
]

* Moving a missing start later converts inferred service into inferred waiting.

Nonmissing medians of 30 minutes at S1 and 40 minutes at S2 suggest, as a **hypothesis**, that P07/P15 and especially P05/P11 may be nearer the service-heavy end. For example, P05’s entire feasible window is only 30 minutes and P11’s only 25 minutes, both shorter than the nonmissing S2 median. This makes the service-light zero-duration limit mathematically useful but operationally unlikely.

## Rounding sensitivity

Applying all (\pm2.5)-minute errors adversarially and independently gives broad coordinate-wise envelopes:

| station | mean queue outer envelope | mean WIP outer envelope |           busy-time outer envelope | utilization outer envelope |
| ------- | ------------------------: | ----------------------: | ---------------------------------: | -------------------------: |
| S1      |                 3.81–4.60 |               5.50–6.08 | approximately 443–708 resource-min |       approximately 61–98% |
| S2      |                 1.89–2.54 |               3.49–3.96 | approximately 450–645 resource-min |       approximately 64–91% |

These are deliberately conservative and wider than the jointly feasible region because they allow every rounded start and end to take its most extreme duration-increasing or duration-decreasing position simultaneously. They should not replace the reported-clock imputation ranges as the main estimates.

Rounding also affects exact event order. The most notable tie is P07’s C1 completion and P09’s C1 start, both reported at 11:00. Their actual times could imply a gap, a direct handoff, or an apparent overlap of up to five minutes. The non-overlap assumption resolves the physical schedule without needing undocumented assistance.

## Right censoring

P21, P15, and P17 contribute elapsed busy time but no departure. P22, P16, P18, P19, and P20 contribute elapsed queue exposure and WIP but no completed wait. Their final waits and services have finite lower bounds but no data-supported upper bounds.

Consequently:

* The completed-service means exclude long-running open services and may be downward biased.
* The completed-case total-sojourn mean excludes the later, congested cases and is not a full-cohort performance estimate.
* Treating open cases as if they completed at 14:00 would incorrectly turn elapsed lower bounds into final durations.
* Dropping open and waiting cases would reduce S1 WIP by two and S2 WIP by six at the cutoff, materially distorting the bottleneck conclusion.

## Conclusions that are stable

The following survive all three missing-start scenarios:

* S1 accumulates the early external-arrival burst.
* S1 drains after external arrivals taper.
* S2 accumulates the delayed S1 output late in the window.
* At 14:00, S2 has three times the WIP of S1: six versus two.
* Completed station sojourn, completed-case total sojourn, total station WIP, and whole-system WIP do not depend on missing-start imputation.
* The maintenance outage contributes to S2 pressure but cannot alone explain the late backlog.
* A time-varying S1-to-S2 bottleneck interpretation is stronger than a single static bottleneck classification.

The exact full-window utilization ranking, the wait/service split for four cases, and the case-level delay attributable specifically to maintenance are not stable or identifiable.

The most valuable measurement improvements would be reliable start capture, explicit resource-availability events, and logging the dispatch decision that assigns a waiting case to C1 or C2.

def derive_missing_start_bounds(frame, station):
    """Central reported-clock bounds using entry and same-resource neighbors."""
    start_col = f"{station}_start"
    end_col = f"{station}_end"
    resource_col = f"{station}_resource"
    entry_col = "arrival_s1" if station == "s1" else "arrival_s2"

    out = []
    missing = frame[f"{station}_start_status"].eq("MISSING")

    for i, row in frame[missing].iterrows():
        resource = row[resource_col]
        own_end = row[end_col]
        entry = row[entry_col]

        peers = frame[
            (frame[resource_col] == resource) & (frame.index != i)
        ]

        prior_ends = peers.loc[
            peers[end_col].notna() & (peers[end_col] <= own_end), end_col
        ]
        next_starts = peers.loc[
            peers[start_col].notna() & (peers[start_col] >= own_end), start_col
        ]

        lower_candidates = [entry]
        if not prior_ends.empty:
            lower_candidates.append(prior_ends.max())

        upper_candidates = [own_end]
        if not next_starts.empty:
            upper_candidates.append(next_starts.min())

        lower = max(lower_candidates)
        upper = min(upper_candidates)

        if lower > upper:
            raise ValueError(
                f"No feasible missing-start interval for {row['case']} {station}"
            )

        out.append({
            "case": row["case"],
            "station": station,
            "lower": lower,
            "upper": upper,
        })

    return pd.DataFrame(out)

def state_after_time(case_data, timestamp):
    """Right-continuous state after all events at timestamp have been applied."""
    arrival = case_data["arrival"]
    start = case_data["start"]
    end = case_data["end"]

    queue = (
        arrival.notna()
        & (arrival <= timestamp)
        & (start.isna() | (start > timestamp))
    ).sum()

    in_service = (
        start.notna()
        & (start <= timestamp)
        & (end.isna() | (end > timestamp))
    ).sum()

    wip = (
        arrival.notna()
        & (arrival <= timestamp)
        & (end.isna() | (end > timestamp))
    ).sum()

    return int(queue), int(in_service), int(wip)

def peak_state(case_data):
    event_times = pd.concat([
        case_data["arrival"].dropna(),
        case_data["start"].dropna(),
        case_data["end"].dropna(),
        pd.Series([T]),
    ]).drop_duplicates().sort_values()

    states = [state_after_time(case_data, t) for t in event_times]
    return tuple(max(state[i] for state in states) for i in range(3))
