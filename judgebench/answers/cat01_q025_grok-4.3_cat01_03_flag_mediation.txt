We use interventional (controlled/separable) analogues rather than natural direct/indirect effects because the flag is produced by a policy bundle; cross-world counterfactuals that hold the flag fixed while changing downstream routing are not well-defined without additional assumptions on reviewer behavior.


Interference arises because manual and escalation queues are shared; one case’s routing changes waiting times for others (U_queue_state). Exposure-induced confounding occurs because flag affects both manual_review occurrence and subsequent queue state. Treatment-confounder feedback exists if reviewers update perceived risk after seeing flag-induced delays. Timestamp semantics are local; simultaneous events across resources are not synchronized.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|----------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------|-------------|----------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| within-risk-score effect | local regression at 0.65–0.75 threshold | risk_score, complexity, arrival_load | continuity of potential outcomes | McCrary density test; placebo on pre-flag data | discontinuity in triage time disappears |
| queue-state-aware | include lagged queue length at arrival | arrival_load, prior 4h case count | queue measure captures interference | add future arrivals as negative control | coefficient on lagged queue changes sign |
| policy-change encouragement | difference-in-differences around flag rollout | time, case mix | parallel trends absent anticipation | pre-trend test on cycle_time | parallel pre-trends violated |
| mediation-oriented | interventional g-computation of P2 vs P0 | flag, complexity, arrival_load | no exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding | compare with natural effects estimator | large discrepancy indicates feedback |
| negative-control outcome | time from close to next unrelated event | all observed covariates | control shares same confounding structure | association with flag after adjustment | residual association appears |
| sensitivity for unobserved risk | Rosenbaum bounds or E-value on cycle_time contrast | observed covariates | unobserved risk bounded | E-value < 1.8 | conclusion reverses within plausible bound |
| interference-aware | cluster by day or simulate queue | day-level load | clusters capture spillover | within-day vs between-day contrast | between-day contrast larger |

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. Hidden risk: if U_risk drives both flag and escalation need, removing mandatory review increases rejects.
2. Queue interference stronger than estimated: protecting flagged capacity lengthens unflagged waits beyond tolerance.
3. Decision quality drops: pilot shows >2 pp rise in incorrect rejects.
4. Timestamp semantics: local clocks reveal review start after escalation start in >5 % cases.
5. Positivity violation: no overlap in high-load normal-risk cells, making P3 effects unidentified.
6. Reviewer behavior change: reviewers begin manual review on priority-lane cases even without flag, nullifying P2.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed are faster triage starts and longer manual/escalation intervals for flagged cases, plus shared-queue occupancy. A plausible mechanism is priority-lane acceleration offset by mandatory review routing and contention on shared resources. What remains unidentified is the portion of extra cycle time attributable to the flag itself versus the bundle of routing rules, reviewer reactions, and unobserved risk. The most useful next evidence comes from the P2 pilot: randomizing removal of mandatory review while preserving priority triage yields a controlled contrast on both cycle time and decision quality under realistic queue interference, directly informing whether the current policy bundle can be safely slimmed.
