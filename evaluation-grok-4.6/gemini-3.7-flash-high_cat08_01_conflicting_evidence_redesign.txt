**5.2**

The answer is well-structured and often specific, but it is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading it has material data errors, overconfident quantification the prompt forbids, and several redesign claims that contradict the same evidence it cites.

**Evidence use.** The diagnosis does use the required artifacts: frequency DFG, performance DFG, token replay (0.91 / 68%), alignment deviations, low precision, rework rates (14.1% vs 3.3%, 26% repeat evidence, 12.5% high-value repair rework), the 31%→9% cost erosion, segment outcomes, and resource constraints. That is a real strength. It is then undermined by avoidable misreads. Intake→auto→fast approval→dispatch is treated as a 2,550-case, ~5-hour *median* path; 2,550 is only `Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval`, while `Fast approval → Dispatch replacement` is 2,300, and the given medians sum to about 2.7 hours (0.5+0.4+1.8). ~5 hours is the *mean*. The diagram then labels `Dispatch replacement → Quality/compliance close` as 2,320 / 98% of 2,300 and `→ complaint` as 260 / 11% of the same 2,300. Those percentages cannot both be right and already exceed inflow; 2,320+260=2,580. R3 is misstated: the log’s frequent deviation is reopen *after close*, and 310 of 510 reopens already go to manual review. The answer claims reopens “bypass mandatory Manual eligibility review, routing straight to ad-hoc resolutions or premature closure,” which the DFG does not show. “32% of claims violate internal operating rules” flattens 68% fully-fitting traces and 0.91 fitness into a headcount of 1,600 policy breaches. Manual-review “2,600 touches” is computed from the 5,000-case log with no observation window, then used as if it proved utilization above 82%.

**Process-mining reasoning.** Control-flow, conformance, rework, and long-tail complaint latency (p90 150–180 hours) are handled more seriously than in a generic ops memo. The core contradiction is named. But several inferences are sloppy. Low precision is reduced to “staff bypass SOP to clear queues,” which is one hypothesis, not a finding. Move-on-log deviations are mapped onto R1–R3 more tightly than the alignment text supports. Fitness “>0.98” and “exactly 0” infractions are invented targets, not implied by replay. Capacity conclusions are not process-mining results; they are arithmetic without a time base.

**Trade-off handling.** Each redesign has a sacrifice paragraph, which is the right shape. The content is weaker. A claims a large *drop* in manual touches (2,600→~1,100) after hard-blocking auto-approval for all >EUR 400 work, while the prompt itself warns that tightening auto-screen rules can *raise* manual inflow 15–35%. Accessories are already mostly on the auto path (3,600 vs 1,400). Forcing the 35% high-value share plus unclear-defect work onto review is more likely to load the 7 reviewers and the 2 fraud specialists, not free them. B treats the 260 post-dispatch complaints as if they were almost all wrong-part shipments and promises them “near 0”; the temporal profile only *links* the long tail to part selection. C treats a credit-card hold plus ex-post dock audit as satisfying the *intent* of R1/R2. R1 is a sequencing rule (manual or fraud/risk review before fast approval above EUR 400), not a financial-hold rule. That is a compliance substitution, not a conformance fix. The table then assigns tight percentage bands (−18% to −24% cost, +50% to +65% cycle-time reduction, complaints “~5%” / “<3%”) the prompt says not to fabricate.

**Redesign logic.** A/B/C are actually different mechanisms (hard segment guardrails, in-process quality engineering, digital intake with ex-post assurance), not parameter tweaks, and they are tied to named segments and edges. That meets the non-generic bar. They are still oversold: “100% R1/R2/R3,” “eradication” of replacement losses, quoting “real-time,” 140 hours → “<24 hours,” “prevents the 320 abandoned claims,” “manual review eliminated for 75%.” The portal range in the prompt is 30–45% fewer repeat evidence requests *and* a risk of more abandonment; C repeatedly takes the optimistic bound and assumes the assisted-hub fully cancels the literacy risk. B’s “repair-first” stance is not justified for low-risk accessories, where the data say the fast path is cheaper and complaints are much lower (6–8%). A’s 48–72 hour high-value target is also confused: current *reviewed* medians are already 96–120 hours, so 48–72 hours would be an acceleration of the compliant path, not only a sacrifice of the 20–28 hour illicit fast path.

**Synthesis / table / experiments.** The table’s “current is strictly dominated; A/B/C are all Pareto-optimal” is a direct violation of the instruction not to claim exact ROI or Pareto dominance from this evidence. “Dominated?” never uses *conditional*, which is the only defensible entry given missing period, overlapping segments, and unknown mix inside the 2,550 fast approvals. Sign notation is ambiguous (“+15% to +25% overall reduction”). Failure modes are the best section: A’s 15–35% inflow shock and fraud-specialist migration, B’s 2-inspector gate, C’s returns/chargebacks are grounded. The experiment sequence then blurs the three alternatives by piloting A+B together in weeks 1–4, so Stage 1 cannot attribute complaint or cost movement to guardrails versus the pre-dispatch gate. Stop/go thresholds are mostly usable, and the 60-day hold against the 180-hour complaint tail is the right process-mining caution. Green-light rules (fitness ≥0.98, net cost ≥15%, HV complaints ≤6%) are again more precise than the log can support.

**Net.** This is a fluent sponsor memo that looks evidence-rich and then repeatedly overfits, misreads the DFG, and asserts dominance the question forbade. Those are not cosmetic defects. A nearly flawless answer would keep every cited number internally consistent, refuse false precision, treat R1–R3 as control-flow constraints rather than commercial workarounds, and keep the three redesigns experimentally separable.
