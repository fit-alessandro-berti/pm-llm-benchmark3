**6.2**

This is a strong, well-structured advisory that uses the artifacts, keeps the three redesigns mechanistically distinct, and generally respects segment-specific trade-offs. It is not nearly flawless. Several errors are definitional or methodological, including one that violates an explicit constraint, so a high score is not warranted.

**What it does well.** The diagnosis is anchored in real objects (frequency DFG, performance DFG, token replay, alignments, model precision, temporal profile, rework stats, segment outcomes, resource constraints) rather than Lean slogans. The central contradiction is resolved by segment and error cost (accessories vs high-value), not by picking a single winner. A/B/C attack different layers—authorization, evidence timing, fulfillment/recovery—and each names a sacrifice. Failure modes include bottleneck migration. The experiment section’s *philosophy* is good: fully loaded cost, post-close windows, alignment counts, holdouts, watch the next queue.

**Critical reasoning errors.**

1. **Pareto dominance is used incorrectly, against the stated constraint.** Fast-approval cases remain *9% cheaper after* complaints, reshipments, and credits. That is a shrunken advantage, not a vanished one, and it is still a cost *win*. Current also wins raw speed on the dominant path. A design that is better on speed and residual cost and worse on quality/compliance is the textbook *non-dominated* trade-off. Marking current “Dominated? Yes,” then writing that A/B/C “each dominate the current process,” is a definitional mistake *and* a direct violation of “do not claim that exact ROI or exact Pareto dominance can be known.” “Loading” compliance into cost without monetizing it does not create dominance.

2. **“Economically irrational for high-value” is not supported.** The 9%/31% figures are aggregates, not a segment P&L. EUR 410 × an 18–22% complaint rate is a *risk pointer*, not a proof that high-value fast paths lose money. The same overclaim appears as “cost advantage mostly evaporates.”

3. **Medians cannot be chained into an E2E median.** `0.5+0.4+1.8+3.2 ≈ 6h` is invalid (median of sums ≠ sum of medians; edge times are not a full sojourn profile). The segment table already gives 8h/13h accessory fast-path medians; those should have been primary. The 6h figure then overstates how uniquely “fast” the DFG happy path is.

4. **DFG edge counts are treated as variant frequencies.** “2,300 of 5,000 (46%) run Intake → Auto → Fast approval → Dispatch → Close” is not something a DFG can certify. Fast approval also has 240 inflows from fraud/risk; dispatch has 65 inflows from escalation; dispatch→close is 2320, not 2300. Happy-path *trace* volume is strictly less than 2300, and several nodes fail flow conservation.

5. **Censoring bias is stated in the wrong direction.** 3015+220+390=3625 terminal vs 5000 starts is a real gap, but completed-case / naive DFG times usually make slow work look *better* (faster), not worse. The more important local fact is missed: Manual eligibility has ~2600 inflows vs ~1400 listed outflows (~1200 missing successors). That is the main sink, and it is exactly where Redesign A dumps more volume. Treating the gap as settled “right-censoring” overstates what the DFG supports.

**Redesign and table problems.**

- **A is ambiguous on the high-value outcome.** “Fast approval disabled” vs “review before dispatch” vs the 96–120h “reviewed/repair” medians are not the same policy. R1 allows Fast approval *after* manual or fraud review. If A still replaces after a short review, “modest” slowdown may hold; if it forces the repair path, Premium high-value 20h→96h is not “slightly slower” and is a major CX hit for a strict-SLA segment. That fork is never nailed down.
- **A’s first experiment does not test A.** Accessories already live on the fast lane. Stage 1 “pilot A on accessories” is a rules-engine canary, not a test of withdrawing high-value from auto-approval—the actual intervention and the 15–35% manual-inflow risk.
- **B over-claims the portal.** The prompt offers a 30–45% cut in *repeated* evidence requests, not first-time completeness or the whole 140h tail. External customer wait remains the constraint; “bounded front-end step” is too strong. The 78% “clean cases” figure is a reasonable inference from the 22% unclear-defect share, not a measured path statistic.
- **C plays loose with R3.** R3 requires Manual eligibility review after reopen, not a “templated rapid re-review” or empowered instant replacement. Instant goodwill for low-risk can also skip R2. The part-price automation is a fair inference from the stated wait cause, but it is not an offered lever on par with the portal, rule update, or pre-dispatch gate.
- **Missed, data-given lever:** Standard accessories “tolerate 72 hours if status visibility is good.” None of the three designs uses that as a load-shedding valve against the 82% manual-review cliff—the obvious capacity hedge if A’s rules tighten.
- **Table glyphs are broken** (` for accessories`, ` modest`), so cycle-time and cost directions are partly unreadable. Best-fit shares (43%, 22%, 35%) are arithmetic-correct.

**Experiment design.** Stage 0 instrumentation and “next-queue” checks are the right instinct. The 60-day post-close metric fights the data: complaint edges are mean 72–96h and p90 150–180h (~1 week). A weeks 2–6 pilot cannot observe 60-day tails, so Stage 1 go/no-go on complaints is either using a different (unstated) construct or is infeasible. Several numeric targets (97–98% gate pass, 30% complaint cut, fitness 0.95) are arbitrary; that is allowed, but they should not be implied by the log.

**Net.** Format-complete, non-generic, and unusually literate about fitness vs precision vs alignments. The score is held down by a Pareto/constraint violation, invalid E2E-median arithmetic, DFG-as-variant overclaim, reversed censoring logic, an under-specified high-value policy in A, R3 slippage in C, and a pilot that does not test the redesign it is named after. Those are not nits.
