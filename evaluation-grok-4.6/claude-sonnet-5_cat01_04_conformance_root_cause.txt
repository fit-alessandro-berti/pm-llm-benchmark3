**6.2**

The answer is well-structured and, in outline, does the right process-mining work: it uses the staged fitness recovery, refuses to indict employees from moves-on-log alone, treats missing compliance checks as unresolved, and flags an optional sign-off model as potentially overpermissive. Under a strict reading it is still not close to excellent. Several errors are conceptual, not cosmetic.

**Ranking and causal attribution are inconsistent with the chronology.** The sudden collapse is June–July (93% → 32%). That period recovers to 89% after label map, `Queue Routed` removal, and source-sequence ordering—*with the original model still in place*. The policy and `Supervisor Sign-off` arrive on 2026-08-01. Ranking “obsolete normative model” #2 (85) as an explanation of the measured collapse, above a dated ETL reorder with a 70=70 reverse DFG and an 81% → 89% repair, mixes two different phenomena. Worse, “real behavioral process change” is then scored 38 even though the same August policy *is* a real process change; obsolescence and genuine process change are complements here, not alternatives. The prose later tries to separate “cause of the drop” from “operational concern,” but the table does not actually do that.

**There is a clear logic reversal in the counterfactuals.** The source-sequence audit row says that if screenshots *match the recorded order*, the ETL-defect hypothesis is “fully confirmed.” If the recorded order is Notify-then-Decision and the source UI matches that order, the warehouse is *not* fabricating the reversal. That result would weaken the timestamp hypothesis, not confirm it. A discriminating-test table that gets the polarity of a provenance check backwards is a serious abductive failure.

**Section 6 is not executable as written.** `pm4py.parse_event_log_string` returns an `EventLog`, not a DataFrame. `log.copy(deep=True)`, `normalized["concept:name"].replace(...)`, and boolean row filters are pandas operations. The required “transparent normalization pipeline” would throw before any raw-vs-normalized comparison. The code also never produces alignment *diagnostics* (sync/log/model moves); it only prints aggregate fitness/precision. It never uses the April–May log as a baseline, and it never implements the third staged repair (Decision/Notify reordering), which is expressible even from variant strings. The candidate tree and the EUR 50,000 caveat are the strongest part of the block.

**Quantitative evidence is systematically garbled.** The ledger and later tables collapse contrasts into unreadable concatenations (`32%58%81%89%`, `72.071.871.5`, `1.25.96.4`, `Classify``Risk Screen`). In a forensic evidence ledger this is not a formatting nit; it destroys the very contrasts the argument depends on.

**Smaller but real slips.** Stable approval/appeal rates are treated as weakening “real process change,” which is too strong: a mandatory high-value sign-off need not move those KPIs. “Business outcomes unaffected” is listed as contradicting evidence for the label-change hypothesis; it is compatible, not contradictory. The 180/180 baseline of Enhanced Review → Compliance Check is never used as a contrast to the later 25/30 missing-check variants. Residual June `Classify` traces (n=30) are underused as mixed-logging evidence. Timestamp/order is under-weighted relative to its documentary and alignment support.

**What remains good, and why that is not enough.** The ledger coverage, the hidden-variable adjacency list, the four-phase isolation design, the DPN/precision warning, the 30–40% residual-compliance stance, and the six governance rules are all competent. They show the author understood the assignment. They do not offset a mis-ranked causal story, a backwards falsifier, and a non-runnable PM4Py section. On a generous rubric this would be high-7s to low-8s. Under the required standard it is a mid-6.
