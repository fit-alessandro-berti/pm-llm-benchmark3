**5.5**

The write-up is complete, on-format, and often thoughtful, but it is not a reliable policy: several assignments break the stated rules, the scoring procedure is not the procedure that produced the table, and training/compliance claims are inflated.

**What holds up.** Sections 1, 5, and 6 are the strongest. Hard safety rules are mostly separated from preference trade-offs. The worker note is plain and non-punitive. The audit list covers the required indicators (handover, reassignment, skill mismatch, load, overtime, SLA, rework/appeal, training share, specialist queues, policy deviations). The PM4Py sketch names the right families of functions. C001, C002, C004, C005, C007, C008, and C011 are sensible. Refusing Eva/Jonas on C015 because Security skill is 0 is the one sharp constraint reading.

**Material assignment errors.** C016 is the clearest miss. Constraint 5 says keep the owner on a high-complexity, high-handover case unless that owner is skill 0/1 and cannot be supervised. Eva is skill 2, the notes say continuity matters, and a handover has a 2.7h wait penalty against a 7h SLA. Moving it to Celine is the opposite of the rule, and it also contradicts the answer’s own exception (“override only if owner skill &lt; 2”). C015 then strips Hugo, the current Security-3 owner with low handover cost, so Celine can “train” on Security—her documented priority is negotiation. Celine ends up with C007 (must-keep, high rework, 6h), C015, C016 (high, 7h, complexity 0.79), and implied supervision of C009, on 6.0h remaining. That is a bottleneck the policy claims to avoid.

**False training accounting.** Constraint 3 is about *documented* training priorities. C009 (Amal / diagnosis) is valid. C010 is approval; Bruno’s priority is quality. C013 is eligibility; Jonas’s priority is security. C015 is security; Celine’s priority is negotiation. Treating those as training hits makes the 20% claim look met when, on a fair reading, only one normal/low case is aligned. C012 is explicitly “suitable for trainee” and is left with a Quality specialist.

**Policy ≠ decisions.** Step 1 says drop high-overtime resources unless the case is urgent and no safer option exists, then C003 stays with David (14 cases, 3.0h left, high OT, *normal* 18h SLA) while Celine is a free Diagnosis-3 alternative. That may be a continuity judgment, but it is not the algorithm as written. High-priority language also conflicts with itself (skill 1 forbidden vs allowed if supervised). An explainable, auditable policy cannot have a different rule in the prose, the pseudocode, and the table.

**Comparison is one-sided.** Section 4 never actually runs first-available or specialist-only on C001–C016. It then scores the proposed policy best on every axis. That violates the request not to pretend there is an obvious optimum. First-available is also caricatured (it still forbids skill 0). Throughput/SLA/resilience claims sit poorly next to Farid’s two urgent SLAs plus C010 supervision, and Celine’s triple load.

**Weaker but real gaps.** The assignment column rarely records a supervised pair, though the prompt asked for that. Hours remaining are not used as a hard capacity check. `discover_batches` / `filter_case_performance` are unused. The PM4Py block explores networks and times; it does not compute the audit metrics the policy would need (skill-mismatch rate, training share, OT exposure, deviation from recommended assignee).

Structurally this is a solid exam answer. As an auditable assignment policy for this queue, it is not close to flawless.
