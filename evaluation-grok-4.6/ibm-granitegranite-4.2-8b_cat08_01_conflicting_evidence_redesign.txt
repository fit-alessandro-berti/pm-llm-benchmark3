**5.2**

The write-up looks complete and data-heavy, but it is not a reliable executive recommendation. Several errors are large enough to change the diagnosis, and the three “redesigns” are not three different designs.

**Material factual errors.** The fast path is not “~26% of cases.” Auto eligibility → Fast approval is 2,550 of 5,000 (**51%**); Fast approval → Dispatch replacement is 2,300 (**46%**). 26% is the share of evidence-request cases with **repeat** requests, and that statistic appears to have been reused as path volume. Manual eligibility → Repair quote is **260** cases, not “2500.” Redesign A then adds Premium high-value 14% + Standard high-value 21% + **Standard low-risk 25%** and calls that “60% mid/high-value.” That is a segment misclassification, not a rounding issue. These mistakes sit in the diagnosis and in targeting, so later trade-off claims rest on a distorted case mix.

**Process-mining reasoning is sloppy in places that matter.** Auto screen → Fast approval (2,550) and Auto screen → Request missing evidence (650) are **alternative outgoing edges** that already add to 3,600. Treating that split as proof of “Fast approval before evidence completeness” confuses a directly-follows split with the alignment diagnostic. The conformance finding is real and should have been used on its own. Edge waiting times (Auto → Fast approval mean 1.0h) are also described as “Fast approval duration.” Observational complaint rates (14.1% on Fast approval vs 3.3% on repair) are treated as if routing every high-value case onto repair would produce the repair-path rate. That is selection, not a treatment effect; the experiment section later admits the distinction, but the redesign logic does not.

**The three redesigns are not materially different.** A, B, and C share the same core: evidence portal at intake, Fast approval reserved for low-risk accessories, devices/high-value defaulted to Repair quote. B is A with a stricter “always evidence-first” rule. C adds a gate but **applies it to low-risk Fast candidates**, while the prompt’s costly error is wrong replacement on **high-value** devices (EUR 410 vs EUR 95). C’s own targeting paragraph says the gate is for Premium/Standard high-value; the flow says the gate does **not** apply to the repair path those cases are sent to. That is an internal contradiction, not a third design. Rule R1 requires review **before** Fast approval for >EUR 400, not a ban on replacement. All three over-correct by abandoning a compliant, gated replacement path for high-value claims—the one place the contradiction (speed vs. complaints) most needed a hybrid, not a retreat to the slow path.

**Constraint violations.** The table invents “~5–10% / ~7–12% / ~3–8% cheaper” figures the data do not support. The prompt forbids pretending to calculate such values. Cycle-time cells mix current manual medians (96h / 120h) with speculative “clean repair” medians (50–80h) without saying which loops were removed. “Dominated? no” for the current process is defensible as Pareto language, but the surrounding fake precision undermines it.

**What still works.** Structure matches the required sections. The contradiction is stated in the right direction (fast path cheap/fast upfront, worse on complaints, rework, and R1/R2). Segment outcomes, the 31%→9% cost erosion, fitness 0.91 / 68% fitting traces, reviewer utilization >82%, and the unclear-defect 140h tail are used. Failure modes and the staged pilot are the strongest parts: bottleneck migration, portal abandonment, and path-specific complaint tracking are the right evaluation logic.

Those strengths do not offset wrong volumes, a duplicated redesign, a mis-aimed gate, causal overclaim, and invented cost deltas. Under a strict reading of evidence use and non-generic redesign, this is a mid-pack answer that would mislead a sponsor on how common the fast path is and on what is actually being changed.
