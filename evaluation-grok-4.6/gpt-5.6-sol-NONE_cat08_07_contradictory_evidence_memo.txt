**7.6**

A strong, decision-oriented memo that hits the required structure and avoids the forbidden moves (national scale of the full bundle; indiscriminate rollback). It is not close to flawless. Under a strict standard the defects below are enough to keep it well clear of the 9–10 band.

**What it does well**

The recommendation (E with a D-style learning agenda) is the most defensible reading of conflicting evidence. The memo is actually a decision, not a recap: scale the low-risk path, keep coordinator ownership only with safeguards, stop survey-skip and post-billing activation where they are doing harm. Metric conflicts are explained in causal language rather than restated. The VoI section distinguishes decision value from implementation sequence (M7/M2 start now despite mid/low rank), which is the right idea. Process-mining artifacts are used, not namedropped: low precision as an overly permissive exception system, alignments as specific control failures, DFG as a shift from survey to failure/rework edges, social network as centralization/key-person risk.

**Material analytical gaps**

The executive memo underweights the first-order identification problem. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline mix. That compositional bias affects every *overall* comparison (median, tails, FTR, complaints, cost, SLA). Segment tables remain the right basis for E, but the memo treats overall deterioration as a clean pilot effect and simple-house gains as fully demonstrated. It never tells the reader that the headline deltas are confounded by mix.

Worse for a recommendation that *depends on segmentation*: customer-segment and building-risk attributes are incomplete before week 3 of a six-week pilot. Roughly the first half of the evidence that justifies “scale only for simple low-risk houses” is weakly labeled. The table flags this as an assumption; the memo and the rollout logic do not. That is overconfidence dressed as synthesis.

**Fact / interpretation hygiene is not clean enough**

The evidence table is the right design and uses more than eight items, but several “facts” are compressed in ways that mislead:

- Apartment and old-building FTR changes are written as “8pp” and “11pp” with no minus. In a table whose job is to separate fact from spin, omitted signs are an accuracy error.
- The apartment fact line drops the median *improvement* (−2.0 days) and keeps only the tail/quality damage. That erases the within-segment median-vs-tail conflict the next section is supposed to explain.
- “Billing-before-successful-test is a frequent move-on-log” is a fact; “every such alignment deviation is operationally improper” is listed as an assumption, which is good — but the memo then treats the deviation as a hard stop without noting that post-billing activation was an *intentional* design choice in R, not only drift.

A few interpretation cells still smuggle design narrative as mechanism (“coordinator ownership simplified accountability”) without the prompt having isolated that cause.

**Smaller but real flaws**

Baseline model quality (fitness 0.87 / precision 0.79 / generalization 0.70) is never placed next to the pilot scores in the memo or in §3, so “fitness rose / precision fell” is asserted without the comparison a reader needs. Business and rural segments are almost unused despite being the prompt’s evidence that manual activation creates billing disputes and that fewer handovers can make rescheduling harder. NPS is ignored. Alternative C’s “reasonable when” column states implementation conditions, not a decision rule for choosing C over E. Combining E+D is intellectually fine, but E already includes measurement-before-scale; presenting them as a novel blend slightly inflates distinctiveness.

**VoI ranking is defensible, not tight**

M4/M6/M1 at the top is reasonable. Ranking M7 fifth is awkward: if billing-before-activation and missing permits are rollout-killers (as the threshold section later claims), their incidence is high-VoI, not merely a control to “start anyway.” The write-up papers over that tension instead of resolving it. M8 last is correct.

**Bottom line**

Strategically competent and better than a generic synthesis. It would be a high-8 or low-9 if the memo had treated case-mix and incomplete segment labels as first-class threats to E, and if the evidence table had not dropped signs and inconvenient segment facts. Those are not polish issues; they are the difference between a careful decision under uncertainty and a clean story that slightly overfits the segment table.
