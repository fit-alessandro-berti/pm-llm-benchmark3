The answer is unusually rigorous for this prompt: it refuses a good/bad default, treats East as a different baseline from North/South, refuses average cycle time as a success metric, and ties structure to measurable log artifacts. The remaining deductions are for real gaps, not style.

**What is strong.** The handover arithmetic is correct (343 vs 359 events per 100 cases; tech loops 67→77; approval 81→92) and the caveat that these are event counts, not case shares, is the right one. Catching that a 15% utilization lift can push Legal/Risk through 100%, that security is routed with no named capability, that single timestamps are not service time, and that summing edge medians is not cycle time, is expert-level. Censoring, origin-team freezing, contamination in a shared-specialist pilot, and “easy cases close first” are the right threats to inference. The recommendation (modify, phase, do not treat utilization as a KPI) matches the constraints.

**Section 1.** The table is a mechanism map, not a causal graph, but it covers the required nodes. Two nits: (1) the 4.7% figure is false precision given management estimates, possible overlapping categories, and omitted triage/QC edges the author themselves flags; (2) “simple technical average” 73.7% is the unweighted mean of 71/68/82. Pooled utilization is hours-weighted; case-weighted it is ~72.6%. The author labels this an illustration, so it is a slip, not a contradiction.

**Section 2.** Optimistic/central/adverse are coherent and East-aware. The required forecast axes are not treated equally. Handovers, queues, cycle-time shape, rework, role clarity, and fairness have rows; **learning** and **resilience** do not. They appear as conditions or warnings, which under-delivers the question’s request to forecast those effects. Management’s wait-time anchors (3.2h→4.0h tech, 2.4h→3.0h approval, 9.6h→10.5h legal) are barely used as quantitative scenario inputs. There is also no even rough queueing argument (utilization → nonlinear wait, Little’s Law, variability) despite having utilization and wait columns.

**Section 3.** Leading/lagging split is sound and the schema-enrichment list (queue lifecycle, frozen origin, SLA definition, max vs sum of `reassignment_count`) is necessary. Several “leading” items (skill retention, role spread, collaboration breadth) are not actually observable in days without extra instruments the log does not contain; the later lagging row partly corrects this, but the table over-claims speed of detection.

**Section 4.** Nine risks, all required themes present, plus a careful note on not “adjusting away” the treatment. Dense but not inaccurate. Exception-handling and unfinished-work points are the ones most answers miss.

**Section 5.** Phasing (tech pool first, keep the supervisor, delay Legal/Risk expansion, simulate full load) is the right design. Randomization with shared specialists is acknowledged as leaky. Weaknesses: 4–6 weeks of follow-up is tight against East’s 22.8d p90 even with the extension clause; guardrails (±10–15% p90, +2pp SLA/rework) are arbitrary despite the disclaimer; no sample-size or multiple-testing rule beyond a sentence; no explicit power/stopping plan for East’s smaller volume.

**Section 6.** Matches the evidence. Conditional expansion criteria are stated. The warning about what not to infer from early averages is the actual decision-relevant paragraph.

**Section 7.** Uses every named PM4Py family and, more importantly, says when those APIs cannot recover wait/utilization from one timestamp. Cohort-by-arrival rather than event-window is correct. Gaps under a strict reading of “compare before and after”: SNA objects are computed, not differenced; `handover_reason` and `local_expertise_flag` never enter the code; working-together/subcontracting/role discovery are interpreted in prose but not operationalized as metrics; `filter_event_attribute_values(..., level="case")` is version-sensitive and unexplained. The `explicit` rework indexer is unnecessarily awkward. Acceptable as a sketch, not as a complete comparison script.

**Net.** Near the ceiling on judgment and on not cheating with averages. Below the ceiling because learning/resilience are not first-class scenario outputs, queueing is qualitative only, a few numbers are over-precise, and the PM4Py block sets up comparison more than it performs it. Those are minor relative to the whole, but they are real.
