The answer is a competent, format-compliant case write-up with a genuinely evidence-based diagnosis and three structurally different interventions. It is not close to flawless. Under a hypercritical reading, a major design-logic error, several quantitative misuses of the log, and an incomplete experiment plan keep it well below the top of the scale.

**Diagnosis is the strongest section, but it is not as precise as it looks.** The central contradiction is stated clearly and tied to real artifacts: the Auto → Fast approval arc (2,550), Fast approval / Dispatch → complaint tails (72 h / 96 h means, p90 150 / 180), R1/R2 alignment deviations, 14.1 % vs 3.3 % reopen rates, and the 9 % net cost residual after credits. That meets the five-artifact bar. It still overstates the “2,550-case happy path.” Auto → Fast approval is 2,550, but Fast approval → Dispatch is only 2,300; another 350 skip to close and 140 already reopen, and Fraud/risk contributes another 240 fast approvals. Calling that one dominant cheap path blurs three different outcomes. Token replay (fitness 0.91, only 68 % fitting traces), R3 (reopens must pass Manual before close/denial), the temporal-profile finding that Manual → Repair quote is too slow for Premium, Quality check under-utilization, and high-value repair rework (12.5 % vs 4.0 %) are almost unused. R1 is repeatedly treated as identical to “high-value device,” which the log never states: R1 is a EUR 400 threshold, not a product-segment attribute.

**Redesign A is directionally right and operationally sloppy.** Segment-aware screening plus a pre-dispatch gate is a real, non-generic response to R1/R2 and wrong-part selection. Two problems undermine it. First, the flow is internally inconsistent: high-value and unclear cases are sent to Manual/Fraud “followed by” a pre-dispatch gate, while “repair paths remain unchanged.” After review, the as-is DFG already splits to Fast approval, Repair quote, or denial. The redesign never says which of those survives, so it is unclear whether high-value still gets replacement (gate makes sense) or is forced into repair (gate is mostly irrelevant). Second, load math is borrowed from the wrong lever. The prompt’s 15–35 % manual-inflow increase is for a two-week rule refresh. Explicitly pulling *all* high-value (35 %) and unclear (22 %) off the auto path is a much larger reroute onto seven reviewers already above the 82 % utilization cliff and two Fraud specialists who already fail on Mondays. The text treats those as the same shock. Cycle-time sacrifice is also understated: Premium high-value is 20 h median on the fast path vs 96 h on the reviewed/repair path. A 6–18 h gate is not the main delay if those cases actually leave the fast path.

**Redesign B is the cleanest of the three, with one material measurement error.** An evidence-portal-first design for the 140 h unclear segment, aimed at the 890-case Request-missing → Manual loop and the 26 % repeat-evidence statistic, is a different mechanism from A and uses the stated 30–45 % repeat-reduction lever. The volume claim “22 % + 26 %” is wrong: 26 % is a *conditional* rate among cases that already enter Request missing evidence, not a second population that can be added to the 22 % unclear share. Identification is also underspecified: unclear/missing evidence is discovered at screening today, not at Intake, so “portal immediately after Intake for all unclear cases” assumes an oracle the log does not provide. The table’s −40 to −70 h effect on a 140 h median is not supported. Cutting 30–45 % of *repeat* requests, which themselves occur in only 26 % of the evidence-entering subset, cannot be assumed to remove a third to half of that segment’s cycle time. Compliance is not “unchanged”: restricting Fast approval to complete evidence is an R2 control. That is a missed credit, not just a missed risk.

**Redesign C is the decisive reasoning failure.** Dual tracks are “materially different,” but the split contradicts the segment outcomes the diagnosis just cited. Premium high-value is the *worst* fast path (22 % reopen, high R1/R2 exposure, EUR 410 wrong-replacement cost). C *accelerates* Premium cases, including that segment, through Auto → Fast approval → Dispatch and then “protects” them with escalation-board priority. Escalation is a post-complaint, one-slot-per-day forum with poor scalability (120 cases in the log). Using it as a Premium perk treats the symptom and will congest the only recovery mechanism. Standard high-value is dumped onto Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair (120 h median) without touching the actual repair wastes: part-price waits (30 h mean to approval), 12.5 % high-value repair rework, and two underused inspectors. That is exactly the mistake the prompt forbids: treating the slow reviewed path as automatically better. The EUR 410 error cost is invoked for Standard high-value and ignored for Premium high-value, where it is the same number. Mid-risk devices disappear.

**The table performs the required comparison and then invents precision.** Several cells are not qualitative ranges grounded in the log. C’s “−10 pp overall complaints” is impossible on a 9.2 % base rate; even a generous incidence calculation (Standard high-value 21 % × an 18 % → 6 % drop) is about 2.5 pp at the population level, while Premium high-value is left on the 22 % path. A’s “+6–18 h high-value” ignores the 20 h vs 96 h path switch. A’s “+EUR 30–60” is unsupported arithmetic. “Dominated?” is used as a segment-fit tag (“conditional (unclear segment)”) rather than a Pareto statement, so the column does not do the work the heading promises. The table also never flags that exact dominance cannot be known from this evidence—an explicit constraint of the question.

**Failure modes are present but incomplete in a way that matters.** A’s gate queue and misclassification are fair; the more certain failure is the already-quantified reviewer utilization cliff plus the two-person Fraud bottleneck, which A feeds directly. B’s adoption/abandonment pair is the right risk. C lists technician overload and “perceived inequity” and omits the first-order failure: systematic R1/R2 breach and 22 % Premium high-value reopens by design. Bottleneck migration is named for each redesign, which is good; unused Quality check capacity is never treated as a destination or a slack resource.

**The experiment sequence is only half a plan.** Piloting A on Premium high-value is a defensible first cut at the highest *rate* problem, but Standard high-value produces more complaint *volume* (roughly 21 % × 18 % vs 14 % × 22 %), so the sequencing rationale is incomplete. Stop/go wording is broken: “violation rate drops 40 % and complaint rate 12 % with p90 cycle time 48 h” mixes relative and absolute thresholds and never says versus what baseline. Abandonment is a stop metric on a Premium high-value pilot where abandonment is not the failure mode. C is never piloted. The hold-out / 90-day all-in cost language is the one part that actually answers “how do we know we did not just shift cost downstream.”

**Net.** This is not a generic Lean laundry list, and it does synthesize conflicting DFG, conformance, and segment artifacts into three distinct bets with stated sacrifices. That is why it is not a low score. It is also not a high score: it mis-measures path volume and evidence-loop volume, mis-applies the 15–35 % inflow figure, over-claims cycle-time and complaint effects, and then proposes a third redesign that fast-tracks the segment the same answer just showed is the compliance and quality disaster. Those are not nits. They are process-mining reasoning errors.
