**6.0**

The write-up is structurally complete and operationally literate, but it is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading it overclaims empirical support, contains several audit-failing capacity errors, and ships a Python scaffold that does not meet the mandatory specification.

**What is actually strong.** Sections 1, 3, 5, 6, and 9 are the best parts. Observed vs assumed is clean. Uniform-within-range and Poisson-within-block are labeled as modeling choices, not facts. O1 (mean + SLA tail) and O2 (maximin P1 / peak utilization / fairness, with the prompt’s 90% base-utilization constraint) are the right pair of objectives, and the recommendation is not “pick pooling because base mean delay is lowest.” The three policies are specified as dispatch rules rather than slogans. The Deepa-only mechanical window after 16:00, the 18:00 mechanical coverage hole, Chen as the morning software single point of failure, and Validation becoming the downstream constraint after 16:00 are the correct bottleneck story. Secondary effects (SERPT gaming, P3 starvation, skill erosion, Validation flood, interpretability) are treated as first-class, and the pilot design actually protects P1 instead of proposing an unsafe full cutover.

**Section 2 does not survive audit.** Daily diagnostic loads for M/E/S are consistent with the type mix and mid-range means, but Validation is not: \(42 \times 15.74 = 661\), not 561. Intake capacity is given as 2919 base-minutes; converting available time by multiplier yields about 2367. Several 2-hour capacity cells are simply the wrong quantity: Electrical/Software “346” in 16:00–18:00 is Emil’s *daily* capacity, not the block. Mechanical 12:00–14:00 lists Chen as contributing when Chen cannot do Mechanical. U2 is described as doubling E/ES then renormalizing (which keeps block volume fixed and only lifts 14:00–16:00 electrical load from ~203 to ~241), then the text quotes 280–320 as if absolute E/ES demand had also grown. Block Validation loads implicitly use 15.74 while the daily total uses 561, so the section is internally inconsistent. The qualitative bottleneck ranking mostly survives; the numerical offered-load table does not.

**Section 4 presents a simulation it did not run.** The comparison table is the decision artifact, and it is labeled as coming from “100 replications” plus capacity analysis. Section 8 uses 20 replications, seeds with `hash(scenario)` (not stable), never executes a true event queue, and leaves preemption as `penalty=0`. The ranges are rhetorically convenient: B always wins mean, C always wins P1/fairness/resilience, A always fragments. That ranking is *plausible*, but presenting it as Monte Carlo output with ±15–25% error bars is not honest uncertainty handling. P2 SLAs near 100% in base are almost uninformative given a 8-hour target on a same-day process; the table treats them as discriminating evidence.

**Policy and modeling nits that matter.** Setup is defined on diagnostic skill-family switches; folding Intake/Validation into an “IO” family is an extra assumption that will mis-count setups. Policy A both disables preemption and calls Farah a “floater but not pooled.” Hybrid’s “~0.6 FTE idle reserve” is not derived. Parallel-vs-sequential uses ordinary means and ignores that the 8-minute coordination penalty is paid on the *case* critical path while sequential can use one cross-skilled person and avoid a second queue wait — the trade-off is discussed, but the threshold rule in the prose and the code do not implement the same test.

**Section 7 is mostly competent, then overreaches.** The required attributes are there, and the warning that `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is case-level rather than queue-level is exactly the distinction the prompt asked for. Invented or hand-waved calls (`pm4py.discover_bottlenecks`, `pm4py.algo.conformance` as if it were a function) weaken an otherwise good monitoring design.

**Section 8 fails the spec on its own terms.** Required: encode tables (yes); three policies (yes, as a greedy scaffold); explicit handover/setup/parallel/preemption (handover/setup/coord constants are explicit; preemption is not actually applied); comparable Base/U1–U5 metrics (printed if the run worked); interval log with the five required columns (yes); `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network` (the insert call is **absent**; handover is probed as if the object were a dict). `random.poissonvariate` does not exist, so arrival generation is broken or silently replaced by a rounded Gaussian. Cases are scheduled to completion in arrival order with `next_free` patching, so later P1s cannot interrupt, parallel branches are not true concurrent contention, and overtime/break logic is only approximately enforced. A scaffold is allowed if omissions are listed — they are listed — but a scaffold that does not call a mandated API, cannot sample Poisson demand, and is then used to underwrite Section 4’s table is not an acceptable substitute.

**Net.** This is a strong operator-facing design note with the right robustness framing and the wrong numerical hygiene. The errors are not cosmetic: they sit in offered load, shock magnitudes, and the evidence for the policy ranking. Strictly graded, that caps the work well below the 8–10 band.
