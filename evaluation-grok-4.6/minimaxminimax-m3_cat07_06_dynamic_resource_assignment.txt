**6.2**

The write-up is complete, on-format, and conceptually in the right family: multi-objective, hard vs soft rules, no single-optimum claim, and a usable worker explanation. It is not close to flawless. Several assignment and method errors are material.

**What holds up**

Hard/soft separation is clear, and the safety rules for Security (skill 3 or supervised 1–2), Approval (no finalize at skill 1), the 4-assignment cap, and high-complexity/high-handover continuity are stated correctly. Most of the queue is routed in line with those rules: C001 off R3, C002/C011 kept with the Approval/Negotiation specialist who already owns them, C007 kept after three reassignments, C016 kept despite a specialist alternative, C015 not given to Eva/Jonas at skill 0. The comparison section does argue trade-offs instead of declaring a winner. Section 5 is the strongest part: non-punitive, override-friendly, and actually explainable. The audit list covers every required indicator.

**Assignment errors (largest deduction)**

C006 is wrong on its own terms. Quality Check, normal, 36h remaining is explicitly batch-eligible, and C012 is the batch mate (Quality, low, 48h, owner R10, Quality = 3). The table’s claim of “no batch-mate at right skill today” is false. Keeping it on R9 (11 cases, 4.0h, overtime = high) also fights the overtime rule when a safer specialist is sitting on the natural batch.

C003 is justified as an “H6-style override.” H6 does not apply: complexity is 0.66, handover is medium. Continuity after two reassignments is a fair *preference*, not a hard override. Treating it as one hides a real alternative (R3, Diagnosis = 3, overtime low).

R6 is left with two same-day urgents (C002 in 2h, C011 in 4h) plus C010, and 6.5h remaining. Continuity explains each row; it does not make both finishable. That is exactly the exception path the policy claims to have, and it is not used.

C013 is a new, low, 72h case piled onto R3, who already owns C007 (high, 6h, complexity 0.81, three prior handovers) and is named as C009’s supervisor. That is a resilience and continuity own-goal. R10 (Eligibility = 2, 4 cases, 6.0h) is the obvious spare capacity.

The post-assignment load table adds “new” cases onto `Current open cases` as if owned queue items were not already in that count. If they are, the “after” column overstates load and the fairness story is sloppy. If they are not, the overtime/capacity story is sloppy the other way. Either reading needed an explicit convention.

Training lands at 1/9 and is confessed. Constraint 3 is soft, but Quality batching and the C010/C012 notes were unused levers, so “structural gap” over-explains a design choice.

**Policy and comparison gaps**

The scorer double-counts continuity (continuity term plus handover-cost term). Weights are asserted, not stress-tested. Supervision is a filter, not a scored object, so C009’s pair never competes with R3’s other work. First-available and specialist-only are compared only as archetypes. The question gave a 16-case snapshot; not even a compact baseline column for this queue makes Section 4 more rhetorical than evidentiary.

**Section 7 is a sketch with broken calls**

`filter_event_attribute_values` on exact complexities `{0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0}` misses every real value in the log. `filter_case_performance(..., None, None)` does not measure SLA misses against `sla_deadline`. `discover_performance_dfg(..., performance="waiting_time")` and `insert_case_service_waiting_time(..., timestart=...)` do not match PM4Py’s actual signatures. `discover_batches` is named in prose and then omitted from the code that should audit Rule 8. For a required PM4Py section, that is more than cosmetic.

**Net**

This is a competent policy memo with the right headings and the right instincts, undermined by a false batch claim, a mis-fired continuity rule, an unresolved dual-urgent clash on R6, a bad C013, fuzzy load accounting, and an inaccurate audit sketch. Under a nearly-flawless bar, that is a mid-6, not an 8+.
