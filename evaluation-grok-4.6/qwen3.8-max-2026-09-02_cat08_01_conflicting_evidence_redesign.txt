**7.2**

The answer is a strong, format-compliant process-mining redesign. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several design gaps, a sequencing contradiction, and a few conceptual slips keep it well below the top of the scale.

**What it does well**

The required seven-section structure is followed. Diagnosis uses far more than five artifacts, with specific counts and times: the frequency DFG (3,600 auto screen, 2,550 fast approval, 2,300 dispatch), performance DFG medians/p90s, fitness 0.91 vs 68% fitting traces, alignment deviations, low precision, temporal-profile notes, rework rates (14.1% vs 3.3%; 26% repeated evidence; 12.5% high-value repair rework), the 31%/9% cost-after-rework figure, segment outcomes, and resource constraints (82% utilization, two-week rule change, 15–35% manual inflow, 6–18 hour gate, €95/€410 replacement-error costs). The central contradiction is stated clearly and is not collapsed into “fast is bad, slow is good.”

The three redesigns are materially different *as layers*: eligibility/routing (A), evidence capture (B), fulfillment quality (C). They are process-specific rather than generic Lean/Six Sigma. Each states targets, mechanism, and what is left unresolved. The table is qualitative and correctly refuses exact ROI/Pareto claims. Failure modes and bottleneck migration are concrete. The experiment plan includes shadow scoring, segment metrics, stop/go ranges, and a post-close window so upstream speed is not mistaken for end-to-end gain.

**Issues that prevent a high score**

1. **Premium high-value is not actually balanced.** The prompt gives that segment strict service expectations, 20h vs 96h paths, and 22% vs 5% complaint rates. Redesign A largely dumps these claims onto the slow reviewed path. The table’s “unless expedited” is not designed in the flow: no parallel manual/fraud/quote, no Premium SLA queue, no reserved capacity. That violates the instruction not to treat the slow path as generally better and leaves a core speed–compliance–CX trade-off unresolved rather than designed.

2. **The answer contradicts its own capacity diagnosis.** It warns that tightening auto rules can raise manual inflow 15–35% and that queues grow sharply above 82% utilization, with only seven reviewers and two fraud specialists. The first *live* pilot is still A, before B has reduced evidence loops. Shadow mode measures inflow; it does not create capacity. Stop/go rules mitigate this on paper, but the recommended sequence still fires the known failure mode first.

3. **Alternatives vs. a stacked program are mixed.** The Pareto table treats A/B/C as mutually comparable options (with “conditional” dominance). Section 7 sequences them as complementary stages of one program. Combinations are never compared, so “dominated?” is not well-defined. That is a real comparison-logic flaw, not just a presentation choice.

4. **A overlaps B.** A already blocks dispatch until evidence completeness. B’s distinctive contribution is *how* evidence is collected. The “materially different” claim is still defensible, but A is doing part of B’s control job, which blurs the redesign contrast.

5. **Process-mining slip on precision.** Low precision of the *discovered* model means the model is too permissive relative to the log. Reducing allowed variants is a control objective for a *to-be* model; calling that a fix for discovered-model precision is slightly confused.

6. **Fraud and part-price bottlenecks are named more than designed.** A routes more work into an already constrained fraud team with little beyond acknowledgment (no sampling, campaign staffing, or Monday load design). C’s catalog for quote delay is better, but A’s high-value lane still inherits `Manual → Repair quote` p90 of 60 hours, the temporal deviation the diagnosis itself flags.

7. **Pilot design is optimistic.** A 4–8 week live A cohort plus a 30–60 day complaint window is a thin basis for movement from 18–22% toward 5–6% reopen, or for fitting-trace targets of 75–80%. Those ranges are allowed qualitatively, but they are presented as if they were tight operational gates.

8. **Small omissions.** Fast approval → close without dispatch (350 cases) is unused. Premium low-risk R2 exception is kept in A without the post-dispatch audit that would make the exception safe (fast-path complaint is still 6% vs 3%). Mid-risk devices disappear. These are not fatal, but they are gaps in an answer that otherwise claims completeness.

**Net**

Evidence use, segment distinction, and non-generic levers are well above average. The remaining problems are not typos: incomplete Premium high-value design, a sequence that ignores its own bottleneck math, and a comparison table that does not match the experiment logic. That is a solid 7, not a 9.
