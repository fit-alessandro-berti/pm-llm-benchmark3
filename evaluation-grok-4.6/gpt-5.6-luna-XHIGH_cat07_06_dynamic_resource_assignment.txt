**6.3**

The answer is well structured, complete against the required heading list, and stronger on socio-technical framing than most policy write-ups. It is not close to a high score. The policy text and the actual schedule contradict each other on the cases that matter most, the comparison with the named baselines is not actually computed, and the PM4Py sketch contains clear technical errors.

**Section 1 is the strongest part**, but it already overclaims rigor. Hard vs soft constraints are separated cleanly, and the refusal to treat the problem as a single optimum is correct. Several “hard” rules are then invented (access/SoD, a supervision budget, escalate-rather-than-violate) without being operationalized later. That would be acceptable if the later schedule obeyed the rules that *were* stated. It does not. Hard constraint 7 — do not take a forecast SLA miss when a safer eligible alternative exists — is abandoned in Section 3. Hours remaining are listed as capacity context and then treated as decoration.

**Section 2 looks complete and is not internally consistent.** All requested score terms appear, and batch-aware dispatch is the right idea. Problems:

- The weights are 35+25+20+15+10+10 = **115**. If the components are on a 0–1 scale, SLA is ~30%, not 35%. Presenting them as interpretable importance weights is sloppy.
- Prose says SLA is lexicographic; the pseudocode is a flat weighted sum. Those are different policies.
- The lexicographic sentence is also backwards: “an assignment that is forecast to **breach** an urgent deadline is **not rejected** in favor of a slightly better fairness or training score.” As written, that *protects* a breaching assignment. The intended rule is the opposite.
- Quality-check batching (constraint 8) is absent from the algorithm. So is a hard hours/capacity check. The 4-assignment cap is applied to primary ownership only after the fact.
- Tie-breaks start with “lower predicted breach risk” after the score already includes SLA, which double-counts and muddies explainability.
- “Whole-batch search would be better” is noted and then ignored on a 16-case instance small enough to inspect.

**Section 3 is where the answer fails as a demonstration of its own policy.**

`C001` is the decisive error. Urgent Security Review, 3h slack, complexity 0.88, notes “needs specialist,” owner `R3_Celine` at Security skill **1**. Historical evidence in the prompt: weak-skill continuity adds a median **3.3h** of service time and more rework. Safer alternatives exist: `R7_Gita` and `R8_Hugo` are skill 3. The answer’s own hard SLA rule forbids this assignment. Supervision by Gita does not erase a 3h deadline against a documented 3.3h penalty, and Gita already has 10 open cases, 4.5h left, and `C008`. Continuity is being used to put a non-specialist, whose training priority is *negotiation*, on an enterprise security task. That is the scenario’s trap, and the answer walks into it.

`R3_Celine` is then overloaded past feasibility. She receives `C001` (urgent, skill-1 security), `C003`, `C007` (high-priority rework, complexity 0.81, 6h slack, already 3 reassignments), `C014`, and supervision of `C009`, with **6.0 hours remaining**. `C001` and `C007` alone cannot both be finished today at those complexities. The schedule creates the specialist-style bottleneck it claims to avoid, just on Celine instead of Gita/Hugo.

`C014` is wrong *under the written scoring*. David is the owner, not Celine; continuity does not favor her. Hugo is Rework skill 3, low overtime, and at that dispatch point almost unused. Workload and resilience should have sent `C014` to Hugo (or another unloaded Rework-capable resource). Giving it to Celine shows the algorithm was not actually applied.

`C003` is at least labeled as a trade-off, but it is still poorly chosen. Reassignment count is already 2; the prompt says >2 reassignments bring 1.8× rework and 1.4× appeals. David is the current *specialist* owner. Avoiding his overtime is reasonable; making this Celine’s third/fourth case is not the only alternative. Hugo (Diagnosis 2) or Eva (Diagnosis 2) were less loaded.

Other assignment nits that should have been caught:

- Hours remaining are never used as a binding filter (`Eva` 5.0h with `C005` + `C016` both high-priority; Celine as above).
- `C012` is explicitly “suitable for trainee” and is given to a Quality specialist. Bruno’s Quality training is dropped entirely. The 20% target is then rescued by counting `C015` **skill-0 shadowing** as a training assignment. Observation-only by a person the matrix marks as “not allowed” is a generous reading of constraint 3.
- `C010` is described in the queue as a trainee-approval candidate and is not used for training.
- `C013` to Jonas is legal but unmotivated beyond “low load”; organizational-role evidence the toolkit is supposed to use is never consulted.
- Hugo (Security/Quality/Rework 3, Diagnosis 2, low OT, 5.5h) is left on a single normal Security case. That is a resilience own-goal.

Several other rows (`C002`, `C004`, `C005`, `C007` keep, `C008`, `C011`, `C006`/`C012` batching, `C016` continuity) are defensible. A mix of good local reasons does not salvage a globally infeasible Celine bundle or the `C001` SLA contradiction.

**Section 4 does not do the comparison the question asked for.** The seven-dimension table is directionally sensible and correctly refuses a single winner. It never instantiates first-available or specialist-only on C001–C016. That matters, because first-available would, for example, be at risk of sending urgent Approval (`C002`) to a skill-1 person with the fewest open cases (Jonas), and specialist-only would pile Security on Gita/Hugo. Without those concrete counter-assignments, the comparison is an essay, not an evaluation. Claims about “usually a balanced result” are unearned.

**Section 5 is good in tone** and uses real cases. It is not fully honest: it does not tell workers that Celine is carrying two time-critical complex cases plus extra diagnosis/rework and a coaching load. An explanation that hides the main fairness problem is only partly explainable.

**Section 6 is close to the required audit set** and is one of the better sections: decision-time snapshots, policy version, stratified mismatch vs recorded supervision, overtime exceptions, and “rework is association not blame” are all the right instincts. Remaining gaps: no explicit waiting-vs-service decomposition in the indicator definitions despite later citing those PM4Py calls; no indicator for *supervision load* even though the schedule depends on it; little on batching compliance for Quality.

**Section 7 name-drops the required PM4Py functions and then misuses them.**

- `filter_case_performance` filters **case duration**, not complexity. `complex_log` is therefore wrong.
- Log column `required_skill` is a skill *name* in the scenario; the code compares a numeric `observed_skill` to `required_skill_score` that is never derived.
- `training_share` groups a filtered boolean Series by an index taken from the *unfiltered* frame. That is not a correct 20% metric.
- Handover rate uses `diff().notna()` on resource IDs; the first event in every case becomes a false handover depending on how `diff` is handled, and alignment with the groupby key is fragile.
- Networks, roles, similarity, batches, service/waiting time, and performance DFGs are discovered and then not used to estimate service times, find mentors, detect specialist cut-vertices, or calibrate weights. The question allowed “learn or audit”; this is a thin audit wrapper plus comments.
- There is no replay of the six-component scorer against log snapshots, despite Section 2 depending on it.

**Net.** This reads like a strong policy memo that would fail a tabletop dry-run. The framework, worker language, and audit list are well above average. The actual assignment — the only part that can be checked against the snapshot — violates the answer’s own SLA rule, capacity limits, and scoring function, and the baseline comparison was not worked. Under a strict reading, that caps the grade in the low 6s.
