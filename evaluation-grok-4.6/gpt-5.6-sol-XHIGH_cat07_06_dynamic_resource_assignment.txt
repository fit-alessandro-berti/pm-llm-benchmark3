**6.6**

The answer is unusually complete and well written: it hits every required heading, separates hard rules from preferences, refuses a single optimum, and is strong on explainability, audit design, and PM4Py. Under a strict reading it is not nearly flawless. The main recommendation contradicts the policy the author just specified.

**Section 3 is the decisive failure.** The text treats capacity fit as a hard filter and cites the 3.3-hour weak-skill service penalty, then assigns **C001** to R3 (Security skill 1) as performer on an urgent 3-hour, complexity-0.88 enterprise review. Predicted slack is negative before supervision wait: 3h remaining minus a 3.3h skill penalty cannot meet the SLA. The author’s own exception path (“escalate if no feasible compliant option”) is not used. A specialist-led review with Celine briefing would honor continuity of *context* without making a skill-1 worker the performer on a case that “needs specialist.” Calling Hugo a “co-signer” also blurs accountability: if Hugo must effectively redo the review, Celine-as-performer is a fiction.

That choice then collides with **C007**. R3 already keeps a high-complexity rework due in 6 hours (correct under the 3-reassignment / high-handover rule) and is also given **C003** plus a **C009** checkpoint. Four commitments and 6.0 hours cannot absorb urgent complex Security *and* high-complexity Rework today. R5 is similarly oversubscribed: **C005** (high, 8h) and **C016** (high, 7h, complexity 0.79) against 5.0 hours. The hedge that “forecast service times fit the stated hours” is asserted, not shown.

**C003** is weakly justified even on the author’s terms. David already owns the case; keeping him is not a *new* assignment and the SLA is 18 hours, so overtime avoidance does not require a third reassignment on a diagnosis loop (1.8× rework / 1.4× appeal). Moving it onto an already overloaded specialist trades a soft overtime preference for a historically expensive handover.

**C010 / C015 training design is thin.** Two of the three “training” cases are skill-0 observation. The author correctly forbids skill-0 performance, but then leans on those observations to claim a 33% training share. If they do not count, the share is 1/9 and misses the 20% guardrail. Using scarce R8 (needed for C001) as a non-performing Approval observer is a resilience own-goal. **C012** is labeled “suitable for trainee” and is unused; R2’s quality priority is dropped solely for same-day overtime even though the SLA is 48 hours.

**Section 2 overclaims precision.** The weighted score is written as if operational, but SLA “combines” unspecified terms, continuity/training/resilience are qualitative, and **throughput is never defined** despite a 0.10 weight. There is no worked score for even one case and no visible batch optimizer, so the table reads as expert judgment retrofitted to a formula. Tie-breaks and a stability margin are described, then not applied transparently to C003/C006/C010/C014.

**Smaller issues.** The 0.70 complexity cutoff is arbitrary and untested against C003 (0.66) vs C016 (0.79). First-available is order-dependent; that is never stated, and baseline *assignments* are not produced, only tendencies. Worker text is the right tone but does not explain the contestable C003 move to the people it affects. PM4Py coverage is relevant and cautiously caveated (lifecycle timestamps, versioned skills, roles vs reason-string hacks), but “learn” is mostly “audit,” `audit_assignment` is a stub, and `log` is used as a DataFrame without pinning the API.

**What remains strong:** hard vs soft constraints; refusal of a unique optimum; overtime resources get no new work; Security/Approval rules are mostly applied; 11/15 owners retained; first-available correctly flagged as unsafe without a compliance wrapper; specialist-only correctly shown to pile Diagnosis onto R3 past the cap; audit indicators (including stability, calibration, and “not a league table”) are better than the assignment table; worker explanation is non-punitive and challengeable.

The piece is a high-quality policy essay with a broken schedule. That is not a 9. It is a carefully argued design that fails its own SLA model, capacity rule, and exception handler on the most urgent case.
