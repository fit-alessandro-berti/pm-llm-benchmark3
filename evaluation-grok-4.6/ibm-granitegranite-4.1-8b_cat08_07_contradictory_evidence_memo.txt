5.7

The memo is structurally complete and lands in the right decision family, but it is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading it has material inconsistencies, at least one category error, a technically shaky process-mining explanation, and a value-of-information ranking that contradicts its own rationale.

**What works.** The author refuses national scale (A) and full rollback (B), treats the result as segment-heterogeneous rather than uniformly good or bad, and recommends a hybrid path with guardrails. The five metric-conflict writeups are directionally correct: median vs tail, visible vs downstream cost, and simple-house vs apartment/old-building effects are tied to the right mechanisms. Alternatives A–E are all scored, and the final package (keep coordinator ownership where it helped, stop the two most damaging rule changes, measure before broader scale) is defensible.

**Internal contradictions in the decision itself.** Section 1 tells management to scale Redesign R for simple houses with “survey skipping disabled,” then to suspend skip-survey and post-billing activation “for all other segments.” Section 6 disables those two changes only “for all other cases,” which implies the skip rule stays on for simple houses. That is not a wording quibble: it is two different operating models. The writeup also labels the choice as E while operationalizing something closer to C+E, then immediately scales a “safe” segment nationally while standing up experiments in new regions. That contaminates identification, ignores that segment/risk attributes were incomplete before week 3, and ignores that the pilot mix is not nationally representative (more apartments and older buildings). Scaling even the beneficiary slice nationwide is stronger than the measurement base supports.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are not cleanly separated.** The table meets the eight-item minimum, but it is thin on the artifacts the prompt required. Fitness/precision, alignments, DFG edge shifts, temporal-profile deviations, and the handover vs working-together networks barely appear as facts. Item 6 drops the “+” from “3+ handovers.” Item 13 is misclassified: sparse start timestamps are a stated data fact, not an assumption. Item 9 mixes a fact (0.94 / 0.51) with a causal gloss. Several important facts never enter the table at all: SLA 13%→16%, NPS +21→+17, coordinator utilization 93%, survey-team utilization 78%→52%, second-visit utilization 66%→92%, cost-data cutoff after week 4, and the specific alignments (billing before activation, missing permit checks, complaint after billing).

**Process-mining explanation is imprecise.** High fitness and low precision are not well described as “the model captures ad-hoc variations broadly” and therefore “cannot distinguish them.” Fitness is replay of the log; low precision means the model permits much unobserved behavior. The prompt already gives the right reading: a simplified coordinator path is common, so fitness rises, but the model also allows too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start. Alignments and DFG changes are used lightly, if at all, in the conflict section.

**Value-of-information ranking is the clearest analytical failure.** M1 is ranked 1 and M6 is ranked 6, then the rationale says M1 and M6 “directly test the predictive rule” and that resolving that uncertainty is “crucial.” That is self-contradictory. M6 is also cheaper and faster than a randomized skip trial and should sit near the top, not below start-timestamp instrumentation. M7 is ranked 7 even though billing-before-activation and missing permit checks are the live control failures that decide whether post-billing activation can ever be scaled. M4 (full 60-day cost) is more decision-flipping than M2, given that visible cost already improved while the incomplete fully-loaded estimate worsened. The ranking therefore does not follow from the uncertainties that would actually change A–E.

**Thresholds and prose are sloppy.** “Median tail improvement,” “downstream complaint cost baseline,” and “coordinator utilization 85%” are missing comparators; as written they are not operational stop/go rules. Parity on p90/p95 in every high-risk segment is an extreme bar that is neither justified nor consistent with accepting FTR ≥80% (already below baseline 82%). Hyphens and operators are stripped throughout (“firsttimeright,” “skipsurvey,” “6days,” “34%12%,” “+21+17”), which in places changes meaning. Grammar errors (“while suspends”) and the memo/action mismatch on which measurements launch now (M1–M6 vs a different subset) add avoidable unclarity.

**Net.** Competent consulting structure and a basically right directional call, undermined by an inconsistent operating recommendation, a mis-ranked VoI section, incomplete use of the process-mining evidence, and repeated precision failures. That is a mid-pack memo, not a high-score one.
