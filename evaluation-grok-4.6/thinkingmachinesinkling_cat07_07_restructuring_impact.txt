**6.8**

The write-up hits the required skeleton and is substantively better than a generic “shared services good/bad” essay: it treats East differently from North/South, refuses to treat mean cycle time as the decision variable, keeps fairness/learning/resilience in view, and does not green-light a full rollout. That is the floor for a passing answer on this prompt. It is not close to flawless.

**What is actually strong.** The mechanism table correctly recasts “nominal handover reduction” as relocation of edges from short, high-context local loops to longer central queues, and it ties that to concrete log fields and PM4Py objects. The three scenarios are internally consistent and use the supplied baseline numbers as anchors rather than inventing a new process. Hidden-risk section 4 is the best part: tail vs median, complexity-weighted harm, deskilling, accountability diffusion, and absence-day fragility are the right failure modes. The recommendation is appropriately conditional and lists inferences management must not draw from early averages.

**Material analytical flaw: the pilot does not test the intervention.** Merging `N_Tech`, `S_Tech`, and `E_Tech` into one pool is the proposal. Running “full shared service” only in East while North stays fully embedded and South keeps `S_Tech` is a *different* design. You cannot observe the capacity, queueing, or utilization effects of the actual merge. Worse, East is the wrong site for the main stated risk. The text itself says North/South hold the local-expertise rents and East is already overloaded; putting the only full treatment on East systematically under-samples expertise loss and over-samples “relief of a saturated local specialist.” Hybrid South is a partial fix, not a substitute for a design that can identify the North/South effect (e.g., within-team randomization of routing, or a time-boxed shared pool that still includes North/South cases). Difference-in-differences is never specified despite non-equivalent baselines (820/760/520 cases, different tails, different utilization).

**PM4Py sketch is only partly real.** Coverage of the listed APIs is broad, but several calls are fabricated or mis-specified: `get_rework_cases_per_activity(..., rework_flag_key=...)` is not how that function works (it infers rework from repeated activities, it does not consume `rework_flag`); `filter_case_attribute_values(..., filter_type='gte')` is not in the given API set; SLA misses are invented as `outcome` tokens (`sla_missed`) instead of being derived from timestamps against a threshold; `insert_case_service_waiting_time` / `get_service_time` are used with hand-wavy signatures; `forecast_comparison` returns stubs. `filter_variants` and `handover_reason` are barely used. For a section whose job is to show you can operationalize the forecast in PM4Py, this is a real deduction, not a nit.

**Smaller but real problems.** “Rework” is not a first-class mechanism row even though the prompt listed it as required. Several scenario numbers (loops 31→8, waits 3.0–3.5h, “working-together density >70%”) are false-precision decorations, not derived. Case-level filters on event attributes like `team` will mis-handle cross-team cases. The mechanism “map” is a flat action table, not a causal graph with feedback (lost informal advice → more TSS referrals → longer queues → more clarification loops → more rework). Bot_Intake / Bot_Eligibility and already-maxed Legal/Risk (93%/91%) are under-used relative to how much they constrain the +15% utilization story.

**Net.** Strong exam-style structure and the right normative stance (pilot, monitor tails/fairness/roles, do not trust averages). Not a 8+ piece because the pilot cannot identify the effects the rest of the memo says matter, and the PM4Py section would not run as written.
