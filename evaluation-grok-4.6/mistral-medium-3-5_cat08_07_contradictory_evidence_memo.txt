**5.4**

The recommendation family (partial scale, not A or B) is the only part that is clearly aligned with the decision constraints. The rest of the memo is too sloppy, internally inconsistent, and technically wrong to score much higher.

**Material factual errors.** Segment effects are repeatedly stated as levels, not changes, and the signs are dropped. Simple houses did not achieve a “median cycle time [of] 6 days” or “complaints 1pp”; the table shows **−6.0 days** and **−1pp**. Old buildings did not see “p90 cycle time 25 days” and “first-time-right 11pp”; they saw **+25.0 days** and **−11pp**. Repeating this in the executive memo and the evidence table is disqualifying in a decision document. A reader would take the wrong magnitudes.

**Alternative C is inverted.** The prompt defines C as scaling coordinator ownership **and suspending** survey skipping and post-billing activation. The memo treats C as leaving those mechanisms in place and lists that as C’s main risk. That is not a nuance error; it is a mis-specification of the decision set. Because of it, the A–E comparison cannot be trusted, and the later blend of “E plus coordinators everywhere” is never acknowledged as a C/E hybrid.

**Process-mining concepts are confused.** High fitness / low precision is explained as the model allowing “non-conformant but observed” paths and “unrealistic paths not seen in the baseline.” That mixes three different things: discovered-model precision (extra *unobserved* behavior), fitness (replay of the log), and alignments to a normative model (move-on-log / move-on-model). The pilot’s **high fitness, low precision** is overgeneralization of combinations the miner cannot constrain. Billing-before-activation and missing permits are **conformance** findings, not a definition of precision. This is a core artifact the prompt required to be handled correctly.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions leak.** The table exists, but several “facts” are misstated deltas, and at least two “assumptions” are already in the prompt as findings (segment note: “Skip rule often wrong”; segment label: “Simple house, low building risk”). Data-coverage limits that should have been first-class facts—biased pilot mix (more apartments/older buildings), incomplete risk attributes before week 3, complaint costs cut after week 4, sparse start timestamps—are barely used. Without them, baseline-vs-pilot aggregates are treated as cleaner causal evidence than they are.

**Internal contradictions.** The memo launches **M1, M3, M4, M7**; the VOI ranking puts **M6 first** and M1 fifth. Section 6 then scales coordinator ownership to **all** cases at an invented 80% utilization cap, which is not E as defined and makes M5 more decision-relevant than the last-place ranking implies. Thresholds (80% accuracy, 85%, EUR 190, EUR 185, 5%, 2%) are arbitrary, sometimes self-contradictory, and several comparison operators are missing, so the stop/go rules are not even parseable.

**Conflicts section is incomplete, not just uneven.** Median-vs-tail and segment split are directionally right, but the memo underplays mix shift as an alternative explanation for a fatter aggregate tail. Handover-vs-resilience and visible-vs-downstream cost are acceptable, then weakened by garbled symbols (“Handovers (34% 12%)”, “EUR 184 161”) and sloppy VOI language (“downstream costs > EUR 161 savings”). DFG shares, temporal-profile edges, resource reallocation (survey 78%→52% vs second-visit 66%→92%), complaint-type shift, and generalization 0.56 are mostly unused.

The skeleton of a good memo is there: E-type caution, no national scale, no blanket rollback, all six required sections. That is not enough. An executive memo that misquotes the segment results, misdefines a formal alternative, and fumbles fitness/precision is not near-flawless; it is a B-minus draft with a sensible headline.
