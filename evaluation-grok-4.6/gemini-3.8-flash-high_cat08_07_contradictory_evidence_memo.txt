**6.4**

The memo chooses the right family of action (hybrid, not full scale or full rollback) and covers every required section, but it is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading of the brief, several material defects should pull the score well below the top band.

**Format and discipline.** The executive memo is decision-oriented, but it likely exceeds the 700-word cap (roughly 750–800 words) and spends too much of that budget on rhetoric (“failure spiral,” “catastrophic,” “accounting illusion,” “existential”). Alternative E is also rewritten into a much larger package (billing hard-lock, 3-person pods, 48–72 hour OSS/BSS change, surveyor redeployment). That is synthesis, but it is not the option as specified.

**Facts vs. interpretation.** The evidence table is the strongest part of the answer: 12 items, three-column split, and most log-backed numbers are accurate. The memo and later sections undo that discipline. Billing-before-activation is treated as *illegal*, a *regulatory* exposure, and a source of *fines*. The prompt does not say that. It describes a designed exception (“activation checks moved after billing start if the network signal looked acceptable”) plus customer and alignment effects. Calling it non-compliance-like is fair; calling it unlawful is an invention. Other smuggled “facts” include a 1980 building cutoff, “two-way optical” telemetry, risers/heritage conduits, and “a second truck roll costs 3 to 4 times more than a field survey.” Table item 2 also misstates fitness: high fitness is not evidence of spaghetti; low precision is. The team’s own interpretation (simplified coordinator path plus an overly permissive combination of skips/rework/billing) is better than the table’s version.

**Small but real inaccuracies.** The diagnostic section pairs “failed visits (up to 19%)” with “manual activations (up to 19%).” Manual activation is 15% of cases; 19% is the DFG edge share. Coordinator utilization is 93% in *two* regions, not a global saturation figure. Apartment median duration *improved* (−2 days); only the tail, FTR, and complaints worsened. “Catastrophic” is not a description of that pattern. Kingman’s formula is stated incorrectly (\(W_q \approx \rho/(1-\rho)\), missing service time and variability) and then treated as the mechanism for the p95 of 62 days.

**Metric-conflict explanations.** Sections on fitness/precision, median vs tail, handovers vs resilience, visible vs loaded cost, and house vs apartment/old-building split are directionally right and use the required artifacts (fitness/precision, alignments, DFG, temporal profile, resource/social networks, segments). They would be stronger if they stayed inside the log. Queueing at 93% utilization is a *plausible* contributor to failed-visit → second-visit delay, not a proven law for the whole cycle-time tail. The 3–4× cost ratio and the claim that missing post-week-4 costs “will” fully negate savings are assumptions in the explanation, not resolutions.

**Decision quality.** Rejecting unconstrained national R and rejecting blunt rollback is correct and matches the constraints. Evaluating A–E with conditions is good. Two strategic problems remain. First, the answer labels E “Optimal” and says it “maximizes risk-adjusted ROI,” which violates “do not pretend one intervention dominates.” Second, it scales merged intake and coordinator ownership *nationally* immediately, despite a 6-week, four-region pilot, incomplete segment/risk attributes before week 3, a biased apartment/old-building mix, sparse start timestamps, and a drop in generalization (0.70 → 0.56). The stated Alternative E includes measurement *before wider scale*. Immediate national scale of the “good” slice overweights a confounded 34% pilot share. Rural (11%) and business (8%) deterioration is mostly ignored. Pausing second visits that lack reason codes, while those codes do not yet exist, would likely worsen the tail the memo is trying to fix. The 48–72 hour billing-lock / pod / capacity package is operational fiction; the table itself flags reassignment and absence-rate uncertainty.

**Value of information.** Rankings are written in the right *shape* (uncertainty, pivot, threshold) but the order is conceptually wrong. M7 is put first, yet alignments and the DFG already show billing-before-activation and missing permit checks as frequent. Further monitoring is operational control, not high decision VOI. The uncertainties that could still *change* E vs C vs later expansion of skipping are M6/M1 (is the skip rule wrong, and for which strata?), M4 (is fully loaded cost actually worse?), and M3 (why visits fail). M5 is ranked as a test of pod vs single ownership, while the memo already implements 3-person pods in 48 hours — an internal contradiction. Thresholds (0.0% premature billing, ≤3% false negatives, EUR 35, 75–82% utilization) are false precision.

**Net.** This is a competent executive package with the correct headline recommendation and a genuinely useful evidence table, undermined by overclaim, invented mechanisms and legal conclusions, a VOI ranking that confuses control with decision value, and a rollout pace the data cannot support. Not a weak answer; far from a nearly flawless one.
