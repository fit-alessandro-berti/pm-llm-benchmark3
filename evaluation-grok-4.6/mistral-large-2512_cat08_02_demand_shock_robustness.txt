**4.5**

The answer is complete in outline and sounds operationally serious, but it repeatedly misreads the process, invents causal chains the log does not support, and then builds policies on those errors. Under a strict process-mining standard that is a mid/low score, not a near-miss.

**Scenarios are only partly valid.** The four required disruption families are present, and the mix is not just “more volume.” That is the strongest part of the paper. Almost everything else in Section 1 is loose. Weekly volume is internally inconsistent: normal throughput is \(18400/8 = 2300\) cases/week and observed peak is \(9900/3 = 3300\) (\(\approx 1.43\times\)), yet the multipliers and the stated weekly counts do not match (e.g., “1.8× = 3300”, “2.2× = 4000”). Scenario D treats a *proposed* digital registration system (I8) as if it were current infrastructure that can outage. Scenario A stacks mix shift, arrival compression, and a 20% recollection shock without separating which stress is doing the work. “M1 at 120% with 30% higher failure” during an M2 outage is also poorly grounded: M2 is the high-failure machine.

**Section 2 fails the actual assignment.** The judge asked for bottleneck *migration*: if this station is relieved, which downstream or upstream station overloads, using DFG frequencies, performance edges, variants, and queues. The answer instead lists four invented bottleneck sequences with made-up queue hours.

The worst error is process-order confusion. Registration precedes sample receipt. Courier compression and courier delay hit courier intake, Receive sample, and Pre-analytical check (`Receive → Pre-check` 2h/9h; courier peak queue 7.5h). They do not create an 8–12h registration-clerk queue. That mistake appears in A and D and then contaminates policies and the dashboard.

Other causal breaks:
- In B, an M2 outage constrains *assay*. Throughput into technical and medical validation should fall, not rise. Repeats consume scarce assay slots; they do not “increase volume from M1” into validation. A registration backlog does not follow from a machine outage.
- In C, specialist absence inflates `Hold → Medical validation` (already 18h/48h). It does not create M2 repeats or assay fragmentation.
- Sequences often run *downstream then back upstream* with no WIP/blocking mechanism. Relieving one station is almost never analyzed.

Critical evidence is unused. Technical validation is the only station above 100% utilization (101%). Specialist review has the worst peak queue (36h). Peak already shows `Assay → Repeat` rising from ~6.7% to ~14.8%, recollection from ~4.2% to ~8.0%, `Tech val → Medical val` from ~25% to ~42%, `Tech val → Hold` from ~1.8% to ~7.6%, and Urgent batch-wait bypass falling from 74% to 58%. Accession correction is two people at a 14h peak queue. None of this is used as a migration engine. The 60h recollection loop and 26h `Repeat → Assay` edge are essentially ignored.

**The “robust” package has a policy that defeats the Urgent SLA.** Time-capping Urgent priority at 12 hours, then handing priority to Complex-panel, is incompatible with an 18-hour contractual limit. The 30% assay WIP cap is also in tension with a peak mix that is already 29% Urgent (35% in A), especially since Urgent work is less batchable and therefore consumes a disproportionate share of machine slots. Claiming “++ / meets 18h” in the table is inconsistent with the rule itself.

Conflict hints are mishandled. I4’s stated risk is starving technical validation (already 101%); the table lists the next bottleneck as accession correction—the opposite direction. I8’s stated risk is exposing pre-analytical (96%) and assay (98%); the write-up only says registration may return if digital intake fails. I3’s M2-repeat risk is mentioned, then softened with unspecified “predictive maintenance.” I6 is the right family of intervention, but “image analysis,” a physician portal, and a new coordinator are extra capacity/tech, and a 40% rework cut is an invented number. Recollection is only one rework source; repeats and accession corrections remain.

**Scenario policies contain hard logic bugs.** An M2 *outage* cannot be triggered by M2 utilization \(>110\%\); a down machine sits at 0%. “Route all Routine to M1” during an M2 outage is not a policy, it is the only remaining machine. Increasing batch size to 24 on that remaining machine is exactly the I2 failure mode for Urgent cases. “Hold Routine releases” (I9) does not relieve specialist review; release is not the scarce resource, and Routine p90 is already 120h against a 72h target. Outsourcing is 30% in Section 3 and 50% in Section 4. Several triggers sit on top of *already observed* peak values (Urgent ~29%, medical-val queue 22h, rework 11.8%, repeat ~15%), so they would chatter through any historical peak rather than mark a regime change.

**The table and dashboard look complete and are not trustworthy.** Rework polarity flips (+ sometimes means “more repeats,” -- sometimes means “fewer”). The combined row is promotional: I1, I3, and I9 all tax Routine, yet the bundle is scored Routine “+” and rework “--”. The dashboard is not minimum, over-weights registration, and omits the hottest current stations (technical validation 101%, accession correction 14h). Repeat \(>10\%\) and batch-wait \(>12h\) would be permanently red in the provided peak log, so they are not useful leading switches. Mix share, arrival concentration, and accession-correction queue are the obvious missing leading signals.

**What remains defensible:** format compliance; refusal to optimize only the historical mean; an attempt (poorly parameterized) not to prioritize every case type at once; I6 and I7 as genuinely cross-scenario levers; residual risks that include redesign-induced failures; and a leading/lagging split. Those are real, but they sit on top of a misread DFG and several policies that would breach the Urgent contract or fire on the wrong station. That is why the score is not in the 6–8 band.
