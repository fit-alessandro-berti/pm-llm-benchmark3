**7.3**

The answer is a strong, well-structured sponsor brief that hits the required format, cites many of the right artifacts, keeps the three redesigns mechanistically distinct, and mostly respects the “no fake ROI / no universal slow path” constraints. It is not close to flawless. Several analytical slips would matter to an executive, and a strict process-mining reading exposes gaps the write-up papers over.

**What it gets right.** Diagnosis names the real contradiction: the modal path (Auto screen → Fast approval 2550 → Dispatch 2300) is fast (median approval 0.4h) and looks 31% cheaper, but complaint/reopen is 14.1% vs 3.3% on the repair route and the cost edge shrinks to 9% after rework. It uses conformance (fitness 0.91, 68% fitting traces; Fast approval before evidence; dispatch without risk review; reopen after close), the evidence loop (890 / 34h mean / 88h p90 / 26% repeat / 320 abandons / 140h unclear-defect median), segment splits (Premium high-value 22% vs 5%; low-risk 8h vs 46h), and resource constraints (7 reviewers, 82% queue cliff, 15–35% inflow, 6–18h gate, portal 30–45%). It correctly refuses to shove low-risk accessories onto the manual path. A/B/C are actually different (entry gating + dispatch gate; evidence-first sequence; reopen as a first-class process). The table stays qualitative. The experiment plan looks for displacement, not just faster averages.

**Substantive design error in A.** R1 requires Manual or Fraud/risk *before* Fast approval; it does not forbid replacement after that review. The answer’s flow does still allow replacement after review plus a gate, but the surrounding argument keeps borrowing “reviewed/repair” outcomes (5% complaints, 96h median) as if that were A’s implied destination. Cycle-time claims then disagree with each other: sacrifice text says “same-day replacement,” the table says “+6–18h,” and segment evidence says the reviewed/repair path is 96h. None of those is a clean estimate of “mandatory review + optional evidence + 6–18h gate” on a path that is already 20h/28h median. The main delay in the slow path is Repair quote → Customer approval (mean 30h, p90 80h) and part-price confirmation—not the gate. That bottleneck is almost unused, even though A, if it works, will push more high-value volume toward quote/repair capacity (3 technicians).

**Factual slippage.** The 24h service-credit trigger is stated for *Premium low-risk accessories*. The answer applies it to Premium generally / high-value. Premium high-value is already at 96h on the reviewed path; the stated risk there is R1/R2, not a 24h credit clock. Small, but it warps A’s trade-off story.

**Overclaim on the fast path.** After rework the fast path is *still 9% cheaper*. Calling it “not actually the most efficient” or a blanket “trap” oversells the data. The correct claim—which the answer sometimes makes and sometimes blurs—is that the advantage is segment-conditional and does not survive compliance/CX for high-value devices.

**Process-mining reasoning is thinner than the citation density suggests.** No token/edge conservation. Ends are 3015+220+390=3625 against 5000 starts. Complaint in 510 / out 430. Fast approval → Quality/compliance close (350) is a large, undiagnosed path. Almost all denials (210/220) come from Fraud/risk; that path is not redesigned, only avoided. **Other deducts.** Stop/go numbers are invented and internally inconsistent (“at least one-third” vs “25–30%”). Calling the current process “dominated” is stronger than the prompt allows. R2’s Premium low-risk *exception* (dispatch without complete evidence) is clear in B and muddy in A. Mid-risk is a one-liner. This is competent, evidence-heavy redesign advice with a real architectural idea in each option. It is not tight enough on the normative rules, the time arithmetic of A, or unused DFG structure to score in the high 8s or above under a strict bar.
