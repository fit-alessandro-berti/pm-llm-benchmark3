**7.3**

The answer is a strong operations redesign: it uses the DFGs and queues as evidence, treats mix and disruption (not volume) as the stress, refuses average-cycle-time optimization, and almost always names the next station a change will overload. Under a strict reading it is not near-flawless. Several headline numbers and one core control rule are wrong, and those errors matter.

**Factual errors**

The S1 claim that weekly volume is “+80%” is false. Normal throughput is \(18400/8 = 2300\) cases/week; peak is \(9900/3 = 3300\) (+43.5%). That slip is then contradicted by the author’s own P1 trigger (1.4×). For a piece that otherwise computes rates carefully, a wrong opening stress magnitude is a serious miss.

“2-deep specialist” in S1 mixes up two pools. The table’s two people are accession-correction specialists. Specialist review is a different, apparently thinner resource (“one specialist unavailable every second Friday”). Later the same answer calls it “the single specialist.” The identity of the scarcest validation resource is not stable.

Accession “throughput *falls*” in peak is also wrong. Successful correction→prep is \(430/8 \approx 54\)/week normally vs \(310/3 \approx 103\)/week in peak. What rises is the *escalation yield* (\(70/500=14\%\) → \(110/420=26\%\)). Yield and throughput are not interchangeable; the migration story still works, but the mechanism is misstated.

Routine p90 does not uniquely explode versus Complex: \(68\to120\) (1.76×) vs \(118\to210\) (1.78×). “Routine only doubles” is rhetoric, not evidence.

**Design logic flaws**

The 40h Routine age-escalation in R3 is miscalibrated. Peak Routine median cycle time is already 62h, and the peak path to preparation alone is roughly \(8+9+13=30\)h median. A large share of Routine work would already be “aged” when it hits the reserved-slot dispatcher, so the reservation would be eaten by Routine in the scenario it is meant to protect. That is not a nit; it can invert the policy.

R3’s 20% Complex slot share treats cases as unit load. The brief says Complex-panel orders need multiple assays. Slot share ≠ capacity share; Complex is under-reserved at the hard machine constraint.

R4 cites the 74% / 56% technical-validation→Release branch as the autoverification candidate set. That branch is *after* technical validation and measures skip of *medical* validation. The 101% station is technical validation. Auto-release of simple Routine results can still be justified, but the cited DFG edge does not identify those candidates. The evidence is attached to the wrong hop.

P7’s “exclude Complex already >48h old” is likely self-defeating. P7 only turns on when the specialist queue exceeds 24h; many of the cases that triggered it will already be older than 48h. The rule can refuse the work that needed the partner.

**Smaller unclarities**

P1’s “weekly arrivals … for 3 consecutive days” is not an operational definition. R1’s “~13h” to defect discovery is not reconstructed from the given edges (\(9+7=16\)h, or the 9.5h pre-analytical queue). R1’s line that Routine “tolerates the current delay worst” is backwards relative to slack (Urgent 18h vs Routine 72h); a 60h recollection destroys Urgent first. Sixteen “leading” indicators violate the request for a *minimum* / *few* daily signals. An extra framing section sits above the mandated seven headings.

S5 never gets its own bottleneck sequence in §2, despite being named as the design case. The author later admits the scenarios were analysed mostly one-at-a-time.

**What still holds**

WIP non-clearing (\(9900\) in vs \(8040+540\) out), the pre-analytical defect-rate jump (\(7.0\%\to12.3\%\)), the repeat-assay jump (\(6.7\%\to14.8\%\)), the validation-tail reshape (\(74\%\to56\%\) direct release; specialist hold \(1.8\%\to8.4\%\)), cancellation bias on cycle-time denominators, I1→reserved capacity, I3 inverted under M2 degradation, I8 gated behind correction capacity, rate-limited recovery after outage, and the redesign-caused vulnerabilities (autoverify as silent quality failure; shared people correlating two pools; policy hangover) are all the right kind of process reasoning. Those strengths are why this is not a mid-pack answer. The grade is held down because a strict judge cannot treat a wrong volume shock, a mixed-up resource, and a self-collapsing priority rule as minor.
