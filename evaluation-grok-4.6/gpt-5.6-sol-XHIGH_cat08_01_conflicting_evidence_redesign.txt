**8.1**

This is a strong, artifact-grounded answer. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several material gaps, a few overclaims, and one sequencing contradiction keep it well below a top-band score.

**What it does well.** The diagnosis is the best part. It treats the frequency DFG as edge counts rather than a single variant, uses the performance DFG to show that complaint latency sits *after* first close, and states the core contradiction clearly: the dominant `Auto eligibility → Fast approval → Dispatch` corridor is genuinely fast and cheap on first pass, then only 9% cheaper after rework, with 14.1% reopen versus 3.3% on the completed repair path. Fitness 0.91 versus 68% fitting traces, alignment moves against R1–R3, low precision, temporal-profile deviations, segment tables, and the 82% reviewer threshold are all used as evidence rather than decoration. The three redesigns are actually different mechanisms (routing control, evidence-system redesign, high-value work cell), not parameter tweaks. Trade-offs are explicit. The experiment plan’s insistence on episode-level outcomes and refusing a noncompliant control is the right scientific instinct.

**Diagnosis gaps.** Mid-risk devices are named in the process context and then almost disappear. That is a real hole: the whole argument is “don’t treat all claims alike,” yet a stated product class never gets a lane, a quality rule, or a best-fit cell. Several listed edges are also left unused. `Fast approval → Quality/compliance close` (350) is a first-class path that skips dispatch; it is never diagnosed (credit, software, or silent close?). `Customer complaint/reopen → Escalation board` (120) is a plausible R3 bypass and is only handled implicitly. Incoming/outgoing DFG counts do not balance on dispatch (2300+65 in, 2320+260 out) or repair quote (570 in, 540 out); a careful miner would flag incompleteness instead of treating the listed graph as a closed system. Fraud/risk is described as a capacity bottleneck, not as a control problem: the answer never says *when* the 400 auto-screen and 360 manual handoffs to fraud are the right control versus waste.

**Redesign A is the obvious policy-enforcement design, and it overclaims.** Segment-aware STP plus hard stops on R1–R3 is the first idea anyone should have. That is not a defect by itself, but the mechanism section is too confident: it “should preserve the 8-hour Premium low-risk experience” and “should improve both fitting-trace percentage and model precision.” Those are hopes. Mis-coded value/segment data, which the answer itself lists as a failure mode, would erase both effects. The high-value lane still allows Fast approval *after* review, so it does not touch the wrong-SKU mechanism that the temporal profile ties to dispatch→complaint. Calling review “selective concentration of scarce capacity” while citing a 15–35% inflow increase is internally tense: that is not obviously selective. Mid-risk and the 350 skip-dispatch cases have no rule. “Both reviews only where separate triggers justify both” is unspecified, so the design is not implementable as written.

**Redesign B is the cleanest of the three, with one conceptual leak.** The target (22% unclear, 26% repeat evidence, 780/890 ping-pong, 34h/88h p90) matches the artifact. Collapsing many reviewer loops into one manifest, reason-coding a second request, and keeping an assisted channel are process changes, not slogans. The leak: “complete” evidence is treated as if it were a compliance state that mostly solves R2. The answer admits files can be valid and still useless, then continues to sell a recorded evidence-complete event as auditor-ready lineage. That overstates what a portal proves. It also leaves auto-decision logic, part selection, repair rework, and quote-price wait untouched; as a *standalone* balance of speed/cost/quality/compliance/CX it is incomplete, which the table acknowledges but the section title does not.

**Redesign C is directionally right and operationally thin.** A case-owned cell, catalog pricing, serial/BOM check, and a *targeted* 6–18h gate on EUR 410 errors is the correct reading of the high-value artifacts. The volume math is not done. Premium+Standard high-value is 35% of 5,000 cases. The dedicated cell is drawn from 2 fraud specialists, 3 quote technicians, and 2 inspectors. “Higher direct labor” and “dedicated capacity may be difficult” is not a design; it is a shrug. Without a capacity envelope (how many cases per owner per week, what is shed to the old path, what happens in a campaign week when inspectors are not “underused”), C is a target operating model, not a process redesign. “Repair is selected where diagnosis and economics support it” is also underspecified relative to the 12.5% high-value rework rate—the feedback loop is named, the decision rule is not.

**Complementarity is a feature and a dodge.** Ending by composing A+B+C is analytically honest, but the question asked for three redesigns that each *balance* the five outcomes. A, B, and C are layers. None is a full alternative process. That makes the Pareto table less of a choice set than it pretends to be: you cannot really ask whether A dominates C if the recommended end-state is “all three.”

**Table.** Qualitative ranges and refusal of false precision are correct. “Dominated?” does not stay in `{yes, no, conditional}`: A is “No clear dominance,” current is a paragraph. Compliance for A/C is “Low by design” / “Low,” which is stronger than the failure-modes section supports. Cost effects for A and C are too even-handed given that the prompt gives no implementation cost, no hours per review, and no error-rate-by-rule.

**Failure modes** are specific and mostly good (master-data bypass, 82% queue break, digital exclusion as denominator bias, gate that burns 12–18h). Four-to-five modes per design is thorough. What is missing is interaction risk: Stage 0 + A + C all consume the same 7 reviewers and 2 fraud specialists. The bottleneck discussion is per-design, not joint.

**The experiment sequence has a real logical flaw.** Stage 0 implements the R1–R3 hard stops immediately—“should not be randomized.” That *is* the queue shock Redesign A later treats as its main risk (15–35% more manual inflow, utilization over 82%). The plan then pilots B first *in order to free capacity before A*. Those two statements do not coexist: the non-negotiable Stage 0 already fires the A-shock, and B is only a Standard-family pilot, not a system that can absorb a global hard-stop. “Post-containment baseline” papers over this. Other nits: 20–30% relative complaint cuts and 1–2pp abandonment caps are invented precision; 30 days is barely longer than the 180-hour complaint p90 plus 31–42 hours of reopen handling, so late reopens will still be censored; shadow-mode for A is right, but “near zero” alignment moves as a go criterion ignores that Stage 0 already forced that outcome, so A’s incremental value becomes hard to see.

**Strictness summary.** Evidence use and PM literacy are high. The answer is not generic Lean. It does not worship the slow path. The misses that matter are: mid-risk ignored, Stage 0 vs B sequencing contradiction, A overclaiming a routing engine as a quality fix, C with no capacity design, and three complementary layers sold as a Pareto choice set. That is a very good executive memo with several design holes, not a near-flawless synthesis.
