The final write-up is a strong, identifier-grounded comparison that follows the required structure, does not invent events, and does not treat longest traces as correctness. The event-copy counts (Order 42, Item 24, Package 12, Delivery 6, Invoice 7, Payment 4), the reconstructed traces, and the required distortion categories are mostly accurate. Decision B is argued with real criteria, a real counterargument, and change conditions. That is not enough for a high score under a strict reading.

The most serious factual error is in the Package row: the text claims shared package events are **not** duplicated across packages. They are. `e16` and `e24` are directly related to both `P2` and `P3` because `D2` carries both packages. Package flattening therefore copies one dispatch and one confirmation into two package cases. That is exactly the highlighted business fact about consolidated delivery, and the matrix plus the distortion list miss it. The Package “convergence” cell discusses only `P3` absorbing two orders, which is a different phenomenon.

Divergence is defined and then applied inconsistently. Section 1 treats divergence as fragmentation, truncation, *and* splitting. The Order row then rates divergence “Medium” for `O1` being split across `P1`/`P2`/`D1`/`D2` while admitting those events remain in one `O1` case. The self-loops from repeated `Pack`/`Dispatch`/`Confirm` on `O1` are the better divergence example, but they are also parked under convergence/performance distortion. Classic OCEL convergence (one event, many same-type objects) is handled better than divergence.

Several smaller problems compound this. DFG edges are written without arrows (`Pack Package  Pack Package`, `e11e12`), which makes the matrix and §4 harder to read than the analysis deserves. Saying `O1`’s inclusion of `e16` “may implicate `O2`/`O3` fulfillment” overstates the contamination: `e16` belongs on `O1` via `I12`/`P2`; the main distortion is tripling `D2` across orders, not importing foreign work into `O1`. The lead-object argument contrasts Order’s “moderate duplication” with other types’ “severe truncation,” mixing two different failure modes. Payment is a true singleton lifecycle, not a near-singleton. The Item “duplication” cell reports 24/31, a coverage ratio below 1, under a column about duplication. The code outline meets the checklist and usefully computes copy ratios, but it imports `Counter` and never uses it.

The result is a coherent, mostly careful portfolio argument with one clear error on a required business fact and enough conceptual/presentation slack that it is not close to flawless.
