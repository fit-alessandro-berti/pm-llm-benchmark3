**7.4**

The answer is complete, well-structured, and conceptually strong: it obeys control-flow and four-eyes rules, treats K-337 as a decision-policy change rather than a pure time cut, and repeatedly distinguishes earlier readiness from earlier completion. Those are the right instincts. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, quantitative care and scale analysis are materially weaker than the qualitative reasoning.

**What is actually good**

Format compliance is high. The partial order is not forced into one sequence; K-104 is treated as preparation-plus-timing, K-219 as routing, K-337 as policy, K-411 as concurrency/logging. Counterfactual paths stay close to the factual traces. Compliance still follows the latest assessment; A1/C1 four-eyes is preserved; originals still gate document check; preliminary work is not allowed to replace final review. Queue snapshots are treated as forecasts. Best/base/worst ranges and reverse-evidence paragraphs are present. The ranking is not by cycle time alone, which is the correct emphasis: K-411 first, K-337 last despite the largest speed gain. The 100-case writeup hits the required interference themes (C1 reservation, D1/D2 substitution, rework, assessment bottleneck, weekend/external delay, timestamps vs true concurrency).

**Concrete inaccuracies**

The K-104 hour math does not add up. The derivation says C1 starts Wednesday 08:00, finishes around midday, and that this saves 8–10 hours versus factual notify at 18:40. Midday versus 18:40 is about 6–7 hours, not 8–10. Even with a 2–3 hour shorter C1 service, notify around 11:00 is still ~7.5 hours. The table repeats the inflated 8–10 hour best case. That is a real temporal inconsistency, not a rounding nit.

Operating hours are under-modeled. D1 works overnight on K-104 (21:40–05:40) but waits until Tuesday morning on K-219 despite originals at 17:30. The answer waves this into “Monday evening or Tuesday morning” without using the snapshots: Monday 16:00 D2 queue is 5 hours, so a continuous-work reading implies a start near 21:00, while a shift-boundary reading does not. That uncertainty is first-order for K-219 and is not pinned down.

The 100-case analysis stays qualitative where an operations answer should do resource-hour arithmetic. One C1 cannot absorb 100 reserved high-risk reviews (roughly 300–600 service hours versus one person-week). One D2 cannot absorb 100 handwritten checks (roughly 400–800 hours). The text says queues “grow” and calendars “fragment” but never shows infeasibility. For a capacity question, that is a substantial gap.

**Smaller logical and clarity issues**

K-104’s Wednesday 08:00 reserved slot is assumed rather than argued against the snapshot (“two urgent compliance cases added,” C1 still 8 hours at Wednesday 08:00). A reserved slot that jumps those cases is exactly the priority inversion the answer names, yet the target-case clock still assumes the jump.

S1 is treated as essentially unconstrained locally; at 100 packets it is another single-person bottleneck, mentioned only in passing.

Time units are mixed (elapsed hours vs business days), which blurs comparison. K-337’s “3–4 business days” best case counts a weekend as if it were business time.

K-219’s assumption that “D2 can start soon after originals arrive” sits in tension with D2’s 5-hour Monday-afternoon queue. The derivation is more careful than the table.

The K-104 over K-219 ranking is defensible but not tightly argued. K-104 loads the unique certified reviewer; that spillover is more systemic than D2’s probabilistic handwriting rework. Section 7 correctly calls the C1 slot least transportable, which slightly undercuts ranking it second.

Pilot thresholds (2 h, 2%, 20%) are arbitrary. For K-411, powering a 2–4 hour cycle-time test is optimistic if the base effect is 0–3 hours and assessment-queue variance is high.

**Net**

This is a good counterfactual writeup with the right feasibility and interference ideas, and almost no process-rule violations. It is not a 9+ answer: the headline K-104 savings contradict its own clock times, and the required scale analysis never does the capacity math that would make interference quantitative. Those are enough, under a strict bar, to keep it well below “nearly flawless.”
