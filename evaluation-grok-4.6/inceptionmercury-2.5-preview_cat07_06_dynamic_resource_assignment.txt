## Section 1 — Objectives and constraints

The split between objectives, hard constraints, and soft constraints is the right idea, but it is sloppy in ways that later cause bad assignments.

- SLA protection, quality, fairness, continuity, and training are listed, but **hours remaining / feasible same-day completion is not an objective or a constraint**, despite being in the resource table and in the first-available baseline. That omission is fatal in Section 3 (Farid, David).
- Training is parked as a *soft* preference. The scenario states a 20% quota “if SLA risk remains acceptable”—a conditional constraint, not a vague preference. Quality-check batching (rule 8) is also missing from the constraint list.
- Security supervision is under-specified. The scenario requires supervision **by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo**, not “a Skill 3 monitor.”
- Continuity is written as a hard “do not break” rule. The scenario is a preference with a skill-0/1 exception. Over-hardening it would forbid C001; they then break it anyway without reconciling the wording.
- “High-priority cases require skill ≥ 2 (or supervised)” is correct in spirit, but the algorithm later treats supervision as an afterthought rather than an eligibility gate.

This section is competent scaffolding, not a precise constraint system.

## Section 2 — Assignment policy

The bullet list *names* every required component, then implements them incorrectly.

**SLA urgency scoring does not rank resources.**  
`1 / (SLA_remaining_hours + 1)` is a property of the case. For a given case it is identical for every candidate, so it cannot choose an assignee. There is also **no case ordering** (urgent first). Without that, specialists can be consumed by easier work before C001/C002/C011 are placed.

**Weights are arbitrary, incommensurable, and contradicted by the table.**  
Skill is `×10` (0–30), continuity `+15`, training `+20`, workload `-5 × open_cases`. David’s load term is `-70` before any positive score. Under this formula he would almost never win a contested case—yet he is given C003 and C014. Training `+20` can beat a specialist. Resilience `+5` is negligible next to load. None of this is normalized, justified, or stress-tested.

**Hours remaining are unused.** Eligibility does not check whether the work can fit in the day. Workload uses open-case *count*, not remaining hours or overtime band.

**Eligibility is incomplete.** “Score > 0” is right, then Security/Approval rules are restated, then high-overtime resources are excluded for non-urgent work—**and Section 3 ignores that exclusion repeatedly.** Supervision is a post-hoc check, not part of the feasible set (supervised pair vs solo specialist).

**Tie-break / training override is a hole.** “If the training quota is not on, override selection for normal/low cases” can smash SLA, overtime, and continuity with no conflict-resolution order. Exception handling is one sentence.

The algorithm is a checklist of names, not an executable policy. A worker or auditor could not replay it and recover the table in Section 3.

## Section 4 — Comparison with baselines

The question forbids claiming a single obvious optimum and asks for trade-off reasoning. The table makes the proposed policy **win or tie on all seven metrics**. That is exactly the claim that was prohibited.

Two baseline characterizations are **inverted**:

- First-available (lowest open cases, skill ≠ 0) is a **load-balancing** rule. Calling its fairness “Low (overloads fast workers)” is wrong unless “fast” means something not in the spec.
- Specialist-only **concentrates** Security/Approval/Quality on a few people. Calling its fairness “High (distributes expertise)” is wrong; its actual failure mode is specialist queues, which the historical notes already state.

Other problems: no worked baseline assignments for C001–C016; first-available SLA risk is not “Low” once mismatch-driven rework is admitted; specialist-only bottlenecks are not only R7/R8 (Farid/Celine/Iris are also magnets); resilience of first-available is arguably higher because work is spread. The comparison is a scorecard for the authors’ policy, not an analysis.

## Section 6 — Process-mining audit indicators

All ten names are present. Several definitions would audit the **wrong thing**.

- **Handover frequency:** “count of `case:concept:name` changes between tasks” is false. That field is the case ID. Handover is a change of `org:resource` on successive events. `discover_handover_of_work_network` is the right function attached to the wrong sentence.
- **Skill mismatch:** “`required_skill` < assigned resource skill” is backwards **and** type-wrong (`required_skill` is a skill *name*). Mismatch is resource skill below the rule for that activity.
- **`filter_case_performance`** is a duration filter, not a way to read `reassignment_count` or rework/appeals.
- **`filter_event_attribute_values`** does not compute workload variance or OT exposure; it subsets events.
- **Rework/appeal** collapsed onto `reassignment_count > 2`, which the historical notes treat as a *predictor*, not the outcome. Appeals are not in the indicator logic.
- **Deviations:** `discover_performance_dfg` does not test policy rules.
- Listed relevant APIs largely unused: `discover_working_together_network`, `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity`, `discover_organizational_roles`, `discover_batches`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_variants`, SNA viz.

An audit layer this imprecise cannot support the “explainable and auditable” claim.

It is a stub, not an implementation sketch.
