**8.0**

The memo is a strong, constraint-compliant synthesis: it unbundles Redesign R, refuses both national scale and blanket rollback, and recommends a segment-gated hybrid with immediate containment of the skip rule, post-billing activation, and missing permit checks. Numbers, DFG edges, alignments (move-on-log vs move-on-model), model quality (fitness/precision/generalization), social-network contrast, utilization shift, and segment table are used accurately and in the service of a decision.

What keeps it from a higher score under strict grading:

1. **Implementation contradiction.** Scaling coordinator ownership while cutting utilization from 93% to an unexplained 85% cap is not operationalized (hire, throttle, or both). That is a real hole in a decision memo, not a stylistic nit.
2. **Blunt thresholds.** Section 6 copies overall baseline levels (FTR ≥ 82%, p90 ≤ 31 days, etc.) onto every segment without noting that segment baselines are unavailable, that simple houses already beat those bars, and that “zero” conformance is brittle.
3. **One fact/interpretation slip.** Billing → complaint as a new long-tail relation is a stated temporal-profile finding, but it is placed in the interpretation column.
4. **Under-examined heterogeneity in the “keep” bundle.** Simple houses improved on FTR and complaints; suspending deferred activation for that segment is conservative but not argued. Business and rural mechanisms are described, then folded into a generic “don’t extend” bucket.
5. **Minor overclaim.** “A third of volume” for simple houses is the *pilot* mix; the memo later notes the pilot over-represents apartments, so the national beneficiary share may be larger.

VoI ranking (M6 → M3 → M4 → M1 → M2 → M7 → M5 → M8) is well justified, including treating M7 as a control rather than new information and sequencing M1 after M6. Metric-conflict explanations are coherent and technically sound. The grade is held down by the capacity/threshold gaps and a few discipline slips, not by a wrong strategic call.
