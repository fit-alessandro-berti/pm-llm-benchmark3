**7.8**

The headline diagnoses are correct and well sequenced: April is a schema/pipeline break, June is a routing-mix change with calibration lag rather than concept failure, July–September stack process-performance, conditional-outcome, and (from September) population effects, and October is not a comparable performance month. That is the core of the rubric, and the answer does not collapse ten months into one drift label.

It still falls short of a high score under a strict reading.

**Prediction-time semantics are blurred.** The model scores cases *immediately after diagnosis*. Realized `Order Part → Part Received` time is then a downstream process outcome, not a legitimate diagnosis-time covariate. Treating “parts wait” as a shifting input (“if in the model”) and recommending **realized** wait as a retrain feature mixes a valid *diagnostic* residual test with feature leakage. Expected supplier lead time, parts-required, or a contemporaneous queue/SLA signal would be admissible; realized wait would not, unless the prediction point itself is moved. Test H’s claim that omitting wait should drop within-V3 AUROC is therefore operationally misleading. This is the largest conceptual fault.

**Falsification is only half-delivered.** The task asked to separate observation from hypothesis and state what would *falsify* each explanation. The table’s “contradicting or insufficient evidence” column is related, and Section 3 mostly supplies the tests, but Section 1 rarely states a crisp killer result (e.g., “April pipeline is false if repaired prefixes still show AUROC ~0.67 at 100% maturity”). That is a specified requirement, not a nicety.

**The diagnosis table is sloppy where an audit must be exact.** Arrows and contrasts collapsed into unreadable tokens (`35%52%`, `0.180.15`, `0.350.46`, `Order Part Part Received`). A reader who does not already know the source table can misread the evidence. Covariate-drift months are also drawn too widely (June through October), which dulls the contrast the rest of the memo works hard to create.

**The Python sketch matches the requested API surface but is not an audit-grade extract.** `variants.items()[:5]` is not the top variants unless sorted by count. `discover_performance_dfg` is treated as if `.get(edge)` were the *median* seconds used in the case; pm4py’s default performance DFG is not that object. Grouping events by calendar month, then discovering a performance DFG, silently truncates waits that *span* months—the exact pattern of the July–October parts shock. The sketch also never emits V1/V2/V3 shares, the main process-mix statistic in the brief. The calendar vs case-start note is the right idea; the implementation does not protect the inference it is meant to support.

**Smaller diagnostic nits.** April’s jump in mean predicted risk (0.28 vs 0.19) is used as pipeline evidence without a mechanism for *why* broken prefixes bias scores up rather than toward the prior. May is called residual pipeline while ECE remains ~2× baseline; that may be right, but it is asserted more cleanly than the residual metrics justify. June’s over-prediction is well explained; the same table could have been tighter that June is a *negative* control for concept drift on V1/V2.

**What remains strong, and why this is not a mid-range score.** Sections 2, 4, and 6 are disciplined. October’s 0.16 observed breach is never treated as recovery. Alert gating (DQ and maturity suppress retrain) is the correct control architecture. Safe/unsafe training months, stratum-first calibration, and the six failure modes (including June mix-retrain and false comfort from censored October) are proportionate. Those pieces are why the grade stays in the high 7s rather than the mid-6s.

A near-ceiling answer would keep this timeline, fix prediction-time feature legality, put explicit falsifiers in the table, restore readable contrasts, and make the monthly extract report median edge times, sorted variant mass, and V1/V2/V3 shares without breaking cross-month waits.
