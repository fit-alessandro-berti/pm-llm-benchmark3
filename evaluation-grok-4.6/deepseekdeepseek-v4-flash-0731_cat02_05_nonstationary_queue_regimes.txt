**6.4**

The write-up is competent, well structured, and arithmetically careful, but it is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading it has several real analytical mistakes, several places where it overstates what the aggregates can support, and some required ideas that are only half-done.

**What is actually strong.** The eight required headings are present and used as specified. The Python block is self-contained, rebuilds both tables, computes regime rates, two dispersion measures, lag correlations, a transparent CUSUM heuristic, and \(L/\mu\) only for named windows, and it correctly refuses to treat the daily table as a PM4Py log. Most headline rates check out (baseline weekdays \(8.35/8.30\) per hour, high-mix weekdays \(9.50/8.38\), intervention weekdays \(9.58/10.08\), pooled \(7.50/7.17\), Fano factors \(2.96/3.79/9.28/1.18\)). Outage recovery is not sold as a new steady high-throughput regime. Little’s Law is not applied to median service time, and the applicability matrix is appropriately harsh.

**Material errors and overclaims.**

1. **Wrong serial-dependence story.** Lag-1 autocorrelation of daily arrivals is not negative “because high weekdays are followed by low weekends.” Most lag-1 pairs are weekday-to-weekday and stay high; only Fri–Sat and Sun–Mon are large opposite-sign jumps. The raw lag-1 correlation should be clearly positive. The weekend cycle belongs at lag 7 or in day-type residuals. This is a concrete process-mining / time-series mistake, not a wording slip, and the prose never even reports the numbers the code would print.

2. **Missed stock–flow inconsistency.** Over 28 days, arrivals exceed completions by \(2519-2409=110\), yet mean WIP starts near 18 and ends near 19. That is a first-order threat to any WIP or Little’s Law claim (incomplete census, uncounted exits, or definition mismatch). It is not in the data-audit threats.

3. **“Simpson-like reversal” is mislabeled.** Averaging an outage day with a recovery day is mixture bias, not a Simpson reversal. The required comparison — an association that flips when weekday/weekend, mix, or intervention subgroups are pooled — is never constructed.

4. **Two-day regimes are treated like estimated operating points.** Baseline weekends, post-outage weekends, high-mix weekends, and intervention weekends are \(n=2\). The text still reports point rates to two decimals and qualitative “pressure” labels, after the task explicitly asked for ranges when means are unsupported. Capacity pressure is defined as \(\mathrm{WIP}/\mathrm{FTE}\) and then never computed.

5. **Change-point narrative does not match the heuristic.** The code is a single CUSUM on `complex_pct`. That can justify a break near 20 Apr; it cannot justify the claimed second break on 29 Apr, which is a staffing change while complex% stays in the low-to-mid 40s. Apr 16 already had 7.0 FTE, so “+1 FTE at the intervention” is also over-identified.

6. **Case-mix is over-read.** The prompt says the complex% jump may be real mix, a coding change, or both. The primary table still gives “High for mix change” and then builds the high-mix regime as if the variant shift were established.

**Smaller but real gaps.** `peak_08_10_pct` is almost unused despite being the only systematic intraday feature. The Apr 21 completion vector is much smoother than arrivals and is not used. Hourly Fano is reported; daily overdispersion after day-type/regime adjustment is not, even though that is the interesting overdispersion question. Opening-hours vs calendar-time conventions for Little’s Law are flagged, then the matrix still writes \(L/\mu\) in opening-hour units without a clear units/boundary paragraph. The “risky pooled policy” (weekend 2.5 → 3.0 FTE) is weak: May 2–3 already ran 3.0 FTE with WIP similar to the prior high-mix weekend at 2.5. A sharper risk is staffing to the pooled 7.17 completions/hour or treating 16 Apr’s 10.75/h as sustainable capacity.

The piece is a solid B-level analysis with clean arithmetic and the right Little’s Law instincts. It is not nearly flawless, and the lag-1 error plus the ignored +110 stock–flow gap are enough to keep it well below the top of the scale.
