**6.7**

The answer is well-structured, segment-aware, and clearly grounded in the DFG, performance DFG, conformance rules, rework statistics, and resource constraints. It does not collapse into generic Lean advice, does not optimize only average cycle time, and does not fake exact ROI. Those are real strengths. Under a strict reading, they are not enough, because several core claims treat distinct paths as if they were the same, and that error then contaminates the comparison table and the experiment thresholds.

**1. Current-process diagnosis**

The diagnosis is the strongest section. It correctly reconstructs the dominant happy path (`Intake → Auto eligibility → Fast approval → Dispatch → Close`), quotes the 31% / 9% pre- vs post-rework cost gap, and uses the reopen split (14.1% on Fast approval vs 3.3% on `Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair device`). It also uses fitness 0.91 vs 68% fitting traces, R1–R3, alignment deviations, repeated evidence requests (26%), and the 140-hour unclear-evidence segment.

Issues:
- Fitness and precision are smeared together. Low precision of the *discovered* model is not what the 68% fitting-trace statistic measures; that statistic is about replay against the *normative* net. The sentence that “implies” policy-illegal auto/risk/repair combinations attributes a precision diagnosis to a fitness result.
- p90 is described as “up to ~180 hours.” The p90 *is* 180 hours; 10% of cases are longer.
- The temporal-profile finding that `Manual eligibility review → Repair quote` is too slow for Premium is not used in the diagnosis, even though it is one of the few Premium-specific mining artifacts.
- Mid-risk devices are named in the process context and then ignored for the rest of the answer.
- “Auto-screen underused” later in the table contradicts the diagnosis itself: auto-screen is the high-volume path (3,600 / 5,000). The underused resource is Quality check.

**2–4. The three redesigns**

They are materially different in intent: A = segment-aware gating of the existing replacement machine; B = repair-first for high-value; C = evidence/reopen cell. That contrast is real. The execution is not clean.

**Redesign A overclaims path substitution.** A still allows Fast approval and dispatch after review, plus a 6–18 hour pre-dispatch gate. That is *not* the observed “reviewed/repair” mix whose median is 96–120 hours and whose complaint rate is 5–6%. Those figures belong to a mix that includes `Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair device` (customer-approval wait mean 30h / p90 80h, then repair, then quality check). A’s own flow should land closer to “fast path plus review plus gate,” on the order of tens of hours, not the full repair median. The answer states both: the sacrifice paragraph says 96–120 hours; the table says “tens of hours.” That internal contradiction is the largest analytical flaw.

The quality claim is the same error in another form. Routing high-value cases through review + a part-check does not, on this evidence, inherit the 5–6% complaint rate of the repair path. The 3.3% / 5–6% figures are tied to the repair sequence, not to “any case that touched Manual or Fraud.” Fraud already feeds Fast approval today (240 cases). A therefore promises repair-path quality without taking the repair path.

A also bundles the evidence portal (C’s lever) and the pre-dispatch gate (shared with B). It is less a distinct operating model than a portfolio of all available interventions, which weakens the “materially different” requirement.

Minor invention: “good history” as a fast-lane criterion is not in the data.

**Redesign B is more coherent, but underweights capacity math.** Sending Premium and Standard high-value devices (35% of volume, ~1,750 cases) through Fraud/risk (2 specialists, already peaked on Mondays/campaigns) and then into Repair quote is a much larger load shift than the prose admits. Repair-quote delay is already “mostly waiting for part-price confirmation,” so adding technicians does not obviously remove the wait that dominates that edge. B would also aggravate the unused temporal-profile warning: `Manual/Fraud → Repair quote` is already too slow for Premium. The answer flags cycle-time sacrifice and later names the part-price wait as a migrated bottleneck, but the *mechanism of improvement* still talks as if labor hours are the scarce factor.

The cost argument is appropriately hedged (“potentially lower”), which is correct given the global 9% residual fast-path advantage and the lack of segment-level cost.

**Redesign C is the clearest independent design**, and it is the only one that takes R3 and the 140-hour evidence segment as first-class objects. Two problems:
- The portal evidence only supports a 30–45% cut in *repeated* requests, plus a risk of more abandonment. C converts that into a hard rule: subsequent `Request missing evidence` events are “disallowed.” That overfits a capability estimate into a control that can produce worse eligibility decisions or hidden denials. That failure mode is not listed.
- C explicitly leaves the fast-vs-repair contradiction unresolved. That is an honest sacrifice, but it means C is a supporting system, not a full alternative operating model. The table’s “never strictly best” note is therefore doing a lot of work.

**5. Comparison table**

Required columns are present; “all Conditional” is consistent with the no-exact-dominance constraint. Weak cells:
- A’s cycle-time row still wobbles between “faster” for low-risk (weakly supported; extra evidence confirmation is more likely neutral-to-slower) and an understated high-value delay.
- “Auto-screen underused qualitatively” is false.
- A’s best-fit segment is nearly the whole portfolio, so the column stops discriminating.
- Quality effects for A again assume repair-path reopen rates.

**6. Failure modes**

Each redesign has two failure modes and a bottleneck migration, and several are specific (82% utilization, digital-literacy abandonment, Escalation board’s 45-minute slot). Missing or underplayed:
- A: Premium low-risk service-credit risk if the new evidence step blows the 24-hour line (currently 8-hour median).
- B: Premium temporal-profile breach on quote/repair, and Escalation-board overflow as a *predicted* volume effect rather than only a customer-resistance side effect.
- C: first-shot evidence lock-in; abandonment that *improves* reported cycle time and reopen rate while destroying legitimate demand.

**7. Experiment sequence**

Staging A (two-week rule update) → B on the same high-value slice → C on unclear/reopen cases is defensible. End-to-end close, segment slices, and replay of R1–R3 are the right way to detect displaced failure.

The stop/go logic re-imports the path error: A is told to scale if high-value reopens fall “toward the 5–6% range” and cycle time stays “below or around” 96–120 hours. Those are B’s outcome signatures. Under that rule, A can “succeed” by accidentally dumping cases into full repair, at which point the A-vs-B contrast is already gone. A proper A-pilot threshold would be: review/gate compliance up, wrong-replacement cost down, utilization not through 82%, cycle time in a band consistent with *review-then-replace* (not the repair median), and reopen rates improving *without* having to match the repair-path 5–6%.

**Net:** strong artifact coverage and real redesign differentiation, undermined by a repeated confusion of “gated replacement” with “reviewed/repair,” a kitchen-sink Redesign A, a fitness/precision slip, and several table/threshold statements that the given numbers do not support. Not generic, but not tight enough for a high score under a strict standard.
