**7.4**

The answer is unusually complete, format-compliant, and quantitatively careful. The arithmetic on baseline-relative deltas and fragment L1 distances is correct, the timeline separates mechanism-specific onsets instead of inventing one global break, and the monitoring design is usable. It still falls well short of a high score under a strict reading because several process-mining claims are conceptually loose, one required change-point is padded, and the PM4Py plan would mis-measure monthly control flow.

**What is actually strong.** March is treated as a semantic/provenance shock (raw 0.925→0.55, mapped ~0.90, labels 9→11, duplicate `Open Case`, missing resources), and later movement is not dismissed as logging. May is tied to Supplier Z and rework edges (12→24 per 100), June–August to capacity/backlog (service ~4.8h→5.4h vs much larger waiting), July to unobserved priority redistribution, and October–November to seasonal mix plus a senior-assessment surge. Confounding is repeatedly named rather than resolved. The hypothesis, discrimination, and segmentation tables are the right artifacts. The seven decision rules are operational and mechanism-specific.

**Process-mining rigor failures.** Mapped fitness is treated as if a drop from 0.93 to 0.86 were evidence of more rework. Fitness tests whether traces remain in the model, not how often an allowed loop fires. If `Request Info` is already in the normative model, the May–July rework boom should move DFG rates and variant shares, not fitness. The 0.07 mapped-fitness decline is more consistent with incomplete traces, residual technical events, or poorly modeled `Senior Assessment`—and October undercuts their story: senior-assessment jumps to 22/100 while mapped fitness *improves* to 0.88 versus July 0.86. That tension is ignored.

The cycle-time split also blurs activity-level service with case-level assess effort. A 29% rework variant implies many second `Assess` executions at ~5.4h each. That extra work is real service load, not “queueing,” and is only loosely parked inside a 3–6h rework bucket. The component ranges are also too wide and overlapping to be a decomposition rather than a narrative.

**Weak or manufactured structure.** The second logging change point (July) is a 5%→6% duplicate wobble and a 6.2%→6.5% missingness wiggle on a post-migration plateau. That is threshold-hunting to satisfy “at least two” logging breaks. The MoM rules in the code would also miss the 1 July priority change (July median +3.4h, p90 +9h), so the automated detector and the prose change-points are not the same analysis.

**PM4Py plan defects.** `compute_monthly_dfgs` groups *events* by month, then discovers a DFG on truncated traces. Cross-month cases become extra start/end activities and broken edges. They already wrote `filter_time_range` and do not use it there. De-duplicating `Open Case` while keeping the first occurrence also fails to match the stated mapped-fitness procedure if `Open Case` is a new technical start (the March raw DFG’s 26 `Open Case → Register` cases). `get_variants` / `discover_declare` are only lightly version-guarded.

**Smaller unclarities.** Variant percentages come from operational tables that were not guaranteed to use the mapped labels; March’s stability is used implicitly rather than argued. The October senior-assessment rate rose far faster than the high-complexity share (4/100 at 21% HC vs 22/100 at 41% HC), which they note only qualitatively. Hypothesis rank 1 is supplier rework even though their own section 9 says March logging is the strongest attribution. Evidence scores of 95–98 on monthly aggregates are theatrical.

These are not formatting nits. They are exactly the places a drift diagnosis is supposed to be precise: what conformance measures, what waiting versus extra work, what a change point is, and how a monthly DFG must be sliced. The piece is a very good practitioner memo, not a near-flawless one.
