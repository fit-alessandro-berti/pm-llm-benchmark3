**5.0**

The write-up has the right skeleton and a defensible anti-frequency framing, but it is not a reliable process-mining recommendation. Several load-bearing claims are backwards, the recommended set does not do what the closing argument says it does, and control/dependency analysis is repeatedly attached to the wrong activities.

**What is acceptable.** The seven required sections exist. All twelve candidates appear. Three named portfolios are defined. A11 is correctly treated as unsafe without upstream controls. The four-module budget, a control-strengthening module, and the two-deep-integration cap are acknowledged. The political case against automating registration first is the right *question*, even though the supporting mechanics are wrong.

**Matrix and dependencies.** The table is in A1–A12 order, not a ranking. Many “dependencies” run upstream-to-downstream backwards: A5 does not depend on A6; A6 is not a prerequisite for classification (classification sits after register, normalization sits after document review); A7 does not depend on contract generation; A4 does not depend on A12. Downstream-bottleneck cells are misused as benefit statements (A5 “reduces analyst load”). A1’s downstream risk is stated as overloading back office. Registration *is* the back-office step; the stated candidate risk is exposing classify / KYC / document-review queues.

**Resource-demand inversion (material).** The same error is reused as the final trade-off: automating A1 “increases load on 12 FTE back office.” A1 removes registration labor from that pool. The visible queue plus utilization falling below 65% after 15:00 is a batching/scheduling signal, not a reason that A1 floods clerks. The correct frequency-trap argument is slack capacity plus faster dirty inflow into classify, KYC, and analysts. That argument is never stated cleanly.

**Synchronization thesis abandoned.** The prompt’s core finding is that low-frequency blocking work dominates cycle time. Entity mismatch (31h wait, 38% rework, critical path in 82% when present, 3 FTE / two-specialist concentration) and external valuation (58h wait, 19% late, required in 71% of equipment finance) are the clearest such points. The recommended portfolio is A5, A12, A6, A9. A4 and A7 are out. Section 6 then asserts A4 is “handled within A12.” That is false: A12 is a contract-stage remediation workbench (~650 loops); A4 is a KYC-path specialist activity (1750 cases, mismatch ↔ KYC loop 1160). Section 7 then claims the portfolio “reduces the longest waits (Valuation/Docs)” with no valuation module. That is a direct contradiction of the process-level claim being sold.

**Controls.** Four-eyes applies to credit analysis vs committee packaging on high-exposure cases, not “A9 cannot fire until the A8 user differs.” A12 is not the audit bus for A2/A6 suggestions. A3 false negatives are not reliably caught by a late contract workbench before signing; provisional signing (6.5%, 1.1% remediations) is a *timing* control, not a packaging defect. “Control violations reduced by >80%” and Phase-1 gates of 95% complete files / <1% violations are invented precision. Quick-win and bottleneck portfolios are not checked against the mandate that at least one first-wave module strengthen controls.

**Effects and artifacts.** Cycle-time cuts of ~20% / ~35% / ~15% are unsupported. “Eliminates” the 31h mismatch wait and the 8h missing-doc wait overstates assistant/nudge tools. DFG use is thin: the classify split (7250 KYC vs 3940 financial review), KYC/docs concurrency, credit analysis → collateral 2400 vs → packaging 2700, weekly committee cadence, RM-driven incomplete instructions, and legal’s idle-then-month-end pattern are mostly unused. A6’s medium feasibility and bad-PDF exceptions are not designed as a residual analyst workload.

**Net.** Competent exam structure, unsafe process reasoning. The frequency-trap conclusion is directionally right for the wrong reasons, and the chosen four modules do not address the delays the answer claims to prioritize.
