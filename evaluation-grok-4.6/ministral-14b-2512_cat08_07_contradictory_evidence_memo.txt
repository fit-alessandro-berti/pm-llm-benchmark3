**5.4**

The memo gets the strategic posture roughly right—do not scale nationally, do not fully roll back, protect simple-house gains, stop the harmful skip/post-billing mechanisms, and buy information—but it is not a reliable decision document. Under a strict reading it has material numerical errors, mislabeled alternatives, internally contradictory gates, invented statistics, and VoI reasoning that does not actually show how new evidence would flip the choice.

**Strategic call is only half-clean.** The defensible move is closest to **E** (segmented deploy + controls + measurement), with **C** as a coherent operational pause of the two toxic design choices. The memo instead endorses a blurry **D+E** mash-up and, in the executive section, **misidentifies the letters**. It calls selective coordinator ownership plus reversal of skip/post-billing “Alternative D,” and calls the controlled experiment “Alternative E.” In the prompt, those are **C** and **D**; **E** is the hybrid. A leadership memo that cannot keep A–E straight is not decision-ready.

**Several numbers are simply wrong.**
- Simple houses are **34%** of the pilot, not “60%.” That error props up the median-vs-tail story as if a majority path explains the median. Apartments (29%) also improved on median (−2 days) while blowing up the tail. The conflict is within segments, not just between a 60/40 split.
- Billing start → complaint escalation went **3% → 8%** (~2.7×, +5pp), not “5×” and not “DFG edge +3%.” Two different wrong statements of the same edge.
- The “~60% implied” skip-rule accuracy is **not in the evidence**. Segment deterioration does not identify rule precision. Inventing a baseline to then set an “85% accuracy” scale-gate is circular.

**Go/no-go thresholds contradict themselves and are logically garbled.** The exec memo requires a “**5%** increase in p90” to scale; the final section requires “**3%**.” Utilization is capped at **80%** in one place and **85%** in another. Worse, an *increase* in p90 is written as a condition **for** national rollout. If they meant “no more than 3–5% worse,” they did not say that. As written, the gate rewards the failure mode the memo exists to stop. EUR 200 as a cost ceiling is also naive: the EUR 207 figure already **excludes** complaint handling after week 4, so it is biased *against* detecting pilot harm.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are only loosely separated.** The table meets the eight-item minimum and the three-way split, but it omits first-class facts that change inference: pilot mix (more apartments/older buildings than baseline), incomplete segment/risk attributes before week 3, sparse start timestamps, cost truncation after week 4, generalization **0.70 → 0.56**, SLA split, NPS, and working-together centralization. Compositional bias is almost unused: median gains despite a harder mix would *strengthen* the simple-path case; overall tail deterioration is harder to interpret without mix-adjustment. Treating those as optional color rather than identification problems is a synthesis failure.

**Causal links are over-claimed.** Coordinator *absence* (context not shared) is collapsed into *utilization* (93%) and into the failed-visit → second-visit delay. Those are different mechanisms. “The redesign relaxed compliance checks for speed” is presented as settled explanation of low precision; the PM finding is that the discovered model *permits* too many combinations. Fitness/precision is otherwise the strongest PM paragraph.

**Alternative evaluation is uneven.** A and B are handled adequately. **C is undersold**: suspending skip and post-billing activation *is* the direct response to the two alignment violations that drive rework, complaints, and the new billing→escalation tail. Saying C “still misses root causes” while recommending a slower experiment is not argued. Combining D and E as “top recommendation” ducks a primary choice. E already contains measurement-before-scale; D+E is not a distinct strategy.

**Value-of-information ranking is weak against the rubric.** The requirement is which uncertainty each item resolves **and how it could change the decision**. The table mostly restates the measurement. There are almost no decision-flip counterfactuals (e.g., if M4’s 60-day cost is €230 → B/C; if M6 shows the skip rule is well-calibrated and apartment failure is activation sequencing → keep skip, fix billing; if M5 shows absence stalls are rare → keep single-owner). Ranking **M1 (RCT) above M6 (validate the existing rule)** inverts standard VoI: M6 is cheaper and can kill or reshape M1. M7 (conformance on billing-before-activation and missing permits) is operationally cheap and decision-critical; parking it at 4 with thin “legal/quality” language underweights it.

**Other defects.** Comparison operators disappeared throughout (“EUR 184  EUR 161”), which in a cost memo is not cosmetic. Customer-experience shift is used qualitatively, but M8 is dismissed as “secondary” even though complaint *composition* already changed and NPS fell. Resource reallocation (survey 78%→52%, second-visit 66%→92%) is cited but not turned into a capacity implication. Immediate actions say “deploy” coordinator ownership for simple cases without specifying whether that is the four pilot regions, a new cutover rule, or a national simple-house rollout—an execution hole in a memo that claims to be operational.

The skeleton of a good memo is here: required sections exist, national scale is correctly refused, full rollback is correctly refused, and the five metric conflicts are named. The work fails the bar for a high score because a decision-maker cannot trust the letters, the percentages, or the rollout gates.
