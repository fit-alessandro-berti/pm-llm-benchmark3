7.5

The business analysis is strong: it follows the required headings, interprets all 12 cases, keeps raw cost separate from severity, treats silent moves as fitting, flags zero-cost P1/P2 failures, and does not treat a missing event as proof of non-occurrence. Patterns, hypotheses, and the cost-vs-risk section match the prompt. It is not nearly flawless.

**Code is the largest miss.** Section 7 is required to be executable. `pm4py.parse_event_log_string` expects a list of activity-sequence strings (e.g. `["Register Claim,Validate Coverage,..."]`), not one `case,activity,timestamp` string. The fallbacks still pass strings, and the last-resort list-of-dicts is not an EventLog. A dataframe log, which the prompt allowed, would have been reliable. As written, the reproduction call is unlikely to run.

**Severity and priority are internally inconsistent.** Amount/exposure is in the framework, but CL-610 (EUR 4,500, wrong approval path) and CL-602 (EUR 480,000, no fraud evidence) are both 5. The portfolio then ranks auto-approval containment above the 480k fraud-evidence case and above P3 payment-before-approval (CL-604), despite P3 being called out as severe even when alignment is cheap.

**Alignment semantics are slightly underspecified.** For CL-604/CL-602, `M(Fraud Screen)` is required by the tree once `Manual Approve` is observed (XOR branch), not only by P2. For denials/withdrawals, the cheapest XOR repair inserts `Auto Approve` rather than the fraud/manual path; that is not explained. Pattern tagging in code is hardcoded by case id rather than derived from attributes.

Sections 1–4 and 6 are otherwise accurate, complete, and well caveated. The score is held down by the non-executable PM4Py log construction and the ranking/calibration gaps.
