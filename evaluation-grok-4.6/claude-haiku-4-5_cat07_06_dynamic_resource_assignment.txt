The answer is complete on format and socio-technical framing, but it is not close to a reliable policy design. The scoring function is internally inverted, several recommended assignments violate the stated hard/soft constraints, the baseline comparison mischaracterizes first-available routing, and the quantitative “simulation” is invented. **Section 1 is the strongest technical section.** Objectives are multi-criteria, and hard rules are separated from preferences. That said, estimated task durations are introduced as a hard filter without a duration model, the overtime rule is loosened from “urgent and no safer alternative” to “urgent/high,” and the soft cap of 13 open cases is immediately abandoned in the assignment table.

**Section 2 is a failing algorithm.** Continuity and workload are both implemented as penalties that are then *added* under `argmax`. A non-owner facing a high-handover, high-reassignment case therefore gets a *higher* continuity score; a loaded, high-overtime resource gets a *higher* workload score. The SLA term is a constant across candidates, so it never ranks anyone. Security supervision is coded as “the assignee is Gita/Hugo,” not “Gita/Hugo can supervise a skill-1/2 worker,” which excludes the only continuity-preserving option for C001 before the later supervision block can run. High-priority skill-1 work is banned outright except for that broken Security carve-out, contradicting the “unless supervised” rule. The comments marked `(1 .. 1)` and `(0.5 .. 0.5)` are meaningless. Because of all this, the pseudocode cannot produce the table in section 3. An explainable policy whose formal rule and worked example diverge is not auditable.

**Section 3 has several indefensible assignments.** C013 (low priority, new case, 72h slack) to high-overtime R2_Bruno is the clearest failure: Jonas, Amal, Celine, and Eva are all safer. C003, C010, and C014 also load high-overtime resources on non-urgent work despite explicit safer alternatives, violating constraint 4 three more times.
