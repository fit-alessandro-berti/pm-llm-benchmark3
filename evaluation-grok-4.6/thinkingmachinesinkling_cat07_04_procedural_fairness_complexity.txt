**6.5**

The write-up hits the required structure, respects the constraints, and is generally well calibrated. It is not close to flawless. Several interpretive, identification, and technical errors are material.

**What it does well.** Raw approval gaps are not treated as discrimination. Case mix is not treated as a full excuse. `risk_score_band` is treated as potentially biased. Rights-preserving steps are treated as sources of burden, and the mitigations do not propose deleting them. The eight fairness dimensions are actually used, not merely listed. The bottom line (“indeterminate overall, with specific procedural concerns”) matches the evidence strength.

**Serious analytical flaw: misreading the adjusted model.** The preliminary controls are risk, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior-case count, region, and month. They omit `language_preference` and `disability_accommodation_requested`. Residual cycle-time gaps of +2.6 days (B) and +4.1 days (C) therefore still include the mechanical effect of Language Support and Accommodation Review. The answer repeatedly treats those residuals as leftover unexplained unfairness. A careful strategy would split the problem: (i) compare groups on *shared* paths after matching; (ii) judge exclusive support paths against capacity and SLA standards, not against Group A. That distinction is missing.

**Matching design ignores common support.** Group A almost never has language need (2%) or accommodation (4%). You cannot match A to B/C on the attributes that generate V4/V5. The right move is stratified comparison: B/C cases *without* those flags versus A; and, separately, burden *within* flagged cases. The proposed CEM/PSM list copies the preliminary model and does not solve this.

**Evidence is used unevenly, and a few claims overreach.**
- “Group membership predicts path more strongly than complexity” is not identified.
- “Even after controlling for outcomes” (V6) is false; the table controls case mix, not outcomes.
- 57% of B “needs language support” but V4 is only 24%. That gap (under-delivery, alternative variants, or a coarse flag) is never discussed.
- Group C’s adjusted appeal residual is only +1.1 pp; B and D are +4.4 and +5.7. C is repeatedly bundled with B on appeal burden.
- Section 8’s remark about Group D and “routing transparency” is not grounded in the tables.

**Section 7 is the weakest required part.** Calling it “pseudocode” does not excuse conceptual errors. `format_dataframe(..., other_attributes=...)` is not the API. `filter_case_performance` is a duration filter, not a matching procedure. `filter_paths_performance` is used as if it returned a path-level metric. `filter_variants` is left as a broken stub. Log-skeleton/Declare is named but not specified as testable constraints (e.g., language-flagged cases must contain Language Support Contact; accommodation-flagged cases must contain Accommodation Review; then measure violation rates and inserted waiting time). That is exactly how those tools would support a fairness claim, and it is not done.

**Smaller defects.** Resource fairness is described well qualitatively but never turned into a concrete metric (caseload Gini, unique resources per 100 cases, handover count per case). Sensitivity analysis is sketched but not operationalized (what would a change in residual after dropping `risk_score_band` mean, and what would *not* settle). Mitigation side effects are generic. There is no discussion of statistical uncertainty or small cells (C = 820; bilingual pool = 7).

**Net.** Competent, constraint-compliant, and usable as a briefing draft. It is not a rigorous fairness identification strategy, and the PM4Py plan would not run or fully answer the question. Under a strict standard that reserves high scores for nearly clean work, this is mid-to-upper 6.
