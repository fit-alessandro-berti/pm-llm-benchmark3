**6.5**

The answer is structurally complete and strategically defensible: it recommends hybrid E, refuses national scale, refuses a full rollback, and treats the redesign as segment-conditional. That is the right decision under the stated constraints. The write-up is not nearly flawless, and several errors and omissions are material.

**What works.** The memo is decision-oriented rather than a recap. The five metric conflicts are all named and mostly well explained. The A–E section has the right directional judgments. The evidence table uses a real fact / interpretation / assumption split and cites at least eight prompt items with correct headline numbers (median 14.2→10.8, p90/p95, FTR, second visits, fitness/precision, billing→complaint, simple-house deltas, missing permit checks).

**Factual and logical errors.** Alternative C is misstated: C suspends survey skipping and post-billing activation, not merged validate/schedule. The memo’s “reduced handovers for 34% of cases classified as simple, low-risk houses” conflates the simple-house share with the handover metric (3+ handovers fell 34%→12% overall). “Eliminated sequential handovers” is false. Coordinator utilization of 93% is stated as if process-wide; the source limits it to two regions. The final recommendation is internally inconsistent: other segments are told to “immediately revert to the baseline process,” then coordinator ownership is scaled anyway. Shared-team backup is implemented before M5, which the VoI section still treats as an open uncertainty. Thresholds (95% rule accuracy, zero billing-before-activation, p90 < 35 days, complaints < 7%) are invented and presented as if they were implied by the evidence.

**Required artifacts ignored or underused.** The prompt forbids ignoring process-mining artifacts. The answer never uses the working-together / handover-of-work network finding, the resource shift (survey 78%→52%, second-visit 66%→92%), generalization (0.70→0.56), SLA (13%→16%, better for simple houses), NPS (+21→+17), or the complaint-mix shift from “waiting for appointment” to “installed but not working” / “billing before activation.” The DFG table is barely used. Data-coverage facts that should have been first-class evidence are almost absent: pilot mix (more apartments/older buildings), incomplete segment/risk attributes before week 3, complaint cost truncated after week 4, sparse start timestamps, 6 vs 10 weeks. Without those, the memo over-interprets pilot-vs-baseline deltas and underplays selection bias.

**VoI is incomplete.** All of M1–M8 are ranked, but the required “how this could change the decision” test is only met for a couple of items. Ranking the randomized skip experiment (M1) 7th is hard to defend: survey skipping is the main value-destroying mechanism, and M6 cannot substitute for a causal test. M7 is treated as a control system more than as information. M2’s dismissal is too casual given sparse starts and the visit/activation delays.

**Separation of claims is only adequate.** The table structure is correct, but several “facts” smuggle causal packaging, item 1’s assumption is confused given the simple-house p90 already improved, and the memo still writes as if skip-rule error and permit absence are established causes rather than well-supported interpretations.

Net: a competent, correctly aimed decision memo with real synthesis, weakened by misstatement of C, several overclaims, a thin VoI section, and systematic neglect of social/resource, data-quality, and several core PM diagnostics that the prompt required.
