**7.0**

This is a strong, format-compliant causal-policy memo with accurate arithmetic on the windows it chose, genuine process reasoning, and an appropriately refused “X% cycle-time” claim. It is not nearly flawless. Several identification and quantitative choices are sloppy, one windowing decision materially distorts the post-period, and part of the numerical story is not reproduced by the code.

## What is strong

Section 1 correctly separates availability, intensity, targeted selection, co-occurring shocks, and measurement limits, and it stays descriptive. The estimand table covers ITT availability, intensity, median vs tail, rework, escalation, CSAT, dynamics, and heterogeneity, and it is honest that dose and CSAT effects are not identified from these aggregates. Mechanisms are the best part of the memo: fast-path substitution, specialist congestion, learning, routing/case mix, staffing, rework displacement, tail amplification, and survey selection are distinguished, with the right missing artifacts (path shares, queue times, response counts). Threats are broad and mostly well signed. The claim ladder is disciplined. The rollout choice (`expand with an embedded evaluation`) is the right family of recommendation.

## Material analytical flaws

**1. Post-period windows omit the worst treated month.**  
“Early” is event times 0–1 and “late” is 3–4. For Alpha/Beta that drops June (event time 2): Alpha median 53.5h / p90 119.9h, Beta 49.0h / 106.7h. Those are the worst post months. All-post Alpha median is about −0.4h vs pre, not the advertised late −3.3h; all-post Alpha p90 is about +9.7h vs pre. The late-window “learning” story is therefore aligned with falling August arrivals, not with a complete post series. The identification write-up even flags May–Jun as a sensitivity exclusion, then the main tables quietly skip June. That is a quantitative-integrity problem, not a rounding issue.

**2. Demand and capacity are under-analyzed relative to their first-order role.**  
Arrivals/FTE is computable and jumps hard (Alpha from ~30 cases per FTE in January to ~43 in June). August “recovery” in Alpha coincides with arrivals falling from 1367 to 1234. Treating late median gains as implementation fidelity, without a load-normalized contrast, overstates process substitution versus transitory congestion and then easing demand. This is exactly the process distinction the prompt asked for.

**3. Control-group construction is weaker than the identification rhetoric.**  
The primary design is described as staggered/group-time ATT using not-yet-treated *and* never-treated units, but the numbers use only Delta+Epsilon. Gamma is available as a not-yet-treated control for April–June and is not used. Epsilon’s complexity shock (30%→38%) is as large as Alpha’s; if the May product change hit high-complexity branches harder, DE as a pooled control biases DiD in favor of automation. The 0 to −5h median interval is a 50% haircut, not a bound.

**4. March is mixed into the pre-period.**  
The fraud desk starts in March and lifts escalation everywhere. Jan–Mar is therefore not a clean pre-policy baseline. The memo notices the March jump, then still uses that contaminated window as the main pre period.

**5. Numbers in §5 are not fully generated by §9.**  
The −0.66h per +10pp intensity figure is plausible as a descriptive OLS output, but the +0.12pp escalation dose result is not estimated in the code at all. DiD vs DE is also narrative-only. That breaks the “reproducible analysis” requirement and makes those coefficients unverifiable as presented.

## Design, code, and decision issues

Calling stacked DiD, event-study, and group-time ATT one “primary strategy” blurs estimators that differ in weighting and in how they use never-treated units. The event-study regression adds relative-time dummies *and* calendar month FE on 5 clusters / 40 rows; that is saturated and should not be treated as a serious diagnostic engine. Never-treated adoption dates are encoded via `NaT` then `.astype("int64")`, which is fragile and can fail or create sentinel integers. Intensity regressions condition on post-treatment `arrivals` (and possibly complexity), which is a collider/mediator if routing and measured mix respond to rollout; that assumption is not in the code comments.

Escalation confidence of 75 is too high for a causal reading even though the April jump is concentrated in adopters: the estimand confounds triage policy, fraud-desk sending rules, and coding. Median confidence of 55 is also high once June, load, selection, and differential case mix are taken seriously. Expanding *both* remaining branches, even with a 7-week stagger, burns the only long-run controls. Epsilon is the closer analogue to treated sites; Delta is the natural holdout. Case-level encouragement is a good idea; pairing it with simultaneous expansion of the last two branches is not.

## Format and estimand nits

Required headings, tables, and most word bands are met. E1’s population is “all cases” while the unit is branch-month. E6 is written as a ceteris-paribus structural contrast rather than an implementable intervention. Pre-existing 4–6% automated-fast-path share is noted as intensity, not as a treatment-version/anticipation problem. The code prints trends rather than plotting them, which the prompt allows, but it does not compute the late-post or DiD tables that carry the argument.

## Bottom line

The memo knows the right causal vocabulary and refuses the indefensible leadership sentence. It would still not be safe to hand to executives as the quantitative record, because the featured pre/post windows omit peak congestion, load is not treated as a primary alternative mechanism, and some reported estimates cannot be recovered from the supplied script. That combination caps the score well below the near-flawless range.
