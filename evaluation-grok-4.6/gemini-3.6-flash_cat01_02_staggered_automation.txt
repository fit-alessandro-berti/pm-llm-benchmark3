**5.7**

The answer looks like a complete, fluent causal-policy memo and hits the required skeleton. Under a strict reading it is not close to reliable: several published numbers are wrong, the identification story and the code do not match, and the effect assessment walks back the paper’s own “not identifiable” conclusions.

**Format and coverage.** Headings, tables, estimand list, mechanism list, threat list, and the four-rung claim ladder are present. That is organizational competence, not scientific quality. Section 1 slightly overruns the word cap and is otherwise the strongest part: availability vs `automated_fast_path_pct`, endogenous targeting of slow high-volume branches, co-treatments, and CSAT/aggregation limits are cleanly separated and stay descriptive.

**Estimands.** E1–E7 cover the requested contrasts, including ITT, intensity, tail, rework/escalation, CSAT, and dynamics. Two conceptual slips matter. E2 is labeled LATE without a well-defined complier or instrument in the estimand itself. Almost every row is marked “No,” which is defensible as caution, but then Sections 5, 7, and 10 treat a numeric median effect as if it were identified under mild extra assumptions. Interference and treatment-version notes are appropriate; they are not carried into the quantitative claims.

**Identification.** Naming Callaway–Sant’Anna / Sun–Abraham and warning about naïve TWFE is the right instinct. The justification then misstates what that design does: binary cohort-time ATTs do **not** “explicitly model progressive adoption intensity.” Conditioning on `arrivals/fte` is treated as fixing the April routing campaign; that campaign is a co-intervention, and the load ratio is partly post-treatment. SCM with two donors and three pre-periods, and 2SLS with a rollout instrument whose exclusion restriction the author already rejects, are not credible alternatives—they are named estimators. With two treated cohorts, one singleton late adopter, and five clusters, CS/SA is a description of a few paths, not a robust design.

**Mechanisms.** This is the best scientific content. Fast-path substitution vs specialist congestion, learning vs July–August volume decline, staffing at Alpha, rework displacement, tail-ratio widening, and survey selection are the right process stories. The evidence column is more honest than Section 7 (common May–June p90 spike; Ambiguous August recovery). The header rule is malformed (eight alignment cells for six columns).

**Quantitative discipline—material failures.** Several control and pre-period figures are simply incorrect and then reused.

- Delta Jan–Mar vs Apr–Aug median is about **+5.99 h**, not +5.87 h; p90 is about **+17.51 h**, not +17.31 h.
- Epsilon p90 change is about **+14.58 h**, not +13.58 h.
- Beta pre `escalation_pct` is **8.17**, not 8.27; Gamma Jan–Jun escalation is **8.70**, not 8.78.

The never-treated DiD numbers (−5.56 h / −5.80 h, etc.) inherit those errors. Gamma’s “pre” is defined as Jan–Mar in the header and computed as Jan–Jun in the table, so May–June shock months are baked into the baseline and the −8.3% median move is not comparable to Alpha/Beta. Pre-trends are called “parallel” and “stable” after three noisy months in which Alpha’s median moves 50.4 → 46.9 → 49.4 and March escalation jumps in every branch. The “sensitivity” interval is only raw pre/post vs this naïve DiD; the −6.0 h “full DiD adjusting for routing volume” is not shown. Intensity results (−0.42 h and +1.85 h per 10 pp, with p-values) do not come from the code that follows and appear to be unclustered OLS.

**Internal contradiction on the policy estimand.** Section 2 says the ITT on median cycle time is not identifiable. Section 7 calls it a “moderate beneficial effect,” interval **−2 to −6 h**, confidence **65**, and allows leadership to say automation “helps stabilize and moderately reduce” medians. Beta’s own post mean is **+1.86 h**. Epsilon, the high-complexity never-treated branch, recovers by August without automation. That is not a 65-level finding. Escalation is the one outcome with a clear descriptive shift, but March’s fraud desk already added ~2 pp everywhere; “definite,” 85, and +1.8 to +3.0 pp overstate how cleanly the extra rise is attributable to the tool.

**Code (Section 9) fails stated requirements.** The CSV embed, validation, printed (not plotted) trends, and pre/post contrasts are fine. `rel_time` is built and never used—no event-study, no cohort-time ATT. The fitted models are the TWFE specifications the prose rejected. Statsmodels can cluster; the fits do not. The required small-N and aggregated-median warnings appear, but reporting Section 5 p-values after those warnings is inconsistent. Unused `numpy` is trivial; the missing cluster covariance and unused relative time are not.

**Rollout.** `expand with an embedded evaluation` is the only defensible family. Case-level timestamps, fraud-desk waits, and hard p90/CSAT guardrails are good. The design is not: two remaining branches are not a stepped-wedge RCT; Delta is “randomly assigned” and then **named** as the September unit; a Wild-cluster *p* < 0.05 rule for a ≥4 h median effect with ~7 clusters is a decision rule that cannot be met honestly. Transfer from endogenously weak Alpha/Beta to historically faster Delta/Epsilon is barely discussed.

**Claim ladder.** The “Observed” rung is fair. “Suggestive” already locks in a 3–5 h relative median gain. “Conditionally causal” restates that gain as a policy effect. “Not supported” correctly rejects a single “X%” slogan and CSAT/p90 improvement—then sits next to a leadership sentence that is the slogan with extra words.

Net: strong process vocabulary and the right qualitative caution about tails, intensity, and targeting; too many wrong numbers, a design–code split, and an effect table that overclaims what the author earlier said cannot be identified. That is a competent draft, not a trustworthy analysis.
