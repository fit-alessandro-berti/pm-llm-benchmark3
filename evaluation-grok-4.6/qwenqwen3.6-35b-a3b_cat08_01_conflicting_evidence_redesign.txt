**6.0**

The write-up is well structured and hits every required section, and it is clearly better than a generic Lean/Six Sigma dump. It also gets the central contradiction right in qualitative terms: the high-volume auto/fast path looks cheap and fast until complaints, reshipments, and credits are included (31% cheaper before rework, 9% after), and segment outcomes show the damage is concentrated on high-value devices (22% / 18% reopen on the fast path vs 5% / 6% on the reviewed/repair path). Redesigns A and B are genuinely different mechanisms (intake routing vs evidence-frontloading), sacrifices are named, the table columns match the spec, and the experiment plan includes isolation, stop/go rules, and downstream-shift checks. That is the ceiling of what is good here.

Under a strict evidence-use standard, the diagnosis is not reliable enough to score much higher.

The most serious problem is mishandling of the DFG itself. “Intake → Auto eligibility → Fast approval handles 3,600 cases (72%)” collapses two different edges: 3,600 cases enter auto screen; only 2,550 go auto → fast approval (plus 240 from fraud/risk). Treating the auto-screen volume as the fast-approval path overstates how dominant that cheap path is and understates the 650 evidence and 400 fraud exits already present. The same section then cites “Customer complaint/reopen → Manual eligibility review (890 cases)” and gives that loop a mean of 34h. Those numbers belong to **Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review** (890; mean 34h / p90 88h). The actual complaint edge is 310 cases and mean 40h. That is not a rounding issue; it conflates the evidence ping-pong with the reopen loop and then hangs R3 on the wrong artifact. A third swap assigns Request missing evidence a “mean 14.0h / p90 36.0h,” which is **Manual → Request missing evidence**, not the customer wait. The real evidence stall is much worse. Separately, Auto → Fast approval’s 1.0h / 0.4h / 4.0h is presented as if it were fast-path cycle time; the performance DFG plus segment medians (8–28h) show it is only one hop.

Those errors matter because the rubric is process-mining reasoning, not prose quality. Once the loops are crossed, later claims about “the” bottleneck and what each redesign attacks become harder to trust.

Redesign logic is uneven. A is the most defensible idea and is grounded in R1/R2, outdated non-segmented auto rules, and the high-value complaint split. It still overclaims: routing high-value work off the fast path would be expected to move reopen rates toward the 5–6% reviewed-path band, not “eliminate” the 22% rate. It also gets the resource direction wrong. Pulling high-value cases out of auto into manual/fraud is exactly the 15–35% manual-inflow risk in the prompt; with 7 reviewers and a sharp queue above 82% utilization, A is more likely to **raise** specialist load than reduce it. The table’s “net neutral/positive” resource line contradicts both that prompt fact and the author’s own failure-mode note about the manual queue. The invented “Part-Match Score” is at least motivated by the wrong-replacement / temporal-profile finding; it is still underspecified.

B is the strongest of the three designs: it targets the 26% repeat-evidence statistic, 320 abandonments, the 22% unclear-defect segment, and the portal’s 30–45% reduction versus digital-literacy abandonment. The table then undermines that specificity by naming “Standard low-value” as best-fit, which is not where the evidence pathology lives.

C is the weakest and is not fully materially distinct from A. “Run risk in parallel” plus a pre-dispatch gate is standard BPM parallelism. Fraud/risk has **two** specialists and is already a Monday/campaign bottleneck; fanning more work into that path without a hard filter is a capacity error. If the parallel check is “lightweight automation,” C collapses toward A’s better auto-rules. The prompt’s pre-dispatch gate cost (6–18 hours) is not used, and “proof of delivery” is not a conformance issue in the log (R1/R2/R3, evidence-before-dispatch, high-value without risk review). Escalation-board commentary in the diagnosis also invents a purpose the artifact does not support: it is a scarce reactive CSAT mechanism, not an unused proactive risk control.

Smaller defects still count under the instructed standard: 26% is of cases **that enter** evidence request, not a global rate; fitness 0.91, 68% fitting traces, and low precision are barely used in the required diagnosis section; several stop/go numbers (`<1%` violations, `>5%` cost savings) are not recoverable from the given data; A’s first pilot stop at 90% utilization sits **after** the stated 82% cliff.

The piece is a competent executive narrative with real process-mining vocabulary and three non-identical bets. It is not a nearly flawless synthesis of the artifacts. The crossed DFG edges and the wrong resource implication on the main redesign are enough, on a hypercritical reading, to keep it in the mid range.
