**6.7**

The memo is strategically strong and mostly well structured, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it has a hard format violation, one reversed causal inference, a process-mining conceptual error, and several overclaims presented as if they were settled.

**Format.** Section 1 exceeds the 700-word cap (body alone is over the limit; with headers it is closer to ~780). That is a stated constraint, not a style preference.

**Best work.** The core decision is right and well argued: unbundle R, reject A and B, stop the compliance-like paths now, bank the simple-house gains, and buy information before any wider rollout. The evidence table is the strongest section: 14 items, facts mostly clean, interpretations labeled, and the important uncertainties (mix confounding, truncated cost, sparse start times, incomplete segment attributes) are explicit. Sections 3 and 5 are mostly high-quality synthesis. Using E with D embedded is better than treating those alternatives as mutually exclusive.

**Material errors.**

1. **Case-mix logic is reversed in 4A.** The prompt says the *pilot* had more apartments and older buildings than baseline. A national mix closer to baseline should therefore contain *fewer* hard cases, not more. Claiming that “national mix contains more apartments… so measured damage would likely grow” is the opposite of the only mix fact given. “In some markets” is unsupported speculation. This matters because it is used to reject A.

2. **Fitness/precision is conflated with normative conformance.** Alignments such as “Billing start before Activation test” (move-on-log) and “expected Permit check not observed” (move-on-model) are deviations from a reference model. Discovered-model precision 0.51 means the inductive model allows unobserved combinations. Those are different diagnostics. Section 3’s closer — “a process in which billing-before-activation and missing permit checks are possible is exactly a process whose model has low precision” — is wrong. The same confusion reappears when low precision is said to make conformance monitoring unreliable. M7 does not require a precise discovered model.

3. **M6 is oversold.** Validating the skip rule against field-survey outcomes cannot be a clean retrospective on skipped cases: those cases have no survey outcome. A back-test on *surveyed* cases is selected on the rule itself. Ranking M6 first, and calling it fast with “no waiting for new cases,” ignores that selection problem.

**Smaller but real issues.**

- “Flower-like” is the wrong PM metaphor for a permissive optional-path model.
- Generalization 0.56 is described as “one-off variants.” Textbook generalization is about overfitting / unseen behavior, and the triad high fitness / low precision / low generalization is messier than the memo admits.
- “Legally mandatory,” “regulatory exposure,” and “unbounded” cost go beyond the evidence. The log shows missing permit checks and billing-before-activation; it does not establish a legal duty.
- “Absorb nearly all of the deterioration” / “badly matched to roughly half” understates the segment table. Four of five segments worsen on FTR and complaints (apartments, old buildings, business, rural). Only simple houses improve across the board.
- Alternative C is slightly misread. The prompt suspends survey skipping and post-billing activation; it does not clearly suspend merged validate/schedule. Treating that merge as automatically discarded is an interpolation.
- Several action thresholds are invented and then treated as if they were implied by the data: 85% utilization, p95 ≤49.5, precision ≥0.75, zero deviations over 8 weeks. Zero defects sits uneasily next to the table’s own “logging gap” assumption. Using discovered-model precision as a hard rollout gate is especially weak after the memo itself notes that low precision may be a short-log artefact.
- M7 is ranked as high decision-value after the memo has already decided to stop those two deviations. At that point it is a control, not a pivot experiment.
- A few memo sentences state interpretations as facts (“work was not eliminated; it was displaced”; EUR 207 “is a lower bound”) even though the table correctly parks them as interpretation/assumption.

**What still works.** The recommendation does not scale nationally, does not roll back the whole design, does not pretend one KPI wins, and uses the PM artefacts (fitness/precision/generalization, alignments, temporal profile, DFG, social/resource networks, segments). VoI sequencing is mostly sensible, and the “information cannot legalize billing-before-activation” point is correct.

**Net.** This is a good executive memo with a right-shaped decision and unusually clean fact/interpretation hygiene in the table. It is not a 9–10. The word-count breach, the reversed national-mix argument, and the precision/conformance mix-up are enough to keep it well out of the top band.
