**7.3**

The memo is strategically competent and hits the required structure, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it has a real inconsistency in the core operating policy, several analytical omissions, and repeated small overclaims.

**What works.** Alternative E is the only option that respects the hard constraints, and the memo actually operationalizes it instead of restating the prompt. Segment evidence is used as the reason to keep simple-house value rather than as decoration. Fitness versus precision, median versus tail, handover count versus resilience, and visible versus downstream cost are explained as different quantities, not as a vague “mixed picture.” The evidence table mostly keeps facts, interpretations, and assumptions apart. The rollout gates are decision-oriented.

**Core logical flaw.** The recommendation is internally inconsistent on survey skipping. The memo suspends skipping “across all expansion” except inside a controlled experiment, then immediately authorizes a “streamlined path” for verified simple low-risk houses and requires surveys only for addresses “outside validated low-risk eligibility.” A reader cannot tell whether simple-house rollout already includes skip. That is the central design choice in R, so the ambiguity is material.

**Analytical gaps.** Generalization (0.70 → 0.56) is never used, despite being one of the named process-mining artifacts and the one most relevant to scaling. The 6-week intake window versus a p95 of 62 days is not interrogated; right-censoring, completed-case bias, and unequal follow-up are ignored. Sparse start timestamps are deferred to M2 instead of being treated as a limit on every duration claim already cited. Composition bias is invoked symmetrically (“neither median nor tail can be attributed wholly to R”) even though a harder pilot mix would typically make the median gain harder to produce and the tail deterioration easier. That is sloppy causal reasoning.

**Fact / interpretation bleed.** Several “facts” smuggle causation: the redesign “reduced” handovers; the simple-house segment “performed better under the pilot than its comparator”; the “typical” case got faster. The prompt gives before/after and segment deltas, not a concurrent controlled contrast. “Skip rule often wrong” is a note in the source table, not a validated causal finding, yet it is used as if it were established mechanism.

**VOI ranking does not match the decision.** If eligibility routing is the operating model, M6 and M7 are the binding uncertainties. Ranking a new skip RCT first, ahead of validating the risk rule and monitoring billing-before-activation / missing permits, is backwards relative to the policy the memo just adopted. M4 is correctly high. The later experimental note not to randomize post-billing activation is sound and should have disciplined the ranking.

**Other deductions.** Rollout thresholds (2-day p90 band, +1pp complaints, 85% utilization, zero unapproved billing-before-activation) are asserted, not derived from SLA, cost, or observed gaps. Rural and business cases are dumped into the same mandatory survey/permit bucket even though the stated failure modes are rescheduling fragility and billing disputes. The complaint-mix shift (wait-for-appointment → installed-but-not-working / billed-before-activation) is stronger evidence than the memo uses. Minor polish failures (broken DFG arrows, “comparator” language) add to the sense that the write-up was not fully tightened.

The piece would score much higher with a generous rubric. Strictly, it is a strong but imperfect decision memo: right alternative, incomplete causal hygiene, and an unclear rule on the intervention that most needs a rule.
