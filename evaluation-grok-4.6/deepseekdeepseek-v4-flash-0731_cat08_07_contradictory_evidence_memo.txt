**6.5**

The memo is format-compliant, decision-oriented, and strategically defensible: it rejects A and B for the right reasons, keeps segment gains, and suspends the two components most tied to tail/rework/activation integrity. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are mostly separated. The five metric conflicts are explained coherently, and M1–M8 are all ranked with a decision-impact story.

It is not close to flawless.

The largest analytical error is the median story. Four of five segments improved on median; only old/permit-sensitive buildings got worse. Claiming the overall median fell “mainly because simple houses improved” overstates concentration of the central-tendency gain and understates that the real split is median vs tail/quality, not “only simple houses got faster.”

Process-mining coverage is incomplete relative to the brief. Fitness, precision, alignments, DFG, and social/resource findings are used. The temporal profile is not treated as evidence: install→activation worsening for apartments, billing→complaint as a new long-tail relation, and failed-visit→second-visit delay under high coordinator utilization are barely used as such. Business and rural rows are waved at, not analyzed.

C vs E is blurred. “C with E guardrails” never cleanly says whether coordinator ownership is national (baseline steps restored) or limited to simple houses. An invented 85% utilization cap is written as if it were an evidenced control.

VoI ranking is only partly decision-relevant. After choosing a constrained hybrid, a randomized skip trial is option value for later reintroduction, not the highest-value next measurement for choosing among A–E. M3/M4/M5 sit too low given incomplete cost, missing reason codes, and the resilience failure they themselves flag.

Smaller defects: “illegal” paths overclaim the alignment evidence; SLA’s simple-house improvement is underused; DFG arrows are garbled. Strong synthesis, not a 8–10 answer under a strict reading of the rubric.
