**7.4**

The answer is a strong process-mining redesign: mix is separated from volume, DFG frequencies are mostly computed correctly, standing rules are distinguished from triggered policies, and it refuses to optimize the historical average. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several factual and causal errors keep it well below the top band.

**What it gets right.** S1–S4 match the required disruption types; S5 is a useful compound case. Weekly volume +43% versus Urgent/Complex more than doubling, with Routine weekly volume almost flat, is the right framing. Recollect, batch-wait, assay-repeat, medical-validation, and specialist-hold rates are derived from the DFGs rather than asserted. Bottleneck-migration is treated as a chain (I8→pre-anal, I3→M2 repeats, I4→101% technical validation, I1→Routine/Complex starvation). I2, I9, standing I1/I3, and standing I5/I10 are rejected with downstream reasons. The trade-off table has the required columns and does not pretend every class can be prioritized at once. Residual vulnerability #5 is genuinely redesign-induced. The dashboard separates leading switches from lagging SLA/rework outcomes.

**Material reasoning error: I5 is attached to the wrong edge.** In S1 the write-up claims evening medical capacity “shortens `Medical validation → Release result` (already only 2h / 5h) **after** a 24h tech-to-medical wait.” That 2h/5h edge is post-validation release. The medical-validator queue (22h) sits on `Technical validation → Medical validation` (8h / 24h). I5’s first-order effect is exactly that wait, not the release hop. The follow-on claim that “the queue simply moves back to technical validation” is a plausible later migration, but it is justified with a misread performance DFG. Later sections treat I5 as helping the medical queue, so the piece is internally inconsistent, not merely imprecise.

**Repeated factual error: recollection cancel rates.** S4’s “780 peak, 47% of which cancel” and the dashboard’s “38% → 47%” divide cancels by `Pre-analytical check → Request recollection` only. Accession correction also feeds recollection (70 / 110). Dispositions at `Request recollection` are 290/830 = 34.9% normal and 370/890 = 41.6% peak. The qualitative point (more lost loops, clerk time) survives; the cited percentages do not.

**Causal sloppiness.** I8 and I2 are bundled as the same “WIP dump.” Unmetered registration can flood downstream stations; larger Routine batches do not increase arrivals—they lengthen batch-wait and trap work that cannot bypass. Medians on `Test preparation → Batch wait` and `Batch wait → Assay run` are added as if Urgent non-bypass cases pay 14+22 hours. I10 is first said to remove “610 peak” specialist-hold entries, then correctly restricted to early-stage Complex only.

**Utilization versus sequence.** Peak utilization is already highest at technical validation (101%), then assays (98%), then pre-anal (96%). S1 still presents pre-anal and assays as firing first. A process-order story can be defended; not acknowledging the tightest station first is a gap in the bottleneck section.

**Policy specification holes.** Several triggers are invented (15% Urgent >12h, 8h accession, 70% bypass) without tying them to the 18h/72h clocks. `N` for Urgent-waiting-for-medical is undefined. On/off bands leave a dead zone (e.g. Urgent mix 20–25%). R3’s standing reservation size is unspecified except under P-M1. Releasing unused M1 tokens after 14:00 is a hidden policy whose late-courier failure mode is never discussed.

**What keeps it from collapsing.** The package is still implementable in outline, grounded in the logs more often than not, and disciplined about not adding capacity everywhere. The remaining vulnerabilities (technical validation as ceiling, specialist SPOF, I10 custody, M2 quality) are the right leftovers. Those strengths are why this is a mid-to-high 7, not a 5. The I5 edge error, the cancel-rate arithmetic, and the underspecified switches are why it is nowhere near 9.
