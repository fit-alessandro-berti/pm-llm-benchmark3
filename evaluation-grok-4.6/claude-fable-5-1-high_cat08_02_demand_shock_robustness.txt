**7.0**

This is a strong, operationally serious redesign that hits the required structure and the spirit of the brief: mix-aware scenarios, bottleneck migration, selective use of the candidate interventions, explicit priority scarcity, and a leading-indicator dashboard. It is not nearly flawless. Several load-bearing numbers are computed inconsistently, a few causal claims are stronger than the log supports, and some policies are quantitatively underspecified. Under a strict reading those are not nits.

## What is actually good

The weekly conversion of the 8-week vs 3-week windows is the right process-mining move, and the headline insight is correct: Routine volume is essentially flat (~1,403 vs ~1,386 per week); the peak is an Urgent/Complex/Repeat mix shock. Assay runs +50%, repeat triggers +194%, medical-validation entries +158%, and specialist-hold entries +460% are the right derived facts to drive design.

Scenarios vary by **case type and disruption**, not just volume. S1–S4 cover the required shocks; S5 as the compound week is the right design case. Bottleneck-migration writeups are the best part of the answer: Urgent-first starving Routine/Complex and feeding repeats; I8 exposing pre-analytical at 96%; I7 removing slack hours that accession and benches used to catch up; machine-recovery waves dumping onto technical/medical validation; Hold used as a parking lot when medical validation is congested. Downstream overload is named for every robust intervention and every scenario policy.

The robust vs contingent split is disciplined. I1/I2/I3/I9 are rejected or rewritten rather than swallowed. Slack-based dispatch with an Urgent reservation cap is a better answer than class-absolute priority. Guard rails on I4, time-boxed batching instead of a permanent size-24 rule, and I10 used at registration rather than after 48h in Hold all follow the conflict hints. Residual vulnerabilities include redesign-caused ones (slack synchronisation, technical validation as the new sink, automation moving errors onto two accession specialists, stricter triage raising cancellations). The dashboard separates leading from lagging and is actually usable at 08:30/15:00.

## Critical issues

**1. Inconsistent denominators on the central “behavioural escalation” claim.**  
The answer states that Technical validation → Medical validation rose from 24.5% to **47% of TV output** (1.9×) and Hold from 1.8% to 8.4% of TV output (4.7×). Normal-period shares are correctly taken from TV *outflow* (3,900 / 15,890 = 24.5%; 290 / 15,890 = 1.8%). Peak shares use TV *inflow* (3,420 / 7,240 ≈ 47.2%; 610 / 7,240 ≈ 8.4%). Peak outflow is 8,080, which they themselves report in the same paragraph. On a consistent outflow basis the MV share is **42.3%** (1.73×), and Hold is **7.6%**. They chose the denominator that maximises amplification, then labelled it “output.”

That matters because R4 (escalation hygiene, auto-release, “keep direct-release above 65% rather than falling to 56%”) and the 75–80% medical-validator utilisation recovery are justified by “escalation growing faster than mix.” Mix already went 31% → 49% Urgent+Complex (1.58×), Complex is 3.1× more likely to need medical validation, and winter samples are plausibly more abnormal. A mix-adjusted expected MV rate is never computed. After correcting the percentage, the residual “behavioural” gap is modest and may be zero. Hold growth is the stronger parking-lot signal; the MV-share argument is oversold.

**2. The 840 TV inflow/outflow gap is first sold as hidden loops.**  
Peak TV exits exceed Assay → TV entries by 840. The first interpretation offered is unobserved Medical → Technical send-backs. A 3-week peak window with 13.3% of cases lacking END is much more consistent with opening WIP and right-censoring. Section 6 later admits snapshot truncation, so the author knows this, but section 2 and the preliminary already used the gap to colour validator-capacity decisions. That is sloppy process-mining practice on a point they flagged as something to verify “before any validator-capacity decision.”

**3. Overclaim on Urgent p90.**  
The trade-off table says R1 should return Urgent p90 under 18h at peak mix if medical validation is not saturated. Peak Urgent median is already 17h and p90 33h. Bypass of batch wait is still 58% at peak, so **most Urgent cases were not in the 36h batch path** and still missed the SLA. Performance edges are population medians, not Urgent-specific: treating “Batch wait 14h + 22h = 36h loss” and “TV → MV 24h alone” as Urgent clocks is imprecise (36h is path duration, not incremental penalty versus the unreported direct Test preparation → Assay edge; Urgent inside those paths may already be expedited). Assay median queue is 18h at 98% utilisation. R1 can stop batching Urgent work and reserve slots; it cannot, by itself, put p90 back under 18h.

**4. Capacity arithmetic on R1 is thin to the point of infeasibility risk.**  
Peak Urgent volume is ~137/day (if a 7-day week; ~191/day if weekdays only — the calendar is never stated). “Express runs at least every 2h” plus “one MV sign-off slot per hour” is not sized against that arrival rate, against Complex sharing the same machines, or against the problem’s own warning that Urgent bypass **fragments machine schedules**. One MV slot per hour is ~10–16/day. If a large fraction of Urgent cases need medical validation, the reservation is far too small; P1 raises the *assay* cap to ~40% but never resizes the MV slot. This is exactly the class of “intervention without a downstream load model” the brief asked to avoid.

## Smaller but real issues

- Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure are given a 72h due time in the slack rule. The log gives no such target; Complex median is already 64h in the *normal* period (p90 118h). Ranking Complex on a 72h clock either makes them look late from the start or fights the specialist calendar the same answer tries to protect.
- “Halving repeats takes machines from 98% to ~93% and the assay queue from 18h to ~6h” is a back-of-envelope Kingman story, not a model. Directionally right at high utilisation; numerically decorative.
- Cross-training registration/pre-analytical staff into accession correction assumes a two-person specialist pool is a staffing problem rather than a skill problem. The log only supports 4-week cross-training for *technical validators*.
- I8 “not worth it below 40% digital metadata” is an invented threshold with no sensitivity.
- Extra “Preliminary” section violates “exactly the following top-level sections.” Minor, but it is also where the worst percentage claim is planted before the required analysis.
- Batch-wait share “13.3%” is 12.7% of Test preparation outflow (1,180/9,320) or 13.3% of cases reaching preparation — another small denominator slide.

## Constraint compliance

The answer does **not** sprinkle FTE on every station; I5 is gated. It does **not** optimise mean cycle time; it works with p90, SLA clocks, and who yields. It does **not** try to put every case type first. Those are real strengths and are why this is a 7 rather than a 5.

It does, however, rest a major robust package (R4) on a mislabelled share, and it does promise Urgent p90 recovery the peak log does not support. A strict judge should treat those as reasoning failures, not formatting residue.

**Net:** excellent process diagnosis and a credible control-plane (modes, triggers, floors, residual risks), undermined by motivated percentages, unadjusted mix comparisons, and policy numbers that do not add up at peak Urgent volume. That is a good answer with material flaws, not a near-flawless one.
