**6.8**

The memo chooses the right strategic posture — keep the simple-house gain, kill the dangerous shortcuts, do not treat R as a single vote — and the evidence table, alternatives, and VoI section are mostly disciplined. It is not nearly flawless. Several defects are material for a process-mining decision memo.

**Format.** The executive memo is well over the 700-word cap (on the order of 830–850 words). That is not a cosmetic miss. The section is supposed to be an executive product; the overrun is unused compression, repeated metric recitation, and a second copy of the action list that Section 6 already has to provide.

**Process-mining concepts are sloppy in the place they matter most.** Fitness vs. precision is the one conflict that must be technically exact. Precision is extra behavior relative to the *log*, not “behavior that is not a sensible or intended process.” The write-up then says alignments show skipped surveys, repeated installs, manual activation, and early billing *actually occur*. Observed deviant traces raise fitness and show non-conformance; they do not explain a precision of 0.51. Low precision means the discovered model still licenses many *unseen* interleavings of those optional steps. Conflating precision, normative soundness, and move-on-log diagnostics is a real error, not a wording quibble.

Generalization is mishandled in the same paragraph. High fitness + low precision is a model that is too permissive. Calling generalization 0.56 “overfitted to a short, exception-heavy pilot” uses the wrong diagnosis. Overfitting is the high-precision / low-generalization pattern. The pilot model is closer to an unstable, under-constrained language than to a model that memorized the log.

**Design vs. deviation.** R’s specified changes are coordinator ownership, merged validate/schedule, predictive survey skip, and activation after billing when the signal “looked acceptable.” Missing permit checks are a move-on-model finding — expected work not observed — not a stated redesign feature. The memo still says the bundle “deleted … permits,” treats permit omission as a policy to “pause,” and tells leadership to “reinstall” a control that may never have been removed. That merges a conformance failure into the intervention itself and overstates how cleanly the three “design choices” explain the tail.

**Smaller factual and logical slips.** Simple low-risk houses are 34% of the pilot, not an “easy majority path.” The opening sentence also assigns the median win to coordinator + merge + fewer handovers as if those effects were identified. The rest of the memo correctly says R was a bundle; the lead sentence does not. Business and rural segments (billing disputes; harder rescheduling) are in the record and then largely dropped, so E is specified as a two-class factory when the evidence table has five. Section 5 ranks M5 as refinement, “not to decide the next 90 days,” then Section 6 makes an M5-style absence test a hard gate for broader rollout.

**What still works.** Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are separated in the table, and the classifications are mostly right — including the incomplete cost figure, the unvalidated risk rule, and the non-identifiability of bundled treatments. The five metric conflicts are the right conflicts; the cost and handover/resilience write-ups are the cleanest. A–E are evaluated as genuine alternatives with conditions, not as a fake menu around a predetermined winner. VoI is ranked by whether a result would change A–E or the gates, not by scientific niceness, and M1 after M6 is the correct dependence. The final recommendation is decidable: scale a slice, pause skip and post-billing activation, instrument M7/M6/M3/M4, and do not nationalize R while p90/p95, first-time-right, rework, and activation order are worse.

Those strengths are why this is not a mid-pack memo. The word-limit breach, the precision/generalization confusion, and the promotion of a permit deviation into a designed feature are why it is nowhere near a high score under a strict standard.
