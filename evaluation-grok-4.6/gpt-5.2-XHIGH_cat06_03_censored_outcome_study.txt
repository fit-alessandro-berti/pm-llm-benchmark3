7.0

This is a competent, process-aware design that avoids the main failure modes (treating rejects as non-default, training uncritically on the 21,600-row export, leaking post-outcome fields, random splits). Under a strict reading it is not close to flawless: the primary estimand, cohort accounting, validation mechanics, and skeleton all have real gaps.

**Target.** Conditioning on eventual activation, \(A=1\), is statistically honest for warehouse `default_90d` and is clearly distinguished from a multi-state outcome and from a counterfactual “default if activated.” That is the right refusal of the informal “rejects = non-default” request. It is not, however, operationally aligned with “early warning from the first few events of **every** application.” \(A\) is realized after \(t_p\), so \(\Pr(Y=1\mid X_{0:t_p},A=1)\) is not a prediction you can form from the prefix alone; scoring all applications with a model fit on booked accounts is selection on the future. The joint \(\Pr(A=1,Y=1\mid X)\) is the quantity that would actually be identified at application time, but it is treated as an afterthought. Prediction time is also left as a menu (\(k\in\{2,3,5,8\}\) or \(\Delta\in\{1h,24h,3d,7d\}\)) rather than one primary as-of rule. Time-to-default after activation with right censoring—explicitly suggested in the brief and the natural fit for the 3,600 immature/delayed activations—is never named as an alternative in §1.

**Cohort.** The 48,000 arithmetic is correct and competing terminals are not relabeled as \(Y=0\). The table still collapses the 3,600 activated-but-unlabeled cases into one row (“right-censored / delayed / missing-feed”) even though the rest of the design insists those mechanisms differ. Partner-originated permanent feed loss never appears as a row. The event window ending 31 Dec 2025 versus label as-of 15 Feb 2026 is not discussed, so it is unclear which activations/events after year-end exist in `applications_events.csv`.

**Censoring.** The taxonomy of mechanisms is good, and MNAR bounds plus transfer-linkage sensitivities are appropriate. Two inconsistencies weaken it. First, IPCW on \(\Pr(\text{label observed}\mid t_a,\text{source},\text{channel},\text{region})\) is the **primary** evaluation strategy in §3 (MAR) and only a **sensitivity** in §7; partner missingness is simultaneously described as MNAR, which would invalidate that IPCW. Second, still-open applications are called “right-censored” under a target that is **undefined** until activation—not censored default. That blurs the brief’s core distinction between ordinary censoring and outcomes that do not exist under the observed policy. Incomplete 90-day follow-up is never given a survival likelihood; in practice the primary analysis remains complete-case binary classification on the 21,600.

**Prefixes and features.** Pre-decision truncation, as-of timestamps, short-trace handling, loop retention, and migration rename/dedup are all sound. Workload and resource-experience caveats are correctly as-of. Underspecified: how `risk_score` is reconstructed if the export backfills the latest score onto earlier events (audit is mentioned, a repair is not); `documents_total` is “repair or drop” without a default.

**Leakage table.** This is the strongest section. Required fields are covered with keep/repair/drop and a rationale. Residual nits: no explicit row for using post-decision `Accepted`/limit-setting as a feature, and `label_available_at` as a **censoring instrument** is correct but easy to misuse as a split key without a declared as-of date.

**Validation.** The critique of random splits (policy, migration, maturity) is right, and splitting by case start with a train cut on 30 Jun 2025 is the correct policy boundary. The folds still do not specify a **label as-of date** distinct from the start-time window, so a Dec 2024 start that activates in Jan 2025 could contribute a label that would not have been in the warehouse at a realistic training freeze. Both text folds keep **all training data pre-policy**, so there is no deployment-like model that has seen the new threshold. Oct–Dec 2025 as a default-prediction test is poorly powered once 90d + 7–35d feed delay is applied; saying “or wait for a later extract” is an unmade design choice. AUROC/AUPRC/Brier/calibration are appropriate for a binary activated-account target; they are not sufficient if the 3,600 are to be used rather than dropped.

**Limitations.** Predictive vs selection vs counterfactual is stated clearly, and the four performativity channels are real. §8 then correctly narrows use to booked-portfolio management—which quietly retracts the §1 claim that the same estimand is the operational early-warning target for every application.

**Skeleton.** It is in range, uses real PM4Py surfaces, builds a case table, truncates at stop activities, drops leaky columns, splits by `case_start`, and refuses a fabricated survival API. It does **not** implement the design: no filter to `terminal_state=="activated"` and `label_observed`; no split of the 3,600 into not-yet-90d / delayed / suspected missing feed; `mature_by_time` is a blunt \(90+35\) day clock; `get_prefixes_from_log` then `groupby(case)` is unsafe if the function emits one trace per prefix length; `insert_case_service_waiting_time(..., start_timestamp_key=TS)` with complete-time = start-time makes service time identically zero, and treating `@@waiting_time` as a timedelta may be wrong if PM4Py stores seconds; weekly temporal features are computed on the full (including future) `df_pred`; the leakage `raise` inspects `df_pred` after columns were already dropped, so it cannot fail. These are not cosmetic.

The piece would be in the high 8s if the primary estimand were either (i) competing-risk paths from application start on all 48,000, or (ii) survival/binary default **from activation** with a declared booked-account use case, the 3,600 were partitioned, validation used start-time **and** label-as-of cuts, and the code materialized that cohort. As written, the conceptual core is right and the execution is leaky enough that a strict grader should not go higher.
