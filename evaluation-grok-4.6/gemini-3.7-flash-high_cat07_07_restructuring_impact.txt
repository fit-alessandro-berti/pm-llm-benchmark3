**6.8**

The answer is structurally complete, balanced, and substantively stronger than a typical response: it refuses a one-sided verdict, treats East as a distinct case, keeps fairness and learning in view, and will not endorse a full rollout. Under a strict rubric those are necessary, not sufficient. Several technical, conceptual, and specification-matching defects keep it well below “nearly flawless.”

**Mechanism map.** The required causal links are present and mostly well joined to observable measures (star-shaped handover topology, subcontracting reciprocity, waiting-time concentration on inbound shared-queue arcs, role-similarity collapse, tail/fairness splits). The Kingman citation is used as decoration more than analysis. Waiting time does not “trigger” at 85–90% utilization; \(W_q\) is continuous and already large at lower \(\rho\) when \(C_a, C_s\) are high. Legal/Risk at 91–93% is the real saturation fact; folding that into a generic 85–90% rule blurs the only capacity constraint that is already in the data. “Absence resilience” is drawn as a structural mechanism in the same column as ping-pong risk, which reads as if pooling is already a net resilience gain rather than a trade-off. The map is richer than required and slightly less disciplined than it looks.

**Scenario forecasts.** Optimistic / central / adverse cover every requested dimension and correctly let East improve while North/South can worsen. Two serious problems. First, the optimistic path smuggles interventions that are not in the proposal (cross-skilling technical specialists into Legal/Risk triage; rapid documentation standardization). That is not a forecast of *this* restructuring; it is a forecast of a different operating model. Second, many point estimates (East 6.9d / 15.2d, Legal wait 14.5h / 36h, reassignment 1.10 vs 1.45 vs 2.30, SLA 16.5%) are presented with false precision and no derivation from the given frequencies, waits, or utilizations. Management’s own table is not stress-tested against Kingman or against the already-saturated Legal/Risk pool except qualitatively.

**Indicators.** The leading/lagging split is the right idea and several operationalizations are usable (inflow/outflow, bounce-back, reassignment, priority gaming, p90/p99, segment SLA). Weaknesses: `local_expertise_flag` decline over 30 days is not a true leading indicator of deskilling; “role overlap index” 0.30/0.75 is not a PM4Py output and is undefined; several thresholds (1.15, 1.35, 8h, 88%) are arbitrary relative to the baseline table. `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` is never used despite being listed.

**Hidden risks.** Seven risks are named and they include the required set. Quality is uneven. “Simpson’s paradox” is misused for a simple mixture/aggregation effect. Informal desk-side advice is treated as if it were in the log and then vanished; the log cannot show unrecorded interactions, so this is a measurement-gap argument, not a PM finding. The section is conceptually right and slightly oversold.

**Pilot.** Scope, control, windows, segmentation, guardrails, feedback, and rollback are all present. Design flaws: pooling South *and* East while holding only North out confounds “East relief” with “South expertise loss,” which is exactly the heterogeneity the rest of the memo insists on. A cleaner first phase is East-only (or a stratified sample of complexity/specialty within one team). Burn-in exclusion is correct; 12+12+8 weeks is reasonable. Rollback triggers are specific but not tied back to pre-period variance, so they will false-alarm or miss a shift depending on seasonality. Shared Legal/Risk capacity is organization-wide; a two-team pilot still contaminates the North control if those queues are truly shared.

**Recommendation.** “Modify and pilot first” matches the constraint. The hybrid (local technical champion + pooled deep work, Legal/Risk intake filter, keep local supervisors) is coherent. It is also a different design from the one being forecast, and the $100k / 0.5 FTE / 20–80% split is invented without evidence. The “do not infer from early averages” caveats are the strongest part of the paper.

**PM4Py sketch.** This is the weakest required section and should have been the most precise. Defects:

- Several listed functions are absent: `filter_event_attribute_values`, `filter_variants`, `get_service_time`, `extract_temporal_features_dataframe`. Subgroup work is pandas `groupby`, not the PM4Py filters the prompt asked to demonstrate.
- Likely API errors: SNA objects expose `connections`, not `.matrix`; `insert_case_service_waiting_time(..., resource_column=...)` is not the usual signature; `get_variants` value type is version-dependent, and `len(x[1])` as case count is often wrong; performance-DFG values are not reliably `stat['mean']`/`stat['median']` dicts.
- Activity names in the sketch (`Local Caseworker`, `Shared Approver`, `Local Technical Assessment` as a from-node into `Specialist Review`) do not match the given `concept:name` list. The performance analysis would simply miss the arcs that matter.
- Unused imports (`dataframe_utils`, `sna`). “Density” is implemented as a nonzero count. No quantitative before/after network comparison (edge-weight deltas, reciprocity, centralization). `handover_reason`, `local_expertise_flag`, and `location` are almost unused in code despite being central to the narrative.
- Pre/post split by case start is reasonable; incomplete in-flight cases and the fact that shared queues affect the control team are ignored.

**Overall.** The memo would be a good internal briefing if the PM code were corrected, the optimistic scenario were constrained to the actual design, queueing claims were calibrated to the Legal/Risk numbers already given, and the pilot isolated East from North/South. Those are not polish items; they are the difference between a persuasive essay and a decision-grade forecast. Strictly, 6.8.
