8.2

The answer is strong, well structured, and mostly faithful to the case. It follows the required seven-section format, uses the timeline and conformance numbers accurately, and generally respects the core distinctions: not every deviation is failure, throughput is not resilience if controls vanish, and post-close audit work is not real-time compliance. The adaptation table, recovery model, indicator set, and management trade-off are decision-usable. It is not near-flawless.

**Factual / DFG errors**

The most important miss is adaptation G. The T2 DFG has two inbound arcs to `Dispatch without scan`: 1,800 from `Manual pack` and 1,450 from `Temporary release control` (3,250 total). The table cites only 1,800. That is not a rounding issue. It also hides a material finding: 1,450 of 1,650 temporary-release cases still left without a custody scan, so F did not contain G. Phone POD then captures only 2,600 of those 3,250 scanless dispatches; the residual gap is never discussed. Fast-lane leakage (1,000 → 920 inventory checks) is likewise ignored.

Hospital outcomes (91% fill, 8h → 10h median) are treated as evidence for A. The data support a *joint* effect of A, B, F, and I. The attribution is too clean.

**Classification problems**

Section 3 calls B, I, and K the “clearest” true resilience, with J resilient if governed. That overstates I. Deferral protected hospitals, but T2 pharmacy fill fell to 61%, wholesale median hit 96h, and cancellations rose to 7.8%. I is a capacity allocation with a fairness cost, not a compensating control on the same footing as manual authorization. The table correctly marks I as *modify*; the prose then promotes it. J is also placed in both the “true resilience” and “conditional” lists without resolving the tension.

K is necessary recovery work, not in-incident resilience. Grouping it with B blurs the same during/after distinction the answer otherwise handles well.

**Incomplete use of the given evidence**

Conformance fitness and fitting-trace rates are used well. The specified temporal deviations are not. `Backlog queue -> Manual pick list` exceeding baseline p95 is barely used. Highly variable `Dispatch without scan -> Phone proof of delivery` is mentioned only in passing. T3 is described qualitatively, which is fair given the missing T3 DFG, but the answer does not lean on the T2→T3→T4 *gradient* in exceptions (traceability 9.5% → 5.4% → 2.2%; dual-control 2.9% → 1.4% → 0.5%) as evidence that reconstruction debt decays slowly.

F is described as meaningful protection for controlled substances. Dual-control missing still rose from 0.2% to 2.9%. That can be reconciled with “checked all CS orders” only if the check was incomplete, poorly evidenced, or not dual-control. The answer does not confront that.

**Recovery design gaps**

The redesigned model is the best section: triggers, risk classes, non-waivable controls, evidence packet, backlog scoring, and a split between reconciliation and audit remediation are concrete and aligned with the case. Weak points remain.

Exit criteria (“no high-risk case remains open with missing critical evidence”) would have kept the firm in degraded mode through most of T4. That is conceptually pure and operationally unusable.

Hard-stops for authorization, temperature, dual-control, and custody are the right compliance default. For a hospital-critical cold-chain or CS order in a multi-day outage, the design offers no accountable emergency-release path (named dual sign-off, reason code, hold-and-investigate if evidence cannot be captured). The original F pattern was an attempt at that. Retiring C and G is correct; refusing any clinical override is an unresolved mission-versus-control trade-off.

Several trigger thresholds (15-minute WMS loss, 98% scan, 99% scan to exit) are asserted without hysteresis. Oscillation risk is ignored.

**What still works**

Retiring C and G, modifying A/D/E/F/H/I/J/L, and keeping B and K is the right keep/modify/retire pattern. The answer does not treat every baseline deviation as bad. The T4 audit tail (2,300 cases) is correctly read as hidden workload and false recovery. Indicators cover service protection, control completeness, backlog age, rework, and cross-segment fairness. The final trade-off paragraph is clear about what to accept, what not to accept, and what evidence would change the call.

**Net**

This is a high-quality advisory memo with one clear DFG miscount, one missed F→G interaction, a slightly too tidy resilience taxonomy, and a recovery model that is stricter than it is operable. Those are enough, under a strict reading, to keep it well short of excellent.
