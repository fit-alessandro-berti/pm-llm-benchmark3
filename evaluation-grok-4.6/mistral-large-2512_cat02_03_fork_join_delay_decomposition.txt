**4.5**

The answer follows the required heading structure and gets the *case-level arithmetic* largely right. That is not enough. Under a strict reading it fails several things the prompt treated as load-bearing: max-based counterfactuals, no double-counting of overlapping waits, downstream masking, and runnable reproduction. The headline claim that B dominates is correct; much of the supporting analysis is not.

**What is actually solid.** Section 1 defines the right clocks: `upstream = fork_release − arrival`, `queue_i = start_i − fork_release`, `service_i = end_i − start_i`, `join_ready = max_i end_i`, `sync_i = join_ready − end_i`, `downstream = final_start − join_ready`, `total = final_end − arrival`. The identity `fork_join = queue_i + service_i + sync_i` for every *i* is the correct way to keep one elapsed interval from being summed three times. The 12-row table and the four worked cases (F02, F04, F07, F11) match the log to the minute: critical branches A/B/C frequencies 2/8/2, no ties, totals 93–130. That core is competent process-mining bookkeeping.

**Aggregate statistics are wrong, and one error is conceptual.** B mean service is 37.5 min, not 38.3; C service is 20.17, not 20.5; B queue is 19.17, not 20.0; C queue is 10.42, not 10.8. Those look like sloppy rounding until the sync-wait column: reported A 18.3 / B 0.0 / C 18.3 versus actual 11.0 / 4.33 / 30.42. Setting B’s *mean* sync wait to 0 contradicts the author’s own definition and the 4/12 cases where B is not last (F01 5, F04 17, F08 20, F11 10). That is not a rounding issue; it treats “usually critical” as “never waits.” Mean sojourns (58.3, 31.3) inherit the same bias.

**Influence ranking of C above A is backwards.** Critical counts are tied (2/12). A’s sojourn is a flat 50 min and A’s slacks are 0, 5, 5, 7, 10, 10, 15, 15, 15, 25, 25 — often near-critical. C’s slacks are typically 22–52 min. High sync wait means C finished *early*; it is evidence *against* control of `join_ready`. The write-up instead cites C’s “long tail in synchronization wait” and, for F04, “only 18 min sync wait.” F04’s C is critical (`sync_C = 0`); 18 min is `downstream_queue`. That is a category error in the one example used to rank C over A. Tail contribution to `join_ready` was requested and not computed (B critical sojourns 55–75; A 50/50; C 57/55).

**Counterfactuals do not use max-based fork-join logic.**  
1. Cut A service 20% (50→40). Claimed −10 min on F01 and F11. On F01, B ends at 08:55, only 5 min behind A, so new `join_ready = 08:55` and the join saving is **5**, not 10. F11 is a true 10 min join saving (and a new A/B tie). “Secondary effects: none” is false once F-station occupancy is considered.  
2. Cut B queue 50%. Claimed a flat 10 min on all 8 B-critical cases. Queues on those cases are 20, 20, 25, 30, 30, 25, 20, 30. After the cut, A becomes (or ties) critical on F05, F06, F10; join savings are 10, 10, **10**, 15, 15, 12.5, **5**, 15 — not 8×10.  
3. Halving downstream is the only mechanically coherent lever, but it is treated as a capacity add without checking which waits are actually F1/F2 blocking.

**Downstream masking — required, and mishandled.** F1 is occupied F03→F05, F07→F09→F11 with zero idle; F2 is occupied F06→F08. F05, F08, F09, and F11 have `final_start` equal to the previous same-resource `final_end`. Speeding the critical branch on F05/F09/F11 cannot move `final_start` unless F policy or capacity changes; measured `downstream_queue` *grows* and **total flow time is unchanged**. The write-up says earlier B completion “may reduce downstream queueing,” which is the opposite of a fixed `final_start`. Several other downstream gaps (~8–15 min with a free server) look like policy or assignment, not a queue — never separated.

**Redesigns fight the scenario.** Redesign 1 pools B and C because they are “both administrative.” The prompt says A and B are technical and C is administrative. Redesign 2 “parallelizes A with B/C” and “reduces A’s effective service” by starting before `fork_release`. Branches already run in parallel after `fork_release`; A’s queue is already 0. That is a start-time / rule-change, not a service cut, and it violates the stated fork semantics. Neither redesign is the obvious pair (cut B queue/sojourn; fix F1 serialization and the unexplained 10–15 min release gaps). Pilot advice is generic.

**Section 6 would not run and would not estimate what it claims.** `apply_counterfactual` is called on `result_df`, which has no `A_start`, `B_end`, `C_end`, `fork_release`, or `final_start` — immediate `KeyError` on all three CFs. Even on a merged frame, holding `final_start` fixed makes  
`new_flow = upstream + (new_join − fork) + (final_start − new_join) + final_service`  
independent of `new_join`, so branch CFs print **zero** savings. `discover_performance_dfg` returns three objects; unpacking into two raises. Performance-DFG values are stat dicts, not floats, so `dur:.1f` fails. `format_dataframe` is called with no keys (may work only because of pre-named columns). Interval rows omit arrival/fork. The required comment that DFG semantics do not recover synchronization wait is a two-line epilogue, not attached to the custom `max` logic. Compact prints are sketched, not evidenced.

**Smaller cuts.** Tie-break is first of `[A,B,C]` and is fine (no ties exist) but never stated as a rule in the prose. Fork-join elapsed time is defined and then dropped from every table. n=12, one day, minute granularity: uncertainty is asserted in §7 more than used in §3–5. §7 itself is the second-best section (causality, hidden capacities, preemption, queue vs release policy).

**Net.** Correct per-case decomposition and the right winner (B) keep this out of the failure band. Wrong aggregates, inverted sync-wait reasoning, non-max counterfactuals, ignored F-congestion masking, a scenario-contradicting redesign, and non-executable “reproducible” code are each independently disqualifying for a high score. This is a coherent first draft, not a finished analysis.
