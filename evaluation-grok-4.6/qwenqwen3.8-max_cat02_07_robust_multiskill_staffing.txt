**6.8**

The write-up hits the required format, uses the tables in an auditable way, and does not pick a winner from base-case mean delay. The main reason it is not a high score is that the quantitative story is not internally consistent, and several implementation choices quietly undo claims made in the prose.

**What is actually strong**

Capacity work is the best part. Mixture means, block loads, and speed-adjusted skill capacities check out (e.g. 16:00–18:00 software ~92 vs ~90; no mechanical capacity after 18:00; day total ~2721 base minutes vs 2490 regular / 2670 with OT). Treating skill capacity as a *non-additive upper bound* is the right caveat. U1–U5 bottleneck migration is reasoned, not just restated. Assumptions A1–A7 are labeled. The two objectives are distinct (tail/SLA vs max-regret + fairness). The three policies are operational: eligibility, score, reservation, preemption, parallelization, overflow. Hybrid plus a crisis mode, with numeric triggers, is a defensible robust choice. Secondary effects and the PM design (especially “PM4Py case service/waiting is not a stage metric”) are competent.

**Where it fails a strict reading**

1. **Section 4 does not follow from Section 2.** After arguing the day is already over capacity (~2721–3000 min of work vs 2490), the table still gives base mean delay 165–210 and Hybrid P1 SLA 94%. If even a modest share of P3 (or late M/ME) sits overnight, mean delay is pulled toward several hundred minutes and p95 is not ~500. Those ranges are invented priors, not model output, and they conflict with the author’s own load bound.

2. **The comparison omits the Hybrid’s actual trade-off.** P3 SLA and completion/censoring rates never appear in the table or the Python metrics, even though reservation is sold as starving P3. Without that, “robust” is only P1-robust.

3. **Code does not implement the policies as specified.** Overtime is unconditionally available to every resource after shift end, so the claimed OT/reservation differences are mostly commentary. Incomplete cases are censored at 1440 with no completion-rate column, which will dominate means under U1/U5 and is never reconciled with the table. The 09:30–10:00 outage freezes the resource on Intake instead of blocking only Intake. Preempted work is logged as one interval from first start to final end, so `get_service_time` and handover analyses mix queue time into service; the `active_service` column does not fix the PM4Py path.

4. **PM4Py usage is a scaffold, not a demonstration.** `format_dataframe` is not given a start-timestamp (or resource) key; `insert_case_service_waiting_time` / `get_service_time` signatures are guessed and wrapped in `try/except`. That fails the spirit of “call these APIs and interpret them correctly.”

5. **Smaller but real issues.** SPEC’s home-skill map (Farah mechanical-only; Chen electrical-only) is an extra assumption that makes SPEC look worse than “fixed queues with qualified servers.” Setup is charged on any diagnostic skill change, not clearly on *skill family*. Three seeds are thin. The resilience column is tautological (Hybrid always High). Objective 1’s math notation is garbled. Parallel coordination is only waiting time, never resource time—fine if labeled, but it understates ME/ES cost when the system is already short of minutes.

**Bottom line:** this is a serious, well-structured operations analysis with real process-mining literacy. It is not nearly flawless. The capacity arithmetic and policy design would support a much higher score if the scenario table, SLA claims, and simulator had been forced to tell the same story.
