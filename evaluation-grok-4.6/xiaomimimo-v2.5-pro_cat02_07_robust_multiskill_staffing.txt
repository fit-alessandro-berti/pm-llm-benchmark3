**3.2**

The submission never becomes the required artifact. After a long planning dump it stops mid-design: there is no Markdown document with the nine mandated headings, no implementable policy specification, no policy-by-scenario table, no recommendation with mode-switch triggers, no monitoring/PM4Py design, and no Python. Under a rubric that scores the finished answer—not the scratchpad—this is a non-delivery with useful fragments.

What is present is intermediate capacity reasoning, not a completed analysis. Some of that reasoning is directionally competent: evening software is pinned on Emil after 16:00; mechanical work after 18:00 has no qualified resource; Chen’s absence collapses the only afternoon software pair; U1/U4 load inflation and U2 renormalization are at least interpreted explicitly. Those points would have been the backbone of Section 2.

They are not enough, and they are not clean.

**Calculation and modeling defects**

- Parallel ME elapsed time is stated two incompatible ways: \(\max(26.3,32.82)+8=40.82\), then later “~41 + 8 = 49”. Coordination is counted once, then again. That is a definitional error, not a rounding issue.
- Aggregate “2720 base-minutes vs 2535 available ⇒ 7% overloaded” treats base minutes as actual minutes and assumes every task can run at multiplier 1.00. Software cannot: Chen is 1.25, Farah is 1.40, Emil is the only 1.00 and is off until 12:00. The “minimum resource-minutes” figure is infeasible.
- The 65 min/case figure is total *resource* work (both ME/ES branches counted). It is then used as if it were in-block elapsed load (14–16 at 114%, 16–18 at 186%). That is an upper bound presented as utilization. Carryover and next-day P3 completion are mentioned later, so the same text contradicts itself on whether block capacity is a hard constraint.
- Block 12:00–14:00 available minutes is written as 600, then 480, then 510. The last is right; the trail is not a settled analysis.
- Preemption is never given a single operational definition (restart from zero vs remaining \(\times 1.10\) vs wasted elapsed + 10% of remaining). A simulation or SLA claim cannot be audited from that.
- U5’s 09:30–10:00 intake freeze is named and not sized. A 30-minute intake outage during a 1.7× morning surge is one of the few shocks that can be bounded from the given tables; it is left as a label.
- Policy 1’s primary assignments drift (Ben as mechanical specialist vs intake/validation utility). “Strict specialization” is not a dispatch rule.

**What the rubric required and is missing**

Section 1 never freezes distributions, parallelism rule, or two complete objective functions. Section 3 never specifies eligibility, priority score, reservation, preemption, parallelization, handover minimization, and overflow triggers as operator-auditable procedures. Section 4’s required table is absent, so mean/tail delay, SLA, utilization, fairness, handover burden, and resilience are not compared on one scale. Section 5 has no primary policy, fallback, or numeric triggers. Section 8 is a commented scaffold that does not run, does not emit an event log, and does not call PM4Py. Section 9 is not written.

**Uncertainty and process-mining competence**

The notes distinguish some observations from hypotheses, which is a credit. They do not produce bounds or scenario estimates that a judge can check against a single set of assumptions. There is no process-mining design: no event attributes, no stage-specific waiting vs service split, no handover-network or drift analysis.

**Bottom line**

A hypercritical reading cannot treat unfinished working notes as a robust policy comparison. The bottleneck identification is the only part that would survive into a real answer, and even that is compromised by the double-counted coordination delay, the infeasible 1.00-everywhere load, and the misuse of summed branch work as elapsed block load. Incomplete plus internally inconsistent is a low score; the 3.2 reflects residual credit for the skill/time-block bottleneck map, not for meeting the task.
