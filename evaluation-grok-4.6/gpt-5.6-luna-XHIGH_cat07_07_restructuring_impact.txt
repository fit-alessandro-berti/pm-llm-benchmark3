**8.0**

The answer is structurally complete, balanced, and unusually well aligned with the prompt: it does not prejudge the redesign, treats East as a different case from North/South, refuses to treat mean cycle time as success, and conditions rollout on a pilot with guardrails. Those are the hard requirements, and they are met. The remaining score is held down by avoidable technical errors, missing quantification, and a few analytical thin spots that a strict reading cannot ignore.

**What is strong.** The mechanism table is the best part of the piece. It correctly refuses management’s “fewer handovers” story: caseworker–technical transfers rise from 36 to 41 per 100 and the return leg from 31 to 36, so a simpler org chart can still produce more recorded queue-mediated interactions. The distinction between fewer organizational boundaries and fewer case-level transfers is the right conceptual cut. Legal/Risk at 93%/91% is used properly as a hard constraint on the 15% utilization claim. The three scenarios are internally consistent and cover every required dimension. The hidden-risk section is broader than asked and includes measurement gaming and common-mode failure, which most answers miss. The recommendation is the only defensible one given the evidence: modified pilot first, do not pull the local supervisor in wave one, do not read early averages as success.

**Technical inaccuracies in §7.** The PM4Py sketch is the weakest section and is not “nearly flawless.” `pm4py.filter_variants(..., variants_filter=selected_cases)` is simply the wrong API: that function filters activity-sequence variants, not case IDs. The comment “adapt to version” does not salvage a conceptual mistake. Case-level segmentation should be a dataframe filter or `filter_trace_attribute_values`. `split_log` is defined and then barely used. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` / `get_service_time` typically need start/complete lifecycle events; the given log only has a single `time:timestamp`. The prose later prefers explicit queue-entry/acceptance events, which is the right instinct, but the code still calls the lifecycle APIs as if they will work. A long list of undefined helpers (`normalize_pm4py_network_to_edges`, `build_case_activity_durations`, `make_case_metrics`, `report_adjusted_gaps`) is acceptable in a sketch only if the *PM4Py* calls themselves are right. They are not, consistently.

**Missing quantification that the data already support.** The forecast stays qualitative where a short capacity argument was available and material. Pooling N/S/E tech (71/68/82% on 820/760/520 cases) implies a workload-weighted utilization near 73%; a 15% lift is then about 84% for the tech pool and is *impossible* as a blanket target for Legal/Risk. Legal/Risk volume is also planned to rise from 9 to 12 per 100 cases (~33%) from a 91–93% base. At that occupancy, wait is nonlinear; the adverse scenario says “unstable” but never makes the obvious point that the queue can go over capacity without new hours. Combining technical, legal/risk, and hard QC in one service is a multi-class queue with heterogeneous service times; that is named as “competing streams” and then not developed (priority, starvation of hard cases, FIFO unfairness). Those are not polish items. They are the mechanism.

**Under-used SNA diagnostics.** The prompt listed handover, working-together, and subcontracting as distinct instruments. The code compares all three; the narrative mostly collapses them into “hub-and-spoke.” Subcontracting is the network that should light up if the shared service does work *for* local teams and returns it; working-together should show higher hub density and weaker local cohesion. Role discovery should be used to test whether the local tech role disappears rather than just whether loops get shorter. That interpretation is thin.

**Pilot design is directionally right, operationally soft.** Stepped-wedge / DiD, keep the supervisor out of wave one, stratify by team/complexity/specialty/segment, and pre-register tail guardrails are all correct. Volumes are known (520–820 cases/month) and high-complexity/specialty cells will be sparse; “set minimum sample sizes” and “extend if sparse” is not a design. Which location loses the supervisor is never specified, even though that is itself a fairness treatment. Rollback triggers are qualitative (“materially worsen”) rather than thresholded.

**Smaller unclarities.** The “mechanism map” is a well-qualified action–effect table, not a causal map with mediators and feedback (local skill loss → more referrals → worse hub congestion is stated in the adverse scenario, not drawn as a loop in §1). Staff-survey “leading indicators” are mixed into a table billed as process-mining measures. Ownership-continuity and wait-to-service ratio are good ideas but require event semantics the current log does not have; that dependency is acknowledged late and should have been a first-class instrumentation requirement in §1.

**Net.** This is a strong policy-and-measurement memo with the right recommendation and the right refusal to worship averages. It is not a 9+: the PM4Py section contains a real API/conceptual error, the capacity math that would make the forecast sharp is missing, and the three organizational networks are not used as separately interpretable evidence. Under the requested standard, that is an 8, not higher.
