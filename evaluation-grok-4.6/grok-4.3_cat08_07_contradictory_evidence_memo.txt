The answer is structurally complete and lands on a defensible option (E), but it is not close to flawless. Several errors are material, not cosmetic.

**Alternative C is misread.** C explicitly *suspends* survey skipping and post-billing activation. The memo claims the opposite: that C “leaves survey skipping and post-billing activation untouched.” That inverts the alternative, so the risk case, the “reasonable if” condition, and the C-versus-E comparison are all invalid. C is the main competitor to E; getting it backwards is a serious synthesis failure.

**Process-mining language is wrong.** High fitness and low precision do not mean the model “overfits the simplified coordinator path.” Low precision means the model is too permissive. The pilot’s *lower generalization* (0.56 vs 0.70) is the overfitting-adjacent signal, and it is ignored.

**Section 5 does not do what was asked.** M1–M8 are listed in a rough order, but almost none explain how the result would *change the decision*. M5 is ranked last on a weak “do it after hybrid is running” rationale even though coordinator absence and 93% utilization are already first-order reasons not to scale ownership blindly.

**Thresholds are sloppy.** Comparison operators are missing. For a simple-house-only cohort that already beat baseline, p90 ≤ baseline+2 days, complaints ≤ 7%, and FTR ≥ 80% are loose. “Four consecutive weeks” plus “full 60-day cost” is temporally incoherent unless sequenced. No gates are set on second visits, manual activation, billing-before-activation, or permit conformance.

**Facts are thinner and leakier than they look.** The “table” is three lists. Business customers are folded into the 12–25 day / 5–7 pp tail story; their actual deltas are +5 days and +4 pp. “Manual activation doubled” is 7% → 15%. Mix bias (pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings), incomplete attributes before week 3, cost truncation after week 4, DFG shifts, temporal profiles, and generalization are underused. Resilience and cost-shifting are presented more cleanly than the data quality that should qualify them.

**Other analytical shortcuts.** D is dismissed as “repeating coverage gaps,” which is the opposite of D’s purpose. Hybrid E is narrowed to “simple houses only” without saying what happens to the other ~66% of volume. Resource reallocation (survey 78% → 52%, second-visit 66% → 92%) is barely used.

The recommendation not to scale nationally or roll back wholesale is correct, and the five metric conflicts are named. That is the floor of a competent memo, not a high score. The C error, the precision/overfit mistake, the hollow VoI section, and the unusable rollout gates keep this well below a strong mark.
