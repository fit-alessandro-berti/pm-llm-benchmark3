# 1. Data audit and estimands

### Operational Context, System Boundary, and Units
The system under analysis is a single-stage, multi-server service center operating under fixed daily opening hours: 07:00 to 19:00 (12 operational hours = 720 operational minutes per calendar day). Outside these hours (19:00 to 07:00 next day), the center is closed; no processing occurs, but cases may persist in queue overnight.

The supplied data covers 28 consecutive days (2026-04-06 through 2026-05-03, exactly four calendar weeks). The operational quantities and their physical dimensions are:
- **`arrivals` ($A_t$)**: Total case intake count during opening hours on day $t$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{day}^{-1}$]. Mean arrival intensity across operating hours is $\lambda_t = A_t / 720$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{min}^{-1}$] or $A_t / 12$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{hr}^{-1}$].
- **`completed` ($C_t$)**: Total completed case departures during opening hours on day $t$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{day}^{-1}$]. Mean completion intensity across operating hours is $\mu_t = C_t / 720$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{min}^{-1}$] or $C_t / 12$ [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{hr}^{-1}$].
- **`net_flow` ($\Delta N_t = A_t - C_t$)**: Daily flow imbalance [$\text{cases}\cdot\text{day}^{-1}$]. A positive value indicates queue growth; a negative value indicates queue drain.
- **`median_service_min` ($S_{50,t}$) and `p90_service_min` ($S_{90,t}$)**: The 50th and 90th percentiles of active execution duration per case handled on day $t$ [$\text{minutes}$]. Crucially, this measures only *touch time* (active work by staff), completely excluding queue waiting time.
- **`mean_wip` ($L_t$)**: Time-weighted average Work-in-Progress inventory sampled from 48 discrete 15-minute snapshots across the 12-hour opening window [$\text{cases}$].
- **`staff_fte` ($c_t$)**: Concurrently active Full-Time Equivalent capacity [$\text{FTE}$]. Maximum nominal labor minutes supplied per day is $c_t \times (720 - \text{outage\_min}_t)$ [$\text{staff-minutes}$].
- **`complex_pct`**: Proportion of day $t$'s arrivals routed to a multi-step, high-touch variant [$\%$].
- **`outage_min`**: Unscheduled infrastructure downtime during opening hours [$\text{minutes}$]. During outages, active processing ceases ($\mu_t \to 0$), though arrivals continue to queue.
- **`peak_08_10_pct`**: Reported percentage of daily arrivals occurring between 08:00 and 09:59 [$\%$].

### Data Audit Findings and Limitations of Aggregates
1. **Discrepancy in Peak Hour Definition**: The hourly arrival vectors reveal an apparent reporting shift. For ordinary days (e.g., 2026-04-07: arrivals of $[3, 5, 12, 20, 16, \dots]$), the reported 31% peak matches indices 2 and 3 (09:00–11:00, where $12 + 20 = 32$, i.e., $32/101 = 31.7\%$) rather than indices 1 and 2 (08:00–10:00, where $5 + 12 = 17$, i.e., $16.8\%$). On 2026-04-21, arrivals spike at index 3 (10:00–11:00 with 34 arrivals) and index 6 (13:00–14:00 with 24 arrivals). Analysts must treat `peak_08_10_pct` as an indicator of morning front-loading rather than an exact literal 08:00–09:59 window.
2. **Missing Boundary States (07:00 Backlog)**: The exact queue length at 07:00 opening ($Q_{07:00}$) is unrecorded. Although no processing occurs overnight, cases carry over. The daily cumulative sum $\sum (A_t - C_t)$ tracks relative inventory drift but cannot determine absolute zero-inventory states without snapshot calibration.
3. **Quantiles versus Means for Capacity Modeling**: Service duration is summarized solely by $S_{50}$ and $S_{90}$. In right-skewed service distributions, the arithmetic mean $\mathbb{E}[S]$ strictly exceeds the median $S_{50}$. Modeling server utilization using median service time underestimates required labor hours by 10% to 25%.
4. **Coarseness of 15-Minute WIP Snapshots**: Snapshot averages smooth out high-frequency queue spikes that occur during bursty arrival hours, masking peak queue buffer saturation.

### Five Major Threats to Naive Inference
1. **Threat 1: Conflating Active Service Time with Flow Time (Cycle Time)**. Naively using `median_service_min` (24–36 min) as a proxy for total case turnaround time ($W$) ignores queue waiting time. In congested regimes, waiting time dominates total sojourn time by a factor of 5 to 10 (flow times reach 200–350 minutes).
2. **Threat 2: Non-Conservation of Daily Flow and Cumulative Carryover**. Assuming each daily record represents a self-contained, closed queueing system is invalid. Over the 28-day window, total arrivals (2,519) exceed completions (2,409) by 110 cases. Between 2026-04-20 and 2026-04-28, daily arrivals systematically outpaced completions by 10 to 16 cases every single day, causing cumulative backlog to soar to +128 cases. Departures on day $t$ contain cohorts that arrived on days $t-1, t-2$, or earlier.
3. **Threat 3: Simpson's Paradox / Case-Mix Confounding of Labor Productivity**. A naive aggregate comparison shows median service time increasing from 24 min on 2026-04-06 to 36 min on 2026-04-27 (+50%), while daily completions per FTE dropped from 16.7 to 14.7. An uninformed reviewer might claim staff productivity declined. In reality, the proportion of complex multi-step cases doubled from ~19% to ~47%. Staff were executing fundamentally more demanding tasks.
4. **Threat 4: Saturated Bottleneck Capacity Interpreted as Demand Ceiling**. Completed counts remained flat at 94–106 cases/day throughout 2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28 despite arrivals surging to 106–121 cases/day. Naive regression would infer stable demand absorption; queueing theory reveals a saturated multi-server bottleneck operating at 100% utilization, where completions reflect maximum server throughput, not customer demand.
5. **Threat 5: Outage and Post-Outage Recovery Distorting Natural Queue Dynamics**. On 2026-04-15, an unscheduled 180-minute outage cut completions to 58 (net flow +47). On 2026-04-16, completions reached 129 (the dataset peak) with 7.0 FTE. Treating 2026-04-16 as an ordinary operational day creates severe bias: the 129 completions were only possible because an enormous queue of 52 cases was pre-buffered at opening, eliminating server starvation, combined with an unrepeated emergency staffing injection.

# 2. Primary regime segmentation

The primary segmentation is an event-driven chronological decomposition that isolates exogenous structural shocks, calendar cycles (weekdays vs. weekends), capacity disruptions, and managerial interventions.

### Operational Phases and Regime Definitions
1. **Regime 1 (R1): Baseline Equilibrium (2026-04-06 to 2026-04-14, 9 days)**
   - *R1-WD (Baseline Weekdays, 7 days)*: Apr 06–10, Apr 13–14. Homogeneous demand ($92\text{--}107$ arr/day, mean 100.1), stable completions (mean 99.6), balanced daily net flow ($\text{mean } +0.6$), low complex mix ($18\text{--}24\%$, mean 20.9%), low service times ($S_{50}=24\text{--}26$, $S_{90}=44\text{--}50$), standard staffing (5.5–6.0 FTE), WIP stable at 18–23.
   - *R1-WE (Baseline Weekends, 2 days in W1, plus Apr 18–19 post-recovery)*: Low volume ($36\text{--}48$ arr/day, mean 41.8), staffing scaled down to 2.5 FTE, WIP 12–16, zero drift.
2. **Regime 2 (R2): IT Outage Shock (2026-04-15, 1 day)**
   - Unscheduled 180-minute system outage (11:00–14:00). Normal arrivals (105), but throughput suppressed to 58 completions. Acute flow dislocation: daily net flow $+47$ cases. WIP spiked to 47.
3. **Regime 3 (R3): Post-Outage Recovery / Flushing (2026-04-16 to 2026-04-17, 2 days)**
   - Transient recovery window. Emergency staffing deployed (7.0 FTE Thu, 6.5 FTE Fri). High carryover backlog allowed continuous zero-starvation processing: completions surged to 129 and 112 (mean 120.5), draining 30 backlogged cases (net flow $-17$ and $-13$). WIP dropped from 52 to 35. Service times slightly elevated ($S_{50}=29\text{--}33$, $S_{90}=60\text{--}72$) due to handling aged, congested cases.
4. **Regime 4 (R4): Case-Mix Surge and Capacity Overload (2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28, 9 days)**
   - *R4-WD (Overload Weekdays, 7 days)*: Apr 20–24, Apr 27–28. Severe non-stationary bottleneck. Complex cases surged to $41\text{--}47\%$ (mean 44.6%). Median service time increased to $34\text{--}36$ min (mean 35.0 min, +40%), and $S_{90}$ escalated to $78\text{--}88$ min (+77%). Morning peaking rose to $40\text{--}50\%$. Nominal staffing remained at baseline (5.5–6.0 FTE). Demand ($106\text{--}121$ arr/day, mean 114.0) exceeded maximum capacity ($94\text{--}106$ comp/day, mean 100.6). Net accumulation averaged $+13.4\text{ cases}\cdot\text{day}^{-1}$, inflating WIP from 37 to 50. Total cumulative backlog built up by $+94$ cases.
   - *R4-WE (Overload Weekend, 2 days)*: Apr 25–26. Weekend demand (43–51 arr/day) also exhibited higher complexity (40–42%). At 2.5 FTE, the center could only complete 39–43 cases, adding $+12$ cases of backlog over the weekend.
5. **Regime 5 (R5): Staffing Intervention & Backlog Burn-Down (2026-04-29 to 2026-05-03, 5 days)**
   - *R5-WD (Intervention Weekdays, 3 days)*: Apr 29 – May 01. Managerial response: capacity boosted to 7.0 FTE (Wed/Thu) and 6.5 FTE (Fri). While arrival volume (109–120) and complex mix (43–45%) remained elevated, completions expanded to $116\text{--}126$ cases/day (mean 121.0), achieving negative net flow ($-5, -6, -7$; total $-18$ cases). WIP dropped from 50 to 32.
   - *R5-WE (Intervention Weekend, 2 days)*: May 02–03. Weekend capacity augmented to 3.0 FTE (+20%). Completions (47–51, mean 49.0) fully balanced arrivals (45–53, mean 49.0). Net flow zero; WIP stabilized at 19–22.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

### State-Space and Drift-Phase Decomposition
An operationally defensible alternative to chronological segmentation is a **State-Space / Flow-Drift Segmentation**. Instead of tracking calendar time, this model partitions operational days by their underlying dynamical queue state: the instantaneous drift velocity ($\Delta N_t = A_t - C_t$), backlog inventory stress ($L_t$), and capacity deployment ($c_t$).

Under this framework, days are assigned to one of five functional states:
1. **State S1: Acute Infrastructure Disruption** (`outage_min` $> 0$). Defined by supply collapse ($C_t \ll A_t$ driven by external outage). Includes 2026-04-15 (1 day).
2. **State S2: Active Backlog Flush / High-WIP Surge Processing** ($\Delta N_t \le -5\text{ cases}$, $c_t \ge 6.5\text{ FTE}$, $L_t \ge 30$). Days where management actively over-allocates labor to draw down an existing queue. This pools the post-outage recovery (2026-04-16, 2026-04-17) and the late-April intervention weekdays (2026-04-29, 2026-04-30, 2026-05-01) into a single functional regime ($N = 5\text{ days}$).
3. **State S3: Super-Critical Accumulation / Capacity Deficit** ($\Delta N_t \ge +10\text{ cases}$, $c_t \le 6.0\text{ FTE}$, $L_t \ge 35$). Days where arrival workload strictly exceeds nominal processing capacity, causing deterministic queue growth. Encompasses all weekdays from 2026-04-20 through 2026-04-28 ($N = 7\text{ days}$).
4. **State S4: Balanced Nominal Weekday Operations** ($|\Delta N_t| \le 3\text{ cases}$, $L_t \in [18, 23]$, $c_t \approx 6.0\text{ FTE}$). Days in stationary flow equilibrium under standard case mix. Covers baseline weekdays (2026-04-06 to 2026-04-10, 2026-04-13, 2026-04-14; $N = 7\text{ days}$).
5. **State S5: Weekend Operations** ($A_t < 60$, $c_t \in [2.5, 3.0]$). Low-volume weekend scale across all four weeks ($N = 8\text{ days}$).

### Operational Comparison of Segmentations
- **Operational Narrative of Alternative Model**: The alternative model views the service center through the lens of nonlinear dynamics and queueing stability theory. It asserts that the system's behavior is fundamentally determined by whether it is in an *attractor state* (S4, S5), an *unstable runaway state* (S3), a *clearing state* (S2), or an *externally perturbed state* (S1).
- **Strengths of Alternative Model**:
  - By pooling all surge-staffed backlog-draining days into S2 ($N=5$), it achieves higher statistical sample size to estimate the maximum system throughput under augmented capacity ($\mu_{\max} \approx 120.8\text{ cases/day}$ at 6.8 FTE).
  - It clearly delineates stable vs. unstable drift boundaries, which directly informs queue-length threshold policies.
- **Weaknesses and Critical Blind Spots of Alternative Model**:
  - *Conflates Heterogeneous Workloads*: State S2 merges post-outage recovery (Apr 16–17, complex mix 26.0%, median service 31 min) with late-intervention recovery (Apr 29–May 01, complex mix 44.0%, median service 32.7 min). In doing so, it hides the fact that the second recovery achieved 121 completions/day despite handling substantially more complex cases, obscuring underlying workflow differences.
  - *Violates Time-Series Continuity*: Slicing days out of chronological order obscures the cumulative inventory trajectory ($A_t - C_t$), which is the physical driver of overnight carryover.

### Three Critical Distortions Created by Pooled Analysis

1. **Weekday/Weekend Aggregation Conceals Structural Bimodality**:
   - The pooled mean arrival rate is $90.0\text{ cases/day}$ with a standard deviation of $30.0$. However, the distribution is strictly bimodal: no single day experiences ~90 arrivals. Weekdays average $106.6\text{ arrivals/day}$ ($\text{range } 92\text{--}121$), while weekends average $44.9\text{ arrivals/day}$ ($\text{range } 36\text{--}53$).
   - Concurrently, staffing drops from 5.5–7.0 FTE on weekdays to 2.5–3.0 FTE on weekends. A single pooled queueing model parameterizing $\lambda = 90/12 = 7.5\text{ cases/hr}$ and $c = 5.1\text{ FTE}$ creates an artificial, non-existent operating regime. It severely overestimates weekend queueing and vastly underestimates weekday congestion.

2. **The Case-Mix Shift and Simpson's Paradox / Productivity Fallacy**:
   - The pooled median service time is $31.0\text{ min}$ ($S_{90} = 67.1\text{ min}$). But this aggregates two completely different case-mix epochs: the Baseline Era (complex mix $20.9\%$, $S_{50} = 25.0\text{ min}$, $S_{90} = 47.0\text{ min}$) and the Overload Era (complex mix $44.6\%$, $S_{50} = 35.0\text{ min}$, $S_{90} = 83.0\text{ min}$).
   - Under pooled metrics, completions per FTE appear to plummet from 16.9 cases/FTE-day in early April to 14.7 cases/FTE-day in late April. Management might erroneously penalize staff for "slacking" or inefficiency.
   - Segmented analysis demonstrates that the 40% jump in median handling time and 77% jump in $S_{90}$ was driven by the doubling of the complex case variant. In fact, staff maintained maximal utilization throughout R4-WD, operating at the physical ceiling of multi-server capacity.

3. **Conflating Outage Recovery with Sustainable Throughput**:
   - The pooled completion average is $86.0\text{ cases/day}$. An unsegmented capacity audit would note that the service center reached 129 completions on 2026-04-16 and 126 on 2026-04-30. Naive regression would conclude the facility has ample headroom to handle 120+ cases/day under standard staffing.
   - Segmented analysis proves that daily throughput exceeding 110 cases was achieved *only* when two transient conditions coincided: (1) temporary emergency staffing of 6.5–7.0 FTE, and (2) massive queue inventory ($L_t \ge 43$) that completely eliminated server idle time / starvation. Under normal 6.0 FTE staffing, sustained capacity for high-complexity cases is hard-capped at $\approx 100\text{ cases/day}$. The pooled model obscures this critical capacity boundary.

### Mathematical Significance of Dispersion and Burstiness Metrics
1. **Fano Factor / Index of Dispersion ($D = \sigma^2 / \mu$)**:
   - For a standard homogeneous Poisson arrival process, $D = 1.0$.
   - **2026-04-07 (Baseline)** exhibits $D = 3.23$. This overdispersion is primarily driven by the smooth deterministic diurnal curve: arrivals rise from 3 at 07:00 to 20 at 10:00–11:00, then gently taper to 4 at 18:00. This is non-homogeneous Poisson behavior with high positive serial correlation ($r_1 = +0.675$).
   - **2026-04-21 (Post-Mix Change Weekday)** exhibits extreme, pathological burstiness: **$D = 10.12$** and $\text{CV} = 1.03$. The arrival stream does not follow a smooth unimodal curve. Instead, it displays multiple massive, erratic shocks: $[2, 3, 8, \mathbf{34}, 8, 5, \mathbf{24}, 5, 3, \mathbf{14}, 5, 4]$. In a single hour (10:00–11:00), 34 cases arrive—nearly 30% of the entire day's demand.

### Why Four Representative Days Cannot Establish a Full Stochastic Model
1. **Severe Small-Sample Censoring**: Four days represent only $14.3\%$ of the 28-day sample. None of the four days represents the post-outage recovery regime (Apr 16–17) or the intervention regime (Apr 29–May 03).
2. **Unobserved Intraday Invariance**: We cannot determine whether the multi-peak burstiness observed on 2026-04-21 was a daily feature of the entire R4 overload era or an isolated idiosyncratic occurrence on that specific Tuesday.
3. **Unrecorded Micro-Timestamps**: The hourly vectors aggregate arrivals into 60-minute bins. Intraday batch arrivals occurring within sub-minute intervals (e.g., 20 cases dropped at 10:00:01) cannot be differentiated from uniformly spaced arrivals across the hour, yet they create vastly different transient queue lengths and server idling probabilities.
4. **Unobserved Carryover Initialization**: Without true 07:00 case counts, intraday queue curves must be anchored to daily mean WIP rather than observed boundary states.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

### Mathematical Foundations and Prerequisites
Little's Law, expressed as $L = \lambda W$, states that the long-term time-average number of items in a stationary queueing system ($L$) equals the long-term average arrival rate ($\lambda$) multiplied by the average time a case spends in the system ($W$).

Strict application of Little's Law requires three mathematical boundary conditions:
1. **Conservation of Flow**: Over the observation window, all arriving cases must eventually depart: total arrivals must equal total completions ($\sum A_t \approx \sum C_t$). The system must neither experience unbounded accumulation ($\rho > 1$) nor drain historical pre-existing backlog ($\sum C_t \gg \sum A_t$).
2. **Stationarity / Ergodicity**: The underlying stochastic arrival and service processes must have stable probability distributions with constant mean rates over the window.
3. **System Boundary Consistency**: The physical boundary where $L$ is measured must coincide exactly with the boundary where $\lambda$ and $W$ are defined.

In our setting, the service center operates 12 hours/day (720 operating minutes/day). When applying Little's Law over a multi-day window with $D$ operating days:
$$\text{Throughput Proxy } \lambda^* = \frac{\text{Total Cases}}{D \times 720\text{ min}} \quad [\text{cases/min}]$$
$$\text{Implied Flow Time } W = \frac{\bar{L}}{\lambda^*} = \frac{\bar{L} \times 720 \times D}{\text{Total Cases}} \quad [\text{operating minutes}]$$
Flow time in operating hours is $W_{\text{hr}} = W / 60$. Flow time represents *total system sojourn time* (queue waiting time + active service time). It must NEVER be equated with `median_service_min`.

# 7. Operational implications

### 1. Regime-Specific Staffing Policies
- **Baseline Conditions (Complex Mix $\le 25\%$)**: Weekday staffing of 6.0 FTE provides $6.0 \times 720 = 4,320\text{ staff-minutes}$. At baseline workload (~28 min/case), 100 cases require ~2,800 labor minutes, yielding a healthy utilization $\rho \approx 65\%$. Weekend staffing of 2.5 FTE ($1,800\text{ staff-minutes}$) comfortably supports ~42 cases.
- **Structural Overload Conditions (Complex Mix $\ge 40\%$)**: When complex cases doubled to 45%, average service duration increased to ~43 minutes. Daily demand of 115 cases requires $115 \times 43 \approx 4,945\text{ staff-minutes}$.
  - Under standard 6.0 FTE staffing (4,320 available minutes), maximum theoretical capacity is $\approx 100\text{ cases/day}$. The system entered super-critical drift ($\rho \approx 1.15$), accumulating ~14 backlogged cases every weekday.
  - **Permanent Weekday Staffing Recommendation**: When complex mix exceeds 35%, weekday capacity must be permanently increased to **at least 7.2 FTE** ($5,184\text{ staff-minutes}$) to maintain utilization below 85% ($\rho = 4945 / 5184 \approx 0.95$, ideally 7.5 FTE for $\rho \approx 0.91$).
  - **Weekend Staffing Recommendation**: Weekend staffing must be permanently set to **3.0 FTE** (as demonstrated on May 02–03), which completely halted weekend backlog growth.

### 2. Backlog Recovery Protocol and Automated Triggers
- **Failure of Passive Clearing**: The data demonstrates that under standard 6.0 FTE capacity, the service center has *zero headroom* to clear existing backlogs. When backlogs formed (whether via outage or mix surge), they persisted indefinitely until active staffing interventions occurred.
- **Automated Circuit-Breaker Trigger**:
  - Deploy an automated monitoring rule: If 48-hour cumulative net flow $\sum_{t-1}^t (A - C) \ge +15\text{ cases}$, or if closing WIP exceeds 35 cases, trigger an immediate **Surge Capacity Intervention (+1.0 to +1.5 FTE)**.
  - As proven by Regime 3 (Apr 16–17) and Regime 5 (Apr 29–May 01), deploying 7.0 FTE yields ~121–129 completions/day, enabling a sustained burn-down velocity of 6 to 15 cases/day without inducing staff burnout.

### 3. Intraday Peak Smoothing and Batch-Release Regulation
- **Mitigating Morning Arrival Spikes**: The representative vector on 2026-04-21 revealed an extreme arrival burst: 34 cases in a single hour (10:00–11:00) and 24 cases at 13:00–14:00, driving arrival Fano factor to $10.12$.
- **Operational Mitigations**:
  - *Staggered Shift Scheduling*: Rather than uniform 07:00–19:00 coverage, implement overlapping staggered shifts (e.g., 2 FTE on 07:00–15:30; 4 FTE on 09:00–17:30; 2 FTE on 10:30–19:00), concentrating 6 to 7 FTE during the critical 09:30–14:30 congestion window.
  - *Upstream Rate Limiting / Virtual Queuing*: The negative lag-1 autocorrelation ($r_1 = -0.137$) on bursty days indicates upstream batch dispatching. Institute intake pacing (token-bucket rate limiting) to meter case arrivals into the active processing queue at a maximum rate of 12 cases/hour.

### 4. Process Mining and Enhanced Data-Collection Architecture
- **Transition from Daily Aggregates to Event-Log Schema**: Daily tables cannot reveal intra-case dependencies, resource handoffs, or rework loops. The service center must capture an event log conforming to:
  $$\langle \text{case\_id}, \text{activity\_name}, \text{start\_timestamp}, \text{complete\_timestamp}, \text{resource\_id}, \text{variant\_type} \rangle$$
- **Mandatory Telemetry Additions**:
  1. *Timestamped Initial Backlog*: Explicitly record $Q_{07:00}$ each morning to ground queue state equations.
  2. *Outage and Intervention Logging*: Capture exact microsecond start and end timestamps of system downtime and staffing level shifts.
  3. *Distinction of Wait vs. Service Time*: Track both `enqueue_timestamp` and `start_service_timestamp` to measure pure queue delay independently from execution touch time.

### 5. Counter-Intuitive Policy Trap: The Pooled Downsizing Fallacy
- **The "Beneficial" Pooled Policy**: A management consulting analysis relying on pooled monthly statistics would observe:
  - Total monthly arrivals = 2,519 across 28 days = $90.0\text{ cases/day}$.
  - Mean service time = ~31 minutes.
  - Total daily labor needed = $90.0 \times 31\text{ min} = 2,790\text{ staff-minutes}$.
  - A staff of 5.0 FTE provides $5.0 \times 720 = 3,600\text{ staff-minutes}$, implying a comfortable theoretical utilization of $\rho = 2790 / 3600 = 77.5\%$.
  - The consultant recommends reducing standard weekday staffing from 6.0 FTE to **5.2 FTE**, projecting significant labor cost savings while maintaining $\rho < 80\%$.
- **The Segmented Catastrophe**:
  - In reality, weekday demand during normal business operations is 114–120 cases/day, with high-complexity variants requiring ~43 minutes of work.
  - Actual weekday labor demand is $115 \times 43 \approx 4,945\text{ staff-minutes}$.
  - A reduced staff of 5.2 FTE provides only $5.2 \times 720 = 3,744\text{ staff-minutes}$.
  - The resulting weekday utilization would be $\rho = 4945 / 3744 = 1.32$ (32% capacity deficit). The center would fail to complete 28 cases per day. Within two weeks, the backlog would exceed 280 cases, causing catastrophic service-level collapse and multi-day customer delays.

# 4. LITTLE'S LAW IMPLIED FLOW-TIME FOR EXPLICIT CANDIDATE WINDOWS
# ==============================================================================
# Service center open 07:00 to 19:00 = 12 operating hours = 720 operating minutes/day.
# Implied flow time: W_min = (Mean_WIP * 720.0 * D) / Completed_Total

candidate_windows = [
    ('Baseline Weekdays W1', '2026-04-06', '2026-04-10', 'applicable'),
    ('Baseline Weekend W1', '2026-04-11', '2026-04-12', 'approximate only'),
    ('Pre-Outage Baseline (9d)', '2026-04-06', '2026-04-14', 'approximate only'),
    ('Outage Shock Day', '2026-04-15', '2026-04-15', 'not defensible'),
    ('Post-Outage Recovery (2d)', '2026-04-16', '2026-04-17', 'approximate only'),
    ('Overload Weekdays W3', '2026-04-20', '2026-04-24', 'not defensible'),
    ('Full Overload Period (9d)', '2026-04-20', '2026-04-28', 'not defensible'),
    ('Intervention Weekdays W4', '2026-04-29', '2026-05-01', 'approximate only'),
    ('Intervention Weekend W4', '2026-05-02', '2026-05-03', 'approximate only'),
    ('Entire Pooled Month (28d)', '2026-04-06', '2026-05-03', 'not defensible')
]

littles_eval = []
for label, start_d, end_d, verdict in candidate_windows:
    sub = df_daily[(df_daily['date'] >= start_d) & (df_daily['date'] <= end_d)]
    n_days = len(sub)
    tot_arr = int(sub['arrivals'].sum())
    tot_cmp = int(sub['completed'].sum())
    net_drift = tot_arr - tot_cmp
    avg_wip = float(sub['mean_wip'].mean())
    
    # Implied W using completions (actual throughput)
    if tot_cmp > 0:
        w_min_comp = (avg_wip * 720.0 * n_days) / tot_cmp
        w_hr_comp = w_min_comp / 60.0
    else:
        w_min_comp, w_hr_comp = np.nan, np.nan
        
    # Implied W using arrivals (intake rate)
    if tot_arr > 0:
        w_min_arr = (avg_wip * 720.0 * n_days) / tot_arr
    else:
        w_min_arr = np.nan
        
    littles_eval.append({
        'window': label,
        'days': n_days,
        'mean_wip': round(avg_wip, 1),
        'tot_arr': tot_arr,
        'tot_comp': tot_cmp,
        'net_drift': net_drift,
        'w_min_via_comp': round(w_min_comp, 1),
        'w_hr_via_comp': round(w_hr_comp, 2),
        'w_min_via_arr': round(w_min_arr, 1),
        'verdict': verdict
    })

df_littles_table = pd.DataFrame(littles_eval).set_index('window')
