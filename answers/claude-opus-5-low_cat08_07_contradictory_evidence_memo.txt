# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee, Consumer Fiber Operations
**From:** Process Intelligence / Operations Design
**Subject:** Redesign R — recommended decision after the 6-week, 9,200-case pilot
**Recommendation:** Alternative E (segmented deployment with controls), executed with an embedded controlled experiment (Alternative D) for the contested segments. Do **not** scale nationally; do **not** roll back.

Redesign R is not one intervention but four bundled changes: coordinator ownership, task merging for simple cases, predictive survey skipping, and post-billing activation checks. The pilot evidence separates cleanly along that seam. The first two changes look genuinely good: cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%, median cycle time fell 14.2 → 10.8 days, and the simple-house / low-risk segment (34% of pilot volume) improved on every dimension we measure — median −6.0 days, p90 −8.0 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints −1pp. That is a coherent, self-consistent win, and it should be locked in.

The last two changes are where the damage sits. p90 rose 31.0 → 38.5 days and p95 45.0 → 62.0 days; first-time-right fell 82% → 76%; complaints within 30 days rose 6.1% → 9.8%; manual activation more than doubled (7% → 15%); second visits rose 11% → 18%. The segment table localises this precisely: apartments/medium-risk (p90 +12 days, FTR −8pp) and old permit-sensitive buildings (p90 +25 days, FTR −11pp) absorb nearly all of the deterioration. Conformance diagnostics point the same way — frequent *Billing start before successful Activation test*, *Installation visit repeated without preceding Field survey*, and a systematic **move-on-model for the expected Permit check in older apartment buildings**. The DFG confirms the mechanism: Installation visit → Activation test fell 78% → 60% while Installation visit → Manual activation rose 8% → 19% and Billing start → Complaint escalation rose 3% → 8%. Work was not eliminated; it was displaced downstream, out of the visible cost line. Mean visible cost improved (EUR 184 → 161) while the fuller estimate worsened (EUR 198 → 207) — and that estimate is itself incomplete, since cost data excludes complaint handling after week 4, so EUR 207 is a lower bound.

Two caveats bound how hard we can lean on any of this. The pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, so part of the tail deterioration is **case mix, not causation** — the comparison is confounded and we cannot currently apportion it. And segment/risk attributes are missing before pilot week 3, so the segment table rests on roughly the last two-thirds of the pilot. Any national business case built on these numbers would be built on sand.

The compliance dimension is the part that cannot wait for better data. Billing customers before verified activation, and skipping permit checks on permit-sensitive buildings, are not merely quality regressions — they are regulatory and contractual exposures whose expected cost is unbounded by the pilot's cost table. These stop now, irrespective of the causal question.

Two structural risks deserve naming. Coordinator utilization hit 93% in two regions, with survey-team utilization falling 78% → 52% while second-visit team utilization rose 66% → 92%: R has moved the bottleneck rather than removed it, and the Failed visit → Second visit delay grows precisely where coordinators are saturated. Separately, fewer handovers bought accountability at the cost of resilience — the working-together network centralised on coordinators, and cases stalled during coordinator absence because context lived in one head rather than in a handover artefact.

**What we propose.** Immediately halt post-billing activation and reinstate mandatory permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings. Suspend the predictive survey-skip rule for medium-risk and old-building addresses and re-validate it (M6, M1). Scale coordinator ownership and the merged validate/schedule step for simple low-risk houses now, with a coordinator utilization ceiling near 85% and a named backup per case. Concurrently instrument what we could not measure: start timestamps (M2), reason codes (M3), and 60-day downstream cost (M4). Decide on wider rollout in one quarter, against pre-committed thresholds stated in Section 6 — not against whichever metric moves first.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness with low precision.** Fitness measures how much of the observed behaviour the model can replay; precision measures how much behaviour the model permits that was never observed. R replaced a fairly constrained sequence with a bundle of optional paths — survey may be skipped or not, activation may be automatic or manual, billing may precede or follow activation, a visit may repeat. A model discovered from such a log becomes a permissive "flower-like" structure around the coordinator path: it replays nearly everything (fitness 0.94) precisely *because* it forbids almost nothing (precision 0.51). The two numbers are not in tension; low precision is the formal signature of a process that has lost its ordering constraints. Falling generalization (0.70 → 0.56) adds that the observed variety is not even well covered by recurring structure — the pilot is generating one-off variants. Operationally, this is the process-mining restatement of the compliance findings: a process in which billing-before-activation and missing permit checks are *possible* is exactly a process whose model has low precision.

**Improved median, worse tail.** Median and tail describe different populations. R shortened the happy path — merging validation and scheduling and removing handover queues cuts several days from the ~34% of cases that are simple houses, which is enough to move the median. The tail is produced by a different, smaller population: cases that enter a rework loop. R made those loops both more likely (survey skipping → failed visits, second visits) and slower (second-visit team at 92% utilization; coordinator at 93%). Queueing theory makes this asymmetric: at 90%+ utilization, small increases in load produce large increases in waiting time, and that waiting lands entirely on the exception path. So the distribution did not shift — it split, with mass moving both left and right. A single average or median cannot represent that, which is why the aggregate SLA breach worsens while simple-house SLA improves.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** Handovers were counted as pure waste, but they performed a second, unmeasured function: each handover forced context to be externalised into a record another person could act on. Concentrating ownership in one coordinator removed the waste *and* the externalisation. The social-network evidence shows this directly — the handover network simplified while the working-together network centralised. The result is a system with fewer interfaces but no redundancy: when the coordinator is absent, no one else holds the case context, so cases stall rather than degrade gracefully. Accountability and resilience are genuinely in tension here; fewer handovers improved the first and damaged the second.

**Lower visible cost, higher downstream cost.** The EUR 184 → 161 figure measures the installation process as scoped; the rework it created (second visits at 18%, manual activation at 15%, complaints at 9.8%, escalations after billing start) is booked to field operations, activation specialists and customer service, and the cost feed excludes complaint handling after week 4 entirely. R therefore did not remove work — it moved work across a measurement boundary and past the observation window. The fuller estimate (EUR 198 → 207) reverses the sign, and because it is truncated it understates the true figure. This is the classic pattern of a local optimisation evaluated with a local cost boundary.

**Simple houses improve while apartments and old buildings deteriorate.** R's mechanisms are risk-blind, but the risk they remove is not. Merging validation and scheduling is safe when address data is unambiguous — true for detached houses. Skipping the field survey is safe only when the predictive rule is accurate, and the apartment segment note says plainly that the rule is "often wrong." Permit checks are irrelevant for most new-build houses and mandatory for old buildings, so the same "simplification" is a rational shortcut in one segment and a control failure in another. Post-billing activation is harmless when signal is genuinely fine and generates a billing dispute when it is not, which correlates with in-building wiring — i.e. apartments. The redesign is therefore correctly matched to 34% of volume and mismatched to roughly 47% (apartments plus old buildings), which is exactly the pattern the segment table shows. This is not noise; it is a treatment-effect that varies systematically by a knowable covariate, which is what makes segmented deployment feasible rather than a compromise.

# 4. Decision alternatives

### A. Scale Redesign R nationally
- **Advantages:** Fastest capture of the median and handover gains; a single national process is simplest to operate and train; visible unit cost falls immediately.
- **Risks:** Nationalises the p95 blow-out (45 → 62 days) and the complaint rate (6.1% → 9.8%); makes billing-before-activation and missing permit checks systemic, converting an operational defect into a regulatory and contractual exposure; national mix contains more apartments and old buildings than the pilot's 34% simple-house share in some markets, so measured damage would likely grow; coordinator utilization already at 93% implies national rollout needs headcount that has not been costed; the low-precision model means we cannot even monitor conformance reliably yet.
- **Reasonable only if:** the tail deterioration were shown to be entirely case-mix artefact, the compliance deviations were shown to be logging gaps rather than real omissions, and full-cost measurement showed net savings. None of these hold today.

### B. Roll back to baseline
- **Advantages:** Restores known compliance behaviour and known FTR/complaint levels immediately; zero further downside risk; simple to execute.
- **Risks:** Discards a large, credible, internally consistent gain for 34% of volume (−6 days median, −8 days p90, +3pp FTR, −1pp complaints — a segment where *no* metric worsened); destroys the coordinator capability just built; wastes the pilot's learning and demoralises the teams; the handover reduction (34% → 12% of cases with 3+ handovers) is a durable structural gain unrelated to the failing components.
- **Reasonable only if:** the damaging components could not be separated from the beneficial ones, or if a regulator required a full return to the prior control environment. Neither applies — the components are separable by design and by segment.

### C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation
- **Advantages:** Directly targets the two mechanisms most strongly implicated by the conformance and DFG evidence; keeps the handover and median gains; removes the compliance exposure; simple to communicate.
- **Risks:** Also suspends the beneficial merged validate/schedule step and survey skipping *for simple houses*, where they appear to work — leaving value on the table; coordinator utilization at 93% is untouched, so the coordinator bottleneck and the resilience problem persist nationally; provides no experimental design, so we still will not learn whether the survey rule can be fixed rather than abandoned.
- **Reasonable if:** management's priority is speed of risk elimination over learning, and appetite for further experimentation is low. This is the sensible fallback if E is judged too complex to operate.

### D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement
- **Advantages:** The only option that produces causal, mix-adjusted answers; resolves the pilot's confounding directly; allows the survey-skip rule to be validated per risk stratum rather than accepted or discarded wholesale.
- **Risks:** As a *standalone* choice it leaves the current pilot running unchanged for the experiment's duration, which means continuing to bill before activation and skip permit checks — unacceptable; delays capture of the simple-house benefit by a quarter; costs measurement effort and management attention.
- **Reasonable if:** combined with immediate suspension of the harmful components — i.e. as a component of E, not as an alternative to acting.

### E. Hybrid — deploy R for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, measure before wider scale
- **Advantages:** Matches treatment to the segment where the evidence supports it, and withholds it where the evidence shows harm; captures ~34% of volume's benefit immediately; eliminates compliance exposure by restoring permit checks and pre-billing activation verification everywhere; preserves the coordinator capability while allowing utilization caps and backup-ownership rules to address the bottleneck and resilience findings; leaves a clean control group for the experiments in D.
- **Risks:** Two operating variants increase training, routing and IT complexity; segment classification must be accurate at intake, and the very attributes needed are currently incomplete — a misclassified apartment gets the wrong process; risks entrenching a permanent two-speed process if the measurement phase is not time-boxed; requires the segment routing rule itself to be governed and audited.
- **Reasonable if:** segment classification can be made reliable at order intake and management commits to pre-agreed decision thresholds and a review date. Both are achievable within a quarter.

**Comparative judgement.** A and B are dominated — A ignores a real and partly compliance-flavoured harm, B ignores a real and segment-localised benefit; both violate the principle that no single intervention dominates. C is defensible and safe but under-exploits the evidence and leaves the coordinator-capacity problem unaddressed. D is necessary but insufficient alone. **E, with D embedded, is the recommendation:** it is the only option that simultaneously stops the harm, banks the benefit, and buys the information needed to decide the remaining question.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranking criterion: how much a measurement can change the decision, weighted by how quickly and cheaply it delivers, and whether it unblocks an otherwise unresolvable question. Note that M2, M3 and M7 are infrastructure — they raise the value of everything else, which is why two of them rank high despite not answering a question on their own.

The apartment segment note ("skip rule often wrong") is currently an inference, not a measured false-negative rate | This is the pivot for the largest single source of harm. If the rule's error rate is low and concentrated in an identifiable stratum, survey skipping can be re-enabled with a tightened threshold, recovering most of R's cost benefit. **Sequencing.** M6, M7 and M3 start this week (retrospective or low-effort). M4 and M2 start immediately because their value accrues only with elapsed time. M1 launches once M3's reason codes exist, so the experiment yields interpretable outcomes. M5 and M8 run alongside without gating the quarter's decision.

**A note on what information cannot fix.** No measurement resolves the ethical and regulatory status of billing before verified activation or of skipping mandatory permit checks. Those are decisions, not uncertainties, and they should be settled now rather than studied.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E, with the Alternative D experiment embedded — segmented deployment plus controlled measurement. Reject A and B.**

### Immediate action (within 2 weeks)
1. Restore **activation verification before Billing start** for all cases, all segments, without exception. Sequence billing after a passed activation test.
2. Reinstate **mandatory permit checks** for permit-sensitive and older buildings, enforced as a hard gate rather than a coordinator judgement call.
3. Cap **coordinator utilization at ~85%** in the pilot regions and assign a **named backup coordinator plus a mandatory structured case note** per case, to address the resilience finding directly.
4. Rebalance capacity: the field-survey team is at 52% while the second-visit team is at 92% — move capacity toward second visits and survey coverage for higher-risk addresses.
5. Turn on conformance monitoring (M7) for the two deviation patterns as a standing operational control with an escalation threshold.

### What to pause
- **Predictive survey skipping** for apartments, medium-risk and old/permit-sensitive addresses — pending M6 and M1. Retain it only for simple, low-risk houses.
- **All post-billing activation checks** — suspended indefinitely, not pending study.
- **National rollout planning and any associated cost-savings commitment** — until the thresholds below are met.
- **Manual-activation-to-billing flows for business customers**, where they generate billing disputes, until ownership after billing start is clearly assigned.

### What to scale
- **Coordinator ownership** and the **merged validate/schedule task** for the *simple house, low building risk* segment (34% of pilot volume), under the utilization cap and backup-ownership rules above. This segment improved on every measured dimension and carries no compliance exposure.
- Extend coordinator ownership — but **not** survey skipping or post-billing activation — to other segments only where staffing supports the utilization cap.
- Retain the reduced-handover design, with the explicit compensating control that context is recorded in the case file rather than held by one person.

### What to measure next
Immediately: M6 (rule validation), M7 (conformance monitoring), M3 (reason codes). Start-now-because-of-lag: M4 (60-day full cost), M2 (start timestamps). Then M1 (stratified randomised survey-skip trial) once reason codes are live. Alongside: M5 (ownership resilience) and M8 (split NPS). Report at 6 weeks (interim) and 13 weeks (decision).

### Thresholds that would justify broader rollout
Wider scaling requires **all** of the following, measured on mix-adjusted or randomised data, not raw pilot comparisons:

1. **Tail:** p90 no worse than baseline (≤31.0 days) and p95 within 10% of baseline (≤49.5 days) in the deployed segments.
2. **Quality:** first-time-right ≥82% (baseline parity) and 30-day complaints ≤6.1% in the deployed segments.
3. **Cost:** full 60-day cost including complaint handling (M4) below EUR 198, with the measurement boundary complete — not an incomplete estimate.
4. **Compliance:** zero Billing-start-before-Activation-test occurrences and zero missing permit checks on permit-sensitive buildings over a continuous 8-week monitoring window.
5. **Survey rule:** M1/M6 demonstrate, per risk stratum, a false-skip rate low enough that skipping does not raise that stratum's second-visit rate above 11% (baseline).
6. **Capacity and resilience:** coordinator utilization sustained below 85%, and M5 shows no material case stalling under coordinator absence with the backup model in place.
7. **Model control:** discovered-model precision recovered to ≥0.75, confirming the process has regained ordering constraints rather than merely being described by a permissive model.

Any segment meeting all seven is scaled. Segments failing them stay on the baseline process — permanently, if the evidence says so. **We explicitly do not claim R is good or bad: it is well matched to about a third of our volume and badly matched to roughly half, and the recommendation is to run it exactly where it works while we buy the information to decide the rest.**
