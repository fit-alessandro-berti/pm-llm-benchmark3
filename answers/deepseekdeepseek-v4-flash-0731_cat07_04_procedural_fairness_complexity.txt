# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A fair comparison should distinguish:

- **Disparity**: observed group differences in outcomes and process metrics.
- **Discrimination**: disparity caused by unjustified differential treatment after accounting for legitimate needs and risk.
- **Legitimate routing**: extra steps required by risk, language preference, or accommodation requests.
- **Uncertainty**: sampling error, unmeasured confounding, and biased covariates.

The analysis should include:

- **Matching/stratification variables**: applicant_group, risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, language_preference, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month, disability_accommodation_requested. Use exact matching or coarsened exact matching within these variables, then compare process metrics within matched strata.
- **Path/variant comparison**: Use `pm4py.get_variants` to identify common variants. Compare the same variant across groups. For example, compare Group B and Group A cases that both follow V2 (Request Docs path) to see whether the same path is slower or more reworked for one group.
- **Resource-assignment comparison**: Use handover-of-work networks and working-together networks to measure handover counts per case. Compare cases with similar complexity but different numbers of handovers across groups.
- **Waiting-time decomposition**: Insert case service waiting time and decompose total cycle time into waiting time at each activity. Focus on waiting at Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents, and Supervisor Review.
- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates**: Re-estimate adjusted models with and without `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`. If results change substantially, those covariates may be absorbing or amplifying bias. Treat `documentation_completeness` as a possible mediator of language barriers, not only as a neutral control.
- **Subgroup checks by region and channel**: Repeat the analysis for online vs. in-person cases and for each region. If disparities are concentrated in a few regions, local resource shortages are likely; if they appear everywhere, systemic routing or decision practices may be involved.

All estimates should include confidence intervals and multiple-testing corrections. The goal is not to prove intent, but to identify where unexplained group differences remain after legitimate complexity is accounted for.

- **Group D and fraud-review routing**: Group D has 28% of cases in V3 (Risk Screening  Fraud Review  Supervisor Review) versus 9% for Group A. This is consistent with the higher measured risk_score_band and prior-case count. Fraud review is legitimate for high-risk cases. However, Group D still has a residual approval gap of 3.1 pp and an appeal gap of +5.7 pp. This suggests that either the risk score is not neutral, or the fraud-review process itself is producing questionable outcomes.
- **Group B and documentation rework**: Group B has 34% in V2 (Request Missing Documents) versus 24% for Group A, and the adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp. Some of this is due to incomplete documentation, but documentation completeness may itself be affected by language barriers and insufficient support.

Justified disparities: higher-risk cases needing Fraud Review, accommodation cases needing Accommodation Review, and language-preference cases needing Language Support Contact.

Concerning disparities: long waits at support steps, extra handovers, residual adjusted differences in approval, supervisor review, request-documents, and appeal rates.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate need-based routing**: Groups B, C, and D have more complex cases requiring additional review steps. Longer cycle times and more reviews are appropriate responses to risk, language need, and accommodation requests.
2. **Resource scarcity and queueing**: There are only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists. The queues at Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review create delays that are not directly caused by case complexity but by insufficient capacity.
3. **Handover fragmentation**: Specialist involvement creates multiple handovers. Each handover adds waiting time, information loss, and potential rework. Group B and C experience more handovers because of the generalist  specialist  generalist pattern.
4. **Differential decision quality and explanation**: Higher appeal rates in Groups B and D may reflect decisions that are less well explained, less accessible, or perceived as unfair. Language barriers and rushed supervisor reviews may contribute.
5. **Biased covariates and feedback loops**: `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities. High risk scores lead to more Fraud Review, which may lead to more denials, which may reinforce the original risk score. Similarly, `documentation_completeness` may be affected by language barriers, so controlling for it may hide the effect of inadequate support.
6. **Discretionary bias**: Caseworkers may unconsciously apply stricter scrutiny to minority-language applicants, accommodation applicants, or applicants with prior case counts. This is not directly observed in the event log, but residual adjusted disparities make it plausible.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in risk_score_band**: The risk score may be based on historical data that reflect past discrimination or unequal access. Treating it as a neutral control can legitimize bias.
- **Documentation_completeness is not independent**: Incomplete documentation may be caused by language barriers, lack of accessible instructions, or insufficient support. Controlling for it may absorb the effect of poor accessibility.
- **Missing applicant needs**: The log does not include cognitive disability, digital literacy, transportation barriers, family responsibilities, or other needs that affect process burden.
- **Unobserved resource workload**: Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. We cannot see real-time queue lengths, caseworker stress, or the actual effort spent on each case.
- **Appeal quality**: `appeal_flag` only indicates that an appeal was filed, not whether the appeal was successful, whether the original decision was wrong, or whether the explanation was adequate.
- **Case outcome does not capture dignity**: Approval and cycle time do not measure whether applicants felt respected, understood, or treated with dignity.
- **Overcontrol/undercontrol**: Adjusted models may overcontrol for mediators like documentation completeness, or undercontrol for unmeasured confounders. Causal conclusions are therefore limited.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Add capacity for language support or accommodation review**  
  Hire and train more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists.  
  *Side effects*: cost, training time, possible dilution of expertise, and risk of creating new silos if not integrated into the main process.

- **Service-level targets for support steps**  
  Set separate timeliness thresholds for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review.  
  *Side effects*: pressure to rush support, timestamp gaming, and possible neglect of other steps. Targets must be paired with quality monitoring.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**  
  Assign a single case owner with language or accommodation expertise from the start, avoiding generalist  specialist  generalist handovers.  
  *Side effects*: cross-training burden, less specialization, and possible slower handling of very complex cases. Needs pilot testing.

- **Decision-explanation improvements**  
  Provide translated, plain-language decision letters and clear appeal rights.  
  *Side effects*: upfront time and cost, possible delay in notification, and need for staff training. May reduce appeals and rework in the long run.

- **Monitoring adjusted disparities**  
  Build a dashboard that tracks approval, cycle time, supervisor review, request-documents, appeal, and handover metrics by applicant group, variant, and resource.  
  *Side effects*: risk of overcorrection or gaming if used as a quota. Should be used as a diagnostic, not a target.

- **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**  
  Validate the risk score for predictive bias across groups; recalibrate if needed. Improve documentation assistance for applicants with language barriers.  
  *Side effects*: resource-intensive, may require external data, and may reveal uncomfortable findings. But necessary to avoid feedback loops.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **indeterminate, but with serious procedural-fairness concerns**. Raw approval differences are not proof of discrimination, and some routing is legitimate because of risk, language need, and accommodation requests. However, adjusted disparities remain for Groups B, C, and D, especially in approval, supervisor review, request-documents, and appeal rates. The waiting-time and resource data show that rights-preserving support steps are also major bottlenecks, and handover fragmentation adds burden to the groups that already face more complexity. Group D’s high appeal rate and residual approval gap require particular scrutiny of the risk-score model.

The evidence is not strong enough to prove intentional discrimination, but it is strong enough to reject a claim that the process is clearly fair. The burden of justification is on the agency to show that support-step delays, handover burdens, and residual adjusted disparities are necessary, proportionate, and not avoidable.
