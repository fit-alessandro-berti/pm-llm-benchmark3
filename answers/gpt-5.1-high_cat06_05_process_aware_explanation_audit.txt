## 1. Leakage and temporal-consistency findings
Item | Finding type | Severity (critical/high/medium/low) | Why | Required remediation
--- | --- | --- | --- | ---
`total_case_duration_hours` | Temporal leakage – complete-trace outcome feature | critical | Calculated only after the claim is closed, so it encodes the realised settlement time and is almost indistinguishable from the target itself. | Remove from any model or explainer at `t0`; use only as a label/benchmark metric, not as an input feature.
`appeal_seen_anywhere` | Temporal leakage – future appeal indicator, misused causally | critical | Defined from the complete trace; at `t0` it is unknown whether an appeal will occur, yet the model treats it as a strong driver and the team interprets it as “appeals cause breaches.” | Drop from early-warning models; if needed, introduce an “appeal possible by policy” flag that depends only on information at `t0`, and keep appeals for ex‑post analytics, not prediction.
`final_handler_team` | Lineage defect / partial temporal leakage | high | Populated in the warehouse only at closure; for ~35% of claims it differs from the team at `t0`, so explanations can rely on a team that has not yet touched the case. | Either reconstruct `handler_team_at_t0` from event/resource logs with explicit timestamps or remove the feature; stop using closure-populated fields without an as‑of timestamp at `t0`.
`num_document_requests_total` | Temporal leakage – complete-trace count | critical | Computed over the entire lifecycle so it includes document requests long after `t0`, overstating “rework” as an early signal. | Replace with `num_document_requests_up_to_t0` (and possibly counts up to later defined prediction points); recompute from truncated prefixes and retrain.
`queue_length_at_t0` | Prefix-time congestion signal with minor timestamp tolerance | medium | Joined from a queue snapshot that is timestamped and within ±5 minutes of `t0`, so it is meaningfully available at prediction time but can occasionally be slightly misaligned. | Keep as a primary, defensible process-state feature; tighten or log the join tolerance and add monitoring for impossible joins (e.g., snapshot after case closure).
`initial_damage_complexity` | Prefix-time complexity signal (correlative, not strictly causal) | low | Entered at registration and frozen before `t0`; it is a genuine early signal of claim complexity, though also correlated with unobserved factors (e.g., type of incident, claimant profile). | Retain as a predictive feature; in explanations, phrase it as “more complex damage is associated with longer handling” and avoid strong causal claims without further study.
`full_trace_variant_id` | Temporal leakage – full-path identifier | critical | Built from the complete trace, so it encodes activities and loops that have not yet occurred at `t0`; high importance shows the model is learning from future behaviour. | Replace with `prefix_variant_id_at_t0` or similar prefix-only encodings; recompute on prefixes and retrain; exclude full-trace variants from early explanations.
`calendar_week` | Drift/calendar proxy, no temporal leakage | medium | Known at `t0`, but strongly aligned with a policy change and a holiday period, so it mixes seasonal workload with structural process changes and may not generalise. | Keep only if explicitly modelled as a temporal-drift control; explain it as “this prediction reflects historical performance in that period” and monitor performance when policies or seasons change.
`claimant_region` | Structural proxy requiring fairness review | high | Available at registration, but correlated with network coverage, language, channel and socioeconomic status, so it can introduce systematic group-level differences unrelated to individual merit. | Subject to fairness/robustness analysis (group metrics, counterfactual tests); if retained, frame explanations around service configuration (e.g., repair-network coverage) rather than “region riskiness,” or constrain its influence.
`elapsed_since_registration_at_t0` | Prefix-time age/process-state indicator | low | Derived only from past timestamps, representing how long the claim has already been in the system when coverage completes; older-at-`t0` cases genuinely have less remaining slack. | Retain as a key timing feature; explain as “this claim was already X hours old at prediction time,” not as a causal mechanism; verify computation strictly truncates at `t0`.
`prior_claim_count_before_t0` | Lineage bug causing subtle temporal leakage and fairness concerns | high | Intended to count only earlier claims, but current SQL uses `claim_created_at <= extraction_date`, so it can include claims created after `t0`; also correlated with socioeconomic and behavioural factors. | Fix the query to `claim_created_at < t0` per case; after repair, subject it to fairness/robustness checks and clearly describe it as “past claim history,” not moralised behaviour.
`same_day_document_count_snapshot` | Partial temporal leakage via nightly snapshot | high | Refreshed at 23:00 and may include documents uploaded after `t0` on the same day; for daytime predictions it can see into the future of that day. | Recompute document counts directly from event timestamps up to `t0`; if snapshots are kept, add an “as_of” time and exclude records where `snapshot_time > t0`.
`Claim Closed -> Damage Assessment Completed` pattern | Process-order inconsistency and closure-based temporal leakage | critical | The normative model requires damage assessment before closure; here the pattern is driven by cases where both events share a timestamp and ordering is arbitrary, and it always refers to post-`t0` events. | Treat such patterns as logging anomalies, not drivers; enforce alignment checks so patterns referencing `Claim Closed` cannot be used for early predictions; capture and use a reliable sequence index.
`Appeal -> Settlement Decision Recorded` pattern | Temporal leakage from appeal and second decision | critical | Appeals can occur only after a (typically delayed or contentious) initial settlement decision and lead to a second decision, all strictly after `t0`; the pattern therefore encodes future escalation. | Remove from the feature space for predictions at `t0`; if appeals are studied, use them only in retrospective analyses and not as inputs to breach predictions.
`Documents Requested -> Documents Received` pattern | Temporal leakage – evidence cycles after `t0` | critical | In the normative process, these evidence cycles occur after coverage check; using their presence from the full trace leaks information about how cooperative or complete the documentation eventually was. | Replace with prefix-based evidence indicators valid at `t0` (e.g., pre-existing documents, prior correspondence) or drop for this prediction point; re-mine patterns on prefix logs if sequence features are still desired.
`Coverage Check Completed -> Fraud Investigation Opened` pattern | Temporal leakage from future fraud investigations | critical | This pattern typically reflects whether a fraud investigation is ever opened after coverage, which is unknown at `t0` but strongly related to long and complex cases. | Replace with a flag for “fraud investigation already open by `t0`”, based on event timestamps; otherwise exclude this pattern from the model and explanations.
`Damage Assessment Completed -> Settlement Decision Recorded` pattern | Temporal leakage from downstream normative path | critical | Both events occur after `t0`; the pattern simply captures whether the case later follows a relatively clean standard path versus some exceptional route, which is not yet known at prediction time. | Restrict sequence features to what has happened at or before `t0`; for this prediction point, drop patterns that reference activities strictly downstream of `Coverage Check Completed`.
`claimant_channel` (e.g., Broker) | Prefix-time channel proxy with fairness/operational nuance | medium | Known at registration; channels such as brokers correlate with communication overhead and claimant mix, but also with intermediated relationships and potential socioeconomic patterns. | Retain only with fairness and stability checks; in explanations, describe operational implications (“broker-handled claims involve extra coordination”) rather than implying the channel itself is inherently risky.
`final_decision` (Approve/Partial/Deny/Withdrawn) | Direct target/outcome leakage | critical | This is the settlement outcome recorded at or after step 6, long after `t0`; using it as an input makes the prediction tautological. | Remove entirely from the feature set for prediction and explanation; treat it solely as a label or downstream outcome in evaluation.
`initial_reserve` | Prefix-time financial/complexity signal | medium | Typically set at or near registration as an estimate of expected claim cost; higher reserves are associated with more severe and complex claims but may also reflect assessor behaviour. | Keep as a predictive feature after verifying it is recorded before `t0`; frame it as “higher estimated claim size is associated with more protracted handling,” and check calibration across reserve bands.
`risk_score_current` | Mutable feature without historical timestamp (temporal leakage) | critical | The “current” risk score can be recalculated multiple times and is joined as of extraction date, so its value may incorporate knowledge acquired well after `t0`. | Introduce versioned, timestamped risk scores and use only the snapshot with `score_time <= t0`; until then, exclude this feature from the model and explanations.
`last_resource_role` (e.g., Special Investigation Unit) | Temporal leakage – uses last role in complete trace | critical | Defined as the last role to touch the case, which is inherently a future attribute at `t0` and strongly correlated with complex/fraud cases that take longer. | Replace with `current_resource_role_at_t0` or prefix-derived role-path summaries; drop the “last role” feature entirely for early predictions.

## 2. Process-model consistency checks
To validate that explanations are process-aware and temporally consistent, a concrete validation suite should combine data-level checks with conformance checking against the normative model.

- **Prefix-time availability checks**
  - Build a prefix log where each case is truncated exactly at `t0` (immediately after the first `Coverage Check Completed`), and recompute all candidate features from this prefix only.
  - For each stored feature, assert that `feature_stored(case, t0) == feature_recomputed_from_prefix(case, t0)` within a small tolerance; any dependence on events after `t0` will appear as a discrepancy (e.g., `appeal_seen_anywhere`, `full_trace_variant_id`, `total_case_duration_hours`).
  - Enforce “as-of” timestamps in the feature store: every row must carry `feature_as_of_time`, and automated checks should reject joins where `feature_as_of_time > t0` for a `t0`-based prediction.

- **Activity-order feasibility checks**
  - Encode the normative ordering constraints (e.g., `Damage Assessment Completed` must precede `Claim Closed`; `Settlement Decision Recorded` must precede `Appeal`; any second `Settlement Decision Recorded` must be preceded by an `Appeal`) as a declarative model (e.g., Declare/LTL rules).
  - Run alignment-based conformance checking between event logs and the normative model to detect traces where these constraints are violated, such as logs with `Claim Closed` before `Damage Assessment Completed`.
  - Use footprint or DFG (directly-follows graph) analyses only to discover frequent edges and candidate rules; explicitly note that a frequent DFG edge is descriptive, not a hard constraint, and must be validated against domain rules before being used to justify explanations.

- **Same-timestamp ambiguity checks**
  - Systematically scan for pairs of events within a case that share the same timestamp but where process logic requires a specific order (e.g., `Claim Closed` and `Damage Assessment Completed` in Case Z).
  - Require a reliable sequence index (or finer-grained timestamp) for such events; if it is missing, mark their relative order as unknown and forbid mining or explaining patterns that depend on that order.
  - Add unit tests that specifically look for impossible DFG edges that can only arise from same-timestamp sorting artefacts (e.g., `Claim Closed -> Damage Assessment Completed`).

- **Complete-trace vs prefix-derived features**
  - For each feature or sequence pattern, run a “prefix extension” test: compare its value on the prefix ending at `t0` to its value on progressively longer prefixes up to the complete trace.
  - Features that change when more of the trace is revealed (`appeal_seen_anywhere`, `num_document_requests_total`, `full_trace_variant_id`, sequence patterns involving closure or appeals) are flagged as complete-trace features and must not be used for predictions at `t0`.
  - Prefix-invariant features (e.g., `initial_damage_complexity`, `claimant_region`, a properly computed `queue_length_at_t0`) are safe with respect to temporal availability, though still subject to fairness and interpretability checks.

- **Explanation-level feasibility at `t0`**
  - When generating explanations, attach each feature or pattern with the earliest time it becomes fully determined; only display drivers whose determination time is ≤ `t0`.
  - Validate that no explanation refers to activities that are impossible or merely future-possible at `t0` (e.g., appeals, final decisions, closure, damage assessment) for a coverage-time prediction.
  - Specifically check that patterns involving `Settlement Decision Recorded` are correctly interpreted: the first occurrence is allowed after damage assessment, the second only after appeal; neither should inform a prediction made immediately after coverage.

- **Repeated `Settlement Decision Recorded` semantics**
  - Implement log checks that: (a) count occurrences of `Settlement Decision Recorded` per claim; (b) ensure that if it appears twice, there is exactly one `Appeal` between them; and (c) ensure no third occurrence exists without another well-defined escalation path.
  - Use these checks to guard against mis-logged decisions (e.g., duplicated events or corrections) being misinterpreted as genuine appeals.
  - In explanations, distinguish clearly between “first decision” and “post-appeal decision,” and ensure that neither is presented as an input to predictions that are defined to occur earlier in the process.

## 3. Alternative feature interpretations
- **Defensible predictive signal**
  - **Examples**: `initial_damage_complexity`, `initial_reserve`, a correctly computed `elapsed_since_registration_at_t0`.
  - **What may be claimed**: These features can defensibly be described as early indicators that are *associated* with higher or lower breach risk (e.g., “claims assessed as highly complex at registration tend to breach more often”). They capture real differences in expected workload and uncertainty.
  - **What may not be claimed**: Without causal analysis, we cannot say that changing the complexity label or the reserve *would itself* prevent a breach; they may reflect underlying severity, recording practices, or unobserved confounders.

- **Process-state or congestion signal**
  - **Examples**: `queue_length_at_t0`, `elapsed_since_registration_at_t0` (as “age in system”), repaired snapshot features like `same_day_document_count_snapshot` when computed strictly up to `t0`, and prefix-based counts of document requests if they exist before `t0`.
  - **What may be claimed**: These features legitimately describe the operational state at prediction time (“the claim is in a long queue,” “it is already several days old”), and they are actionable for capacity management and prioritisation.
  - **What may not be claimed**: We should not attribute blame to the claimant or suggest that queue length is an inherent property of the claim; nor should we treat process-state features as static risk factors that remain valid if scheduling or staffing policies change.

- **Proxy requiring fairness/robustness review**
  - **Examples**: `claimant_region`, `claimant_channel`, repaired `prior_claim_count_before_t0`, indicators related to fraud investigation or SIU involvement, and even risk scores that incorporate such signals.
  - **What may be claimed**: At most, that “historically, claims with this region/channel/history pattern have shown different breach rates,” and that the model is picking up on structural differences in service configuration or claimant interaction patterns.
  - **What may not be claimed**: That particular regions, channels, or claimant groups are inherently problematic or “cause” delays; such statements risk unfairness and may encode or amplify historical biases. These features must be stress-tested for disparate impact and robustness before being used in production explanations.

- **Drift/calendar proxy**
  - **Examples**: `calendar_week`, and to some extent path-based features (e.g., prefix variants) that correlate with specific policy regimes or system versions.
  - **What may be claimed**: That the model’s prediction reflects seasonality and historical performance in similar weeks or regimes (e.g., holiday slowdowns), and may legitimately be used to adjust expectations for certain periods.
  - **What may not be claimed**: That “week 52 causes breaches” or that these patterns are stable under future policy changes; they are contingent on the training period and must be revisited when the process or calendar context changes.

- **Unusable until lineage is repaired**
  - **Examples**: `total_case_duration_hours`, `appeal_seen_anywhere`, `final_handler_team` (as currently sourced), `num_document_requests_total`, `full_trace_variant_id`, the current implementation of `prior_claim_count_before_t0`, `same_day_document_count_snapshot`, `risk_score_current`, `last_resource_role`, and sequence patterns involving closure, appeal, fraud investigation, or settlement decisions (`Claim Closed -> Damage Assessment Completed`, `Appeal -> Settlement Decision Recorded`, `Documents Requested -> Documents Received`, `Coverage Check Completed -> Fraud Investigation Opened`, `Damage Assessment Completed -> Settlement Decision Recorded`).
  - **What may be claimed**: Only that these features are potentially interesting from a retrospective analytics standpoint (e.g., to describe how long or escalated cases behave), but not that they are valid signals *at prediction time*.
  - **What may not be claimed**: They must not be presented as drivers of predictions made at `t0`, nor as causal levers; using them in live predictions or user-facing explanations would misrepresent what was actually knowable when the decision support was provided.

## 5. Recommendations for explanation validation
- **1. Redesign the feature store around explicit as-of timestamps**
  - Ensure every feature (including warehouse joins and risk scores) carries a `feature_as_of_time` and that training/inference pipelines enforce `feature_as_of_time <= t0` for the relevant prediction task.
  - Prohibit inclusion of closure-only or outcome-like fields (`total_case_duration_hours`, `final_decision`, closure-populated teams) in feature views used for early predictions.

- **2. Implement systematic prefix-only recomputation**
  - For each prediction-time definition (here: immediately after `Coverage Check Completed`), build a prefix-log generator that truncates traces at `t0` and recomputes all features from raw events, documents, and master data.
  - Use this generator as the reference implementation for both training data creation and offline validation of the production feature store, ensuring no dependence on future events.

- **3. Apply process-conformance tests before mining or using sequence features**
  - Encode key normative rules (ordering of assessment, decisions, appeals, and closure) as declarative constraints and run alignment-based conformance checking to identify and quarantine anomalous traces.
  - Only mine DFG/pattern features on traces that conform to basic process rules, and limit sequence-based explanations to patterns that are fully determined by the prefix at `t0`.

- **4. Introduce calibration and performance checks after feature removal**
  - After eliminating or repairing leaky features, retrain the model and recalibrate probabilities (e.g., with Platt scaling or isotonic regression) to maintain well-calibrated breach probabilities.
  - Compare performance and calibration across time, regions and complexity bands to ensure the model remains reliable after removing strong but invalid signals like appeals and closure-based patterns.

- **5. Validate explanation stability and temporal robustness**
  - Track how global and local explanations change when the underlying model is retrained on adjacent time windows or when minor noise is added to inputs, to detect fragile attributions.
  - Require that important explanations be stable under small perturbations and that they continue to respect temporal availability and process feasibility constraints.

- **6. Conduct structured proxy and fairness analysis**
  - For region, channel, prior-claims and fraud/SIU-related features, compute group-level performance metrics, error rates, and average predicted risks, and run counterfactual tests where only these attributes are varied.
  - Use these analyses to decide whether to constrain, regularise or remove certain proxies, and to frame explanations in terms of process and service configuration rather than claimant demographics.

- **7. Monitor schema and process drift**
  - Set up automated checks to detect changes in event schemas, activity labels, and process variants (e.g., new paths or policy changes) and to flag when features like `calendar_week` or prefix variants start behaving differently.
  - When drift is detected, trigger revalidation of feature lineage, retraining of models on updated prefixes, and a fresh round of explanation audits before deploying new explanations to users.

- **8. Embed these checks into the MLOps lifecycle**
  - Make prefix-availability tests, conformance checks, fairness analysis, and explanation-stability evaluations part of the standard model approval and periodic review process.
  - Document which features are allowed to appear in explanations for each prediction-time definition, and automatically filter out any drivers that violate temporal, process, or fairness constraints.
