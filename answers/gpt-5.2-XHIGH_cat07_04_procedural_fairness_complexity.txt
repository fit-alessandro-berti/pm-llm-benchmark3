# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in a public-benefits process is multi-dimensional: equal outcomes are neither necessary nor sufficient for fairness because groups can differ in eligibility, needs, and legitimate routing. The evidence supports assessing at least the following dimensions.

- **Outcome parity (raw)**: Group-level approval rates differ substantially (A 78%, B 63%, C 69%, D 58%). These differences are not, by themselves, evidence of discrimination because case mix and legitimate routing differ.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity**: Even after controlling for measured case mix (risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior cases, region, month), approval probability remains lower than Group A (B −5.5 pp, C −2.2 pp, D −3.1 pp). This residual could reflect unmeasured complexity, biased covariates (e.g., risk band), discretionary behavior, or true differential treatment—so it is a signal for deeper procedural audit, not a conclusion.

- **Procedural burden (process “cost”)**: Fairness includes the burdens imposed to obtain a decision—number of steps, document requests, handovers, rework loops, and required contacts. Higher rates of “Request Missing Documents”, “Supervisor Review”, and rework are burdens even when outcomes are equal. Rights-preserving steps (Language Support, Accommodation Review) should not systematically translate into extra administrative burden beyond what is necessary to provide the right.

- **Timeliness (including tail risk)**: Median and 90th-percentile cycle times differ markedly (e.g., B median 13.7d vs A 8.1d; C 15.9d; B p90 34.2d vs A 21.4d; C p90 38.8d). Procedural fairness requires that delays are proportionate to legitimate complexity and that access-related steps do not create excessive queueing.

- **Consistency/neutrality of routing**: Fair procedures apply consistent criteria for routing (e.g., fraud review for high risk, accommodation review when requested). Residual differences in routing probabilities after adjustment (e.g., B +8.0 pp Supervisor Review; B +9.5 pp Request Missing Documents; C +5.5 pp Supervisor Review) raise questions about whether thresholds are applied consistently or whether discretionary escalation is uneven.

- **Quality/accessibility of support (rights in practice)**: The presence of Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review is not enough; fairness depends on speed, continuity, and effectiveness. If support steps are systematically slower due to capacity constraints, applicants who need support experience a de facto penalty for exercising a right.

- **Appeal and rework burden**: Higher appeal rates (B 13%, D 16% vs A 6%) and rework rates (B 22%, C 24% vs A 11%) indicate additional time, stress, and administrative load. Appeals can reflect substantive disagreement, but also unclear explanations, communication barriers, or perceived unfairness—so they are a key procedural outcome.

- **Resource-allocation fairness (capacity and workload)**: Fairness includes whether staffing and queues are proportionate to demand. Concentrating Group B on 7 bilingual caseworkers and Group C on 5 accommodation specialists creates predictable delays and additional handovers; this is a structural, not merely individual, fairness concern—especially if service targets ignore these pathways.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to distinguish (a) disparity due to legitimate differences in needs/complexity, (b) disparity due to uneven procedure (potentially discriminatory or structurally inequitable), and (c) uncertainty driven by measurement limits.

## Define procedural outcomes (beyond approval)

Analyze multiple dependent variables:

- **Outcome**: `case_outcome` (approval/denial/other categories if present).
- **Timeliness**: total case duration; time between specific steps; distribution tails (p90/p95).
- **Procedural burden**: number of events; number of “Request Missing Documents”; number of handovers; number of rework loops (repeated checks/requests); number of distinct resources involved.
- **Routing/controls**: probability of “Supervisor Review”, “Fraud Review”, “Language Support Contact”, “Accommodation Review”.
- **Appeal burden**: `appeal_flag`, and cycle time including appeal path (e.g., variants containing Appeal Intake/Review).

## Matching / stratification variables

Use a stratified design to compare “like with like,” then add modeling:

- **Primary stratification (exact or near-exact)**:
  - `region` (important given known backlogs)
  - `channel` (online vs in-person)
  - month (from `time:timestamp`)
  - `language_preference` (or an indicator for needing language support)
  - `disability_accommodation_requested`
  - `risk_score_band` (policy-relevant, but treat as potentially biased)

- **Additional matching covariates**:
  - `documentation_completeness` (but treat as potentially influenced by language/accommodation)
  - `household_size_band`
  - `prior_case_count` (banded if needed)

Practical approach: exact match on `region × channel × month × (language need) × (accommodation requested) × risk_score_band`, and within each stratum, compare groups; if strata become sparse, relax to coarser bins and use propensity-score matching within region/channel.

## Path/variant comparison

- Compute variants overall and within each stratum; compare:
  - **Variant shares** (e.g., V1–V6) and whether differences persist after stratification.
  - **Edit-distance / similarity** between variants to quantify “process complexity” beyond counts.
  - **Conditional routing checks**: among cases with the same `language_preference`, is “Language Support Contact” consistently triggered? Among those with `disability_accommodation_requested=True`, is “Accommodation Review” consistently included, and does it occur early enough to be helpful?

## Resource-assignment comparison

Within matched strata:

- Compare **resource concentration** (e.g., Herfindahl index of `org:resource` assignments) and **role assignment** (from discovered organizational roles).
- Compare **handover networks** per group/region: density, centralization, and whether certain groups experience more cross-role transfers.
- Identify whether group membership predicts assignment to overloaded resources (proxy: high number of active cases per resource/day or high waiting times for paths serviced by that resource).

## Waiting-time decomposition

Decompose cycle time into:

- **Service time** (time spent “in activity,” as approximated by consecutive timestamps on same resource/activity)
- **Waiting time** (queueing between activities)
- **Step-specific delays**: e.g., `Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment`, `Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment`, `Supervisor Review → Decision Draft`.

Quantify each step’s contribution to the adjusted cycle-time gap by comparing step-level medians/quantiles within matched strata.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

Because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may encode or be affected by historical and access disparities:

- Run **multiple adjustment sets**:
  - **Policy-justified**: adjust for plausibly pre-treatment needs (`channel`, `region`, month, `language_preference`, `disability_accommodation_requested`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`).
  - **Full model**: additionally adjust for `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`.
  - **Stress test**: exclude `risk_score_band` or replace it with alternative proxies (e.g., prior cases) and check stability of group effects.
- Use **bounds / robustness checks** (e.g., Rosenbaum-style sensitivity for unobserved confounding on approval; or report the unobserved confounder strength needed to explain residual gaps).

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

Given known regional backlogs and in-person delays:

- Repeat all key comparisons within:
  - each `region` (especially the two with supervisor backlog),
  - each `channel`,
  - and their intersection (region × channel).

If disparities are concentrated in specific regions or channels, the fairness issue is more likely structural (capacity/routing design) than generalized individual bias.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Using the provided variant, waiting-time, and resource summaries, the most salient procedural disparities are tied to rights-preserving steps and resource bottlenecks, plus elevated discretionary escalation.

## Group B (minority language; high language support need)

- **Path differences**: Variant V4 (“… → Language Support Contact → …”) is 24% for Group B vs 1% for Group A, indicating frequent use of a support pathway—legitimate given “Needs language support” is 57% in Group B.
- **Timeliness disparity concentrated at support queue**: The median wait from **Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment** is **3.8 days** (only 7 bilingual caseworkers). This is a large procedural burden that is not inherently required by the right to language support; it is largely a capacity/design issue.
- **Additional waiting for document-related routing**: **Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents** wait is **1.6d** (B) vs **0.4d** (A), consistent with both (i) higher incomplete documentation share (46% vs 21%) and (ii) queueing for bilingual processing. Some difference is plausibly legitimate (more incomplete submissions), but the magnitude suggests avoidable delay in the mechanism that resolves incompleteness.
- **Residual routing differences**: Even after adjustment, Group B has higher probability of **Supervisor Review (+8.0 pp)** and **Request Missing Documents (+9.5 pp)**. If these were purely driven by measured complexity, these differences should shrink more; persistence suggests inconsistent thresholds, higher uncertainty when cases involve language barriers, or additional (unmeasured) complexity.
- **Resource/handovers**: Group B cases are disproportionately assigned to **7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers**, with more handovers general → bilingual → general. Handovers add delay and error risk; they are a procedural burden that can be reduced without removing support.

## Group C (accommodation requests)

- **Path differences**: Variant V5 (“… → Accommodation Review → …”) is **31%** for Group C vs **2%** for Group A, consistent with **62%** accommodation-request share. This routing is legitimate.
- **Timeliness disparity concentrated at accommodation queue**: **Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment** median wait is **4.7 days**. This is the single largest step-level delay reported and strongly indicates a capacity bottleneck or batching behavior in the specialized team.
- **Compounded by role overlap**: Group C is concentrated among **5 accommodation specialists**, and **two also perform Supervisor Review**. This creates a plausible structural conflict: the same scarce staff are gating both accommodation and escalation, amplifying delays and variation.
- **Residual escalation**: Adjusted probability of **Supervisor Review is +5.5 pp** for Group C, and adjusted cycle time is **+4.1 days**—a substantial residual gap. Some could reflect unmeasured complexity of accommodation needs, but the size and the identified bottleneck make procedural design/capacity a leading concern.
- **High rework**: Reopen/rework rate is **24%** (vs 11% for A), consistent with handover complexity and potential mismatches between accommodation needs and standard documentation/eligibility workflows.

## Group D (higher risk and prior cases)

- **Path differences largely policy-justified**: Variant V3 (“… → Risk Screening → Fraud Review → Supervisor Review → …”) is **28%** for Group D vs 9% for A, consistent with high risk share (51%) and policy routing to Fraud Review.
- **Queueing at fraud review is present but not extreme**: Risk Screening → Fraud Review wait is **2.8d** (D) vs 2.2d (A). This seems plausibly legitimate given higher fraud-queue load.
- **Residual fairness signals remain**: Adjusted approval probability is still **−3.1 pp**, and adjusted appeal probability **+5.7 pp**. This could reflect (i) biased risk scoring or over-suspicion, (ii) poorer explanations or more contested denials, or (iii) unobserved complexity not captured by the covariates.

## Cross-cutting: supervisor backlog and tails

- **Supervisor Review → Decision Draft** waiting is higher for B (1.9d) and C (1.6d) than A (1.0d), consistent with backlog in two regions. Because B is concentrated in overloaded regions/officers, this can create structural procedural unfairness even if individual decisions are neutral.
- **Tail risk**: The 90th-percentile cycle time is much higher for B and C. Procedural fairness is often most threatened in the tail because long delays can mean loss of benefits, missed deadlines, or inability to comply with document requests—especially when delays are tied to support pathways.

Overall: many path differences are legitimate (support and fraud routing), but the **waiting-time concentration at support steps**, **resource concentration**, and **extra handovers/escalations** are difficult to justify as necessary consequences of complexity and are central procedural fairness concerns.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least four mechanisms can generate the observed patterns; the evidence suggests a mix of legitimate policy routing and concerning structural effects.

1. **Legitimate needs-based routing (legitimate)**: Language support and accommodation requests trigger additional steps (V4, V5), and high risk triggers Fraud Review (V3). This explains part of the variant distribution differences.

2. **Capacity bottlenecks in specialized queues (concerning)**: Only 7 bilingual caseworkers and a small accommodation team create queueing. Large step-level waits (3.8d and 4.7d) suggest the process effectively penalizes applicants who need support.

3. **Handover-induced friction and error (concerning)**: Multi-actor handovers (general ↔ bilingual; general → accommodation → supervisor) increase the chance of missing information, duplicated checks, and rework. This fits higher rework rates for B and C.

4. **Documentation barriers and “completeness” as an access artifact (concerning)**: Higher incomplete documentation for B and C can arise from language and accessibility barriers, not just applicant behavior. This can increase “Request Missing Documents” and delays, and controlling for it can mask inequity.

5. **Discretionary escalation under uncertainty (concerning/ambiguous)**: Residual increases in Supervisor Review for B and C may reflect risk aversion, inconsistent thresholds, or implicit bias when cases are harder to interpret (language, accommodation). It can also reflect unmeasured complexity, so it requires targeted audit.

6. **Region/channel operational constraints (legitimate but fairness-relevant)**: In-person submissions have longer verification waits; if minority-language applicants disproportionately use in-person channels, operational design can create indirect procedural inequity even without biased intent.

7. **Historically biased risk scoring (concerning)**: `risk_score_band` may encode past disparities and act as a proxy for group status; this can over-route certain groups to Fraud Review, reduce approvals, and increase appeals.

8. **Communication/explanation gaps (concerning)**: If notification content is not accessible (language, disability), denials may be contested more (higher appeals), and applicants may need multiple contacts/rework to comply.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The log supports strong inference about *where* time is spent and *which paths* occur, but weaker inference about *why* they occur and whether residual disparities reflect discrimination versus unmeasured need. Key limitations:

- **Measurement bias in key covariates**:
  - `risk_score_band` may incorporate historical bias or proxy variables correlated with group membership. Treating it as neutral can “explain away” discriminatory routing.
  - `documentation_completeness` can be downstream of language/access barriers; adjusting for it can remove part of the inequity you want to measure.

- **Missing granularity on applicant needs and complexity**:
  - `disability_accommodation_requested` is binary and does not capture type/severity, cognitive load, communication modality, or whether accommodations were actually provided effectively.
  - `language_preference` does not capture literacy, need for interpretation versus translation, or availability of appropriate support.
  - Household and eligibility complexity (income sources, residency documentation, exceptional circumstances) is not represented.

- **Unobserved or coarsely observed workload and queue states**:
  - Workload is only observed at daily granularity; there is no direct measure of queue length, staffing schedules, within-day peaks, interruptions, or multitasking. This limits causal attribution of waiting time to staffing versus policy batching.

- **Appeal and rework quality is not captured**:
  - `appeal_flag` does not indicate appeal outcome, success rate, reasons, or whether an appeal stems from poor explanation, barriers, or actual error.
  - Rework/reopen may be definitional or inconsistently recorded across regions/resources.

- **Outcome is narrow**:
  - `case_outcome` does not capture the quality of reasoning, dignity/respect, partial approvals, benefit amount, or whether benefits were activated timely and correctly.

- **Historical and structural confounding**:
  - Differences in channel and region are themselves potentially outcomes of structural access barriers; controlling for them can understate inequity.
  - Group definitions (A–D) overlap with attributes (language, accommodations, risk/prior cases), making it hard to separate “group effect” from “need effect” without careful stratification by the specific need indicators.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigations should reduce procedural burden and delays **without removing rights-preserving steps**. Each option has trade-offs that must be managed.

- **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review**
  - **What**: Increase bilingual staffing (hiring, contracting interpreters, cross-training), expand accommodation team capacity, and enable region-agnostic remote support pools.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Cost and hiring lead time; potential dilution of expertise if cross-training is shallow; confidentiality and quality assurance for contracted interpretation; induced demand (more applicants appropriately requesting support) that must be planned for.

- **Introduce service-level targets (SLAs) for support steps**
  - **What**: Set explicit timeliness thresholds for “Language Support Contact” and “Accommodation Review” (not just overall cycle time), monitor breaches, and create escalation paths.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Risk of gaming (performing minimal “contact” to meet SLA); pressure to under-record support needs; shifting delays to other queues; requires auditing to ensure SLAs improve real support quality.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers while preserving support**
  - **What**: Reduce general ↔ specialist bouncing by using (i) single-case “ownership” for support-need cases, (ii) embedded consult models (specialist consults without taking over the case), or (iii) early triage that assigns a bilingual/accommodation-capable owner from the start.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Specialists may become bottlenecks if made owners; ownership models can reduce flexibility; consult models require strong coordination and clear responsibility to avoid diffusion of accountability.

- **Decision-explanation improvements (language- and disability-accessible)**
  - **What**: Improve “Applicant Notification” content: plain-language reasons, checklists of required evidence, translated and accessible formats, and proactive contact options to clarify before appeal.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: More drafting time; potential initial increase in appeals if explanations reveal contestable reasoning; legal/compliance review burden; must ensure templates don’t become rigid inappropriately.

- **Monitor adjusted disparities as ongoing governance**
  - **What**: Build dashboards for stratified, adjusted metrics: step-level waiting times (especially support steps), handovers, rework, supervisor review rates, and appeal rates by region/channel/need. Trigger targeted audits when thresholds are exceeded.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Privacy risks and the need for careful access control; false alarms due to small strata; risk of metric gaming; requires human review processes, not only automated alarms.

- **Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` for bias and endogeneity**
  - **What**: Evaluate whether risk scoring is calibrated across groups and whether it uses proxy features; test alternative thresholds or fairness-aware calibration. Treat documentation incompleteness as a support/assistance signal rather than purely applicant deficiency; provide multilingual pre-check assistance.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Reduced fraud-detection sensitivity if thresholds are loosened; increased workload for human review; governance/approval requirements for model changes; political/operational constraints.

- **Upstream documentation support to reduce avoidable document requests**
  - **What**: Multilingual and accessible intake forms, guided checklists, assisted intake for in-person, and proactive reminders tailored to language/accommodation needs.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Implementation and maintenance cost; risk of increasing complexity of forms; must avoid disadvantaging applicants without digital access.

- **Regional workload balancing and queue management**
  - **What**: Rebalance cases across regions (virtual queues), reserve supervisor capacity for overloaded regions, and separate “support step” queues from discretionary escalation queues to prevent cross-contamination.
  - **Side effects / trade-offs**: Loss of local context; data-sharing constraints; possible inconsistency in policy interpretation; change-management burden.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**Calibrated conclusion: procedurally concerning, with uncertainty about discriminatory intent.** Many group differences are plausibly driven by legitimate routing (language support, accommodations, fraud review), but the evidence strongly indicates that **rights-preserving support pathways impose systematically higher waiting times and handover burden** (notably 3.8d and 4.7d queues) due to scarce specialized capacity and regional backlogs. Residual adjusted disparities in approvals and appeals persist (especially for Group B and D), but given measurement limits and potentially biased covariates, they cannot be cleanly attributed to discrimination versus unobserved complexity. The most defensible inference is that the process is **not procedurally equitable in timeliness and burden**, and it warrants targeted redesign and capacity investment to ensure support rights do not function as a procedural penalty.
