# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring changes not only who does work, but where waiting happens, who is accountable, and what skills remain available. The table maps each major proposed change to the causal mechanism and the observable PM4Py-style measure.

| Required link | Restructuring action | Mechanism | Observable process-mining measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal handover reduction | Merge local technical specialists into Technical Shared Service; route specialist and approval work through shared queues | Local specialist round-trips become queue round-trips. Each “send to specialist” and “return advice” is still a handover, so the number of handovers per case may not fall. The number of distinct local actor-to-actor handovers may fall, but shared-service handovers may replace them. | `discover_handover_of_work_network` edge weights; average handovers per case; share of handovers involving Technical Shared Service vs local embedded specialists; handover frequency per 100 cases. |
| Central queue formation | Create Technical Shared Service, Specialist Review, Legal/Risk, and shared approval queues | Cases wait before specialist work and before approval. Waiting time moves from local handover gaps to central queue waiting. A hidden bottleneck appears if queue arrival rate exceeds service capacity. | Performance DFG edge durations; `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; waiting-time quantiles per queue; queue length inferred from arrivals/departures; oldest open case per queue. |
| Specialist utilization | Pool all specialist demand in one queue; send more cases to specialists | Utilization rises because specialists are less likely to idle. However, at high utilization, queueing increases non-linearly. Utilization rising to 90–95% may be an early bottleneck signal rather than a success signal. | Resource busy time; utilization; concurrent active specialist cases; service time; waiting time; number of specialist completions per week. |
| Rework | Standardize specialist review, legal/risk review, and difficult Quality Check | Straightforward cases may see less rework because decisions are more consistent. Complex or locally sensitive cases may see more rework because the specialist loses local context. | `rework_flag`; `get_rework_cases_per_activity`; rework rate after specific handover types; outcome distribution; rework rate stratified by case complexity and customer segment. |
| Local expertise loss | Remove embedded technical specialists; send high-complexity and legal/risk cases away | Local caseworkers lose informal access to technical advice. Over time, they stop developing local technical judgment. Cases with regional customer conventions may be misinterpreted by central specialists. | `local_expertise_flag` as covariate; activity-based resource similarity; `discover_organizational_roles`; frequency of local informal consultation handovers; trend in complex-case rework over weeks. |
| Role clarity | Replace named local specialists and one local supervisor with shared queues | Work becomes more anonymous. Specialist roles become interchangeable “queue workers.” Ownership of decisions is blurred, especially for approvals. | `discover_organizational_roles`; number of resources per role; `discover_working_together_network`; handover reason distribution; percentage of cases with a named owner remaining from intake to close. |
| Resilience to absence | Central pool can cover a single specialist’s absence if cross-trained | Central pooling can improve resilience if the pool is cross-trained. But if local specialists are removed, a single central specialist absence can slow all teams. Local backup capacity is lost. | Cross-team handover frequency; `discover_subcontracting_network`; effect of known absence periods on queue waiting and rework; recovery time after absence events. |
| Fairness across teams/customer segments | Same routing rules for all teams and segments | Same rules do not produce identical outcomes. East may improve because E_Tech was overloaded; North and South may lose local expertise. Customers needing language or regional accommodation may face more handovers. | Stratified waiting times and cycle times by team, customer segment, priority, and complexity; SLA miss rates by segment; statistical dispersion between segments; customer satisfaction proxy. |

A key forecast is that the “nominal handover reduction” may be partly illusory. The management estimate shows:

- local caseworker  Technical Shared Service: 41 per 100 cases  
- Technical Shared Service  local caseworker: 36 per 100 cases  

That is still a handover pair, just with a central resource instead of a local one. The central change is in who performs the specialist work, not necessarily in how many times the case changes hands.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic scenario

In this scenario, the shared service relieves East, standardizes advice, and does not destroy local context.

- **Handover pattern**  
  Total handovers per case fall about 5–10%. The local technical loop is replaced by a cleaner shared-service pull pattern. Cross-team handovers fall from 6 to about 2–3 per 100 cases. Local caseworkers do not need repeated clarification contacts because specialist advice is complete.

- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**  
  Specialist queue waiting stabilizes at about 3–4 hours after an initial ramp-up. Legal/Risk waiting stays near 9–10 hours because triage improves. Approval queue waiting stabilizes near 2–3 hours. High-complexity cases do not experience a substantially worse tail.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  Median cycle time improves 5–10%. East improves most, with its 90th percentile falling from 22.8 days toward 18–19 days. North and South are stable or slightly better. High-complexity 90th percentile does not worsen.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  Overall rework falls by about 1–2 percentage points. Straightforward cases benefit from standardized decisions. Complex cases are not worse because specialists retain enough local information through better case notes.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  Queue roles are clearly defined with SLAs. A named caseworker remains responsible for the overall case. Supervisor approval is centralized but the approving decision is traceable to a named reviewer.

- **Fairness implications**  
  East improves; North and South remain stable. Regional customer segments do not see materially worse waits because local caseworkers still handle intake and customer contact. High-priority cases are served without starving medium-priority work.

- **Early warning signs**  
  Specialist queue waiting remains below target; queue length does not grow; rework after specialist handover does not rise; local staff report that informal advice is still available through case notes and desk contact; team-level SLA miss rates do not diverge.

## Central scenario

This is the most likely scenario if the restructuring is implemented without strong mitigations.

- **Handover pattern**  
  Total handovers per case increase about 5–10%. The local caseworker  embedded specialist  same caseworker loop is replaced by local caseworker  Technical Shared Service  local caseworker. Legal/Risk handovers rise from 9 to 12 per 100 cases. Cross-team handovers fall, but the overall handover count remains high because shared-service advice usually returns to the same local caseworker.

- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**  
  Specialist waiting rises from about 3.2 hours to 5–6 hours. Legal/Risk waiting rises from 9.6 hours to 11–13 hours because Legal and Risk are already near capacity and now absorb triage. Approval queue waiting settles near 3–4 hours. High-complexity cases wait 8+ hours in the shared service.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  Median cycle time is approximately stable or improves slightly. The 90th percentile worsens 10–20%. East improves because E_Tech was overloaded. North and South see their 90th percentile worsen, especially for complex cases.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  Overall rework is flat or rises about 0.5 percentage points. Simple cases improve. Complex cases and cases requiring regional knowledge worsen. Rework after specialist advice increases because the advice is less contextual.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  Technical roles become clearer as a separate shared service, but accountability becomes weaker. The removed local supervisor creates ambiguous ownership for approvals. Quality checks are split between local and central staff, making it harder to know who is responsible for a missed defect.

- **Fairness implications**  
  East benefits. North and South may lose local expertise and see slower service on complex cases. Customers needing language or regional accommodation experience more handovers. High-priority cases are served first, while medium-priority cases wait longer.

- **Early warning signs**  
  Specialist queue p90 waiting exceeds 8 hours; Technical Shared Service backlog grows; Legal/Risk queue waiting increases; rework after Technical Shared Service handover exceeds the old embedded-specialist rework rate; handover reasons shift toward “queue transfer” rather than “local consultation”; staff report loss of informal advice.

## Adverse scenario

In this scenario, the central shared service becomes a hidden bottleneck and local expertise is lost faster than expected.

- **Handover pattern**  
  Handovers per case increase 15–25%. Cases bounce between local caseworkers, Technical Shared Service, approval queue, and Legal/Risk queue. Cross-team handovers do not fall as expected because local technical support is no longer available to cover absences. The handover network becomes more fragmented.

- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**  
  Technical Shared Service median waiting exceeds 10 hours. Legal/Risk waiting exceeds 15 hours. Approval queue backlog grows. High-complexity cases can wait days. Simple cases still flow quickly, which keeps the average cycle time from exploding.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  Median cycle time may stay stable or improve for simple cases. The 90th percentile and 99th percentile worsen sharply. East initially improves, then degrades as the bottleneck affects all teams. High-complexity cases have the worst cycle times ever recorded.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  Overall rework rises 3–5 percentage points. Rework is highest for complex and regionally sensitive cases. Difficult Quality Check centralization adds another handover and does not catch defects. Cases enter Rework multiple times.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  No one feels like the owner. Specialists are anonymous queue workers. Supervisors are no longer clearly accountable for approvals. When something goes wrong, the event log shows many resources touched the case, but no one is responsible.

- **Fairness implications**  
  East benefits initially, then suffers. North and South are harmed. Smaller customer segments and segments needing regional accommodation receive worse service. High-priority cases starve normal-priority work, and fairness across teams worsens.

- **Early warning signs**  
  Waiting-time p90 exceeds 8 hours for multiple consecutive days; queue length grows monotonically; specialist utilization exceeds 95% while throughput stays flat; handover reasons “escalation” and “exception” increase; local staff report frustration; rework after specialist handover rises sharply; staff begin leaving or requesting transfers.

Leading indicators are more likely to reveal queueing and fairness problems within days. Lagging indicators are necessary to reveal learning loss, role erosion, and long-term rework changes.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

A stable or improved average cycle time can hide serious problems. The following risks are particularly important.

1. **Tail latency**  
   The average cycle time can improve because simple automated cases are completed faster, while difficult cases wait longer. The restructuring may reduce the median but worsen the 90th and 99th percentiles. The event log must be analyzed with quantiles, not only means or medians. Track p90, p99, and oldest open case age.

2. **High-complexity case regression**  
   Because all high-complexity cases are routed to Technical Shared Service, the mix of cases entering that queue changes. High-complexity cases may wait behind routine tasks. The overall cycle time can look acceptable while the most important cases become slower. Segment by `case_complexity` and `required_specialty`.

3. **Local knowledge loss**  
   Local technical expertise is invisible in average cycle time until it is gone. Local caseworkers may stop receiving informal technical advice, but the effect appears later as increased rework on regional or complex cases. The event log may show a decline in local technical handovers. Over time, cases that used to be resolved locally require central specialist help. This is a slow-moving risk.

4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments**  
   A central queue may appear neutral because all cases enter the same queue. But teams have different volumes, complexity mixes, and customer needs. East may improve while North and South worsen. Customers requiring language accommodation may see more handovers. Fairness must be measured by stratified waiting times, SLA misses, and satisfaction by team, customer segment, and priority.

5. **Learning and deskilling**  
   If all hard cases are routed away, local staff stop practicing technical skills. This does not affect average cycle time immediately. It appears later as a reduced ability to handle moderate cases when the central queue is overloaded. Learning effects can be proxied by tracking how often local caseworkers resolve cases without specialist referral, and whether that capability declines over time.

6. **Accountability loss**  
   Shared queues distribute work across many anonymous resources. Average cycle time does not measure who made a decision or who should fix a failure. After the restructuring, it may be impossible to identify which specialist or supervisor was responsible for an error. Use role discovery, named-owner continuity, and handover reasons to monitor this.

7. **Exception handling degradation**  
   Standardized queues work well for normal cases but poorly for edge cases. Exceptions may be handled outside the modeled process, through manual workarounds, repeated reassignments, or unrecorded actions. Rare variants, high `reassignment_count`, and unusual handover reasons are early signs that exception handling is failing.

8. **Capacity illusion**  
   High specialist utilization looks efficient, but utilization approaching 100% means queueing time grows non-linearly. The average cycle time can remain stable while waiting times climb because work is accumulating in queues. Little’s Law implies that queue length will grow unless arrivals slow or capacity increases.

# 5. Pilot design

The restructuring should be tested in a controlled, phased rollout.

- **Pilot scope**  
  Implement a two-phase step-wedge pilot.

  - Phase 0: collect an 8-week baseline under the current model.
  - Phase 1: create shared queues for Specialist Review, Legal/Risk, difficult Quality Check, and approvals, but leave local technical specialists in place. This tests central queue behavior without immediately destroying local expertise.
  - Phase 2: migrate technical specialists into Technical Shared Service one team at a time, starting with East, then South, then North. Teams not yet migrated serve as contemporaneous controls.

- **Comparison group**  
  Use teams not yet migrated as controls. Also use matched cases by `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, `customer_segment`, and `priority`. Where operationally feasible, randomly assign eligible cases to the shared-service route versus the local route for a subset of cases.

- **Pre/post observation window**  
  Use at least 8 weeks before each phase and 12–16 weeks after each phase. Review results in 4-week rolling windows. Include both high-volume and low-volume periods to avoid seasonal bias.

- **Segmentation**  
  Segment every analysis by:
  - team: North, South, East
  - case complexity: low, medium, high
  - required specialty: technical, legal, risk, security, none
  - customer segment: regional, language-sensitive, standard, etc.
  - priority: high, medium, low

- **Guardrail metrics**  
  Define escalation thresholds before the pilot starts. Illustrative thresholds:
  - p90 Technical Shared Service waiting time above 8 hours for 3 consecutive business days
  - high-complexity p90 cycle time worse than baseline by more than 20%
  - team-level SLA miss rate worse than baseline by more than 2 percentage points
  - rework after shared specialist handover worse than baseline local specialist handover by more than 3 percentage points
  - median waiting-time gap between any two teams or customer segments more than 50% above the baseline gap
  - drop of more than 30% in local informal consultation handovers
  - more than 5% of cases with no named owner or accountable supervisor

- **Staff feedback collection**  
  Collect weekly one-question pulse surveys. Conduct biweekly structured interviews with caseworkers, specialists, and supervisors. Ask specifically:
  - Did you get informal technical advice when you needed it?
  - Do you understand who owns the decision?
  - Are you learning or losing skills?
  - Are customers receiving the same regional accommodation as before?

- **Rollback or adjustment triggers**  
  If a guardrail metric is breached for two consecutive weeks, trigger a protocol review. If two or more guardrails are red, roll back the most recent phase and restore embedded specialists temporarily. Adjust the design before retrying, for example by adding capacity, separating Legal/Risk from Technical Shared Service, or reintroducing a named approval owner.

---

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: pilot first, with modifications. Do not approve full rollout yet.**

The proposed restructuring has plausible benefits, especially for East, where E_Tech is already overloaded. However, the risks to North and South, local expertise, learning, fairness, and accountability are too large to accept without evidence.

The recommendation is conditional on monitoring indicators:

- Proceed to the pilot described above.
- Expand only if the pilot shows, for at least 8 consecutive weeks:
  - no guardrail breach,
  - p90 specialist queue waiting below the pre-agreed threshold,
  - complex-case rework no worse than baseline,
  - fairness gaps across teams and customer segments not widened,
  - named ownership and accountability retained for key decisions,
  - staff feedback indicates local expertise is being preserved or intentionally developed.

The proposal should be modified before piloting:

1. Keep a formal local consultation channel so local caseworkers can ask a specialist informally before making a formal referral.
2. Separate Legal/Risk and difficult Quality Check queues from the Technical Shared Service queue to avoid one hidden bottleneck.
3. Retain a named case owner and a named approval owner.
4. Add a rotation or shadowing program so local staff continue learning technical skills.
5. Define queue priority rules explicitly so high-priority work does not starve medium-priority work.

Management should not infer success from early average cycle times. Early averages can appear stable or improved for several misleading reasons:

- Simple cases may be completed faster while complex cases wait longer.
- The case mix may shift because more cases are routed to the shared service.
- High specialist utilization may reflect queue growth, not productive service.
- Phase 1 improvements may come from extra attention rather than from the structural change.

Watch the tails, the strata, the queues, the rework, and the staff. Only then can the restructuring be judged.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

Below is a reproducible analysis sketch. Adapt column names and values to the actual event log. The goal is to compare before and after periods while preserving case- and event-level attributes.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format logs
# ------------------------------------------------------------

def load_and_format(path):
    df = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=["time:timestamp"])
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
        # If you have start_timestamp for each activity, pass it here:
        # start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
    )
    return df

before = load_and_format("before.csv")
after = load_and_format("after.csv")

before["period"] = "before"
after["period"] = "after"

log = pd.concat([before, after], ignore_index=True)
log = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])

# Approximate waiting time since previous event in the same case.
log["wait_since_prev_h"] = (
    log.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
    .diff()
    .dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
)

# If start_timestamp is available, compute service time.
if "start_timestamp" in log.columns:
    log["service_time_h"] = (
        (log["time:timestamp"] - log["start_timestamp"])
        .dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    )

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Case-level summary metrics
# ------------------------------------------------------------

def case_metrics(frame):
    cycle = frame.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].agg(["min", "max"])
    cycle["duration_d"] = (cycle["max"] - cycle["min"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400.0

    n_cases = len(cycle)

    rework_cases = frame.loc[
        frame["rework_flag"] == True, "case:concept:name"
    ].nunique()

    sla_cases = frame.loc[
        frame["outcome"] == "SLA_miss", "case:concept:name"
    ].nunique()

    return {
        "cases": n_cases,
        "median_cycle_d": cycle["duration_d"].median(),
        "p90_cycle_d": cycle["duration_d"].quantile(0.90),
        "p99_cycle_d": cycle["duration_d"].quantile(0.99),
        "max_cycle_d": cycle["duration_d"].max(),
        "rework_rate": rework_cases / n_cases,
        "sla_miss_rate": sla_cases / n_cases,
    }

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Compare before and after
# ------------------------------------------------------------

for period in ["before", "after"]:
    lp = log[log["period"] == period].copy()
    print("\n=== PERIOD:", period, "===")
    print(case_metrics(lp))

    # Handover-of-work network
    hov = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(lp)
    edges = list(hov.edges(data=True))
    total_handovers = sum(w.get("weight", 1) for _, _, w in edges)
    n_cases = lp["case:concept:name"].nunique()
    print("HOV edges:", len(edges))
    print("HOV total handovers:", total_handovers)
    print("HOV per 100 cases:", 100 * total_handovers / n_cases)

    # Working-together network
    wtt = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(lp)

    # Subcontracting network
    sub = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(lp)

    # Activity-based resource similarity
    sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(lp)

    # Organizational roles
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(lp)
    print("Discovered roles (first 3):", roles[:3])

    # Frequency and performance DFGs
    freq_dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(lp)
    perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(lp)

    # Show the slowest performance DFG edges
    slow_edges = sorted(perf_dfg.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:5]
    print("Slowest performance DFG edges:")
    for (act_a, act_b), duration in slow_edges:
        print("  ", act_a, "->", act_b, ":", duration)

    # Variants
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(lp)
    print("Number of variants:", len(variants))

    # Rework per activity
    rework_by_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(lp)
    print("Rework cases per activity:")
    print(rework_by_activity)

    # Waiting/service time summary
    if "start_timestamp" in log.columns:
        lp_with_wait = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(lp)
        wait_cols = [
            c for c in lp_with_wait.columns
            if "waiting" in c.lower() or "service" in c.lower()
        ]
        print("Waiting/service time summary:")
        print(lp_with_wait[wait_cols].describe())
    else:
        print("Using wait_since_prev_h as waiting time proxy.")
        print(lp.groupby("concept:name")["wait_since_prev_h"]
               .agg(["median", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)]))
```

def compare_stratum(log, column, value):
    mask = log[column] == value
    lp = log[mask]
    print(f"\n--- {column} = {value} ---")
    for period in ["before", "after"]:

    compare_stratum(log, "customer_segment", segment)

## Handover and rework connection

```python
# ------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Handover-specific rework
# ------------------------------------------------------------

def analyze_handover_rework(lp):
    lp = lp.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).copy()
    lp["prev_act"] = lp.groupby("case:concept:name")["concept:name"].shift(1)
    lp["prev_res"] = lp.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(1)
    lp["is_handover"] = lp["org:resource"] != lp["prev_res"]

    case_rework = lp.groupby("case:concept:name")["rework_flag"].max()
    lp["case_rework"] = lp["case:concept:name"].map(case_rework)

    handover_rework = (
        lp.loc[lp["is_handover"] & lp["case_rework"]]
        .groupby(["prev_act", "concept:name"])
        .size()
    )
    return handover_rework

for period in ["before", "after"]:
    lp = log[log["period"] == period].copy()
    print(f"\nHandover rework table for {period}")
    print(analyze_handover_rework(lp).head(10))
```

The main principle is to compare not only averages but distributions, stratified by team, complexity, specialty, and customer segment. Use leading queue metrics to detect bottlenecks early, and use lagging metrics to assess long-term learning, fairness, and accountability effects.
