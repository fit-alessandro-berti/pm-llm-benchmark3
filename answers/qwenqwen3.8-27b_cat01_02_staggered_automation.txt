Primary strategy is branch-specific synthetic control with augmented covariates and a dose sensitivity check. It is preferable because the panel has only five branches and two never-treated units, so conventional cluster-robust event-study standard errors would be overconfident. Synthetic control explicitly asks whether Delta and Epsilon (and Gamma before July) can reconstruct each adopter’s pre-adoption trajectory in cycle time, rework, escalation, and CSAT. If the pre-fit is poor, the comparison is weak. It also produces branch-specific dynamic effects rather than a single pooled X%, which is important because Alpha, Beta, and Gamma adopted at different times and their treatment intensity ramped from 4–6% to 25–50%. The method can incorporate arrivals, FTE, and high-complexity shares to account for the May product change and routing campaign. It also makes interference visible: if fraud-desk or specialist-queue spillovers affect controls, the synthetic counterfactual will fail in the same months. The main limitation is the small donor pool; therefore the estimate should be treated as a bounded descriptive counterfactual, not a precise causal point.

| Rework displacement | `rework_pct`, `escalation_pct` | Automation catches missing information early; rework falls | Mis-triage creates new information-request loops; rework rises | Alpha rework falls in Apr then rises; Beta rework falls in Apr then stabilizes; Gamma rework falls post-adoption; controls rework rises | May product change also raises rework in controls |

| Survey selection | `csat_1to5`, response rate | CSAT stable or improves with adequate, representative response | Dissatisfied customers under- or over-respond, masking harm or benefit | CSAT changes are small; response rates vary 20–45%; no branch-month response counts | Cannot identify respondent selection or case linkage |

The Python section fits a descriptive treatment-intensity panel model, but the branch-month aggregate dose is post-treatment and cannot by itself identify a causal dose-response.

### 4. Pre-trends and regression to the mean

Arithmetic facts: Alpha and Beta pre-periods are relatively stable; Gamma worsens from Apr to Jun before adoption; all branches show a March escalation increase and a May complexity increase. Causal interpretation: Alpha and Beta were selected because of historically poor cycle times, so if their pre-period included temporarily elevated times, post-adoption improvement could partly be regression to the mean. Gamma’s post-adoption improvement follows a bad Apr-Jun and could also be regression to the mean or a seasonal reversal; controls also improve from Apr-Jun to Jul-Aug, so part of Gamma’s apparent gain is common to the system. Therefore the naive DiD differences should not be read as clean automation effects.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma followed a pilot | Could bias toward improvement if pre-period was unusually bad, or toward harm if adopters were structurally harder | Pre-trends, synthetic-control pre-fit, comparison with similar never-treated branches, longer history | Over- or under-state automation effect |
| Nonparallel trends | Branches differ in size, complexity, and trajectory; controls may not mirror adopters | Unknown | Event-study pre-trends, placebo tests, synthetic-control diagnostics | DiD/synthetic-control comparison invalid |
| Time-varying confounding | Fraud desk, routing campaign, staffing changes, and product change occur during rollout | Mixed; could bias toward harm or improvement | Include timing of shocks, fraud-desk waits, FTE per arrival, complexity; process logs | Automation effect confounded with operational changes |
| Treatment intensity | `automation=1` does not mean all cases were automated; use ramps from 4–6% to 25–50% | Binary effect may average early low-dose and later high-dose periods | Dose-response analysis, case-level routing, `automated_fast_path_pct` by risk class | Wrong estimand and wrong rollout inference |
| Spillovers through fraud desk | Automated triage sends borderline cases to a centralized fraud desk serving all branches | Could harm controls or adopters depending on queue state | Fraud-desk workload and wait by branch, specialist-queue length, cross-branch routing | Interference invalidates local treatment/control comparison |
| Staffing pressure | FTE changes and arrivals change simultaneously; FTE is productive FTE, not headcount | Alpha pressure may bias toward harm; Beta capacity may bias toward improvement | FTE per arrival, task-level staffing, queue lengths, overtime or backlog | Cycle-time changes attributed to automation instead of capacity |
| Case mix | May product change increases high-complexity share in several branches | Likely biases toward harm if complexity increases cycle time/rework | Adjust for `high_complexity_pct`, case-level risk class, compare same complexity strata | Overstate harm or hide benefit |
| Anticipation | Managers may change local practices before adoption because rollout was known | Unknown | Pre-period local practice changes, communication dates, early `automated_fast_path_pct` movement | Pre-contamination weakens pre/post contrast |
| Survey nonresponse | CSAT response rates vary 20–45% and branch-month counts are unavailable | Unknown | Collect response counts, respondent case IDs, weighting, respondent characteristics | CSAT effect unidentifiable |
| Measurement aggregation | Branch-month medians/p90s are not case-level distributions | Can mask heterogeneity and queue effects | Raw event logs, case-level cycle times, queue-state data | Cannot infer mechanism or tail risk |
| Small number of branches | Only five branches and two never-treated units; five clusters | Overconfidence and unstable standard errors | Permutation tests, bootstrap, synthetic-control placebos, wider credible intervals | False precision and poor policy inference |
| Dynamic treatment effects | Learning, ramp-up, and delayed effects mean early post differs from steady state | Early effects may under- or overstate mature effect | Event-study by relative time, longer post-period, version logs | A single X% effect is not meaningful |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Next branch: Epsilon only, with Delta held out as a never-treated control for at least three months. Epsilon has high complexity and weaker CSAT, so the operational upside is potentially larger, and the evaluation can test whether automation helps or harms high-complexity cases. If Epsilon is judged too risky, use Delta instead, but do not expand both at once because that would remove the last never-treated controls.

Phase-in: enable automation for a stratified 50% of eligible Epsilon cases by customer ID or queue, with the remaining 50% on manual triage, stratified by risk class and expected fast-path eligibility. If the system can only be enabled at branch level, enable Epsilon and keep Delta untreated. Start with low/medium complexity cases and exclude highest-risk or fraud-sensitive cases until guardrails are met.

Measurements: require at least three months of stable pre-period after the May product change, with 12 weeks preferred; collect at least 12 weeks of post-period, preferably 24 weeks, to observe learning and tail effects. Record case-level event logs for Register, triage, specialist review, rework, Resolve, Close; queue lengths and waiting times for fraud desk and specialist review; `automated_fast_path_pct` by risk class; FTE per arrival; cycle-time distributions including median, p90, and p99; rework reasons; escalation reasons; and CSAT with response counts and respondent case IDs.

Spillover monitoring: track centralized fraud-desk workload and wait by branch, specialist-queue length, cross-branch routing, and arrivals. If control-branch fraud waits rise after Epsilon adoption, treat the effect as interfered and widen uncertainty.

Guardrails: pause or roll back if p90 cycle time increases by more than 10% relative to Delta, rework increases by more than 2 percentage points, CSAT falls by more than 0.1 point, escalation exceeds a pre-specified operational threshold, or any privacy/safety incident occurs.

Decision rule: scale to Delta only if pre-trends are parallel, no guardrail is breached, and the event-study/synthetic-control estimate shows median and p90 cycle time are non-inferior or improved with credible intervals that exclude material harm. Otherwise pause and redesign triage routing or staffing.

## 10. Final claim ladder
