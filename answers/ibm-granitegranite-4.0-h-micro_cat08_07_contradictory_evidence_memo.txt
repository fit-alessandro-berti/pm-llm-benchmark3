# Executive Memo

**Subject:** Decision on Next Steps for Fiber-Installation Process Redesign (Redesign R)

After a thorough analysis of process-mining evidence and process-discovery findings, I recommend that management **run a targeted regional experiment (Alternative D)** before deciding on a national rollout of Redesign R. This approach balances the benefits of the redesign with its shortcomings, allowing us to gather more precise data on the process's performance across various customer segments and risk profiles.

## Evidence Table

| Category | Evidence |
|----------|----------|
| **Facts** | - Pilot R reduced median cycle time from 14.2 to 10.8 days (Fact 1).<br>- Pilot R improved first-time-right installation for simple houses (Fact 6).<br>- Pilot R worsened p90 and p95 cycle times (Fact 2, 3).<br>- Pilot R increased complaints within 30 days from 6.1% to 9.8% (Fact 7).<br>- Coordinator utilization reached 93% in two regions (Fact 10). |
| **Interpretations** | - The process model fits the simplified coordinator path well (Interpretation 1).<br>- Many cases in the pilot followed the coordinator path, leading to higher precision in that segment (Interpretation 2).<br>- Simplification of handovers improved accountability but reduced resilience (Interpretation 3).<br>- Segment-specific issues (e.g., apartments, old buildings) were not adequately addressed (Interpretation 4). |
| **Assumptions** | - The predictive rule for field surveys is accurate for medium-risk addresses (Assumption 1).<br>- Building-risk predictive models are reliable (Assumption 2).<br>- Complaint handling improvements are primarily due to process changes (Assumption 3).<br>- Scaling R nationally would replicate pilot conditions (Assumption 4). |

## Explanation of Metric Conflicts

The evidence presents a paradoxical situation where Redesign R shows **improved median cycle time** but **worsened tail duration**. This conflict arises because the redesign:

1. **Improved Efficiency for Simple Cases**: By merging address validation and appointment scheduling, the process reduced the median time for straightforward installations (Fact 1). However, this improvement is limited to a subset of cases (34% of pilot cases).

2. **Worsened Tail Duration**: The redesign inadvertently increased variability. The p90 and p95 cycle times increased significantly (Fact 2, 3). This is likely due to the **skip rule for field surveys** and **manual activation** becoming more common in complex cases (Facts 4, 5). When these exceptions occur, they significantly prolong the overall process.

3. **Fewer Handovers but Lower Resilience**: Fewer handovers (Fact 6) improved accountability and coordination (Fact 8). However, this simplification **reduced resilience**. When coordinators were absent, cases stalled because the process lacked fallback mechanisms (Interpretation 3). This trade-off highlights a need for **balance between efficiency and robustness**.

4. **Visible Cost vs. Rework/Downstream Cost**: The redesign reduced the visible cost (Fact 3) but **increased rework and downstream costs** (Fact 7). Complaints shifted from "waiting for appointment" to "installed but not working," indicating that the redesign did not fully address the underlying quality issues.

5. **Segment-Specific Performance**: The redesign showed mixed results across segments. Simple houses benefited, but **apartments and old buildings experienced worsening performance** (Facts 5, 6). This suggests that the predictive rules and segment-specific handling are **not robust enough**.

## Decision Alternatives

1. **Scale Redesign R Nationally (A)**: This would maximize efficiency gains but risks exacerbating the tail duration, rework, and activation issues across all segments. The high coordination load and low resilience could lead to operational bottlenecks and customer dissatisfaction.

2. **Rollback to Baseline (B)**: This would revert to the established process, ensuring stability and reliability. However, it would forfeit the efficiency gains and fail to address the root causes of the identified issues.

3. **Scale Only Coordinator Ownership (C)**: This would retain the coordination benefits while suspending the problematic aspects (survey skipping, post-billing activation). It would likely improve accountability and reduce handovers but would not address segment-specific performance or downstream costs.

4. **Controlled Regional Experiment (D)**: This approach allows for **targeted learning** by experimenting with better segment/risk measurement. It provides a controlled environment to test the redesign's components across different building risks and customer segments without fully committing to a national rollout.

5. **Hybrid Deployment (E)**: This would involve limited implementation of Redesign R for simple low-risk houses, with controls for more complex cases. While it offers a middle ground, it risks **fragmentation** and inconsistent application across regions.

## Final Recommendation

**Run a controlled regional experiment (Alternative D)** with structured measurements (M1-M3) before deciding on Redesign R's national rollout. The experiment should focus on:

- **Randomized pilot** of survey skipping by building-risk stratum (M1) to validate the predictive rule's effectiveness across different segments.
- **Instrumentation** of start timestamps for critical events (M2) to provide precise timing data and identify bottlenecks.
- **Structured reason codes** for failures and escalations (M3) to dissect the root causes of complaints and rework.

**Immediate Actions**:
- Initiate M1 in two pilot regions with varying building risks.
- Deploy M3 across all pilot regions to capture detailed reasons for process deviations.
- Collect M2 data to refine process timing and identify hidden delays.

**What to Pause**: The rollout of Redesign R nationally pending the results of the regional experiment. This pause ensures we do not introduce further variability or operational strain.

**What to Scale**: Post-experiment, if M1 and M3 confirm the redesign's effectiveness and robustness across segments, consider scaling the successful components (e.g., coordinator ownership) with enhanced monitoring and adjustments.

**Evidence Threshold for Rollout**:
- Median cycle time improvement must be sustained across all segments.
- Tail duration and complaint rates must not exceed baseline levels.
- Coordinator utilization should remain within manageable limits (e.g., 85%).

By adopting this measured approach, management can **validate the redesign's potential** while **mitigating risks** associated with its complex implementation. This strategy ensures a data-driven decision that aligns with operational goals and customer satisfaction.
