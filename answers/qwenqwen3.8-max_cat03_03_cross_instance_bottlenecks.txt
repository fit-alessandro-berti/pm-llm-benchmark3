## 1. Interaction-graph description

The evidence is best represented as a typed, time-stamped object graph rather than as a single case-centric log. The important node types are:

- **Job**: J1–J6, the operational units whose flow times managers experience.
- **Component**: C1–C6, each required by one job.
- **ComponentLot**: L7, L8, which couple otherwise separate jobs through shared quality status.
- **Machine**: CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1.
- **Tool**: FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3.
- **Batch**: B1, B2, which create synchronization joins among jobs.
- **Shipment**: S1, S2, S3, which create terminal consolidation joins.

Important edge types and qualifiers include:

- `requires`: Job  Component. Directly observed in the O2O table.
- `contains`: ComponentLot  Component. Directly observed.
- `batches`: Batch  Job. Directly observed.
- `consolidates`: Shipment  Job. Directly observed.
- `relates_to_event`: Event  multiple objects. Directly observed for events such as `Batch Cure`, `Quarantine Lot`, `Release Lot`, and `Machine Process`.
- `uses_tool` / `holds_tool`: Job or Batch  Tool. Partially observed through acquire/release timestamps and event co-occurrence; waiting for a tool is inferred because no explicit `Wait for Tool` activity exists.
- `processed_on`: Job  Machine. Observed through machine-related events, but not always sufficient to infer when the job was ready.
- `blocked_by_lot`: Job  ComponentLot. Inferred or integration-dependent because lot events are inconsistently linked to jobs.
- `waits_for_partner`: Job  Shipment or Job  Job. Inferred from packing and dispatch timestamps.

Machine capacity is ranked last because the object-centric evidence shows idle machine time coexisting with waiting jobs. That pattern is more consistent with synchronization, tooling, quality policy, and consolidation than with saturated machine capacity.

## 4. What flattening misses

**Job flattening** makes visible the sequence of job-level activities: release, stage, machine process, inspect, rework, pack, dispatch. It hides batch synchronization because a single `Batch Cure` event is attached to multiple jobs. It hides lot quarantine and release because those events are not directly job-sourced in the extraction. It hides tool waiting because there is no explicit wait activity. It can duplicate shared events across jobs and create the misleading conclusion that all jobs are statistically normal, even though J1 waits 355 minutes after packing for S1.

**Machine flattening** makes visible machine utilization, activity sequences, and gaps between machine events. It hides the jobs waiting outside the machine. It can duplicate batch events as machine activity and hide the fact that OVEN-1 is idle from 12:55 to 14:50 because RACK-7 is unavailable or B2 is not ready. A misleading conclusion is that low utilization means no bottleneck. In fact, idle machines can be caused by upstream synchronization or tool unavailability.

**Batch flattening** makes visible batch formation, start, cure, and unload. It hides the dispersion of job readiness inside the batch. It may show B1 starting at 12:05 without revealing that J1 and J2 were ready hours earlier. It can misleadingly suggest that batch cycle time is acceptable while concealing the synchronization penalty paid by early jobs.

**Tool flattening** could make visible FIX-A holds, RACK-7 cleaning/quarantine, and CAL-3 recalibration. With current logging, it hides the jobs affected by those tool states because there is no explicit `Wait for Tool`. It may misleadingly show tools as busy or in cleaning without showing the downstream queue. For example, FIX-A’s hold by J3 from 09:00 to 11:40 becomes meaningful only when linked to J4’s CNC-2 wait.

**ComponentLot flattening** makes visible lot containment and lot status events such as `Quarantine Lot` and `Release Lot`. It hides job impact when lot events are inconsistently related to jobs. It can duplicate lot events across components and jobs, or fail to link them at all. A misleading conclusion is that L7’s quarantine is only a quality event, when it may also be a schedule-coupling mechanism between J2 and J5.

**Shipment flattening** makes visible `Create Shipment`, `Load`, and `Dispatch`. It hides the time between job packing and shipment loading. It therefore makes S1 look efficient: load and dispatch take minutes. The misleading conclusion is that shipment is not a bottleneck, even though J1 waits from 13:30 to 19:25 for J4.

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because the constraint is not machine processing capacity. Jobs can be waiting for batch partners, tools, lot release, rack cleaning, calibration, or shipment partners. Machine event gaps measure only whether the machine is doing something, not whether the production system is moving the job.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

**Narrative 1: FIX-A, CNC-2, batch timing, and S1 shipment**

Objects: Job J3, Job J4, Tool FIX-A, Machine CNC-2, Batch B1/B2, Shipment S1.

- Temporal precedence: J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00. J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50. CNC-2 is idle 10:05–10:47. J3 releases FIX-A at 11:40. J3 is ready for B1 at 11:55. B1 starts at 12:05. J4’s CNC-2 process starts at 11:45. J4 is packed at 19:10. S1 dispatches at 19:25, holding J1 from 13:30.
- Resource feasibility: If FIX-A is singular and required for both J3 and J4, J4 cannot plausibly start CNC-2 while J3 holds it.
- Policy constraints: There is no explicit `Wait for Tool`, and shipment S1 requires both J1 and J4.
- Causal speculation: J3’s long FIX-A hold may delay J4’s CNC operation, which may delay J4 packing, which may delay S1 and therefore J1. This is plausible but not proven because J4 may have had other setup or quality constraints.

**Narrative 2: L7 quarantine, override, B2, TEST-1, and S3**

Objects: ComponentLot L7, Components C2/C5, Jobs J2/J5, Batches B1/B2, Machine TEST-1, Tool CAL-3, Shipment S3.

- Temporal precedence: L7 is quarantined at 10:20. J2 nevertheless enters B1 before release through a supervisor override. B1 starts at 12:05. L7 is released at 13:50. J5 is ready for B2 at 14:40. B2 starts at 14:50. J5 tests 16:10–16:35. CAL-3 is recalibrated until 17:20. J6 tests 17:25–17:50. S3 dispatches after the last job is packed.
- Resource feasibility: L7 contains both C2 and C5, so its status can affect both J2 and J5.
- Policy constraints: Quality quarantine appears to block J5 but not J2, due to override. Final testing requires CAL-3.
- Causal speculation: L7 release may have delayed J5 enough to push B2 later, and CAL-3 recalibration then delayed J6. However, RACK-7 cleaning and B2 synchronization also contribute.

**Narrative 3: RACK-7 cleaning and OVEN-1 idle time**

Objects: Batch B1/B2, Machine OVEN-1, Tool RACK-7, Jobs J4/J5.

- Temporal precedence: B1 starts at 12:05. OVEN-1 becomes idle from 12:55. RACK-7 is cleaning/quarantine until 14:35. J4 is ready at 13:10. J5 is ready at 14:40. B2 starts at 14:50.
- Resource feasibility: OVEN-1 appears available, but B2 requires RACK-7.
- Policy constraints: Cleaning/quarantine may be mandatory, and B2 may require both jobs.
- Causal speculation: The oven idle gap is likely not a machine-capacity problem but a tool-turnaround and batch-synchronization problem. This remains partly conjectural because the exact end of B1 and rack-handling times are not fully detailed.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

1. **Asynchronous batch start / batch splitting**  
   Target mechanism: batch synchronization.  
   Expected benefit: reduce J1/J2-style waits by allowing ready jobs to proceed when batch policy permits.  
   Helped: J1, J2, J4, future early-ready jobs.  
   Potentially harmed: jobs that benefit from shared batching, such as J3 or J5, if smaller batches increase handling.  
   New cost or risk: more setups, more rack movements, possible lower oven efficiency.  
   Perverse metric: batch utilization could encourage managers to wait for full batches.  
   Object-centric evaluation: compare job readiness-to-batch-start time, batch size, and downstream shipment delay before and after policy change.

2. **Tool availability management for FIX-A, RACK-7, and CAL-3**  
   Target mechanism: shared-tool contention.  
   Expected benefit: reduce J4’s CNC wait, B2’s rack wait, and J6’s calibration wait.  
   Helped: J4, J6, B2, CNC-2, TEST-1.  
   Potentially harmed: maintenance or calibration schedules if tools are overused; jobs requiring rigorous cleaning if racks are turned over too quickly.  
   New cost or risk: cost of duplicate fixtures/racks/calibration devices or risk of quality violations.  
   Perverse metric: tool utilization could encourage unnecessary tool holds.  
   Object-centric evaluation: track tool acquire/release, cleaning/quarantine duration, and job waiting between tool release and process start.

3. **Shipment decoupling with dispatch cutoffs**  
   Target mechanism: shipment consolidation.  
   Expected benefit: prevent J1-like waits when one job is packed long before its partner.  
   Helped: J1 and other early-packed jobs.  
   Potentially harmed: J4 if it loses consolidation benefit; logistics cost may rise.  
   New cost or risk: extra freight, split shipments, customer dissatisfaction if consolidation is contractual.  
   Perverse metric: on-time shipment percentage could encourage holding jobs to hit batch shipment targets.  
   Object-centric evaluation: measure packed-to-dispatch time per job, not just shipment load-to-dispatch time.

4. **Improved object-centric logging and integration**  
   Target mechanism: data-integration artifacts.  
   Expected benefit: make waiting visible by adding `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Lot`, `Wait for Batch`, and by linking lot events to affected jobs.  
   Helped: all future analysis; indirectly all jobs by improving control.  
   Potentially harmed: none operationally, but data-entry burden may increase.  
   New cost or risk: event noise, inconsistent coding, or operators gaming reason codes.  
   Perverse metric: number of logged waits could encourage underreporting.  
   Object-centric evaluation: compare interaction summaries before and after logging change; check whether lot, tool, and shipment waiting edges appear and correlate with delays.

5. **Lot-release SLA and override governance**  
   Target mechanism: component-lot release coupling.  
   Expected benefit: reduce uncertainty for jobs sharing lots, such as J2 and J5; make overrides visible and consistent.  
   Helped: J5, future jobs on shared lots, quality and planning teams.  
   Potentially harmed: quality risk if releases are accelerated; supervisors may lose flexibility.  
   New cost or risk: more inspection resources or more scrap if holds are shortened improperly.  
   Perverse metric: lot hold time alone could pressure quality to release too early.  
   Object-centric evaluation: track quarantine-to-release time, override events, post-release defects, and downstream batch readiness.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization explanation:

1. J1 or J2 were not actually ready at 08:10/08:25 because of unrecorded quality holds, missing components, or incorrect readiness timestamps.
2. OVEN-1 had unlogged maintenance, setup, or recipe constraints before 12:05, making the B1 start time independent of job readiness.
3. B1 required a fixed full-rack configuration that could not be satisfied until 12:05 for reasons unrelated to J3 readiness.
4. J3’s readiness at 11:55 was caused by a missing component or lot issue, not by FIX-A, weakening the fixture-to-batch cascade.
5. RACK-7 cleaning/quarantine was mandatory and no alternative rack existed, making B2 delay primarily a tool-turnaround problem rather than batch synchronization.
6. S1 dispatch at 19:25 was contractually required to wait for J4, meaning J1’s shipment delay is policy-driven and not reducible by operational batching changes.

Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:

1. Explicit `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Batch`, `Wait for Lot`, and `Wait for Shipment Partner` activities with start/end times and reason codes.
2. Complete tool state logs for FIX-A, RACK-7, and CAL-3: acquire, release, cleaning, quarantine, calibration, maintenance.
3. Lot quality lifecycle events linked consistently to jobs: quarantine reason, sample results, release approval, supervisor override.
4. Batch policy data: minimum batch size, maximum wait time, oven slot schedule, rack requirements, allowed asynchronous starts.
5. Shipment rules: customer consolidation requirements, carrier cutoffs, earliest dispatch time, split-shipment permissions.
6. Machine downtime and setup logs with reason codes, distinguishing planned setup, unplanned fault, changeover, and external blocking.
