# 1. System boundary and assumptions

I use an order-to-delivery operational boundary. Inside the boundary are the activities and impacts visible in the event log from `Receive order` to `Close` or `Cancel due to delay`: warehouse handling, `Pick from stock`, `Plan production`, `Batch wait`, production on `CNC_A` and `CNC_B`, `heat_treat`, `inspection_cell`, packaging, route-mode transport, partial shipments, backorder waits, rework production, packaging rework, and the observed order-level attributes `energy_kwh`, `material_scrap_kg`, `package_material_kg`, and `transport_kgco2e`.

I also treat two hidden but operationally relevant effects as inside the decision boundary even though they are only proxied in the data: rework/return waste and batch-driven obsolescence. The prompt gives direct evidence that rework loops consume extra energy and scrap, and that 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong. Those effects should count against any "green" change that simply shifts waste later in the process.

Outside the boundary are full upstream embodied impacts of purchased stock and raw materials beyond the scrap proxy, customer use-phase impacts, the economic cost of downtime, end-of-life treatment of parts and packaging, shared facility overheads not allocated to orders, and any post-close reverse logistics not captured in the log.

Main uncertainties are: the flattened order-level log hides line-item count and exact part weights; route emissions do not expose carrier load factors or empty-return legs except indirectly; not all damage returns are explicitly modeled; and the discovered model has low precision during disruptions because emergency and partial-shipment paths recombine in many ways. Therefore the redesign should optimize by segment and path with guardrails, not by assuming one universal lowest-carbon path.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

| Indicator | Event-log definition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operational energy intensity | `avg(energy_kwh)` per closed order by `case_type`, `customer_type`, `machine_group`, and dominant path | Separates low-energy stock flows from high-energy MTO and shows whether batching and machine-choice changes actually reduce kWh without shifting waste elsewhere. |
| Transport intensity | `avg(transport_kgco2e)` per delivered order by `route_mode`, segment, and path | Main lever for stock/service and critical orders, especially the gap between ground/truck and air or dedicated van. |
| Avoidable expedite rate | Share of `expedite_flag = true` or `route_mode in {parcel_air, dedicated_van}` where promised lead time could still be met by ground or consolidated truck with >85% probability | Measures SP1 leakage and prevents using transport speed to compensate for internal delays. |
| Material loss intensity | `avg(material_scrap_kg)` and `avg(package_material_kg)` per order, split by production path and packaging type | Captures hidden waste from rework, over-packaging, and scrap-heavy machine choices. |
| Rework loop rate | Share of orders with `rework_flag = true`, `Inspection -> Rework production`, or `Rework packaging` loops | Rework is a compound sustainability loss because it adds energy, scrap, packaging, and often extra transport. |
| Batch efficiency vs delay | Distribution of `batch_size` and `Batch wait` by `machine_group`, plus energy per order and late-delivery rate by batch-size bucket | Needed because larger batches reduce kWh/item but increase waiting and obsolescence. |
| Obsolescence proxy | Share of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days, by batch-size bucket and segment | Prevents "efficient" batching from creating non-moving inventory and embodied waste. |
| Service reliability | Median cycle time, p90 cycle time, late-delivery rate, cancellation due to delay, and partial-shipment rate by segment/path | Sustainability changes are unacceptable if they materially damage contracted service. |
| Quality conformance | Q1 violation rate, damage/return proxy, packaging rework rate, and rework by machine/part combination | Distinguishes real waste reduction from under-packaging or rushed inspection. |
| Resilience capacity | Share of Service-contract orders with at least one viable alternate route, route concentration by carrier/mode, emergency production share, and machine-load concentration on `CNC_A` | Ensures lower emissions do not remove backup options during disruptions, consistent with R1. |

# 3. Intervention options

1. `S1 + S4: Green shipping recommender for stock orders.` Target path is mainly `Stock -> standard pack -> air`, especially Standard-stock Service-contract orders where SP1 is violated in 22% of cases. The mechanism is to combine `promised_lead_time_hours`, current pick queue, route reliability, and customer segment to default to `parcel_ground` or `consolidated_truck` when on-time probability remains above 85%, while using priority picking so transport speed is not compensating for warehouse delay. Environmental gain comes from reducing avoidable air, which is about 4-6x the transport footprint of ground on affected orders. Service impact should be small if the switch is limited to slack-positive cases and air remains an override. Monitor route choice versus slack, late delivery by slack bucket, `transport_kgco2e`, `expedite_flag`, and R1 route availability.

2. `S5: Twice-daily consolidated truck departures for non-urgent stock orders.` Target segment is Standard-stock Regular and slack-rich Service-contract orders now shipped as individual ground parcels. The mechanism is fixed consolidation windows with a hard maximum wait of 12-24 hours. Environmental gain is directly supported by the evidence that consolidated trucks reduce transport emissions by about 35-55% versus individual ground parcels. Service risk is the consolidation wait, so this should stay off tight-SLA or disruption-affected orders. Monitor consolidation wait, truck share, transport emissions per order, missed-promise rate, and whether delayed consolidation later rebounds into air shipment.

3. `S11: Availability-check redesign to reduce partial shipments.` Target path is `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait`, which has 7% share, 79-hour median cycle time, 24% late delivery, and a second shipment. The mechanism is improved available-to-promise logic: reserve scarce stock for Critical and Service-contract orders, allow short hold-and-ship-complete windows for non-critical orders, and use approved substitutes where feasible. The environmental gain is fewer duplicate shipments, fewer packaging events, and less customer-contact churn. Service can improve for orders that currently fall into long backorder loops, but it can worsen if hold windows are too aggressive; critical orders must still allow partial shipment. Monitor partial-shipment rate, second-shipment rate, cancellation due to delay, fill rate by segment, and transport emissions.

4. `S2: Dynamic batching with segment-specific waiting caps.` Target path is `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production` and the heat-treatment queue for MTO orders. The mechanism is not "maximize batch size"; it is to cap `Batch wait` by segment and due-date slack, with the strictest caps for Service-contract and for jobs waiting on heat treatment, while still allowing medium batches where slack exists because `CNC_A` energy per item falls from 2.8 kWh at batch size 1-3 to 1.3 kWh at 12-20. Environmental gain comes from avoiding too many tiny urgent runs without letting wait create late deliveries, emergency production, or obsolescence. Service effect should be positive at the tail if extreme waits fall, but regular orders may accept slightly longer planned waits. Monitor batch-size distribution, waiting time, energy per order, late delivery by batch bucket, emergency-production share, and 14-day no-ship obsolescence.

5. `S3: Shift eligible MTO work from CNC_B to CNC_A.` Target path is the scrap- and rework-heavy MTO variants that use `CNC_B`, especially `MTO -> batch wait -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework -> ground`. The mechanism is due-date-sensitive machine assignment: use `CNC_A` whenever queue length and customer slack allow it, and keep `CNC_B` for genuinely urgent cases or as resilience backup. Environmental gain comes mainly from cutting rework and scrap because `CNC_B` has an 18% rework rate versus 9% on `CNC_A`. Service impact is mixed: some jobs will wait slightly longer, but many should avoid the far larger delay of rework loops. Monitor machine assignment, queue time by machine, rework rate, `material_scrap_kg`, `energy_kwh`, p90 cycle time, and machine concentration risk.

6. `S7: Risk-based inspection quality gate.` Target is the `Inspection -> Rework production` loop and the evidence that rushed inspections miss defects that later cause packaging or customer returns. The mechanism is an extra inspection step or stricter standard only for high-risk combinations, such as `expedite_flag = true`, `CNC_B`, certain part families, or recent defect clusters. Environmental gain comes from preventing repeat production, extra scrap, and extra shipments caused by latent defects. Service impact is a small added inspection time on selected orders, but p90 cycle time can improve if avoided rework exceeds that delay. Monitor inspection queue length, downstream rework, damage/return proxy, repeat production energy, and defect escape rate by risk rule.

7. `S6: Packaging-route compatibility check and hard Q1 enforcement.` Target paths are the packaging rework loops (`Package standard -> Rework packaging`, `Package reinforced -> Rework packaging`) and MTO orders over 8 kg that violate Q1. The mechanism is a pre-ship rule that uses part geometry, weight, and `route_mode` to choose compatible packaging the first time, plus mandatory reinforced packaging for >8 kg MTO unless inspection certifies low damage risk. Environmental gain comes from eliminating avoidable repacks and reducing damage-related return flows; reinforced packaging should not be removed from heavy MTO because the prompt states it cuts damage-related returns by 40%. Service and quality both improve if repacks and damages drop. Monitor Q1 violations, packaging rework rate, `package_material_kg`, damage/return proxy, and route-specific packaging failures.

8. `S8 + S9: Split critical urgency and replace some vans with shared emergency lanes.` Target segment is Critical-downtime Service-contract, especially the `Emergency production -> ... -> dedicated van` path with very high transport impact. The mechanism is to distinguish true downtime from precautionary urgent demand at order capture. True downtime keeps emergency production and dedicated-van or fastest feasible service. Precautionary urgent orders are routed to shared emergency courier lanes, stocked alternatives, or even ground exceptions when promised service can still be protected. Environmental gain comes from lowering empty-return and low-load van usage. Service risk is acceptable only if classification is robust and an override remains available. Monitor dedicated-van share, missing SP2 justification, route performance by urgency class, empty-return proxy, and downtime incidents.

9. `S10: Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular MTO orders.` Target segment is Regular MTO orders with little slack that currently force immediate production, small batches, or air shipment. The mechanism is to offer slightly longer promise windows or commercial incentives for greener fulfillment windows at order acceptance. Environmental gain is indirect but important: more feasible `CNC_A` allocation, better heat-treatment batching, and more ground shipments. Service impact is acceptable because it changes the promise before commitment and is limited to Regular customers, not contracted service obligations. Monitor accepted promise-date changes, actual versus promised cycle time, air share, batch-size shifts, and whether later cancellations or customer churn rise.

10. `S12: Sustainability exception log for SP1, SP2, and Q1 overrides.` Target is every case where air, dedicated van, or non-compliant packaging is used despite policy. The mechanism is to add an explicit override event with reason, approver, segment, and expected service risk. This has limited direct environmental effect but is essential for distinguishing justified resilience actions from routine drift, especially because 31% of dedicated-van cases lack the required justification event today. Service effect is neutral if overrides remain fast. Monitor override volume, missing justification rate, outcomes of overridden versus compliant cases, and repeated override patterns by planner, customer segment, and disruption context.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. Larger batches can reduce kWh per item and still worsen total waste if they increase `Batch wait`, trigger late deliveries, and recreate the 6% not-shipped-within-14-days obsolescence problem. A batching policy without waiting caps is not a sustainability improvement.

2. Replacing air with ground or consolidated truck can backfire if the slack model is optimistic. Missed promises can cause emergency reshipments, customer escalations, or even cancellations, which may erase the original transport savings.

3. Consolidation and hold-and-ship-complete rules can shift impact from transport to service failure. If non-urgent logic is accidentally applied to downtime-sensitive orders, the environmental gain is bought by customer downtime or later expediting.

4. Cutting packaging material too aggressively can increase damage, packaging rework, and customer returns. That creates more transport and scrap than the original packaging saved.

5. The opposite packaging risk also matters: applying reinforced packaging too broadly doubles package material. For heavy MTO it is justified by fewer damage returns, but using it as a blanket default for light or low-risk parts may raise total footprint.

6. Moving work from `CNC_B` to `CNC_A` can reduce rework but create queue concentration, larger `CNC_A` waits, and lower resilience during machine downtime. That can indirectly drive more emergency production and transport.

7. A stricter inspection gate can reduce rework and still worsen performance if the `inspection_cell` becomes the next bottleneck. Rushed bypasses around a congested inspection step could recreate the same defect escapes in a less visible way.

8. Reducing partial shipments may improve transport intensity but can worsen service if exceptions for Critical and Service-contract orders are too hard to obtain. In the worst case it shifts the burden from carrier emissions to customer downtime.

9. Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation can change customer behavior. Customers may inflate urgency, place earlier precautionary orders, or over-order to hedge against longer promises, which can increase inventory, cancellations, and obsolescence.

10. Tight control of dedicated vans can itself create hidden risk if planners start misclassifying orders as "true downtime" to bypass approval. Governance has to audit behavior, not just policy text.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

The best redesign is a segmented control package, not a blanket "ship slower" program. The first priority is to remove avoidable expedited transport from orders that already have slack: deploy `S1 + S4` for Standard-stock Service-contract and Regular orders, add `S5` for non-urgent stock consolidation, and use `S8 + S9` so dedicated vans stay reserved for true downtime rather than precautionary urgency. This directly attacks one of the largest visible footprint gaps in the log while respecting the constraint that not all air or van transport is unjustified.

The second priority is to attack hidden waste in made-to-order flows, because MTO has far higher `energy_kwh`, scrap, and rework than stock orders. Implement `S2`, `S3`, `S7`, and `S6` together: dynamic batching with waiting caps, preferential use of `CNC_A` when slack allows, targeted inspection reinforcement, and packaging-route compatibility with Q1 enforcement. These measures work as a package because batching alone can create lateness and obsolescence, machine reassignment alone can overload `CNC_A`, and packaging or inspection changes alone only treat symptoms. Together they reduce the `CNC_B` rework path, the inspection loop, and packaging rework without sacrificing quality.

The third priority is to stabilize planning and governance. Use `S11` to reduce wasteful partial shipments, `S10` to create planned slack on Regular MTO orders before a promise is made, and `S12` to make every SP1/SP2/Q1 override explicit and auditable. This protects against the common failure mode where sustainability gains are erased by hidden exceptions, gaming, or repeated last-minute recovery actions.

In short, keep the high-emission emergency options, but narrow them to the cases that truly need them. Shift non-urgent stock flows toward ground and consolidation, shift MTO away from rework-heavy machine and inspection patterns, and govern exceptions tightly. That combination is much more likely to reduce total environmental impact without unacceptable deterioration in service, quality, or resilience than any single green rule.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

Start with a pre/post baseline using the same segment and path structure already visible in the log: Standard-stock Regular, Standard-stock Service-contract, Made-to-order Regular, Made-to-order Service-contract, and Critical-downtime Service-contract. Compare not just overall averages but the main path shifts the redesign is supposed to create, for example a reduction in `Stock -> standard pack -> air`, `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait`, `MTO -> ... -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework`, and `Rework packaging` loops.

Validate through a phased rollout rather than a big-bang change. The cleanest design is a stepped implementation by segment, site, planner group, or customer cohort so untreated cases remain a control. Because the discovered model has low precision during disruptions, comparisons should be stratified by segment, promised-lead-time bucket, disruption status, and route reliability conditions instead of relying on one aggregate average.

Conformance monitoring should be explicit:

- `SP1`: for each air or dedicated-van shipment, calculate whether ground or consolidated truck still had >85% on-time probability at ship decision time. Track violation rate by segment and planner, and distinguish justified disruption overrides from routine misuse.
- `SP2`: every dedicated-van case must have a preceding explicit Critical-downtime justification event. Reduce the current missing-justification share and compare service outcomes of justified versus unjustified van usage.
- `Q1`: detect every MTO order above 8 kg shipped without reinforced packaging unless there is an inspection certification of low damage risk. Link violations to packaging rework, damage-return proxy, and rework loops.

Segment-level service monitoring should remain a hard guardrail. Weekly or daily dashboards should show median cycle time, p90 cycle time, late delivery, cancellation due to delay, partial-shipment rate, emergency-production share, and customer complaints by segment. Critical and Service-contract segments should be treated as non-inferiority cohorts: if those cohorts lose service or route optionality, rollout should pause even if the average footprint improves.

Sustainability monitoring should use the event attributes directly: `energy_kwh`, `transport_kgco2e`, `material_scrap_kg`, `package_material_kg`, `rework_flag`, `batch_size`, `Batch wait`, and the 14-day not-shipped obsolescence proxy. Resilience monitoring should track alternative route availability for Service-contract customers, carrier concentration, `CNC_A` load concentration, and whether emergency fallback paths remain available during disruptions, as required by R1.

Finally, govern exceptions as a process, not a report. Every SP1/SP2/Q1 override should enter the `S12` exception log with reason code, approver, expected service risk, and actual outcome. Review high-volume or repeatedly unsuccessful exceptions in a joint operations-quality-sustainability meeting. That closes the loop between process mining evidence and policy tuning, and it is the main way to stop environmental improvements in one path from reappearing as hidden service, quality, or resilience failures elsewhere.
