## 1. Disruption timeline

The incident produced **selective service resilience, followed by incomplete recovery**. Hospitals retained relatively strong service, but pharmacies and bulk customers absorbed substantial delay. Control gaps and remediation work persisted after hospital median cycle time returned to baseline.

| Measure | T0: baseline | T2: manual workaround | T3: partial recovery | T4: stabilization |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Cases reported | 42,000 | 5,600 | 11,400 | 35,000 |
| Hospital-critical cycle time, median / p90 | 8h / 15h | 10h / 21h | 9h / 18h | 8h / 16h |
| Pharmacy-standard cycle time, median / p90 | 23h / 49h | 54h / 118h | 38h / 90h | 28h / 74h |
| Wholesale-bulk cycle time, median / p90 | 36h / 72h | 96h / 180h | 84h / 160h | 58h / 120h |
| Traceability exceptions | 0.8% | 9.5% | 5.4% | 2.2% |
| Cold-chain temperature documentation missing | 0.4% | 4.8% | 2.3% | 0.9% |
| Controlled-substance dual-control missing | 0.2% | 2.9% | 1.4% | 0.5% |
| Rework / correction cases | 3.5% | 12.5% | 9.0% | 5.8% |
| Cancellations | 2.1% | 7.8% | 5.2% | 3.0% |
| Normative fitness / fitting traces | 0.96 / 91% | 0.71 / 44% | 0.82 / 59% | 0.90 / 76% |

- **T0, Day −30 to Day −1:** The dominant variant follows authorization, reservation, picking, packing, release, dispatch, delivery proof, and invoice close. Compliance holds, backorders, and rechecks are established exceptions. The baseline is relatively controlled, but its nonzero exception rates and 91% fitting traces mean that it is not a perfect compliance standard.
- **T1, Day 0 morning:** WMS availability fails, disrupting inventory visibility and workflow execution. The immediate risk is that physical operations continue without trustworthy authorization, stock, or traceability information. No separate T1 performance summary is provided; T2 figures should not be assigned to this initial period.
- **T2, Day 0 afternoon–Day 2:** Emergency triage, hospital fast lanes, offline authorization, printed stock snapshots, manual picking, unscanned dispatch, phone delivery proof, and spreadsheet invoice holds dominate. Hospital-critical fill remains a reported 91%, while Pharmacy-standard fill falls to 61%. This supports service protection for hospitals, but the hospital p90 still rises from 15h to 21h. Traceability exceptions rise almost twelvefold, and missing temperature and dual-control evidence also increase sharply. The variant ending at “Spreadsheet invoice hold” is a provisional endpoint, not equivalent to normal invoice completion.
- **T3, Day 3–Day 6:** Intake returns, but unreliable synchronization and scanning create a hazardous hybrid workflow. Service and control metrics improve, yet all remain worse than baseline. Wholesale median cycle time is still 84h versus 36h. Duplicate reservations, conflicting records, and premature trust in restored systems are plausible risks requiring investigation; the supplied data do not establish their occurrence. No T3 DFG is supplied, so its exact variants cannot be reconstructed.
- **T4, Day 7–Day 21:** The normal sequence largely returns, with backlog prioritization, manual-reservation reconciliation, delivery-proof remediation, and post-invoice audit remediation added. Hospital median cycle time recovers, but Pharmacy-standard p90 remains 74h versus 49h and Wholesale-bulk p90 remains 120h versus 72h. Traceability exceptions remain 2.75 times baseline. Fitness improves to 0.90, but only 76% of traces fit. Operational restoration therefore precedes service normalization for all groups and completion of assurance work.

The DFGs reinforce this interpretation. T2 contains 1,800 Manual pack → Dispatch without scan occurrences and another 1,450 Temporary release control → Dispatch without scan occurrences: even some manually checked orders lose the normal dispatch evidence. T4 contains 1,400 transitions into manual-reservation reconciliation, 1,200 into delivery-proof remediation, and 2,300 from invoice close into audit remediation. These are material recovery activities, not incidental administrative noise.

**Interpretation limits:** DFG frequencies count transitions, not necessarily distinct orders, and do not establish complete case paths, product mix, or causal effects. They should not be forced into a balanced flow or used to infer that every fast-lane order lacked authorization. The 2,300 audit entries versus 2,100 audit exits do not, by themselves, prove exactly 200 open cases. Likewise, the periods have different durations, and T0 uses business days: raw case totals do not establish comparable throughput. Product-specific missing-control rates need their eligible-product denominators.

## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience preserves an important service under disruption while maintaining required safety and compliance functions through demonstrable alternatives.** It also makes its costs and unresolved obligations visible. A different activity sequence can satisfy this definition: a documented offline authorization decision can perform the same essential function as an electronic authorization check.

**Concealed noncompliance preserves the appearance of completion by dropping controls, assuming they occurred, or closing the measured process before their consequences are resolved.** Deliberate deception is not necessary. A dashboard that counts spreadsheet invoice holds as completed orders, or ignores post-invoice audit work, can conceal risk even when staff acted in good faith.

The adaptations divide as follows:

- **Strong resilience candidates:** B preserves authorization; I provides deliberate capacity protection; J organizes recovery; K restores trustworthy inventory and order state. Their value depends on documented execution, current information, fair rules, and adequate staffing. B and K also create real work that must be included in incident capacity planning.
- **Risky shortcuts in their observed form:** C replaces verification with assumption. G accelerates handoff without the normal traceability evidence and is unacceptable when no adequate manual substitute exists. Any A route that actually omits required authorization belongs in this category. Reconstructing E events as if original scans existed, or using L to certify controls that were never performed, would also be concealed noncompliance.
- **Conditional resilience:** A with proper authorization; D with controlled stock updates; E with contemporaneous identifiers; F with complete product-specific release controls; and H with sufficient authenticated delivery evidence can all be legitimate alternatives. Their acceptability depends on the product, deadline, evidence quality, duration, and approved control equivalence. Management can choose among permissible operating risks, but risk appetite cannot erase mandatory authorization, temperature, security, or recall obligations.
- **Necessary recovery work, with limits:** L is valuable when it verifies original evidence, investigates gaps, and triggers corrective action. It is not evidence that shipments were compliant at dispatch. K and L are part of resilience only when their workload and unresolved findings remain visible and are brought to a defensible conclusion.

Several abductive explanations fit the observations. The relatively small hospital slowdown alongside large pharmacy and bulk delays is consistent with successful triage and capacity redistribution. The traceability spike, unscanned-dispatch edges, and variable delivery-proof delay are consistent with weak contemporaneous evidence capture. Continuing reconciliation, rework, and audit queues are consistent with accumulated operational and compliance debt. These are plausible explanations, not identified causal effects: changes in demand, stock availability, staffing, product mix, and case maturity could also contribute.

Conformance supports diagnosis rather than supplying the final verdict. T2 fitness of 0.71 and 44% fitting traces show substantial divergence. Some move-on-log activities are useful substitutions; a phone proof or temporary release control is not automatically unsafe merely because the baseline model lacks it. Conversely, missing release and temperature records require investigation even where service is fast. Assess each deviation against the **control function, applicable product requirements, original evidence, and timing**, using case-level alignments and records. A missing record does not prove that a physical check failed, but it leaves the check unsubstantiated.

The T4 figures rule out a broad claim of completed recovery. The hospital median conceals other groups' long tails, while improving exception percentages can coexist with substantial absolute workload. If traceability percentages use all reported cases, T4 represents approximately 770 affected cases, versus approximately 532 in T2. These counts are illustrative, not counts of unresolved cases, but show why declining rates alone do not establish that remediation capacity is sufficient.

## 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned model should have explicit operating modes and maintain separate milestones for physical delivery, financial completion, and compliance resolution.

**Trigger conditions and authority**

1. **Declare degraded operation immediately** when WMS unavailability, untrusted synchronization, scanner failure, or unavailable authorization services prevent a required control from being reliably executed. Record the incident time, affected capabilities, trusted data sources, and accountable incident lead.
2. **Authorize the manual mode by capability and product class.** Operations and quality/compliance confirm that staff, stock information, evidence capture, release checks, and courier handoff can support the proposed route. If a required control has neither a functioning system nor an approved manual equivalent, hold the affected shipment and seek another qualified supply route.
3. **Escalate within manual mode** when a clinical deadline is forecast to be missed, an unexplained stock discrepancy occurs, product identity or custody is uncertain, a required temperature/security record is absent, or the evidence queue exceeds assigned processing capacity. Stale or disputed information triggers re-verification of affected stock; it does not authorize guessing.
4. **Enter partial recovery only for verified capabilities.** Security confirms that restored components are trustworthy; operations validate authorization data, reservation integrity, scanning, event synchronization, and duplicate prevention. A working intake screen alone is insufficient.
5. **Exit incident recovery through explicit gates:** reliable systems, service and aged backlog within agreed thresholds by group, physical/manual/system records reconciled, and high-risk compliance exceptions resolved. Any remaining bounded administrative work must retain owners, deadlines, and an approved residual-risk decision. Distinguish this from the earlier milestone of restoring ordinary dispatch.

**Order flow**

Receive order → assign a persistent order ID and identify repeat submissions → triage → verify authorization → confirm eligible stock and create reservation → pick → pack → product-specific quality/release decision → documented courier handoff → verified delivery evidence → reconcile records and resolve exceptions → financial/compliance closure.

Every step uses a trusted electronic capability or its approved manual equivalent. A failed gate routes to a visible hold, reassessment, verified alternative supplier/depot, or reason-coded cancellation. Routing through another location must preserve the same control obligations.

**Triage rules**

- Rank immediate clinical need and time to supply failure first, then product handling deadlines, order age, and stock/transport feasibility. Hospital-critical designation is a strong initial signal, but an urgent pharmacy patient need can outrank nonurgent hospital demand.
- Separate **priority to receive service** from **requirements to release product**. Faster queue position does not lower the release standard.
- Use explicit deferral limits and aging escalation for standard and bulk orders. Preserve a defined capacity allocation for essential pharmacy service, adjusted transparently when immediate clinical demand requires it.
- Record priority, reason, decision-maker, promised review time, customer notification, and every override. Reassess deferred orders as their urgency changes.

**Minimum controls before release and through delivery**

| Risk class | Required control floor in manual or hybrid operation |
|---|---|
| All orders, including ordinary ambient | Verified customer/product authorization; unique order ID; correct item, quantity, lot/batch and other applicable identifiers; expiry and saleable/released status; recorded reservation and stock movement; documented release decision; accountable courier handoff and recipient evidence. |
| Hospital-critical | The same product controls, plus clinical deadline, named escalation owner, and an alternative supply plan if controls or supply cannot be completed in time. |
| Cold-chain | All applicable general controls plus validated packaging/route conditions, required temperature evidence throughout handling and transport, and an explicit decision for any excursion or missing evidence. Unknown temperature integrity remains unresolved until adequate evidence supports release or disposition. |
| Controlled-substance | Verified product-specific customer eligibility, contemporaneous required dual-control evidence, accountable staff identities, secure storage/transport, stock accountability, and authenticated custody/receipt. A single pharmacist check or unsupported phone call does not establish these controls. |
| Recall-sensitive | Identified lot/serial as applicable, sufficiently current recall/quarantine status, destination linkage, and the ability to locate and contact recipients promptly. Uncertain lot or recall eligibility triggers a hold. |

Product attributes can overlap. A cold-chain, controlled, recall-sensitive item must satisfy all applicable requirements. Sampling may supplement permitted inspection practices but cannot replace a required per-order control or release decision.

**Manual evidence capture**

Use prepared, uniquely numbered forms or a controlled offline application on trusted equipment. Capture order/customer IDs, product and lot identifiers, quantity, location, authorization source and decision, handlers, required second checker, release decision, temperature evidence, courier/recipient identities, and custody times.

Maintain one authoritative manual reservation and movement ledger, with designated responsibility across shifts. Treat the incident snapshot as its timestamped opening state; append movements and exceptions, and physically verify uncertainty. Secure original forms, signatures, and attachments, with controlled access and backup.

On restoration, preserve both **when the physical action occurred** and **when its evidence was entered or imported**, together with source and confidence. Distinguish contemporaneous manual evidence from later testimony and from irrecoverable evidence. Never invent a scanner event or backdate a control to make the trace fit.

**Backlog and reconciliation flow**

1. At restart, partition work into unfulfilled orders, dispatched orders lacking delivery evidence, fulfilled orders needing record reconciliation, and unresolved safety/compliance exceptions.
2. Before issuing a new reservation or pick, match persistent IDs across intake, manual records, inventory, courier records, and invoices. Confirm whether physical fulfillment already occurred; prevent duplicate picking, dispatch, or billing.
3. Prioritize unfulfilled orders using the triage rules, refreshed customer need, age, and handling deadlines. Obtain explicit reasons for cancellations and communicate outcomes.
4. Reconcile stock by product, lot, location, and custody state. Investigate discrepancies and quarantine affected stock where identity, release, or security is uncertain. High-risk adjustments receive independent review.
5. Route evidence gaps to an owned exception queue. Link each gap to its original order and identify severity, required evidence, next action, deadline, and disposition.
6. Validate recovered evidence. When evidence establishes that a control occurred, repair the record with provenance. When a control was not performed or remains unprovable, record the deviation and arrange a risk-based product/customer response, potentially including containment, retrieval, customer contact, or applicable reporting.
7. Close the exception only with reviewer sign-off on evidence or a documented disposition. A corrective-action plan does not convert an unperformed historical control into compliance.

Allocate separate capacity for live service, backlog clearance, and reconciliation/audit work. Otherwise, improved dispatch will continually displace the work needed to finish recovery.

Delivery and invoicing can remain visible operational milestones, but they must not make an unresolved case disappear. Any permitted financial close with an administrative exception must retain the linked open compliance status. Define **recovery complete** at incident level only after the exit gates are met.

## 5. Resilience indicators

Measure indicators by operating phase, customer group, product risk, and workaround route. Use both elapsed-time views and incident-order cohorts so late remediation is not lost across period boundaries.

| Dimension | Leading indicators during the incident | Lagging indicators and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughput** | Safely released orders per staffed hour; pick/release bottlenecks; compliance and courier capacity; inventory ledger freshness. | Delivered orders and units per hour/day; completion rate against demand; end-to-end cycle time. Report accepted, deferred, cancelled, and still-open demand separately. Intake volume is not fulfillment. |
| **Service protection** | Critical orders forecast to miss their clinical deadline; time to hospital/pharmacy stockout; availability of verified alternative stock and transport. | Critical fill rate, on-time-in-full delivery, stockout duration, and documented service impact. The T2 hospital 91% and pharmacy 61% require separate reporting. |
| **Traceability** | Share of current picks and handoffs with complete identifiers and contemporaneous evidence; disputed custody events; delay between action and evidence capture. | Traceability exceptions, unknown destinations/lots, time to locate an affected shipment, and success of trace/recall exercises. Separate original capture from retrospective reconstruction. |
| **Control completeness** | Authorization, release, temperature, and dual-control evidence present at the required decision point; blocked orders and attempted overrides. | Missing-control rates with eligible-product denominators; confirmed excursions, security discrepancies, unauthorized releases, and substantiated versus unresolved deviations. |
| **Backlog age** | Oldest open order; age bands; orders approaching their promise, clinical, or handling deadline; forecast clearance time using available capacity. | Median/p90/p95 waiting and completion time by group; overdue, cancelled, and never-fulfilled demand. Include unresolved orders through open-age measures instead of dropping them from cycle time. |
| **Rework** | New discrepancies and repeat corrections per shift; duplicate-ID/reservation alerts; reconciliation entries versus completions. | Correction case rate, number of repeat corrections, labor hours, inventory adjustments, and rework-related cancellations. Track cost as well as case incidence. |
| **Fairness** | Deferral age and capacity allocation by group within comparable urgency/product classes; priority overrides and their reasons. | Fill-rate, delay, cancellation, and complaint gaps between groups, adjusted for clinical need and product risk. Compare deterioration against each group's baseline; equal service regardless of urgency is not the objective. |
| **Recovery debt** | Number, age, severity, and estimated effort of evidence/reconciliation exceptions; queue inflow versus resolution; reviewer availability. | Time from delivery and invoice close to compliance resolution; total remediation hours; unresolved high-risk cases; incident time to full recovery. Show the after-the-fact tail explicitly. |

Targets should have two levels:

- **Release gates:** Required authorization, traceability, temperature, and security controls must be satisfied before the relevant action, or the affected order follows the hold/alternative-supply route. An unexplained failure is not made acceptable by a favorable average.
- **Performance and recovery alerts:** Use baseline values as comparison points and define incident tolerances, deadlines, and queue limits in advance. For example, T4 pharmacy and bulk p90 values remain well above baseline and should prevent a claim of service normalization. Baseline exception rates are diagnostic references, not permission to omit mandatory controls.

For throughput and service, use common time units, staffing exposure, actual demand, and comparable case maturity. Report control completeness at dispatch separately from completeness after remediation, preventing later repair from erasing the original exposure. Audit a sample of the underlying evidence so that improved recording does not masquerade as improved control execution.

In the present incident, the first action is to review still-open or weakly evidenced high-risk shipments and contain any unresolved product risk. In parallel, reconcile the manual-period orders, assess aged pharmacy/bulk demand, and assign capacity to the audit queue. Close the review with an analysis of which workarounds produced safe service, which produced gaps, and how much recovery labor each required.

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept transparent, time-bounded deferral of lower-urgency demand, added manual-control cost, and slower release when these protect critical supply safely. It should not accept assumed authorization, unknown required temperature or security conditions, unsupported custody, or a recovery declaration that excludes open compliance work. Queue priority can change; mandatory control obligations remain. Evidence of current offline eligibility checks, complete original lot/custody records, validated temperature history, required dual control, fair service allocation, and a demonstrably clearing remediation queue would support retaining more manual adaptations. Evidence of unauthorized release, compromised product, untraceable destinations, or growing recovery debt would justify tighter release gates and more use of verified alternative supply.
