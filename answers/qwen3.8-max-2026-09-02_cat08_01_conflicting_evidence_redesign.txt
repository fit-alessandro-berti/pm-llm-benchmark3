## 1. Current-process diagnosis

The process-mining artifacts show not one stable warranty process, but several competing operating modes forced through the same flow. The core problem is that routing is not risk-aware: fast automation, manual review, evidence collection, and fulfillment quality are applied inconsistently across customer segments, device values, defect types, and evidence certainty.

### Main performance, quality, compliance, and customer-experience problems

1. **The dominant fast path is attractive upstream but expensive downstream.**  
   The frequency directly-follows graph shows a strong fast lane:  
   - `Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen`: 3,600 cases  
   - `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval`: 2,550 cases  
   - `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement`: 2,300 cases  

   The performance DFG shows why this path is seductive: median time is low for `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval` (0.4 hours) and `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement` (1.8 hours). Segment outcomes also show fast medians of 8–28 hours depending on segment, versus much longer reviewed/repair paths.

   But the same fast path carries poor downstream outcomes:
   - Customer complaint/reopen occurs in **14.1% of Fast approval cases**, compared with only **3.3%** for the `Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device` path.
   - `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` has a long tail: mean 96 hours, median 70 hours, p90 180 hours.
   - Fast approval cases are **31% cheaper before rework**, but only **9% cheaper after accounting for complaints, reshipments, and service credits**.

   This is the central contradiction: the most frequent path looks best on average cycle time and direct cost, but worse on total cost, rework, compliance, and customer experience.

2. **The slower reviewed path has better quality but is not universally acceptable.**  
   The manual and fraud paths are slower:
   - `Intake claim -> Manual eligibility review`: median 5.0 hours, p90 18.0 hours.
   - `Manual eligibility review -> Request missing evidence`: median 10.0 hours, p90 36.0 hours.
   - `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review`: median 26.0 hours, p90 88.0 hours.
   - `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote`: median 15.0 hours, p90 60.0 hours.

   Yet quality outcomes are better for high-value devices:
   - Premium high-value device: complaint/reopen is **22% on fast path**, but only **5% on reviewed/repair path**.
   - Standard high-value device: complaint/reopen is **18% on fast path**, but only **6% on reviewed/repair path**.

   The temporal profile also warns against treating the slow path as universally better: `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote` is often too slow for Premium cases. For Premium low-risk accessories, service-credit exposure becomes high if cycle time exceeds 24 hours, while the manual path median is 46 hours. So the slow path is better for high-value quality, but often unacceptable for Premium speed expectations.

3. **Evidence completion is a major source of delay, abandonment, and repeated work.**  
   The frequency DFG shows a large evidence loop:
   - `Auto eligibility screen -> Request missing evidence`: 650 cases
   - `Manual eligibility review -> Request missing evidence`: 780 cases
   - `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review`: 890 cases
   - `Request missing evidence -> Claim abandoned`: 320 cases

   The rework statistics show that **Request missing evidence occurs at least twice in 26% of cases that enter it**. The “Unclear defect / missing evidence” segment is 22% of cases, has a median cycle time of **140 hours**, and has the highest abandonment and repeated evidence requests.

   This is not merely a speed problem. It is a compliance and customer-experience problem because normative rule **R2** requires evidence completeness before replacement dispatch, except for Premium low-risk accessory claims. The alignment diagnostics explicitly identify **Fast approval before Evidence completeness** as a frequent deviation.

4. **Comformance is superficially acceptable but structurally weak.**  
   Token-based replay shows:
   - Log fitness: **0.91**
   - Percentage of fitting traces: **68%**

   The gap between fitness and fitting traces is important: many traces have small deviations that average into a decent fitness score, but only 68% of cases fully conform. The alignment diagnostics identify frequent deviations:
   - Fast approval before evidence completeness.
   - Customer complaint/reopen after close.
   - Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices.

   Normative rules are being violated in exactly the places where the fast path is most attractive:
   - **R1**: claims above EUR 400 must have Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review before Fast approval.
   - **R2**: replacement dispatch must not occur before evidence completeness, except Premium low-risk accessories.
   - **R3**: reopened claims must pass Manual eligibility review before final close or denial.

   The discovered model’s low precision is also a warning sign: many low-frequency paths combine auto approval, risk review, and repair in ways not allowed by policy. This suggests variant explosion and weak process control, not just a few isolated exceptions.

5. **Resources are mismatched to the actual risk profile of claims.**  
   The resource information shows several constraints:
   - Manual eligibility review has only **7 reviewers**, with queue growth when utilization exceeds **82%**.
   - Fraud/risk review has only **2 specialists** and bottlenecks on Mondays and after campaigns.
   - Repair quote delays are caused mostly by waiting for part-price confirmation.
   - Quality check inspectors are underused except after repair campaigns.
   - The escalation board has strong customer-satisfaction effect but poor scalability because it has only one daily 45-minute slot.

   Meanwhile, updating auto-screen rules may increase manual-review inflow by **15–35%**, and a pre-dispatch quality gate could add **6–18 hours** depending on design. Therefore, any redesign must explicitly consider displaced bottlenecks, not just ideal flow logic.

### Summary diagnosis

The current process overuses a fast, cheap, low-evidence path for claims that often need more control, while under-supporting the evidence-completion loop that causes delay and abandonment. The fast path is appropriate for some low-risk accessory claims, especially Premium low-risk accessories where R2 allows an exception. But the same fast path becomes dangerous for high-value devices, unclear defects, missing evidence, and claims above EUR 400. The redesign challenge is therefore not “choose fast versus slow.” It is to create distinct, risk-aware lanes that preserve speed where safe, enforce review where risky, improve evidence collection where unclear, and add fulfillment quality control where wrong replacements are costly.

## 2. Redesign A: Segment-aware eligibility firewall with a guarded low-risk fast lane

### Redesigned process flow

Redesign A changes the eligibility and approval logic by introducing a segment-aware firewall at intake and before fast approval.

1. **Intake claim -> Segment/risk classifier**  
   At intake, each claim is classified using:
   - Customer segment: Premium or Standard.
   - Product risk: low-risk accessory, mid-risk device, high-value device.
   - Claim value: above or below EUR 400.
   - Defect type and evidence completeness.
   - Prior complaint/reopen history.

2. **Low-risk accessory fast lane**  
   For low-risk accessory claims:
   - **Premium low-risk accessory**: may use the R2 exception and receive immediate replacement dispatch if basic warranty, identity, and part-confidence checks pass. This preserves the current 8-hour fast-path median and avoids service-credit exposure above 24 hours.
   - **Standard low-risk accessory**: may receive fast approval only after automated evidence completeness confirmation. This respects R2 while still allowing a fast path aligned with the customer tolerance for 72-hour completion if visibility is good.

3. **High-value and above-EUR-400 review lane**  
   For high-value devices or claims above EUR 400:
   - Auto eligibility screen may screen and route, but may not directly approve.
   - The claim must pass either **Manual eligibility review** or **Fraud/risk review** before Fast approval, satisfying R1.
   - Dispatch replacement is blocked until evidence completeness is confirmed, unless the case explicitly qualifies for the Premium low-risk accessory exception under R2.

4. **Reopen and complaint lane**  
   Any `Customer complaint/reopen` is automatically routed to **Manual eligibility review** before final close or denial, satisfying R3. The reopen must carry a root-cause code, such as wrong replacement part, missing evidence, incorrect eligibility decision, or fraud suspicion.

5. **Conformance enforcement**  
   The process engine blocks known invalid transitions:
   - No Fast approval for high-value claims without Manual or Fraud review.
   - No Dispatch replacement before evidence completeness unless the Premium low-risk exception applies.
   - No final close after reopen without Manual eligibility review.

### Targeted segments, paths, and activities

This redesign primarily targets:
- High-value device claims, especially Premium high-value and Standard high-value.
- Claims above EUR 400.
- Cases currently receiving Fast approval without required risk review.
- Reopened claims that currently bypass Manual eligibility review.
- Low-risk accessory claims that should remain fast but need clearer rule boundaries.

It changes these activities:
- `Intake claim`
- `Auto eligibility screen`
- `Manual eligibility review`
- `Fraud/risk review`
- `Fast approval`
- `Dispatch replacement`
- `Customer complaint/reopen`

### Mechanism of improvement

The mechanism is **risk-based routing and hard conformance gates**.

The current fast path is cheap because it uses the EUR 0.20 auto screen and avoids manual labor. But the process-mining evidence shows that this cheapness is partly illusory: fast approval has a 14.1% complaint/reopen rate, and fast approval cases are only 9% cheaper after rework. Redesign A keeps the fast lane where the expected cost of error is low—low-risk accessories—but blocks the same lane for claims where the cost of error is high, especially high-value devices where wrong replacement costs EUR 410 and complaint/reopen rates are 18–22% on the fast path.

This redesign also directly addresses the conformance findings:
- R1 violations are reduced by forcing high-value or above-EUR-400 claims into Manual or Fraud review before Fast approval.
- R2 violations are reduced by requiring evidence completeness before dispatch, except where policy explicitly allows the Premium low-risk accessory exception.
- R3 violations are reduced by forcing reopened claims through Manual eligibility review.

It also reduces variant explosion. The low precision of the discovered model suggests many unauthorized combinations. A segment-aware firewall reduces the number of allowed path combinations by making routing dependent on explicit segment and risk attributes.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

Redesign A deliberately sacrifices some speed for high-value and above-EUR-400 claims. The current fast-path medians for high-value claims are 20 hours for Premium and 28 hours for Standard, while reviewed/repair paths are 96 and 120 hours. Moving high-value claims away from the fast path will likely increase their cycle time, even if it reduces complaints and compliance risk.

It also increases pressure on manual and fraud review capacity. The prompt warns that updating auto-screen rules may increase manual-review inflow by **15–35%**. With only seven reviewers and two fraud specialists, this redesign could create queues if capacity is not adjusted or if evidence loops are not fixed separately.

Finally, Redesign A does not fully solve:
- Poor evidence collection.
- Customer abandonment during evidence requests.
- Wrong replacement part selection.
- Repair quote delays caused by part-price confirmation.

Those issues are addressed more directly in Redesigns B and C.

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first completion and ambiguity triage

### Redesigned process flow

Redesign B changes the front end of the process by making evidence completeness and defect clarity a first-class stage rather than a repeated loop.

1. **Intake claim -> Evidence requirement engine**  
   At intake, the system determines the evidence required based on:
   - Defect type.
   - Device value.
   - Customer segment.
   - Claim amount.
   - Serial number / product identifier.
   - Prior claim history.

   Instead of a generic `Request missing evidence`, the customer receives a tailored evidence request.

2. **Guided evidence completion lane**  
   If evidence is incomplete or the defect is unclear, the claim enters a guided evidence lane:
   - Customer receives a portal-based checklist.
   - Required items may include photos, videos, serial number, proof of purchase, defect description, and error codes.
   - The system validates completeness before the claim returns to eligibility.
   - Status visibility is provided to the customer, which is especially important for Standard low-risk customers who tolerate 72-hour completion if visibility is good.

3. **Assisted evidence capture for at-risk customers**  
   For customers with low digital literacy, Premium customers, or cases with repeated failed evidence submissions, the process offers assisted capture:
   - Agent-assisted upload.
   - Chat or phone-guided photo capture.
   - Store or service-partner assistance where available.

   This mitigates the risk that a portal increases abandonment.

4. **Evidence completeness gate**  
   Evidence completeness becomes an explicit gate before:
   - Manual eligibility review, where possible.
   - Fast approval.
   - Dispatch replacement, except for the Premium low-risk accessory exception under R2.

   This directly supports R2 and reduces the alignment deviation “Fast approval before Evidence completeness.”

5. **Evidence dead-end handling**  
   If evidence cannot be obtained after two structured attempts, the claim is not allowed to loop endlessly. It is routed to one of:
   - Manual evidence concierge review.
   - Repair quote with conditional customer approval.
   - Escalation board for disputed Premium cases.
   - Denial with clear explanation, where justified.

   This reduces the current pattern of repeated evidence requests and abandonment.

### Targeted segments, paths, and activities

This redesign primarily targets:
- The “Unclear defect / missing evidence” segment, which is 22% of cases and has a median cycle time of 140 hours.
- Cases entering `Request missing evidence`.
- Cases where `Request missing evidence` occurs at least twice, currently 26% of cases entering that activity.
- Claims that abandon after evidence requests, especially the 320 cases moving from `Request missing evidence` to `Claim abandoned`.
- Manual eligibility review cases delayed by poor evidence.

It changes these activities:
- `Intake claim`
- `Request missing evidence`
- `Manual eligibility review`
- `Fraud/risk review`, where evidence inconsistency triggers risk review.
- `Fast approval`, by requiring evidence completeness before approval.
- `Claim abandoned`, by adding assisted and alternative resolution paths.

### Mechanism of improvement

The mechanism is **front-loading evidence quality and reducing loop variability**.

The current evidence loop is slow and repetitive. `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` has a median of 26 hours and p90 of 88 hours. Repeated evidence requests occur in 26% of cases entering the activity. The unclear/missing-evidence segment has the highest abandonment rate and a 140-hour median cycle time.

Redesign B reduces these problems by making evidence requests specific, validated, and customer-friendly. The prompt states that a new evidence portal could reduce repeated evidence requests by **30–45%**, but may increase abandonment for customers with low digital literacy. The assisted-evidence component is therefore not optional; it is the control that prevents the portal from simply shifting the problem into abandonment.

This redesign also improves compliance. R2 requires evidence completeness before replacement dispatch, except for Premium low-risk accessories. By making evidence completeness explicit, the process reduces one of the frequent alignment deviations: Fast approval before evidence completeness.

It also improves decision quality. Better evidence reduces incorrect eligibility decisions, unnecessary fraud reviews, and later complaints caused by misunderstanding the defect.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

Redesign B deliberately sacrifices immediate speed for ambiguous claims. Instead of approving quickly and fixing later, it requires sufficient evidence before major decisions. This may increase initial waiting time while the customer submits evidence.

It also introduces a digital-channel risk. If the portal is poorly designed or customers lack digital literacy, abandonment could increase. The prompt explicitly warns about this. Therefore, assisted support is necessary, but it adds cost and resource pressure.

This redesign does not directly solve:
- Wrong replacement part selection.
- High-value repair quote delays caused by part-price confirmation.
- Fraud review bottlenecks, except where better evidence reduces unnecessary fraud referrals.
- Manual capacity pressure if Redesign A is also applied broadly.

It is therefore materially different from Redesign A: Redesign A changes who may approve a claim; Redesign B changes what information is collected and when.

## 4. Redesign C: Pre-dispatch quality gate and repair-first high-value fulfilment

   - High-value device.
   - Claim value above EUR 400.
   - Defect types with high replacement uncertainty.
   - Serial number / model variants.
   - Prior wrong replacement or complaint history.
   - Low confidence in part selection.

2. **Pre-dispatch quality gate for high-risk fulfillment**  
   For high-risk cases, dispatch is not automatic. The claim passes through a lightweight pre-dispatch gate:
   - Automated part compatibility check using serial number, model, defect type, and replacement part.
   - QC inspector or technician verification where automated confidence is low.

3. **Repair-first logic for high-value devices**

   - `Repair quote`
   - `Customer approval`
   - `Repair device`
   - `Quality check`
   - `Quality/compliance close`

   The repair quote delay is addressed by introducing:
   - Pre-approved part-price catalog.
   - Technician authority to approve standard repair prices.
   - Automated part availability check.

   This addresses the temporal deviation where `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote` is too slow for Premium cases.

4. **Low-risk accessory bypass**  
   Low-risk accessory claims with high part-confidence may bypass the pre-dispatch gate. This prevents the gate from harming the fast lane where the expected cost of wrong replacement is only EUR 95 and customer speed expectations are strong.

5. **Complaint feedback loop**  
   Complaints and reopens are coded by root cause. Wrong replacement incidents feed back into the pre-dispatch gate rules, improving future part-selection logic.

### Targeted segments, paths, and activities

This redesign primarily targets:
- Premium high-value device claims.
- Standard high-value device claims.
- Claims where wrong replacement cost is high: EUR 410 for high-value devices.

It changes these activities:

- `Dispatch replacement`
- `Repair quote`
- `Customer approval`
- `Repair device`
- `Quality check`
- `Rework repair`
- `Customer complaint/reopen`

### Mechanism of improvement

The mechanism is **catching expensive fulfillment errors before they reach the customer**.

The temporal profile links this long-tail pattern to incorrect replacement part selection. The rework statistics show that fast approval cases have a 14.1% complaint/reopen rate, while the repair path has only 3.3%. For high-value devices, the cost of a wrong replacement is EUR 410, compared with EUR 95 for low-risk accessories.

Redesign C uses the underused quality-check resource to add a lightweight gate before dispatch. The prompt states that such a gate could catch wrong replacement selection but adds 6–18 hours. This is a deliberate trade-off: accept a modest upstream delay for high-risk cases to avoid a much larger downstream complaint, reshipment, service credit, and compliance exception.

The repair-first logic also uses the evidence from customer outcomes. The repair path is slower, but complaint/reopen is much lower. For high-value devices, the slower path may be superior on total cost and customer experience, especially when wrong replacement costs are high.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

Redesign C deliberately sacrifices some fulfillment speed for high-risk claims. Adding 6–18 hours may be unacceptable if applied to low-risk accessories or Premium low-risk claims, so the gate must be risk-scoped.

It also increases pressure on QC inspectors and technicians. The prompt says QC is underused except after repair campaigns, but if the gate is applied too broadly or during campaign peaks, QC could become a new bottleneck.

It does not directly solve:
- Missing evidence at intake.
- Eligibility compliance for claims above EUR 400.
- Reopened claims that bypass Manual eligibility review.
- Fraud review bottlenecks.

It is materially different from Redesigns A and B because it changes the physical fulfillment and quality-control layer, not the eligibility rules or evidence-collection process.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

### Redesign A: Segment-aware eligibility firewall

**Failure mode 1: Manual review capacity breach.**  
If the updated auto-screen rules route too many cases to Manual eligibility review, the seven-reviewer team may exceed the 82% utilization threshold. The prompt warns that rule updates may increase manual-review inflow by 15–35%. If this happens without capacity adjustment, queue times may grow sharply, harming Premium cases and creating new service-level breaches.

**Failure mode 2: Misclassification and rule brittleness.**  
If segment, claim value, device risk, or defect attributes are inaccurate, the firewall can make wrong routing decisions. High-value claims may slip into the fast lane, preserving the current compliance problem. Conversely, low-risk claims may be unnecessarily routed to manual review, destroying the speed advantage and increasing cost without quality benefit.

**Likely bottleneck migration.**  
The bottleneck is likely to move from the fast approval/dispatch path to:
- `Manual eligibility review`
- `Fraud/risk review`, especially on Mondays and after marketing campaigns.
- Possibly `Repair quote`, if more high-value claims are pushed into reviewed/repair paths.

---

### Redesign B: Evidence-first completion and ambiguity triage

**Failure mode 1: Digital exclusion and increased abandonment.**  
If the evidence portal is difficult to use, customers with low digital literacy may abandon claims. The prompt explicitly warns that the portal could reduce repeated evidence requests but may increase abandonment. If abandonment rises, the process may look faster for completed cases while silently failing vulnerable customers.

**Failure mode 2: False evidence completeness.**  
The portal may accept evidence that is technically present but diagnostically useless. This would create a false sense of compliance and still lead to wrong eligibility or wrong replacement decisions. Alternatively, overly strict validation may create a new evidence-rejection loop, reproducing the current problem in a different form.

**Likely bottleneck migration.**  
The bottleneck is likely to move from repeated `Request missing evidence` loops to:
- Evidence validation and exception handling.
- Agent-assisted evidence capture.
- Fraud/risk review, if inconsistent evidence triggers more risk referrals.
- Manual evidence concierge work for complex or disputed cases.

---

### Redesign C: Pre-dispatch quality gate and repair-first high-value fulfilment

**Failure mode 1: Gate applied too broadly.**  
If the pre-dispatch gate is applied to low-risk accessories or low-value claims, the added 6–18 hours may destroy the fast-path benefit without meaningful quality gain. This would be especially damaging for Premium low-risk accessories, where service-credit exposure increases after 24 hours.

**Failure mode 2: Inspector or part-data limitation.**  
QC inspectors are currently underused except after repair campaigns. If the gate is scaled during campaign peaks, inspectors may become a bottleneck. Also, if part-compatibility rules are inaccurate, the gate may produce false passes or false failures, either allowing wrong replacements or delaying correct ones.

**Likely bottleneck migration.**  
The bottleneck is likely to move from downstream complaints and reopens to:
- Pre-dispatch QC inspection.
- Technician verification.
- Repair quote and part-price confirmation, especially if repair-first logic is expanded.
- Repair device capacity, if more high-value claims move from replacement to repair.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

### First pilot

**First pilot: Redesign A in shadow mode, then a controlled live pilot.**

The first experiment should be Redesign A because it directly tests the central contradiction revealed by the mining artifacts: the fast path is cheap upstream but risky downstream. The auto-screen rules can be updated quickly, but because they may increase manual-review inflow by 15–35%, the safest first step is shadow mode.

**Stage 1: Shadow-mode segment-aware routing.**  
For 2–4 weeks, the updated auto-screen rules score all incoming claims but do not block the current process. The system records:
- Which claims would have been routed to Manual eligibility review.
- Which Fast approvals would have been blocked.
- Which Dispatch events would have been stopped for evidence completeness.
- Which high-value claims would have required Fraud/risk review.
- Estimated manual inflow increase.
- Estimated compliance improvement.

**Stage 2: Controlled live pilot.**  
If shadow mode shows manageable capacity impact, move to a live pilot for a controlled cohort:
- Enforce the high-value firewall for a sample of Premium and Standard high-value claims.
- Preserve the Premium low-risk accessory fast lane.
- Require evidence completeness for Standard low-risk accessories before dispatch.
- Automatically route reopens to Manual eligibility review.

### Metrics to monitor for the first pilot

The pilot should monitor five metric groups.

1. **Compliance metrics**
   - Percentage of fitting traces.
   - R1 violations: Fast approval above EUR 400 without Manual or Fraud review.
   - R2 violations: Dispatch before evidence completeness outside allowed exception.
   - R3 violations: Reopened claims closed without Manual eligibility review.
   - Frequency of alignment deviations such as Fast approval before evidence completeness.

2. **Cycle-time metrics**
   - Median and p90 cycle time by segment.
   - Premium low-risk accessory cycle time relative to 24-hour service-credit threshold.
   - Standard low-risk cycle time relative to 72-hour tolerance.
   - High-value cycle time compared with current 20–28 hour fast path and 96–120 hour reviewed path.
   - Queue time for Manual eligibility review and Fraud/risk review.

3. **Quality and downstream rework metrics**
   - Complaint/reopen rate by path and segment.
   - Wrong replacement rate.
   - Reshipment rate.
   - Rework repair rate.
   - Escalation board usage.

4. **Cost metrics**
   - Direct labor cost for Manual and Fraud review.
   - Cost of complaints, reshipments, and service credits.
   - Total cost after rework, not just pre-rework cost.
   - Cost difference between fast approval and reviewed path by segment.

5. **Resource pressure metrics**
   - Manual reviewer utilization, especially relative to the 82% threshold.
   - Fraud specialist backlog, especially Mondays and post-campaign periods.
   - Repair quote waiting time.
   - Escalation board overload.

### Stop/go thresholds for Redesign A pilot

Use qualitative ranges rather than false precision.

**Go conditions:**
- Simulated or actual manual inflow increase remains below about 15–25%, or capacity is explicitly added.
- Manual reviewer utilization stays below roughly 82–85%.
- R1/R2 violations fall materially, for example by more than half in the pilot cohort.
- Percentage of fitting traces improves from 68% toward at least 75–80%.
- High-value complaint/reopen begins moving from the fast-path range of 18–22% toward the reviewed-path range of 5–6%, allowing for sample-size uncertainty.
- Premium low-risk accessory cycle time remains mostly under 24 hours.

**Stop or adjust conditions:**
- Manual utilization consistently exceeds 85–90%.
- Manual queue p90 age doubles or creates visible Premium SLA breaches.
- Low-risk accessory claims are unnecessarily routed to manual review at a high rate.
- High-value p90 cycle time worsens by more than about 25–30% without compensating quality improvement.
- Service credits or escalations rise sharply.

### Second pilot: Redesign B evidence-first completion

After the shadow/early live A pilot, pilot Redesign B on:
- Unclear defect / missing evidence claims.
- Claims entering `Request missing evidence`.
- Claims with repeated evidence requests.
- High-abandonment customer groups.

**Metrics:**
- Repeated evidence request rate.
- Abandonment rate.
- Median and p90 cycle time for unclear/missing evidence claims.
- Evidence completeness at eligibility.
- R2 violations.
- Manual time spent on evidence clarification.
- Digital completion rate.
- Assisted-support volume.

**Stop/go thresholds:**
- Go if repeated evidence requests fall within the expected 30–45% reduction range.
- Go if abandonment is flat or falls; if abandonment rises by more than about 5–10 percentage points, pause and add assisted support.
- Go if unclear/missing evidence median cycle time falls materially from the current 140-hour baseline.
- Stop if the portal creates a new rejection loop or if evidence quality remains poor despite completion.

### Third pilot: Redesign C pre-dispatch quality gate

Pilot Redesign C on:
- High-value device replacements.
- Claims with high wrong-replacement risk.
- Defect types historically linked to dispatch complaints.
- Cases where part confidence is low.

**Metrics:**
- Wrong replacement rate.
- `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` rate.
- Rework repair rate for high-value devices.
- Gate lead time, expected 6–18 hours.
- QC inspector utilization.
- Repair quote cycle time.
- Total cost after rework.
- Premium high-value customer satisfaction and escalation rate.

**Stop/go thresholds:**
- Go if wrong replacement and dispatch complaints fall enough to justify the added 6–18 hours, especially for high-value claims where a single avoided EUR 410 error is significant.
- Go if high-value complaint/reopen moves toward the 5–6% reviewed-path range.
- Go if QC utilization remains manageable and gate lead time stays within the 6–18 hour design range.
- Stop or narrow the scope if the gate is applied to low-risk claims, causes broad cycle-time deterioration, or creates a QC bottleneck during campaigns.

### Sequencing logic

The sequencing is deliberate:

1. **Redesign A first in shadow mode** because it tests the main fast-versus-reviewed contradiction and quantifies the manual-capacity impact before forcing large-scale routing changes.
2. **Redesign B second** because evidence completeness is a prerequisite for durable compliance. It also reduces repeated manual work and abandonment, which can create capacity headroom for Redesign A.
3. **Redesign C third** because once eligibility routing and evidence quality are cleaner, the remaining high-value problem is fulfillment accuracy. The pre-dispatch gate and repair-first logic target the downstream complaint tail without unnecessarily slowing low-risk claims.

This sequence avoids the mistake of simply slowing everything down. It preserves speed where the mining evidence supports it, improves evidence where the process is ambiguous, and adds quality control where the cost of error is high.

### How to know whether improvements are real rather than shifted downstream

To avoid mistaking local improvement for end-to-end improvement, each pilot must be evaluated over the full case lifecycle, not just until first closure.

Use the following methods:

1. **Track outcomes after close.**  
   Keep each claim observable for a post-close window, for example 30–60 days, to capture:
   - Customer complaint/reopen.
   - Reshipment.
   - Rework repair.
   - Service credits.
   - Escalation board usage.

   A redesign that closes cases faster but increases post-close complaints is not a real improvement.

2. **Measure total cost after rework.**  
   The prompt already shows that fast approval is 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after rework. Therefore, pilots must compare total landed cost, including:
   - Labor cost.
   - Replacement cost.
   - Wrong replacement cost.
   - Reshipment cost.
   - Service credits.
   - Rework cost.
   - Escalation cost.

3. **Use segment-matched comparisons.**  
   Compare pilot cases with similar current-process cases by:
   - Customer segment.
   - Device risk.
   - Claim value.
   - Defect type.
   - Evidence status.

   This prevents misleading conclusions caused by case-mix changes.

4. **Monitor abandonment and denial, not only completed cases.**  
   If a redesign improves median cycle time only because more customers abandon, that is not a real improvement. Track:
   - Claim abandonment.
   - Denial rate.
   - Reopen after denial.
   - Customer complaints after denial.

5. **Replay the new event log against the normative Petri net.**  
   Use process mining again after the pilot. Check whether:
   - Percentage of fitting traces improves from 68%.
   - Alignment deviations decrease.
   - Low-frequency unauthorized variants decline.
   - Precision improves, indicating fewer illegal path combinations.

6. **Watch for bottleneck migration.**  
   Improvement is not real if it merely moves the queue. Monitor:
   - Manual eligibility utilization.
   - Fraud review backlog.
   - Evidence support workload.
   - Repair quote waiting time.
   - QC gate waiting time.
   - Escalation board overload.

The final decision should therefore be based on end-to-end segment outcomes, not on a single average cycle-time number. The evidence supports a hybrid conclusion: keep low-risk accessory claims fast, make high-value claims more reviewed and quality-gated, and fix the evidence loop before assuming either speed or review is the universal answer.
