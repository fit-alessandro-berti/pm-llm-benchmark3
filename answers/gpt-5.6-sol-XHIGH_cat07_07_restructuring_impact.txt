# 1. Mechanism map

Management’s expected routing model does not automatically imply fewer observed handovers:

- Technical outbound/return handovers rise from **67 to 77 per 100 cases**.
- Approval outbound/return handovers rise from **81 to 92 per 100 cases**.
- Legal/Risk referrals rise from **9 to 12 per 100 cases**.
- Cross-team handovers fall from **6 to 3 per 100 cases**.
- If the categories are comparable and non-overlapping, listed handovers rise from about **343 to 359 per 100 cases**. Thus, ad hoc cross-team transfers may fall while formal queue interfaces increase.
- A rough “wait-pressure index”—frequency multiplied by median wait—rises from approximately **588 to 722 handover-hours per 100 cases**, about 23%. This is not a cycle-time forecast because medians are not additive, but it indicates greater queue exposure in management’s own estimates.

The utilization target also needs clarification. Current technical utilization is roughly 73% when weighted by team volumes. A 15% relative increase implies about 84%; a 15-percentage-point increase implies about 88%. Either level leaves less surge capacity, especially because Legal and Risk already operate above 90%.

| Restructuring action | Mechanism | Expected process-mining signature | Conditions and countereffects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move embedded technical specialists into a shared service | Replaces several local resource relationships with a hub-and-spoke network | Fewer local specialist edges; higher in/out centrality and workload concentration around shared-service resources in the handover network | This is a **nominal handover reduction** only if management counts organizational boxes rather than event-level resource changes. Caseworker  specialist  caseworker remains two handovers |
| Route more cases formally to specialists | Informal local advice is replaced by logged queue referrals | Technical referrals rise from 36 to 41 per 100; return edges rise from 31 to 36; subcontracting patterns such as caseworker  specialist  caseworker may become more frequent | Better first-time routing could reduce repeated loops, but loss of informal screening could create avoidable referrals |
| Put Technical, Legal/Risk triage, and difficult Quality Check into a central service | Multiple arrival streams compete for a common or closely coupled capacity pool | Rising performance-DFG times into shared activities; increasing queue-age percentiles; central resource utilization and WIP concentration; more queue-related variants | Pooling can absorb East’s peaks, but common capacity creates a hidden bottleneck and priority competition |
| Increase specialist utilization by 15% | Less idle time and broader pooling may increase throughput | More service time and cases per specialist; more cross-team working-together links; more similar activity profiles across specialists | Utilization is not equivalent to throughput. At high utilization, small arrival spikes, absences, or complex cases can produce nonlinear tail waits |
| Standardize routing and specialist decisions | Less variation in who handles a case and how decisions are recorded | Fewer low-frequency variants, more concentrated DFG paths, more consistent outcomes and rework rates conditional on case mix | Over-standardization may mishandle regional, language, security, or unusual exceptions |
| Remove local technical availability | Reduces immediate local consultation and regional knowledge | Declining local specialist–caseworker working-together links; lower use of `local_expertise_flag`; increased formal referral rates; narrower local resource activity profiles | North and South may lose a capability that currently prevents rework. East may benefit from access to a wider pool |
| Centralize approvals and remove one supervisor | Creates a standardized decision point but separates approval from local ownership | Approval edge frequency rises; approval wait rises; more resources associated with approval; possible increase in reassignment and return-for-clarification variants | Role clarity improves only if there is an explicit queue owner, decision authority, escalation path, and responsibility for post-approval errors |
| Pool staff for absence cover | Any available specialist can handle demand from all teams | Less sensitivity to the absence of one local specialist; denser working-together network among central specialists; fewer ad hoc cross-team handovers | The organization exchanges local single points of failure for a central common-mode failure. A central outage or simultaneous absence affects all teams |
| Transfer hard cases away from local staff | Local workers receive fewer learning opportunities | Over time, declining independent handling of medium-complexity work; greater activity-role polarization; increasing referral rates after controlling for complexity | Central specialists may learn faster, while local capability and career progression weaken |
| Standardize specialist and approval outputs | Better advice may reduce errors and rework | Lower repeat-activity counts, fewer `Rework` events, lower `rework_flag`, and fewer specialist-return loops | Missing local context may instead increase rework, especially for complex or accommodation-sensitive cases |
| Apply one shared priority policy | Decisions become more uniform across teams | Reduced raw team differences if routing is fair; similar queue waits within comparable priority/complexity strata | Low-priority, language-sensitive, or region-specific cases may be repeatedly displaced. Fairness must be assessed by team and customer segment, not only globally |

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic scenario

**Assumptions:** Technical skills are sufficiently interchangeable, queue ownership is explicit, specialist capacity includes surge headroom, Legal/Risk and difficult Quality Check have protected lanes, notes are standardized, and local consultation remains available.

- **Handover pattern:** Ad hoc cross-team transfers fall. Formal technical and approval handovers rise as forecast, but repeated ping-pong loops and reassignment fall. The subcontracting network shows mostly one clean caseworker  shared specialist  caseworker round trip rather than repeated cycles.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Median waits remain at or below the management estimates. P90 and P95 waits do not diverge sharply from the median, and queue age remains stable. East gains access to spare North/South capacity.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East’s median and P90 improve. North and South remain broadly stable because longer specialist waits are offset by better first-time decisions and fewer exception loops. The overall tail narrows rather than merely shifting easy cases forward.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** East’s rework approaches North/South levels. Decision consistency improves, and complex-case rework does not increase. Difficult Quality Check catches errors without creating many additional loops.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** A named queue owner, service-level rules, and a clear RACI make it apparent who owns the case while advice or approval is pending and who resolves conflicting advice.
- **Fairness implications:** East’s historical disadvantage narrows. Comparable customer segments receive similar waiting times and outcomes after controlling for complexity, specialty, and priority. Regional accommodation cases do not receive extra avoidable handovers.
- **Early warning signs:** Even in this scenario, rising P90 queue age, growing return-for-clarification variants, a falling rate of first-time routing acceptance, or worsening North/South complex-case rework would invalidate the optimistic assumptions.

## Central scenario

**Assumptions:** Pooling works partially, but the service operates near its planned utilization and local knowledge cannot be fully encoded in standard notes.

- **Handover pattern:** The network becomes more centralized. Cross-team handovers fall, but total resource handovers remain stable or rise because technical, approval, and Legal/Risk queue interfaces become more frequent. Shared-service resources become dominant network hubs.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Medians are close to management’s estimates: approximately 4.0 hours into Technical Shared Service, 2.2 hours on return, 3.0 hours into shared approval, and 10.5 hours for Legal/Risk. P90 waits rise more than medians during demand peaks.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** The global median is stable or improves slightly because simple cases move quickly and automation increases. High-complexity P90/P95 times worsen. East improves from its current constrained position, while North and South experience somewhat longer specialist and approval waits.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Overall rework is approximately flat, concealing divergent effects: East improves, while complex North/South cases lose some benefit from local knowledge. Standard decisions reduce some variation but increase clarification work for regional exceptions.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Formal roles appear standardized, but ownership is temporarily ambiguous when advice has been returned but not claimed, or when specialist and approver decisions conflict. Reassignment counts rise during the transition.
- **Fairness implications:** Team gaps narrow because East improves, but some customer segments—particularly those requiring local or language context—experience more handovers and longer tails. Low-priority cases are more vulnerable to queue displacement.
- **Early warning signs:** Central-resource edge concentration rises rapidly; utilization approaches 90%; arrival rates periodically exceed completions; specialist return edges are followed by clarification or reassignment; local referral rates rise after controlling for case complexity; segment-specific wait ratios widen.

## Adverse scenario

**Assumptions:** The shared service absorbs all proposed work without adequate capacity separation, informal local screening disappears, notes remain inconsistent, and shared approval lacks a clear owner.

- **Handover pattern:** The apparent reduction in cross-team handovers masks repeated caseworker  shared service  caseworker  shared service loops. Reassignments and low-frequency variants increase. The central resources dominate handover and subcontracting networks.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Technical, approval, Legal/Risk, and difficult Quality Check compete for capacity. Median waits rise, but P90/P95 and oldest-case age rise much faster. High-priority arrivals repeatedly displace lower-priority work.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Easy completed cases keep the mean or median deceptively stable. High-complexity and specialty cases accumulate in WIP, causing SLA misses and a strongly right-skewed distribution. East may initially improve but loses that benefit once the common queue saturates.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Local-context errors, inconsistent specialist advice, and return-for-clarification loops increase rework. Quality problems may appear only weeks later at implementation, Quality Check, or customer complaint.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases remain unclaimed after advice is returned; approval decisions are attributed to a queue rather than a responsible person; local workers assume the shared service owns the case while shared staff assume local workers do.
- **Fairness implications:** Low-priority, high-complexity, regional, or accommodation-sensitive cases wait disproportionately long. Raw team averages may improve while particular customer segments deteriorate.
- **Early warning signs:** Sustained utilization above 90% accompanied by backlog growth; oldest-case age increasing every week; Legal/Risk waits exceeding their already high baseline; reassignment spikes; repeated activities and variant proliferation; rising staff reports of unclear ownership; widening SLA and rework gaps by segment.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Queue entry, assignment, work-start, and completion timestamps should be added before the pilot. With only one event timestamp, inter-event duration combines waiting and part of the successor’s service time.

## Leading indicators available within days or weeks

| Indicator | Event-log measure | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Handover frequency and mix | `discover_handover_of_work_network`; normalize edge weights per 100 cases; separate local, central, approval, Legal/Risk, and cross-team edges | Tests whether handovers really fall or are merely relabeled |
| Central network concentration | Share of handover edges touching central resources; resource workload HHI; in/out degree and betweenness | Rapid concentration indicates dependence on a central hub |
| Specialist round trips | `discover_subcontracting_network`; count caseworker  specialist  caseworker patterns and repeated versions | One clean round trip may be desirable; repeated round trips indicate missing information or unclear decisions |
| Collaboration structure | `discover_working_together_network` by phase and team | Shows whether central specialists provide broad cover and whether local collaborative ties disappear |
| Queue waits and age | Median, P75, P90, P95, maximum age, and number above SLA for each queue | Tail growth is an earlier saturation signal than average cycle time |
| Arrival/completion balance | Cases entering versus leaving each shared queue per day/week; WIP and oldest-case trend | Several periods with arrivals above completions indicate accumulating backlog |
| Utilization with capacity context | `get_service_time`, active work intervals, cases per specialist, concurrent workload | Higher utilization is beneficial only if backlog and tail waits remain controlled |
| Routing accuracy | Percentage of eligible cases correctly routed first time; reroutes; queue rejection; `handover_reason`; `reassignment_count` | Detects weak routing rules and avoidable referrals |
| Role-clarity proxy | Number of resources touching approval/specialist activities; resource/activity entropy; unclaimed intervals; reassignment and return-for-clarification rates | Must be combined with staff feedback because event logs cannot directly establish perceived accountability |
| Local expertise precursor | Use of `local_expertise_flag`; formal referral rate conditional on complexity; loss of local working-together edges | A rapid decline may predict later rework or deskilling |
| Early fairness gaps | Queue-wait, handover, and reassignment distributions by origin team, customer segment, location, complexity, and specialty | Identifies differential burden before final outcomes accumulate |
| Resilience concentration | Share of work handled by the top one and top three specialists; performance during leave or outage periods | Distinguishes useful pooling from common-mode dependency |

## Lagging indicators available after outcomes accumulate

| Indicator | Event-log measure | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Full cycle-time distribution | Median, mean, P75, P90, P95, maximum, and survival/WIP age by case cohort | Determines whether apparent improvement is broad or limited to easy completed cases |
| SLA performance | Overall and segmented SLA miss rates, including open cases already older than SLA | Must be reported by team, complexity, specialty, priority, and customer segment |
| Rework | `get_rework_cases_per_activity`, explicit `Rework` events, repeated activities, and `rework_flag` | Separate rework after technical advice, approval, implementation, and Quality Check |
| Outcome quality | `outcome` by route and segment, adjusted for case mix | Tests whether standardization improves actual decisions rather than only speed |
| Decision consistency | Outcome and rework variation for comparable cases; variant and DFG differences | Lower variation is positive only if outcomes remain appropriate for exceptions |
| Fairness of outcomes | SLA, rework, adverse outcome, and cycle-tail gaps across teams and customer segments | Compare like-for-like strata and report both absolute gaps and ratios |
| Local learning trajectory | Complexity-adjusted specialist referral rate and independent resolution quality over several months | A steadily rising referral rate may indicate local deskilling |
| Customer experience | Satisfaction or complaint data joined by case identifier | Needed because customer burden is not fully visible in the process trace |
| Resilience outcomes | Cycle time, backlog recovery, and quality during actual absence or demand-spike periods | Tests whether pooling provides recovery capacity rather than just higher steady-state utilization |

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** A stable mean can coexist with sharply higher P90/P95 cycle times. Central queues commonly create a long right tail when utilization is high.

2. **High-complexity deterioration:** Automation may accelerate simple cases enough to offset slower high-complexity cases. Complexity-stratified distributions are therefore necessary.

3. **Completion and censoring bias:** Early post-change reporting includes cases that finished quickly but excludes cases still waiting in the shared queue. The average among completed cases can improve while backlog worsens.

4. **East/North/South composition effects:** East may improve while North and South deteriorate, or vice versa. A global average can hide both outcomes through Simpson’s paradox.

5. **Local knowledge loss:** Regional conventions, language needs, and customer history may not be represented in formal notes. Resulting errors may appear later as rework, customer contact, or implementation failure rather than immediate delay.

6. **Fairness effects:** Particular customer segments may experience more handovers, clarification requests, or priority displacement even when the overall cycle average improves.

7. **Learning and deskilling:** Routing every difficult case away can reduce local workers’ ability to handle future medium-complexity work. This may take months to appear and can gradually increase specialist demand.

8. **Accountability gaps:** A case can move quickly through a queue while no individual accepts responsibility for the correctness or implementation of the advice. Reassignments and conflicting decisions may arise later.

9. **Exception-handling failure:** Standard paths may become faster while unusual legal, security, language, or regional cases proliferate into rare, slow variants.

10. **Reduced resilience:** A pooled service may absorb one local absence but create a common-mode dependency on a small number of central specialists, systems, or queue coordinators.

11. **Quality lag:** Rework, failed implementation, complaints, and adverse outcomes may occur weeks after the initial specialist decision, outside an early cycle-time report.

12. **Hidden customer effort:** Customers may be contacted repeatedly by different roles or asked to restate information. This can increase burden without substantially changing internal cycle time.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope and sequencing

1. **Telemetry and shadow phase**
   - Add queue-entry, assignment, work-start, completion, queue owner, routing-decision, and return-reason data.
   - Standardize case notes and `handover_reason` values.
   - Run proposed routing in shadow mode for approximately four weeks without changing actual ownership.
   - Estimate arrival rates, service-time distributions, skill constraints, and likely queue load.

2. **Technical shared-service pilot**
   - Start with East because it has the strongest plausible benefit from pooling, but also route a stratified sample of eligible North and South cases through the shared service. An East-only pilot would not measure the loss of strong local expertise in North and South.
   - Retain protected local technical consultation hours and an emergency fallback.
   - Do not initially combine Technical, Legal/Risk, and difficult Quality Check in one undifferentiated queue.

3. **Approval pilot**
   - Add shared approval only after technical queue guardrails have remained stable.
   - Do not permanently remove the supervisor position during the experiment; use redeployment or temporary vacancy management so the decision is reversible.

4. **Integrated-load test**
   - Introduce Legal/Risk triage and difficult Quality Check in controlled increments, preferably through separate capacity lanes.
   - Test simultaneous demand peaks before considering wider rollout.

## Comparison group

- Use a deterministic case-level allocation, such as a hash of case ID, to route an agreed percentage of eligible cases to the pilot or current process.
- Stratify allocation by team, complexity, specialty, priority, and customer segment so neither route receives systematically easier cases.
- If case-level randomization is operationally infeasible, use a stepped-wedge design by team-week with concurrent untreated weeks.
- Keep a North and South control because their embedded-specialist model currently performs relatively well.
- Estimate both within-team treatment effects and difference-in-differences relative to the concurrent control. East’s result should not be generalized directly to North and South.

## Observation windows

- **Baseline:** At least 8–12 weeks before the change, using the same seasonal period where possible.
- **Live pilot:** At least 12–16 weeks, long enough to observe queue buildup and several monthly demand cycles.
- **Outcome follow-up:** An additional 4–8 weeks for rework, Quality Check, implementation failures, and complaints.
- Report both completion cohorts and open WIP. Do not wait only for every case to close, but do not treat incomplete cases as successful.

## Required segmentation

Report outcomes at least by:

- Origin team: North, South, East
- `case_complexity`: especially high versus low/medium
- `required_specialty`: technical, legal, risk, security, and none
- `customer_segment`
- Team × complexity and team × specialty interactions
- Priority, location, `local_expertise_flag`, and accommodation/language category where lawfully available

Raw comparisons should be supplemented by stratified or risk-adjusted comparisons because the shared queue is expected to receive a harder case mix.

## Guardrails and triggers

Thresholds should be preregistered using baseline variation and concurrent controls. Illustrative triggers are:

| Guardrail | Pause or adjustment trigger |
|---|---|
| Central queue wait | Median exceeds the management estimate by more than 20% for two consecutive weeks, or P90 exceeds the matched control/baseline by more than 20% |
| Backlog | Arrivals exceed completions for multiple weeks and oldest-case age continues to rise |
| Utilization | Sustained utilization above 90% accompanied by backlog or worsening P90 wait |
| Cycle-time tail | P90/P95 deterioration beyond the agreed control limit, even if the median improves |
| SLA misses | Increase of more than an agreed absolute margin, such as 2 percentage points, overall or in a material segment |
| Rework/quality | Material increase in `rework_flag`, repeated activities, failed Quality Check, or any critical legal/security error |
| Fairness | Segment wait ratio above an agreed level such as 1.25, or a widening SLA/rework gap not explained by case mix |
| Role clarity | Reassignment or unclaimed-case intervals increase materially; staff clarity scores fall |
| Local capability | Complexity-adjusted referral rate rises persistently while local independent resolution declines |
| Resilience | Absence or demand spikes cannot be cleared within the agreed recovery period |

Triggers should lead to defined actions: cap new arrivals, add capacity, separate specialty lanes, restore local technical sessions, revise routing, restore local approval authority, or pause the next rollout wave. Critical quality or fairness failures should allow immediate rollback.

## Staff and customer feedback

- Weekly short pulse survey on workload, ownership clarity, advice quality, and access to local expertise.
- Fortnightly structured discussions with local caseworkers, specialists, supervisors, implementation staff, and Quality Check staff.
- Case debriefs for the oldest cases, rework cases, disputed approvals, and accommodation-sensitive cases.
- Record structured reason codes rather than relying only on free text.
- Join customer satisfaction and complaint data to case IDs where governance permits.

# 6. Recommendation

**Modify the design and pilot first.**

Pooling has credible benefits: East may gain access to broader capacity, absence cover may improve, and decision outputs may become more consistent. However, management’s own estimates show higher waits for every major centralized interface and do not demonstrate a true reduction in event-level handovers. The proposal also places additional demand near Legal/Risk resources that are already above 90% utilization.

The pilot should therefore:

- Keep Technical, Legal/Risk, difficult Quality Check, and approval as separately measurable capacity lanes.
- Retain local technical consultation and knowledge-development mechanisms.
- Assign an explicit owner to every shared queue and every returned case.
- Delay permanent supervisor removal.
- Maintain surge capacity rather than treating maximum utilization as the primary success measure.
- Scale East, North, and South separately if their effects differ.

Proceed to the next phase only if median **and tail** waits remain controlled, backlog is stable, rework and outcome quality do not deteriorate, fairness gaps do not widen, and local capability indicators remain acceptable. If East improves while North/South deteriorate, a hybrid model—greater pooling for East with retained embedded expertise elsewhere—may be preferable.

Management should not infer success from an early improvement in average cycle time. Early averages will disproportionately represent simple completed cases, exclude queued WIP, and precede many rework, learning, fairness, and resilience effects.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
START = "start:timestamp"          # add during pilot if possible
RES = "org:resource"

LOCAL_TEAMS = {"North", "South", "East"}

def prepare(raw, phase):
    df = raw.copy()
    df[TS] = pd.to_datetime(df[TS], utc=True)

    if START in df.columns:
        df[START] = pd.to_datetime(df[START], utc=True)

    df["phase"] = phase

    # Preserve the origin team on every event. Otherwise filtering team="North"
    # after centralization could truncate traces when shared-service events have
    # team="Shared Service".
    local_events = df[df["team"].isin(LOCAL_TEAMS)].sort_values([CASE, TS])
    origin_team = local_events.groupby(CASE)["team"].first()
    df["case_origin_team"] = df[CASE].map(origin_team)
    df["case_origin_team"] = df["case_origin_team"].fillna(df["team"])

    kwargs = dict(
        case_id=CASE,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS
    )
    if START in df.columns:
        kwargs["start_timestamp_key"] = START

    return pm4py.format_dataframe(df, **kwargs)


before = prepare(pd.read_parquet("before.parquet"), "before")
after = prepare(pd.read_parquet("after.parquet"), "after")
```

A transition table allows direct analysis of handover delays:

```python
def transition_table(log):
    x = log.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    g = x.groupby(CASE, sort=False)

    x["next_activity"] = g[ACT].shift(-1)
    x["next_resource"] = g[RES].shift(-1)
    x["next_timestamp"] = g[TS].shift(-1)

    x["is_handover"] = (
        x["next_resource"].notna() &
        (x[RES] != x["next_resource"])
    )

    # Completion-to-completion elapsed time is only a proxy: it includes
    # the next event's service time.
    x["elapsed_to_next_hours"] = (
        x["next_timestamp"] - x[TS]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600

    if START in x.columns:
        x["next_start"] = g[START].shift(-1)
        x["queue_wait_hours"] = (
            x["next_start"] - x[TS]
        ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    else:
        x["queue_wait_hours"] = np.nan

    return x[x["is_handover"]]


trans_before = transition_table(before)
trans_after = transition_table(after)

wait_report = (
    trans_after
    .groupby([ACT, "next_activity"])
    ["queue_wait_hours"]
    .agg(
        count="size",
        median="median",
        p90=lambda s: s.quantile(0.90),
        p95=lambda s: s.quantile(0.95),
        maximum="max"
    )
)
```

## Rework, cycle time, and SLA misses

```python
def case_level_table(log, sla_days_by_priority, observation_end=None):
    x = log.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    observation_end = (
        pd.Timestamp.now(tz="UTC")
        if observation_end is None
        else pd.Timestamp(observation_end)
    )

    first_event = x.groupby(CASE).first()
    last_event = x.groupby(CASE).last()

    c = pd.DataFrame(index=first_event.index)
    c["start"] = x.groupby(CASE)[TS].min()
    c["last_event"] = x.groupby(CASE)[TS].max()
    c["closed"] = x.groupby(CASE)[ACT].apply(
        lambda s: (s == "Close").any()
    )

    c["cycle_days"] = (
        c["last_event"] - c["start"]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 86400

    c["open_age_days"] = np.where(
        c["closed"],
        np.nan,
        (observation_end - c["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    )

    for attr in [
        "case_origin_team", "case_complexity", "required_specialty",
        "priority", "customer_segment", "location",
        "local_expertise_flag", "outcome"
    ]:
        if attr in first_event.columns:
            c[attr] = first_event[attr]

    if "rework_flag" in x.columns:
        c["rework_flag"] = x.groupby(CASE)["rework_flag"].max()
    else:
        c["rework_flag"] = x.groupby(CASE)[ACT].apply(
            lambda s: (s == "Rework").any()
        )

    if "reassignment_count" in x.columns:
        c["reassignment_count"] = (
            x.groupby(CASE)["reassignment_count"].max()
        )

    c["sla_days"] = c["priority"].map(sla_days_by_priority)

    # Closed cases that exceeded SLA.
    c["sla_miss_closed"] = (
        c["closed"] &
        (c["cycle_days"] > c["sla_days"])
    )

    # Open cases that are already beyond SLA.
    c["sla_miss_open"] = (
        ~c["closed"] &
        (c["open_age_days"] > c["sla_days"])
    )

    c["sla_miss"] = c["sla_miss_closed"] | c["sla_miss_open"]
    return c.reset_index()
```

```python
def segmented_summary(cases, group_cols):
    return (
        cases.groupby(group_cols, dropna=False)
        .agg(
            cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
```
