# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

All durations below are in minutes. Let (c) denote a case and (i in \{A,B,C\}) a branch. Let (t^a_c), (t^r_c), (t^s_{ci}), and (t^e_{ci}) be, respectively, arrival, fork release, branch start, and branch end timestamps. Let (t^f_c) and (t^{fe}_c) be final start and final end.

The non-overlapping case decomposition is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
U_c &= t^r_c-t^a_c &&\text{(upstream elapsed time before fork release)},\\
Q_{ci} &= t^s_{ci}-t^r_c &&\text{(branch-specific queue waiting)},\\
S_{ci} &= t^e_{ci}-t^s_{ci} &&\text{(branch elapsed service interval)},\\
J_c &= \max_i(t^e_{ci}) &&\text{(join-ready time)},\\
W_{ci} &= J_c-t^e_{ci} \geq 0 &&\text{(synchronization waiting after branch completion)},\\
L_c &= J_c-t^r_c=\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}) &&\text{(elapsed fork-join stage)},\\
D_c &= t^f_c-J_c &&\text{(downstream queueing, conditional on the stated model)},\\
F_c &= t^{fe}_c-t^f_c &&\text{(final service)},\\
T_c &= t^{fe}_c-t^a_c=U_c+L_c+D_c+F_c &&\text{(total case flow time)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The critical branch is any branch attaining (J_c). In the supplied data there are no ties; for reproducibility, the code below retains all tied branches in `critical_branches` and chooses the first in the fixed order A, B, C as `critical_branch`.

Synchronization waits are descriptive branch-level measures, not additional serial elapsed time. For every branch,

\[
Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci}=J_c-t^r_c=L_c.
\]

Therefore summing the three branch paths gives

\[
\sum_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci})=3L_c,
\]

not (L_c). The waits overlap in calendar time: while one branch is waiting, the other branch's remaining work or completion defines the same join interval. Adding (sum_i W_{ci}) to (L_c) would count parts of the already measured fork-join interval again. For example, F02 has (L=60), but its synchronization waits are (10+0+35=45); the fork-join elapsed time remains 60, not 105.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

| branch | service mean (min; min–max) | queue mean (min; min–max) | queue + service mean; maximum | critical cases (frequency) | synchronization wait mean; maximum | path when critical: mean; maximum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| A | 50.00; 50–50 | 0.00; 0–0 | 50.00; 50 | F01, F11 (2/12, 16.7%) | 11.00; 25 | 50.00; 50 |
| B | 37.50; 30–45 | 19.17; 5–30 | 56.67; 75 | F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12 (8/12, 66.7%) | 4.33; 20 | 65.00; 75 |
| C | 20.17; 18–30 | 10.42; 5–35 | 30.58; 57 | F04, F08 (2/12, 16.7%) | 30.42; 52 | 56.00; 57 |

B is the strongest controller of total completion time. Its service mean is not the largest—A's 50 minutes is larger—but B's queue plus service path averages 56.67 minutes, has the largest maximum (75), and is the last branch in two-thirds of cases. B is critical in both F07 and F12, the two cases with a 75-minute fork-join stage.

A and C are tied in observed critical frequency, so they should not be reported as strictly ordered from frequency alone. A is a persistent 50-minute technical path with no observed queue; it controls F01 and F11. C's all-case path is shorter, but its queue tail reaches 35 minutes and makes it critical in F04 and F08. C's high mean synchronization wait (30.42) mostly means that C often finishes early and waits for A or B; it is not evidence that C is the main completion controller. A reasonable influence ranking is B first, then A and C as a near-tied second tier, with A the more stable baseline constraint and C the more variable, queue-sensitive exception.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

For the direct calculations below, the changed branch endpoint is recomputed and the new join is the maximum of the three endpoints. The observed downstream queue (D_c) and final service (F_c) are held fixed for the A and B counterfactuals. This isolates branch-path leverage; in a real capacity change, the final queue may absorb, reduce, or occasionally reorder the benefit. The third counterfactual is an idealized `D=0` scenario, representing complete removal of downstream waiting rather than a claim that one extra resource will achieve it.

| case | A service reduced 20%: flow-time saving | B queue reduced 50%: flow-time saving | downstream queue reduced to 0: flow-time saving |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| F01 | 5 | 0 | 8 |
| F02 | 0 | 10 | 15 |
| F03 | 0 | 10 | 15 |
| F04 | 0 | 0 | 18 |
| F05 | 0 | 10 | 10 |
| F06 | 0 | 15 | 15 |
| F07 | 0 | 15 | 15 |
| F08 | 0 | 0 | 15 |
| F09 | 0 | 12.5 | 15 |
| F10 | 0 | 5 | 20 |
| F11 | 10 | 0 | 20 |
| F12 | 0 | 15 | 10 |
| **total** | **15** | **92.5** | **176** |
| **mean per case** | **1.25** | **7.71** | **14.67** |

Reducing A service from 50 to 40 minutes helps only F01 and F11. In F01, B becomes the new limiting endpoint after a 5-minute improvement; in F11, the new A endpoint reaches 11:20, matching B, and the join advances 10 minutes. The other ten cases retain a later B or C endpoint. Thus the large A service time is not by itself a reason to prioritize A acceleration.

Halving B's queue delay is more leveraged. It produces positive direct savings in all eight B-critical cases, although F10 gains only 5 minutes because A becomes the later endpoint after B's improvement. F01, F04, F08, and F11 have zero direct benefit because A or C remains later. The 92.5-minute total saving is much larger than the A-service counterfactual, even though B's mean service is shorter.

Removing all downstream waiting would save the observed (D_c) in every case, 176 minutes in total and 14.67 minutes per case. A 50% reduction instead would save 88 minutes under the same static assumption. This is a useful ceiling for a downstream intervention, but the data do not show that all 14.67 minutes is caused by insufficient final capacity. Earlier branch completion can simply move a case's waiting location or leave the final station schedule unchanged. Conversely, reducing branch completion times could reduce (D_c) as well, so the actual total-flow benefit can differ from the isolated branch numbers.

As an additional sensitivity check, reducing C's service alone by 20% would move only F04 and F08 in this static calculation, by 4.4 and 5 minutes respectively (9.4 minutes total). That is smaller than either the B-queue intervention or the A-service intervention, while C's queue—not its service—is what makes F04 especially late.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

1. Prioritize B queue reduction through dispatching, work release, or incremental B capacity. B has the highest critical frequency and the largest queue-plus-service tail, so reducing its queue should improve throughput and flow-time tail more than a blanket A service reduction. The risk is a bottleneck shift: once B is faster, A will control cases such as F01/F11 and C can control cases such as F04/F08. B's resource utilization may fall if capacity is added, while A, C, or the final station may become more heavily loaded. Synchronization waits for A and C may increase in individual cases because they finish earlier relative to B, even though total case completion improves. Pilot one scheduling or capacity change and measure B queue by case, B critical frequency, join-ready time, final-station queue, and throughput; do not use B service time alone as the success metric.

2. Pilot a final-station capacity or release-policy change, but first validate that (D_c) is a physical resource queue. The observed downstream gap averages 14.67 minutes and reaches 20, so reducing it should improve every case in the idealized calculation and should reduce the flow-time tail directly. More final capacity can lower F1/F2 utilization and create underutilization if branch release is the real constraint; alternatively, it can expose B or A as the next bottleneck. It will not reduce branch synchronization waits, because those occur before join-ready. It may, however, prevent branch improvements from being masked downstream and make the benefit of B changes visible in final completion times. Pilot with final-resource busy intervals, queue-entry/queue-exit timestamps, reason codes for delayed starts, and a controlled comparison of final-start minus join-ready. A release-policy change should be tested alongside capacity because the same gap could be approval or batching delay rather than a resource shortage.

# 7. Limits of inference

- The timestamps establish temporal relationships and permit the max-based decomposition, but they do not establish causality. A B queue may be caused by an unobserved resource, a release rule, batching, or a prior case; its correlation with late joins is not proof that a particular intervention will produce the counterfactual savings.
- Branch resource capacity, resource identity, staffing, and assignment rules are not supplied. The observed starts show waiting intervals but cannot identify how many A, B, or C resources exist, whether capacity is shared, or which resource constraint caused each wait. Final resource labels F1/F2 are useful descriptive fields, but the full final-station schedule and all competing work are not present.
- `end - start` is treated as active service because that is the available interval. The timestamps do not reveal work preemption, pauses, setup, inspection, or hidden idle time inside an activity. If an activity can be preempted, the reported service column is elapsed activity time, not necessarily hands-on work.
- `final_start - join_ready` is called downstream queueing only under the stated model. It could instead include a release policy, approval, batching, transport, or a deliberate hold. Without a queue-entry reason, resource-state log, or competing-case log, adding final capacity cannot be assigned a unique effect.
- The data cover 12 cases over one day at one-minute resolution. They are insufficient for stable tail estimates, utilization estimates, seasonality, or a throughput law under increased arrival volume. The counterfactuals are deterministic sensitivity calculations, not causal experiments or a capacity simulation.
- Branch synchronization waiting is identifiable descriptively from the logged branch ends and the max rule, but it is not an independent physical activity. It should not be summed with fork-join elapsed time, and it does not reveal whether a waiting branch occupied a resource while waiting.
