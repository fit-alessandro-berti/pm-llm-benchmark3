# 2. Integrated explanation  

No single mechanism explains the entire arc from the first to the 100-th event of a shift.  

1. At the opening of a shift, workers face a carry-over backlog that is both **large** and **complex**. Senior resources deliberately “front-load” those cases. Higher service times (19 min) and slightly elevated errors (3.4 %) reflect **case-mix**, not fatigue or pressure.  

2. During the next 1–2 hours, complexity falls and workers warm up. Speed improves (to 13 min) while quality stabilises. This is classic **learning / warm-up** aided by simplified work.  

3. From the middle of the shift onward, **work-in-process starts to climb**—particularly in Day and Late shifts—because arrival rate exceeds service rate. Workers accelerate (down to 11 min) but begin to cut corners. Error rate rises first for Mid-levels and Juniors, less for Seniors, signalling a **queue-pressure speed–accuracy trade-off** rather than pure fatigue.  

4. Beyond roughly 75 events or after 6–7 h on task, an additional quality drop occurs (errors 7–14 %). Service time keeps shrinking (10–11 min). That pattern—faster yet worse—matches empirical fatigue findings: cognitive control weakens, so workers **over-speed** precisely when they should slow down. Queue pressure intensifies the mistake.  

5. **Circadian effects** modulate the above: Night staff work slower throughout (likely reduced alertness and more exception-type cases) while Early shift clears queue effectively with moderate quality loss. Still, time-of-day is never the sole driver; it interacts with WIP and complexity.  

6. Intermittent **batching** of repetitive low-risk tasks lets some Juniors “bank” quick wins, temporarily lowering service time without harming quality. However, end-of-shift Quality-Check batching does the opposite— compresses review into a short window, swelling late errors.  

7. Finally, **automation** (Bot_Risk) adds a subtle risk: humans trust the bot less when pressure is low and inspect carefully, but under high WIP they accept the bot’s output, leading to more downstream corrections.  

Therefore the joint pattern—early slowness, mid-shift efficiency, late-shift error surge—emerges from the interaction of (a) changing case-mix, (b) learning, (c) rising WIP, (d) fatigue, (e) shift timing, and (f) local workarounds. Any explanation that names only one factor is incomplete.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions  

• Error rates are heavily confounded by case complexity and queue pressure; punishing workers for late-shift errors risks blaming them for system congestion created by management.  
• Selection bias: seniors purposely take the hardest early cases; raw performance comparisons mislead.  
• Measurement error: some “errors” are caught only because quality checks are batched; fewer checks could simply hide errors.  
• Queue pressure is partly managerial—staffing and arrival smoothing—not worker choice.  
• Automation quality: if Bot_Risk misclassifies, downstream human “error” may just correct the bot; do not conflate.  
• Privacy and fairness: dashboards that name individuals can foster surveillance anxiety; aggregate or anonymise when feasible.  
• Interventions like random assignment or penalties can erode tacit coordination and intrinsic motivation.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification  

A process-mining dashboard (updated hourly) should show:

1. Mean service time by activity family and shift (pm4py.get_service_time).  
2. Mean waiting time from arrival to start (pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time).  
3. Current WIP and queue_length_at_start distribution; highlight WIP>70.  
4. Error rate and rework_flag share, overall and by resource group.  
5. Case complexity and priority mix over time.  
6. Batch detector: count of batches >5 consecutive same-activity events; visual link to dotted chart.  
7. Handover matrix showing transfers between resources and shifts; spikes indicate backlog.  
8. Resource load fairness: Gini index of event counts per resource in last 4 h.  
9. Automation correction rate: proportion of Bot_Risk cases later touched by humans.  
10. SLA lead-time percentile bands to ensure quality interventions do not erode timeliness.

Each indicator should support drill-down by shift, resource group, and complexity band, with exportable event subsets via pm4py.filter_* functions for root-cause analysis.
