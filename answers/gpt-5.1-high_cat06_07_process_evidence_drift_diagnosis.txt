## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

**January–March (baseline, stable system)**  
Variant mix (V1 ~45%, V2 ~35–36%, V3 ~20%) and data quality (unknown activity ≤0.3%, missing queue feature 1%) are stable. Label maturity is 100% with median label delays ~32–34 days. Observed breach rates hover around 0.18–0.19 overall, and per-variant breach rates are consistent (V1 ~0.15–0.16, V2 ~0.10–0.11, V3 ~0.30–0.32). Model performance is strong and well-calibrated (AUROC ~0.81–0.82, Brier ~0.13, ECE ~0.02–0.025), giving a high-quality reference period with no evident drift.

**April (schema/pipeline incident, not genuine performance change)**  
On 1 April, the workflow migration renames the diagnosis completion activity and introduces duplicate and `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` events. The feature pipeline, still expecting the old label, builds many prefixes at the wrong point or not at all and fails to compute some derived features. This manifests as: unknown activity share jumping to 17%, missing queue feature to 14%, 18% of cases assigned to “unmapped/other” variants, mean predicted risk jumping to 0.28 despite an observed breach rate of only 0.19, AUROC collapsing to 0.67, Brier worsening to 0.184, and ECE rising to 0.110. Matured per-variant breach rates and DFG performance do not show a step-change commensurate with this predictive degradation. Taken together with the pipeline audit, April looks like a **data-pipeline failure plus schema drift** with only modest underlying process variation, and should not be treated as evidence of concept drift.

**May (partial pipeline repair, residual artifacts)**  
The mapping patch on 6 May largely normalizes the diagnosis label, and April records are mostly backfilled, but not all cached features are recomputed. Unknown activity drops to 2%, missing queue to 3%, variant distribution returns close to baseline (unmapped ~1%), and AUROC/Brier/ECE partially recover (0.78, 0.146, 0.050). Observed breach remains at 0.18, and per-variant breach rates revert to their baseline levels. However, metrics do not fully reach January–March quality, consistent with lingering feature-cache artifacts from April. May thus reflects **pipeline healing** more than new process or concept drift; including it in training or evaluation requires recomputing features for the affected period.

**June (genuine routing/process mix change, not primarily concept drift)**  
On 1 June, a new remote-routing policy increases the share of V2 from ~34% to 52%, while V1 falls to 30% and V3 to 18%. Overall breach rate drops to 0.15, consistent with emphasizing the low-risk V2 path (V2 breach 0.09) without major changes in per-variant breach (V1 ~0.16, V3 ~0.35). AUROC remains high (0.79) and ECE is still acceptable (0.060), indicating that within the new mix the model still ranks cases reasonably well, albeit with mild overprediction (mean risk 0.20 vs 0.15 breach). Process evidence shows no major change in key edge times other than gradual growth in `Order Part -> Part Received` (42 h vs 38 h in May). June is best interpreted as **process drift via routing and covariate drift in the case mix**, not concept drift: performance improves because more cases follow a favorable path, not because the underlying relation between features and breaches changes.

**July (onset of supplier-driven degradation and emerging concept drift in V3)**  
On 10 July, a parts supplier starts missing delivery targets, and the effect is visible by month-end: median `Order Part -> Part Received` time jumps to 65 h (from 42 h in June), and V3’s matured breach rate surges to 0.46 (vs ~0.35 in June). The share of V3 rises to 22%, while V2 remains high (50%). Label maturity remains high (98%), so July metrics are mostly reliable. Model calibration is still acceptable (mean predicted 0.18 vs observed 0.19, ECE 0.042), but AUROC slips to 0.76 and Brier worsens to 0.151, signaling a loss of discrimination, especially where parts delays occur. July is a **transition month** where true process-performance drift in the parts path begins to induce **local concept drift for V3**; however, most labels are mature, so this is real performance degradation, not an artifact.

**August–September (overlapping process, concept, and population shifts)**  
By August, the supplier issue becomes severe: `Order Part -> Part Received` median time doubles again to 130 h and then to 148 h in September. V3 share grows to 35% in August and 42% in September. V3 breach rate peaks at 0.64 in August and remains extremely high at 0.61 in September, while V1 and V2 rates stay near baseline. Overall observed breach jumps to 0.28 (Aug) and 0.31 (Sep), but the model’s mean predicted risk only creeps up to 0.19–0.21, with AUROC dropping further to 0.68 and 0.66 and ECE worsening to 0.120–0.140. In September, Partner Z is onboarded, contributing 24% of cases; Partner Z routes 58% of its cases through V3 (vs 36% for others) and exhibits a higher observed breach rate (0.45 vs 0.27), though labels for Partner Z mature more slowly. Rural travel distance and device age are higher for Partner Z, and these features were not present in the original model. Label maturity declines to 93% in August and 82% in September, and label delays lengthen, partially confounding metrics, but not enough to explain the scale of deterioration. Thus August–September show **compound drift**: severe **process drift** in the parts path, **local concept drift** for V3, **population shift** and **covariate drift** with Partner Z and changed variant mix, and **increasing label delay** that complicates evaluation.

**October (heavily confounded by label backlog and censoring)**  
October continues the operational pattern: V3 share remains high (~41%), and `Order Part -> Part Received` median time worsens to 155 h, so operational performance clearly is not recovering. Partner Z share rises further to 27%. However, the outcome API backlog causes label maturity to collapse to 55%, with median label delay expanding to 52 days and the backlog disproportionately affecting long-resolution (high-risk) cases. Observed breach rate paradoxically drops to 0.16—lower than even the pre-supplier baseline—while mean predicted risk rises to 0.23, AUROC falls slightly to 0.64, and ECE remains poor (0.130). Because the missing labels are concentrated in the riskiest cases, October’s apparent improvement is almost certainly illusory; it should be treated as **label-delay and outcome-pipeline failure**, overlaid on the existing August–September process and concept drift. October must not be interpreted as a simple return to January–March conditions or used naively for retraining.

In summary, April is dominated by a **schema/pipeline incident**; June reflects a **routing and mix change** without clear concept drift; August–September combine **true operational degradation, local concept drift in V3, and population shift**; and October is **dominated by label censoring**, making its apparent metrics incomparable to mature months.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

Below, each proposed test includes expected results under at least two competing hypotheses.

**3.1 Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting**  
- **Test**:  
  - For each case-start month, plot the cumulative fraction of cases with mature breach labels as a function of days since `Case Created`. Compare curves for Jan–Oct, and stratify by variant and partner (especially V3 and Partner Z).  
  - Implement fixed-vintage backtesting: for a given reporting date (e.g., 31 December 2026), evaluate model performance only on cohorts with at least a fixed label-lag (e.g., 90 days), and compare to the original-as-of metrics.  
- **Hypothesis A – October deterioration is real concept/performance drift**:  
  - Label-maturity curves for October will look similar to earlier months; by 90 days, most labels will be present. Fixed-vintage backtests will still show low observed breach and similar miscalibration.  
- **Hypothesis B – October metrics are driven by label delay/censoring**:  
  - October’s maturity curves will be significantly right-shifted relative to earlier months, with a large long tail. At the original cutoff, October will show low maturity, but fixed-vintage backtests (after enough time) will show observed breach and calibration lines more similar to September, disproving the idea of a real improvement.  
- **Use**: Confirms that label delay, not model improvement, explains October’s low observed breach and that September’s metrics are closer to the truth once enough time has passed.

**3.2 Recomputation after activity-label normalization and duplicate cleaning**  
- **Test**:  
  - Rebuild features for April–May with the following fixes: map both `Initial Diagnosis Completed` and `Diagnostic Assessment Completed` to a canonical diagnosis label; remove duplicates (e.g., by (`case_id`, `activity`, `timestamp`)); recompute all derived features (including “number of events before diagnosis”) from the raw events; and ensure duplicate-event removal works consistently across sources. Then recompute predictions and performance metrics for April–May.  
- **Hypothesis A – April’s degradation is mostly pipeline/schema failure**:  
  - After recomputation, unknown activity and missing queue feature rates fall to baseline levels. Variant distribution aligns with Jan–Mar. April–May AUROC, Brier, ECE, and mean predicted risk move much closer to January–March, and April no longer appears as an outlier month.  
- **Hypothesis B – April reflects genuine new behavior or concept drift**:  
  - Even after recomputation, April–May retain substantially worse AUROC or calibration, or a genuinely different variant mix that cannot be explained by mapping errors, and per-variant breach rates show real shifts.  
- **Use**: Determines whether to exclude April–May from training as “bad data” or to retain them as legitimate process-evolution months.

**3.3 Calibration and discrimination stratified by variant, source, and month**  
- **Test**:  
  - For each month, compute AUROC, Brier, and calibration curves separately for V1, V2, and V3, and for Partner Z vs other sources (where available). Examine how these metrics evolve over time within each stratum.  
- **Hypothesis A – Concept drift is localized to V3 and Partner Z**:  
  - V1 and V2 retain near-baseline AUROC and calibration across months (allowing for label delay), while V3 exhibits steadily degrading AUROC and systematic underprediction from July onward. Partner Z shows worse calibration than non-Partner-Z in September–October even after aligning label maturity.  
- **Hypothesis B – Global concept drift or mislabeling**:  
  - All variants and sources show similar degradation patterns, with AUROC and calibration worsening across the board, not just in V3 and Partner Z.  
- **Use**: Justifies whether to focus remedial modeling and process interventions on V3 and Partner Z, rather than retraining a single global model.

**3.4 Comparison of process variants and DFG/performance-DFG edges**  
- **Test**:  
  - For each month, use `pm4py.discover_dfg` and `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` on the diagnosis-anchored prefixes and full cases to examine:  
    - Frequencies of V1, V2, V3 and any new variants;  
    - Edge performance on key transitions, especially `Order Part -> Part Received` and `Initial/Diagnostic Assessment Completed -> Remote Fix`.  
  - Compare these metrics over time and by partner.  
- **Hypothesis A – Supplier-driven process drift explains the V3 surge**:  
  - The `Order Part -> Part Received` edge time increases sharply from July onward, most strongly in V3 and especially for Partner Z cases, while `diagnosis -> Remote Fix` times remain stable. Variant frequencies show a notable increase in V3 share.  
- **Hypothesis B – Data-pipeline artifacts or routing noise**:  
  - Edge times remain stable, but counts or case assignments fluctuate (e.g., sudden rise in "unmapped" paths) without corresponding performance degradation, suggesting logging issues more than genuine process problems.  
- **Use**: Distinguishes true operational bottlenecks in parts logistics from artifacts and clarifies whether V3 risk increases are operational in origin.

**3.5 Tests of whether the outcome relationship conditional on the same path changed**  
- **Test**:  
  - For a fixed process path (e.g., canonical V3 without rework) and a fixed set of pre-diagnosis features, fit simple logistic or calibration models separately for pre-supplier months (Jan–Jun) and post-supplier months (Jul–Sep). Compare intercepts and calibration slopes, or use statistical tests to assess whether P(breach \| X, path=V3) changed.  
- **Hypothesis A – Genuine concept drift in V3**:  
  - Conditional on the same V3 path and similar features, the post-July cohort shows a significantly higher breach probability and different calibration slope (e.g., intercept shift upward), even after controlling for label maturity.  
- **Hypothesis B – Only covariate/variant-mix drift or label delay**:  
  - Once you hold path and features fixed and ensure mature labels, the breach probability remains similar across time slices; differences are explained by more cases entering the V3 region or by label censoring.  
- **Use**: Provides direct evidence for or against local concept drift, determining whether retraining must change the functional form or only the case mix.

**3.6 Population reweighting or transport-style checks for Partner Z**  
- **Test**:  
  - For September–October, construct two analyses:  
    1. Reweight non-Partner-Z cases to match Partner Z’s distribution of observable features (variant mix, geography, device age, initial queue) and compare breach rates and calibration between reweighted non-Partner-Z and Partner Z.  
    2. Train a model on non-Partner-Z data only and evaluate it on Partner Z with and without reweighting, looking at calibration and AUROC.  
- **Hypothesis A – Difference driven mainly by observable population shift**:  
  - After reweighting non-Partner-Z to match Partner Z’s observables, breach rates and calibration curves converge, and the transported model performs similarly on both groups (excluding label delay).  
- **Hypothesis B – Additional unmodeled Partner-Z-specific risk or concept drift**:  
  - Even after reweighting, Partner Z remains higher risk and less well-calibrated, indicating either unobserved covariates or a genuinely different relationship P(breach \| X, partner).  
- **Use**: Informs whether Partner Z needs its own model or enriched features (e.g., distance, device age) and guards against misattributing operational differences to unfair model behavior.

**3.7 Feature missingness and lineage audits**  
- **Test**:  
  - For each key feature (activity labels, queue features, time-to-event features), compute monthly missingness and anomaly scores, stratified by source system and partner. Audit lineage: confirm which raw fields feed which features, when mappings changed, and whether caches were recomputed.  
- **Hypothesis A – April–May anomalies are pipeline-driven**:  
  - Missingness and abnormal distributions spike in April–May and drop sharply after recomputation, with lineage records showing clear schema transitions. No similar spikes occur in later months (except the explicit outcome backlog).  
- **Hypothesis B – Broader ongoing data issues**:  
  - Elevated missingness or inconsistent feature construction persists beyond April–May, perhaps differing by source or partner, suggesting deeper systemic data problems.  
- **Use**: Ensures that any retraining or recalibration rests on reliable features and that we do not calibrate around an undiagnosed pipeline bug.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**4.1 Immediate containment (now)**  
- Treat October’s performance numbers as unreliable. Do **not** retrain or recalibrate using October labels until the outcome API backlog is cleared and label maturity returns to a defined SLO (e.g., ≥95% at the evaluation lag).  
- Freeze automated retraining or hyperparameter tuning pipelines that trigger purely on aggregate metric changes; require human review when label-maturity monitors fire.  
- For high-risk contexts (V3 and Partner Z), apply operational guardrails: stricter SLA monitoring, priority queues, or manual review for borderline predictions, recognizing that the model underestimates their breach risk in August–September.

**4.2 Short-term recalibration (post-backfill, prior to full retraining)**  
- Once April–May features are recomputed and October labels catch up, perform **segment-specific calibration** rather than global recalibration:  
  - Separate reliability curves and calibration functions for V1/V2 vs V3, and for Partner Z vs others.  
  - Use only cohorts with mature labels—e.g., Jan–Jun plus July–September evaluated with fixed-vintage label lags—to fit or adjust calibration (Platt scaling or isotonic regression).  
- Because June’s changes are mainly routing/mix and V1/V2 per-variant performance is stable, recalibration alone may suffice for V1/V2. For V3, however, the magnitude of concept drift (breach up to ~0.6) suggests recalibration is a stop-gap; the model likely lacks features (supplier identity, expected lead time) to represent the new risk.  
- During this phase, avoid updating the underlying model weights solely on post-July data; instead, correct the probability scale and update business dashboards to emphasize segment-specific risk.

**4.3 Feature repair and enrichment**  
- **Repair**:  
  - Recompute April–May features from raw events with canonical diagnosis labels and complete deduplication; re-derive all derived features (including event counts and time-from-case-start to diagnosis).  
  - Verify feature consistency across sources and partners (including Partner Z) via lineage and missingness audits.  
- **Enrich**:  
  - Add features that can capture the new operational realities: expected part supplier, estimated shipping lead time, travel distance, device age, rural/urban flag, and routing policy indicators (e.g., early decision to route to remote vs on-site).  
  - Represent V3-specific structure explicitly (e.g., indicators that a part will be ordered, expected stock availability) to give the model leverage on the new V3 risk regime.

**4.4 Full retraining (once data and labels are reliable)**  
- **Safe training windows (once cleaned)**:  
  - **Good baseline data**: January–March (high data quality, no major drift).  
  - **Post-routing, pre-severe-supplier data**: June provides information about the new remote-routing policy and V2-heavy mix without severe V3 degradation.  
  - **Drifted but mature data**: July–September, after label maturity stabilizes (using a fixed-vintage cutoff) and after verifying that no unrepaired pipeline bugs remain, are essential to capture the supplier-driven V3 concept drift and Partner Z population shift.  
- **Unsafe or conditionally safe windows**:  
  - April is **unsafe** until all features are recomputed and duplicate handling verified; even then, it may be safer to exclude or down-weight if anomalies persist.  
  - Early May may be partly contaminated by incomplete backfills; use only the recomputed version or consider starting from mid-May.  
  - October is **unsafe** for training until the outcome API backlog is cleared and label maturity approaches prior norms.  
- **Retraining strategy**:  
  - Use a **time-aware** training strategy (e.g., including month or policy phase indicators or time-sliced models) so the algorithm can learn both pre- and post-drift regimes.  
  - Consider **segment-specific models**:  
    - One model for V1/V2 (where risk levels and processes are relatively stable and concept drift is minimal).  
    - A specialized model for V3, potentially including supplier- and logistics-related features, to handle severe and evolving concept drift.  
    - Optionally, a Partner-Z-specific calibration or submodel if transport checks show persistent differences after controlling for observable features.  
  - When combining segments in a single model, ensure that variant, partner, and geography are strong explicit features, and that training samples from July–September are adequately represented so V3’s new risk regime is learned.

**4.5 Long-term maintenance**  
- After retraining, implement a schedule where new models are only trained on cohorts whose labels have passed an agreed maturity threshold and whose data-quality monitors are green.  
- Keep a fixed evaluation “vintage” (e.g., quarterly backtests on cohorts at least 90 days old) so future episodes of label delay or pipeline changes can be distinguished from genuine drift.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

**5.1 Components and signals**

- **Predictive-performance monitors**:  
  - Track AUROC, Brier score, precision-recall, and lift over time, stratified by month, variant (V1/V2/V3), and partner (including Partner Z). Use both rolling windows and fixed-vintage cohorts.  
- **Calibration monitors**:  
  - Maintain monthly reliability diagrams and ECE/maximum calibration error per segment. Watch specifically for underprediction in V3 and Partner Z.  
- **Label-maturity and delay monitors**:  
  - For each cohort month and segment, track label maturity (%) at the reporting cutoff and the distribution of label delays. Raise alerts when maturity for any cohort or key segment drops below threshold or delay distributions shift.  
- **Data-quality monitors**:  
  - Track unknown-activity share, missingness per feature, duplication rates, and sudden shifts in value distributions (especially around schema changes). Tie these to lineage metadata to catch unannounced workflow-system migrations.  
- **Process-variant and DFG/performance-DFG monitors**:  
  - Monitor variant frequencies (V1/V2/V3/unmapped) and detect emerging variants. Use performance DFGs to track median and percentile edge times (e.g., `Order Part -> Part Received`, `diagnosis -> Remote Fix`) by month and partner.  
- **Population monitors**:  
  - Track partner mix (including Partner Z), geography (rural vs urban), device age, and other key covariates. Monitor for large shifts in distribution relative to the training window.  
- **Intervention/deployment monitors**:  
  - Record major operational policy changes (e.g., routing policies, supplier changes) and model deployment actions (retraining, recalibration, rollbacks) with timestamps, and overlay them on all above dashboards.

**5.2 Alert dependencies and logic**

- **Label-delay gate**:  
  - If label-maturity or label-delay monitors are in alarm (e.g., October-like backlog), then:  
    - Suppress or downgrade alerts from predictive-performance and calibration monitors that rely on partially matured cohorts.  
    - Hard-block any automated retraining or calibration jobs that include the affected cohorts.  
  - Only after label maturity recovers should predictive-performance deterioration be considered as evidence for concept drift.  
- **Data-quality gate for features**:  
  - If unknown-activity or missing-feature rates breach thresholds (as in April), automatically halt training and flag April-style incidents. Require recomputation and lineage validation before re-enabling modeling jobs.  
- **Process vs model attribution**:  
  - When performance-DFG monitors show significant degradation on process edges (e.g., `Order Part -> Part Received` exploding), raise process/operations alerts first. Treat concurrent predictive-performance drift as possibly downstream of process drift and evaluate whether a **process fix** (e.g., supplier replacement) will reduce risk before committing to major model changes.  
- **Population-shift-aware model alerts**:  
  - When population monitors detect a large shift (e.g., Partner Z onboarding), automatically enable more granular calibration and fairness dashboards and postpone global retraining until segment-level performance is understood.  
- **Escalation rules**:  
  - Only when data-quality and label-maturity monitors are green, but predictive performance and calibration monitors show persistent degradation in a stable process and population context, should the system escalate to “model retraining required” alerts.

**5.3 Example Python sketch for monthly process-evidence extraction**

```python
import pandas as pd
from datetime import datetime
import pm4py


def build_monthly_process_evidence(csv_path: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # 1. Read raw events
    df = pd.read_csv(csv_path, parse_dates=["timestamp"])

    # 2. Normalize diagnosis labels before feature extraction
    df["activity"] = df["activity"].replace({
        "Initial Diagnosis Completed": "Diagnosis Completed",
        "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": "Diagnosis Completed",
    })

    # 3. Basic duplicate handling (schema-specific refinements can be added)
    df = df.drop_duplicates(subset=["case_id", "activity", "timestamp"])

    # 4. Format for PM4Py (keeps an auditable DataFrame representation)
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case_id",
        activity_key="activity",
        timestamp_key="timestamp",
    )

    # 5. Derive cohort keys
    df["event_month"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()
    case_start = df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].transform("min")
    df["case_start_month"] = case_start.dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()

    summaries = []
    for month in sorted(df["event_month"].unique()):
        start = pd.Timestamp(month)
        end = (start + pd.offsets.MonthEnd(1)) + pd.Timedelta(seconds=1)

        # Event-calendar cohort: all events occurring in this calendar month
        df_event = pm4py.filter_time_range(
            df, start_timestamp=start, end_timestamp=end, timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
        )

        # Case-start cohort: cases whose first event occurs in this month
        df_case = df[df["case_start_month"] == start]

        # Discover DFG and performance DFG on event-calendar cohort
        dfg, start_acts, end_acts = pm4py.discover_dfg(df_event)
        perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df_event)

        # Variants (trace patterns) and their counts
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(df_event)

        # Temporal features (e.g., edge durations), extracted in a reproducible way
        temporal_features = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df_event)

        # Example: median duration on Order Part -> Part Received (if present)
        edge_duration = None
        if ("Order Part", "Part Received") in perf_dfg:
            edge_duration = perf_dfg[("Order Part", "Part Received")]

        summaries.append({
            "month": start,
            "cases_started": df_case["case:concept:name"].nunique(),
            "events_in_month": len(df_event),
            "n_variants": len(variants),
            "median_order_part_to_received": edge_duration,
            "n_v1_standard": sum("Dispatch Technician" in v for v in variants.keys()),
            "n_v2_remote": sum("Remote Fix" in v for v in variants.keys()),
            "n_v3_parts": sum("Order Part" in v for v in variants.keys()),
            # Additional auditable statistics can be computed from temporal_features
        })

    return pd.DataFrame(summaries)
```

This sketch keeps raw events and derived artifacts auditable, normalizes the renamed diagnosis activity before feature extraction, demonstrates a basic duplicate-handling approach, constructs both event-calendar and case-start cohorts, and uses PM4Py functions to produce monthly process evidence suitable for drift diagnosis without fitting any predictive model.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

**Risk 1 – Retraining on immature or censored labels (especially October)**  
- *Failure mode*: Interpreting October’s low observed breach rate (0.16) as real improvement and using October data (55% label maturity, backlog biased toward long, high-risk cases) for retraining or model selection. This would push the model toward underestimating risk precisely for the longest, most complex cases, and could suppress the model’s sensitivity to severe V3/Partner Z issues.  
- *Mitigation*: Enforce label-maturity gates for all training and evaluation; do not include cohorts with maturity below a threshold or with flagged backlog anomalies. Rely on fixed-vintage backtests (e.g., cohorts >90 days old) and compare them to as-of metrics before updating models.

**Risk 2 – Calibrating around a pipeline bug (April–May)**  
- *Failure mode*: Treating April’s miscalibration as genuine drift and applying calibration fixes or retraining on features generated from the faulty pipeline (wrong diagnosis activity, incomplete cached features, inconsistent deduplication). This would encode April’s artifacts into the model and might degrade performance in otherwise healthy months.  
- *Mitigation*: Make data-quality and lineage checks hard prerequisites for calibration and retraining. When a schema change or data incident is detected (like April’s migration), freeze modeling updates, recompute features from raw logs with normalized labels and deduplication, and only then revisit calibration. Maintain the ability to exclude or down-weight affected months.

**Risk 3 – Ignoring genuine supplier-driven concept drift in V3**  
- *Failure mode*: Attributing the August–September breach surge solely to routing or population mix and continuing to rely on an unchanged model and process, while parts logistics remain severely degraded (V3 breach ~0.6, `Order Part -> Part Received` >130–150 h). The model would systematically underpredict high-risk V3 cases, causing more breaches and undermining trust.  
- *Mitigation*: Use performance-DFG and variant-stratified calibration to show that V3’s conditional risk has changed. Escalate supplier issues as operational incidents and plan targeted model updates for V3 (including enriched features and possibly a dedicated V3 model). In the interim, apply rule-based overrides or higher manual scrutiny for high-V3-risk predictions.

**Risk 4 – Misattributing Partner Z’s population shift to unfair model behavior (or vice versa)**  
- *Failure mode*: Seeing Partner Z’s higher observed breach as proof that the model is unfair, without adjusting for variant mix, geography, device age, and label maturity; or conversely, dismissing Partner Z’s issues as purely operational and ignoring poor calibration on its cases. Either mistake could lead to inappropriate business responses (e.g., blocking Partner Z, overcompensating with manual overrides, or ignoring a real fairness/performance gap).  
- *Mitigation*: Perform stratified performance and calibration analyses that condition on observable factors and adjust for label delay. Use reweighting/transport tests to separate pure population shift from Partner-Z-specific concept drift. If substantial residual gaps remain, consider partner-specific calibration or modeling; otherwise, address operational and SLA differences directly.

**Risk 5 – Overreacting to June’s routing change as model failure**  
- *Failure mode*: Interpreting June’s drop in overall breach rate and mild calibration changes as a sign of model degradation and retraining prematurely, when the main change is the routing policy (more V2 remote cases). A hasty retrain could overweight short-lived patterns or conflate policy effects with model effects.  
- *Mitigation*: Always analyze performance stratified by variant and policy phase. Recognize that routing and other interventions can legitimately change baseline rates without implying concept drift. Prefer recalibration or policy-aware evaluation over immediate retraining when per-variant performance remains stable.

**Risk 6 – Treating the entire period as one homogeneous drift event**  
- *Failure mode*: Applying a single label such as “concept drift” to the whole January–October period and responding with a monolithic solution (e.g., one global retrain) that ignores April’s pipeline incident, June’s routing change, August–September’s supplier and Partner Z issues, and October’s label backlog. This risks both under- and over-correcting in different months and segments.  
- *Mitigation*: Maintain a month-by-month, segment-aware narrative combining predictive, process, population, and label-maturity evidence. Use the monitoring architecture and diagnostic tests above to decompose drift into pipeline, process, population, and concept components, and align remediation (pipeline repair, process change, recalibration, or retraining) to each specific cause.
