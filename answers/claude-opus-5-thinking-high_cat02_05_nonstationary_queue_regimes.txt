# 1. Data audit and estimands

## 1.1 What is actually supplied

Two objects, of very different evidential weight:

- **28 daily aggregate rows** (2026-04-06 to 2026-05-03), each mixing *flow counts* (`arrivals`, `completed`), *time-average state* (`mean_wip`), *distributional summaries of one duration* (`median_service_min`, `p90_service_min`), *capacity* (`staff_fte`), and *composition* (`complex_pct`, `peak_08_10_pct`).
- **4 hand-picked representative hourly vectors**, one per operating condition. These are *illustrations*, not a sample: they were selected because they exemplify something, so nothing distributional can be estimated from them.

There is no case-level data: no case identifiers, no arrival/start/complete timestamps, no per-case waiting times, no 07:00 backlog series. Everything below is therefore an inference about a queue from its *moments*, not a measurement of the queue.

## 1.2 Definitions and units (fixed for the whole answer)

| Symbol | Definition used here | Unit | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening window `T` | 07:00–19:00, 12.0 h/day, service only inside it | h | given |
| `A(t)` | arrivals inside opening hours | cases/day | `arrivals` |
| `D(t)` | completions inside opening hours, **regardless of arrival day** | cases/day | `completed` |
| `λ` | `arrivals / 12` — arrival intensity per **opening** hour | cases/opening-hour | derived |
| `X` (throughput) | `completed / 12` per opening hour | cases/opening-hour | derived |
| `L` | mean number in system, from 15-min snapshots inside opening hours (≈48 snapshots/day) | cases | `mean_wip` |
| `S` | *active handling* time per case, waiting excluded | min | `median_service_min`, `p90_service_min` |
| `E[S]` | **estimated** mean handling time, not observed | min | fitted from median+p90 (§1.4) |
| `C` | capacity offered = `staff_fte × 12 × 60` | FTE-minutes/day | derived |
| `ρ` | offered load ratio = `arrivals × E[S] / C` | dimensionless | derived |
| `W` | mean time in system, **measured in opening hours** | opening-h | only via `L/λ` or `L/X`, never observed |
| `net` | `arrivals − completed`; `cum_net` its running sum | cases | derived |

**Critical unit convention.** Because both `L` and `λ` are defined only inside opening hours, any `W = L/λ` is expressed in *business hours in system*. Overnight residence is invisible to both numerator and denominator. For a case that carries over one night, the calendar flow time exceeds the computed `W` by 12 h. Every flow time below must be read as "business hours", and any SLA stated in calendar hours cannot be derived from these aggregates.

## 1.3 The estimands we can and cannot target

Estimable at defensible precision: regime-level arrival intensity, throughput, net flow, WIP level, capacity utilisation, and the *shape* change of the handling-time distribution.

**Not** estimable: mean flow time `W` (only imputable, and only where Little's Law is licensed), waiting time, abandonment, per-case-type service times, the 07:00 backlog, the within-day arrival distribution on 24 of 28 days, and any variance component of daily counts that is not confounded with regime drift.

## 1.4 Handling-time mean is an estimate, not a datum

Only the median and p90 of `S` are given. The mean is required for load calculations and is not the median. I bracket it with two two-parameter fits to the same (median, p90) pair:

- **Lognormal** (heavier tail): `σ = ln(p90/median)/1.2816`, `E[S] = median·exp(σ²/2)` → upper end.
- **Weibull** (lighter tail): `k = ln(ln10/ln2)/ln(p90/median)` → lower end.

Baseline weekdays: `E[S] ∈ [26.9, 28.2]` min against a median of 25. Mix-shift weekdays: `E[S] ∈ [41.6, 43.9]` min against a median of 35. Note that **the mean inflates by ~57 % while the median inflates by 40 %** — tail growth, not just level shift. All load figures below are reported as this bracket, never as a point.

## 1.5 Threats to naive inference (at least five; I list ten, ordered by severity)

1. **The system is open at the day boundary.** Cases carry over, no service occurs overnight, and the 07:00 backlog is unknown. A single day is therefore *not* a closed window; `arrivals` and `completed` describe two partially disjoint populations, so any per-day balance argument carries an unmeasured boundary term.
2. **`completed` is contaminated as a throughput proxy.** On recovery days it is inflated by backlog drawn from earlier days (2026-04-16: 129 completions against 112 arrivals). Reading it as "demand served today" over-states capability exactly when the system is *behind*.
3. **The WIP account and the flow account do not reconcile.** Cumulative `arrivals − completed` reaches **+128 cases by 2026-04-28**, yet `mean_wip` peaks at **52**. A 128-case net inflow cannot be stored in a system whose observed occupancy rose from ~20 to ~50. At least one of these holds: cases leave without a `completed` record (abandonment, transfer, cancellation); the 15-min snapshot excludes suspended/parked/back-office cases; or the two counters cover different scopes. **Until this is resolved, no queueing-law claim about levels is safe** — this is the single largest defect in the dataset.
4. **Median service ≠ mean service ≠ flow time.** Three distinct quantities. `median_service_min` excludes waiting by construction, so it can be flat while flow time explodes. Substituting it for `W` would understate baseline flow time by roughly a factor of 6 (25 min vs ~2.4 h).
5. **Non-stationarity at two nested scales.** Within the day the rate is strongly time-varying (§5); across days there are at least three level shifts. A pooled `λ = 7.50/h` describes no hour of no day: weekday hours run 8.3–9.6/h, weekend 3.3–3.9/h, and the 04-21 peak hour reaches 34/h.
6. **Serial dependence collapses the effective sample size.** `mean_wip` has lag-1 autocorrelation **+0.60**; `complex_pct` **+0.88**. Twenty-eight rows are perhaps five or six effectively independent observations. Any confidence interval computed as if `n = 28` is wrong by roughly a factor of two.
7. **Definitional drift in the composition columns.** `complex_pct` jumps 23 → 41 between 04-19 and 04-20 — an overnight *step*, which is more typical of a coding/classification change than of organic demand drift, though a genuine intake change (new referral source, campaign, upstream policy) is equally consistent. Both may be true simultaneously, and the data cannot separate them.
8. **`peak_08_10_pct` is inconsistent with the hourly vectors.** The outage zeros on 04-15 sit in bins 5–7 of the vectors, i.e. 11:00–14:00, which *confirms* that the vectors are labelled 07-08 first, exactly as stated. Given that labelling, the reported `peak_08_10_pct` reproduces the **09:00–11:00** share, not 08:00–10:00, on 04-07 (31.7 vs 31), 04-15 (33.3 vs 32) and 04-25 (21.6 vs 22). On 04-21 it matches **no** adjacent pair (best gap 10.5 pp). So the column is at best mislabelled by one hour, and on the bursty day it is not reproducible at all. Treat it as an ordinal "morning concentration" indicator, never as a literal window share.
9. **`staff_fte` is a mean of concurrent availability, not a schedule, and not pure service capacity.** Breaks, meetings, admin, and non-queue work are inside the FTE-hour but outside `S`. Effective capacity is plausibly 0.80–0.90 of nominal, which shifts every `ρ` upward by 11–25 %. All `ρ` values below are therefore **lower bounds on true pressure**.
10. **The intervention has no timestamp.** "Began during 2026-04-29" means that day is a *mixture* of pre- and post-intervention operation and must not anchor a change point. Also, four representative days were *chosen*, not sampled — they are the strongest evidence about intraday shape and the weakest possible basis for a stochastic model.

# 2. Primary regime segmentation

**Segmentation principle.** Cut where an *exogenous, independently documented* driver changes: the calendar (staffed day type), the outage, the case-mix step, and the staffing intervention. This keeps regime boundaries interpretable and auditable, at the cost of forcing calendar-contiguous blocks onto a system whose real state variable (backlog) evolves continuously.

Weekend days are separated inside every era, because staffing (2.5–3.0 vs 5.5–7.0 FTE) and volume (36–53 vs 92–121) differ by a factor of 2–3; merging them would be the largest single pooling error available.

## 2.1 Explicit outage and recovery treatment

The outage and its recovery are handled as a **matched pair that must be analysed jointly and excluded from all steady-state estimation**:

- **Do not** treat 04-16 (129 completions, 1.49 completions/FTE-hour — the highest in the dataset) as evidence of achievable throughput. It is a *transient* fed by a 47-case queue that had already accumulated, run at 7.0 FTE with near-100 % server occupancy. Extrapolating it would justify a staffing level that fails as soon as the queue is empty and servers idle waiting for arrivals.
- **Do not** treat 04-15's service times as a service-time observation. With 3 of 12 hours dead, the cases that completed are a non-random subset, and `median 31 / p90 68` is a mixture of genuine handling and disruption artefacts.
- **Accounting.** The shock injected +47 cases. Recovery returned −30 over two days; the weekend returned a further ~0. By 04-19 `cum_net` stood at **+22 vs +5 pre-outage**, i.e. roughly **17 cases of the shock were never recovered** before the next regime began. The mechanical capacity loss was 3 h × 6 FTE = 18 of 72 FTE-hours (25 %), which at baseline would cost ~25 completions; the observed shortfall was ~42. The extra ~17 is ramp-down/ramp-up loss, and it is almost exactly the residue that was never cleared.
- Consequently, **regime D1 does not start from an empty system.** Some of D1's WIP and pressure is inherited. That is a genuine confound between "mix shift" and "unrecovered backlog", addressed in §3.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## 3.1 Alternative I (serious): load-state regimes, calendar-agnostic

Classify each day by *where it sits relative to its own capacity*, not by the date. Rules, applied per day: outage present → **S0**; `ρ_low > 1` → **S3 saturated**; `net < −3` → **S1 draining**; `ρ_high < 0.85` → **S2 balanced**; otherwise **S2b marginal**.

| State | Days | Membership |
|---|---|---|
| S0 capacity shock | 1 | 04-15 |
| S1 draining | 5 | 04-16, 04-17, **04-29, 04-30, 05-01** |
| S2 balanced | 10 | 04-06 … 04-14 (all), **04-19** |
| S2b marginal | 4 | 04-18, **04-20**, 04-26, 05-03 |
| S3 saturated (ρ>1) | 8 | 04-21…04-25, 04-27, 04-28, **05-02** |

**Operational story.** The service centre has three operating *states*, not four calendar *eras*: a stable state where servers are ~65 % busy and queue length is set by variability; a saturated state where servers are ~100 % busy and queue length is set by the integral of the imbalance (i.e. it grows without bound and *time*, not variability, becomes the driver); and a drain state that only exists because someone deliberately created headroom. Under this reading, "weekend" and "weekday" are not regimes at all — they are two different scalings of the *same* load ratio, which is why 04-25/04-26 (weekend) land in the same bucket as 04-22 (weekday).

**Strengths versus the primary segmentation.**
- It groups 04-16/04-17 with 04-29/05-01 as one phenomenon — *engineered over-capacity draining a queue* — which is exactly what a staffing model needs and what the calendar view splits apart.
- It exposes that the *weekend* went unstable in the mix era (ρ 1.10–1.16). The calendar view puts weekends in their own low-volume box and encourages the false conclusion "weekends are fine".
- It is defined by a monotone, continuous statistic, so it degrades gracefully: a day near the boundary is *reported* as near the boundary (S2b) rather than forced into a block.

**Weaknesses.**
- It is **derived from the very quantities being explained**. `ρ` is built from `arrivals`, `staff_fte` and an *estimated* `E[S]`; classifying by `ρ` and then reporting `ρ` per class is partly circular, and the S2b/S3 boundary moves if the Weibull rather than lognormal fit is used (05-02 and 04-20 are the fragile cases).
- It **discards the causal narrative**. It cannot answer "did the mix change?" because it never looks at `complex_pct`.
- It **breaks temporal contiguity**, so it cannot be used for change-point inference or for communicating "what happened on which date" to operations staff.

## 3.2 Alternative II (sketch): two-factor design instead of segments

Model each day as `day_type (weekday/weekend) × era (pre-mix / post-mix / intervention)`, with 04-15…04-17 flagged as **excluded outliers** rather than a regime. This is a 2×3 factorial with 3–7 cells populated, and it makes the mix effect and the day-type effect *separately* estimable, including their interaction (does the complex-case burden hurt weekends more, given only 2.5 FTE? The ρ figures say yes). Its weakness is that it assumes era effects are additive on some scale and, with 3–7 days per cell and strong autocorrelation, the interaction term is not statistically supportable — it is a *framing* device, not a fitted model.

## 3.3 Which to prefer

For **diagnosis and communication**, the primary calendar segmentation, because its boundaries are exogenous and falsifiable. For **staffing decisions**, Alternative I, because capacity policy should key on the load ratio, not on the date. They disagree in one operationally important place — the weekend of 04-25/04-26 — and that disagreement is itself the finding.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Pooled over all 28 days: median arrivals 104 (range 36–121), median completions 99, median `mean_wip` 23, `ρ` 0.87–0.91, implied `L/λ` 3.98 h. Each of these is a number that describes no actual operating condition. Four concrete failures:

## 4.1 Weekday/weekend composition

Weekends are **8/28 = 29 % of rows but only 14 % of arrivals** and about 12 % of capacity. Because the table is *daily*, every unweighted daily average silently weights a 40-case Sunday equally with a 121-case Monday.

| Statistic | Pooled | Weekday | Weekend | Distortion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arrivals (median) | 104 | 108 | 44 | Pooled sits in a **gap** between two modes; the pooled distribution is bimodal, so "average day" is not a day |
| λ (per opening hour) | 7.50 | ~8.9 | ~3.6 | Any single-server-equivalent sizing from 7.50 over-staffs weekends and under-staffs weekdays simultaneously |
| `mean_wip` (median) | 23 | 37 | 19 | — |

**Simpson-style reversal (staffing).** `corr(staff_fte, mean_wip)` is **+0.65 pooled** — which reads as "adding staff raises WIP". Within weekdays it is +0.29, within weekends +0.46, and within the weekday days most relevant to the intervention (04-20 → 05-01) it flips to **−0.43**. The pooled positive is pure composition: weekends have both low staffing and low WIP. A naive pooled regression of WIP on FTE would recommend *cutting* staff.

## 4.2 Complex-case mix — how much of the service inflation is mix?

`complex_pct` moves 21 % (A1) → 45 % (D1) while estimated `E[S]` moves 28.2 → 43.9 min. Direct standardisation, solving for a within-type mean given an assumed complex:simple ratio `r`:

| `r` = E[S\|complex]/E[S\|simple] | Implied E[S\|simple] | Implied E[S\|complex] | Predicted D1 mean | Share of observed rise explained by mix alone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 23.4 | 46.7 | 33.8 | **35 %** |
| 2.5 | 21.5 | 53.7 | 35.9 | **49 %** |
| 3.0 | 19.9 | 59.8 | 37.7 | **60 %** |
| 4.0 | 17.4 | 69.4 | 40.6 | 79 % |
| 5.0 | 15.4 | 76.9 | 42.8 | 93 % |

Reading: for any *plausible* complexity contrast (2×–3×), **mix explains roughly a third to two thirds of the handling-time increase; the remainder is not mix.** Candidate residual causes: congestion effects on handling itself (interruptions, multitasking, context switching under a long queue), re-work on aged cases, or a coding change that reclassified previously-simple work. Pooling the two eras into one "service time got worse by 40 %" statement destroys this decomposition entirely — and it matters, because the mix component is fixed by demand while the congestion component is *recoverable by adding capacity* (and indeed E[S] fell from 43.9 to ~39.0 in E1 with no change in `complex_pct`, which is itself evidence for a real congestion component of roughly 10 %).

## 4.3 The staffing intervention and the outage

| Metric | A1 baseline | C recovery | D1 mix overload | E1 intervention | Pooled |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Completions/FTE-hour (median) | 1.403 | 1.486 | 1.431 | **1.487** | 1.424 |
| Net flow (cases) | +4 / 7 d | −30 / 2 d | **+94 / 7 d** | −18 / 3 d | +110 / 28 d |
| ρ (bracket) | 0.63–0.66 | 0.76–0.80 | **1.11–1.17** | 0.89–0.94 | 0.87–0.91 |

The productivity metric is **nearly flat across regimes (1.32–1.49) and ranks the worst operational states highest**: recovery (1.486) and intervention (1.487) top the table precisely because completions during those windows are drawn from a queue rather than from fresh arrivals — a server never waits for work. Pooled, the centre looks like a stable shop doing ~1.42 completions per FTE-hour throughout April. Segmented, it spent 8 days with `ρ > 1`, accumulated **+128 cases**, and has cleared only 18 of them.

Two further pooled artefacts worth naming:

- **Pooled ρ = 0.87–0.91 < 1** is the most dangerous number in the dataset. It says "we have capacity". It is an average over 10 days at ρ ≈ 0.65 and 8 days at ρ > 1. Queues do not average: a system that is 30 % over capacity for a week and 35 % under for a week does **not** behave like a system at 0.9 — the overload period contributes an unbounded, integrating backlog that the underload period drains only linearly.
- **Pooled service time (median 32.5, p90 72)** is a mixture of two well-separated distributions (24–26/44–50 and 34–36/78–88). Its p90/median ratio, 2.2, is larger than either component's (1.8 and 2.4 respectively for A1 and D1 — note D1's is genuinely higher, but the pooled figure exaggerates baseline dispersion by nearly 25 %). Fitting a single service-time distribution to the pooled summary would over-state variability in the good regime and under-state the level in the bad one.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## 5.1 Intraday dispersion from the four representative days

| Day | Note | Arrivals | Fano (raw hourly) | CV | max/mean | Top-2-hour share | **Profile-detrended dispersion** | Completions Fano | Completions max/h |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 04-07 | ordinary weekday | 101 | 3.23 | 0.62 | 2.38 | 35.6 % | 0.00 (reference) | 1.70 | 15 |
| 04-15 | outage | 105 | 4.14 | 0.69 | 2.51 | 37.1 % | **0.21** | 2.25 | 10 |
| 04-21 | bursty, post-mix | 115 | **10.12** | **1.03** | **3.55** | **50.4 %** | **5.36** | 0.98 | 13 |
| 04-25 | weekend | 51 | 1.29 | 0.55 | 1.88 | 29.4 % | **0.43** | 0.78 | 6 |

**The raw Fano factor is the wrong statistic and must not be read as overdispersion.** For a non-homogeneous Poisson process, the index of dispersion of hourly counts is inflated purely by the deterministic time-of-day rate profile: even a perfectly Poisson arrival stream with a morning peak will show Fano ≫ 1. Every one of these days would show Fano > 1 for that reason alone.

The right comparison removes the shape first. I use 04-07 as the reference diurnal profile, rescale it to each day's total, and compute a χ²-like index `Σ(x−e)²/e / (H−1)`. The result is decisive and is the key intraday finding:

- **04-15 (0.21) and 04-25 (0.43) are shape-compatible with the ordinary weekday.** The outage day's *arrivals* were entirely normal — the outage is a service-side event, visible only in the completion vector (hard zeros at 11-12, 12-13, 13-14, exactly matching the stated note, which also validates the bin labelling used throughout §1.5-8).
- **04-21 (5.36) is not a scaled version of the weekday profile at all.** Its excess is *structural*: a 34-case spike at 10-11 and a second 24-case spike at 13-14, i.e. a **bimodal, appointment- or batch-like pattern**, against 2–5 cases in surrounding hours. This is the signature of scheduled/batched intake (a referral drop, a batch upload, a slot-based booking system), not of a heavier Poisson rate.
- 04-21's single peak hour delivers 34 arrivals against a sustainable service rate of `6 FTE × 60 / 43 min ≈ 8.4` completions/h. **A 34-case hour deposits ~26 cases of pure queue that no amount of within-day recovery removes.** Queue formation here is deterministic, not stochastic — which means it is also *predictable and preventable*, unlike variability-driven queueing.

**Completions are far smoother than arrivals** (04-21: completions CV 0.34 vs arrivals 1.03). The server acts as a low-pass filter with a hard ceiling. Note one internal tension: 04-21's *daily* completions, 101/12 h = 8.4/h, match the capacity-implied 8.4/h almost exactly — a strong confirmation of the `E[S] ≈ 43` estimate — yet the *hourly* peak is 13/h, which exceeds it by 55 %. Either completions are registered in batches (clerical closure at the end of a session), or the peak hours cleared a selected set of short/simple cases while complex ones were deferred. Both would matter operationally; the aggregates cannot distinguish them.

## 5.2 Daily-level serial dependence

| Series | lag-1 autocorrelation | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| `mean_wip`, all 28 days | **+0.60** | Backlog is a state variable: today's queue is yesterday's queue plus a small increment. This is *mechanical*, not statistical, dependence |
| `complex_pct`, weekdays | **+0.88** | Almost entirely a single level shift, not smooth persistence |
| `arrivals`, weekdays only | +0.51 | Mostly regime drift, not day-to-day memory… |
| `arrivals`, weekdays, after removing regime means | **−0.18** | …confirmed: once regime means are removed, the residual dependence is weak and slightly negative. **Arrival counts are close to independent within a regime; the apparent memory is the trend** |
| `net_flow`, all days | +0.02 | The *daily imbalance* is near-white; its **cumulative sum** is what persists — a random-walk/integrator structure |

The pattern "arrivals near-independent within regime, WIP strongly persistent, net flow white but integrating" is exactly the signature of a queue driven by a stepwise change in the load ratio. It also means daily rows are **not** independent for any purpose involving WIP or backlog, and standard errors computed from 28 rows over-state precision by roughly √(1+2·0.6/(1−0.6)) ≈ 2×.

## 5.3 Change points

A transparent binary-segmentation scan on weekday rows (04-15 excluded), standardising `complex_pct`, `peak_08_10_pct`, `median_service_min`, `p90_service_min`, `net_flow`, and choosing the split maximising within-segment SSE reduction:

| Candidate split (first day of second segment) | SSE reduction | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| **2026-04-20 (Mon)** | 70.4 | **74.1 %** |
| 2026-04-16 (Thu) | 59.5 | 62.6 % |
| 2026-04-17 (Fri) | 59.4 | 62.6 % |
| 2026-04-21 (Tue) | 58.7 | 61.8 % |
| Level-2, right segment: 2026-04-29 (Wed) | 10.3 | — |
| Level-2, left segment: 2026-04-14 (Tue) | 6.2 | — |

Honest reading:

- The **04-20 boundary is real and multi-signal**: three nominally independent columns step on the same Monday. A single-column drift would be far less convincing.
- But the runners-up (04-16, 04-17, 04-21) score 62 %, only modestly below 74 %. **With 17 weekday points and five correlated features, the scan cannot cleanly separate "the mix changed on 04-20" from "the system never fully recovered from the 04-15 outage."** The competing explanations are two weekday points apart. The reason I still prefer 04-20 is *not* the scan — it is that `complex_pct` and the morning-concentration indicator both step discretely on 04-20 and have no mechanical connection to an IT outage five days earlier, while `cum_net` had already fallen back to +22 by 04-19.
- The 04-29 secondary split is weak (10.3) and corresponds to the intervention. It should be treated as **confirmatory of a known event, not as independent discovery** — and it cannot be located to within better than ±1 day anyway, since the implementation time was never logged.
- No penalty term, no significance test, no multiple-comparison correction has been applied. This scan can *locate* a boundary; it cannot demonstrate that one exists.

## 5.4 Why four representative days cannot establish a stochastic model

They are chosen, not sampled, so their dispersion statistics carry selection bias in an unknown direction — 04-21 was explicitly labelled "bursty", so its Fano of 10.12 is a statement about *why it was picked*, not an estimate of the mix-era distribution. There is one day per condition, so within-regime intraday variability is unidentified: I cannot tell whether every mix-era day is bimodal or whether 04-21 was the one day a batch landed. Twelve hourly bins give ~11 degrees of freedom for a shape with at least two peaks. And hourly bucketing destroys the information a point process needs — inter-arrival times, clustering *within* the hour, and the batch-size distribution that the 34-case hour hints at. **What is supportable: "intraday arrivals are strongly time-varying, and at least one mix-era day exhibits batch-like bimodality that a single morning-peak KPI does not capture."** Anything more requires the raw event stream.

## 6.1 Three rules this matrix encodes

1. **Never substitute `median_service_min` for `W`.** Baseline median service is 25 min; the Little's-Law flow time is ~2.4 opening-hours. The ratio is ~5.8 — i.e. **~83 % of a baseline case's time in system is waiting**, and that share rises to ~86 % in D1 (E[S] ≈ 43 min against W ≈ 5 h). Confusing the two would hide the entire problem.
2. **Proxy divergence is the diagnostic.** `|L/λ − L/X| / (L/λ)` is 0.4 % (A1), 12 % (D1), 81 % (outage day). Publishing a single flow time without this spread asserts a stability that the data denies.
3. **`W` is in business hours.** For D1, W ≈ 5 opening-hours already exceeds one third of a service day; for a case arriving after ~14:00 the *calendar* flow time necessarily includes an overnight, so a calendar-hour SLA cannot be evaluated from these aggregates at all.

# 7. Operational implications

## 7.1 Staffing

- **Weekday capacity is the binding constraint and it is quantifiable.** In D1, offered load is 106–121 cases × 41.6–43.9 min ≈ **4 400–5 300 FTE-minutes/day against 4 320 available at 6.0 FTE.** Restoring ρ ≈ 0.85 at current demand and mix requires roughly `115 × 43 / (0.85 × 720) ≈ **8.1 FTE**, i.e. about +2.1 over the pre-intervention 6.0 and **+1.1 over the current 7.0**. The intervention moved the system from ρ ≈ 1.14 to ρ ≈ 0.91 — from divergent to *barely* convergent. Because effective capacity is below nominal FTE (breaks, admin), the true requirement is higher still; treat 8.1 as a floor.
- **The weekend needs attention that volume-based reporting will never surface.** At 2.5 FTE with 45 % complex cases, ρ reached 1.10–1.16 on 04-25/04-26. The move to 3.0 FTE brought it to 0.91–0.97 — balanced, but contributing **zero drain**. Weekends are the cheapest available drain capacity precisely because arrival pressure is low; ~4.0 weekend FTE would convert them from neutral to ~+15 cases/week of recovery.

## 7.2 Backlog recovery

- **Size the problem explicitly.** Residual `cum_net` is ~110 cases (with the §1.5 caveat that this figure is not reconciled with `mean_wip` and may over-state true outstanding work). At the observed E1 drain of ~6 cases/weekday and ~0 at weekends, clearance takes **~18 weekdays ≈ 3.5–4 weeks** — and only if demand and mix do not worsen.
- **To clear in two weeks** requires ~11 extra completions/weekday ≈ 11 × 40 min ≈ 440 FTE-min ≈ **+0.6 FTE/day on top of the current 7.0** (≈ 7.6 FTE), sustained. This is arithmetic, not a forecast: it assumes E[S] stays at ~40 min, which itself depends on congestion easing.
- **Do not declare victory on WIP.** `mean_wip` fell 50 → 32 in three days, which will look like success on a dashboard. `cum_net` fell only 128 → 110 over the same period. WIP recovered ~5× faster than the backlog account — another reason the two counters must first be reconciled.
- **Age the queue before draining it.** With ρ near 1 for two weeks, some cases are now old. Recovery that works FIFO on total volume will produce a tail of very aged cases; recovery that works shortest-job-first will drain the *count* fastest and the *complex* cases never. Choose deliberately and measure both.

## 7.3 Peak smoothing

- The 04-21 profile shows two spikes (34 at 10-11, 24 at 13-14) against a ~8.4/h service ceiling. **Roughly 26 + 16 ≈ 42 cases of queue are created deterministically on such a day** — more than a third of daily volume, from two hours. Smoothing those two hours is worth more than any within-shift efficiency initiative.
- Concretely: move batch/scheduled intake off the 10-11 and 13-14 slots; stagger break rotations so that server availability *peaks* where arrivals peak rather than dipping at midday; and triage complex cases into a separate, appointment-based stream so they do not block simple work during spikes.
- **Fix the peak KPI first.** `peak_08_10_pct` reproduces the 09:00–11:00 share on three of four days and matches nothing on the bursty day (§1.5-8). It is a *unimodal* metric applied to a *bimodal* pattern. Replace it with the top-2-hour concentration share and a detrended dispersion index, both of which separate 04-21 (50.4 %, 5.36) from an ordinary day (35.6 %, ~0) unambiguously.

## 7.4 Data collection (highest long-run value)

1. **Record the 07:00 backlog and the closing WIP daily.** This alone converts every daily row from an open, unbalanced window into a closable one and makes Little's Law testable rather than assumed.
2. **Reconcile the three counters.** Explain the 128-vs-52 gap: instrument abandonment, transfer-out, cancellation, and whatever states the 15-min WIP snapshot excludes. Nothing downstream is trustworthy until this is closed.
3. **Emit case-level events with lifecycle timestamps** (arrival, start, complete, plus suspend/resume). Start+complete separates waiting from handling; without both, service time is not identifiable and `W` can only ever be imputed.
4. **Version and date every classification change.** Record whether the 04-20 `complex_pct` step was a taxonomy change, a real intake change, or both. This single fact decides whether the extra ~15 min/case is permanent demand or a measurement artefact.
5. **Log intervention start times to the minute**, and log outage start/stop. "During 2026-04-29" cost us the ability to use that day as a clean boundary.
6. **Retain full hourly (ideally per-event) vectors for all days**, not four representatives. The intraday story is where the actionable levers are, and it is currently 14 % observed.

## 7.5 Policies that look good pooled and are dangerous segmented

**Primary example — "level-load staffing to the pooled average."** Pooled ρ is 0.87–0.91 and pooled staffing is ~6.0 FTE (mean ~5.4 across all days). A pooled analysis therefore concludes: *capacity is adequate on average; flatten staffing to reduce cost and roster complexity.* Segmented, this is the worst available decision. Applying a uniform 5.4 FTE would put weekday ρ at 115 × 43 / (5.4 × 720) ≈ **1.27** — the system would diverge at ~20 cases/weekday, roughly 100/week, indefinitely. Averaging a load ratio across regimes is invalid because the overloaded regime *integrates* its excess into a backlog while the underloaded regime only drains linearly; the two do not cancel. The pooled 0.87 is real arithmetic and a false conclusion.

Three further traps:

- **"WIP is back to normal, withdraw the extra FTE."** By 05-01, `mean_wip` = 32 versus a pooled median of 23 and a mix-era 44 — it looks nearly recovered. But demand (109–120/day) and mix (43–45 %) are unchanged, so removing the +1.0 FTE returns ρ to ~1.14 immediately, *and* ~110 cases of backlog remain. The intervention is currently the only thing making the system stable; it is not a recovery bonus, it is the new baseline requirement.
- **"Managing to completions per FTE-hour."** This metric peaks (1.487) during recovery and drain, when servers never wait for work, and it is flat (1.32–1.49) across regimes that differ by a factor of two in flow time. As a target it rewards being behind and invites cherry-picking short cases — which the 04-21 completion vector (13/h against an 8.4/h capacity-implied ceiling) suggests may already be happening.
- **"Shift weekend staff to weekdays, since weekend WIP is low."** Pooled, weekends look like slack. Segmented, weekends ran ρ 1.10–1.16 in the mix era and are the only low-arrival window in which backlog can be drained without competing with fresh demand.

    theta = med / (math.log(2) ** (1.0 / k))
    return theta * math.gamma(1.0 + 1.0 / k), k

# 6. Burstiness / dispersion / serial dependence

def fano(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    return x.var(ddof=1) / x.mean()

def cv(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    return x.std(ddof=1) / x.mean()

def profile_detrended_dispersion(x, profile):
    """Chi-square-like index AFTER removing a common diurnal shape.
    Raw Fano on hourly counts is inflated by the deterministic time-of-day rate,
    so a non-homogeneous Poisson stream shows Fano >> 1 with no overdispersion."""
    x = np.asarray(x, float)
    e = np.clip(np.asarray(profile, float) / np.sum(profile) * x.sum(), 1e-9, None)
    return float(np.sum((x - e) ** 2 / e) / (len(x) - 1))

profile = np.array(hourly.loc[hourly.date == "2026-04-07", "arr"].iloc[0], float)

rows = []
for _, r in hourly.iterrows():
    a = np.array(r["arr"], float); c = np.array(r["comp"], float)
    rows.append({
        "day": r["date"], "note": r["note"],
        "arr_total": int(a.sum()), "comp_total": int(c.sum()),
        "arr_fano": round(fano(a), 2), "arr_cv": round(cv(a), 2),
        "arr_max/mean": round(a.max()/a.mean(), 2),
        "top2_hr_share": round(np.sort(a)[-2:].sum()/a.sum(), 3),
        "detrended_disp": round(profile_detrended_dispersion(a, profile), 2),
        "comp_fano": round(fano(c), 2), "comp_cv": round(cv(c), 2), "comp_max": int(c.max()),
    })

def sse(a):
    return float(((a - a.mean(axis=0)) ** 2).sum()) if len(a) else 0.0

def scan_splits(block, offset=0, min_seg=3):
    tot = sse(block)
    out = [(i + offset, tot - (sse(block[:i]) + sse(block[i:])))
           for i in range(min_seg, len(block) - min_seg + 1)]
    return sorted(out, key=lambda t: -t[1])
