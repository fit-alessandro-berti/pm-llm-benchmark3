## 1. Decision rule

The model prediction creates a **candidate for intervention**, not an intervention decision. Every 30-minute refresh updates one continuing alert episode unless genuinely new evidence creates a new episode.

**1. Operational eligibility gate**

A case may receive a swarm only when all of the following hold:

* The swarm will start before `Diagnosis Complete`. Stage is checked again immediately before dispatch.
* There is enough time for the swarm to affect the outcome. Define the last responsible moment as:

[
LRM_i = SLA_i - 45\text{ minutes} - Q_{0.75}(\text{remaining Resolution Work + Customer Validation})
]

The final term should be estimated from comparable historical cases. Until that estimate is available, time to SLA is used as an urgency ordering rather than assuming that all pre-deadline interventions are equally useful.

* No swarm has already been performed for the current evidence version. A second swarm requires materially new evidence and incident-commander approval.
* Critical input fields are observed or manually verified. A sharp prediction change caused by imputation does not authorize automatic intervention.
* The customer can participate without disproportionate disruption, and any required language support is available.

Cases at or after `Diagnosis Complete` must be routed to a different downstream-recovery playbook, not given an ineffective diagnostic swarm.

**2. Alert persistence and evidence gate**

A routine alert becomes actionable after two consecutive refreshes satisfy the intervention criteria. It can become actionable immediately when:

* a material process event occurs, such as a new diagnostic log, customer response, dependency discovery, or stage change;
* a validated model-input change produces an absolute calibrated-risk shift at least as large as the greater of 0.10 and the model’s 95th-percentile refresh-only jitter;
* the case reaches its last responsible moment; or
* a legal, safety, or regulatory obligation creates an override.

Repeated model refreshes without a new event remain part of the same alert episode. Alert count itself never raises priority.

**3. Calibration adjustment**

The supplied calibration findings are used as sensitivity bands, not treated as exact corrections:

* **SME:** working adjusted-risk interval
  [
  [p_{\text{now}}+0.04,\ p_{\text{now}}+0.09]
  ]
  capped at 1.
* **Strategic Enterprise:**
  [
  [p_{\text{now}}-0.12,\ p_{\text{now}}-0.05]
  ]
  floored at 0.
* **Public Sector:** use `p_now` as the center, but widen uncertainty using the segment’s moderate-sample calibration interval.
* **Non-Native-Language Support:** do not make a numerical point correction from the sparse evidence. Treat the prediction and intervention effect as low-confidence and require human validation.

For example, the working risk interval is approximately 0.76–0.81 for I-07, 0.54–0.61 for I-02, 0.36–0.43 for I-04, and 0.31–0.38 for I-08.

These calibrated probabilities support urgency assessment. They are **not** used as estimates of risk under intervention, and the supplied risk-reduction ranges are not assumed to be identified causal effects.

**4. Marginal priority rule**

For case (i), define:

[
D_i^L=\Delta_i^L V_i-450-B_i
]

where (\Delta_i^L) is the lower estimated effect, (V_i) is the value of avoiding breach, and (B_i) is the listed false-alarm burden. This is a conservative direct-value floor before spillover.

At the current congestion level, let (s_t) be the estimated breach-probability increase for each of six untreated queue cases and let (\bar V_U) be their mean value of avoiding breach. Then:

[
M_i^{base}=\Delta_i^{mid}V_i-450-B_i-6s_t\bar V_U
]

and the breach-count margin is:

[
G_i^{base}=\Delta_i^{mid}-6s_t
]

A routine slot normally requires:

* positive lower-bound direct value (D_i^L>0);
* positive base marginal economic value (M_i^{base}>0);
* positive breach-count margin (G_i^{base}>0), unless a documented regulatory or severity consideration justifies trading additional breach count for breach severity;
* acceptable data and effect-estimate confidence.

Cases are then ordered lexicographically by:

1. regulatory or safety obligations;
2. last responsible moment;
3. robust direct value and breach reduction after spillover;
4. evidence quality and effect uncertainty;
5. a fairness safeguard preventing value alone from controlling all slots.

The portfolio is re-scored after every dispatched swarm. Capacity is not automatically exhausted merely because slots exist.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

**Alert episodes and suppression.** Each alert is assigned an `evidence_version`. Refreshes every 30 minutes update the same alert episode until there is a material event. Notifications from an unchanged episode are suppressed for two refresh cycles, or 60 minutes, unless:

* the priority tier changes;
* the case reaches its last responsible moment;
* the process stage changes;
* a critical field is corrected;
* calibrated risk changes by the defined materiality rule; or
* a regulatory override appears.

The case remains visible on the portfolio dashboard while notifications are suppressed. Suppression prevents alert fatigue; it does not imply that the risk has disappeared.

**Escalation.** Escalation is based on evidence and expiring intervention opportunity, not alert count. A case moves upward when new evidence strengthens the plausible intervention mechanism, the last responsible moment approaches, or the externality-adjusted benefit becomes favorable. A prediction increase caused solely by an unverified imputation creates a data-quality escalation, not a swarm escalation.

**Post-swarm cool-down.** A case receives at most one swarm per evidence version. A second swarm requires all three conditions:

1. at least two refresh cycles have passed;
2. a material event creates a new evidence version and a different actionable diagnostic question; and
3. the incident commander documents why the first swarm’s work is not merely being repeated.

Time passage or another high probability by itself is insufficient. Where the swarm is no longer suitable, escalation must switch to a different intervention.

**Re-entry after a no-action decision.** A waiting case can re-enter when new evidence arrives, its last responsible moment is reached, congestion declines, a critical input is repaired, or its externality-adjusted score changes materially. The system preserves the original alert timestamp so repeated suppression cannot hide prolonged unresolved risk.

**I-05 versus I-07**

* **I-05:** The move from 0.55 to 0.57 without a new event is refresh noise or continued persistence within the same episode. Its third alert is collapsed into the first alert episode and receives no priority bonus. The SME correction means its risk should not be dismissed—it is approximately 0.61–0.66—but five hours remain, allowing a planned review rather than another urgent notification.
* **I-07:** The new customer diagnostic log creates a new evidence version even though the prediction fell from 0.75 to 0.72. The evidence may reveal a tractable diagnostic problem, and the intervention window closes in 1.5 hours. Its repeated alert is therefore substantively different and is re-scored immediately. The slightly lower probability does not negate the new information.

Thus, the policy responds to **changes in the evidence and intervention opportunity**, not to whether the latest probability arrow points upward.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**Value-based prioritization can starve lower-value cases.** A pure euro ranking would favor I-04 and I-08 and could repeatedly defer SME or lower-contract-value customers even where more breaches could be prevented per slot.

Safeguards:

* Maintain two allocation lanes: an economic-severity lane and an urgency/breach-reduction lane.
* For a four-slot day, at least one routine slot should be decided primarily by urgency and absolute breach reduction, provided the lower-bound direct value is non-negative.
* Cap contract value at the current portfolio’s 90th percentile for operational ranking while retaining uncapped value for reporting and executive overrides.
* Audit intervention rates, time-to-action, breaches, and false-alarm burdens by value decile.
* Require a written override when an urgent robust-positive case is displaced solely because another case has a larger contract value.

Selecting I-07 while the higher-value I-04 waits is consistent with this safeguard.

**Non-Native-Language Support has greater uncertainty.** Penalizing I-09 simply because its confidence interval is wider could institutionalize under-service. Conversely, assuming the same effect as other segments could expose customers to ineffective disruption.

Safeguards:

* Use the lower effect bound for robust screening but do not apply an additional automatic “language uncertainty” penalty.
* Provide a specialist or facilitator able to work in the relevant language.
* Include communication quality, information completeness, and customer burden in the intervention record.
* When several candidates have overlapping priority intervals, include eligible language-support cases in a randomized or rotating tie-break rather than always choosing the group with more historical data.
* Estimate effects hierarchically so sparse groups borrow information cautiously without being forced to equal the majority segment.

**Calibration differs by segment.** A common probability threshold would over-alert Strategic Enterprise cases and under-alert SME cases.

Safeguards:

* Maintain segment- and stage-specific calibrators with uncertainty intervals.
* Evaluate decisions under both endpoints of the correction band.
* Report calibration intercept, slope, and observed breach rate within risk bands separately for SME, Strategic Enterprise, Public Sector, and language-support cases.
* Use partial pooling for smaller groups and do not interpret noisy small-sample differences as proof of equal or unequal treatment.
* Audit selection-rate differences both before and after conditioning on stage, urgency, effect range, data quality, and customer burden.

**Selective intervention changes future labels.** Cases receiving successful swarms may be recorded as non-breaches even though they would have breached without treatment. Training a future prediction model on these outcomes without treatment information could teach the model that high-risk patterns are safe. Conversely, repeatedly withholding treatment from lower-ranked groups can make their observed outcomes look intrinsically worse.

Safeguards:

* Record treatment assignment, treatment time, priority score, eligibility, decision reason, propensity of selection, and queue conditions.
* Retain the frozen pre-intervention prediction.
* Do not train a standard breach predictor on treated outcomes as though they were untreated labels.
* Use randomized near-tie allocation, inverse-propensity methods, or doubly robust causal estimators to construct policy-learning data.
* Monitor model performance separately in untreated cases and through counterfactual estimators in treated cases.

**Spillover can systematically harm untreated groups.** Diagnosis queues may be segmented by customer type, language, team, geography, or complexity. Senior-specialist withdrawals could therefore repeatedly transfer delay to the same groups.

Safeguards:

* Record every untreated case exposed to each swarm, including exposure duration, queue, team, segment, language context, and change in expected deadline risk.
* Impose a per-queue externality budget rather than only a global slot limit.
* Pause additional swarms when the estimated probability uplift for exposed cases approaches 0.03 each or aggregate delay moves into the adverse band.
* Audit spillover exposure and outcomes by segment, not merely direct intervention rates.
* Rotate ordinary specialist coverage where feasible so the same queue or team does not repeatedly absorb the interruption.
* Include untreated-case customer harm in post-incident review and portfolio economics.

**Effect-estimation design.** When more cases are eligible than there are slots and priority intervals overlap, use a randomized tie-break within the tied risk set. This produces known treatment propensities without withholding intervention from cases that clearly dominate. Because treatment affects neighboring cases, the unit of analysis must include both the treated case and its queue-time block.

Estimate:

* the direct treatment effect on breach and remaining-time outcomes;
* heterogeneous effects by stage, time to SLA, segment, diagnostic problem, and data quality;
* customer-disruption effects;
* spillover as a function of the number and timing of swarms in the queue.

A doubly robust time-to-event estimator with time-dependent treatment and recorded assignment propensity is preferable to a naive treated-versus-untreated comparison. Queue block or team should be modeled as an interference cluster. Where operationally acceptable, controlled variation in whether the reserve slot is released can help estimate the marginal effect of the third or fourth swarm on untreated queues.

**Detecting policy-induced drift.** Preserve both the production decision and a frozen “no-policy-change” shadow score. Monitor whether:

* high-risk patterns increasingly receive treatment;
* diagnosis durations change because specialists anticipate swarms;
* customer behavior changes in response to alerts;
* treated non-breaches become overrepresented in training data;
* segment composition changes among untreated cases.

Calibration should be assessed directly in untreated cases and counterfactually for treated cases, not by mixing both populations.

**Review cadence and change control**

* Re-score the portfolio before every dispatch.
* Review operational capacity and spillover daily.
* Review calibration, missingness, and alert persistence weekly.
* Review causal effects, customer burden, and fairness monthly using uncertainty intervals.
* Conduct formal policy and model change control quarterly, or immediately after a safety, regulatory, or severe spillover trigger.

The action gate should be tightened—or fewer slots used—when the estimated direct lower effect is no longer larger than the induced breach risk, the net-benefit interval is predominantly negative, or queue delay moves into the adverse range. It can be relaxed, and the reserve released earlier, when the lower confidence bound of marginal portfolio benefit remains positive across comparable periods and the reserve is repeatedly unused. Changes should be tested through a champion–challenger policy rather than silently altering a single breach-probability threshold.
