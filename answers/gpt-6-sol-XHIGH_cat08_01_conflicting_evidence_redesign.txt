# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The fast route is attractive at the point of approval: the frequency DFG records 2,550 Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval transitions and 2,300 Fast approval → Dispatch replacement transitions. Intake → Auto screen has a 0.5-hour median, Auto screen → Fast approval 0.4 hours, and Fast approval → Dispatch 1.8 hours. Fast approvals cost 31% less before rework. Yet their advantage falls to 9% after complaints, reshipments, and credits; 14.1% of fast-approval cases reopen versus 3.3% of repair-quote/approved-repair cases. These groups have different case mixes, so that contrast is a warning, not proof that repair causes better outcomes. In particular, fast approval serves Premium low-risk accessories well (8-hour median; 6% reopen), while Premium high-value devices have a 22% fast-path reopen rate against 5% on their reviewed/repair path. A blanket switch to manual review would sacrifice speed, increase cost, and expose Premium accessory claims to service credits after 24 hours.

The fast route also contains compliance defects. Alignment diagnostics find approval before evidence completeness, high-value dispatch without risk review, and complaints/reopens after close. These matter under R1 (review before fast approval above EUR 400), R2 (complete evidence before dispatch except for Premium low-risk accessories), and R3 (manual review of every reopened claim before its final disposition). Fitness of 0.91 coexists with only 68% fitting traces; the discovered model's low precision means its unusual combinations of approval, risk review, and repair must not be treated as authorized variants. The 350 Fast approval → Quality/compliance close transitions also warrant case-level examination to establish whether fulfillment was omitted, merely represented elsewhere, or legitimately unnecessary.

The slow routes have distinct causes. Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review has a 26-hour median and 88-hour p90; 26% of cases entering evidence requests receive at least two. The DFG records 890 returns to manual review and 320 abandonments after an evidence request. Manual review → Repair quote reaches a 60-hour p90 and is already too slow for some Premium claims; part-price confirmation delays three quote technicians. Repair quote → Customer approval adds a 21-hour median. Downstream, Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen has a 70-hour median and 180-hour p90, linked to wrong part selection; a high-value wrong replacement has an expected EUR 410 error cost. Repair quality is imperfect too: 12.5% of repaired high-value devices require rework. Seven manual reviewers provide 252 productive hours per week, but their queue grows sharply above 82% utilization; adding review indiscriminately risks moving the delay there. Edge counts can include loops and do not give case-level causal effects or exact capacity needs.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-aware fast and reviewed lanes

**Flow.** Replace the outdated auto rules with intake classification by customer segment, claim value, defect clarity, product risk, and evidence status. Keep a rapid auto eligibility → technical decision → dispatch route for clear low-risk accessories. Apply the R2 early-dispatch exception only to Premium low-risk accessories; confirm evidence before dispatch for everyone else. Route claims above EUR 400 through manual eligibility or fraud/risk review *before* any fast approval, using fraud specialists for actual fraud signals rather than every high-value claim. Give Premium high-value claims a reserved, timed manual-review queue and a prompt technical decision on repair versus replacement. A reopen always enters manual eligibility before final close or denial, with its original case history attached.

**Targets and mechanism.** This protects the 18% Premium low-risk accessory cohort's 8-hour fast-path median and avoids the 46-hour manual median, while changing the risky fast treatment of high-value devices (22% Premium and 18% Standard reopen rates). Rule checks prevent R1/R2/R3 violations at the decision points. Segment-aware routing preserves cheap automation where its quality penalty is modest and assigns costly review where wrong replacements and compliance exceptions matter most.

**Sacrifice.** More high-value cases wait for reviewers, so their initial cycle time and labor cost may rise. A rules update may add 15–35% to manual inflow; the seven-reviewer queue and two fraud specialists need load limits and surge scheduling. Routing alone does not fix repeated evidence requests or incorrect part selection after a sound eligibility decision.

# 3. Redesign B: One complete evidence request and assisted resolution

**Flow.** At intake, use product and defect attributes to present a specific evidence checklist and validate serial, purchase, and diagnostic information before routing. For incomplete claims, assign one owner to make a consolidated request, offer upload plus phone/store-assisted submission, show the customer what remains and when review will resume, and return completed evidence to that owner without restarting eligibility review. Recheck completeness before dispatch under R2. Where a repair seems likely, request part-price confirmation in parallel with evidence gathering; issue a quote only after the technical decision confirms the part and price. Escalate unanswered requests through a human contact path before abandonment.

**Targets and mechanism.** Start with the 22% unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort and Standard low-risk accessory claims, whose customers can tolerate up to 72 hours when status is visible. The portal's estimated 30–45% reduction in repeated requests attacks the 26% repeat-request rate and the 26-hour median/88-hour p90 return from evidence request to manual review. Consolidation and a stable case owner remove avoidable handoffs; early price lookup addresses the manual-review → repair-quote delay. Premium claimants get an assisted, timed route rather than a portal-only queue.

**Sacrifice.** Intake takes more effort, assisted service adds cost, and a portal can increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy. This redesign does not by itself correct high-value fast-approval rules or wrong replacement parts; R1/R3 controls remain mandatory alongside it.

# 4. Redesign C: Verify the fulfillment choice before release

**Flow.** After a compliant eligibility and technical decision, require a conditional pre-dispatch match of model/serial, defect, selected part, and stock for high-value replacements and ambiguous part families. Use a short automated match for clear accessories and send exceptions to a cross-trained quality inspector or technician. For high-value repairs, record the diagnostic/part choice, perform a targeted functional quality check before release, and feed failure codes back to technicians. Confirm fulfillment and provide a direct correction channel; link any later complaint to the original claim, route it through manual eligibility under R3, and reserve the daily escalation board for genuinely complex cases. Close only after the applicable quality/compliance checks are recorded.

**Targets and mechanism.** Pilot on Standard high-value device replacements and high-value repairs, then consider Premium high-value claims if the gate meets their service promise. This attacks the long-tailed Dispatch replacement → complaint transition, the EUR 410 expected cost of a wrong high-value replacement, and the 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate. The two quality inspectors' unused capacity outside repair campaigns can catch selection errors before shipment and supply defect-specific feedback.

**Sacrifice.** A human pre-dispatch gate can add 6–18 hours and labor/inspection cost; its value depends on catching enough errors. Applying it indiscriminately would threaten Premium low-risk accessory speed and service-credit exposure. It does not remove evidence-request loops or eligibility-policy defects upstream.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

- **A:** An overbroad EUR 400 or risk rule can flood manual review, pushing utilization past 82% and harming even unrelated cases. Misclassified low-risk or Premium claims can lose the fast service they need; scarce fraud specialists can accumulate Monday/campaign work. **Likely migration:** from fast-path complaints to manual eligibility or fraud-review waiting time, followed by repair-quote demand.
- **B:** Customers with low digital literacy may abandon despite fewer repeat requests. A checklist can falsely mark evidence complete or generate irrelevant requests for unusual defects; parallel part-price work may be wasted if the technical decision changes. **Likely migration:** from repeated evidence requests to assisted-intake capacity or part-price confirmation at the quote desk.
- **C:** The gate may miss compatibility errors or merely add 6–18 hours to correct shipments. Inspectors can be overwhelmed after repair campaigns, and premature close can still hide late complaints. **Likely migration:** from reshipment/reopen work to the QA, technician-exception, or part-lookup queue.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

1. **Establish a trustworthy baseline and contain mandatory exceptions.** Link each event, shipment, repair, reopen, and service credit to one case; validate timestamps and the apparent post-close complaints. Measure case-level p50/p90 time, queue wait, end-to-end labor and downstream cost, wrong-part reships, abandonment, service credits, reopens within a common follow-up window, and R1/R2/R3 violations by segment, defect, and value. Enforce the three rules for all cases immediately; do not use a noncompliant control group. Track reviewer hours against the 82% utilization warning level.
2. **First pilot: A, with a bounded high-value cohort and a low-risk fast-lane check.** Compare eligible cases assigned to the new routing with concurrent, policy-compliant handling, stratified by Premium/Standard, defect, value, and weekday; randomize among compliant alternatives if feasible. Hold or roll back the *routing expansion* if review utilization approaches 82%, another segment's p90 wait rises materially (for example, over 20%), or Premium commitments deteriorate. Require zero confirmed R1–R3 violations; look for a substantial high-value reopen reduction (a prespecified target might be at least 25–30% relative) and no material rise in fully loaded cost after follow-up. Never roll back the mandatory rule controls.
3. **Pilot B on incomplete-evidence claims once A's review load is stable.** Randomly offer guided portal plus assisted submission versus the existing compliant evidence service, tracking take-up separately from assignment. A reasonable go signal is roughly a 25–30% relative fall in repeat requests, shorter evidence p90, and no more than a small rise in abandonment (for example, 1–2 percentage points), especially for assisted customers. If abandonment rises, expand assisted access before expanding the portal.
4. **Pilot C on Standard high-value fulfillment, then test a faster version for Premium.** Compare gated and ungated cases only where both are policy-compliant, with matched part/defect mix. Proceed if wrong-part reships and repair rework fall materially enough to offset gate labor and if added p90 time stays within an agreed service promise; a prespecified wrong-part reduction target could be 25% relative. If the QA queue builds or the gate delays correct deliveries without lowering late complaints, narrow it to ambiguous parts.

For every stage, use the same post-dispatch follow-up window long enough to capture the 180-hour p90 complaint transition and later post-close reopens; report early operational measures separately from mature outcomes. Compare **total** cycle time and cost through any reopen, reshipment, repair rework, and credit, not merely time to first approval or first close. Check spillovers in non-pilot queues and segment-specific p90s, and inspect failed cases before combining A, B, and C: otherwise a local improvement may simply have displaced work downstream.
