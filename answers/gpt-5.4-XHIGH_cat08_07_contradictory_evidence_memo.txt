# 1. Executive memo

Management should not scale Redesign R nationally now, and it should not fully roll it back. The best next step is a constrained version of alternative E: keep the redesign only for clearly defined simple, low-risk houses; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation for higher-risk segments; and run a short controlled experiment with better measurement before any wider rollout.

This recommendation follows the shape of the evidence rather than any single headline metric. The pilot delivered real value in predictable work. For simple low-risk houses, median duration improved by 6.0 days, p90 improved by 8.0 days, first-time-right improved by 3 percentage points, and complaints fell by 1 point. Handovers also dropped sharply overall, which is consistent with the coordinator model improving flow and accountability for simpler cases. A full rollback would throw away gains that appear both operationally and commercially meaningful.

At the same time, the harms are too large and too concentrated to ignore. Overall p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days. First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, second visits increased from 11% to 18%, and manual activation rose from 7% to 15%. Estimated total cost, once rework and complaints are considered, appears worse even before full downstream cost is measured. These are not just data quirks. Process-mining diagnostics point to specific failure modes: billing starting before successful activation, repeated installation visits without survey, absent permit checks in older apartment buildings, and a new long-tail path from billing start to complaint escalation.

The process-mining model conflict is especially important. High fitness but low precision in the pilot means many cases do follow the new simplified path, but the redesigned process also permits too many loosely controlled exception combinations. That is consistent with the DFG shifts toward manual activation, failed visits, second visits, and complaint escalation after billing. In other words, the pilot seems faster for the middle of the distribution because it compresses straightforward cases, while becoming less controlled and more expensive in the difficult cases that drive tail risk and customer harm.

The organizational evidence points the same way. Coordinator ownership reduced handovers, but utilization hit 93% in two regions, cases stalled during absence, and work became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. That is a sign of lower resilience, not just higher utilization. The redesign appears to have shifted the bottleneck from waiting for appointments to recovering from exceptions after installation.

The decision, therefore, should be to preserve proven benefits where process uncertainty is low and stop the elements that create avoidable rework and control risk. Keep coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule only for simple low-risk houses, with backup coverage to reduce single-person dependency. Pause survey skipping outside validated low-risk strata. Pause post-billing activation as a standard path; billing should follow successful activation except governed exceptions. Reinstate explicit permit-check controls for old or permit-sensitive buildings and monitor conformance on those rules continuously.

Management should then run a targeted regional experiment, not a national rollout. The key questions are whether survey skipping is actually safe by building-risk stratum, whether the apparent cost savings survive 60-day downstream measurement, and whether the coordinator model still outperforms alternatives once peak load and absence are included. If those questions resolve well, rollout can expand segment by segment. If not, management should keep the simple-house path and redesign the complex-case path rather than reverting everything.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- High fitness but low precision: The pilot has a common streamlined path, so many observed traces align well with the discovered model, raising fitness. But the same model must also admit many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, billing start, and complaint escalation, so it becomes too permissive and precision falls.
- Improved median but worse tail duration: Straightforward cases moved faster, especially simple low-risk houses. Complex cases accumulated more failed visits, activation exceptions, permit issues, and rescheduling delays, so the upper tail deteriorated even while the middle improved.
- Fewer handovers but lower resilience: Reducing handovers simplified accountability, but it also concentrated knowledge and decision load in coordinators and activation specialists. When those people were overloaded or absent, cases stalled because the system had less redundancy.
- Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost: Skipping surveys and moving activation work after billing can reduce immediate labor and elapsed time. Those savings can be overwhelmed later by second visits, complaint handling, billing disputes, and manual exception work that sit outside the visible cost window.
- Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings: The redesign fits predictable cases where merged steps and coordinator ownership reduce friction. It fits poorly where building complexity, permit sensitivity, and activation uncertainty make surveys and controls more valuable.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Conditions under which it would be reasonable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Scale Redesign R nationally | Captures speed and handover benefits quickly; simple operating story; may improve many simple cases. | Scales known tail, rework, billing-before-activation, permit, and resilience problems nationwide. | Only reasonable after tail, rework, and control metrics are fixed and validated by segment, which is not the current state. |
| B. Roll back to baseline | Stops further exposure to current pilot failure modes; restores a known, more controlled process. | Throws away real gains for simple low-risk houses; preserves older waiting and handover inefficiencies; loses learning. | Reasonable only if activation/permit deviations are judged intolerable immediately and segmentation controls cannot be enforced quickly. |
| C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation | Preserves accountability and fewer handovers while removing the two most suspect design choices. | Does not solve coordinator overload by itself; may still underperform in complex segments without backup design and permit controls. | Reasonable if management wants one near-term operating model and is prepared to add backup capacity, absence coverage, and strict activation controls. |
| D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better measurement | Produces stronger causal evidence; directly addresses confounding, incomplete cost, and missing timing data. | Slower path to broad benefits; if badly scoped, may prolong ambiguity. | Reasonable if the organization can contain risk while learning and is disciplined about experimental design. |
| E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and run measurement before wider scale | Best fit to the segment evidence; preserves proven upside while limiting harm; enables learning without full rollback. | Operational complexity; depends on reliable routing and risk classification; partial rollout can create governance overhead. | Reasonable now, provided the eligibility rules are tight, survey skipping is paused outside validated strata, and conformance is monitored actively. |

# 6. Final recommendation

Recommended decision: choose alternative E, implemented as a guarded hybrid with a short controlled experiment.

- Immediate action: Continue the pilot only with strong controls. Enforce billing after successful activation except governed exceptions, and reinstate explicit permit-check controls for old or permit-sensitive buildings.
- What to pause: Pause survey skipping outside clearly validated low-risk strata. Pause post-billing activation as a standard operating path. Do not scale the current complex-case flow nationally.
- What to scale: Scale coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule only for simple low-risk houses, with backup ownership and absence coverage so utilization and stall risk are controlled.
- What to measure next: Prioritize M1, M6, M4, and M7 first; then M5 and M2; then M3 and M8.
- Threshold for broader rollout: Broader rollout should require, for any segment being added, p90 and p95 cycle time no worse than baseline, first-time-right and complaint rate at least as good as baseline, full 60-day cost no worse than baseline, coordinator utilization sustainably below about 85%, and conformance violations for billing-before-activation and missing permit checks reduced to rare governed exceptions rather than recurring patterns.

If those thresholds are met in a controlled regional test, management can expand segment by segment. If they are not, management should keep the simple-house path and redesign the complex-case path rather than either scaling the current pilot nationally or reverting the entire redesign.
