# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring changes the organizational topology from distributed local teams with embedded specialists to a centralized shared-service hub. This fundamentally alters queuing dynamics, knowledge flows, and accountability structures. 

*   **Nominal Handover Reduction:** Management expects fewer handovers by merging technical specialists and routing complex cases directly to a central pool. However, process mining will likely reveal that physical or structural distance between the local caseworker and the shared service increases the number of communication and re-assignment steps (e.g., missing informal "desk-side" checks).
*   **Central Queue Formation:** Pooling N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech creates a central queue. If the pooled team absorbs high-complexity cases *and* difficult Quality Checks, demand variability will spike. Queuing theory dictates that wait times grow exponentially as utilization exceeds ~85%. 
*   **Specialist Utilization:** Current average tech utilization is roughly 73%. A 15% increase (relative or absolute) pushes this pool toward the 85%+ threshold. Process mining will track this via the `time:timestamp` delta between "Specialist Review" start and end, combined with queue wait times.
*   **Rework:** Removing embedded specialists eliminates local knowledge sharing. Cases requiring regional or language accommodations will suffer. Process mining will observe rising `rework_flag` rates specifically for `customer_segment`s with unique local needs.
*   **Local Expertise Loss:** Routing all high `case_complexity` and `required_specialty` cases to the center deprives local teams of learning opportunities. Over time, local caseworkers become simple routers, degrading their ability to catch upstream errors.
*   **Role Clarity:** Moving Supervisor Approval to a shared queue diffuses accountability. PM4Py’s organizational mining will show "spaghetti" resource assignments where multiple supervisors touch a case without clear ownership, observable in the `reassignment_count`.
*   **Resilience to Absence:** A shared pool provides better coverage for specialist absence (mitigating the current 8.9h cross-team handover wait). However, it creates a single point of failure. If the central shared service is overwhelmed or a key specialist is absent, *all* teams are impacted, rather than just one.
*   **Fairness Across Teams:** East currently struggles (82% tech utilization, 8.6d cycle time). They will likely see improvement as their load is shared. North and South, which currently have stable, fast local loops, may see degradation as their 71% and 68% utilized specialists are pulled away, creating fairness and equity issues in service delivery across regions.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1.  **Tail Latency (High-Complexity Cases):** Average cycle time masks the 90th percentile. The restructuring specifically targets high-complexity cases for the central queue. If this queue bottlenecks, simple cases will drive a beautiful average cycle time while complex cases languish for weeks, destroying the experience for the most valuable or vulnerable customers.
2.  **Fairness Across Segments:** An average cycle time combines North, South, and East. If North and South degrade by 1 day (losing embedded specialists) and East improves by 2 days (gaining shared resources), the *average* looks like a success. Process mining must segment by `team` and `location` to expose this zero-sum dynamic.
3.  **Local Knowledge Loss & Rework:** If the shared service provides technically correct but contextually blind advice, local caseworkers will execute it and close the case. The customer suffers a bad outcome, or a downstream error occurs weeks later. The average cycle time and immediate `rework_flag` might look fine, hiding the erosion of quality.
4.  **Learning and Career Stagnation:** Routing all complex cases away from local teams starves junior staff of learning opportunities. Process mining cannot easily measure "learning," but a declining rate of local staff successfully resolving complex cases over time is a hidden precursor to a brittle organization that lacks internal talent pipelines.
5.  **Accountability Diffusion:** In a shared approval queue, multiple supervisors might briefly touch a case (high `reassignment_count`). The case remains "active" in the system, but no single person owns it. Average cycle time might be stable, but the *cost of coordination* (invisible in the event log) skyrockets.
6.  **Exception Handling & Process Bypass:** When central queues fail, staff create workarounds (e.g., emailing a specific supervisor directly). These exceptions don't hit the shared queue. Average cycle time for the *tracked* queue looks fine, but untracked ad-hoc work increases, leading to burnout and unmeasured delays.

# 5. Pilot design

To mitigate the risks of a systemic collapse, management must reject a full immediate rollout and adopt a phased pilot.

*   **Pilot Scope:** Migrate **Team East** to the proposed shared-service model first. East is the ideal candidate: they have the lowest baseline customer satisfaction (3.8/5), the longest cycle times (8.6d), and E_Tech is already overloaded (82% utilization). They have the most to gain and the lowest risk profile to the overall business if the pilot struggles.
*   **Comparison Group:** Teams **North and South** will remain under the current local model as the control group.
*   **Pre/Post Observation Window:** 
    *   *Baseline:* 6 weeks of pre-pilot data capturing current performance.
    *   *Pilot:* 8 weeks of post-implementation observation to allow for a 2-week "break-in" period and 6 weeks of stable-state analysis.
*   **Segmentation:** All analysis will be heavily segmented using PM4Py filters on:
    *   `team` (East vs. North/South)
    *   `case_complexity` (High vs. Medium vs. Low)
    *   `required_specialty` (Technical vs. Legal vs. Risk vs. None)
    *   `customer_segment` (Standard vs. Regional/Language needs)
*   **Guardrail Metrics (Hard Stops):**
    *   If East's 90th percentile cycle time for high-complexity cases exceeds 25 days (current: 22.8d).
    *   If East's `rework_flag` rate exceeds 13% (current: 11.5%).
    *   If North or South's cycle time or SLA miss rate degrades by >10% due to shared resource starvation.
*   **Staff Feedback Collection:** Bi-weekly qualitative interviews with East caseworkers and shared-service specialists to gauge role clarity, informal communication breakdowns, and perceived bottlenecks.
*   **Rollback/Adjustment Triggers:** If two guardrail metrics are breached simultaneously, the pilot is paused. If the central queue wait time consistently exceeds 8 hours, routing rules are dynamically adjusted to bypass the shared service for medium-complexity cases.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: Pilot First (Strictly Conditional)**

Management should **not** proceed with a full rollout. The theoretical benefits of pooling specialists are heavily outweighed by the queuing risks introduced by high utilization targets and the loss of contextual local knowledge. 

However, because Team East is currently struggling with overloaded local specialists and poor cycle times, a **Team East Pilot** is highly recommended. This allows the organization to test the shared-service hypothesis where the potential upside is highest.

**What Management Must Not Infer from Early Averages:**
Management must be explicitly warned that early average cycle times will almost certainly look positive. When a shared queue is first opened, it clears backlogs and prioritizes easy wins. Furthermore, a stable average cycle time post-implementation does *not* mean the system is healthy. Management must look at the **90th percentile cycle time for high-complexity cases** and the **rework rates for regional customer segments**. If they optimize for average cycle time, they will inadvertently sacrifice fairness for vulnerable teams and complex customer needs, creating a two-tiered system that looks efficient on a dashboard but fails in practice.
