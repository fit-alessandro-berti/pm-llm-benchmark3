# 1. Data audit and estimands

## 1.1 Observational basis

The supplied evidence consists of 28 daily records from 2026-04-06 to 2026-05-03, four representative hourly vectors, and textual caveats. The service centre is open 07:00–19:00, i.e. 12 opening hours per day and 336 opening hours in total. Every day contributes one aggregate row; rows are therefore time-ordered observations, not independent replicates.

| Symbol | Quantity | Unit | Supplied information |
|---|---|---:|---|
| $N^a_d$ | Arrivals during day $d$ | cases/day | Daily count, opening hours only |
| $N^c_d$ | Completions during day $d$ | cases/day | Daily count; a completed case need not have arrived that day |
| $\lambda^a$ | Arrival intensity | cases/open hour | Estimated as $N^a/12$, unless an outage-adjusted window is explicitly stated |
| $\lambda^c$ | Completion intensity | cases/open hour | Estimated as $N^c/12$ |
| $S$ | Active service/handling time | min/case | Only daily median and p90 are supplied; mean and full distribution are not supplied |
| $L$ | Work in process, WIP | cases | Mean of 15-minute snapshots; supplied as one daily mean, without snapshot values |
| $W$ | Mean time in system, waiting plus service | hours | Not supplied; may only be inferred under restrictive conditions |
| $c$ | Staffing capacity | concurrent FTE | Daily mean concurrently available FTE |
| $\rho$ | Utilisation | dimensionless | Cannot be estimated exactly because $E[S]$ is unknown |
| $a=\lambda E[S]$ | Offered load | Erlangs | Cannot be estimated exactly without $E[S]$ |

“Opening-hours convention” means that intensities are per 07:00–19:00 hour. No service is assumed to occur while closed, but cases may wait overnight. Consequently a flow time expressed in opening-hour units is not automatically a calendar-time flow time.

The following are **estimates**, calculated from the supplied daily aggregates: regime arrival and completion intensities, ranges of daily medians and p90s, mean WIP, and a clearly labelled median-based workload proxy,
\[
\text{median workload proxy}
=\frac{N^a \times \text{median}(S)}{c\times 720},
\]
where 720 is staff-minutes in a 12-hour day if $c$ FTE were available for the whole day. This proxy is **not** utilisation, because the numerator should use $E[S]$, not the median.

## 1.2 Assumptions needed for particular calculations

**A1 — Staffing availability.** For the median-workload proxy only, $c$ is treated as dedicated, uniformly available queue capacity. This is needed because within-day rosters are not supplied. It will be wrong if staff are shared, absent, or concentrated in particular hours.

**A2 — Snapshot representativeness.** For any Little’s-Law ratio, the daily mean of 15-minute WIP snapshots is treated as a time-average of $L$. This is needed because snapshot-level values are unavailable. It may be biased if snapshots miss opening, closing, or overnight backlogs.

**A3 — Outage capacity.** For one sensitivity calculation on 2026-04-15, all six FTE are assumed unable to complete queue work during the three-hour outage. This is a scenario, not an observed fact.

**A4 — Primary-regime homogeneity.** Within each proposed regime, daily values are summarised descriptively despite small samples and serial dependence. This is needed to obtain regime-level ranges; it does not establish that the regime is a true stationary stochastic process.

No assumption is made that arrivals equal completions, that the system is closed, or that median service equals mean service.

## 1.3 Internal-consistency audit

Two material inconsistencies are observed.

First, cumulative mass balance fails for a closed queue. Total arrivals are 2,519 and total completions are 2,409, a difference of 110 cases over 28 days, or 4.4% of arrivals. If there were no abandonment, cancellation, transfer, duplicate counting, or other exits, end-of-period backlog would exceed start-of-period backlog by 110. Yet mean WIP is 18 on 2026-04-06 and 19 on 2026-05-03. The discrepancy does not prove an error; it proves that the queue cannot be treated as closed without additional information about exits or definitions.

Second, the daily `peak_08_10_pct` field cannot be reconciled with the representative hourly vectors. From the hourly bins labelled 08–09 and 09–10, the implied 08:00–09:59 shares are: 2026-04-07, $17/101=16.8\%$ versus 31% in the daily table; 2026-04-15, $19/105=18.1\%$ versus 32%; 2026-04-21, $11/115=9.6\%$ versus 47%; and 2026-04-25, $6/51=11.8\%$ versus 22%. The hourly totals do match daily arrivals, so this is not a simple total-count error. Either the peak field has a different operational definition, timestamps or bins are misaligned, or the representative vectors are stylised rather than literal. Intraday timing conclusions must therefore be downweighted until this is resolved.

The temporary staffing intervention is also imprecisely located: staff FTE already reads 7.0 on 2026-04-29, but the implementation time was not logged. Thus 2026-04-29 may be a mixed transition day.

## 1.4 Threats to naïve inference

1. **Non-stationarity:** demand, case mix, service tail, intraday concentration, staffing, and backlog change at different times. A pooled mean averages unlike operating states.
2. **Unknown opening backlog and carry-over:** cases cross midnight and weekends. Daily arrivals and completions are flows, whereas WIP is a stock; neither $N^a-N^c$ nor a daily snapshot mean recovers the opening stock.
3. **Non-closed system:** the cumulative 110-case imbalance implies possible abandonment, cancellation, transfer, redefinition, or measurement error. Little’s Law in its simple closed-system form is therefore unsafe.
4. **Median/p90 are not the mean:** queue stability depends on $E[S]$, which is unobserved. Substituting the median will generally understate workload for right-skewed service, but the size of the bias is unknown.
5. **Outage censoring and recovery contamination:** on 2026-04-15 completions are structurally suppressed; on the following days completions draw down a pre-existing backlog. Neither day estimates ordinary demand-driven throughput.
6. **Confounding and possible coding change:** the complex-variant percentage jumps at the same period as service times and intraday concentration change. It may reflect case mix, coding, routing, or all three. The staffing rise on 2026-04-29 is likewise confounded with backlog state and a possible change in which cases are completed first.
7. **Small samples and serial dependence:** several regimes contain only two to four days. Consecutive days share backlog, staff practices, and case-mix conditions, so daily rows are not independent and conventional independent-sample uncertainty would be misleading.
8. **Selected representative days:** only one weekday, one outage day, one bursty complex weekday, and one weekend day are supplied hourly. They cannot estimate regime-specific hourly profiles, variances, or tail behaviour.
9. **Within-day staffing unknown:** a daily mean FTE cannot reveal whether capacity is present during the morning or midday bursts.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## 3.1 Materially different story: flux/backlog-state segmentation

A serious alternative is to segment by the sign and persistence of the daily flow imbalance rather than by calendar cause:

| Alternative state | Dates | Operational story | Descriptive flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balanced | 2026-04-06–04-14, 04-18–04-19, 05-02–05-03 | Inflows and outflows roughly match; no strong clearing or building signal | 74.3 arrivals/day and 73.9 completions/day, heavily affected by weekday/weekend composition; mean WIP 18.3 |
| Interrupted | 2026-04-15 | Service capacity is structurally unavailable for part of the day | 105 arrivals, 58 completions |
| Building / congested | 2026-04-20–04-28 | Arrivals persistently exceed completions; backlog stock rises | 99.1 arrivals/day, 87.3 completions/day; shortfall 11.8/day |
| Clearing | 2026-04-16–04-17, 04-29–05-01 | Completions exceed arrivals; backlog stock is drawn down | 111.2 arrivals/day, 120.8 completions/day; surplus 9.6/day |

This segmentation represents an operations-control story: the queue is balanced, building, clearing, or interrupted. It is useful for deciding whether a temporary surplus is needed, and it avoids assuming that the calendar labels are the true causes.

Its strengths are that it directly tracks stock accumulation, treats recovery as clearing rather than normal performance, and remains meaningful even if the complex-variant coding changed. Its weaknesses are substantial. First, it is outcome-defined: using completions to define a regime and then estimating completion intensity is partly circular. Second, it lumps unlike causes: the balanced group mixes low-complex baseline days with high-complex post-intervention weekend days. Third, it discards staffing and day-of-week information needed for planning. Fourth, a day can be “balanced” in flow while still carrying an elevated WIP stock, as on 2026-05-02–03. Finally, it can encourage the false inference that clearing days reveal sustainable capacity.

Compared with the primary segmentation, the alternative is better for short-term backlog control but weaker for causal explanation, staffing design, and forecasting. A defensible operational view uses both: primary regimes to plan capacity by demand and mix, flux states to monitor whether the stock is actually falling.

A second, simpler alternative—segmenting only at complex percentage $\ge 40\%$—would classify 2026-04-15–19 as baseline despite p90 service rising to 60–72 min. That would hide the service-time deterioration preceding the coded complex jump and is therefore not preferred.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

## 4.1 Weekday/weekend composition is obscured

Pooled over all 28 days, arrivals average 90.0/day (7.50/open h), completions 86.0/day (7.17/open h), mean WIP is 29.9, and staff FTE is 5.14. Those pooled values describe no actual regime well.

Segmented arrivals are about 100–115/day on weekdays but only 42–49/day on weekends. Segmented WIP is 13.8–21.5 on weekends but 20.3–44.3 on weekdays. Staffing is 2.5–3.0 FTE on weekends and 5.9–6.8 FTE on weekdays. A pooled staff figure of 5.14 FTE would overstate weekend capacity by roughly 70–105% while understating weekday capacity. Likewise, pooled WIP of 29.9 hides the difference between a weekend system near 14 cases and a complex weekday system above 44 cases.

## 4.2 Complex-case mix is bimodal, not pooled

The pooled mean complex percentage is 32.9%, and the pooled median of the daily percentages is about 33%. Yet observed regimes are either around 21–23% or around 40–46%. There is no observed regime centred near 33%. Similarly, the mean of daily median service times is about 31.0 min, but baseline weekdays are centred near 25 min and complex weekdays near 35 min. Pooling creates a fictitious “average case” that is neither the baseline case nor the complex case.

The service tail also changes: p90 is 44–50 min in baseline weekdays but 78–88 min in complex pre-intervention weekdays. The p90/median ratio rises from about 1.9 to about 2.4, indicating a widening tail rather than a simple proportional shift.

## 4.3 Outage, recovery, and intervention distort completion intensity

The pooled completion/arrival ratio is $2409/2519=95.6\%$. Segmented ratios are 99.4% in baseline weekdays, 55.2% on the outage day, 114.4% in recovery, 88.2% in complex pre-intervention weekdays, and 105.2% in post-intervention weekdays. Pooling therefore turns a sequence of interruption, clearing, accumulation, and renewed clearing into an apparently mild 4.4% shortfall.

A naïve pre/post comparison around 2026-04-29 would also be misleading. Post-intervention weekday completions average 121/day versus 100.6/day in complex pre-intervention weekdays, while arrivals are almost unchanged (115.0 versus 114.0/day). That contrast is real descriptively, but it is not a clean causal effect of one extra FTE: the post period begins with a large backlog available to complete, median service falls from 35 to about 32.7 min, and case selection for completion may have changed. Conversely, recovery completions of 120.5/day on 2026-04-16–17 show that a similarly high completion rate can occur without the later intervention label.

## 4.4 Simpson’s-paradox-like reversals

Across all days, higher staff FTE is associated with higher WIP because 6–7 FTE occur on busy weekdays and 2.5 FTE on quiet weekends. A pooled regression would misleadingly suggest that more staffing accompanies more backlog. Within comparable complex weekdays, however, raising staff from about 5.9 to 6.8 FTE coincides with completions rising above arrivals and WIP falling from a mean of 44.3 to 37.0. The pooled association is driven by assignment of staff to high-demand days.

A second reversal concerns weekends: baseline weekend median service (28–30 min) is higher than baseline weekday median service (24–26 min), yet weekend WIP is much lower. The explanation is not necessarily better weekend flow; weekend arrival intensity is only about 3.5/open h versus 8.4/open h on baseline weekdays. Stock levels reflect both service behaviour and inflow.

The 2026-04-21 arrival vector is exceptionally bursty: 34 arrivals in the 10–11 bin and 24 in the 13–14 bin, while completions in those bins are 13 and 12. The completion vector is much smoother (Fano about 0.98), consistent with a capacity-constrained system buffering rather than instantly matching arrivals. The weekend vector is comparatively flat.

These Fano factors must not be read as pure overdispersion. If arrivals were conditionally Poisson with a strongly varying hourly rate $\lambda_h$, the marginal variance across hours would exceed the mean even without extra-Poisson randomness. The correct dispersion question is whether counts vary more than expected around a regime-specific hourly profile. That requires multiple days per regime to estimate both the profile and residual variance; one vector per selected day cannot do so.

The intraday timing evidence is additionally compromised by the peak-percentage discrepancy documented in Section 1.3. The hourly vectors suggest bursts at 10–11 and 13–14 on 2026-04-21, whereas the daily peak field purports to measure 08:00–09:59 concentration. This audit failure prevents a confident recommendation to move staff specifically into 08:00–10:00.

## 5.2 Daily serial dependence and change points

Positive lag dependence is expected for WIP, median service, and complex percentage because backlogs, working practices, and case mix persist across adjacent days. Arrivals contain a strong day-of-week pattern, so lag-1 and lag-7 behaviour cannot be interpreted as a simple stationary autoregression. Only four weeks are available, giving at most four observations of each weekday and no reliable basis for estimating weekly dynamics.

The clearest change-point evidence is the complex-percentage jump between 2026-04-19 (23%) and 2026-04-20 (41%), accompanied by peak percentage rising from 18% on the Sunday to 42% on Monday and median service reaching the 34–36 min range. Service behaviour, however, already deteriorates around the outage: median service is 31–33 min on 2026-04-15–16 and p90 reaches 68–72 min. It is therefore not defensible to claim one single change point for all variables. Staffing changes around 2026-04-29. The defensible summary is multiple overlapping breaks: service/outage around 2026-04-15, coded mix and peak concentration around 2026-04-20, and staffing around 2026-04-29.

Four representative days cannot establish a full stochastic model because they provide no replication within most regimes, no joint distribution of arrivals and service times, no abandonment information, and no case-level links across days. They can illustrate mechanisms—burst, outage zeros, smoothed completions—but cannot estimate arrival-process family, service distribution, or dependence parameters.

# 6. Little’s-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law states $L=\lambda W$ for a stable system when $L$ is the time-average number in system, $\lambda$ is the long-run throughput, and $W$ is the mean time in system over the same accounting window. It requires boundary stability, a defensible throughput proxy, comparable WIP measurement, and no unobserved exits. The ratios below use $W_{\text{open h}}=L/\lambda$ and therefore express time in opening-hour units, not calendar time. They are not substitutes for observed mean flow time, and median service time must not be subtracted from them to obtain waiting time.

# 7. Operational implications

## 7.1 Staffing by regime, not by pooled average — Recommendation

Staffing decisions should be made separately for baseline weekdays, complex weekdays, weekends, and outage recovery. Complex pre-intervention weekdays have a median-workload proxy near 0.94 before accounting for the unknown mean-service tail; post-intervention weekdays at about 6.8 FTE bring the proxy to about 0.76 and produce a completion surplus. The operational conclusion is to retain the higher weekday staffing level while the complex mix and elevated WIP persist, rather than reverting to the pooled 5.14 FTE or the old 6.0 FTE because pooled figures look manageable.

Weekends should not be treated as spare capacity merely because arrivals are low. Complex pre-intervention weekends already have a median-workload proxy around 0.93 at 2.5 FTE, with p90 service of 81–85 min. Cutting weekend staff would risk creating Monday carry-over. The post-intervention weekend level of 3.0 FTE is more consistent with the segmented workload.

## 7.2 Backlog recovery — Recommendation

Outage recovery requires scheduled surplus capacity on the following day. On 2026-04-16, 7.0 FTE completed 129 cases; on 2026-04-17, 6.5 FTE completed 112. Those figures should be used to plan an outage playbook, not as ordinary productivity benchmarks. For the complex regime, the relevant control target is a sustained daily completion surplus until WIP returns toward the baseline weekday range near 20, while monitoring whether the surplus is achieved by completing complex cases or merely selecting easier cases.

## 7.3 Peak smoothing and intraday flexing — Recommendation with uncertainty

The daily peak field indicates that complex weekdays concentrate up to 40–50% of arrivals in the stated peak window, implying roughly 26 arrivals/hour in that window versus 9.5/hour on average. That would justify morning flexing if the field is correct. The hourly vector for 2026-04-21 instead shows extreme bursts at 10–11 and 13–14. Before moving staff, the timestamps, bin labels, and peak-field definition must be reconciled. Interim measures that do not depend on the disputed window are to stagger non-urgent arrivals, triage the complex variant at intake, and keep a midday flex option for bursty days.

The complex-variant triage recommendation is a **hypothesis**, not an established causal finding. If the increase is a coding change rather than a true service-relevant mix shift, triage based on the label could waste capacity. A case-level audit linking variant, active handling time, waiting, and completion is needed.

## 7.4 A pooled-beneficial but segmented-risky policy

A pooled analysis might recommend reducing weekend or average staffing because pooled arrivals per FTE look low and pooled WIP is only 29.9. Under the segmented view, that policy is risky: complex weekdays need about 6.8–7.0 FTE to clear backlog, recovery after an outage needs temporary surplus staffing, and complex weekends are already near the median-workload proxy of 0.93 at 2.5 FTE. Cutting staff toward the pooled mean would recreate the building regime of 2026-04-20–28.

A related risky policy would be to judge the intervention solely by the fall in daily median service from about 35 to 32.7 min. The median may fall because the system completes a different subset of cases while complex cases remain in WIP. Monitoring must include p90, WIP by variant, age of backlog, and completion mix.

## 7.5 Data-collection recommendations

The minimum event-level dataset should contain case identifier, arrival timestamp, service-start and service-end timestamps, waiting versus active time, variant, completion/abandonment/cancellation/transfer status, staff on duty in 15-minute intervals, outage flags, and opening backlog by case age. Daily reporting should include mean service time and mean flow time in addition to median and p90, end-of-day backlog, and exits by reason. Without those fields, Little’s Law, utilisation, and causal staffing effects remain under-identified.

                   [1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 6, 5, 4, 4, 3, 2, 2]),

def dispersion(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    mean = x.mean()
    var = x.var(ddof=1)  # sample variance across the 12 hourly bins
    return {
        "mean_per_h": mean,
        "fano_factor": var / mean,
        "cv": np.sqrt(var) / mean,
        "max_over_mean": x.max() / mean,
        "top2_share": np.sort(x)[-2:].sum() / x.sum(),
        # Bins 2 and 3 are 08-09 and 09-10. This exposes, rather than hides,
        # the conflict with the daily peak_08_10_pct field.
        "hourly_implied_08_10_share": x[1:3].sum() / x.sum(),
    }

burst_rows = []
for day, (arr, comp) in hourly.items():
    da = dispersion(arr)
    dc = dispersion(comp)
    burst_rows.append({
        "day": day,
        "arr_mean": da["mean_per_h"], "arr_fano": da["fano_factor"],
        "arr_cv": da["cv"], "arr_max_over_mean": da["max_over_mean"],
        "arr_top2_share": da["top2_share"],
        "arr_hourly_08_10_share": da["hourly_implied_08_10_share"],
        "comp_fano": dc["fano_factor"], "comp_cv": dc["cv"],
    })
burst_df = pd.DataFrame(burst_rows)
print("REPRESENTATIVE HOURLY DISPERSION")
print(burst_df.round(3).to_string(index=False))

# These are descriptive only: regime blocks, weekends, trends and
# carry-over confound any pooled lag-1 correlation.

def lag1(values):
    s = pd.Series(np.asarray(values, dtype=float))
    return s.corr(s.shift(1))

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Transparent one-change-point heuristic
# For each candidate split, minimise total within-segment SSE.
# This is a search heuristic, not a significance test or ground truth.
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
def best_single_split(values, min_segment=3):
    v = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    best = None
    for k in range(min_segment, len(v) - min_segment + 1):
        sse = ((v[:k] - v[:k].mean()) ** 2).sum() + ((v[k:] - v[k:].mean()) ** 2).sum()
        if best is None or sse < best[1]:
            best = (k, sse, v[:k].mean(), v[k:].mean())
    return best

print("ONE-SPLIT SSE HEURISTIC (NOT DEFINITIVE)")
for col in ["complex_pct", "median_service_min", "peak_08_10_pct", "mean_wip"]:
    k, sse, left_mean, right_mean = best_single_split(daily[col])
    print(
        f"{col}: split after {daily['date_str'].iloc[k-1]} "
        f"(next day {daily['date_str'].iloc[k]}); "
        f"left mean={left_mean:.2f}, right mean={right_mean:.2f}, SSE={sse:.1f}"
    )
print()
