# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring is a change in **resource topology** (who may execute which activity) and in **routing policy** (which cases must reach which pool). Every organizational claim below is translated into something observable in the event log, so that the forecast can be falsified rather than argued.

**Structural inputs**

| Action | Log-visible change |
|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech / S_Tech / E_Tech into Technical Shared Service (TSS) | `org:resource` values collapse into a TSS pool; `team` of the specialist event decouples from `team` of the preceding caseworker event |
| Centralize Specialist Review, Legal, Risk, difficult Quality Check into one queue | Four activity types share one server pool → arrival streams superpose |
| Keep Intake + simple Eligibility local | Head of the trace unchanged; divergence begins at the caseworker→specialist edge |
| Routing rules (all high complexity → TSS; specialty ∈ {legal, risk, security} → shared queue) | Deterministic edges keyed on `case_complexity` and `required_specialty`; `local_expertise_flag` should fall toward 0 on those edges |
| Remove one local supervisor; shared approval queue | Approval edge changes from intra-team to inter-pool; approval becomes a queueing station rather than a hallway conversation |

## 1.1 Nominal handover reduction

Management's stated benefit is "fewer local-specialist handover loops." **The management estimate table does not show this.** Summing the frequency column:

- Current: 92+88+36+31+42+39+9+6 = **343 handovers per 100 cases**
- Expected post-change: 92+83+41+36+49+43+12+3 = **359 handovers per 100 cases**

So even under management's own optimistic assumptions, nominal handovers rise ~5%. What falls is *cross-team ad hoc* handovers (6→3) and *local intake→caseworker* (88→83). What rises is every edge that touches a central pool (36→41, 31→36, 42→49, 39→43, 9→12).

The mechanism: centralization does not remove a handover, it **relabels an intra-team handover as an inter-pool handover**. In `discover_handover_of_work_network` terms the *edge count per case* is roughly preserved, but edge weight concentrates on a few hub nodes. The right measure is therefore not the number of handovers but the **out-degree/in-degree concentration** of the handover graph (Gini or Herfindahl over edge weights) and the fraction of handovers that cross an organizational boundary.

Expected: handover graph density falls (fewer distinct resource pairs), handover graph **centralization rises sharply**. Both are computable before any outcome data exists.

## 1.2 Central queue formation

This is the dominant mechanism and it is non-linear. Waiting time at a pooled server scales approximately with ρ/(1−ρ). Two separate pressures:

**Technical Shared Service.** Current utilizations 71% / 68% / 82% (weighted mean ≈ 73%). Management targets +15% utilization → ≈ 85–88%. The queueing factor moves from 0.73/0.27 ≈ 2.7 to 0.86/0.14 ≈ 6.1, i.e. **waiting time roughly doubles to triples**, not the +25% (3.2h → 4.0h) assumed in the estimate table. Pooling does give a variability-reduction credit (three M/M/1 queues merged into one M/M/3 is genuinely more efficient), which partially offsets this — but the credit is spent, not banked, if utilization is simultaneously pushed to 85%. You can have the pooling benefit *or* the utilization benefit, not both.

**Legal/Risk queue.** Already at 93% / 91% utilization. Demand rises 9 → 12 per 100 cases (+33%) with **no stated capacity increase**. At ρ = 0.92, a 33% demand increase drives ρ > 1: the queue is unstable. The estimate of 9.6h → 10.5h (+9%) is not credible. Expect wait to grow by a multiple, and expect it to grow *without bound* until either arrivals are throttled or capacity is added. This is the single most concrete flaw in the proposal.

**Superposition effect.** Point 2 puts Specialist Review, Legal, Risk, *and* difficult Quality Check into one queue. Quality Check arrivals are correlated with implementation completions, which are themselves bursty. Merging a bursty stream into a near-saturated queue raises the *variance* of waiting time far more than the mean — the classic signature of "average is fine, P90 is terrible."

Expected weighted handover wait per case, using management's own numbers: current Σ(freq×wait)/100 = **5.88 h/case**; expected = **7.22 h/case**, i.e. **+23% total handover waiting** before any queueing correction. With realistic queueing the figure is materially worse.

## 1.3 Specialist utilization

Higher utilization is the mechanism *by which* waiting time worsens; it is not independently a benefit. Utilization is an input, not an outcome. The honest framing: 15% higher utilization purchases roughly 15% more specialist throughput per FTE, at the cost of a super-linear increase in queue delay for every case that touches a specialist. Whether that trade is good depends entirely on whether specialist capacity was the binding constraint. Evidence in the data says it was binding **for East only** (E_Tech 82%, 8.6d median, 11.5% rework) and **not** for North/South (71%/68%).

Observable: `get_service_time` per specialist should stay flat (same work, same person) while waiting time before specialist activities rises. If *service* time also rises, that signals context-switching cost from pooling four activity types — a second-order penalty.

## 1.4 Rework

Three opposing forces.

- **Downward:** standardized decisions and higher specialist exposure per case should reduce specialty-specific errors. Currently the specialist edge carries 9% rework; the legal/risk edge 14%. If routing genuinely catches cases that were previously mishandled locally, `rework_flag` on those cases falls.
- **Upward — loss of informal advice.** Current row "embedded tech specialist → same local caseworker, 31/100, 1.6h, 8%" is a *tight local loop*. That loop is cheap (1.6h) and it filters cases: many referrals are resolved by a two-minute conversation. Post-change the same clarification costs a queue round-trip (4.0h + 2.2h = 6.2h by management's own estimate). Rational caseworkers respond by either (a) not asking, and guessing — which raises rework, or (b) asking formally, which raises queue load. Both are bad; (a) is the more likely and the less visible.
- **Upward — context loss.** The log already documents this: cross-team handover carries **18% rework** versus 8–9% for local loops, and the stated cause is "case notes are inconsistent across locations." The restructuring converts ~36 handovers per 100 cases from the 8–9% class into a structurally similar to the 18% class. If even a third of that gap materializes, rework rises by ~3 percentage points on those cases — which alone could offset the entire efficiency gain.

Net forecast: rework falls for legal/risk/security-flagged cases and **rises for medium-complexity cases that previously used the informal local loop**. Aggregate rework rate may look flat; the composition changes. This must be measured with `get_rework_cases_per_activity` **segmented by `case_complexity` and `local_expertise_flag`**, never in aggregate.

## 1.5 Local expertise loss

Mechanism: skill is maintained by doing hard work. Routing all high-complexity cases away from local caseworkers removes the practice substrate. This is a **slow variable** — it degrades over quarters, not weeks, which is exactly why a short pilot will not detect it and why early averages are misleading.

Log signature (12–24 months): the *distribution of `case_complexity` handled by local resources* shifts down; the share of cases where a local caseworker completes Local Technical Assessment without a TSS handover falls; `reassignment_count` on the residual complex cases handled locally (absence cover, out-of-hours) rises; rework on locally-handled complex cases rises over time even as volume falls. Also: `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` will show local caseworkers becoming *more* similar to each other and *less* similar to specialists — the role space polarizes.

Second-order: recruitment and promotion pipelines. Today's specialists were yesterday's caseworkers who saw hard cases. Removing that path is a capability decision disguised as a workflow decision.

## 1.6 Role clarity

Two counteracting effects.

- **Clarity up:** explicit routing rules keyed on `required_specialty` and `case_complexity` remove ambiguity about *who should do what*. `discover_organizational_roles` should return fewer, cleaner role clusters with less overlap.
- **Ownership down:** a shared approval queue means no named person owns the case outcome. Removing a local supervisor removes the single accountable escalation point. This shows up as *pull-based* rather than *push-based* handovers, and empirically as **rising `reassignment_count`** — cases bouncing between pools because nobody's remit clearly covers them. Watch also for the appearance of a subcontracting pattern (`discover_subcontracting_network`: A → B → A) where the caseworker retains nominal ownership but does none of the substantive work: high subcontracting weight with low caseworker service time is the fingerprint of accountability without authority.

Forecast: **activity-level** role clarity improves; **case-level** ownership clarity degrades. The two are frequently confused. The proposal's own risk list acknowledges the second.

## 1.7 Resilience to absence

Genuinely improved — this is the strongest argument for the change. Today a single absent E_Tech is a single point of failure, and cover across teams fails (18% rework, 8.9h wait). A pool of three-plus specialists absorbs absence gracefully; cross-team handovers 6 → 3 reflects this.

But resilience is **redistributed, not created**. The failure mode moves from *idiosyncratic* (one person out, one team hurt) to *systemic* (queue saturates, all teams hurt simultaneously). At 85%+ utilization there is no slack to absorb a demand spike or a two-person absence — the pool has no reserve. And local resilience is actively destroyed: after local expertise decays, the pool cannot be backstopped locally when it does fail. Resilience improves against the *common* small shock and worsens against the *rare* large shock.

Measurable: variance of daily specialist waiting time; recovery half-life after a demand spike; whether any case ever completes Local Technical Assessment locally anymore (if not, the fallback path is untested — an untested fallback is not a fallback).

## 1.8 Fairness across teams and customer segments

**Across teams.** Asymmetric by construction.

- **East (520 cases/mo, E_Tech 82%, 8.6d, 11.5% rework, 3.8/5 satisfaction) is the clear beneficiary.** Its specialist is the constraint; pooling relieves exactly that. East should see cycle time and rework fall.
- **North and South (71%/68% utilization, 6.8d/7.4d, 4.3/4.1 satisfaction) are net contributors.** They surrender slack capacity and local expertise to subsidize East. Their cycle times should worsen slightly and their rework rise on medium-complexity cases.

This is a *transfer*, and it may well be a defensible one — total welfare can rise. But it must be named as such. If it is presented as "everyone wins" and North/South then see their numbers degrade, the credibility cost is large and staff cooperation collapses. Note also that pooling North/South slack is precisely what makes the 15% utilization target achievable; the target and the transfer are the same fact.

**Across customer segments.** Two risks.

1. **Complexity-based.** High-complexity cases are mandatorily routed to the most congested resource. They gain decision quality and lose time. Since complex cases are already the slow tail, this *widens* the cycle-time distribution — average may improve while P90 worsens. Segment `case_complexity` always.
2. **Regional/linguistic.** The scenario flags customers needing language or regional accommodation. Local teams currently hold that knowledge ("local teams know regional customer conventions; this reduces rework in complex cases"). A central pool does not. These customers face more handovers, more re-explanation, and higher rework — a fairness harm concentrated on an already-disadvantaged group and invisible in any aggregate metric. This must be measured by `customer_segment` × `location`, explicitly, from day one.

**Fairness within the queue.** A shared queue needs a discipline. FIFO across superposed streams means low-priority simple cases can delay high-priority complex ones and vice versa. Priority scheduling favours `priority`-flagged cases at the direct expense of everyone else's tail. Whichever is chosen, measure waiting time *by segment*, not pooled: a queue can be efficient in aggregate and systematically unfair by class.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

All three assume the same structural change; they differ in whether capacity is added, whether informal advice survives, and how fast local expertise decays. Probabilities are judgmental, conditional on the proposal being implemented **as written** (notably: no stated Legal/Risk capacity increase, one supervisor removed).

## 2.1 Optimistic scenario (~20%)

*Requires: Legal/Risk capacity added; TSS utilization capped near 80% not 88%; a fast informal-advice channel preserved; case-note standardization completed before cutover.*

- **Handover pattern.** Total edges per case roughly flat (~340–355). Handover graph density falls, centralization rises but hub nodes stay unsaturated. Cross-team ad hoc handovers 6 → 3 as predicted. `reassignment_count` stable or slightly down. Subcontracting network shows clean A→B→A advice loops with short turnaround, not ownership churn.
- **Queue/waiting.** TSS wait 3.2h → 3.6–4.0h (pooling variability credit roughly offsets the modest utilization increase). Legal/Risk wait *falls* to ~7h because added capacity moves ρ off the cliff. Total handover wait per case ≈ 5.8–6.3h, i.e. flat to marginally worse — offset by fewer rework cycles.
- **Cycle time.** Median improves ~8–12% overall. East improves most (8.6d → ~7.2d). North/South roughly flat. **P90 improves too** (22.8d → ~19d for East), because the tail was driven by specialist unavailability, which pooling genuinely fixes. Distribution narrows.
- **Rework/quality.** Aggregate rework 9.2% → ~7.5%. Legal/risk/security cases improve most (14% → ~10%) because detection broadens and decisions standardize. Medium-complexity local cases hold flat because the informal channel survived.
- **Role clarity/accountability.** `discover_organizational_roles` returns 4–5 crisp clusters. Named case owner retained locally despite shared approval; approval SLA met so ownership friction stays latent. Escalation path documented and used.
- **Fairness.** East gains, North/South approximately neutral, no segment materially worse. Regional-accommodation cases protected by an explicit routing carve-out. Waiting time by `customer_segment` shows no divergence beyond noise.
- **Early warning signs that this scenario is holding.** Legal/Risk wait falling within 4 weeks; TSS P90 wait under ~2× median; `reassignment_count` flat; rework on medium-complexity North/South cases flat; local caseworker service time unchanged (they are still doing substantive work).

## 2.2 Central scenario (~50%)

*Assumes: implementation broadly as proposed, partial capacity relief under pressure, informal advice partially decays.*

- **Handover pattern.** Total handovers rise ~5–8% (343 → ~360–370), close to management's own table. Centralization index rises sharply; two hub nodes (TSS, approval queue) carry ~35% of all edge weight. `reassignment_count` rises ~15–25% as cases bounce on ambiguous routing (is this "high" complexity? is this "difficult" Quality Check?). Cross-team ad hoc handovers do fall as predicted.
- **Queue/waiting.** TSS wait 3.2h → **5–7h** (management's 4.0h is optimistic by ~50%). Approval queue wait 2.4h → **3.5–5h**; the removed supervisor position means approval capacity fell while approval volume rose 42 → 49. Legal/Risk wait 9.6h → **14–20h and drifting upward**, because ρ approaches 1. Total handover wait per case ≈ **8–9.5h vs 5.88h today (+40–60%)**.
- **Cycle time.** Median roughly flat to modestly better (7.3d → ~7.0d), driven almost entirely by East (8.6d → ~7.5d) and by fewer failed cross-team covers. North/South median *worsens* ~5–10% (6.8d → ~7.3d; 7.4d → ~7.9d). **P90 worsens overall** (18–19d → ~21–23d), concentrated in high-complexity and legal/risk cases. The distribution becomes right-skewed and bimodal: a fast local-only mode and a slow shared-service mode. **This is the diagnostic signature to look for — average flat, variance up, bimodality emerging.**
- **Rework/quality.** Aggregate rework roughly flat (9.2% → 8.5–9.5%), masking a real compositional shift: legal/risk cases improve (14% → ~11%), medium-complexity cases handled without the informal loop worsen (~8% → ~11%), regional-accommodation cases worsen more. `rework_flag` correlation with `local_expertise_flag = 0` becomes statistically clear by month 4–6.
- **Role clarity/accountability.** Activity-level roles clearer; case ownership blurs. Subcontracting network shows caseworkers as nominal owners with declining service time — accountability without authority. Complaints and escalations take longer to resolve because no single resource owns the case history. Supervisor removal shows up as approval-queue P90 spikes at month-end and during absence.
- **Fairness.** Clear East-gains / North-South-pays transfer. Complex cases pay in time what they gain in decision quality. Regional/language-accommodation customers measurably worse: more handovers per case, higher rework, lower satisfaction. North/South satisfaction drifts down (4.3 → ~4.0; 4.1 → ~3.9) while East rises (3.8 → ~4.0) — aggregate satisfaction looks unchanged.
- **Early warning signs.** `reassignment_count` up >15% by week 6; TSS queue length trending up week-over-week rather than stabilizing; Legal/Risk wait exceeding 12h; ratio P90/median cycle time rising; rework on `local_expertise_flag=0` cases diverging; North/South satisfaction falling while aggregate holds.

## 2.3 Adverse scenario (~30%)

*Assumes: no capacity added; utilization target enforced; informal advice channel dies; case-note standardization deferred.*

- **Handover pattern.** Handovers rise 15%+ (343 → ~395) as ambiguous routing generates ping-pong. `reassignment_count` up 40%+. A pathological pattern appears in the handover network: caseworker → TSS → caseworker → TSS (re-referral because the first response lacked local context). Cross-team handovers do fall, but are replaced by longer central round-trips.
- **Queue/waiting.** Legal/Risk **saturates**: ρ > 1, waiting time grows without bound, work-in-progress accumulates, and the queue becomes the process constraint. Wait 9.6h → 30h+ and rising. TSS wait 3.2h → 8–12h once it also absorbs difficult Quality Check bursts. Approval queue becomes a second constraint after supervisor removal. Total handover wait per case doubles or worse.
- **Cycle time.** Median worsens 10–20% (7.3d → 8.2–8.8d) despite East improving. P90 blows out: 18–19d → **28–35d**, driven by cases needing legal/risk/security. A visible long tail of cases exceeding 60d appears — cases that previously did not exist as a category.
- **Rework/quality.** Rework rises to 11–13%. Cause: caseworkers stop asking for advice (the round trip is too expensive), guess, and are corrected at Quality Check — which is itself in the congested queue, so correction is slow, which lengthens the tail further. A reinforcing loop: congestion → less advice → more rework → more Quality Check load → more congestion.
- **Role clarity/accountability.** Ownership collapses. Cases with no clear owner age in the queue. Complaint resolution time doubles. Staff report they cannot tell customers when a decision will come — which is itself an SLA and trust failure. Local caseworkers deskill visibly within 2–3 quarters; the residual complex work they must handle during outages goes badly.
- **Fairness.** Severe and structured. High-complexity cases, legal/risk cases, and regional-accommodation customers absorb nearly all the degradation. Simple, standard-segment cases are unaffected or slightly better — so **the aggregate can still look acceptable while the worst-served customers are much worse served**. North/South staff perceive the change as extraction; attrition of experienced caseworkers begins, which permanently removes the local-expertise option.
- **Early warning signs.** Legal/Risk WIP rising monotonically for 3+ consecutive weeks (the unambiguous saturation signal); re-referral edges appearing in the handover network; rework rate rising while advice-request rate falls (the deadly combination — it means people stopped asking); P90/median ratio above ~3.5; any case category with >2× its pre-change waiting time; resignation or transfer requests from senior local caseworkers.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

Average cycle time is a poor instrument here for a structural reason: the restructuring **moves work between distributional regions** — it speeds up the previously-blocked East cases and slows down the previously-fast complex and specialist-touching cases. Those effects offset in the mean and compound in the tail.

**1. Tail latency (P90/P95/P99).** The central mechanism is a queue, and queues damage the tail far more than the mean. A shift from ρ=0.73 to ρ=0.86 roughly doubles mean wait but can quadruple P95 wait. Concretely: East's median could fall 8.6d → 7.5d while the overall P90 rises 19d → 23d, and the average barely moves. Additionally the queue *superposes* four activity streams (Specialist, Legal, Risk, difficult QC) — superposition raises variance more than mean. **Measure:** full quantile curve (P50/P75/P90/P95/P99) per period, plus the P90/median ratio; and check for bimodality, which an average cannot represent at all.

**2. High-complexity cases.** These are mandatorily routed to the most congested resource. They are simultaneously the lowest-volume (so they barely move the average), the highest-value, and the most time-sensitive. They can degrade by 50% while the aggregate improves. There is also a compounding effect: complex cases touch *multiple* central queues (TSS + legal/risk + shared approval + difficult QC), so they accumulate queue delay multiplicatively, not additively. **Measure:** cycle-time quantiles stratified by `case_complexity`, and count of distinct central-queue touches per case.

**3. Local knowledge loss.** The slowest and most consequential variable. It degrades over quarters and is invisible in any short window. Worse, its early effect is *masked*: while the incumbent local caseworkers still hold their expertise, everything looks fine; the loss appears only as those people are replaced or as their skills atrophy. By the time it shows in cycle time, the option to reverse is gone — you cannot re-embed expertise quickly. The scenario already tells us the magnitude: cross-team cover currently carries **18% rework versus 8–9% local**, and the cause is context loss. **Measure:** rework and cycle time on locally-handled complex cases over rolling quarters; complexity mix handled locally; and explicitly track the *outage* cases where locals must cope alone.

**4. Fairness.** An average is a weighted mean, so a harm concentrated on a minority segment is, by construction, nearly invisible. If regional/language-accommodation customers are 8% of volume and their cycle time rises 40%, the aggregate moves ~3% — within noise. Yet this is the group with the strongest equity claim and the group whose protection depended precisely on the local knowledge being removed. Similarly, North/South degradation can be exactly offset by East improvement in the aggregate while both teams' staff correctly perceive a change in their own numbers. **Measure:** distributions by `customer_segment`, `location`, `team`, `priority`; report min/worst-segment performance alongside the mean, and treat the worst-served segment as a first-class metric.

**5. Learning and capability.** Cycle time measures today's throughput with today's people. It says nothing about whether the organization can still produce specialists in three years. Routing all hard cases away from local staff removes the apprenticeship substrate; the effect on current-period cycle time is *positive* (locals handle only easy work quickly), so the metric actively rewards the capability loss. This is the clearest case of a measure pointing the wrong way. **Measure:** complexity distribution per resource over time; number of resources qualified for each activity (`discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` — watch the specialist cluster shrink and fail to be replenished); time-to-competence for new hires.

**6. Accountability and ownership.** A shared approval queue can process approvals quickly on average while nobody owns the case. The failures are concentrated in exceptions, complaints, and ambiguous cases — low-volume, high-salience, invisible in the mean. The log signature is `reassignment_count` and subcontracting patterns, not duration. A case can also be *fast and wrong*: shared approval that rubber-stamps to keep queue time down improves cycle time while degrading decision quality. **Measure:** `reassignment_count`, single-owner share, decision reversal rate, complaint resolution time, and approval service time (a *falling* approval service time under queue pressure is a rubber-stamping warning, not an efficiency win).

**7. Exception handling and resilience.** Averages are dominated by the standard path. The restructuring's failure mode is systemic and rare: a demand spike or multi-person absence at 85%+ utilization, with no local fallback because it has atrophied. Between such events the average is excellent — which is exactly what makes the fragility invisible. This is the classic efficiency-fragility trade: slack looks like waste until it is the only thing preventing collapse. **Measure:** WIP and waiting-time behaviour during actual shocks; recovery half-life; explicit fallback drills; share of cases still completable without the central pool.

**8. Rework composition (bonus).** Aggregate rework can hold at ~9% while its composition inverts — legal/risk rework falling and medium-complexity rework rising. The first is a genuine gain, the second a genuine loss, and they are not equivalent: the second class is larger and its cause (people stopped asking for advice) is self-reinforcing. **Measure:** rework by activity × complexity × `local_expertise_flag`, never pooled.

# 5. Pilot design

The proposal contains one well-evidenced win (East relief, cross-team cover), one arithmetic error (handovers rise, not fall), and one likely-fatal capacity gap (Legal/Risk at ρ>1). A pilot should be designed to **confirm the win and expose the gap cheaply**, not to validate the whole package.

## 5.1 Pilot scope

**Phase 0 — prerequisites, before any structural change (4–6 weeks).**
These are not optional and several are cheap wins in their own right:
- Standardize case notes across locations. The 18% cross-team rework is explicitly attributed to inconsistent notes; centralization multiplies exposure to exactly this defect. Fixing it first is the highest-value, lowest-risk action in the entire programme, and it improves the status quo even if the restructuring is abandoned.
- Resolve the Legal/Risk capacity question. Demand +33% onto a 91–93% utilized pair is not viable. Either add capacity, or narrow the routing rule, or drop point 2 of the proposal.
- Instrument the log: confirm `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, `customer_segment`, `local_expertise_flag`, `reassignment_count`, `handover_reason` are reliably populated. Establish 6–12 months of segmented baseline. **You cannot detect a fairness or tail effect you have no baseline for.**
- Define routing rules operationally — "high complexity" and "difficult Quality Check" must be decidable without judgment, or `reassignment_count` will rise on ambiguity alone.

**Phase 1 — East-first partial pilot (12 weeks).**
Pilot the **Technical Shared Service only**, seeded with East plus *one* of North/South. Rationale: East is where the evidence says pooling helps; including one of North/South tests the contributor side of the transfer while retaining the other as a control.
- **In scope:** merge tech specialists for participating teams; route high-complexity cases to the pool.
- **Explicitly out of scope in Phase 1:** supervisor removal, shared approval queue, centralized Legal/Risk/difficult-QC. Bundling these makes attribution impossible and stacks the two capacity risks simultaneously. Sequencing is the main risk control available.
- **Mandatory design feature:** preserve a fast informal advice channel (a low-friction "quick question" path with a target response under 30 minutes, logged as a distinct activity). This is the cheapest insurance against the rework mechanism in §1.4, and logging it separately lets you measure whether advice-seeking survives.
- **Capacity setting:** cap pool utilization at ~80%, not 88%. Take the pooling benefit as *reduced waiting*, not as *higher utilization*. If the case for the change only works at 88%, the case is weak.

**Phase 2 (only if Phase 1 guardrails hold, +12 weeks).** Add the third team; add shared approval **while retaining all three supervisors** to isolate the queue effect from the capacity cut. Supervisor removal is evaluated last and separately — it is a permanent capacity reduction justified by an unproven efficiency claim.

**Phase 3.** Legal/Risk centralization only if capacity was added and Phase 1–2 metrics hold.

## 5.2 Comparison group

- **Primary: concurrent control.** The non-participating team continues unchanged. This absorbs seasonality, demand shifts, and policy changes that a pre/post comparison would misattribute.
- **Secondary: pre/post within pilot teams**, using difference-in-differences against the control. Check the parallel-trends assumption on 6–12 months of baseline before relying on it.
- **Tertiary: within-team, within-period.** Non-routed cases (low complexity, no specialty flag) inside pilot teams act as an internal control for site-level shocks.
- **Caveat to state up front:** East and North/South differ systematically (volume, utilization, satisfaction), so pooled DiD estimates will be misleading. Report **per-team effects always**; a pooled average across a known-heterogeneous transfer is precisely the error §4 warns about. Cluster standard errors by team and accept that with three teams, statistical power is limited — this is a reason to weight guardrail thresholds and staff feedback more heavily than significance tests.

## 5.3 Pre/post observation window

- **Pre:** 6 months minimum, 12 preferred — enough to characterize P90/P99 and segment-level baselines, not just means. Tail statistics need volume.
- **Washout:** 4 weeks post-cutover excluded from inference. Expect transient degradation from learning and new routing; judging in this window produces false alarms. *Guardrails still apply during washout* — safety monitoring never pauses, only inference does.
- **Post:** 12 weeks minimum for queueing and rework effects; **cases must be followed to closure**, so cohort-based analysis (cases *started* in window) is required, not period-based, or you will systematically undercount long cases and flatter the result. This survivorship bias would create exactly the "average looks fine" illusion.
- **Extended:** 12 months for learning, expertise decay, satisfaction, and reversal rates. Decide the full rollout on 12-week data if guardrails are clean, but keep the expertise indicators under review for a year with a pre-committed reassessment point.

## 5.4 Segmentation plan

Every metric reported across this full grid; no aggregate-only reporting is accepted:

| Dimension | Strata |
|---|---|
| Team | North / South / East (never pooled) |
| Complexity | low / medium / high (high always reported separately) |
| Specialty | none / technical / legal / risk / security |
| Customer segment | standard / priority / regional-or-language-accommodation |
| Priority | low / medium / high |
| Local expertise flag | 0 / 1 |
| Time | weekly, to detect drift and saturation trends |

Minimum reporting unit: **team × complexity**, plus a standing report on the accommodation segment regardless of its volume.

## 5.5 Guardrail metrics

Breach of a red threshold triggers the response in §5.7 automatically — not a discussion about whether it counts.

| Guardrail | Amber | Red |
|---|---|---|
| P90 cycle time, any team | +10% | +20% |
| High-complexity median cycle time | +15% | +30% |
| Rework rate, any team | +1.5pp | +3pp |
| SLA miss rate, any segment | +2pp | +4pp |
| TSS median waiting time | >5h | >7h |
| Legal/Risk WIP | rising 2 wks | rising 3 wks *(halt)* |
| `reassignment_count` mean | +15% | +30% |
| Any customer segment cycle time vs others | >1.3× | >1.5× |
| Advice-request rate (medium complexity) | −15% | −25% |
| Customer satisfaction, any team | −0.2 | −0.4 |
| Local-only completion share | falling fast | ~0 (no fallback) |
| Staff "clear case owner" score | <85% | <70% |

Note the guardrails are deliberately asymmetric: they are tuned to catch *concentrated harm* (a segment, a team, a complexity class), because the aggregate will not.

## 5.6 Staff feedback collection

Staff raised the substantive risks in this proposal; they are the best-calibrated early sensor and their signal leads the log data by weeks.

- **Weekly pulse (3 questions, <60s), all pilot + control staff:** Do you know who owns each of your cases? Could you get specialist advice when you needed it today? Are you handling work you don't feel qualified for? Track by team; the control group provides the counterfactual.
- **Fortnightly structured interviews**, 4–6 staff rotating, covering: informal advice, ownership ambiguity, note quality, cases that "fell between" pools.
- **Structured exception log.** Every case where routing was unclear or a handover felt wrong: free text plus a `handover_reason` code. This is the fastest route to identifying routing-rule defects and it feeds directly back into rule refinement.
- **Specialist-side feedback.** Are inbound requests well-formed? Is context sufficient? Deteriorating request quality is the leading edge of the local-expertise mechanism and shows up in specialists' experience long before it shows up in rework.
- **Customer-facing staff on the accommodation segment** — a dedicated channel, since this group's harm is statistically invisible.
- **Anonymity and non-retaliation guaranteed**, and results published back to staff. If staff believe negative feedback jeopardizes the programme, the sensor goes silent exactly when it matters.

## 5.7 Rollback and adjustment triggers

**Immediate halt and revert (any one, sustained 2 weeks after washout):**
- Legal/Risk WIP rising 3+ consecutive weeks (queue instability — do not wait for cycle-time confirmation; by then a backlog exists that takes months to clear).
- Any red guardrail on rework, SLA, or P90 cycle time.
- Any customer segment at >1.5× others' cycle time.
- Evidence that decisions are being rubber-stamped to clear the approval queue.

**Adjust and continue (do not revert, but change a parameter):**
- TSS wait amber → add pool capacity or tighten the "high complexity" routing definition to reduce arrivals.
- `reassignment_count` amber → routing rules are ambiguous; rewrite them, don't reorganize further.
- Advice-request rate falling → the informal channel is failing; make it faster and cheaper before anything else.
- Rework rising only on `local_expertise_flag=0` cases → mandate a local-context field in the referral and re-measure.

**Do not proceed to the next phase unless:** all guardrails green or amber-and-improving for 4 consecutive weeks; East benefit confirmed at the expected magnitude; North/South not materially degraded; staff ownership score above 85%; and the Legal/Risk capacity question resolved with actual capacity, not an assumption.

**Preserve reversibility explicitly.** Do not eliminate the supervisor post, dissolve local team structures, or relocate staff during Phase 1. Every irreversible step should come *after* its supporting evidence, not before. The proposal as written front-loads the irreversible actions (supervisor removal) alongside the unproven ones — that ordering should be inverted.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: pilot first, in modified and unbundled form. Do not proceed with the proposal as written.**

The proposal bundles a well-supported change with an unsupported one and an arithmetically unsound one. Separating them is the whole recommendation.

**What the evidence supports.** East is genuinely constrained: E_Tech at 82% utilization, 8.6d median, 22.8d P90, 11.5% rework, 3.8/5 satisfaction — the worst on every dimension, with a single-point-of-failure specialist. Pooling technical specialists directly addresses this. The resilience gain is real: cross-team cover currently fails (8.9h wait, 18% rework), and a pool fixes that failure mode. **Proceed to pilot on the Technical Shared Service.**

**What the evidence contradicts.**
1. *"Fewer handovers."* Management's own table implies **343 → 359 handovers per 100 cases, an increase**, and weighted handover waiting of **5.88h → 7.22h per case, +23%**. The stated benefit is not present in the stated plan. Centralization relabels handovers; it does not remove them. This arithmetic should be reconciled before the proposal is approved.
2. *"Legal/Risk +33% volume at unchanged capacity."* Specialists at 91–93% utilization cannot absorb a third more work. The estimated 9.6h → 10.5h is not a plausible queueing outcome; ρ exceeds 1 and the queue diverges. **Point 2 of the proposal must not be implemented without added capacity.** This is a hard blocker, not a monitoring item.
3. *"+15% specialist utilization as a benefit."* Utilization is a cost driver for waiting time, not a benefit. At ~86% the queueing factor roughly doubles versus today. The pooling efficiency gain and the utilization increase draw on the same reserve; management is counting it twice.

**What is unproven and should be deferred.** Supervisor removal is a permanent capacity cut (approval volume rises 42 → 49 while capacity falls) justified by an untested standardization claim. Centralizing difficult Quality Check into the same queue as Specialist/Legal/Risk superposes a bursty stream onto a near-saturated server — the worst available design choice for tail latency. Both should be evaluated separately and last.

**Conditionality.** Continue past Phase 1 only if: Legal/Risk WIP is stable, TSS median wait stays under 5h with P90/median under 3, `reassignment_count` has not risen more than 15%, no team's rework or P90 has breached amber, no customer segment is at >1.3× others, the advice-request rate has not fallen more than 15%, and staff ownership clarity stays above 85%. Revert immediately on any red trigger in §5.7. Prerequisites (case-note standardization, Legal/Risk capacity, log instrumentation, operational routing definitions) must be complete before cutover — case-note standardization in particular is worth doing regardless of the outcome.

**What management should not infer from early averages.**

- **"Average cycle time improved, so it's working."** In the central scenario the average improves *because East improved*, while North/South degrade and the P90 worsens. A flat or improving mean is fully consistent with the adverse mechanism running. Read the quantile curve and the per-team split; if you are shown one number, ask for the distribution.
- **"Aggregate rework is flat, so quality held."** Rework composition inverts before the level moves. Flat aggregate rework masks legal/risk improving while medium-complexity cases degrade — and the second group is larger.
- **"Handovers went down."** Only *cross-team ad hoc* handovers fall. Total handovers rise. Check the total and the network centralization, not the one edge that moved favourably.
- **"Specialist utilization hit the target."** That is confirmation the cost was incurred, not that the benefit was delivered. Pair it with waiting-time quantiles.
- **"Satisfaction is unchanged."** Aggregate satisfaction can hold while East rises and North/South fall. Segment it.
- **"Weeks 1–4 look fine."** The two most serious mechanisms — queue saturation and expertise decay — have long time constants. Saturation shows as a *trend in WIP* before it shows in cycle time; expertise decay takes quarters and is masked while incumbents remain. Early stability is uninformative about both.
- **"No complaints from the affected segment."** Low-volume segments generate few complaints even under significant harm. Measure them directly.
- **"The pilot succeeded, so scale it."** The pilot deliberately tests the *favourable* case (East-first, capacity-capped, informal advice preserved, supervisor retained). Success there does not license the bundled version. Each deferred element needs its own evidence.

The change is not obviously good or bad — it is a **transfer of slack and expertise from North/South to East**, with a genuine resilience gain against small shocks and a genuine fragility increase against large ones. That trade may well be worth making. It should be made deliberately, sequenced so the reversible steps come first, measured by distribution and segment rather than by mean, and accompanied by honest communication to North/South about what they are being asked to contribute.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

```python
"""
Before/after comparison for the shared-service restructuring.

Design principles:
  * cohort-based windows (cases STARTED in window, followed to closure) --
    period-based slicing truncates long cases and flatters the result;
  * every metric segmented by team / complexity / specialty / customer_segment --
    aggregates are the failure mode this analysis exists to avoid;
  * distributions (P50/P75/P90/P95/P99), never means alone.
"""

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

CASE, ACT, TS, RES = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "org:resource"

CENTRAL_ACTS = {"Specialist Review", "Legal Review", "Risk Review", "Quality Check"}
QUANTILES = [0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99]


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- load / prepare
def load(path):
    df = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=[TS])
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS)
    return df.sort_values([CASE, TS])


def cohort(df, start, end):
    """Cases whose FIRST event falls in [start, end); all their events returned."""
    first = df.groupby(CASE)[TS].min()
    keep = first[(first >= start) & (first < end)].index
    return df[df[CASE].isin(keep)].copy()


def case_attrs(df):
    """One row per case: first-observed value of each case-level attribute."""
    cols = ["team", "location", "required_specialty", "priority", "case_complexity",
            "customer_segment", "local_expertise_flag", "outcome"]
    cols = [c for c in cols if c in df.columns]
    out = df.groupby(CASE)[cols].first()
    out["reassignment_count"] = df.groupby(CASE)["reassignment_count"].max()
    out["rework_flag"] = df.groupby(CASE)["rework_flag"].max()
    return out


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.1 social networks
def networks(df, label):
    """Handover / working-together / subcontracting, with concentration measures."""
    ho = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df)   # resource -> resource, weighted
    wt = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df)
    sc = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(df)     # A -> B -> A (delegation)

    n_cases = df[CASE].nunique()
    edges = pd.DataFrame(
        [(a, b, w) for (a, b), w in ho.__dict__.get("connections", ho).items()],
        columns=["source", "target", "weight"])

    total = edges["weight"].sum()
    # KEY CHECK: management claims handovers fall (343 -> 359 in their own table).
    per100 = 100.0 * total / n_cases
    share = edges["weight"] / total
    hhi = float((share ** 2).sum())          # concentration -> hub formation / fragility

    edges["cross_team"] = edges.apply(lambda r: _team(r.source) != _team(r.target), axis=1)
    cross_per100 = 100.0 * edges.loc[edges.cross_team, "weight"].sum() / n_cases

    # Re-referral: caseworker -> central -> same caseworker -> central again.
    return dict(label=label, handovers_per_100=per100, hhi=hhi,
                distinct_edges=len(edges), cross_team_per_100=cross_per100,
                subcontracting=sc, working_together=wt, edges=edges)


def _team(resource):
    return {"N": "North", "S": "South", "E": "East"}.get(str(resource)[:1], "Shared")


def compare_networks(pre, post):
    a, b = networks(pre, "pre"), networks(post, "post")
    return pd.DataFrame([a, b])[["label", "handovers_per_100", "cross_team_per_100",
                                 "hhi", "distinct_edges"]]


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.2 waiting / service
def waiting_and_service(df):
    """Waiting before each activity + service time; the queue signal lives here."""
    df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
        df, service_time_column="@@service_time", waiting_time_column="@@waiting_time")

    d = df.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    d["prev_res"] = d.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift()
    d["prev_end"] = d.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift()
    d["wait_h"] = (d[TS] - d["prev_end"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    d["is_handover"] = d["prev_res"].notna() & (d["prev_res"] != d[RES])
    d["to_central"] = d[ACT].isin(CENTRAL_ACTS)
    return d


def queue_report(d, by=("concept:name",)):
    """Median AND tail waiting per station -- the mean hides queue behaviour."""
    g = d[d.is_handover].groupby(list(by))["wait_h"]
    rep = g.quantile(QUANTILES).unstack()
    rep.columns = [f"p{int(q*100)}" for q in QUANTILES]
    rep["n"] = g.size()
    rep["tail_ratio"] = rep["p90"] / rep["p50"]        # >3 => bottleneck forming
    return rep


def handover_wait_per_case(d):
    """Total handover waiting per case: 5.88h pre vs 7.22h post in mgmt's own table."""
    return d[d.is_handover].groupby(CASE)["wait_h"].sum().describe(
        percentiles=[0.5, 0.9, 0.95])


def wip_trend(d, activity):
    """Legal/Risk saturation detector: monotonic WIP growth => rho >= 1. HALT signal."""
    arr = d[d[ACT] == activity].set_index(TS).resample("W").size().rename("arrivals")
    svc = d[(d[ACT] == activity)].set_index(TS).resample("W")["@@service_time"].sum()
    backlog = (arr - svc.div(np.timedelta64(1, "h")).fillna(0)).cumsum()
    return pd.concat([arr, backlog.rename("cum_backlog")], axis=1)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.3 cycle time
def cycle_times(df):
    t = df.groupby(CASE)[TS].agg(["min", "max"])
    return ((t["max"] - t["min"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400.0).rename("cycle_d")


def cycle_by_segment(df, dims=("team", "case_complexity")):
    ct = cycle_times(df).to_frame().join(case_attrs(df))
    g = ct.groupby(list(dims))["cycle_d"]
    rep = g.quantile(QUANTILES).unstack()
    rep.columns = [f"p{int(q*100)}" for q in QUANTILES]
    rep["mean"] = g.mean()
    rep["n"] = g.size()
    rep["tail_ratio"] = rep["p90"] / rep["p50"]   # rising ratio = tail blowout
    return rep


def diff_in_diff(pre, post, dims=("team", "case_complexity")):
    """Per-segment pre/post deltas. NEVER report the pooled average alone --
       East gains can fully mask North/South losses."""
    a = cycle_by_segment(pre, dims).add_prefix("pre_")
    b = cycle_by_segment(post, dims).add_prefix("post_")
    j = a.join(b, how="outer")
    for q in ("p50", "p90", "p99", "mean"):
        j[f"d_{q}_pct"] = 100 * (j[f"post_{q}"] / j[f"pre_{q}"] - 1)
    return j


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.4 variants / DFG
def variants_and_dfg(df, label):
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)
    vs = pd.Series({k: len(v) if hasattr(v, "__len__") else v
                    for k, v in variants.items()}).sort_values(ascending=False)
    dfg, s, e = pm4py.discover_dfg(df)
    pdfg, ps, pe = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)   # mean duration per edge

    perf = pd.DataFrame([(a, b, v) for (a, b), v in pdfg.items()],
                        columns=["from", "to", "avg_sec"])
    freq = pd.DataFrame([(a, b, v) for (a, b), v in dfg.items()],
                        columns=["from", "to", "count"])
    edges = freq.merge(perf, on=["from", "to"], how="outer")
    edges["label"] = label
    return vs, edges


def variant_shift(pre, post, top=15):
    """Bimodality check: a fast local-only mode + a slow shared-service mode."""
    vp, _ = variants_and_dfg(pre, "pre")
    vq, _ = variants_and_dfg(post, "post")
    j = pd.concat([vp.rename("pre"), vq.rename("post")], axis=1).fillna(0)
    j = j.assign(pre_sh=j.pre / j.pre.sum(), post_sh=j.post / j.post.sum())
    j["delta_sh"] = j.post_sh - j.pre_sh
    return j.reindex(j.delta_sh.abs().sort_values(ascending=False).index).head(top)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.5 roles / similarity
def roles_and_similarity(df, label):
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df)
    sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df)
    rows = [{"label": label, "role_id": i,
             "activities": sorted(r[0]), "resources": sorted(r[1].keys())}
            for i, r in enumerate(roles)]
    return pd.DataFrame(rows), sim


def expertise_drift(df_by_period):
    """Local expertise decay: share of HIGH-complexity work still done locally.
       Slow variable -- run on rolling quarters, expect months before signal."""
    out = []
    for label, d in df_by_period.items():
        a = case_attrs(d)
        loc = d[~d[ACT].isin(CENTRAL_ACTS)]
        hi = a[a.case_complexity == "high"].index
        out.append({
            "period": label,
            "high_cx_touched_locally":
                loc[loc[CASE].isin(hi)][CASE].nunique() / max(len(hi), 1),
            "local_only_cases":
                1 - d[d[ACT].isin(CENTRAL_ACTS)][CASE].nunique() / d[CASE].nunique(),
            "n_resources_doing_tech":
                d[d[ACT] == "Local Technical Assessment"][RES].nunique(),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(out)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.6 filtering
def slice_log(df, team=None, complexity=None, specialty=None, segment=None):
    if team:
        df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "team", team, level="case")
    if complexity:
        df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "case_complexity", complexity, level="case")
    if specialty:
        df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "required_specialty", specialty, level="case")
    if segment:
        df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "customer_segment", segment, level="case")
    return df


SEGMENTS = {
    "east_high":        dict(team=["East"],           complexity=["high"]),
    "north_medium":     dict(team=["North"],          complexity=["medium"]),
    "south_medium":     dict(team=["South"],          complexity=["medium"]),
    "legal_risk":       dict(specialty=["legal", "risk", "security"]),
    "accommodation":    dict(segment=["regional_accommodation", "language_support"]),
}


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.7 rework / SLA
SLA_DAYS = {"low": 10.0, "medium": 14.0, "high": 21.0}


def rework_report(df):
    rw = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(df)
    n = df[CASE].nunique()
    per_act = pd.Series(rw, name="cases_with_rework").to_frame()
    per_act["rate"] = per_act.cases_with_rework / n

    a = case_attrs(df)
    seg = a.groupby(["team", "case_complexity", "local_expertise_flag"]).agg(
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean"),
        reassign=("reassignment_count", "mean"),
        n=("rework_flag", "size"))
    return per_act, seg      # composition matters more than the aggregate level


def sla_report(df):
    a = case_attrs(df).join(cycle_times(df))
    a["sla_days"] = a["case_complexity"].map(SLA_DAYS).fillna(14.0)
    a["sla_miss"] = a["cycle_d"] > a["sla_days"]
    return a.groupby(["team", "customer_segment"]).agg(
        sla_miss_rate=("sla_miss", "mean"),
        p90_cycle=("cycle_d", lambda s: s.quantile(0.9)),
        n=("sla_miss", "size"))


def fairness_report(df):
    """Worst-served segment is a first-class metric, not a footnote."""
    a = case_attrs(df).join(cycle_times(df))
    g = a.groupby("customer_segment")["cycle_d"].median()
    return pd.DataFrame({"median_cycle_d": g, "ratio_to_best": g / g.min()})


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7.8 temporal features
def temporal(df):
    return pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df)   # ML-ready drift features


# ---------------------------------------------------------------- orchestration
def run_comparison(path, cut, pre_start, post_end, washout_weeks=4):
    df = load(path)
    pre = cohort(df, pre_start, cut)
    post = cohort(df, cut + pd.Timedelta(weeks=washout_weeks), post_end)

    report = {}
    report["networks"]      = compare_networks(pre, post)
    report["variant_shift"] = variant_shift(pre, post)
    report["cycle_dd"]      = diff_in_diff(pre, post, ("team", "case_complexity"))
    report["fairness_dd"]   = diff_in_diff(pre, post, ("customer_segment",))

    for lbl, d in (("pre", pre), ("post", post)):
        w = waiting_and_service(d)
        report[f"queues_{lbl}"]   = queue_report(w, ("concept:name",))
        report[f"ho_wait_{lbl}"]  = handover_wait_per_case(w)
        report[f"rework_{lbl}"]   = rework_report(d)[1]
        report[f"sla_{lbl}"]      = sla_report(d)
        report[f"fair_{lbl}"]     = fairness_report(d)
        report[f"roles_{lbl}"]    = roles_and_similarity(d, lbl)[0]

    # Saturation watch -- the single most important guardrail.
    report["legal_wip"] = wip_trend(waiting_and_service(post), "Legal Review")
    report["risk_wip"]  = wip_trend(waiting_and_service(post), "Risk Review")

    # Per-segment drilldown; NEVER stop at the aggregate.
    report["segments"] = {
        name: diff_in_diff(slice_log(pre, **f), slice_log(post, **f), ("team",))
        for name, f in SEGMENTS.items()
    }

    report["expertise"] = expertise_drift({"pre": pre, "post": post})
    return report


def guardrail_check(rep):
    """Translate §5.5 thresholds into automated red/amber flags."""
    flags = []
    dd = rep["cycle_dd"]
    for idx, row in dd.iterrows():
        if row.get("d_p90_pct", 0) > 20:
            flags.append(("RED", "p90_cycle", idx, row["d_p90_pct"]))
        elif row.get("d_p90_pct", 0) > 10:
            flags.append(("AMBER", "p90_cycle", idx, row["d_p90_pct"]))
    for q in ("legal_wip", "risk_wip"):
        b = rep[q]["cum_backlog"].tail(3)
        if b.is_monotonic_increasing and len(b) == 3:
            flags.append(("RED", "queue_saturation", q, float(b.iloc[-1])))
    fair = rep["fair_post"]["ratio_to_best"]
    if (fair > 1.5).any():
        flags.append(("RED", "fairness", fair.idxmax(), float(fair.max())))
    return pd.DataFrame(flags, columns=["level", "guardrail", "where", "value"])
```

**How to read the output.** Look first at `guardrail_check` and the WIP trends — queue saturation is the fastest-moving fatal risk and shows in backlog before it shows in cycle time. Then read `cycle_dd` and `segments` **row by row**: a pooled improvement with East strongly positive and North/South negative is the central scenario, not a success. Check `networks.handovers_per_100` against the claimed reduction, and `hhi` for hub formation. Finally, `rework_*` segmented by `local_expertise_flag` is the earliest quantitative trace of the expertise mechanism — an aggregate rework rate that holds flat while its composition inverts is precisely the outcome that a mean-based dashboard will report as "no change."
