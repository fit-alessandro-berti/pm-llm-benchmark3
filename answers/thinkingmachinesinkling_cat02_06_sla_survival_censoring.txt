Section 4: Statistical analysis plan.

Compare methods:
- Kaplan-Meier (KM): Estimates overall survival (time to any event?) No, KM is for single event with competing risks treated as censoring. Not appropriate here because competing events (Withdraw, Reject, Transfer) are not independent censoring. Also doesn't distinguish causes.
- Aalen-Johansen / Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF): Appropriate for competing risks. Estimates probability of Resolve by t accounting for competing events. This is the primary recommendation for Estimand 1.
- Cause-specific hazards (CSH): Models hazard of Resolve treating others as censoring. Good for understanding etiology/drivers of resolution rate, but doesn't directly estimate probability.
- Subdistribution hazards (Fine-Gray): Models hazard of Resolve in presence of competing risks without treating them as censoring. Useful for predicting absolute risk (CIF) with covariates. Good for secondary analysis with covariates.
- Restricted Mean Time (RMT): Mean time lost or restricted mean survival time up to 72h. Useful for workload forecasting (expected open hours). Good secondary.
- Landmarking: Fix analysis at a landmark time (e.g., 24h) to include time-varying covariates like Escalate without immortal time bias. Good for process-aware analysis.
- Multi-state model: Models transitions between stages (Submit -> Screen -> AutoCheck/Review -> Escalate/Rework -> Resolve/Withdraw/Reject/Transfer). Captures intermediate states and allows state-specific hazards. Best for understanding process dynamics, but requires more parameters. Good for exploratory/secondary.

**Estimand 3 (secondary — cause-specific restricted mean open time, RMT).**
*Definition:* The expected hours spent in an open/non-terminal state within the 0–72h window, averaged over all submitted cases. This translates directly into workload-hours for staffing.

*Assumption (labeled):* I assume `duration_or_age_h` is measured continuously in hours from `Submit`. Without intermediate event-level timestamps, stage-specific durations within the prefix are unobservable; the RMT therefore uses only the overall case-level time.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| Observed status | Example IDs | Time `t` | Resolve event (Est. 1) | Competing-risk code (Aalen–Johansen) | Open-state code (Est. 2) | Observation / censoring rule | Transfer T1 (base: competing) | Transfer T2 (independent censor) | Transfer T3 (informative / worst-case) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Resolved** | S01,S02,S03,S04,S07,S13,S16,S17,S18,S19,S20,S27,S28 | `duration` | 1 (`R`) | 1 | 0 (terminal) | Event observed at `t` | — | — | — |
| **Rejected** | S05,S14,S23 | `duration` | 0 (competing `J`) | 3 (`J`) | 0 (terminal) | Event at `t` | — | — | — |
| **Withdrawn** | S06,S12,S22,S30 | `duration` | 0 (competing `W`) | 2 (`W`) | 0 (terminal) | Event at `t` | — | — | — |
| **Transferred** | S08,S21,S32 | `duration` | 4 (`T`) / 0 / 5 | 4 (`T`) / censored / 5 (`F`) | 0 (terminal) / unknown / 0 | Event or censor at `t` | Competing event at `t` | Right-censored at `t` (independent) | If `t`  72: count as failure to resolve by 72; if `t` > 72: breach observed at 72 regardless |
| **Open, age < 72** | S10,S15,S24,S29,S31 | `age` | 0 (censored) | 0 | 1 (open at cutoff) | Right-censored at `age` (<72) | — | — | Weight by `last_event_gap_h`: long gap suggests informative stall |
| **Open, age  72** | S09,S11,S25,S26 | `age` | 0 (censored for overall; observed non-event at 72) | 0 | 1 (open at 72; observed breach) | Right-censored at `age` (72); known not resolved by 72 | — | — | Treat gap > median (e.g., S26 gap 72h) as likely failure; apply worst-case weight |

**Notes on interpretation (not false certainty):**
- **T1 (base):** Transfer ends internal observation; it competes with resolution. Defensible if the external organization’s resolution is not part of the internal SLA metric.
- **T2 (censoring):** Defensible only if transfer is administratively random with respect to resolution time. Given that escalated, high-priority cases are more likely to be transferred (S08 P1 escalated at 68h; S21 P1 escalated at 26h; S32 P1 escalated at 96h), independence is questionable—this is a **hypothesis** that likely fails here.
- **T3 (informative/worst-case):** A sensitivity bound. If transfers are selected for difficulty, treating them as failures (or weighting them downward) lowers the estimated success rate, especially for Cohort B which has two transfers (S21, S32) versus Cohort A’s one (S08).

**Withdrawal/rejection encoding:** Both are terminal internal outcomes. In Estimand 1 they are competing risks. In Estimand 2 they remove the case from the open-state risk. I do **not** treat them as non-informative censoring without discussion; they are clearly informative of case difficulty (e.g., S05 rejected quickly at 9h; S30 withdrawn near the boundary at 70h).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis that keeps only cases with a terminal internal outcome (`resolved`, `rejected`, `withdrawn`, `transferred`) and ignores the 9 open cases introduces multiple biases observable in the concrete IDs.

**1. Survivor / selection bias (open long-runners excluded).**
The open group includes 4 cases already past 72h: S09 (92h, gap 31h), S11 (81h, P1 escalated, gap 55h), S25 (79h, B P1 escalated, gap 48h), and S26 (104h, B P3, gap 72h). These are **observed SLA breaches**. Removing them makes the breach rate appear lower than it is.

**2. Competing-risk omission (terminal non-resolution treated as missing).**
Completed cases include withdrawals (S06 27h, S12 15h, S22 44h, S30 70h) and rejections (S05 9h, S14 63h, S23 11h). If we compute mean resolution time only among resolved cases, we implicitly assume withdrawn/rejected cases would have resolved eventually—this is unsupported. For example, S30 is withdrawn at 70h, just before the SLA boundary; excluding it inflates the perceived success rate near 72h.

**3. Cohort imbalance masked by missing open cases.**
Cohort A has 6 resolved out of 15 total (40% resolved in raw counts), but 3 open cases (S09, S10, S11, S15—actually 4 open). Cohort B has 7 resolved out of 17 (41%), but 5 open cases (S24, S25, S26, S29, S31), including two past 72h (S25, S26). A completed-case comparison might declare the cohorts “similar,” but Cohort B carries a heavier open-case tail that includes high-gap stalls (S26 gap 72h). Because assignment was **not randomized** and rollout compliance was incomplete, any cohort comparison must be descriptive, not causal.

**4. Difference between open cases below vs. above 72h.**
- Below 72 (potential future successes): S10 (47h escalated), S15 (35h, AutoCheck path), S24 (67h, close to boundary), S29 (51h escalated), S31 (23h).
- Above 72 (observed breaches): S09, S11, S25, S26. A naive analysis that drops all open cases removes both future potential successes *and* observed failures, distorting the risk profile asymmetrically.

**5. Informative censoring / inactivity gap.**
S26 (gap 72h) and S11 (gap 55h) have large `last_event_gap_h` values. This suggests the censoring mechanism is not random: cases stall, then remain open. Ignoring these cases removes the very signal (inactivity) that predicts breach.

**6. Immortal-time / leakage risk in naive feature use.**
Even if open cases were included, a naive regression might treat `escalated = yes` or `rework_count > 0` as baseline predictors. For S03, `Escalate` occurs after `Screen` and `Review`; for S13, `Rework` occurs after the first `Review`. Coding these as present at `t=0` gives cases “immortal time” during which they are labeled escalated/reworked before the event actually happens, and it conditions on post-baseline outcomes (leakage).

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

I compare methods for these specific data (N=32, competing risks, non-randomized cohort, sparse intermediate events, open cases with long gaps).

| Method | What it estimates | Suitability for this task | Key limitation with N=32 / this design |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan–Meier (KM)** | Overall “time to any event” if competing events are censored. | Poor for Estimand 1. Treating `Withdraw`/`Reject`/`Transfer` as independent censoring violates the competing-risks assumption (e.g., S06 withdrawn at 27h is not randomly lost). | Overestimates resolution probability; not recommended as primary. |
| **Aalen–Johansen / CIF** | Cause-specific cumulative incidence, e.g., CIF_R(t) and CIF_open-state(t). | **Primary recommendation.** Non-parametric, handles competing risks correctly, works with small samples (wide CIs expected), and directly answers Estimand 1 and 2. | Does not naturally include time-varying process covariates without extension. |
| **Cause-specific hazard (CSH)** | Hazard of `Resolve` treating other events as censoring. | Secondary, for etiologic understanding (e.g., does escalation change the cause-specific rate?). | Does not translate directly to absolute 72h probability; same censoring concerns as KM for competing events if misinterpreted. |
| **Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard** | Hazard contributing to CIF_R in presence of competing events. | **Secondary recommendation for covariate adjustment.** Allows inclusion of baseline `Priority`/`Cohort`, but requires proportional-subdistribution assumption. With N=32, point estimates are exploratory; bootstrap CIs are essential. | Small-N instability; cohort is non-randomized, so coefficients are associations, not causal effects. |
| **Restricted Mean Time (RMT)** | Mean open hours up to 72h (workload metric). | **Secondary / operational.** Directly interpretable for staffing: “expected open-case hours per submission.” | Sensitive to the largest observed times (S26 104h); use with 72h restriction. |
| **Landmark analysis** | Survival / CIF from a fixed landmark (e.g., 24h or 48h), conditioning on being open and having reached certain stages. | **Recommended for process-aware covariates.** Solves immortal-time bias for `Escalate` and `Rework`: classify cases by status at 24h, then predict 24h–72h risk. | Reduces sample size further (landmark subsets). |
| **Multi-state model** | Transition hazards between stages (`Screen`  `Review`  `Escalate`/`Rework`  outcomes). | **Conceptually best** for understanding stage hazards (see Section 5), but with 32 cases and sparse transitions (e.g., only S07, S13, S27 have `Rework`), formal estimation is overparameterized. | Recommend as a **structural/diagnostic framework only**, not for formal inference here. |

**Primary recommendation:** Compute the **Aalen–Johansen CIF for `Resolve`** (Est. 1) and the complementary open-state probability (Est. 2), stratified descriptively by `Priority` × `Cohort`. Report point estimates with bootstrap 95% intervals. Use `t_max = 72`.

**Secondary recommendation:** Apply a **24-hour landmark analysis** for process-aware risk stratification. At 24h, classify cases by whether they have reached `Review`, `Escalate`, or `Rework` (using the prefix up to that conceptual point—acknowledging that exact intermediate timestamps are missing). Estimate the conditional 24h72h CIF for resolution and open-state survival. Combine with a **Fine–Gray exploratory model** including `Priority`, `Cohort`, and landmark `Escalated` status, with explicit caveats that cohort is not randomized.

**Justification (data-specific):** A-J does not require proportional hazards and respects the competing nature of `Withdraw`/`Reject`/`Transfer`. It also handles the open cases correctly: those with `age < 72` are right-censored; those with `age  72` contribute to the risk set at 72 without being events. Given the small N, adding many covariates in a multi-state model would produce unstable transition probabilities; the landmark approach balances process awareness with statistical feasibility.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Proposed feature taxonomy (derived from prefixes, `escalated`, `rework_count`, `last_event_gap_h`).**

| Feature | Derivation | Type (baseline / time-varying / concurrent / post-treatment) | Risk / interpretation | Leakage / bias warning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Cohort` (A/B) | `cohort` column | **Baseline** | B introduced automated screening and escalated-policy change; rollout incomplete. | Non-randomized—do not interpret coefficient as causal effect of automation. |
| `Priority` (P1/P2/P3) | `priority` column | **Baseline** | P1 has faster resolution in some cases (S04 29h, S18 12h) but more escalations/transfers (S08, S11, S21, S25, S32). | — |
| `AutoCheck-first` | First activity in `prefix` (`AutoCheck` vs `Screen`) | **Baseline path variant** | Cohort B shows more `AutoCheck`-first paths (S16, S18, S19, S21, S24, S25, S27, S28, S29, S31, S32). | Path may be a consequence of the cohort policy, not an independent predictor. |
| `Escalated` | `escalated` flag; also present in `prefix` (`Escalate`) | **Time-varying** (occurs after `Submit`) | Once escalation occurs, the path often extends (S03, S08, S11, S18, S28). | **Immortal-time risk:** If coded as present at `t=0`, cases are treated as escalated before the event. | `Path length` | `len(prefix.split('>'))` | **Concurrent / state** | Longer paths tend to have longer durations (S03 5 steps 76h, S13 6 steps 110h, S28 5 steps 88h). | Correlated with outcome by construction; predictive but not necessarily causal. |
| `Inactivity gap` | `last_event_gap_h` | **Concurrent (for open/terminal)** | Large gaps predict stall: S26 (72h gap at 104h open), S11 (55h gap at 81h open), S09 (31h gap at 92h open). | For terminal cases, gap is 0 by definition; comparing gap across open vs terminal requires careful conditioning. |

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

I compare three analyses that vary the treatment of transfers, competing outcomes, informative censoring, and cohort-policy interpretation. These are **defensible alternatives**; none is uniquely “correct.”

**Analysis A — Base competing-risks (T1, non-informative censoring for open cases).**
- Transfer = competing event (`code = 4`).
- `Withdraw`/`Reject` = competing events.
- Open cases: right-censored at `duration_or_age_h`.
- Cohort: descriptive stratification only.

*Expected qualitative result:* Cohort B may show a slightly lower 72h resolution CIF than Cohort A because B has two transfers (S21 at 26h, S32 at 96h) that compete out cases, including one (S32) that is long-running. The open-state probability at 72h may be similar because B also has more fast resolutions (S16, S17, S18).

**Analysis B — Transfer as independent censoring (T2) + composite non-resolution endpoint.**
- Transfer = right-censored at `t` (`code = 0`).
- For a secondary view, combine `Withdraw`/`Reject`/`Transfer` into a composite “terminal non-resolution” competing event (`code = 2`).
- Open cases: same censoring rule.

*Expected reversal risk:* If transfers are actually selected for difficulty (not random), censoring them increases the estimated resolution rate for Cohort B relative to A, potentially reversing the base ranking. The composite endpoint makes Cohort B look worse if S32 and S21 are counted as “failures” rather than removed.

**Analysis C — Informative open-case weighting + worst-case transfer + cohort adjustment.**
- Transfer: worst-case (T3). For `t`  72, treat as failure to resolve; for `t` > 72, already observed breach.
- Open cases with `last_event_gap_h` > 48h (S11 55h, S25 48h, S26 72h): apply a worst-case sensitivity where they are counted as unresolved at 72h (not just censored). This is a **bound**, not an estimate.
- Cohort: included as a stratification variable with explicit acknowledgment of non-random assignment and incomplete rollout.

*Expected reversal risk:* Cohort B’s open-state risk could rise substantially because B holds S25 (79h open) and S26 (104h open, 72h gap). If these are weighted as likely failures, any naive conclusion that “Cohort B performs similarly or better” could reverse. Additionally, P1 cases (S11, S25, S32) cluster in high-gap/escalated paths; fairness checks should flag whether P1 receives disproportionately long open times despite high priority.

**Which conclusions could reverse?**
- **Cohort ranking:** Under T1, B might appear comparable to A; under T3 + worst-case open weighting, B could show a higher 72h breach rate due to S25/S26.
- **Priority effect:** P1 cases resolve quickly when they do (S04, S18, S27), but they also dominate escalations and transfers (S08, S11, S21, S25, S32). A model that ignores competing risks might conclude P1 is “fast”; a CIF/model that accounts for competing transfers/rework reveals a bimodal risk profile (fast resolution vs. long stall/transfer).
- **Transfer interpretation:** If T2 is adopted without justification, the estimated internal resolution rate for Cohort B improves artificially because S21 (26h) and S32 (96h) are removed from competition rather than counted.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**From survival outputs to actions (without causal overclaim).**

**Estimand 1 (CIF of resolution by 72h)  SLA governance and forecasting.**
- Report the 72h CIF_R stratified by `Priority` and `Cohort`, with bootstrap confidence intervals. Example interpretation: “In this frozen extract, the observed 72-hour resolution incidence for P1 is X% (CI …), but 3 of 8 P1 cases experienced `Transfer` or `Escalate` before 72h, reducing the observable internal success pool.”
- Do **not** present the cohort difference as “the automated-screening policy improved resolution by Y%.” The rollout was non-randomized and incomplete; the association is descriptive.

**Estimand 2 (open-state probability at 72h)  Workload and alert triggers.**
- Compute the predicted number of open cases at 72h per 100 submissions. For this extract, there are 9 open cases; 4 are past 72h (S09, S11, S25, S26). This is a **bound** on workload backlog.
- **Alert rule (landmark-based):** At 48h, flag cases that are still open, have `escalated = yes`, and `last_event_gap_h` > 24h (e.g., S11 at 81h with 55h gap, S26 with 72h gap). These have high conditional risk of remaining open past 72h.
- **Workload action:** Prioritize manual review for open P1 cases past 48h with `Escalate` in the prefix (S11, S25), and for cases with `Rework` plus long gap (none open here, but S07 and S13 are resolved with rework—suggesting rework is a complexity signal).

**Calibration and fairness checks.**
- **Calibration:** Bin cases by `Priority` × `Cohort` and compare observed counts at 72h (resolved / competing / open-past-72 / open-before-72) against the CIF predictions. With N=32, use leave-one-out cross-validation or bootstrap resampling rather than holdout.
- **Fairness:** Compare the restricted mean open time up to 72h by priority. P1 should not systematically accumulate more open hours than P2/P3 if priority is functioning; in this extract, P1 includes both very fast resolutions (S04 29h, S18 12h) and very long stalls (S11 81h open, S25 79h open, S32 96h transferred). The bimodal pattern suggests priority is associated with path complexity, not uniformly faster service.
- **Cohort fairness:** Check whether Cohort B’s `AutoCheck`-first path is associated with faster resolution only for simple cases (S16, S18, S19) but not for escalated ones (S28 88h, S29 51h open). Any automation benefit must be reported conditionally, not as an overall cohort effect.

**Governance framing.**
- Present risk scores as “predicted probability of remaining open at 72h given observed path to date,” not as “causal effect of escalation.”
- Update estimates as intermediate timestamps become available; the current analysis relies on aggregate duration and prefix order, which limits stage-level precision.

    open_past_72 = ((sub["status_raw"] == "open") & (sub["t"] > 72)).sum()
    open_before_72 = ((sub["status_raw"] == "open") & (sub["t"] <= 72)).sum()
    print(f"\nScenario: {scen}")
    print(f"  Cases with event <=72 (any terminal): {any_term_72}")
    print(f"  Resolved <=72: {resolved_72}")
    print(f"  Open past 72 (observed breach): {open_past_72}")
    print(f"  Open before 72 (censored): {open_before_72}")

# Stratified table (base scenario T1)
base = risk_df[risk_df["scenario"] == "T1_competing"]
strat = base.groupby(["cohort","priority","status_raw"]).size().unstack(fill_value=0)
print("\n=== STRATIFIED COUNTS (T1 BASE) ===")
print(strat)

# Sensitivity comparison: resolution counts at 72h across scenarios
sens = risk_df.groupby("scenario").apply(
    lambda x: pd.Series({
        "resolved_by_72": ((x["t"] <= 72) & (x["event"] == 1)).sum(),
        "any_event_by_72": ((x["t"] <= 72) & (x["event"] != 0)).sum(),
        "open_past_72": ((x["status_raw"] == "open") & (x["t"] > 72)).sum(),
    })

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Missing data needed for stronger inference, prioritized by impact:**

1. **Exact event-level timestamps.** The current data provide only aggregate `duration_or_age_h` and `last_event_gap_h`. Without `Screen`  `Review`  `Escalate` times, stage-specific hazard models are approximate; landmarking can only be approximate.
2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`.** For S08 (68h), S21 (26h), and S32 (96h), we do not know whether the case eventually resolves externally. This is the core ambiguity driving T1/T2/T3.
3. **Observation cutoff / enrollment dates.** We do not know when the extract was frozen relative to `Submit`. This affects whether `open` cases are genuinely recent or long-stalled.
4. **Time-varying workload / staffing.** `last_event_gap_h` hints at stall, but without workload measures we cannot distinguish resource constraint from case difficulty.
5. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance.** Cohort B was not randomized; incomplete rollout means observed B cases may differ systematically from A cases in unmeasured ways (e.g., only compliant units adopted `AutoCheck`).
6. **Repeated measurements / updates.** A single frozen extract prevents dynamic updating; a true survival benchmark would require periodic updates to observe eventual outcomes for open cases.

**Bounds and hypotheses:**
- **Bound:** The 72h breach rate is at least the count of open cases past 72h divided by N (4/32 = 12.5%) plus any resolved cases past 72h that breached the timeline (S03, S13, S20, S28 = 4 more). So at least 8/32 (25%) breached the 72h resolution timeline in some form.
- **Hypothesis:** Cohort B’s automated screening reduces time-to-resolution for simple paths (`AutoCheck`  `Screen`  `Resolve`) but does not reduce escalation/transfer rates for complex cases (S28, S32).
- **Recommendation:** Before deploying causal claims, collect assignment logs, exact intermediate timestamps, and external resolution tracking for transferred cases.
