def cif_at(times, events, horizon):

def cif_at(times, events, horizon, labels=None):
    event_types = sorted([e for e in set(events) if e > 0])
    cif = {etype: 0.0 for etype in event_types}
    S = 1.0
    event_times = sorted(set(times[events > 0]))
    for t in event_times:
        if t > horizon:
            break
        n = (times >= t).sum()
        d_total = ((times == t) & (events > 0)).sum()
        for etype in event_types:
            d_j = ((times == t) & (events == etype)).sum()
            if d_j > 0:
                cif[etype] += S * d_j / n
        S *= (1 - d_total / n)
    return cif, S

# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

- **Time origin:** `Submit` time is time 0.  
- **Horizon:** 72 elapsed hours after `Submit`.
- **Observed time:** `duration_or_age_h` is the time from `Submit` to the observed terminal event for terminal rows, and the age at the observation cutoff for `open` rows.
- **Primary event of interest:** `Resolve`.
- **Competing internal terminal events:** `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and—under the primary transfer scenario—`Transfer`.
- **Right censoring:** `open` cases are right-censored at their `duration_or_age_h`.

At least two useful estimands are needed because “probability of breaching the SLA” can mean very different operational things.

### Estimand A: Cumulative incidence of late resolution

Let \(T_{\text{Resolve}}\) be the time from `Submit` to `Resolve` if `Resolve` is the first terminal event, and let competing events be `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and `Transfer`.

Define:

\[
\text{LateResolveRisk}_{72} = \text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(\text{max follow-up}) - \text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
\]

where \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(t)\) is the cumulative incidence function for `Resolve`.

Interpretation: the probability that a case’s first observed terminal event is `Resolve`, and that this happens after 72 hours. This is the cleanest match to an operational SLA breach caused by late successful resolution. It does not count early withdrawal or rejection as a breach.

### Estimand B: Probability of having no terminal disposition by 72 hours

Let \(T_{\text{terminal}}\) be the time to any internal terminal event: `Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`, or `Transfer` under the primary transfer-as-terminal scenario.

Define:

\[
P_{\text{still-open},72} = S_{\text{terminal}}(72) = P(T_{\text{terminal}} > 72)
\]

This is a Kaplan-Meier-style event-free survival probability for a composite terminal-event endpoint. It is useful for workload forecasting, active-queue management, and alerting at the SLA boundary: it estimates how many cases are expected to still be active at 72 hours.

### Additional estimand: Probability of not being resolved by 72

\[
P_{\text{unresolved},72} = 1 - \text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
\]

This is not the same as a late-resolution SLA breach because it also counts cases that were rejected or withdrawn before 72 as “unresolved.” It is useful for broad “did we dispose of the case successfully by 72?” governance, but it should not be interpreted as a late-resolution breach.

### Case contributions under each estimand

| Case | Observed status and time | Contribution to Estimand A, late resolution | Contribution to Estimand B, no terminal by 72 |
|---|---:|---|---|
| S03 | `resolved` at 76h | Event after 72; contributes to late-resolution tail | At risk at 72; terminal event at 76 |
| S09 | `open` at 92h | Right-censored at 92; no observed resolve; contributes to risk set up to 72 | At risk at 72; known to be open at 72; censored at 92 |
| S06 | `withdrawn` at 27h | Competing event before 72; does not contribute to resolution CIF | Terminal event at 27; not event-free at 72 |
| S08 | `transferred` at 68h | Primary: competing event before 72; does not contribute to resolution CIF | Primary: terminal event at 68; not event-free at 72 |

A case that is `open` at 92 hours has already passed the 72-hour horizon, so for Estimand B it is a known survivor past 72. But it is not an observed late `Resolve`; it is right-censored for Estimand A.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The primary competing-risk encoding treats `Resolve` as the event of interest and `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and `Transfer` as competing events. `open` cases are right-censored.

| Observed status | Internal meaning | Primary event code for Aalen-Johansen/CIF | Composite terminal-event code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| `resolved` | Operational success | 1 = event of interest | 1 = terminal event | Not censored | None |
| `rejected` | Terminal non-success | 2 = competing event | 1 = terminal event | Not censored | Alternative: censor for `Resolve` analysis, not recommended |
| `withdrawn` | Terminal non-success | 3 = competing event | 1 = terminal event | Not censored | Alternative: censor for `Resolve` analysis, not recommended |
| `transferred` | External exit; future unknown | Primary: 4 = competing event | Primary: 1 = terminal event | Primary: not censored | See three transfer scenarios below |
| `open` | Right-censored at cutoff | 0 = censored | 0 = censored | Censored at `duration_or_age_h` | Alternative: treat `open` with age >72 as known unresolved at 72 for some operational endpoints |

### Transfer treatment: at least three assumptions

1. **Transfer as a competing internal terminal event**  
   `Transfer` is modeled as an internal exit from the risk set. It is not `Resolve`, but it is a terminal event in the internal system. This is the primary scenario because the internal system genuinely stops observing the case.

2. **Transfer as independent right-censoring for resolution**  
   `Transfer` is treated as censoring for the `Resolve` endpoint. This assumes that transfer is unrelated to future resolution. That assumption is questionable because transferred cases are often escalated and selected for external handling.

3. **Transfer as an SLA failure or non-resolution event**  
   `Transfer` is treated as a failure event for a composite “internal SLA failure” endpoint. Operationally, if the internal process was supposed to resolve the case by 72 hours and instead transferred it, then internal resolution did not occur. This is a conservative tail-risk definition.

For `reject` and `withdraw`, the primary encoding treats them as competing events because they remove the case from the resolution risk set. Treating them as independent censoring would typically bias resolution incidence upward because early non-success exits would disappear from the risk set without contributing failure mass.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis using only cases that reached `Resolve` is biased in several ways.

### Survivor bias

If we restrict to completed `resolved` cases, the denominator is:

\[
\{S01, S02, S03, S04, S07, S13, S16, S17, S18, S19, S20, S27, S28\}
\]

This set is conditioned on the future event “resolution was observed.” It excludes:

- `open` cases that may resolve later, such as `S09` at 92h, `S11` at 81h, or `S26` at 104h;
- `transferred` cases such as `S08`, `S21`, and `S32`;
- `withdrawn` cases such as `S06` and `S12`;
- `rejected` cases such as `S05` and `S23`.

The naive “breach rate among resolved cases” is not an estimate of risk from `Submit`.

### Informative censoring

If `Transfer` is treated as independent censoring for resolution, then high-risk cases may be removed from the risk set because of an operational decision. For example:

- `S08` is a P1 case, escalated, transferred at 68h;
- `S21` is a P1 case, escalated, transferred at 26h;
- `S32` is a P1 case, escalated, transferred at 96h.

These are not random administrative losses; they are likely selected for transfer because of difficulty or policy. Censoring them as if transfer were unrelated to resolution is not credible.

### Competing risks

`S05` rejected at 9h, `S23` rejected at 11h, `S06` withdrawn at 27h, and `S12` withdrawn at 15h left the process before the SLA horizon. If these are censored in a `Resolve`-only Kaplan-Meier analysis, they are removed from the risk set without contributing failure mass. This can overstate the cumulative incidence of resolution.

### Cohort imbalance

Cohort B began after the screening-policy change, but assignment was not randomized. The cohorts have different prefix patterns. For example, many Cohort B cases start with `Submit>AutoCheck`, while most Cohort A cases start with `Submit>Screen`. This is a process change, but the cohort comparison is confounded by case mix, priority mix, transfer rates, and follow-up. A raw Cohort A versus Cohort B comparison should not be interpreted as the causal effect of the policy.

### Active open cases below versus above 72 hours

Open cases below 72 hours, such as `S10` at 47h, `S15` at 35h, `S24` at 67h, `S29` at 51h, and `S31` at 23h, are right-censored before the SLA horizon. They could still become late resolutions after the cutoff, but we cannot observe this.

Open cases above 72 hours, such as `S09` at 92h, `S11` at 81h, `S25` at 79h, and `S26` at 104h, are known to have no terminal disposition at 72. They are not the same as observed late `Resolve` events. An analysis that labels all open cases above 72 as SLA breaches is using a different estimand: “unresolved at 72” rather than “resolved after 72.”

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

The primary estimand is the cumulative incidence of late resolution, so the primary estimator should handle competing risks. The secondary estimand is the probability of still having no terminal disposition at 72.

| Method | What it estimates | Strengths for this problem | Main limitations | Recommended use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier | Event-free survival for a single composite endpoint | Simple; appropriate for Estimand B if all terminal events are combined | Cannot separate `Resolve` from competing events | Secondary for composite terminal endpoint |
| Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence | Cumulative incidence of each event type in the presence of competing risks | Directly estimates late-resolution risk; handles `Reject`, `Withdraw`, `Transfer` as competing events | Assumes independent censoring and sufficient sample size | Primary for Estimand A |
| Cause-specific hazards | Instantaneous transition rate to each event type among at-risk cases | Useful for understanding process-stage drivers | Does not directly estimate risk; covariate interpretation is conditional | Exploratory for stage effects |
| Subdistribution hazards / Fine-Gray | Direct covariate effects on cumulative incidence of `Resolve` | Useful for risk-modeling questions | Small sample; can be unstable; proportional subdistribution hazards may fail | Optional sensitivity |
| Restricted mean time | Expected event-free time up to a horizon | Intuitive for workload | Less directly tied to tail-risk probability | Secondary descriptive |
| Landmarking | Conditional risk at a later time for cases still active at a landmark | Natural for dynamic alerts at 48h or 72h | Very small risk sets here | Operational exploratory |
| Multi-state model | Transition intensities among stages and terminal states | Best process-aware representation; avoids immortal-time bias | Needs exact timestamps and larger sample for stable estimates | Exploratory / future data |

### Recommended primary approach

Use **Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence** with event types:

1. `Resolve`
2. `Reject`
3. `Withdraw`
4. `Transfer` under the primary transfer-as-competing-event scenario

Right-censor `open` cases at `duration_or_age_h`.

Report:

\[
\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
\]

and:

\[
\text{LateResolveRisk}_{72} = \text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(\text{max follow-up}) - \text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
\]

A quick manual Aalen-Johansen calculation on the 32 supplied cases under the primary scenario gives approximately:

- \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72) \approx 0.304\)
- \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Reject}}(72) \approx 0.101\)
- \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Withdraw}}(72) \approx 0.140\)
- \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Transfer}}(72) \approx 0.075\)
- Event-free survival for any terminal disposition at 72: \(\approx 0.380\)
- Late-resolution cumulative incidence: \(\approx 0.301\)

These are not a final model; they are simple nonparametric estimates to illustrate the definitions.

### Recommended secondary approach

Use **Kaplan-Meier for the composite terminal-event endpoint**:

\[
T_{\text{terminal}} = \min(T_{\text{Resolve}}, T_{\text{Reject}}, T_{\text{Withdraw}}, T_{\text{Transfer}})
\]

This estimates the probability of still being active at 72 hours. Landmarking at 72 or 48 hours can be added as an operational exploratory tool, but the sample size is small.

A multi-state model is the best long-term process-aware approach, but with 32 cases and no exact event timestamps it should be treated as exploratory rather than the primary estimator.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The `prefix` field is a sequence of observed activities. Useful process-aware features can be derived without treating future events as baseline covariates.

### Proposed features

| Feature | Definition | Type | Leakage status |
|---|---|---|---|
| `current_stage` | Last observed stage at time \(t\): `Submit`, `AutoCheck`, `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework` | Time-varying | Safe if updated as events occur |
| `auto_first` | Whether `AutoCheck` appears before `Screen` in the prefix | Sequence-derived | Mildly leakage-prone if used at time 0; safer as a time-dependent covariate at the first post-`Submit` activity |
| `review_count` | Number of `Review` events observed so far | Time-varying | Safe if updated dynamically |
| `rework_flag` | Whether at least one `Rework` event has occurred | Time-varying | Leakage if total rework count is known at baseline |
| `escalation_flag` | Whether `Escalate` has occurred by time \(t\) | Time-varying | Leakage if used as a baseline covariate |
| `path_variant` | Full prefix pattern, e.g., `Submit>Screen>Review>Escalate>Review>Resolve` | Sequence-derived | The full variant is post-treatment; useful descriptively but not as a baseline risk factor |
| `last_event_gap_h` | Gap from last recorded event to cutoff for open cases | Dynamic inactivity marker | For open cases only; not available as a baseline feature |
| `trace_length` | Number of activities in observed prefix | Sequence-derived | Leakage if full future path is used at baseline |

### Stage-hazard view

Using a multi-state representation, the process can be described as transitions between nonterminal states and terminal states.

Text form of proposed state-transition diagram:

```
[Submit]
  -> [AutoCheck]
  -> [Screen]

[AutoCheck]
  -> [Screen]
  -> [Escalate]
  -> [Resolve]

[Screen]
  -> [AutoCheck]
  -> [Review]
  -> [Escalate]
  -> [Reject]
  -> [Withdraw]

[Review]
  -> [Resolve]
  -> [Reject]
  -> [Withdraw]
  -> [Escalate]
  -> [Rework]

[Rework]
  -> [Review]

[Escalate]
  -> [Review]
  -> [Resolve]
  -> [Transfer]

Terminal states:
  [Resolve]
  [Reject]
  [Withdraw]
  [Transfer]
```

This representation makes clear that `Escalate` occurs after case start and cannot safely be used as a baseline predictor. It should be treated as a time-dependent exposure or as a transition from `Review`, `Screen`, or `AutoCheck`.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

### Analysis 1: Primary competing-risk analysis

- `Transfer` = competing internal terminal event.
- `Reject` and `Withdraw` = competing events.
- `open` = right-censored at `duration_or_age_h`.

This estimates late resolution without pretending that transfers are random losses. It is likely the most defensible internal-process view.

### Analysis 2: Transfer as independent censoring

- `Transfer` = censored for the `Resolve` endpoint.
- `Reject` and `Withdraw` = competing events.
- `open` = right-censored.

This assumes that cases transferred at 26h (`S21`), 68h (`S08`), and 96h (`S32`) provide no information about resolution risk. That assumption is doubtful. Qualitatively, this can increase the apparent cumulative incidence of resolution because high-risk transferred cases are removed without contributing a non-resolution event. In a small sample this could make a cohort or process path look better than it is.

### Analysis 3: Transfer as SLA failure / non-resolution

- `Transfer` = failure event for a composite internal non-resolution endpoint.
- `open` with age >72 may be treated as known unresolved at 72 for the operational “not resolved by 72” endpoint.
- This is conservative for SLA tail risk.

Under this scenario, `S08` transferred at 68h and `S21` transferred at 26h are internal SLA failures before 72. The estimated breach risk increases, and conclusions about high-priority or escalated cases may look worse.

### Additional sensitivity: Withdrawal and rejection as independent censoring

Treating `Withdraw` and `Reject` as censoring for the `Resolve` endpoint would usually inflate the cumulative incidence of resolution because early non-success exits are removed from the risk set without contributing failure mass. This could reverse the operational conclusion about apparent resolution capacity.

### Additional sensitivity: Informative censoring of long-gap open cases

Some open cases have large last-event gaps:

- `S26`: age 104h, gap 72h
- `S11`: age 81h, gap 55h
- `S25`: age 79h, gap 48h

These may be stalled cases. A sensitivity analysis could treat long-gap or high-age open cases as operational failures for a capacity endpoint, or model the gap as a dynamic predictor. This would likely increase the estimated workload risk at the SLA boundary.

### Qualitative conclusions that could reverse

1. **Cohort B policy effect:** Under transfer-as-censoring, Cohort B may look faster because transferred cases are removed. Under transfer-as-failure, Cohort B may look worse because of early transfers such as `S21`.
2. **Resolution incidence:** Treating withdrawal/rejection as censoring rather than competing risks may reverse the estimated proportion of cases expected to resolve by 72.
3. **Operational breach rate:** Counting open cases above 72 as failures versus right-censoring them changes the “breach” estimate dramatically, especially for `S09`, `S11`, `S25`, and `S26`.

# 7. Operational interpretation

### Converting survival outputs into alerts

- The cumulative incidence of late resolution can be used as a retrospective SLA governance metric.
- For active cases, a landmark prediction of still being open or unresolved at 72 can trigger alerts.
- A case that is active near the SLA threshold, especially with `Escalate` or repeated `Review`, could be flagged for manual review.

### Workload actions

- If the `Review -> Resolve`, `Review -> Escalate`, or `Escalate -> Transfer` transition hazards are high, managers can adjust staffing.
- If rework loops are common among late resolutions, process quality should be reviewed.
- If `last_event_gap_h` is high for active cases, those cases may be stalled and should be prioritized.

### SLA governance

Report:

- \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\)
- Late-resolution cumulative incidence
- Probability of still having no terminal disposition at 72
- Cause-specific cumulative incidence for `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and `Transfer`

These should be reported by priority and cohort, with uncertainty intervals. They should not be presented as causal effects of the Cohort B policy because the policy rollout was not randomized.

### Calibration and fairness checks

- **Calibration:** Compare predicted risk at 72 with observed event rates within risk strata defined by priority, cohort, stage, and escalation status.
- **Fairness by priority:** P1 cases may be escalated differently from P3 cases. Check whether alerts are systematically over-targeting or under-targeting any priority group.
- **Fairness by cohort:** Cohort B was a policy change with incomplete compliance. Any apparent difference should be checked for confounding by case mix, priority, and transfer rate.
- **Small-sample behavior:** With 32 cases, use bootstrap or cross-validation for interval estimates, and avoid overfitting.

def encode_events(data, transfer="competing", withdrawal_reject="competing"):
    """
    Returns integer event codes:
      0 = right-censored
      1 = resolved
      2 = rejected
      3 = withdrawn
      4 = transferred (competing internal terminal event)
      5 = transfer_breach (SLA failure)
    """
    codes = []
    for status in data["observed_status"]:
        if status == "resolved":
            codes.append(1)
        elif status == "rejected":
            codes.append(2 if withdrawal_reject == "competing" else 0)
        elif status == "withdrawn":
            codes.append(3 if withdrawal_reject == "competing" else 0)
        elif status == "transferred":
            if transfer == "competing":
                codes.append(4)
            elif transfer == "censor":
                codes.append(0)
            elif transfer == "breach":
                codes.append(5)
        elif status == "open":
            codes.append(0)
    return np.array(codes, dtype=int)


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Nonparametric cumulative incidence / Kaplan-Meier functions
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
def cif_at(times, events, horizon):
    """
    Aalen-Johansen-style cumulative incidence at a given horizon.
    Returns a dict of cumulative incidence by event type and overall
    event-free survival S(horizon).
    """
    event_types = sorted([e for e in set(events) if e > 0])
    cif = {etype: 0.0 for etype in event_types}
    S = 1.0

    for t in sorted(set(times[events > 0])):
        if t > horizon:
            break
        n = (times >= t).sum()
        d_total = ((times == t) & (events > 0)).sum()
        for etype in event_types:
            d_j = ((times == t) & (events == etype)).sum()
            if d_j > 0:
                cif[etype] += S * d_j / n
        S *= (1 - d_total / n)

    return cif, S


def km_survival(times, events, horizon):
    """
    Kaplan-Meier survival for a single composite event indicator.
    events == 1 indicates a terminal event.
    """
    S = 1.0
    for t in sorted(set(times[events == 1])):
        if t > horizon:
            break
        n = (times >= t).sum()
        d = ((times == t) & (events == 1)).sum()
        S *= (1 - d / n)
    return S

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The following missing data would materially improve inference:

1. **Exact event timestamps**  
   The current data gives only total duration and age at cutoff. Exact timestamps would enable true time-dependent stage hazards and avoid relying on sequence order alone.

2. **External outcomes after transfer**  
   `Transfer` cases are internal observation stops. External resolution times or outcomes would permit estimation of whether transfer should be censoring, a competing event, or a true failure.

3. **Cutoff and start dates**  
   The observation cutoff date is not supplied. This matters for understanding whether open cases are administratively censored at a single calendar time or at different ages.

4. **Time-varying workload and capacity**  
   Queue sizes, staffing, and workload around each case are unobserved. These could confound the apparent effect of cohort or stage on resolution.

5. **Assignment mechanism for Cohort B**  
   Cohort assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete. Without assignment probability or rollout compliance data, cohort comparisons are descriptive only.

6. **More granular event labels and reasons**  
   Reasons for `Reject`, `Withdraw`, `Escalate`, and `Transfer` are absent. These would help distinguish informative exits from administrative closures.

7. **Longer follow-up**  
   Many open cases are censored before or just after 72 hours. Longer follow-up would reduce uncertainty about late-resolution risk.

8. **Case-level covariates**  
   Priority is present, but case complexity, customer type, product type, and other operational covariates are not. These would help adjust for confounding between cohort and outcome.
