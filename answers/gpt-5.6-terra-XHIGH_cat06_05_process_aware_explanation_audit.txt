## 2. Process-model consistency checks

A credible audit should evaluate both the data lineage and the process semantics. A process-conformant event is still unusable if it was learned after `t0`; conversely, a temporally available field may be a proxy rather than a causal driver.

1. **Define an unambiguous scoring cutoff.**  
   For each claim, define `t0` as the event position immediately after the **first** `Coverage Check Completed`. Store:

   - claim ID;
   - cutoff event ID;
   - event occurrence timestamp;
   - reliable source sequence index;
   - event ingestion/publication timestamp;
   - feature computation timestamp;
   - feature source snapshot/version.

   A feature is admissible only if all of the information needed to compute it was available at or before `t0`. Event time alone is insufficient: a document may have an earlier business timestamp but arrive in the warehouse later.

2. **Perform prefix-only feature recomputation.**  
   Recompute every training and scoring row from an event prefix satisfying:

   ```text
   (event_time, sequence_index) <= (t0_time, t0_sequence_index)
   AND event_available_at <= t0_available_at
   ```

   Where the sequence index is missing for tied timestamps, use a conservative rule: do not include tied events whose order relative to Coverage Check Completed cannot be established. The model score should be invariant if all events after `t0` are deleted or permuted. This “future-suffix invariance” test is a strong leakage test.

3. **Apply feature-lineage tests.**  
   For every feature, inspect SQL, upstream dependencies, and feature-store metadata for:

   - use of case closure timestamps;
   - unbounded trace aggregates;
   - fields populated at closure;
   - joins against extraction date rather than event time;
   - nightly snapshots;
   - slowly changing dimensions joined at extraction date;
   - use of “final,” “last,” “total,” or “anywhere” fields.

   `full_trace_variant_id`, `total_case_duration_hours`, `appeal_seen_anywhere`, and `num_document_requests_total` should fail this test by design.

4. **Validate the observed prefix against the normative process model.**  
   At the stated `t0`, the observed prefix is only:

   ```text
   Claim Registered -> Coverage Check Completed
   ```

   The validation suite should classify referenced activities as follows:

   - **Observed and admissible:** Claim Registered and the first Coverage Check Completed.
   - **Branch-dependent future possibilities:** Damage Assessment Completed for the fast-track branch, or Documents Requested for the evidence branch.
   - **Conditionally future possible:** Fraud Investigation Opened, but only after a trigger. A future fraud opening is not evidence that the trigger was known at `t0`.
   - **Not enabled in the displayed prefix:** Documents Received and Documents Validated, because no Documents Requested event has yet occurred.
   - **Impossible at `t0`:** Settlement Decision Recorded, Appeal, and Claim Closed.
   - **Process-order violation even retrospectively:** `Claim Closed -> Damage Assessment Completed`, because closure follows a final decision, and a decision follows damage assessment.

   A future-possible activity must not be treated as an observed condition. “Could happen next” is not equivalent to “has happened.”

5. **Use declarative and lifecycle constraints for activity order.**  
   Useful checks include:

   - `Claim Registered` precedes first `Coverage Check Completed`.
   - A Documents Received event must have an earlier unmatched Documents Requested event.
   - Documents Validated must follow Documents Received.
   - Damage Assessment Completed must precede Settlement Decision Recorded.
   - Appeal may occur only after a Partial or Deny decision.
   - A second Settlement Decision Recorded may occur only after a valid appeal path.
   - Claim Closed must follow withdrawal or the final decision.

   These checks should allow fraud investigation to run in parallel with evidence collection or damage assessment; it should not be falsely forced into a single sequential lane.

6. **Handle repeated Settlement Decision events explicitly.**  
   A coarse `Settlement Decision Recorded` label is not necessarily duplicate noise. The validation must assign decision ordinals:

   - `decision_1` occurs after damage assessment;
   - if `decision_1` is Partial or Deny, an Appeal may occur;
   - a later `decision_2` may then legitimately occur;
   - Claim Closed follows the final decision or withdrawal.

   Thus, `Appeal -> Settlement Decision Recorded` is a semantically legitimate later-stage pattern if the preceding Partial/Deny decision exists. It is nonetheless leaked at the early prediction point.

7. **Treat same-timestamp relations as partial orders, not observed sequences.**  
   For Case Z, the equal timestamp and missing sequence index do not justify inferring `Claim Closed -> Damage Assessment Completed`. The relation is ambiguous and conflicts with mandatory process precedence. The audit should:

   - prefer a reliable source sequence index;
   - otherwise retain a timestamp tie as unordered;
   - check source lifecycle states and audit logs;
   - prohibit sequence features derived from the tie.

8. **Use process-mining diagnostics carefully.**  
   Alignments can identify deviations from the normative model; footprints and declarative constraints can identify precedence and co-existence violations; and DFGs can reveal common observed transitions. Their limitations matter:

   - an alignment is only as reliable as the event labels, timestamps, and process model;
   - DFGs are descriptive frequency summaries, not normative rules;
   - a frequent edge can result from timestamp ties, batching, coarse labels, or a repeated-decision appeal loop;
   - conformance does not prove that an event was available at `t0`.

   Process conformance is therefore a necessary audit dimension, not a substitute for point-in-time lineage.

## 3. Alternative feature interpretations

The following are interpretation classes rather than mutually exclusive approval labels. A feature can be temporally valid and still be a proxy requiring fairness review.

| Class | Signals | What may be claimed | What may not be claimed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defensible predictive signal | `initial_damage_complexity`; `elapsed_since_registration_at_t0`; possibly `initial_reserve` after frozen pre-`t0` lineage is proven; corrected prior-history count after SQL repair | These variables were available in the prefix and have an empirical association with breach risk in a properly time-split evaluation. | That complexity, reserve, or claimant history independently causes delay, or that changing the feature would change the outcome. |
| Process-state or congestion signal | `queue_length_at_t0`; a historically reconstructed team or role assigned at `t0`; verified operational capacity indicators; later-stage prefix document state in a dynamically rescored model | Queue length can describe current workload and help forecast operational risk. A current assignment may describe routing state. These can be useful for capacity planning. | That queue length or team assignment is the proven cause of a particular breach. Operational interventions require experimental or quasi-experimental evaluation. |
| Proxy requiring fairness and robustness review | `claimant_region`; `claimant_channel`; possibly initial reserve and corrected prior-claim history | These may improve prediction if they are available at `t0` and stable across time. Their use can be evaluated transparently for calibration and error disparities. | That a region, channel, socioeconomic characteristic, language, claimant behavior, or demographic attribute is an intrinsic “delay driver.” They should not be presented as a claimant-blaming operational explanation. |
| Drift/calendar proxy | `calendar_week`; policy-period, holiday, and seasonal indicators | Calendar variables can help forecast staffing or policy-period demand if validated out of time. They may support aggregate planning. | That a calendar week itself causes claims to breach, or that a relation observed during one policy/holiday period will persist. |
| Unusable until lineage is repaired, or permanently unusable in this form | `total_case_duration_hours`, `appeal_seen_anywhere`, `final_decision`, `num_document_requests_total`, `full_trace_variant_id`, all reported future sequence patterns, `final_handler_team`, `last_resource_role`, current `risk_score_current`, current prior-count SQL, nightly `same_day_document_count_snapshot`, extraction-date employee experience | Some can be replaced by different, point-in-time features: elapsed duration instead of total duration; assigned team at `t0` instead of final team; a versioned risk score at `t0`; document counts up to `t0`; or prior claims strictly before `t0`. | That the existing retrospective values are early warning signals. Renaming a complete-trace field as an “indicator” does not repair leakage. |

There are legitimate prefix-derived process signals at this prediction point: frozen intake complexity, elapsed time since registration, a safely as-of queue snapshot, and possibly documented intake fields. The observed prefix itself contains no evidence of document rework, fraud investigation, a decision, an appeal, or closure. In particular, a full-trace document-request count is not an “early warning” merely because it becomes important in retrospective SHAP output.

Predictive validity, causal interpretation, and operational actionability should be kept separate:

- **Predictive validity:** Does a point-in-time feature improve out-of-time prediction without leakage?
- **Causal interpretation:** Would an intervention on the feature change settlement timeliness, after accounting for confounding and process feedback?
- **Operational actionability:** Is there a feasible intervention, such as staffing or routing, with evidence that it improves outcomes?
- **Fairness review:** Does use of the feature, including proxies, create unacceptable group disparities, missingness differences, or differential calibration?

## 5. Recommendations for explanation validation

1. **Immediately quarantine leaked features and retrain rather than merely hiding their explanations.**  
   Remove complete-trace, post-closure, final-state, nightly end-of-day, and extraction-date fields from both training and scoring. A model trained on leaked features is invalid even if its local explanation UI suppresses those features.

2. **Build a point-in-time feature-store contract.**  
   Every feature should carry at least:

   - entity and claim ID;
   - feature value;
   - feature effective time;
   - source event time;
   - source availability/publication time;
   - feature computation time;
   - source snapshot/version;
   - feature-definition version;
   - cutoff event ID and sequence index.

   Immutable intake fields should be explicitly frozen. Mutable fields such as assignment, role, reserve, risk score, and employee experience require historical versions or slowly changing dimensions.

3. **Replace retrospective SQL with cutoff-aware joins.**  
   Enforce `available_at <= t0` for all sources. For prior claims, require `claim_created_at < t0`, exclude the current claim, and use claimant-specific identity resolution available at the cutoff. Replace nearest-snapshot joins with the latest prior available snapshot.

4. **Run prefix-only recomputation in training, backtesting, and explanation generation.**  
   Construct each historical training row exactly as it would have existed immediately after the first Coverage Check Completed. Add automated suffix-invariance tests: appending, removing, or shuffling future events must not alter the score or local attribution.

5. **Add process-conformance tests to the data pipeline.**  
   Implement lifecycle and declarative checks for document cycles, assessment-before-decision, appeal eligibility, repeated-decision semantics, and closure ordering. Track violations separately from model features. Missing sequence indexes, timestamp ties, and impossible reversals should create data-quality flags rather than sequence-pattern features.

6. **Recalibrate and re-evaluate after leakage removal.**  
   Use temporal holdouts, not random claim splits. Reassess discrimination, calibration curves, Brier score, threshold performance, and workload impact after retraining. The repaired model will likely have lower apparent retrospective accuracy but higher real deployment credibility.

7. **Require explanation stability and completeness checks.**  
   Store complete per-feature local attributions rather than bundled “other features.” Test whether explanations remain stable under harmless changes to future suffixes, small queue-snapshot timing shifts, equivalent timestamp ordering, and feature imputation. Report uncertainty where lineage or ordering is unresolved.

8. **Conduct proxy and fairness analysis before operationalizing region, channel, reserve, or history.**  
   Evaluate calibration, false-positive rates, false-negative rates, missingness, and explanation distributions across relevant protected or vulnerable groups and operational segments. Test whether regional effects disappear after accounting for repair-network coverage, channel, language support, or capacity. Do not use proxy features as claimant-level causal explanations.

9. **Monitor schema, lineage, and process drift continuously.**  
   Monitor missing sequence indexes, same-timestamp frequency, changed activity labels, changing process variants, snapshot freshness, post-closure field population rates, feature availability lag, policy changes, holiday periods, and queue-source changes. Revalidate model calibration and feature semantics whenever process routing, policy, staffing, or warehouse schemas change.
