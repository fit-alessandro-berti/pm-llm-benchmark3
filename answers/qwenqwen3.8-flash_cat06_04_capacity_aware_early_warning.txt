## 1. Decision rule

**Objective:** allocate scarce Senior Diagnostic Swarm slots to maximize portfolio-level avoided breach value while respecting process-stage eligibility, alert persistence, calibration uncertainty, intervention-effect uncertainty, false-alarm burden, queue spillovers, non-monetary obligations, and fairness.

**Core principle:** the model probability `p_now` is an observational baseline-risk estimate, not a post-intervention probability and not a causal effect. A slot is justified only if the expected causal risk reduction, after calibration and uncertainty adjustments, exceeds the direct cost, false-alarm burden, and expected harm to untreated queue cases.

### A. Hard eligibility gates

A case is eligible for a swarm only if all of the following hold:

1. **Process-stage eligibility**
   - Current stage is `Triage` or `Diagnosis Started`.
   - Cases at or after `Diagnosis Complete` are ineligible because the swarm has near-zero effect.  
   - Example: I-03 is high-risk but ineligible.

2. **Time feasibility**
   - Time to SLA deadline must be at least 1.0 hour, so that a 45-minute swarm can plausibly change diagnosis or resolution work before the deadline.
   - If time is shorter, use lower-cost actions: information request, escalation to current assignee, customer follow-up, or pre-swarm preparation.

3. **Intervention history gate**
   - No second swarm on the same case unless a **materially new event** has occurred.
   - A materially new event includes:
     - new customer diagnostic log or artifact;
     - new error, trace, configuration change, or data correction;
     - change in SLA clock or regulatory status;
     - assignment/team change that materially changes diagnosis path;
     - confirmed data-quality correction that changes the baseline risk estimate.
   - Repeated alerts caused only by model refreshes are not evidence of new causal state.

4. **Data-quality gate**
   - If a major feature is missing or imputed, the case is placed in a **conditional wait** state until the feature is validated or replaced.
   - Example: I-06 has a sharp increase but a missing live queue feature. Do not spend a scarce slot on an imputation artifact unless the queue signal is confirmed.

5. **Minimum evidence-change rule for repeated alerts**
   - For a repeated alert without a materially new event:
     - suppress the alert for swarm allocation if the calibrated probability change is small, defined as  
       `p_adj = p_adj_low(now) - p_adj_low(previous) < 0.10`.
     - do not increase priority merely because the case has been alerted multiple times.
   - For a repeated alert with a materially new event:
     - allow re-entry even if `p_now` is flat or slightly lower, because the causal state may have changed.
   - For a first alert:
     - require adjusted baseline risk floor `p_adj_low >= 0.35` and median estimated absolute risk reduction `ARR_med >= 0.10`, unless there is a regulatory/safety override.

### B. Calibration and probability robustification

Use segment-specific calibration adjustments and confidence discounts. These are not “true” corrected probabilities; they are decision intervals.

| Segment / context | Calibration adjustment | Confidence multiplier |
|---|---:|---:|
| SME | Underprediction: add `+0.04` to `+0.09` to `p` | 0.9 |
| Strategic Enterprise | Overprediction: subtract `0.05` to `0.12` from `p` | 0.9 |
| Public Sector | Broadly acceptable, moderate sample: widen by about `±0.05` | 1.0 |
| Non-Native-Language Support | Sparse calibration and effect uncertainty: widen by about `±0.10`; do not assume stable bias | 0.7 |
| Major feature imputed / data-quality issue | Widen additional `±0.15` and require validation | 0.5 |

For each case, compute:

- `p_adj_low`: conservative lower bound on baseline breach risk.
- `p_adj_high`: conservative upper bound on baseline breach risk.

Use `p_adj_low` for risk floors and tie-breakers. Do not use `p_adj_high` alone to justify intervention.

### C. Effect and economic scoring

For each eligible case, use the supplied absolute risk reduction range as uncertain causal effect bounds:

- `ARR_low`
- `ARR_med`
- `ARR_high`

Define a conservative direct economic benefit net of case-specific false-alarm burden, before swarm cost and spillover:

```text
Direct benefit net of burden  ARR × Value of avoiding breach  False-alarm burden
```

Define expected direct net benefit at the median effect:

```text
Median direct net = ARR_med × Value  €450  False-alarm burden
```

Add queue externality:

```text
Expected spillover harm per slot  6 untreated diagnosis-stage cases × p_spill × average untreated case value
```

Base spillover assumption:

- `p_spill = 0.01–0.03` per used slot.
- Use midpoint `0.02`.
- Use average untreated diagnosis-stage case value of about €20k, proxied from visible diagnosis-stage cases and queue assumptions.
- Therefore base spillover harm per slot  `6 × 0.02 × €20k = €2.4k`.

A deployable ranking score is:

```text
Risk-adjusted score =
  Confidence multiplier ×
  [
    Median direct net
     expected spillover share
     uncertainty penalty
  ]
```

The uncertainty penalty should be larger when:

- the ARR interval is wide;
- the segment calibration is sparse;
- the alert is repeated without new evidence;
- key features are imputed;
- the false-alarm burden is high;
- the case is Strategic Enterprise and the model has recently overpredicted.

Do not use a single `p_now` threshold. The rule is:

1. Pass hard gates.
2. Adjust and widen probabilities by segment and data quality.
3. Require material evidence change for repeated alerts.
4. Rank by risk-adjusted expected net benefit after spillover.
5. Apply fairness and non-monetary overrides.
6. Reserve capacity when the marginal case has weak positive or negative expected net benefit and there is meaningful regulatory/uncertainty option value.

### D. Portfolio capacity rule

- Use a slot only if the selected portfolio has positive expected net benefit after spillover.
- If the fourth marginal case has negative or near-zero expected net benefit after spillover, keep the slot in reserve.
- Keep one slot reserved when an unlisted regulatory or high-obligation incident is plausible before the next decision cycle.
- If queue congestion is high, increase the spillover estimate and reduce automatic allocation.
- If a case has regulatory, safety, or contractual obligations not captured by economic value, allow a human override with logged rationale.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

### A. Cool-down

- After a swarm is used on a case, impose a **minimum 4-hour cool-down** or until `Diagnosis Complete`, whichever occurs first.
- A second swarm during cool-down is forbidden unless there is a materially new event and a supervisor logs the reason.
- If a case remains high-risk after a swarm, prefer non-swarm actions:
  - request missing information;
  - change assignee;
  - customer validation pre-work;
  - escalation to current diagnosis owner;
  - re-run prediction after data correction.

### B. Suppression

- Repeated alerts caused only by model refreshes are suppressed for capacity allocation.
- A case may appear in the alert list, but it does not receive priority unless:
  - there is a materially new event; or
  - calibrated risk changes by at least `0.10`; or
  - time-to-deadline becomes critical and adjusted risk is very high; or
  - a regulatory/safety override applies.
- Suppressed alerts remain on a watchlist and are re-evaluated each cycle.

### C. Escalation

Escalate a case from wait to active candidate if any of the following occurs:

1. A materially new event arrives, such as a new customer log or confirmed data correction.
2. The adjusted lower-bound risk crosses a high-risk band, for example `p_adj_low >= 0.70`, and time to deadline is under 4 hours.
3. The case has regulatory, safety, or contractual obligations not captured by monetary value.
4. Capacity is released because a reserved slot is not needed.
5. A previous data-quality hold is resolved and the new feature confirms the risk signal.

### D. Re-entry logic

A case that was suppressed or denied due capacity can re-enter if:

- the intervention window is still open;
- evidence materially changes;
- queue congestion has decreased;
- a slot is released;
- fairness monitoring indicates systematic under-service for that segment or value band.

Re-entry does not mean automatic intervention. The case must be re-scored using the current adjusted risk, ARR range, false-alarm burden, and spillover estimate.

| I-05 | Third alert; no new event since previous alert | `p_prev 0.55  p_now 0.57`; SME-adjusted change is tiny | **Suppress; do not intervene now** | The repeated alert is likely refresh noise. Even though SME underprediction and ARR are favorable, there is no new causal evidence. Spending a scarce slot would reward alert fatigue and impose spillover harm. |
| I-07 | Third alert; new customer diagnostic log uploaded | `p_prev 0.75  p_now 0.72`, but new artifact changes the evidence state | **Eligible, but wait unless reserve released or fairness/urgency override** | The new log is materially new information, so the repeated-alert suppression rule does not apply. However, monetary value is low, so it ranks below I-04, I-02, and I-09 unless time urgency or non-monetary customer impact justifies override. |

The key distinction is **evidence change**, not alert count. I-05 has repetition without new information. I-07 has repetition with new information, even though the point prediction moved slightly downward.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

### A. Value-based prioritization may starve lower-value cases

**Risk:** ranking by `ARR × value` systematically favors Strategic Enterprise or high-contract-value cases and under-serves SME, Public Sector, or low-value but high-risk cases.

**Safeguards:**

1. Use a **fairness floor**: if two or more cases have overlapping risk-adjusted scores, prioritize the case from a segment or value band that has received fewer interventions over the last 30 days.
2. Track intervention receipt by value band:
   - low value: `< €10k`
   - medium value: `€10k–€50k`
   - high value: `> €50k`
3. If low-value cases have high adjusted risk and positive expected net but are repeatedly denied due capacity, create a small reserved allocation or alternative lower-cost intervention path.
4. Require human review before denying a low-value case solely because of low monetary value when there is customer-safety, regulatory, or contractual obligation.

### B. Non-Native-Language Support uncertainty

**Risk:** sparse calibration and effect estimates for Non-Native-Language Support could cause both over-intervention and under-intervention. The segment may be systematically disadvantaged if uncertainty is treated as a reason to avoid allocation.

**Safeguards:**

1. Apply a confidence discount to Non-Native-Language Support scores, but do not automatically exclude them.
2. Require specialist pre-brief for selected Non-Native-Language cases to confirm that the alert reflects a genuine diagnosis blocker rather than language or data-access artifacts.
3. Use conservative ARR bounds and monitor outcomes separately.
4. Add a temporary **fairness test allocation**: when capacity is not binding, allocate some borderline Non-Native-Language cases to improve causal-effect estimation.
5. Ensure language support and validation steps are included in the intervention workflow so that the swarm does not create additional customer burden.

### C. Differential calibration across segments

**Risk:** Strategic Enterprise overprediction may waste slots on cases that do not need intervention; SME underprediction may delay intervention until too late; Public Sector moderate sample may hide drift.

**Safeguards:**

1. Use segment-specific calibration intervals rather than raw `p_now`.
2. Monitor calibration by segment weekly.
3. If a segment’s bias moves outside its expected range, suspend automatic ranking for that segment and require human review.
4. For Strategic Enterprise, require stronger evidence change or higher ARR before overriding the downward calibration adjustment.
5. For SME, account for underprediction but do not treat every SME case as high priority; ARR, false-alarm burden, and time window still matter.

### D. Selective intervention changes future training labels

**Risk:** intervened cases are less likely to breach because of the intervention, not because the original model was correct. If the model retrains on these outcomes without treatment indicators, it may underestimate risk for similar future cases.

**Safeguards:**

1. Log a treatment indicator:
   - swarm used;
   - swarm suppressed;
   - swarm denied due capacity;
   - data-quality hold;
   - human override.
2. Do not train the baseline breach model on post-intervention outcomes as if they were natural outcomes.
3. Use treatment-aware modeling:
   - predict pre-intervention baseline risk;
   - estimate intervention effect separately;
   - avoid using intervened outcomes to recalibrate baseline risk without adjustment.
4. Maintain a small randomized exploration set for eligible borderline cases to estimate causal effect.
5. Use counterfactual evaluation: compare intervened cases with similar untreated cases, accounting for stage, time-to-deadline, segment, and congestion.

### E. Spillover harms may fall systematically on untreated groups

**Risk:** each swarm delays other diagnosis-stage cases. If untreated cases are systematically lower-value, SME, Public Sector, or non-native-language cases, the intervention policy may transfer harm to already under-served groups.

**Safeguards:**

1. Include expected spillover harm directly in the allocation score.
2. Monitor untreated diagnosis-stage breach rates before and after swarm allocation.
3. If untreated cases in a segment show increased breach probability, throttle swarm use in that queue period.
4. Use congestion-aware allocation:
   - when diagnosis queue delay is high, reserve more slots;
   - prefer cases where the swarm can redirect work without removing a specialist from a critical queue bottleneck.
5. Report fairness impact by:
   - segment;
   - value band;
   - language support;
   - process stage;
   - untreated case breach rate.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

### A. Operational monitoring

**Metrics:**

- slots used per day;
- slots reserved and reserve utilization;
- swarms attempted after `Diagnosis Complete` — should be zero;
- time from alert to swarm start;
- specialist interruption minutes;
- aggregate delay added to diagnosis queue;
- number of repeated alerts suppressed;
- number of data-quality holds;
- human overrides and reasons.

**Cadence:** real-time dashboard plus daily operational review.

**Triggers:**

- Any swarm after `Diagnosis Complete`: fix stage gate immediately.
- Reserve slot unused for more than 24 hours with no regulatory incident: release to next-ranked eligible case.
- Average specialist delay per slot exceeds 20 minutes: reduce automatic allocation and increase reserve.

### B. Predictive monitoring

**Metrics:**

- calibration by segment: observed breach rate vs adjusted probability interval;
- SME bias: expected underprediction `+0.04` to `+0.09`;
- Strategic Enterprise bias: expected overprediction `0.05` to `0.12`;
- Public Sector coverage: actual risk within adjusted interval;
- Non-Native-Language Support sample size and interval coverage;
- feature missingness and imputation rate;
- drift in `p_now - p_prev`;
- alert persistence rate.

**Cadence:** weekly calibration report; monthly model review.

**Triggers:**

- SME observed bias outside `+0.04` to `+0.09`: update SME adjustment.
- Strategic Enterprise observed bias outside `0.05` to `0.12`: update Strategic adjustment.
- Non-Native-Language Support coverage below 80%: widen intervals and require human review.
- Imputed-feature cases show materially different breach rates from complete-data cases: block automatic allocation for those features.

### C. Causal-effect monitoring

**Goal:** estimate whether swarms actually reduce breach probability and by how much, separately by segment, stage, and congestion level.

**Methods:**

1. **Randomized exploration**
   - When capacity exceeds eligible cases, randomly assign some borderline eligible cases to swarm or no-swarm.
   - Use randomization to estimate ARR without assuming the model’s observational risk equals causal effect.

2. **Regression discontinuity / capacity threshold analysis**
   - Compare cases just above and just below the allocation cutoff.

3. **Difference-in-differences**
   - Estimate effect on diagnosis duration and breach probability using pre/post periods and untreated comparison cases.

4. **Hierarchical Bayesian shrinkage**
   - Use for Non-Native-Language Support and small segments to avoid overreacting to sparse data.

**Metrics:**

- estimated ARR by segment;
- estimated ARR by process stage;
- estimated ARR by congestion level;
- estimated delay caused to untreated cases;
- estimated harm from false alarms;
- confidence intervals for all effects.

**Cadence:** monthly causal review; quarterly policy update.

**Triggers:**

- If ARR lower confidence bound is near zero for a segment, stop automatic allocation for that segment and require human review.
- If ARR is high for one stage but low for another, tighten stage eligibility.
- If intervention effect varies strongly with congestion, make spillover estimate congestion-dependent.

### D. Capacity and spillover monitoring

**Metrics:**

- expected spillover harm per slot;
- actual delay minutes added to diagnosis queue;
- breach probability change for untreated diagnosis-stage cases;
- number of untreated cases harmed;
- value-weighted harm to untreated cases;
- marginal net benefit of the last used slot.

**Cadence:** real-time congestion dashboard; daily capacity review.

**Triggers:**

- If the marginal case’s expected net benefit is below zero after spillover, do not use the slot.
- If untreated diagnosis-stage breach rates rise by more than `0.02` during high swarm usage, throttle swarm allocation.
- If queue congestion is high, increase spillover estimate to the upper end and reserve more capacity.

### E. Fairness monitoring

**Metrics:**

- intervention receipt rate by segment;
- intervention receipt rate by value band;
- intervention receipt rate by language support;
- breach rate by segment among intervened and untreated cases;
- spillover exposure by segment;
- repeated-alert suppression rate by segment;
- low-value high-risk cases denied due capacity.

**Cadence:** monthly fairness report.

**Triggers:**

- If a segment receives interventions at materially lower rates than its share of eligible high-risk cases, investigate calibration, data quality, and value weighting.
- If low-value cases have high adjusted risk but repeatedly receive no intervention, create a fairness reserve or alternative intervention path.
- If Non-Native-Language Support cases are either over- or under-intervened relative to observed outcomes, update confidence discounts and require human review.

### F. Policy-induced drift detection

**Metrics:**

- change in baseline risk distribution after policy rollout;
- change in model confidence for cases similar to previously intervened cases;
- change in alert suppression rates;
- change in observed breach rates for untreated comparison cases;
- correlation between intervention indicators and future predicted risk.

**Cadence:** weekly drift monitoring; monthly root-cause review.

**Triggers:**

- If predicted risk drops mainly for previously intervened cases without corresponding structural changes, suspect feedback-loop bias.
- If calibration shifts after policy rollout, pause automatic allocation and retrain or recalibrate with treatment indicators.
- If repeated-alert suppression becomes too aggressive and high-risk cases are missed, relax the evidence-change threshold and add human review.

### G. When to change threshold or allocation rule

Change the allocation rule if any of the following persists for two consecutive review cycles:

1. **Expected net benefit is negative**
   - The lower confidence bound of portfolio net impact is below zero after spillover.
   - Action: reduce slots, increase reserve, or raise evidence requirements.

2. **Calibration error grows**
   - Segment bias moves outside expected ranges.
   - Action: update segment adjustment, widen intervals, or require human review for affected segment.

3. **Intervention effect is weaker than assumed**
   - Causal ARR estimates fall below supplied ranges.
   - Action: require stronger evidence change, restrict to earlier stages, or increase false-alarm burden penalty.

4. **Spillover harm is higher than modeled**
   - Untreated diagnosis-stage cases show increased delay or breach.
   - Action: make spillover congestion-dependent, reduce automatic slots, or delay non-urgent swarms.

5. **Fairness disparity emerges**
   - A segment or value band is systematically starved or over-intervened.
   - Action: add floors, caps, confidence discounts, or randomized exploration.

6. **Feedback loops are detected**
   - Model labels become contaminated by intervention effects.
   - Action: switch to treatment-aware modeling and maintain randomized holdouts.

The policy should remain in shadow mode whenever major changes are made: compute recommended allocations without executing them, compare to human decisions, and deploy only after monitoring confirms improved expected net benefit and acceptable fairness impact.
