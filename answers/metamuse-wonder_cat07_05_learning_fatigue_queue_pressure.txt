Complexity and priority fall monotonically over the shift while queue length falls then rises sharply. That co-movement is the core identification problem.

# 2. Integrated explanation

## The pattern is a sequence of regimes, not a single curve

**Start of shift (positions 1-15): slow and moderately error-prone because of what is inherited, plus warm-up.** Workers begin with backlog from the previous shift: complexity 0.72, high-priority share 41%, queue 46, and seniors deliberately taking complex carryover, especially after nights and weekends. That alone predicts slow work — high complexity takes 22.4 min vs 12.7 min even at low WIP. On top of this, there is plausibly genuine warm-up: re-orienting to systems, cases, and handover context. The evidence that warm-up is real but secondary is that errors *improve* from 3.4% to 2.5% by positions 16-30 even as speed improves — consistent with settling in — but the size of the early slowdown tracks complexity too closely to attribute to worker state alone.

**Mid-shift (16-50): the "learning" sweet spot is partly favourable mix arriving.** Service reaches 13.6 then 12.9 min and errors are at their minimum. But complexity has already fallen to 0.54-0.50 and queue to 31-33. Some within-shift learning/warm-up is likely, especially because seniors speed up substantially while keeping errors flat under low WIP, and juniors' errors actually improve later under low WIP (5.1%  4.4%). Experienced workers appear to convert familiarity into speed without quality loss *when pressure allows*.

**Late shift (51+): speed and errors rise together because the constraint changes.** Queue rebuilds to 62 then 88 (Day shift: 38 at start to 75 at end), so workers now face WIP >70, the zone where every complexity band shows the speed-quality tradeoff. Under high WIP, even low-complexity work is done in 9.4 min with 5.8% errors — faster *and* worse. This is the signature of queue-pressure behaviour: shorter handling, less verification, deferred Quality Checks, batching of Document Review, and rushed correction of Bot_Risk output. Fatigue may add to this — elapsed time and pressure are collinear late in shift, and juniors' collapse to 12.4% errors under high WIP suggests less reserve to absorb either — but fatigue alone cannot explain why people get *faster*, nor why later errors under low WIP remain modest (seniors 2.2%, mid 2.9%, juniors 4.4%). If pure fatigue dominated, late low-WIP work should still be poor. It is not, in these aggregates.

**Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions:** the two outcomes respond to different drivers. Service time falls with (a) falling complexity, (b) warm-up/learning, and (c) pressure-induced shortening. Errors fall with warm-up/learning but rise with (d) pressure, (e) fatigue, and (f) complexity. Early: (a-inverse) and warm-up dominate — slow, slightly error-prone. Mid: learning + easy mix — fast and accurate. Late: complexity is lowest, so speed keeps falling, but pressure (and possibly fatigue) pushes errors up. The interaction with experience is diagnostic: seniors buy speed without much quality loss except under high WIP (4.9%); juniors gain least speed and lose most quality under pressure (12.4%). Night work is a separate mixture: slower service (15.2 min in last two hours), urgent-exception mix, and plausible circadian cost, but fewer events — speed there should not be benchmarked against Day.

In short: early slowness is mostly *selected work plus warm-up*, mid-shift performance is *learning on an easing mix*, and late-shift behaviour is mostly *queue-pressure adaptation, amplified by fatigue and inexperience, and distorted by batching and automation corrections*. Batching also means some late "speed" and late "error bursts" are timestamp and workflow artefacts: Quality Checks deferred to the last 45 minutes make errors *visible* late even if the originating error occurred earlier.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Design principles

- Unit of analysis: event, with case-level downstream outcomes (rework within 7 days) linked back to the originating event — never attribute a case outcome only to the last event.
- Always separate: Bot_Risk vs human; activity families; batched vs non-batched events.
- Report raw *and* adjusted results side by side to expose Simpson's paradox.

## Within-resource comparisons

Fit each resource separately and pooled with resource fixed/random effects: log(service time) and error (logistic) on position splines. This removes stable skill differences. Compare juniors' within-person slopes to seniors'.

## Within-shift position effects

Model `sequence_position_within_shift` and `minutes_since_shift_start` separately (they diverge when event rates vary), using splines/bins 1-5, 6-15, 16-30, 31-50, 51-75, 76+, with shift random effects. Add time-since-last-break proxy (gap >10 min between events).

## WIP / queue stratification

Reproduce every position curve within WIP bands (<30, 30-70, >70) and test WIP × position and WIP threshold effects. Use queue at shift start and arrival rate as less endogenous exposures alongside `wip_at_start`.

## Complexity adjustment

Risk-adjusted models: outcome ~ position + WIP + complexity (continuous + band) + priority + activity + case age + carryover flag. Also standardise: expected performance from a mix-only model, then observed/expected ratios by position.

## Shift and time-of-day controls

Include both clock-hour and position fixed effects; shift-type effects; weekday/weekend; interactions hour × shift. Exploit overlap (e.g. 14:00 is end-Early, start-Late, mid-Day) to separate clock time from position.

## Batching detection

Run batch discovery; flag batch size, batch service-time compression, and Quality Check burst concentration in final 45 min. Re-run all headline analyses excluding Bot_Risk and excluding flagged batches as sensitivity.

## Resource-experience interactions

Interactions: experience group × position, experience group × WIP, and three-way with complexity. Test whether juniors are *assigned* harder early cases or *perform* worse on identical cases via assignment models.

## Rework / error downstream analysis

Trace `error_flag` at event  Quality Check outcome  Rework Correction  rework within 7 days at case level, by originating position/WIP, not detection position. Analyse Bot_Risk  human correction paths.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias makes raw comparisons unfair.** Early cases are more complex, seniors take the hardest carryover, juniors receive Document Review batches, Night staff receive urgent exceptions. A worker's raw error rate substantially describes *what they were given and when*, not only how they worked. Any person-level inference without complexity, priority, activity, WIP, and shift adjustment is misleading.
- **Queue pressure is a management-created exposure.** WIP >70 results from demand, rostering, and absence cover, not individual choice. Workers speeding up under an 88-case queue are adapting to the system; penalising the resulting errors transfers organisational risk onto individuals and will predictably produce concealment and avoidance of complex or high-priority cases.
- **Measurement error is real and structured.** Batch execution can compress recorded service times (stacked timestamps), Quality Check deferral moves error *detection* to shift end regardless of error *origin*, and `error_flag`/rework capture only detected errors — rushed periods may under-detect, so true late error rates may be higher than recorded, while QC bursts may over-attribute errors to late workers. Bot_Risk corrections are not ground truth about either bot or human accuracy without audit.
- **Ecological and small-number fallacies.** Shift-level tables aggregate different workers and days; resource-group tables hide individual variation; Night staff have fewer events so their rates are noisier. Use shrinkage/uncertainty intervals, not league tables.
- **Punitive use corrupts the data itself.** Once error rates carry sanctions, error flags, QC referrals, and rework logging become gamed, destroying the log's value for safety improvement. This is specifically why intervention 8 based on raw late-shift rates must not proceed.
- **Customer fairness is at stake.** Interventions optimising average service time (deferring checks, moving complex cases, random assignment) can silently worsen waiting and outcomes for complex, high-priority, and night-time claimants. Every efficiency metric needs a distributional counterpart.
- **Do not remove quality checks to improve throughput.** Quality Check is the detection mechanism for the failure mode this analysis identifies. Weakening it would improve measured speed and measured errors while worsening actual customer harm; deferral (intervention 6) is only defensible with tracked ownership and detection-lag limits.
