# 6. Mitigation options and side effects
1. **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review**  
   *Action*: Hire more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; ensure specialists are not simultaneously required to perform supervisor reviews.  
   *Side effects*: Requires budget and recruitment lead time. If demand is seasonal, permanent hires might be underutilised later. Does not address upstream bias in documentation or risk scores.

2. **Servicelevel targets for support steps**  
   *Action*: Set specific timeliness thresholds (e.g., 2 days) for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review.  
   *Side effects*: May pressure staff to rush contacts, reducing quality. Could lead to “gaming” (e.g., early closure of cases after minimal contact) and might shift delays to subsequent steps.

3. **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**  
   *Action*: Empower bilingual caseworkers to handle entire cases endtoend, and likewise for accommodation specialists, minimising handoffs to generalists.  
   *Side effects*: Requires crosstraining and may create separate siloed tracks, which could be perceived as unequal. Specialists might become overloaded if case volumes are high; workload balancing mechanisms would be needed.

4. **Improve decision explanations**  
   *Action*: Provide plainlanguage, translated explanations of decisions and reasoning, perhaps using automated generation.  
   *Side effects*: Increased translation cost and time initially; if explanations reveal inconsistencies, appeals could rise temporarily as applicants become better informed about their rights.

5. **Monitor adjusted disparities regularly**  
   *Action*: Publish internal dashboards with process and outcome metrics adjusted for legitimate case complexity, broken down by applicant group.  
   *Side effects*: Risk of “adjustment skepticism” if stakeholders disagree with the control variables; could lead to complacency if adjusted differences appear small while raw burdens remain high. Must be accompanied by audits of the adjustment model itself.

6. **Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness for bias**  
   *Action*: Conduct disparateimpact analysis on the risk scoring algorithm; test whether documentation completeness ratings differ systematically for minoritylanguage applicants after controlling for actual submitted documents (through doubleblind regrading).  
   *Side effects*: If bias is found and the score is changed, fraud detection could temporarily weaken, potentially increasing improper payments. May require a complete model rebuild, which is timeconsuming and politically sensitive.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment
The process shows **probable procedural unfairness** for minoritylanguage and accommodationrequesting applicants (Groups B and C) driven primarily by underresourced support steps and routing designs that amplify waiting time and handovers, even after accounting for documented complexity. These delays are not inherent to the applicants’ needs but reflect a structural capacity deficit and an organisational choice to concentrate these cases on a tiny pool of specialists. For Group D, the fairness of the routing is ambiguous because much of the additional burden hinges on a *risk_score_band* whose neutrality is uncertain; an audit of the risk model is essential before drawing firm conclusions. The evidence is sufficient to identify inequitable procedural burdens but not to precisely quantify the causal effect of discrimination, owing to missing data on support quality, appeal outcomes, and potential bias in risk and documentation metrics. The process therefore **does not currently meet reasonable standards of procedural fairness** and warrants prompt, targeted mitigation, especially around language and accommodation pathways, while further investigation into riskscore bias proceeds.
