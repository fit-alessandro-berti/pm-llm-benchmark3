## 1. What is observed

The supplied branch-month panel observes **automation availability**, not case-level automation exposure: `automation=1` means the branch had the system available that month, while actual treatment intensity is better proxied by `automated_fast_path_pct`. Before formal adoption, every branch already shows a small 4–6% automated fast-path share, so “manual” months are not pure no-automation months. After adoption, actual use rises gradually: Alpha and Beta move from 4–6% to roughly 25–50%, while Gamma moves from 4% to 25–32% after July. 

Rollout was not random. Alpha and Beta adopted first because they had high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma adopted later after a readiness pilot; Delta and Epsilon remained untreated through August. The timing also overlaps with several operational shocks: the fraud desk began in March, Alpha and Beta received more routed demand from April, staffing changed in several branches, and a May product change increased high-complexity shares. These changes affect throughput, escalation, rework, and tail times independently of automation.

Outcome measurement is also limited. The panel contains branch-month aggregates rather than event-level records, so medians and p90s cannot be decomposed by path, queue, or resource. CSAT is a monthly survey mean with response rates varying between about 20% and 45%, but response counts are unavailable. Therefore, observed pre/post movements are informative process signals, not direct causal effects.

The primary staggered-adoption event-study approach is preferable for the supplied panel because the rollout is staggered, treatment effects are likely dynamic, and simple before/after comparisons are clearly confounded by calendar shocks. A modern group-time design avoids the most misleading feature of a naïve two-way fixed-effects regression: already-treated branches should not serve as clean controls for later-treated branches when treatment effects change over time. It also keeps Alpha/Beta and Gamma as separate adoption cohorts, which matters because Alpha/Beta were targeted for poor performance while Gamma adopted later after a readiness pilot.

That said, this strategy should be presented as a disciplined descriptive-causal sensitivity framework, not as a definitive estimator. The identifying assumptions are strained by the March fraud desk, April routing campaign, staffing changes, May complexity increase, gradual learning, and only five branch clusters. Its value is that it makes these weaknesses visible: one can compare cohort-specific effects, inspect pre-trends, test sensitivity to control sets, and separate immediate adoption from later learning. It is therefore the best primary strategy for honest assessment, but not enough to justify a single universal “X% reduction” claim.

Pre-trends do not strongly support a simple parallel-trends story. From January to March, median cycle-time slopes differ: Alpha decreases slightly, Beta increases slightly, Gamma and Delta worsen, and Epsilon improves slightly. Escalation rises in March for all branches, consistent with the centralized fraud desk change. Alpha and Beta were also selected because they were high-volume, weak performers, so regression to the mean is a serious concern. However, Alpha/Beta did not simply improve immediately after adoption; they worsened in April–June, but less than untreated branches.

A defensible sensitivity summary is therefore bounded. For `median_cycle_h`, raw adopter changes range from -6.5 h to +1.9 h; comparison-adjusted changes are around -3.8 to -4.2 h; the intensity model implies roughly -1.5 to -2.1 h for a 25–34 pp increase. A cautious causal scenario interval is about 0 to -5 h for comparable branches, not a universal percent reduction. For `p90_cycle_h`, Alpha/Beta raw tails worsen by about +10 h while Gamma improves by about -9 h; comparison-adjusted contrasts are about -6 to -7 h. Because specialist-queue congestion is unmeasured, a reasonable scenario interval is wider, roughly +5 h harm to -8 h benefit.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

`expand with an embedded evaluation`

Epsilon should be the next priority branch, but not as a full uncontrolled switch. By August, Epsilon still has high complexity, high rework, and worse tail performance than Delta, so it has more operational upside. Delta should be used as a delayed comparison branch for a short, pre-registered phase, unless leadership permits a stronger design: randomized enablement by team, queue, weekday, or eligible case cohort within both Delta and Epsilon. The design should avoid turning on automation for every eligible case on day one.

Use at least three full months of pre-period measurement and at least three full months of post-period measurement before making a scale-up decision; six months would be better if operationally tolerable. The evaluation should measure not only branch-month outcomes but event-level process-mining data: activity timestamps, path variant, automated vs manual triage, manual overrides, risk class, priority, rework loops, specialist-review waits, fraud-desk waits, queue length and WIP at activity start, resource/shift, FTE by skill, and routing source. CSAT must include response counts and respondent linkage to case type and outcome.

Spillover monitoring is mandatory. The fraud desk and specialist pools should report arrivals, wait times, backlog, and branch of origin daily, because automation can improve local medians while exporting delay to shared queues. Guardrails should stop or throttle expansion if p90 or p95 cycle time worsens materially, if escalation exceeds specialist capacity, if rework or complaints increase, or if CSAT falls after accounting for response mix.

A reasonable scale-up rule would be: after the pre-registered post period, expand only if median cycle time improves by at least about 3 hours or an equivalent operational threshold, p90 does not worsen by more than 2 hours, rework does not increase, escalation queues remain within capacity, and CSAT shows no credible decline. The final decision should be based on both the randomized or delayed-comparison estimate and process diagnostics, not on a single “automation reduces cycle time by X%” statement.

## 10. Final claim ladder

* Observed: Automation availability and actual automated-fast-path use rose after rollout, while adoption coincided with demand routing, staffing, complexity, fraud-desk, and learning changes.
* Suggestive: Median cycle time and rework look more favorable in adopter comparisons than in untreated comparisons, especially for Gamma and in intensity-based descriptions.
* Conditionally causal: Under strong conditional parallel-trend, no-spillover, and stable-treatment-version assumptions, automation may have mitigated median and p90 deterioration by several hours, but this is not robust enough for a single percent claim.
* Not supported: “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a general leadership statement is not defensible from the supplied data.
