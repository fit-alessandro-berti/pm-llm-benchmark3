# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

All calculations are in minutes. Let case be \(c\), branch be \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\). Use:

- \(arrival_c\), \(fork_c\), \(S_{c,i}\), \(E_{c,i}\), \(final\_start_c\), \(final\_end_c\).
- Branch queue:  
  \[
  Q_{c,i}=S_{c,i}-fork_c
  \]
- Branch service:  
  \[
  P_{c,i}=E_{c,i}-S_{c,i}
  \]
- Branch elapsed from fork release:  
  \[
  R_{c,i}=E_{c,i}-fork_c=Q_{c,i}+P_{c,i}
  \]
- Join ready under max-join logic:  
  \[
  join\_ready_c=\max_i E_{c,i}=fork_c+\max_i R_{c,i}
  \]
- Critical branch set:  
  \[
  K_c=\arg\max_i E_{c,i}
  \]
  A branch in \(K_c\) has zero synchronization wait. Ties are handled deterministically by sorting branch names and reporting the first as primary, while retaining all tied branches.
- Branch synchronization wait:  
  \[
  W_{c,i}=join\_ready_c-E_{c,i}\ge 0
  \]
- Fork-join stage elapsed time:  
  \[
  T^{FJ}_c=join\_ready_c-fork_c=\max_i(Q_{c,i}+P_{c,i})
  \]
- Upstream elapsed time:  
  \[
  U_c=fork_c-arrival_c
  \]
- Downstream queueing before final start:  
  \[
  D_c=final\_start_c-join\_ready_c
  \]
- Final service:  
  \[
  F_c=final\_end_c-final\_start_c
  \]
- Total case flow time:  
  \[
  T_c=final\_end_c-arrival_c
  =U_c+T^{FJ}_c+D_c+F_c
  \]

Why summing all branch synchronization waits would double count elapsed time:  
\[
\sum_i W_{c,i}
= \sum_i (join\_ready_c-E_{c,i})
= 3\cdot join\_ready_c-\sum_i E_{c,i}
\]
This is a measure of slack across parallel branches, not wall-clock delay. The fork-join stage lasts only until the latest branch completes, i.e. \(\max_i(Q_{c,i}+P_{c,i})\). Adding \(\sum_i W_{c,i}\) to all branch queue+service times would count concurrent branch work and slack multiple times. Only the critical branch’s \(Q+P\) equals the fork-join stage; noncritical synchronization waits are parallel slack.

Downstream queue \(=11:50-11:30=20\). Total \(=20+50+20+20=110\).

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

| branch | mean service | min service | max service | mean queue | max queue | mean Q+P | max Q+P | critical count / freq | mean sync wait | max sync wait |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 50.00 | 50 | 50 | 0.00 | 0 | 50.00 | 50 | 2 / 16.7% | 11.00 | 25 |
| B | 37.50 | 30 | 45 | 19.17 | 30 | 56.67 | 75 | 8 / 66.7% | 4.33 | 20 |
| C | 20.17 | 18 | 30 | 10.42 | 35 | 30.58 | 57 | 2 / 16.7% | 30.42 | 52 |

Influence ranking: **B is the strongest controller of total completion time.** B is critical in 8 of 12 cases, has the highest mean \(Q+P\) (56.67 min), and the highest tail \(Q+P\) (75 min in F07 and F12). Its queue delay is also large: mean 19.17 min, max 30 min. A has a larger constant service time than B, but A has zero queue and is critical only twice. C has low average \(Q+P\), but it has a meaningful tail: max 57 min in F04 and 55 min in F08, where it is critical. Therefore a reasonable ranking is:

1. **B — dominant critical branch overall.**
2. **A — second by mean load and constant service, but weakly controllable because its queue is always zero.**
3. **C — lower mean load, but a real tail risk; near A, not far behind in specific cases.**

Important near-tie: A and C both are critical exactly twice. A’s advantage is higher mean \(Q+P\); C’s advantage is a higher maximum \(Q+P\) and stronger tail behavior. B’s lead over A and C is clear in this sample.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

I treat counterfactual savings as direct upstream critical-path savings: reduce \(join\_ready\), then see how much total flow could improve if final capacity/release policy allows final start to move earlier. If final station congestion absorbs the gain, actual flow-time savings can be lower. This distinction is necessary because final-station waiting is substantial: downstream queue sums to 176 min across 12 cases, mean 14.67 min.

| counterfactual | assumption | positive cases | total direct saving | mean direct saving | zero-benefit cases | interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| CF1: reduce A service by 20% | A service 50  40, so A \(Q+P=40\) | F01, F11 | 15 min | 1.25 min | 10 cases | Most cases remain controlled by B or C. F01 saves 5 min; F11 saves 10 min. F11 may be masked by F1 schedule because F1 is busy until 11:50. |
| CF2: reduce B queue delay by 50% | Halve each B queue, keep B service unchanged | F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12 | 92.5 min | 7.71 min | F01, F04, F08, F11 | Strongest tested branch improvement. In F05 and F10, A becomes critical after B improves. In F02 and F06, A and B become tied. |
| CF3: reduce downstream final queue by 50% | Halve `downstream_queue` | all 12 | 88 min | 7.33 min | none | If downstream queue could be eliminated entirely, saving is 176 min, mean 14.67 min. This directly attacks final-station congestion but may expose B as the next bottleneck. |

Key counterfactual detail: speeding a noncritical branch often has zero case-level effect. For example, reducing A service by 20% does nothing in most cases because B or C remains the latest branch. Reducing B queue by 50% helps many cases, but F01, F04, F08, and F11 remain controlled by A or C. Final-station congestion can mask upstream gains: in F11, a B or A improvement that moves `join_ready` earlier may simply increase downstream queue if F1 is not available until 11:50.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

1. **Improve B: Machine Stress Test queue and scheduling.**  
   - Throughput: B is critical in 8/12 cases and has mean \(Q+P=56.67\) min. Halving B queue gives 92.5 min total direct saving in this sample.  
   - Flow-time tail: B has max \(Q+P=75\) min in F07 and F12, so B interventions should reduce the worst cases as well as the mean.  
   - Resource utilization: adding B capacity may reduce B queue but could lower utilization if demand is not steady. Pilot staggered B starts, a second tester, or priority rules before permanent capacity.  
   - Possible new bottleneck: after B improves, A can become critical, as in F05 and F10, and C remains critical in F04/F08. Synchronization waits will shift to A or C.  
   - Pilot/measure first: B queue distribution, B service-time variability, critical-branch frequency after change, and whether final-stage queue absorbs the gain.

2. **Add final-station capacity or reduce final release queueing.**  
   - Throughput: downstream queue totals 176 min across 12 cases. Halving it saves 88 min directly; eliminating it saves 176 min. This is comparable to a strong B intervention.  
   - Flow-time tail: F10 and F11 each have 20 min downstream queue, so final-capacity improvement should reduce tail waiting.  
   - Resource utilization: adding F3 or flexing F2 capacity may reduce queue but can create idle final capacity if upstream branches are late.  
   - Possible new bottleneck: upstream B or A/C join may become the dominant constraint. Synchronization effects may change because final start moves closer to `join_ready`.  
   - Pilot/measure first: final resource utilization by F1/F2, `join_ready` to `final_start`, queue by resource, and total flow time after adding temporary capacity.

Combined redesign is likely best: reduce B queue first, then add enough final capacity so the B improvement is not masked by downstream queueing.

# 7. Limits of inference

- Causality cannot be identified: B being the latest branch does not prove that B capacity caused delay; it may reflect scheduling rules, batching, prioritization, or upstream release policy.
- Branch resource capacity is not logged. We see queue and service timing, but not how many A, B, or C resources existed, their calendars, or whether work was preempted.
- Work preemption and operator switching are unobserved. A long service interval may include waiting, setup, or interruption.
- Whether final-station waiting is truly a queue rather than a release policy is unknown. `final_start - join_ready` is only downstream queueing under the stated model assumptions.
- Final-resource availability is partially visible through F1/F2 assignments, but not resource calendars, breaks, or priority rules.
- Synchronization waiting is a residual, not an observed event. It may include both true waiting and administrative delay.
- Timestamps are minute-accurate and complete-event only. Sub-minute effects and incomplete records are not identifiable.
- Counterfactual savings are conditional. If final capacity is unchanged, moving `join_ready` earlier may only increase downstream queue and leave total flow time unchanged.
- Throughput effects cannot be fully inferred from 12 cases without arrival-rate, demand, utilization, and staffing information.
