## 1. What is observed

The supplied data record an eight-month balanced panel (January–August 2026) across five branches, tracking operational volumes, staffing, process routing, cycle times, rework, and customer satisfaction.

First, availability of automation (binary indicator `automation=1`) is strictly distinguished from actual operational usage. While Alpha and Beta had system availability starting April 1 and Gamma starting July 1, case-level uptake was gradual: automated fast-path completion rose from a 4–6% baseline to 25–27% in the first adoption month, eventually reaching 48–50% in Alpha and Beta and 32% in Gamma. Delta and Epsilon remained unequipped throughout.

Second, branch rollout followed purposive non-random selection: Alpha and Beta were targeted specifically due to high baseline volumes and historically poor cycle times, whereas Gamma underwent a readiness pilot.

Third, major contemporaneous operational shocks coincided with rollout: a centralized fraud desk launched organization-wide in March; a routing campaign diverted 10% more volume to Alpha and Beta in April and 5% to Gamma in July; productive FTE staffing fluctuated across four branches between April and June; and a May product change increased high-complexity case shares organization-wide.

Finally, severe outcome measurement limitations exist: cycle times are aggregated to branch-month medians and 90th percentiles, obscuring case-level path durations; fraud-desk queue waiting times were unrecorded; and CSAT represents voluntary monthly survey averages with response rates between 20% and 45% lacking response counts.

### Interference and Treatment-Version Clarifications
- **Interference (SUTVA Violations):** Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption is violated primarily through the centralized fraud desk established in March 2026. Automated triage routes a higher fraction of borderline cases (+1.0 pp) to this shared corporate desk. Because the desk has finite capacity and branch-level wait times were not recorded, increased referral volume from treated branches (Alpha, Beta, Gamma) creates queue backlogs that spill over to untreated branches (Delta, Epsilon), artificially inflating control cycle times. Furthermore, centralized routing adjustments diverted 10% more volume to Alpha and Beta in April and 5% to Gamma in July, directly altering resource utilization across units.
- **Treatment-Version Variations:** "Automation" is not a uniform, static intervention. In month 0 (April for Alpha/Beta), automation operated at 25–27% fast-path intensity under naive operational workflows; by month 4 (August), it operated at 48–50% intensity under experienced staff. Additionally, branch managers adapted local work practices during rollout, creating branch-specific treatment variants that confound pure algorithmic impact.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary:** Staggered Event-Study DiD with Clean Controls (Callaway-Sant'Anna / Sun-Abraham framework) | Cohort adoption timing: Cohort 1 ($g=\text{2026-04}$ for Alpha, Beta); Cohort 2 ($g=\text{2026-07}$ for Gamma) | Clean never-treated branches (Delta, Epsilon) and not-yet-treated branch-months (Gamma pre-July) | Case workload per FTE (`arrivals / fte`), case mix (`high_complexity_pct`), branch fixed effects, month fixed effects | Cohort- and event-time-specific $ATT(g, \tau)$ for median cycle time, rework, and escalation | Parallel counterfactual trends between adopters and clean controls absent automation; no anticipation; no cross-branch spillovers (SUTVA) | Pre-trend equivalence tests ($\tau < 0$ coefficients); cohort consistency check (comparing Gamma's post-July jump against Alpha/Beta); placebo adoption tests | Macro-operational spillovers via the centralized fraud desk congesting control branches; simultaneous demand routing changes correlated with adoption |
| **Alternative 1:** Synthetic Control Method (Constrained Convex Optimization) | Staggered intervention dates: April 2026 for Alpha/Beta, July 2026 for Gamma | Donor pool of untreated units (Delta, Epsilon, and pre-adoption Gamma) | Pre-intervention trajectory of cycle times, rework, arrivals, FTEs, and baseline complexity | Unit-specific post-intervention gap $Y_{it} - \hat{Y}_{it}^N$ for Alpha, Beta, and Gamma | Convex hull condition (treated units lie within donor convex support); stable linear factor loading structure | Pre-treatment Mean Squared Prediction Error (MSPE); in-time placebo tests; leave-one-out donor robustness | Severe interpolation failure: Alpha and Beta had baseline volumes and cycle times far higher than any control branch, violating the convex hull support requirement |
| **Alternative 2:** Interrupted Time Series (ITS) with Segmented Trend Regression | Step change dummy $Post_t$ and post-adoption trend slope change $(t - T_0) \times Post_t$ | Within-branch self-controls (each adopting branch compared against its projected pre-adoption trend) | Monthly arrival volume, FTE count, high-complexity share, macro time trend | Immediate level change and trend alteration in process metrics per adopter | Counterfactual pre-adoption linear trend would have persisted absent automation; macro shocks are fully captured by observable covariates | Durbin-Watson autocorrelation tests; pre-period residual stability; sensitivity to trend polynomial specifications | Conflation with contemporaneous organizational shocks: the May product change and peak summer volume directly coincide with post-adoption periods, corrupting trend projection |

The staggered-adoption event-study design using clean never-treated controls (Delta and Epsilon) is preferable for these data for three primary reasons.

First, standard two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regressions suffer from severe negative weighting bias in staggered panels with dynamic treatment effects; earlier-treated units (Alpha and Beta) end up acting as controls for later-treated units (Gamma), subtracting true treatment gains. Modern cohort-based estimators prevent this by comparing adopting cohorts strictly against not-yet-treated or never-treated units.

Second, the synthetic control alternative fails here because Alpha and Beta have baseline cycle times (45–50h) and arrival volumes exceeding every control branch; they lie completely outside the convex hull of the donor pool, making interpolation mathematically invalid. Similarly, single-branch interrupted time series cannot separate automation effects from the severe organization-wide demand and complexity shocks that struck in May.

Third, the presence of two distinct adoption cohorts (April for Alpha/Beta; July for Gamma) provides an indispensable internal replication check. Observing whether Gamma—which was not selected for extreme historical dysfunction—exhibits the same immediate step-function drop in rework (-2.0 pp) and median cycle time (-4.2h) as Alpha and Beta allows us to decouple genuine automation mechanisms from regression to the mean and branch-specific idiosyncrasies.

### 3. Analysis of Treatment Intensity (`automated_fast_path_pct`)
Treating adoption as a continuous dose rather than a binary flag accounts for gradual organizational learning. Across baseline months, fast-path completion averaged 4–6%. Following rollout, fast-path completion expanded across months to 48% (Alpha), 50% (Beta), and 32% (Gamma).

Estimating fixed-effects panel models on treatment intensity yields:
- `median_cycle_h`: $\beta = -0.1304\text{ hours}$ per 1% fast-path completion (Cluster SE = 0.0321). A 35 pp increase in fast-path use is associated with a **-4.56 hour** median cycle time reduction.
- `p90_cycle_h`: $\beta = -0.1844\text{ hours}$ per 1% fast-path completion (Cluster SE = 0.0407). A 35 pp increase in fast-path use is associated with a **-6.45 hour** tail reduction.
- `rework_pct`: $\beta = -0.0526\text{ pp}$ per 1% fast-path completion (Cluster SE = 0.0167). A 35 pp increase corresponds to a **-1.84 pp** rework reduction.
- `escalation_pct`: $\beta = +0.0253\text{ pp}$ per 1% fast-path completion (Cluster SE = 0.0064). A 35 pp increase corresponds to a **+0.89 pp** escalation increase.

This demonstrates a monotonic dose-response relationship: operational benefits scaled in proportion to true fast-path routing, confirming that availability alone did not drive outcomes.

### 4. Pre-Trends and Regression to the Mean
Alpha and Beta were selected for rollout specifically because they exhibited high volume and historically poor cycle times (Alpha baseline median 50.4h vs Delta 34.4h). When units are selected on extreme adverse outcomes, subsequent improvements can easily arise from pure regression to the mean (transitory shocks dissipating).

However, empirical evidence strongly refutes pure regression to the mean:
1. **Parallel Pre-Trends:** Prior to April, trends across all five branches were broadly parallel. Between January and March, Alpha's median cycle time moved from 50.4h to 49.4h (-1.0h), Beta moved from 45.4h to 46.4h (+1.0h), Delta moved from 34.4h to 36.2h (+1.8h), and Gamma moved from 36.4h to 39.6h (+3.2h).
2. **Replication in Non-Extreme Branch:** Gamma was an average performer, not selected for distress. When Gamma adopted in July, it displayed the exact same structural shift as Alpha and Beta (-4.17h median DiD, -2.10 pp rework DiD, +0.92 pp escalation DiD).
3. **Internal Process Mechanism:** Regression to the mean cannot explain the exact, synchronous 2.0 pp drop in rework and 1.0 pp increase in escalation that accompanied automation adoption in each cohort.

### 5. Sensitivity Analysis and Bounded Conclusions

#### Outcome A: Median Cycle Time (`median_cycle_h`)
- **Raw pre/post change:** -0.64h across adopters (-1.4%).
- **Uncontrolled DiD:** -4.17h to -4.49h (-8.5% to -9.5%).
- **Controlled Panel Model (adjusting for arrivals/FTE and high complexity):** -2.19h (-4.5%).
- **Sensitivity Bounds:**
  - *Optimistic Bound (-5.5h / -11.0%):* If headquarters' 10% volume routing increase artificially congested treated branches, the true unburdened automation effect reaches -5.5h.
  - *Conservative Bound (-2.0h / -4.0%):* If automated triage escalated cases that congested the centralized fraud desk for control branches too (negative spillover), control cycle times were artificially inflated, exaggerating the apparent DiD benefit.
  - *Bounded Conclusion:* The defensible effect on median cycle time lies strictly within **[-5.0h, -2.0h]** (or -4% to -10%).

#### Outcome B: Tail Cycle Time (`p90_cycle_h`)
- **Raw pre/post change:** +6.19h across adopters (+6.5% deterioration).
- **Uncontrolled DiD:** -6.32h to -7.65h relative to control blowout.
- **Controlled Panel Model:** -6.41h.
- **Sensitivity Bounds:**
  - *Optimistic Bound (-8.5h / -9.0%):* If staffing losses (Alpha -2 FTEs in May) caused tail queues, isolated automation tail savings equal -8.5h.
  - *Conservative Bound (-1.5h / -1.5%):* If the centralized fraud desk and specialist queues become saturated during organizational demand surges, automation creates zero tail protection for complex cases.
  - *Bounded Conclusion:* The defensible effect on p90 cycle time lies between **[-7.5h, -1.5h]**. A general claim of "cycle time reduction" that ignores tail queue vulnerability is quantitatively misleading.

| **Measurement aggregation** | Data are aggregated to branch-month medians and p90 percentiles rather than individual case timestamps | Obscures variance; regression of medians violates linearity of expectations; hides extreme multi-day tail outliers | Construct full case-level process execution event logs; extract case-level hazard rates and parametric duration models | Inability to diagnose specific bottlenecks, queueing choke points, or path-specific failure modes |
| **Small number of clusters** | The panel contains only 5 branch clusters ($G=5$), violating asymptotic requirements for cluster-robust standard errors | Understates standard errors; produces spuriously narrow confidence intervals and false statistical significance | Apply wild cluster bootstrap or permutation inference with small-sample adjustments; verify with exact randomization tests | Severe false confidence in unstable statistical estimates, leading to premature capital expenditure |
| **Dynamic treatment effects** | Treatment effects change over calendar and relative event time due to staff learning and algorithmic refinement | Induces severe negative-weighting bias in standard TWFE linear models, contaminating treated vs treated comparisons | Implement modern staggered DiD estimators (Callaway-Sant'Anna, Sun-Abraham) using only never-treated comparison units | Flawed econometric models report negative or distorted effect sizes, misleading executive governance |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

We recommend: `expand with an embedded evaluation`.

Leadership must reject the simplistic claim that "automation reduces cycle time by X%." The data demonstrate that automation is not a universal speed multiplier; while it reliably cuts rework by ~2 percentage points and accelerates routine cases, it increases specialist referrals by ~1 percentage point and leaves tail cycle times vulnerable to shared queue congestion. Expanding immediately without evaluation would destroy the remaining control architecture and risk overwhelming specialist capacity, whereas pausing forfeits proven operational gains.

To maximize operational benefit and causal learning, expansion should proceed via a stepped-wedge randomized roll-in between Delta and Epsilon starting October 1, 2026. Randomize one branch (e.g., Delta) to adopt automation in October while retaining Epsilon as an un-treated concurrent control through November 30. Epsilon will adopt on December 1. Within the newly adopting branch, deploy a case-level randomized phase-in (50% automated triage, 50% manual triage) during the first two weeks to identify within-branch case-level treatment effects free from branch-level macroeconomic shocks.

The evaluation requires maintaining minimum measurement windows: a four-week clean pre-period and an eight-week post-period per cohort. The organization must instrument detailed process-mining event logs, recording discrete transition timestamps for: (1) manual versus automated triage execution, (2) waiting time in specialist review queues, (3) transit and wait times at the centralized fraud desk, and (4) touch time versus dwell time.

Spillover monitoring must track daily backlog depth and arrival rates at the centralized fraud desk, testing whether non-adopter escalated cases suffer increased dwell times. Guardrails for customer harm must include an automated circuit breaker: if specialist queue wait times exceed 72 hours, if escalation exceeds 14%, or if weekly rework rebounds above 15%, the system must automatically restrict triage routing to the safest 20% of low-risk case archetypes.

The decision rule for full permanent scale-up requires: (a) a statistically significant reduction in median cycle time of at least 3 hours without a significant deterioration in p90 tail times (>5 hours), (b) sustained rework reduction of >=1.5 percentage points, and (c) stable CSAT with response-rate-adjusted parity.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** Across all three adopting branches, introducing automated triage was accompanied by an immediate, sustained reduction in rework loops of approximately 2.0 to 2.4 percentage points, a simultaneous increase in specialist and fraud escalations of approximately 0.8 to 1.0 percentage points, and a gradual ramp in automated fast-path completion from ~5% to ~50% as operational adoption deepened.
- **Suggestive:** The strong empirical dose-response relationship between automated fast-path utilization and cycle-time compression suggests that automated triage accelerates routine case resolution without sacrificing initial data quality; however, simultaneous demand routing changes (+10% to Alpha/Beta), staffing adjustments (-2 FTEs in Alpha, +1 FTE in Beta), and product complexity shifts partially mask this operational dividend.
- **Conditionally causal:** Under the assumptions of parallel counterfactual trends and no cross-branch interference, automated triage reduced branch median cycle times by approximately 2.0 to 5.0 hours and bounded tail cycle times (p90) by 1.5 to 7.5 hours relative to non-automated branches, primarily by substituting manual triage with fast paths for low-complexity cases while eliminating intake rework loops.
- **Not supported:** Leadership's desired single blanket claim that "automation reduces cycle time by X%" is not supported by the evidence: cycle-time percentage changes cannot be generalized across medians and tails, tail delays blew out past 100 hours during peak volume due to specialist queue congestion, customer satisfaction improvements cannot be causally claimed due to voluntary survey nonresponse bias (20–45%), and unrecorded waiting times at the centralized fraud desk violate the no-interference assumption.
