# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

For the calculations below, define observed waiting as \(W_{obs} = start - queue\_arrival\) and observed service as \(S_{obs} = complete - start\). This is an explicit calculation convention using displayed timestamps as exact and assuming aligned clocks. It is not an assertion that these are operational waiting and service times. Resource utilization additionally requires a valid resource identity, capacity, and availability calendar.

**G1 — likely fixture batch.** Observed waits are 6, 5, 4, and 2 minutes, and each observed service time is 3 minutes. If the four fixture positions really began and ended together, these are sensible item waits to fixture admission and a sensible 3-minute cycle duration. A naive capacity-one utilization calculation would sum 12 item-minutes in a 3-minute window and report 400% utilization. That is not an error if the appropriate denominator is four fixture positions: 12 slot-minutes of work over 12 available slot-minutes is 100% fixture occupancy. Throughput of four completions at 09:03 may be genuine batch throughput. A conformance model that assumes one-at-a-time work would falsely report parallelism; a capacity-aware batch model would not. If the PLC stamps all members of a fixture transaction rather than individual execution, item-level service and utilization remain uncertain.

**G2 — common group start.** The observed waits are 18, 14, 11, and 6 minutes, while observed services rise from 8 to 27 minutes. If 10:00 is only a group-start action, later items probably waited inside the group after their displayed start. Thus, item-level waiting is biased downward for later items, while service is biased upward because it includes time waiting for individual attention. The summed displayed service is 70 minutes in a 27-minute wall-clock span, which would imply roughly 259% utilization for a capacity-one T-B resource. That is more likely an artifact of using one group-start timestamp for every item than proof of parallel inspection. Completion-based throughput may be reasonably ordered, but start-based workload, utilization, and bottleneck analysis are distorted. Conformance checking may falsely find four simultaneous inspections or excessive multitasking.

**G3 — common completion/submission.** Observed waits are 22, 18, 19, and 19 minutes; observed services are 28, 20, 13, and 6 minutes. If starts approximately mark the beginning of review, case waiting to review could be usable with caution. But if 11:30 is a folder submission time, service is inflated, especially for E01 and E02, by post-review delay. The displayed intervals total 67 minutes over a 28-minute span, implying more than 200% utilization for a single trusted resource. The 11:30 throughput burst is likely an administrative burst rather than seven-minute operational synchrony. A conformance model may infer overlapping reviews, simultaneous completion, or an unusual finish-order pattern that is purely caused by delayed submission.

**G4 — serial scanner-defined work.** Observed waits rise from 7 to 19 minutes, and each observed service is 4 minutes. This is the least problematic cluster if the scanner start and complete events are truly the beginning and end of packing. The resource appears continuously busy from 12:05 to 12:25, so utilization is near 100% during that observed busy interval, throughput is one case per four minutes, and the growing waits support a capacity-one bottleneck interpretation. The primary caveat is semantic: if scanning is only a handoff or confirmation rather than physical packing, the analysis applies to scanner-defined packing, not necessarily to all pack labor. Conformance analysis is comparatively reliable for the recorded activity because there is no displayed overlap and adjacency is precise.

**G5 — shared analyst account.** Observed waits are tightly clustered around 15–16 minutes, and reported services are 25, 15, 20, and 10 minutes. Case-level waiting to a recorded start may still be useful as a pool-admission measure, but it cannot be assigned to a particular analyst or used to infer one worker’s queue. Treating `analyst_pool` as one person produces 70 reported person-minutes over a 30-minute window, about 233% utilization. If there were three analysts and all lifecycle times were accurate, the same arithmetic would be about 78% of a three-person, 90-person-minute capacity window; however, the apparent four-way overlap means even that is illustrative rather than validated. Throughput can be measured for the pool, but not attributed to one resource. A single-resource conformance model will falsely report impossible overlap; a pool-capacity model may still need to account for multitasking or stale intervals.

**G6 — administrative bulk closure.** Observed waits are 175, 148, 122, 110, 76, 58, and 35 minutes, while every observed service time is zero. This is the classic mechanism by which the same data can create both inflated waiting and near-zero service: if work occurred before 17:00 but the form was entered at 17:00, the recorded start is much later than actual execution start, inflating waiting; because start and complete are both set to the form time, reported service collapses to zero. Utilization appears zero, throughput appears as a seven-case instant burst, and bottleneck detection may show a huge queue with no corresponding work consumption. If tickets truly remained untouched until a coordinated closure, the long waits could be genuine business delay, but service is still unmeasured. Conformance analysis will falsely flag zero-duration activities, simultaneous completions, and potentially severe service-level violations unless administrative closure is modeled separately.

**G7 — quarter-hour coarsening.** Observed waits are 2, -1, -4, -7, and -11 minutes because several queue arrivals occur after the displayed 14:00 starts. These negative waits are strong evidence that displayed lifecycle timestamps cannot be treated as exact execution times. Each displayed service equals 15 minutes, producing 75 reported work-minutes inside a 15-minute interval, or 500% utilization for a capacity-one resource. This could falsely indicate a simultaneous batch, a highly parallel station, or an overloaded worker. Throughput appears to occur in a single 14:15 burst. Queue, service, utilization, bottleneck, and timestamp-order conformance metrics should be interval-censored or excluded until the raw MES time and rounding logic are known.

**G8 — backlog followed by serial recovery.** Observed waits are 38, 43, 48, 54, and 58 minutes; each displayed service is 7 minutes. The exact serial chain from 15:38 to 16:13 is credible for scanner-recorded work. If the safety event actually removed capacity until approximately 15:35, the long waits are a real consequence of unavailable capacity, and the resource is approximately 100% busy during the observed recovery period. In contrast, measured over 15:00–16:13 without an availability calendar, the same workload occupies only 35 of 73 minutes, about 48%, which could misleadingly suggest underutilization despite a queue. Throughput after recovery is one case every seven minutes. Bottleneck and conformance analyses should include an unavailable-capacity state only after clock alignment and operational validation; otherwise the safety link is a hypothesis, not a fact.

# Independent pandas diagnostics.
def overlap_stats(interval_frame):
    valid = interval_frame.dropna(subset=["start", "complete"])
    intervals = list(valid[["start", "complete"]].itertuples(index=False, name=None))
    pairs = 0
    overlaps = 0
    for (s1, e1), (s2, e2) in combinations(intervals, 2):
        pairs += 1
        # Strict overlap: exact adjacency does not count as overlap.
        if (s1 < e2) and (s2 < e1):
            overlaps += 1
    return {
        "intervals": len(intervals),
        "interval_pairs": pairs,
        "overlap_pairs": overlaps,
        "pairwise_overlap_rate": np.nan if pairs == 0 else overlaps / pairs,
    }

def equality_stats(g, timestamp_col):
    valid = g.dropna(subset=[timestamp_col])
    counts = valid.groupby(["activity", "resource", timestamp_col]).size()
    duplicate_groups = counts[counts >= 2]
    return {
        f"{timestamp_col}_equal_group_max": int(duplicate_groups.max()) if len(duplicate_groups) else 1,
        f"{timestamp_col}_equal_rows": int(duplicate_groups.sum()) if len(duplicate_groups) else 0,
    }

def queue_stats(g):
    valid = g.dropna(subset=["queue_arrival", "start"]).copy()
    if valid.empty:
        return {
            "wait_min": np.nan,
            "wait_median": np.nan,
            "wait_max": np.nan,
            "negative_wait_rows": 0,
            "max_backlog_before_reported_start": np.nan,
            "first_start_minus_first_arrival_min": np.nan,
        }

    waits = (valid["start"] - valid["queue_arrival"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
    backlogs = []
    for start_time in valid["start"].dropna().unique():
        arrivals_by_start = (valid["queue_arrival"] <= start_time).sum()
        starts_strictly_before = (valid["start"] < start_time).sum()
        backlogs.append(max(0, arrivals_by_start - starts_strictly_before))

    return {
        "wait_min": waits.min(),
        "wait_median": waits.median(),

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

Two sensitivity dimensions matter more than the precise PM4Py threshold: whether a resource ID is a person, and whether a displayed timestamp is an exact event time.

**If resource IDs are trusted as capacity-one workers.**

- G4 and the post-15:38 portion of G8 are strongly consistent with serial work because no intervals overlap and each start equals the prior completion.
- G2, G3, G5, and G7 produce overlaps that are incompatible with simple one-person, one-case-at-a-time execution. Under this assumption, the alternatives are true multitasking, group/lifecycle artifacts, or invalid timestamps.
- G1 remains plausible because RBT-7 is not an ordinary worker: the supplied context explicitly gives a four-item fixture capacity.
- G6 does not become interpretable merely by trusting T-F. A capacity-one closer may have submitted seven tickets at once, but the source still does not identify when underlying work happened.

**If resource IDs can represent shared accounts, terminals, scanners, or pools.**

- G5 is no longer evidence that one analyst handled four simultaneous cases. It is consistent with multiple analysts using one account, although the stated maximum of three analysts does not fully explain a four-way displayed overlap.
- G2 could be a group tablet used by a worker or team. The group-start button remains strong evidence for start batching, but the resource ID cannot establish whether inspections were physically concurrent.
- G3 could similarly represent a desktop workflow or shared workstation; common submission remains the leading explanation because it is supported by the narrative, not merely by the resource ID.
- G4 and G8 remain temporally serial, but a shared scanner means their serial scan sequence does not conclusively prove one person performed all physical work.
- G1’s physical interpretation depends primarily on fixture capacity, not identity reliability.

**If timestamps are treated as exact.**

This is the strongest possible interpretation of the displayed lifecycle fields. It makes G1 a likely four-item parallel fixture cycle, G4 a serial four-minute operation, and G8 a 35-minute serial recovery sequence. It also makes G2, G3, and G5 look highly overlapping and forces stronger claims about group behavior or concurrency.

However, exactness does not establish semantic correctness. A timestamp can be exact for a group button, form submission, scanner confirmation, or PLC fixture-cycle event while still being an inaccurate timestamp for an individual item’s physical work.

**If timestamps are interval-censored by stated precision.**

This requires an extra assumption. A documented precision is not necessarily a documented error bound or rounding rule. For sensitivity analysis, one may model a timestamp as lying within one reported precision unit of the displayed value. If the system truncates, an interval such as \([t, t+p)\) is appropriate; if it rounds to nearest, a symmetric interval around \(t\) is more appropriate. Neither rule is supplied for most sources.

- For the 1-second PLC and scanners, G1’s equality, G4’s adjacency, and G8’s recovery rhythm remain much stronger than for minute-level data, although sub-second ordering remains unknown.
- For the 1-minute sources, G2 and G3 ties could conceal events spread within a minute. The group-button and folder-submission narratives still provide independent evidence, so their diagnoses do not rest solely on precision.
- G5’s overlaps are much longer than one minute for most pairs, so shared-account interpretation remains important even under interval censoring.
- G6’s seven identical 17:00 times are unlikely to be explained by ordinary one-minute resolution alone; the manual bulk-form source and common 17:01 recording time point to a common administrative action.
- G7 cannot safely be ordered or assigned exact durations. Its undocumented quarter-hour rounding rule prevents a defensible one-sided interval rule. It should remain excluded or broadly interval-censored until raw timestamps are available.
- G8’s repair timestamps may be precise locally, but comparison with safety timing remains uncertain because the two systems are known to have different clocks.

At least five observed patterns support multiple physical explanations:

1. **G1’s equal starts and ends** support a real four-slot fixture batch, but also support PLC group-stamping at the fixture-cycle level.
2. **G2’s equal starts** support a single “begin group” action, a real concurrent group start, or minute-level coarsening. The source-interface fact makes group start more likely, but not certain.
3. **G3’s equal ends** support delayed folder submission, truly parallel reviews converging at 11:30, or coarse minute timestamps. The narrative favors delayed submission.
4. **G5’s broad overlap** supports several analysts working concurrently, shared-account aggregation, one analyst multitasking, or lifecycle intervals that remain open after active work ends.
5. **G6’s common zero-duration closure** supports end-of-shift data entry, a genuine simultaneous administrative close, or a mixture where ticket work finished earlier but formal closure waited until 17:00.
6. **G7’s common quarter-hour window** supports a real batch, serial work hidden by rounding, concurrent work hidden by rounding, or a combination of those.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

1. **Separate execution timestamps from recording timestamps.** Capture immutable event types for assignment, physical/active start, pause, resume, physical completion, review completion, submission, and database insertion. Do not overwrite execution timestamps with form-submission time. This directly addresses G3 and G6.

2. **Define lifecycle semantics per source and activity.** A data contract should state whether “start” means queue admission, group setup, first touch, active work, scanner handoff, or form opening; and whether “complete” means physical completion, quality approval, folder completion, or form submission. G2 cannot be interpreted correctly without this definition.

3. **Synchronize and monitor source clocks.** Use a common time standard, record device clock offset and synchronization health, and preserve clock source/time zone. This is essential for relating G8’s safety event to repair activity and for comparing upstream queue arrivals with local starts.

4. **Capture worker identity separately from workstation identity.** Store badge-level operator ID, authenticated session ID, workstation/scanner/tablet ID, and role. Shared logins should be explicitly flagged, with session-to-badge mapping where policy permits. This is the main correction for G5 and improves interpretation of T-B, T-C, T-D, and T-H.

5. **Add explicit group and batch identifiers.** Record fixture ID, batch/group ID, parent group event, member-item IDs, group-begin time, group-end time, and intended batch capacity. For RBT-7, this would distinguish a genuine four-item fixture cycle from four copied timestamps.

6. **Record capacity and availability states.** Store workstation capacity, staffing level, fixture slots, maintenance state, safety lockout, downtime start/end, and reason codes. This allows G8-style backlog to be separated into demand overload, unavailable capacity, and unlogged alternative work.

7. **Capture pause/resume and preemption.** For knowledge work and reviews, record active-work versus waiting-for-information intervals. This prevents G3 and G5 from turning long elapsed intervals into false service time.

8. **Publish timestamp precision, accuracy, and rounding metadata.** Retain raw timestamps at the finest available resolution and separately document display precision, clock accuracy, rounding direction, and source insertion behavior. The G7 source specifically needs its quarter-hour rounding rule documented.

9. **Retain source sequence and audit history.** Preserve insertion order, edit history, user who entered or amended a record, and whether an event was generated automatically or manually. This helps distinguish actual execution from end-of-shift reconciliation.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High-confidence temporal interpretations.**

- **G1:** likely an operational four-item fixture batch, conditional on confirming that PLC item events represent actual item-cycle execution rather than one fixture-level transaction copied to members.
- **G2:** high confidence that the displayed common start is a group-level start action, not automatically an individual active-work start.
- **G3:** high confidence that common 11:30 completion is affected by folder-level submission behavior.
- **G4:** high confidence that the scanner-defined events are serial and adjacent.
- **G6:** high confidence that 17:00 is a common administrative closure/submission point rather than reliable individual start and completion timing.
- **G7:** high confidence that quarter-hour coarsening prevents literal interpretation of its displayed intervals.
- **G8:** high confidence that a backlog existed before 15:38 and that recorded work is serial after then; only moderate confidence that safety downtime caused the backlog.

**Queue-analysis treatment.**

- **Potentially usable with explicit definitions:** G1 for queue-to-fixture admission; G4 for queue-to-scanner-defined packing start; G8 for repair-queue delay after clock alignment and validation of availability.
- **Usable only as a different measure:** G2 can measure time until group admission, not necessarily time until individual active inspection. G3 can potentially measure time until recorded review start, but not reliable end-to-end service completion. G5 can measure case admission to a pool, but not queueing at one identifiable analyst.
- **Exclude or interval-censor:** G6 should be excluded from execution-based queue and service metrics unless underlying work events are recovered. G7 should be interval-censored or excluded because displayed starts can occur before upstream arrivals and the rounding rule is unknown.
- **Do not infer safety causality from timestamps alone:** G8 requires cross-system clock alignment and operational confirmation before safety downtime is used as the explanation or as a calendar exception.

**Operational validation required before performance conclusions.**

G1 needs fixture-cycle/item mapping validation; G2 needs individual active-start semantics; G3 needs actual-review-completion capture; G5 needs badge-level assignment and pool-capacity validation; G6 needs execution history rather than closure-form time; G7 needs raw MES timestamps and rounding documentation; and G8 needs clock alignment plus evidence that safety state actually removed repair capacity. G4 is the strongest candidate for direct performance analysis, but only for the scanner-defined version of “Pack item,” not automatically for all physical packing work.
