# 1. Mechanism map

This is a **conditional forecast**, not a measured prediction. The supplied summaries do not include hourly arrivals, service-time distributions, staffing coverage, skill substitutability, or case-level routing relationships. Management’s expected frequencies and “15% higher utilization” should therefore be treated as hypotheses to test.

Three measurement distinctions are important:

- **Nominal handover reduction** means fewer changes of responsible resource or team between observed activities. A local caseworker obtaining advice from a central specialist may involve fewer role changes, but consultation time, repeated requests, and clarification loops can increase.
- **Service time is not queue time.** `insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `get_service_time` can decompose elapsed process time, but an adjacent-event gap may include context gathering, customer response, or decision latency. Measuring a shared queue itself requires queue-entry and activity-start timestamps or an explicit queue identifier.
- **Organizational pooling changes network structure.** Three separate technical nodes becoming one node will mechanically produce a simpler handover network. That does not establish better performance.

Skill, complexity, language, and value-based priority affect workload and waiting differently |

The central queueing risk follows from workload, not from the organizational chart alone. If arrivals approach effective capacity, small increases in demand or service-time variability can cause disproportionate waiting. Utilization also must be calculated using **available skill hours**, accounting for leave, shifts, training, and whether Legal, Risk, and Security capacity is genuinely substitutable.

For a valid before/after comparison, preserve each case’s **originating team** separately from its executing team or queue. Otherwise, centralization can make team-level workload and fairness appear to disappear rather than change.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

## Leading indicators: available within days or weeks

| Indicator | Event-log or operational measure | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Queue pressure and capacity** | Hourly/daily arrivals, starts, completions, WIP, oldest case age, and age buckets for each shared queue; arrivals divided by effective available skill hours | Early warning that utilization is becoming queueing. WIP should be segmented by originating team, complexity, specialty, and priority |
| **Specialist capacity** | Productive specialist hours divided by available hours, separately by skill, team, and shift | Tests whether utilization rose through useful throughput or through deferred work and training displacement |
| **Waiting after handoff** | P50, P90, and P99 elapsed time between adjacent activities, reported separately for direct transfers, advice requests, and approval | Detects queue formation before completed-case cycle time changes |
| **Service-time variability** | Activity-level processing-time distributions, especially p90 and coefficient of variation by specialty | Rising variability consumes buffer capacity and can damage tail latency even if median service time is stable |
| **Handover and loop behavior** | Edges per 100 eligible cases; transfers per case; technical return-loop rate; cross-team and cross-queue transfer rates; reassignment reasons | Tests whether nominal handovers actually decline and whether they are replaced by clarification or consultation |
| **Routing quality** | Referral rate by complexity and required specialty; unnecessary or duplicate referrals; referral-to-rework relationship; change in referral timing | Detects loss of early informal diagnosis and over-centralization |
| **Role and ownership coverage** | Proportion of activities with an identifiable responsible resource; intervals without a clear responsible actor; repeated approvals; override frequency | Role discovery is descriptive; explicit ownership requires a case-owner field or defensible responsibility rules |
| **Knowledge and learning activity** | Local consultation availability, case debriefs, mentoring hours, rotations, and access to challenging work for local staff | Detects early erosion of local expertise and development opportunities |
| **Process concentration** | Central-node degree and WIP, co-work density, resource-similarity changes, and inferred role membership | Reveals growing dependence on central nodes and whether new connections represent useful advice or coordination overhead |
| **Case-package completeness** | Missing required context, repeated document requests, notes containing standardized advice, and clarification requests before specialist work | Tests whether standard routing is supported by sufficient information |

**Utilization caveat:** case-processing intervals are not automatically effort. One case can contain pauses or concurrent activities. Reliable utilization requires effort or work-window records and a staffing calendar.

## Lagging indicators: available after outcomes accumulate

| Indicator | Event-log measure | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **End-to-end cycle-time distribution** | P50, P75, P90, P99 case duration, survival-based completion probability, and cohort backlog | Reveals tail degradation hidden by stable or improved averages |
| **High-complexity performance** | The same measures filtered to high complexity, multiple specialties, difficult quality, and repeated review | Tests whether central routing benefits or penalizes the cases that consume most capacity |
| **Rework and outcome quality** | Rework cases per activity, case-level rework rate, repeat QC, reopened cases, and final negative outcomes | Distinguishes faster initial routing from better final quality |
| **SLA performance** | Misses against explicit, timestamped case deadlines, segmented by priority and originating team | Measures deadline performance rather than assuming that high priority implies a particular deadline |
| **Customer-segment fairness** | Waiting, P90 cycle time, rework, misses, transfers, and satisfaction by language, region, segment, and accommodation need | Detects burdens concentrated in smaller or less well-served populations |
| **Organizational fairness** | Cases and active work per local FTE, specialist hours per case, approval volume, after-hours work, learning access, and override rates by origin | Evaluates distributive and procedural fairness, not just equal case counts |
| **Role and resilience outcomes** | Escalations, unresolved ownership incidents, queue survival during absence, fallback activation, recovery time, and cross-team dependency | Tests whether lower nominal cross-team transfers reflect genuine resilience or centralized dependence |
| **Learning and workforce effects** | Skill-assessment results, local technical consultation rates, staff-reported clarity, mentoring participation, and retention intentions | Captures capability effects that do not appear directly in a process model |

All comparisons should use the same observation rules. For example, networks can be compared on **complete, matched cohorts**, while backlog and utilization should additionally include currently open cases. Comparing only closed cases can hide deterioration caused by a growing queue.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency**  
   A stable median can coexist with sharply worse P90 or P99 cycle times. Shared queues create a common failure domain: one busy period can delay many originating teams simultaneously. Approval age, queue aging, and completion probability deserve more attention than the average.

2. **High-complexity cases and adverse queue composition**  
   Routing all high-complexity work centrally can improve average cycle time if routine local work accelerates, while specialist-intensive cases wait longer. An apparent improvement may reflect a changed case mix rather than faster handling of equally difficult cases.

3. **Local knowledge loss**  
   Missing regional conventions may not become visible until a customer rejects an apparently correct technical answer. Relevant signals include repeat customer contact, clarification requests, regional rework, and rework following cases that bypassed local technical advice.

4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments**  
   Equal numbers of referrals do not imply equal workload or equal access. A high-volume originating team can dominate a shared queue; a low-volume team may lack influence over priority. Language-dependent, accessibility-related, or lower-priority customers may experience extra transfers. Staff fairness also includes after-hours work, learning opportunities, and access to interesting work.

5. **Learning and skill erosion**  
   A short-term increase in central specialist utilization can crowd out training and deliberate practice. Local staff may stop developing technical judgment because hard cases are routed elsewhere. The risk is delayed: it may emerge as longer diagnosis time, poor escalation, and dependence on central experts several months later.

6. **Accountability and role ambiguity**  
   A model can show that TechnicalSS, a specialist, and an approval queue participated without showing who was responsible for the final outcome. Standardized approvals can obscure disagreement about authority. Explicit decision ownership, exception authority, and case accountability must be measured separately from activity participation.

7. **Resilience and exception handling**  
   Fewer cross-team handovers can coexist with worse resilience if absence cover now depends on one central service. Routine routes may improve while emergency, security, accessibility, or conflicting-advice cases are left without a fallback. Exception age, queue bypass frequency, outage impact, and recovery time are essential.

8. **Coordination substitution**  
   Lower nominal handover counts can hide extra emails, consultations, repeated questions, or parallel work. Working-together networks may become denser even as handovers fall. The relevant test is whether total coordination effort and time to resolve uncertainty decline—not whether an edge crosses a particular organizational boundary.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot structure

| Design element | Proposed approach |
|---|---|
| **Pilot scope** | Start with the proposed changes in one or two teams, ideally including East to test relief from overload and at least one of North/South to test local-expertise effects. Keep Intake and simple Eligibility local. Introduce the technical pool and specialist queue in separate gated waves so their effects can be distinguished; test shared approval only after ownership rules are established |
| **Comparison group** | Retain at least one comparable team as a contemporaneous business-as-usual group. Prefer randomized rollout timing among teams, or a matched stepped-wedge design. All teams eventually participate only after safety gates are met |
| **Contamination control** | Ring-fence pilot shared-service capacity so the control group is not disadvantaged. If central staff or queues necessarily serve both groups, record that exposure explicitly; a simple pilot-versus-control comparison will otherwise be misleading |
| **Pre-change observation** | Collect at least 8–12 weeks of baseline data, including absence, staffing, seasonality, and major case-mix changes. Freeze routing and staffing rules before the pilot |
| **Post-change observation** | Run for at least 8–12 weeks and extend until critical specialties and customer segments have enough completed cases. Follow each cohort until outcomes mature—for example, until 95% of cases reach Close or a fixed follow-up horizon, with censored cases reported |
| **Comparison method** | Compare matched before/after cohorts within teams and pilot/control differences over time. Adjust for complexity, specialty, priority, customer segment, case value, staffing, and seasonality. Report estimates and uncertainty rather than only statistically significant changes |
| **Segmentation** | At minimum report by originating team, East versus North/South, complexity, required specialty, language/accommodation where available, customer segment, priority, and case value. Do not let the shared queue erase originating-team information |
| **Process variants** | Track variants separately rather than declaring additional variants bad. Examine which local routes disappear, which central variants emerge, and whether a new variant represents a legitimate exception or repeated rework |
| **Guardrails** | Use absolute limits and relative changes, calibrated to baseline. Illustrative triggers are listed below |
| **Staff feedback** | Conduct short pulses during the first two weeks, fortnightly reviews thereafter, and case debriefs. Include anonymous questions about queue rationing, decision rights, learning opportunities, workload, regional exceptions, and whether “specialist” status matches actual skill |
| **Learning protection** | Retain local technical capability during the pilot. Use local technical liaisons, rotations, protected learning time, and shared case debriefs rather than assuming immediate substitutability |
| **Ownership protection** | Assign a named local case owner, a technical decision owner, and an approval accountability owner. Do not remove the local supervisor until shared approval has demonstrated clear decision rights and a functioning escalation path |
| **Rollback capability** | Maintain routing and staffing reversibility, preserve local fallback capacity, and define who can authorize an exception to the routing rules |

## Illustrative adjustment or rollback triggers

These are starting points for governance, not universally valid causal thresholds:

- **Queue pressure:** effective utilization above 85% for several working days, or above 90% for two reporting periods, with arrivals exceeding completions.
- **Technical waiting:** P50 waiting more than 20% above baseline and P90 more than 30% above baseline for two consecutive weeks.
- **Specialist access:** Legal/Risk or Security P90 waiting more than 30% above baseline, or a material increase in the oldest-case age.
- **Case tails and service:** overall P90 cycle time more than 10% above baseline, accompanied by a worsening SLA or backlog measure.
- **Quality:** rework rate increases by at least 2 percentage points or 20% relative to baseline, or repeat quality failures increase.
- **Fairness:** a customer segment experiences a sustained 20% deterioration in P90 waiting, or satisfaction falls by 0.2 points, without a documented case-mix explanation.
- **Workforce:** local technical consultations fall by more than 20% alongside rising misrouting; mentoring participation declines materially; or ownership gaps exceed an agreed threshold.
- **Resilience:** absence of a central specialist causes a breach of the queue-age or case-tail guardrail without an effective fallback.

A handover increase alone should trigger investigation, not automatic rejection: central advice might legitimately replace rework. Conversely, improved averages should not override a sustained fairness, learning, or resilience breach.

Rollback should first mean **pause expansion, restore routing flexibility, add capacity where necessary, and reverse specific centralization rules**. Undoing an entire structure should be reserved for failures that cannot be corrected through those adjustments.

# 6. Recommendation

**Modify the design and pilot first; do not authorize enterprise-wide rollout or irreversible staffing changes on the current evidence.**

The restructuring has a credible rationale: pooled technical expertise could reduce East’s overload, reduce ad hoc cross-team transfers, and improve consistency. However, the proposed queues concentrate already scarce capacity. The management estimates do not demonstrate lower waiting than the present local processes, and they do not resolve local expertise, accountability, fairness, or learning risks.

A modified design should retain:

- A named local case owner.
- Local technical capability, rotations, and access to early advice.
- Separate capacity protection for Legal, Risk, and Security.
- Explicit technical, specialist, approval, and escalation decision rights.
- A standardized but locally adaptable case package.
- Skill-, complexity-, and accommodation-aware routing rather than simple value-based competition.
- Protected learning and fallback capacity.

Expansion should proceed only if monitoring shows that the new model improves or preserves median **and tail** performance, rework, SLA outcomes, customer-segment results, role clarity, and learning—while providing the anticipated relief to East without transferring disproportionate costs to North, South, or other segments.

Management should not infer the following from early averages:

- **Lower average cycle time does not establish better complex-case performance.**
- **Fewer nominal handovers do not establish less coordination effort.**
- **Higher specialist utilization does not establish spare capacity or better service.**
- **A simpler network does not establish resilience or effective knowledge transfer.**
- **Standardized decisions do not establish fairness, accuracy, or accountability.**
- **Stable rework during a short pilot may reflect unfinished or censored cases rather than genuine improvement.**
- **An apparent East benefit does not make the change equitable if North, South, or particular customer groups absorb the resulting queues and learning losses.**

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The following is **version-neutral Python-style pseudocode**. PM4Py versions differ in graph conversion, variant case identifiers, and service-time output schemas; the adapter names below isolate those differences.

Required preparation:

- Classify a case as pre/post using its **start date**, not individual event timestamps.
- Retain the original case ID through variant filtering.
- Normalize activities, resource names, and timestamps; remove duplicate events and resolve simultaneous-event ordering.
- Preserve `origin_team` separately from `executing_team` or `queue_id`.
- Add explicit queue-entry, activity-start, case-owner, and queue-exit timestamps if true queueing is required.
- Add a timestamped SLA deadline or verified deadline policy. The current schema does **not** identify SLA misses by itself; `priority` is not necessarily a deadline.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"
SEGMENTS = [
    "origin_team",
    "case_complexity",
    "required_specialty",
    "customer_segment",
]

# ---------- Schema adapters ----------

def prepare_log(df):
    d = df.copy()
    d[TS] = pd.to_pd.to_datetime(d[TS], utc=True)
    # Normalize activity/resource aliases and resolve duplicate events first.
    d = d.dropna(subset=[CASE, ACT, TS, RES])
    d = d.sort_values([CASE, TS, RES]).reset_index(drop=True)
    d["event_id"] = range(len(d))
    return d

def as_log(d):
    return pm4py.format_dataframe.log(
        d,
        case_id=CASE,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS,
        resource_key=RES
    )

def as_table(obj):
    # Adapter: normalize PM4Py lists/logs into DataFrames.
    return pm4py.convert.log_to_dataframe(obj)

def edge_table(graph):
    # Preserve edge attributes such as weight.
    return pd.DataFrame([
        {"source": source, "target": target, **attributes}
        for source, target, attributes in graph.edges(data=True)
    ])

def case_interval_table(obj):
    """
    Adapter:
    Normalize get_service_time output to:
      case, from_activity, to_activity,
      interval_start, interval_end, elapsed_hours.
    Include the original case ID and stable originating event ID.
    """
    raise NotImplementedError("PM4Py-version-specific service-time adapter")

def complete_case_ids(d, outcome_column="outcome"):
    # This is only an example: define a reproducible completion policy.
    # Exclude unresolved/censored cases from completed-outcome networks.
    return set(
        d.groupby(CASE)[outcome_column]
         .apply(lambda values: values.astype(str).str.lower()
                .isin(["closed", "approved", "completed", "success"]).all())
         .loc[lambda s: s]
         .index
    )

def case_filter(log, selected_case_ids):
    # Case-level filtering: preserve all events belonging to selected cases.
    # Implementation uses filter_event_attribute_values and the PM4Py
    # case-attribute filtering form supported by the installed version.
    return pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log,
        {CASE: list(selected_case_ids)},
        case_id_glue=CASE
    )

def sla_case_ids(log):
    """
    Requires an additional verified field, e.g. case:sla_due_at,
    from which case:sla_miss is derived consistently for both periods.
    """
    missed = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log,
        {"case:sla_miss": ["True"]},
        case_id_glue=CASE
    )
    return set(as_table(missed)[CASE].astype(str))

# ---------- Waiting/service and handover measures ----------

def add_observed_intervals(d):
    d = d.sort_values([CASE, TS, RES]).copy()

    # Adjacent-event elapsed time. This is NOT necessarily pure queue time.
    d["next_timestamp"] = d.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift(-1)
    d["next_resource"] = d.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift(-1)
    d["observed_wait_h"] = (
        d["next_timestamp"] - d[TS]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0

    d["observed_handover"] = (
        d["next_resource"].notna()
        & (d[RES] != d["next_resource"])
    )
    return d

def service_and_waiting(d):
    # Original copy: activity service intervals.
    service = case_interval_table(
        pm4py.get_service_time(as_log(d.copy()))
    )

    # Separate copy: PM4Py case service-waiting-time transformation.
    waiting_trace = pm4py.get_service_time(
        pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(as_log(d.copy()))
    )
    waiting = case_interval_table(waiting_trace)

    # For waiting, retain intervals representing a completion-to-next-start gap.
    # Compute case durations and waiting distributions from these intervals.
    return service, waiting

# ---------- Network, role, variant, and quality profile ----------

def profile(d, period, segment=None):
    log = as_log(d)
    d = add_observed_intervals(d)

    # Handover, co-work, and association/subcontracting-style networks.
    handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
    together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
    subcontract = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log)

    # Resource similarity and descriptive organizational roles.
    similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)

    # Directly and indirectly connected control-flow graphs.
    dfg = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)

    # Compare the same prespecified variant set structurally.
    # Save the original case IDs before filtering because some PM4Py
    # releases use synthetic variant identifiers.
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    selected_variants = prespecify_comparison_variants(variants)
    selected_log = pm4py.filter_variants(
        log, variants=selected_variants
    )

    # Newly appearing variants are reported separately, not silently removed.
    temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)

    rework = as_table(
        pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
    )
    service, waiting = service_and_waiting(d)
    missed_cases = sla_case_ids(log)

    return {
        "period": period,
        "segment": segment or {"all": "all"},
        "n_cases": d[CASE].nunique(),
        "handover": edge_table(handover),
        "working_together": edge_table(together),
        "subcontracting": edge_table(subcontract),
        "resource_similarity": similarity,
        "roles": roles,
        "dfg": dfg,
        "performance_dfg": performance_dfg,
        "variants": variants,
        "temporal_features": temporal,
        "rework": rework,
        "service_intervals": service,
        "waiting_intervals": waiting,
        "sla_missed_case_ids": missed_cases,
    }

pre_ids = set(case_start.loc[case_start < pilot_start].index)
post_ids = set(
    case_start.loc[(case_start >= pilot_start) &
                   (case_start < analysis_cutoff)].index
)

pre = d[d[CASE].isin(pre_ids)].copy()
post = d[d[CASE].isin(post_ids)].copy()

# Do not compare mature completed cases in one period with censored
# backlog-heavy cases in another without an explicit survival analysis.
# Apply the same complete-cohort rule to both periods.

results = [
    profile(pre, "pre"),
    profile(post, "post"),
]

# ---------- Segment by originating team, complexity, specialty, segment ----------

for period, period_df in [("pre", pre), ("post", post)]:
    # One row per case avoids overweighting cases with more events.
    case_attributes = period_df.groupby(CASE)[SEGMENTS].first()

    for dimension in SEGMENTS:
        for value in case_attributes[dimension].dropna().unique():
            ids = set(case_attributes[
                case_attributes[dimension] == value
            ].index)

            if len(ids) < prespecified_minimum_case_count:
                results.append({
                    "period": period,
                    "segment": {dimension: str(value)},
                    "status": "insufficient sample",
                    "n_cases": len(ids),
                })
                continue

            segment_df = period_df[period_df[CASE].isin(ids)]
            results.append(
                profile(
                    segment_df,
                    period,
                    segment={dimension: str(value)}
                )
            )

# ---------- Pooling-aware before/after network comparison ----------

for period, period_df in [("pre", pre), ("post", post)]:
    d_common = period_df.copy()

    # A common unit prevents node consolidation from masquerading as
    # a performance improvement. Use this ONLY for the comparable view.
    d_common[RES] = d_common["origin_team"]

    results.append(
        profile(
            d_common,
            period,
            segment={"network_unit": "originating_team"}
        )
    )
```

Interpretation should follow the structural hypotheses:

- **Handover networks:** compare normalized edge rates and waiting times, not only the number of nodes.
- **Working-together networks:** rising co-work without a workload increase may indicate valuable advice; rising co-work alongside clarification loops may indicate coordination overhead.
- **Subcontracting networks:** these are association structures, not proof of actual procurement, expertise transfer, or management intent.
- **Resource similarity and role discovery:** use them to identify changed coordination patterns, not to infer that role boundaries are necessarily optimal.
- **Variants and performance DFGs:** separate legitimate new routes from repeated rework and exception loops.
- **Service and waiting times:** distinguish elapsed case time, specialist processing time, and true queue residence.
- **Rework and SLA misses:** calculate case-level rates using the same eligible and complete cohorts in both periods.
- **Fairness:** retain originating-team and customer-segment identities through every transformation and report distributions, not only pooled means.
