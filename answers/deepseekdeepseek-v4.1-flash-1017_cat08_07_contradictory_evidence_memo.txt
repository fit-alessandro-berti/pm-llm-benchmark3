# 1. Executive memo

**Recommendation: adopt a controlled hybrid (E), not national scaling or a rollback.** Scale coordinator ownership where evidence is strongest—simple, low-risk houses—while suspending two risky variants immediately: automatic field-survey skipping for apartments, medium-risk and old permit-sensitive buildings, and moving activation checks after billing start without a hard conformance gate. Treat national scaling as contingent on segment-level outcomes, conformance controls, and better measurement.

The pilot shows material benefits that should not be discarded. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%, visible cost fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161, and simple low-risk houses improved by 6 days median, 8 days p90, +3pp first-time-right, and -1pp complaints. Coordinator ownership therefore has a credible safe core.

But the redesign is not uniformly safe. The tail worsened overall: p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days. First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation from 7% to 15%, second visits from 11% to 18%, and SLA breach from 13% to 16%. Apartments and old buildings drive much of the harm: apartments show p90 +12 days and complaints +5pp; old buildings show p90 +25 days and complaints +7pp. The process model’s high fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51) means it replays pilot cases but permits too many exception combinations. Alignments show billing before successful activation, installation repeated without field survey, missing permit checks for older buildings, and complaint escalation after billing. These are operating-control failures, not just model noise.

Immediate action should therefore preserve the simple-house benefits while containing tail and compliance risk. Deploy coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses with standard permit and activation checks. For apartments, old buildings, business customers and rural long-distance installs, keep baseline survey and activation sequencing while testing coordinator ownership in controlled regions. Add hard stops or escalation for billing before successful activation and missing permit checks. Manage coordinator load; 93% utilization and reported stalls during coordinator absence indicate reduced resilience. Do not base the business case on visible pre-complaint cost, because the incomplete downstream estimate already reverses the apparent savings (EUR 198 to EUR 207).

Measure first: validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes, monitor activation/permit conformance, instrument start timestamps and exception reason codes, and measure full 60-day downstream cost and complaints. Broaden rollout only when, by segment, p90/p95 and complaints are no worse than baseline, activation-before-billing is near zero, permit conformance is restored, full downstream cost is at or below baseline, and coordinator absence does not stall cases. The defensible decision is to scale the safe core, pause unsafe variants, and run controlled measurement before national rollout.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision:** Fitness means the discovered model can replay most observed pilot traces; it does not mean the process is well controlled. Precision fell to 0.51 because the model permits many combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeated installs, second visits, and billing-start timing. This is exactly what the alignment diagnostics show: billing before successful activation, installation repeated without survey, missing permit checks, and complaint escalation after billing. The model is “fitting” a more permissive and exception-rich operating reality.

- **Improved median but worse tail duration:** The redesign simplified the common path, especially coordinated ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple cases. That pulls the median down. Complex or risky cases—apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, failed visits, manual activation, and post-billing repair—became more variable. The temporal profile supports this: validation-to-scheduling improved for simple houses, while installation-to-activation worsened for apartments and failed-visit-to-second-visit delays increased under high coordinator utilization. Median improvement and tail deterioration can coexist when average simple work is accelerated but exception paths are made less resilient.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Recorded handovers fell because a coordinator owns more of the case. That can improve accountability and speed for straightforward work. But the working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent. The DFG/model precision also shows more allowed exception combinations, suggesting that context is concentrated rather than systematically shared. Fewer formal transfers can therefore hide a single-point-of-failure and reduce recoverability.

- **Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost:** Visible cost before complaint handling improved from EUR 184 to EUR 161, plausibly because coordinators and merged steps reduced waiting and handover effort. However, first-time-right fell, second visits and manual activation rose, complaints increased, and the incomplete full-cost estimate worsened to EUR 207. The redesign may shift cost from front-end coordination into activation repair, revisits, complaint handling, and billing disputes. Because downstream complaint cost is incomplete after week 4, the visible saving is not yet an economic win.

- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** The redesign is not one intervention; it bundles coordinator ownership, merged scheduling, survey skipping, and post-billing activation. Simple low-risk houses benefit from the streamlined common path. Medium-risk apartments and old permit-sensitive buildings are more exposed to the risky parts: a wrong survey-skip rule, missing permit checks, activation after billing, and longer failed-visit recovery. The segment effects and DFG/alignment evidence point to a heterogeneity problem: the redesign should be segmented, not evaluated only at the aggregate level.

| **8** | **M8. Collect customer experience separately for speed to appointment and working service after installation** | Which customer-experience driver dominates NPS: speed or reliable activation/service. | If “working service” dominates, prioritize activation and rework controls over further speed gains; if speed dominates for simple houses, support segmented rollout. |

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E, a controlled hybrid with hard operating gates.**

- **Immediate action:** Approve Redesign R as a segmented operating model, not a national default. Deploy coordinator ownership for simple, low-risk houses with standard permit checks and mandatory activation test before billing start. For apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and rural long-distance installs, retain baseline survey and activation sequencing while testing coordinator ownership in controlled regions. Place hard stops or escalation on billing before successful activation and on missing permit checks. Address coordinator load and absence backup; 93% utilization and reported stalls are scaling blockers.

- **What to pause:** Pause national scaling of Redesign R. Pause automatic field-survey skipping outside validated simple/low-risk strata, especially for medium-risk apartments and older buildings. Pause post-billing activation unless conformance monitoring can prove activation success before billing and the segment is low-risk with full cost and complaint tracking.

- **What to scale:** Scale coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses; scale measurement instrumentation and conformance monitoring across all pilot regions. Scale operating controls: activation-before-billing gate, permit-check gate, exception reason codes, coordinator backup/context sharing, and capacity management for second-visit and activation teams.

- **What to measure next:** Prioritize M6, M7, M2, M4, M3, M1, M5, and M8 as ranked above. At minimum: validate the risk rule; monitor activation and permit conformance; instrument start timestamps; collect 60-day downstream costs; add exception reason codes; test survey skipping by risk stratum; test coordinator absence resilience; and separate customer speed from working-service experience.

- **Thresholds to justify broader rollout:** By segment, require p90 and p95 cycle time no worse than baseline; complaint rate no worse than baseline 6.1%; first-time-right at least baseline 82%; activation-before-billing near zero; permit-check conformance above 99% for permit-sensitive buildings; full downstream cost at or below baseline; coordinator utilization safely below 93% with tested absence coverage; and second-visit/activation capacity not saturated. If simple houses continue to show strong gains while higher-risk segments meet these gates, expand in stages. If apartment/old-building tails or activation complaints persist, keep R restricted to simple low-risk houses and retain baseline controls for the rest.
