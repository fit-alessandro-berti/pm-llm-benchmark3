## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

The exposure is **assignment to an organizational policy**, not simply changing the stored flag from 1 to 0. In particular:

- **P0:** current priority triage, review/escalation routing, capacity, and reviewer information.
- **P1:** suppress the flag’s display. The flag is still generated, automated routing based on it remains unchanged, the risk score remains visible, and no case is forced to have `flag=0`.
- **P2:** retain priority triage but withdraw automatic mandatory review referral. Human-requested review remains possible; this does **not** force non-occurrence.
- **P3:** retain routing and add a defined number of protected manual-review slots or blocks for flagged cases.
- **P4:** must be specified further: **P4-C** changes senior-referral criteria without changing senior capacity; **P4-K** adds senior capacity without changing criteria. Widening and narrowing criteria are separate interventions.

The common population is all eligible arrivals at participating sites during a prespecified period, analyzed as assigned. Cases are the outcome units, although interference may require cluster assignment and inference. Pending cases should be censored or evaluated using a common restricted-time horizon, not excluded.

I use **assignment-policy total effects and randomized interventional-policy contrasts**, not natural direct/indirect effects. Randomized contrasts can target review requirements or reserved capacity without cross-world assumptions. They are mechanism-targeting **policy analogues**, however: requiring review also changes workload, and protecting capacity also changes queue composition. A conventional controlled direct effect holding actual waiting fixed is not identified because realized waiting is an allocable, interference-dependent quantity. A separable-effect extension could condition on pre-arrival strata, but not on post-flag discretionary decisions.

Shared queues make a case’s potential outcome depend on other assignments and service schedules. A protected slot can therefore change unflagged waiting, violating individual SUTVA. Reviewer assignment and discretionary review/escalation decisions may themselves depend on the flag and predict later processing or outcomes. Conditioning on these post-exposure choices can induce selection and exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding. Flag handling may also change staffing, future referrals, or case mix, creating system-level feedback; coarse `arrival_load` does not resolve it. Waiting is allocable, so fixing its observed value is not a credible individual intervention. These are plausible feedback paths, not established causal relationships. In particular, changing a displayed bit, suppressing automatic routing, and setting the stored flag to zero are different interventions.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Within-risk-score or local-threshold design: is triage acceleration a threshold policy rather than severity composition? | Audit the flag rule. If a genuine cutoff exists, use a prespecified local regression-discontinuity/fuzzy-RD design around it; otherwise use narrow score matching or overlap weighting. This identifies a **whole-policy threshold contrast**, not P1 alone | Exact score or narrow score bands; complexity; site; calendar period; pre-arrival congestion. Do not condition on priority or review when estimating a total effect | Sufficient cases on both sides; score is measured before routing; no other discontinuous policy at the cutoff | Check density, bandwidth sensitivity, pre-flag covariate continuity, and whether scores such as 0.58 unflagged and 0.64 flagged contradict a simple deterministic cutoff | A discontinuity in pre-flag complexity, prior outcomes, or data quality invalidates the RD. A priority/triage jump that vanishes with bandwidth weakens a sharp-threshold interpretation; empty overlap makes extrapolation unsupported |
| Queue-state-aware analysis: how much of the delay is congestion? | Prospectively log review-ready time, actual service start, queue membership, arrivals, abandonments, and staffed minutes. Model time-varying queue exposure using queue survival or discrete-event analysis | Calendar time, resource calendar, cases already waiting/being processed, offered workload, and case complexity. Report these as a mechanism analysis, not as an unmediated total-effect adjustment | Queue-entry timestamps mean readiness, service clocks are stable, and unlogged arrivals are negligible | Compare flag coefficients before and after queue-state adjustment; stratify by initial backlog and queue-depth trajectories | If excess flagged waiting disappears after accounting for queue state, the delay is substantially interference/load-mediated. If flagged cases still start earlier within the same queue state, priority within the queue is more credible |
| Policy-change or randomized-encouragement design: what does P1 or P2 change? | Randomize P1 display suppression versus current display, or randomize mandatory-review referral versus discretionary referral. Use cluster randomization if queues cross cases. Estimate ITT and, with a valid first stage and exclusion relevance, encouragement-based effects | Pre-arrival strata: site/team, volume block, complexity mix, risk-score distribution. Do not balance on post-flag review occurrence | Assignment is implemented as specified; limited crossover; no direct assignment effect on outcome except through the policy; interference is contained or modeled | Check allocation balance, actual display/receipt, first-stage routing, and contamination | No triage change in P1 ITT undermines a display-driven triage mechanism. No change in review occurrence under P2 undermines its occurrence pathway. High crossover or exclusion failure makes encouragement-based effects uninterpretable |
| Mediation-oriented analysis: through occurrence, waiting, escalation, or workload? | Randomize the corresponding downstream policy—required review, reserved review slot, or senior slot—and estimate its effect on stage-specific events and cycle time. Treat observed occurrence as a downstream response, not a baseline adjustment variable | Randomized policy assignment and pre-arrival strata; queue block and resource as designed allocation factors; use repeated event or multistate methods | Randomized assignment removes upstream confounding; the policy has adequate support; spillovers are absent or modeled | Examine temporal ordering, occurrence and waiting responses, total work, and whether any assigned policy changes other stages | If randomized discretionary access neither changes occurrence nor cycling, the occurrence mechanism is weak. If protected slots reduce waiting without improving total cycling, the relevant mechanism is redistribution or lack of binding capacity |
| Negative-control outcome or exposure: is the flag bundle generating suspicious pre-flag differences? | Use pre-flag risk-score/input completeness, registration completeness, and earliest registration fields as negative controls; the flag should not change already-recorded inputs | Exact score band, intake day/site, complexity rule version | Flagging occurs after these fields; audit and timestamp processes are comparable across eventual flag groups | Compare negative controls across eventual flag groups and test for associations with display assignment | Systematic pre-flag differences indicate selection, measurement, or feedback rather than a clean post-flag treatment comparison; no association is reassuring but not definitive |
| Sensitivity analysis for unobserved risk: how strong must hidden confounding be to change conclusions? | Use pre-flag adjustment, rich outcome adjudication, negative controls, and quantitative bias analysis/Rosenbaum bounds. Report E-values only as supplementary diagnostics, not identification | All credible pre-arrival factors: score, complexity, site, calendar congestion, baseline workload, referral source | No unmeasured factor is both a strong flag determinant and an independent outcome determinant after adjustment | Vary plausible confounder strengths and outcome harms; inspect whether similar-severity cases receive different treatment | Easily overturned estimates or large hidden-risk effects requiring implausible assumptions would reverse the mechanism ranking; otherwise conclusions remain more robust, not “proved” |
| Interference-aware design: does protection improve the system or only relocate waiting? | Randomize matched reviewer shifts or queue blocks to protected versus pooled capacity. Measure spillovers to neighboring flagged and unflagged cases and aggregate effects at the operational-unit level | Shift/team, time block, expected arrival volume, complexity and senior-need mix | Clusters have limited cross-arm queue interaction; capacity calendars are known; cluster-level inference is used | Plot control-case waiting against treated-case volume; check temporal autocorrelation and cross-resource spillovers | If controls worsen as protection expands without system throughput improvement, abandon standalone protection in favor of added pooled capacity. Benefits confined to one arm with no spillover support a localized capacity effect |
| Historical policy-change design: what changed when the flag bundle was introduced? | Use an event-time interrupted-time or difference-in-differences design with several pre/post periods and comparable unflagged workflows. Audit whether the flag, review rule, staffing, and staffing levels changed together | Calendar fixed effects, site/team, pre-period workload, complexity and risk-score mix | No concurrent intervention or anticipatory staffing change; composition can be modeled; parallel untreated trends are credible | Inspect pre-trends, anticipation, composition shifts, and whether the “flag effect” coincides with simultaneous capacity changes | Pre-existing trends or anticipatory changes invalidate a simple pre/post attribution. A sharp discontinuity confined to flagged routing supports a policy-bundle effect, but still does not isolate display from priority |
| Decision-quality design: does faster completion improve or harm decisions? | Have independent blinded adjudicators assess case information and the appropriate decision without seeing flag/resource assignment; follow cases for prespecified adverse outcomes and appeals | True residual risk, urgency, evidence completeness, and case mix; adjudication reliability | Decisions can be meaningfully counterfactually assessed; follow-up is comparable; the gold standard is independent of the flag | Inter-rater reliability, blinded versus operational decisions, harmful-error rates, and subgroup harms | If faster cycling increases preventable approval harm or inappropriate rejection without compensating benefits, the preferred policy should favor review/senior capacity despite longer median cycling |

### Limited pilot: P3

Keep risk-score calculation, flag display, priority triage, automatic review referral, and escalation criteria unchanged. Enroll flagged arrivals in matched reviewer-shift blocks, stratifying by arrival load and complexity. Randomize blocks—not reviewers’ discretionary decisions—to current pooled review or P3 protected review slots, with concurrent unflagged cases measured for spillovers. Analyze assignment by block and case.

Cap protected reservation at 40% of scheduled review minutes and release unused reservations after 30 minutes. Stop or modify protection if unflagged review-wait quantiles worsen by more than 20% for two consecutive blocks, or if protected backlog exceeds two hours.

The primary outcome is actual review waiting, measured from logged **review-ready/queue-entry** to service start. Separately retain triage-done-to-manual-start as an upstream discretionary-delay measure. Secondary outcomes are total cycling, senior waiting, review/escalation occurrence, throughput, reviewer behavior, and independently adjudicated decision errors and timely harms.

Collect event timestamps, queue-entry and readiness decisions, active-work intervals, resource calendars, routing reasons, flag/score version, complexity, arrivals, offered workload, abandonment, and blinded outcome adjudication. Use synchronized clocks. Predefine sample-size support and stopping rules; absence of a quality difference is not evidence of equivalence if follow-up is small.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** Independent adjudication shows that flagged cases contain substantially more preventable residual risk, and faster or less-reviewed completion produces more harmful approvals. Reverse the interpretation of longer cycling as unnecessary overhead; restore or strengthen review where it prevents greater harm.

2. **Queue interference:** P3 mainly shifts waiting to unflagged cases, senior work, or future shifts without increasing system throughput. Abandon standalone protection in favor of genuinely additional pooled capacity or revised arrival capacity.

3. **Decision quality:** A sufficiently powered, blinded evaluation shows that P2 or P3 increases inappropriate approvals, inappropriate rejections, or compliance failures without a compensating benefit. Reverse the recommendation even if median cycle time improves.

4. **Timestamp semantics:** Audit shows that stage starts are scheduled appointments, retrospective entry times, or policy milestones rather than actual service starts—or that clock corrections materially change the contrasts. Suspend the acceleration/delay interpretation until corrected event semantics are available.

5. **Positivity or overlap:** The target population has essentially no comparable controls at nearby risk scores, complexity levels, or loads, or few untreated capacity blocks. Restrict conclusions to directly randomized support; do not extrapolate a flag-policy effect to unsupported cases.

6. **Reviewer behavior:** Reviewers systematically change request timing, service intensity, escalation, or documentation in response to flag visibility, protected capacity, or study participation. Revise the intervention around standardized decisions and audited behavior, or pause P1/P2 if salience cannot be separated from routing.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** Flagged cases have faster median arrival-to-triage completion—48 minutes versus 3h15—but longer total cycling, 16h27 versus 7h06. All flagged cases undergo review; 6/10 escalate. Conditional review-start delays are 6h45 versus 1h45, and high-load flagged cases have longer observed intervals. These are descriptive, small-sample contrasts; review and escalation waiting also include discretionary delay.

**Plausible mechanism.** A priority lane may reduce triage waiting and service, while the routing bundle increases review occurrence and senior referrals. Additional shared-queue work can then dominate the initial saving. Resource selection and salience-sensitive reviewer behavior may amplify or offset that process.

**Unidentified.** No observation identifies a flag-only direct effect, a natural indirect effect, or a unique occurrence/waiting decomposition. Hidden risk, changing case mix, nonrandom staffing, interference, local timestamp semantics, and post-flag decision selection remain unresolved. Approve/Reject is not decision quality.

**Next evidence.** Pilot P3 with cluster-randomized protected review capacity, unchanged routing, queue-state measurement, concurrent unflagged controls, and blinded outcome adjudication. Estimate assignment-policy and capacity effects, not a scalar “flag equals zero” effect. The pilot should reveal whether reducing flag waiting improves system throughput without harming other cases or decisions.
