# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Management  
**Subject:** Fiber-installation redesign decision  
**Decision:** Adopt **Alternative E**, treating Redesign R as separable components rather than a single national package.

Continue the simplified flow only for **verified simple, low-risk houses**. Retain coordinator ownership selectively, with workload caps and backup ownership. Immediately suspend survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old or permit-sensitive buildings, businesses, rural cases, and any order with missing risk attributes. Restore the rule that successful activation must precede billing, except for explicitly authorized and auditable exceptions.

**Observed facts:** Pilot R reduced median duration from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with 3+ handovers from 34% to 12%. However, p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. Visible cost declined, but the incomplete downstream-cost estimate increased. Benefits were concentrated in simple houses; every other reported segment had a worse p90 and lower first-time-right performance.

Process evidence reinforces—not proves—the risk pattern. The DFG shows less surveying and more direct installation, manual activation, failed visits, second visits, and post-billing complaints. Alignments flag billing before successful activation and missing expected permit checks. Temporal profiles locate long delays after failed visits and billing. The pilot model’s high fitness does not establish process quality: its low precision and generalization indicate a flexible, unstable exception process.

**Interpretation:** R appears to remove low-value waiting for straightforward orders while shifting risk downstream for complex orders. Survey misclassification, post-billing activation, and overloaded coordinators are plausible mechanisms, but causal attribution is not secure because R was bundled, pilot regions had more difficult buildings, attributes were incomplete, and there was no concurrent randomized control.

**Actions for the next 60–90 days:**

1. **Protect customers and control risk:** Reinstate activation-before-billing and required permit checks; use a normative conformance model rather than the permissive discovered model.
2. **Target benefits:** Continue combined validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership for cases with complete, verified low-risk attributes. All uncertain cases default to the safeguarded process.
3. **Stabilize resources:** Cap coordinator and second-visit utilization at a sustainable target, provision named backup coordinators and shared case notes, and use spare survey capacity for risk validation and first-visit quality.
4. **Embed controlled experiments:** Validate the risk rule, randomize survey skipping only in strata where genuine equipoise remains, measure 60-day downstream cost, and test coordinator versus shared-team resilience.

Do not approve national scaling based on the €161 visible-cost figure or the improved median alone. Conversely, do not fully roll back: the simple-house results are operationally meaningful. Wider rollout should be authorized segment by segment only after concurrent-control evidence shows that speed gains persist without material deterioration in tail time, first-time-right, complaints, SLA, total cost, activation sequencing, or permit compliance.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

Fitness asks whether the discovered model can replay observed cases. Many pilot cases followed the new simplified coordinator path, so a model containing that path can achieve high fitness.

Precision asks whether the model also allows behavior not actually observed. The pilot model permits many combinations of skipped surveys, repeated visits, manual activation, billing, and complaints. It can therefore replay cases while imposing weak behavioral constraints. Its low generalization also suggests that the six-week model may not represent future or national behavior reliably.

Thus, high fitness is not evidence that the process is compliant or well controlled. A discovered, permissive model should not be the control standard; billing and permit monitoring should use a separate normative model.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

The redesign accelerates common, uncomplicated cases, shifting the center of the distribution left. Simple houses improved materially, and other segments often had small median gains.

A minority of failed or misclassified cases then experience manual activation, permit remediation, second visits, complaints, and overloaded exception queues. These cases have disproportionate influence on p90 and p95 but limited influence on the median. Sparse start timestamps further prevent separating active work from queue time within those long cases.

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Single-case ownership reduces transfer delays and unclear accountability. However, fewer handovers do not necessarily mean fewer dependencies.

The working-together network became more centralized, coordinator utilization reached 93%, and context was not consistently shared. An absent or overloaded coordinator can therefore stall an entire case. The design traded distributed knowledge and redundancy for simpler accountability.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

Skipping surveys and reducing handovers remove visible labor and waiting, explaining the lower pre-complaint cost. Some of those savings may be cost displacement rather than elimination.

Failed visits, manual activation, second visits, activation-specialist work, complaints, credits, and billing disputes occur later or in other teams. The visible cost excludes some of these consequences, and the incomplete total-cost estimate has already reversed direction from a €23 saving to a €9 increase.

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

A field survey or permit check has little value when the address is predictable and installation conditions are standard. Combining validation and scheduling is therefore beneficial for simple houses.

Apartments and old buildings have more hidden infrastructure, access, permit, and activation dependencies. A predictive false negative in those segments can save one survey but trigger a failed visit, second visit, manual activation, or complaint. The aggregate result is also affected by pilot regions having more difficult buildings, so stratified concurrent comparisons are needed before attributing all deterioration to R.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Principal risks | Reasonable when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures median speed, lower visible cost, fewer handovers, and simple-case benefits quickly. | Exposes all segments to worse tails, rework, complaints, billing-before-activation risk, permit gaps, and capacity bottlenecks. Assumes pilot effects are causal and nationally generalizable. | Only after segment-level controlled evidence meets quality, tail, total-cost, compliance, and capacity gates. It is not reasonable now. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Rapidly restores known survey, activation, and permit safeguards; may reduce second visits and complaints. Operationally simple. | Gives up strong simple-house benefits and may reintroduce unnecessary waiting and handovers. Aggregate comparison is confounded, so a full rollback may overreact. | If activation or permit controls cannot be enforced immediately, severe customer harm persists, or even verified low-risk cases fail predefined gates. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only** | Preserves accountability and handover reduction while removing the two riskiest design changes. Easier to isolate organizational effects. | Ownership benefits have not been causally separated; 93% utilization, absence stalls, and centralization can create queues and fragility. | With workload caps, backup ownership, shared case context, and an M5 comparison against shared-team ownership. This is a strong default for non-simple segments, but not yet an unconditional national scale. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment** | Produces causal and segment-specific evidence; can isolate survey skipping and ownership. Limits exposure. | Delays benefit realization, requires disciplined assignment, and may suffer contamination across teams. Some risky practices should not be randomized without safeguards. | When eligibility data are reliable and genuine equipoise exists. It should be embedded in the next phase rather than used as a reason for inaction. |
| **E. Segment-targeted hybrid** | Captures demonstrated simple-house benefits, restores safeguards for complex cases, and generates evidence before expansion. Treats R as components rather than an all-or-nothing bundle. | Adds routing and governance complexity; classifier errors or missing attributes can expose risky cases; parallel processes require training and monitoring. | Reasonable now if uncertain cases default to safeguards, activation-before-billing is enforced, capacity is controlled, and experiments use concurrent controls. **Recommended.** |

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranking reflects the likelihood that information will change the **scope of rollout**. Implementation urgency differs: M7 and M3 should begin immediately even though some higher-ranked items provide more causal information.

| Rank | Measurement or experiment | Uncertainty resolved | How it could change the decision |
|---:|---|---|---|
| **1** | **M6. Validate the building-risk rule** | Whether the eligibility mechanism can distinguish safe survey skips from cases needing surveys or permits. Validation should use a prospective holdout or shadow-survey sample to avoid selection bias. | High sensitivity and calibration would support targeted expansion. Poor performance would require suspending survey skipping, improving features, or relying on explicit rules rather than prediction. |
| **2** | **M1. Randomized survey-skipping pilot by risk stratum** | Whether skipping the survey causally affects first-time-right, tail duration, complaints, and total cost within each stratum. | Benign results could expand eligibility; confirmed harm would make surveys mandatory for that stratum. Old permit-sensitive cases should not be randomized until legal and operational requirements are clear. |
| **3** | **M4. Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing** | Whether visible savings survive complaints, second visits, specialist work, credits, and disputes. | A lower full cost with non-inferior quality strengthens the business case. A higher full cost could eliminate the economic rationale even where median speed improves. |
| **4** | **M7. Conformance monitoring for activation and permits** | Whether the observed sequencing reflects persistent control failures or logging artifacts, and whether corrective controls work. | Persistent unauthorized billing-before-activation or missing required permits should trigger affected-segment rollback. Near-zero, reconciled deviations would remove a major rollout blocker, though not prove customer quality. |
| **5** | **M5. Compare coordinator with shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether coordinator ownership itself improves flow and whether backup arrangements prevent single-point-of-failure stalls. | Supports broader ownership rollout if benefits persist under sustainable load and absence. Otherwise, adopt a coordinator-plus-pod or shared-team model. |
| **6** | **M3. Structured reason codes** | Which mechanisms drive failed visits, manual activation, second visits, and complaints—for example access, permit, bad risk classification, network fault, or customer reschedule. | Determines whether to change the predictive rule, staffing, customer preparation, activation policy, or permit routing. It improves intervention design more than causal attribution. |
| **7** | **M2. Start timestamps for installation and activation work** | Whether long durations reflect processing effort, waiting for specialists, travel, customer access, or queueing. | Could justify capacity or scheduling changes and improve cost estimates, but is less likely by itself to reverse the segment-targeting decision. |
| **8** | **M8. Split customer experience into appointment speed and working-service quality** | Whether customers value faster scheduling but are penalizing post-installation reliability or billing. | Helps optimize communications and experience design. Existing complaint, first-time-right, and NPS evidence is already sufficient to block uncontrolled scaling, so its immediate decision value is lower. |

The measurements should use complete pre-treatment attributes, concurrent controls, prespecified segment definitions and non-inferiority margins, and 60-day follow-up. Historical baseline comparison alone is inadequate because of regional and building-mix differences.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Choose Alternative E, supplemented by controlled elements of C and D.**

- **Immediate action:** Freeze national rollout. Reinstate successful activation before billing and required permit checks. Default any case with missing or uncertain risk data to the safeguarded process. Add workload limits, backup coordinators, shared notes, and weekly segment-level conformance review.
- **Pause:** Survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old or permit-sensitive buildings, businesses, and rural cases; routine post-billing activation; any expansion based solely on median duration, handovers, or visible cost.
- **Scale:** Combined validation/scheduling and coordinator-supported flow for verified simple low-risk houses. Expand coordinator ownership elsewhere only within a capacity-controlled M5 test; do not assume the bundled pilot isolated its effect.
- **Measure next:** Start M6, M4, M7, and M3 immediately. Then run M1 in eligible strata and M5 for the ownership model. Add M2 to locate queueing and M8 for experience design.
- **Broader-rollout gate:** Require complete risk attributes and validated rule sensitivity of at least 95% for survey/permit-sensitive cases. Against concurrent controls, require the upper 95% confidence bound to show no more than a 2-day p90 deterioration, 3-day p95 deterioration, or 1-point deterioration in first-time-right, complaints, or SLA; require a meaningful speed benefit or lower 60-day total cost. Also require zero unauthorized billing-before-activation, complete documentation of required permits, and sustained coordinator and second-visit utilization below approximately 85%.

These gates should be met for at least two consecutive cohorts with full 60-day follow-up before expanding a component to a new segment. Post-billing activation should remain prohibited unless separately tested and approved as a controlled exception.
