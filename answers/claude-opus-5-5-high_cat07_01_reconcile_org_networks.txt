# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

Before reading individual metrics, three checks on the evidence itself. They affect how much weight any single number can carry.

- **Handover volumes exceed Mira's event count.** Her outgoing top edges add up to 1,640 (210+180+350+330+290+280) and her incoming top edges to 1,580 (920+160+140+190+170). She has only 960 events. Under PM4Py's default handover (`beta=0`, direct succession within a case), each event can have at most one successor, so her outgoing handovers should be at most 960. The table was therefore probably built with indirect succession (`beta>0`), on a different or unfiltered log (for example with both `start` and `complete` lifecycle events), or with counts from different time windows. Her handover centrality may be inflated compared with her working-together metrics.
- **Working-together denominators are unclear.** If the co-case rate means "share of all 4,100 cases", then Alice–Carla at 27% (≈1,107 cases) is impossible, because Carla appears in only 870 cases (21%). The pair rates probably use different or filtered denominators, so comparisons across pairs are unsafe until the metric is recomputed.
- **`exception_flag` behaves like an event-level or changing attribute.** Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules touch almost every case, yet their exception shares differ: 0.19 against 0.31. If the flag were a fixed case attribute, these two numbers would be almost the same. The flag is probably set or updated during triage or fraud screening, and possibly by Mira herself. This matters when we stratify by it, including in the absence analysis.

There are also smaller signals. "Assign Specialist" appears in Mira's activity profile but not in the official 14-activity catalogue. Close Claim is attributed only to Temp_2 (about 470 cases out of 4,100). Vendor_Pay has out-degree 0.00, which implies that Issue Payment is always the last recorded event, even though Close Claim should follow it. Possible causes are batch-loaded vendor timestamps, closures that are not logged, or ordering errors.

## Handover-of-work network

**What it does:** It records who directly follows whom inside a case. That is sequence adjacency, not collaboration. Mira has the highest out-degree (0.82, about 12 of the 15 other resources), a high in-degree (0.51) and the highest betweenness (0.77). She receives 920 routings from Bot_Rules, escalations from both temporary workers (360), and returned answers from both specialists (300). She sends work to both specialists and to all four adjusters in both teams.

**What it implies:** She is a routing junction on the exception path. She links automated triage, two regional teams, two specialists and the temporary staff. Degree centrality measures the **breadth** of her partners, not the volume or effort of her work. Pickup is fast in normal times: Bot_Rules→Mira takes 3.5 minutes and Omar→Mira takes 11 minutes, so she is not usually a standing queue.

**What it does not imply:**
- It does not show authority, decision quality or workload. A handover edge only means her event was next to someone else's.
- It does not show that the work *must* pass through her. About a third of Omar's cases (316 − 210) and of Priya's cases (251 − 180) reach them without a direct Mira→specialist edge.
- Betweenness is sensitive to how it is computed. If NetworkX treats edge weights as distances, frequent edges look "long", which distorts the result. On a small graph of 16 nodes, normalized betweenness moves a lot with small structural changes.
- Her high centrality is partly **designed by Bot_Rules**. The rules engine sends about 22% of cases to one human node, so centralization may be a property of the routing rules rather than of her behaviour.

## Working-together network

**What it does:** It counts in how many cases two resources both appear. The measure is binary per case and ignores order and duration.

**What it implies:** Her low co-case rates (4–8%) mostly follow from simple arithmetic and from the structure of the process, not from being unimportant:
1. **Coverage ceiling.** She touches only 22% of cases, so no pair that includes her can exceed 22% of all cases. Team leads and senior adjusters appear in 15–21% of cases and work with a small, stable set of partners.
2. **Dispersion.** Her cases are spread across North and South, both specialists, both temporary workers and the bots. Many weak ties add up to a low weighted degree (0.16). Alice has a few strong, repeated ties, which give 0.46.
3. **Parallel channel.** Bot_Rules sends each case to Mira *or* to a team lead (920 against 610+590). Exception cases therefore often bypass the team leads, which explains the low Mira–Alice (5%) and Mira–Bruno (4%) rates. Those cases may also end without reaching settlement approval, for example through denial, withdrawal, or still being open at the end of the 12-week window.
4. **Conditional view.** If the denominator is all cases, Mira–Carla at 8% is about 330 cases, which fits the 350 handovers. Measured against Mira's own cases, Carla then appears in roughly 36% of them (8/22), Diego in about 32% and Elise and Farid in about 27% each. From Mira's point of view these are strong ties. "Peripheral" is partly a result of the denominator.

**What it does not imply:** It does not show that she is disconnected or that her contribution is marginal. The claim that her co-case rate is low *because her events are short* is only partly right. PM4Py's working-together metric counts a short event exactly like a long one, so short events lower **duration-weighted** or effort-weighted measures of collaboration, not the co-case rate. The main mechanisms are the coverage ceiling, dispersion across many partners and the parallel exception path.

**Reconciling the contradiction:** High handover and subcontracting centrality together with low working-together strength is the classic signature of a **broker** that bridges "structural holes" (Burt). She has many brief, non-repeated ties that connect clusters which otherwise barely meet: North, South, the specialists, the temporary workers and the automated triage. Team leads are the opposite: dense, repeated ties inside one cluster. The two networks measure different things, sequence adjacency and co-presence, so the pattern is not a contradiction. Some of the gap may also be a data artifact: inflated handover counts, unclear co-case denominators, and a changing exception flag.

## Subcontracting network (n=2)

**What it does:** It detects A→B→A patterns, where A hands work to B and gets it back within two events.

**What it implies:** Mira appears in the top three return patterns (Omar 126, Priya 98, Bot_Rules 62) and has by far the highest subcontracting betweenness (0.69). About 60% of her handovers to Omar and about 54% of those to Priya come back to her. That looks like a **hub-and-spoke case-manager** pattern: specialists report back to her, not to the adjuster, and she decides the next step (Reopen or Assign Specialist). The Bot_Rules→Mira→Bot_Rules loop ("Fraud Screen → manual exception → Fraud Screen update") shows a human step inside an automated control.

**What it does not imply:** It does not tell us *what* she does between the specialist's answer and the next assignment: integration, quality check, simple forwarding, or overriding a result. It also cannot tell us whether the return hop adds value or only latency. The Omar→Mira hop takes 11 minutes, plus whatever wait follows before the next step. The fraud-screen loop is not evidence of an inappropriate override. It could be a legitimate manual review requested by the rules engine. The loop is simply where the control question sits. The patterns also depend on the window: n=2 hides longer loops, and the Temp_1→Vendor_Verify→Temp_1 pattern (68) shows that loops exist elsewhere too.

## Performance/time statistics

**What they do:** They give her median service time (4.2 minutes per event), her cycle-time contribution ("high variance"), the handover delays, and the results of the absence episode.

**What they imply:**
- **Her recorded effort is small.** 960 events × 4.2 minutes ≈ 67 hours in 12 weeks, about 5–6 hours a week. Either she has substantial unlogged work (phone calls, emails, consulting specialists, reading files), or service time captures only system interaction. Her role is probably **latency-critical and knowledge-critical, not capacity-critical**.
- **Her absence mainly hit exception cases.** During her 6-day absence the median for ordinary low-value claims did not move, but the P90 for exception cases rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. The +5.5-day increase is close to the length of the absence. That fits cases being **parked until she returned** rather than being rerouted to someone else.

**What they do not imply:**
- The absence is a single uncontrolled episode (n=1). Seasonality, other absences, vendor delays and backlog at the start could all confound it.
- P90 is sensitive to a few extreme cases.
- The result depends on whether cycle time was measured for cases *started* or cases *completed* in the window, and cases still open are right-censored.
- If Mira herself sets or clears the flag, stratifying by `exception_flag` is endogenous.
- "Median service time" requires matched `start`/`complete` events. If lifecycle data is incomplete, the figure may actually be the time since the previous event.
- The mean elapsed times on handovers to specialists (42 and 51 minutes) almost equal the specialists' median service times (37 and 51 minutes). That suggests they are measured complete-to-complete, which mixes waiting time with service time.
- Vendors add large amounts of waiting time (2.1 hours for verification and 1.3 hours for payment per event, possibly with batch timestamps). Part of the "high variance" attributed to Mira may come from vendors.

## Role discovery / activity-profile similarity

**What it does:** `discover_organizational_roles` groups resources by the activities they perform. `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` correlates their activity-frequency vectors.

**What it implies:** Her profile is unique: Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist. She is probably a singleton role, or only loosely similar to Temp_1, Elise and Farid (through Request Missing Information) and to Alice (through Reopen Claim). She is the only resource with Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist, which suggests the organization has **no designed substitute** for her.

**What it does not imply:**
- Activity similarity cannot see authority, discretion or knowledge. A similarity score that puts her near the temporary workers and junior adjusters would appear to support the "queue cleaner" view, but that would be an artifact of labels that describe the *type* of event and not the decision behind it.
- Roles found from activity sets reflect how activities are logged. "Assign Specialist" is outside the catalogue and may be a free-text or custom label.
- Alice also performs Reopen Claim and Bruno does not, a practice difference between teams that role discovery would mix into its clusters.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The most likely picture is a mix, with weights that the proposed analyses can estimate.

## H1. Formal coordinator / exception desk (the designed role, working as intended)

- **Mechanism:** Bot_Rules sends every case that does not fit the standard path to a single desk. The desk requests information, involves specialists, handles complaints and reopens claims, then passes the case back to the adjusters.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - The resource description says "exception desk".
  - Bot_Rules→Mira edges are labelled "exception desk routing".
  - She handles 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints.
  - Pickup is fast (3.5 minutes) and service steps are short.
  - Ordinary claims were unaffected by her absence.
  - She neither approves settlements nor releases payments, which is consistent with a designed segregation of duties.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Only 48% of her cases are flagged as exceptions, so the desk also handles many unflagged cases.
  - About a third of specialist work bypasses her.
  - A formal desk would normally have documented backup, but here queues built up during her absence.
  - The process owner does not see her as a formal role holder.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - Her inflow should follow Bot_Rules' routing rules closely.
  - Her routing choices should be predictable from case attributes (`case_type`, `amount_band`, flags).
  - A trained deputy should reproduce her routing with similar downstream outcomes.
  - Her share of exception cases should be stable over time.

## H2. Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

- **Mechanism:** Tacit knowledge about "where hard cases should go" is held by one person. The work passes through her because others cannot or will not route it. The limit is her **availability and knowledge**, not her minutes of labour.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - The P90 for exception cases rose by about 5.5 days during her 6-day absence, roughly the length of the absence, which fits parking.
  - She has the highest betweenness in both the handover and the subcontracting network.
  - Specialist answers return to her (the A→B→A loops).
  - She is the only resource logging Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist.
  - Adjusters say she "knows where hard cases should go".
  - Her cycle-time contribution is "high variance".
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Her normal pickup times are short, so there is no standing queue and no sign of a capacity bottleneck.
  - Her recorded workload is low.
  - Alice also reopens claims, and about a third of specialist cases bypass her, so partial substitutes exist.
  - The absence evidence is a single episode that is open to confounding.
  - Median cycle time did not change.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - Future absences (or partial absences such as training days or half days) will show the P90 for exception cases rising roughly in step with how long she is away.
  - The age of the queue in front of her events will rise sharply when she is away.
  - Cases will wait rather than be rerouted.
  - If a deputy is introduced, the effect of absences should shrink.

## H3. Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker

- **Mechanism:** She links clusters that do not otherwise connect: North and South teams, medical and legal specialists, temporary staff, automated rules and vendors (through verification failures that lead to missing-information requests). She translates between them and matches problems to expertise, a knowledge-brokering role.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - Many weak ties across clusters, with low working-together strength and high betweenness (the structural-holes signature).
  - She receives escalations from both temporary workers (Temp_1 missing-info 190, Temp_2 closure exceptions 170), while the temporary workers rarely work cases together (3%).
  - She sends work to both teams' adjusters in balanced volumes.
  - She is the link between Omar and Priya: the two specialists co-occur in only 4% of cases.
  - Adjusters describe her in knowledge terms.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Brokerage metrics were not computed directly. The inference rests on centrality contrasts, which the data-quality issues above may distort.
  - The North/South split may be designed by Bot_Rules, not bridged by her.
  - We cannot tell whether her ties carry information or only forward work.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - Low Burt constraint and high Gould–Fernandez "liaison" or "coordinator" brokerage scores.
  - Cases that cross teams or specialties are far more likely to involve her.
  - Removing her node from the graph splits it into clusters that are much less connected.
  - After her involvement, fewer specialist ping-pong loops occur and cases reach the right specialist on the first attempt more often than in similar cases without her.

## H4. Quality gatekeeper / specialist scheduler (demand shaping)

- **Mechanism:** She filters and sequences requests to scarce specialists. Omar and Priya have long service times, and 62–71% of their cases are exceptions. She protects specialist capacity and integrates the specialists' answers before the case goes back to an adjuster.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - Specialist answers return to her (126 and 98 A→B→A loops).
  - The operations manager expects specialists to be overloaded without her.
  - A fragment like "Medical Review → Assign Specialist/Reopen" suggests she decides next steps after reviews.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Specialists already receive about a third of their cases through other paths.
  - There is no direct evidence that she *declines* specialist requests, which would be the main test of gatekeeping.
  - The operations manager's claim is a prediction, not an observation.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - A meaningful share of cases with a Mira event have a specialist being considered but not called.
  - Specialist cases routed through her are reopened less often or send fewer repeat requests than specialist cases that bypassed her, after adjusting for complexity.
  - Specialist arrival rates become less erratic when requests pass through her.

## H5. Automation monitor / human-in-the-loop for Bot_Rules

- **Mechanism:** She handles cases that the rules engine cannot decide, catches misrouting, and resolves fraud-screen exceptions. She compensates for gaps in the rules.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - 920 Bot_Rules→Mira routings.
  - 62 Fraud Screen→Mira→Fraud Screen update loops.
  - Bot_Rules' exception share (0.31) is higher than Bot_Ingest's (0.19), so exceptions are created during automated triage and screening, and they land on her.
  - Bot_Rules has 4,750 events for 4,090 cases, so repeated screening happens.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Most of her work is about missing information, specialists, complaints and reopening, not about automation.
  - 62 loops is a small share of her 960 events.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - Her inflow will change when Bot_Rules' rules change.
  - Fraud-screen results after her step differ from those before it in some cases. This is measurable only if the screen result is logged.
  - Better rules (for example routing specialists by claim attributes) should reduce her inflow without harming outcomes.

## H6. Administrative "queue cleaner" (the process owner's view)

- **Mechanism:** She does low-discretion clerical routing and follow-up. Her centrality is a mechanical result of sitting where unfinished work collects.
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - Short service times (4.2 minutes).
  - An activity profile that overlaps with temporary staff and junior adjusters (Request Missing Information).
  - Low working-together strength.
  - Ordinary claims do not depend on her.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Her absence affected exception cases by more than a pure clerical relay would be expected to.
  - Specialists report back to her.
  - She holds the only Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist activities.
  - The adjusters' testimony.
  - Short events do not mean low-value decisions.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - A temporary worker or a rule could take over her routing with no change in how often cases are routed correctly, reopened, or passed from specialist to specialist.
  - Her routing choices would be almost entirely predictable from simple attributes.

## H7. Data or logging artifact (partial explanation)

- **Mechanism:** Part of her centrality comes from how events are recorded. Possible causes:
  - A shared "exception desk" login used by several people.
  - System-generated routing or status events that are attributed to her.
  - Duplicated start/complete events.
  - Indirect-succession handover counting.
  - A custom activity label ("Assign Specialist").
- **Supporting evidence:**
  - Handover totals exceed her event count.
  - Co-case rates are inconsistent.
  - The activity is not in the catalogue.
  - Her events are very short.
  - The exception flag behaves inconsistently.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:**
  - Queues built up during her personal absence. With a truly shared login, events would have continued. Stakeholders describe her as one person.
- **Observable predictions:**
  - If a shared account is involved: Mira events during her absence, overlapping start–complete intervals across cases, activity outside her working hours, and bursts that exceed what one person can do.
  - If system events are involved: identical timestamps across many cases, or events that always occur within seconds of a Bot_Rules event.
  - Recomputing handovers with `beta=0` on events filtered to `complete` should reduce her handover centrality substantially if the counting method caused it.

# 3. Risk assessment

All risks below are **potential** risks that the evidence makes plausible. None of them is established misconduct.

- **Bottleneck risk: moderate, conditional.** In normal operation she does not look like a capacity bottleneck: pickup takes minutes and recorded effort is low. The risk depends on her availability. Her work cannot be parked safely, and the one observed absence added about 5.5 days to the P90 for exception cases. Peaks in exception inflow (for example after a Bot_Rules rule change, a vendor outage or a spike in complaints) could create a queue even while she is present. The "high variance" cycle-time contribution points that way, but part of that variance may come from vendors or specialists.

- **Coordination risk: moderate.** The specialist hub-and-spoke pattern adds a hop: specialist → Mira → adjuster. That hop may add value (integration, next-step decision) or only latency. Because exception cases bypass team leads (Mira–Alice 5%, Mira–Bruno 4%), team leads may lack visibility of hard cases in their region until approval. Information may be lost between her short routing events and the adjuster's work, which could explain part of the rework edges ("rework/missing info"). The temporary workers escalate to her rather than to the team leads, so she acts as their informal supervisor without the formal mandate.

- **Key-person risk: high likelihood, moderate-to-high impact on exception cases.** She is the only resource with Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist, handles 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, and there is no evidence of a trained deputy. Her routing knowledge appears to be tacit. Substitutes exist only in part: Alice reopens claims, and specialists receive some cases directly. Losing her permanently would probably cost more than the 6-day absence did, because a long absence cannot be waited out.

- **Compliance/control risk: plausible exposure, no evidence of wrongdoing.** She both *detects* problems (missing information, complaints, reopen decisions) and *chooses* who reviews them, including specialist selection and manual steps between fraud-screen runs. That combination creates discretion without an audit trail if reasons are not recorded. The sensitive points are the Fraud Screen→Mira→Fraud Screen update loop, reopening claims after closure or payment, and routing complaints. Two factors limit the risk:
  - She does not appear to approve settlements or release payments, which remain with the team leads and Vendor_Pay, so key segregation of duties seems intact at the payment stage.
  - Nothing shows that her choices are inconsistent or biased.

  The main control gap is **traceability**, not integrity. For a regulated process, lack of traceability can itself be a finding.

- **Workload fairness risk: two directions.**
  1. *Toward Mira:* Much of her work appears to be invisible to the log. Recorded effort is about 5–6 hours a week, yet her role is critical and she is labelled a "queue cleaner". She may be under-recognized, and her real load may be under-estimated, which also matters when planning backup.
  2. *Toward others:* Her routing is discretionary. She sends similar volumes to senior and junior adjusters (350/330 and 290/280). The edges to juniors are labelled "rework", which could mean hard or messy cases are pushed down the hierarchy. The temporary workers and specialists (71% exception share for Priya) receive work that is heavily weighted toward hard cases.

  Customer-side procedural fairness also matters: whether her routing is consistent across `region`, `channel` and `amount_band`. Consistency is untested, so no unfairness claim is justified yet.

- **Data-quality risk: high, and it affects every conclusion.**
  - Handover totals do not match event counts.
  - Working-together denominators are inconsistent.
  - `exception_flag` is time-varying or event-level and possibly set by the actors being studied.
  - "Assign Specialist" is not in the catalogue.
  - Close Claim coverage is sparse.
  - Vendor_Pay has zero out-degree (timestamp or ordering questions).
  - Vendor timestamps may be batch-recorded.
  - Service times may lack lifecycle pairs.
  - Her short events may be system-generated, and a shared desk account is possible.

  Decisions based on centrality alone could be driven by artifacts.

**A6. Service/waiting-time analysis**

- Separate vendor waiting time (Vendor_Verify, Vendor_Pay) from her contribution to case variance, for example by regressing case duration on time spent at each resource.

**A7. Routing-consistency (decision-mining) study**
- Model her routing choice (which specialist or adjuster, reopen or not) as a function of case attributes. Use a decision tree or gradient boosting in scikit-learn, or PM4Py's decision mining on a discovered Petri net.
  - If her choices are highly predictable, a rule or deputy can partly codify them (supports H1 and H6).
  - If they are poorly predictable but outcomes are good, she is using tacit knowledge (supports H2 and H3).
  - If they are poorly predictable and outcomes are mixed, a process review is needed (the compliance angle).
- Check whether routing and outcomes differ by `region` and `channel` (a procedural-fairness check).

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately (low-regret, reversible, does not reduce her role):**
1. **Name and train a deputy** for the exception desk (for example a senior adjuster from each team on rotation, or one designated team lead), and set a written absence-coverage rule so exception cases are rerouted rather than parked.
2. **Document her routing heuristics** in a short routing guide covering when to involve Medical or Legal, and how to handle complaints and reopened claims, based on interviews with her and the adjusters. This is knowledge capture, not a performance review.
3. **Require reason codes** for Assign Specialist, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint and the manual fraud-screen exception step. For fraud-screen changes and reopening after payment, add a lightweight second-person review, sampled or threshold-based. This closes the traceability gap without implying misconduct.
4. **Fix logging.** Add "Assign Specialist" to the activity catalogue, make `exception_flag` semantics explicit (with the time it was first set and who set it), make sure `start`/`complete` events are recorded for her activities, and check the Close Claim and Vendor_Pay timestamps.
5. **Give team leads visibility** of exception cases in their region, for example through a shared exception-queue dashboard.

**Monitor (weekly or monthly):**
- Exception queue size and age in front of Mira and the deputy, and the P90 exception cycle time, especially during any absence.
- Her inflow from Bot_Rules and from the temporary workers, and how rule changes affect it.
- Routing consistency and distribution: the share sent to each adjuster (senior vs junior), each specialist, and by region and channel.
- Specialist utilization, repeat specialist requests, and reopen rate after specialist review.
- Rate of changed fraud-screen results after manual intervention, and payments after reopening.
- Her real workload, including time outside the system (short time-tracking pilot), to size the deputy role correctly.
- Delays contributed by vendors, so they are not misattributed to her.

**Do not change until more evidence is available:**
- **Do not remove, downgrade or bypass her.** Do not route specialist answers directly to adjusters, and do not reassign her routing to temporary staff or to Bot_Rules. First establish whether her hop adds integration value (analyses A3, A6 and A7). If a redesign is later justified, replace her function explicitly with: (a) a codified routing matrix in Bot_Rules for cases with clear rules, (b) a pooled exception desk of at least two trained people for cases needing judgement, (c) a direct specialist-to-adjuster return path with a structured handover note, and (d) complaint and reopen authority assigned to team leads, with SLAs.
- Do not reorganize the North/South teams or change the Bot_Rules routing thresholds based on centrality figures, which may be artifacts.
- Do not treat her centrality as either a performance merit or a compliance finding.
- Do not add coordinator headcount until the queue analysis shows whether the problem is capacity or single-person availability. A deputy may be enough.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira is the connecting point for claims that do not fit the standard path: unclear coverage, missing documents, medical or legal questions, complaints and reopened claims. The data show that she passes work to, and gets it back from, many different people and systems, but spends only a few minutes on each step. That is why she looks central in some analyses and marginal in others; both views are partly right. Ordinary claims do not depend on her, but difficult claims do: when she was away for six days, the slowest exception cases took about five and a half days longer. The main risk is not misconduct, for which we have no evidence, but dependence on one person's undocumented judgment. We recommend naming and training a backup, recording the reason for each specialist assignment or manual override, fixing some data inconsistencies, and tracking exception queues weekly. Structural changes to her role should wait until further analysis is complete.
