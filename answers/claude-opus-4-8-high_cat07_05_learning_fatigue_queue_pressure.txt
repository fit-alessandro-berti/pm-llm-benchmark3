# 1. Competing mechanism table

The core analytical problem is that *sequence position within a shift*, *minutes since shift start*, *queue length / WIP*, *case complexity*, *time of day*, and *resource experience* are all correlated with one another. A raw curve of "service time vs. position" or "error rate vs. position" is a superposition of several mechanisms, and each mechanism partially confounds the others. The table below separates them.

| Mechanism | Expected signature | Evidence supporting it | Evidence against / limiting it | How to test it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Learning / warm-up** | Within a single shift, service time falls and error rate falls or stays flat over the first tens of events, *after* complexity is held constant. A durable, long-horizon version: experienced resources (`resource_experience_months`) are faster and more accurate at equal complexity/WIP. | Positions 1–5 → 16–30 show service time dropping 18.8 → 13.6 min and error 3.4% → 2.5%; notes call it "warm-up." Seniors faster than juniors first-20 (15.9 vs 19.6 min) and much lower error (2.1% vs 5.1%). | The early speed-up coincides with **falling complexity** (0.72 → 0.54) and **falling high-priority share** (41% → 28%). So part or all of the "learning" curve is case-mix, not skill. True learning should persist after adjusting for complexity, WIP, and priority. | Fit service time / error as a function of position **with** complexity, priority, and WIP as covariates, using resource fixed effects (within-resource comparison). If the position slope survives adjustment early in the shift, it is learning; if it collapses, it was case-mix. Compare the *shape* across experience groups (juniors should show steeper within-shift warm-up than seniors). |

| **Queue pressure (WIP)** | At high `wip_at_start` / `queue_length_at_start`, service time **falls** (rushing, triage shortcuts) while error and rework **rise**, at equal complexity. This is a speed–quality trade-off, not skill. | The WIP×complexity table is the cleanest signal: at low complexity, error goes 2.1% → 3.0% → 5.8% as WIP rises Low→Med→High, while service time falls 12.7 → 11.6 → 9.4 min. If, holding complexity and resource fixed, higher WIP → faster + more errors, queue pressure is confirmed as a primary driver. |

Attributing bot-caused rework to the *human corrector* would be a measurement error. | Trace cases through the DFG (`discover_performance_dfg`): compare rework/error rates on paths that pass through `Bot_Risk` vs. human Risk Classification, stratified by WIP. Attribute rework to the originating classification, not the corrector. |

# 2. Integrated explanation

No single mechanism explains the data; the observed curves are the **sum of several processes that happen to move together over a shift**, and the key non-obvious fact is that *service time and error rate are driven by partly different mechanisms*.

Early in a shift, service time and error both fall (positions 1–30). This looks like learning/warm-up, and there is probably some genuine warm-up (people are slower on their first few cases, and juniors show a steeper early curve than seniors). But the early improvement is heavily **confounded by case-mix and backlog selection**: the first cases inherit the previous shift's backlog, are the most complex (0.72), the most high-priority (41%), and seniors deliberately pull the hard cases early. So the "improvement" is largely the workforce moving from hard carryover cases to progressively easier, lower-priority routine work — not primarily skill acquisition. This is why the constraint "do not ignore that early-shift cases are more complex" matters: much of the apparent learning is complexity draining away.

Later in the shift, the two metrics **decouple**, which is the crux. Service time keeps falling (down to 10.8 min at 76+) even though error rate and rework climb sharply (7.9% and 13.7%). Falling service time with rising errors is *not* the signature of fatigue-as-slowing; it is the signature of a **speed–quality trade-off under queue pressure**. This is confirmed by the WIP×complexity table, where the cleanest, best-controlled comparison shows that at fixed (low) complexity, rising WIP simultaneously *lowers* service time and *raises* error. The late-shift cases are actually the *easiest* on average (complexity 0.43), so the error spike cannot be blamed on hard cases and cannot be blamed on slow, careful fatigue — it is people **rushing to clear a growing queue** (queue length 88 at position 76+). The constraint "late-shift speed may reflect queue pressure rather than learning" is therefore essential: the same falling-service-time curve that looks like continued learning is actually the visible edge of corner-cutting under load.

Fatigue and circadian effects are real but **secondary and hard to isolate** here because they are collinear with WIP and time-of-day. The resource-experience table is the strongest evidence for this ordering: "error rate later under low WIP" is essentially flat for every group (seniors 2.1→2.2, mid 2.8→2.9, juniors 5.1→4.4), while "error rate later under high WIP" explodes (up to 12.4% for juniors). If pure time-on-task fatigue dominated, late errors would rise even under low WIP — they don't. So the dominant late-shift driver is **load, not hours worked**, though a residual fatigue/circadian component likely amplifies the effect (e.g., Day-shift last-two-hours error of 7.5% at the demand peak, Night's intrinsic slowness).

Layered on top are **measurement-distorting workarounds**: Junior batching of Document Review compresses recorded service times (making late speed look even better than it is), and deferring Quality Check to end-of-shift creates artificial correction/rework bursts in the last 45 minutes that inflate late "error/rework" counts and shift them onto whoever is closing out. The **automation interaction** adds a final twist: Bot_Risk's instant classifications are more often wrong-in-context under high WIP, and the resulting corrections show up as human rework precisely in the high-pressure zone.

The honest integrated story: **early gains ≈ mostly case-mix/selection + some warm-up; late losses ≈ mostly queue pressure (speed–quality trade-off) + automation-under-load + batching/QC-timing artifacts, with fatigue/circadian as amplifiers rather than the root cause.** Different resource groups sit differently on this surface — seniors are robust, juniors are pressure-sensitive — which is itself partly a case-mix/selection effect and must not be read as raw individual blame.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The unifying principle: never interpret a marginal curve. Every effect (position, minutes-elapsed, time-of-day) must be estimated **within resource** and **conditional on complexity, priority, and WIP**. Below, "df" is the formatted event log.

**Setup and feature engineering**

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw_df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)

# Service and waiting times per case; arrival/finish rates; temporal features
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df)
temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df)  # adds hour-of-day, weekday, etc.

# Per-event service time (complete - start)
df["svc_time"] = (df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

# Complexity band and WIP band to mirror/deepen the given tables
df["complexity_band"] = pd.cut(df["case_complexity_score"], [-1, 0.55, 2], labels=["low", "high"])
df["wip_band"] = pd.cut(df["wip_at_start"], [-1, 29, 70, 10_000], labels=["low", "med", "high"])
```

**(a) Within-resource comparisons.** Isolate individual effects from case assignment by comparing each resource to themselves.

```python
# Per-resource, per-WIP-band, per-complexity-band service time and error rate
grp = df.groupby(["org:resource", "wip_band", "complexity_band"]).agg(
    svc=("svc_time", "mean"),
    err=("error_flag", "mean"),
    n=("error_flag", "size"),
)
```
Statistically, fit a mixed-effects / fixed-effects model: `error_flag ~ position + minutes_since_shift_start + case_complexity_score + priority + wip_at_start + activity_family + hour_of_day + (resource fixed effect)`, and a parallel model for `svc_time`. Resource fixed effects remove stable between-worker differences (skill, case-mix they habitually get) so the position/WIP slopes are within-person.

**(b) Within-shift position effects, adjusted.** Reproduce the position table but *residualize* on complexity/priority/WIP first. If the early-position slope vanishes after adjustment, "learning" was case-mix; if the late-position error slope vanishes after adding WIP, "fatigue" was queue pressure.

```python
pos_bins = pd.cut(df["sequence_position_within_shift"], [0,5,15,30,50,75,10_000])
raw = df.groupby(pos_bins).agg(svc=("svc_time","mean"), err=("error_flag","mean"))
# adjusted: regress out complexity/priority/wip, then bin the residuals by position
```

**(c) WIP / queue stratification.** Extend the given WIP×complexity table to WIP × complexity × experience-group, so the speed–quality trade-off is shown to be conditional (juniors steepest). Use `discover_performance_dfg` to see whether specific transitions get slower/faster under high WIP.

**(d) Complexity adjustment.** Always condition on `case_complexity_score` and `priority`. Verify assignment endogeneity: `df.groupby("org:resource")["case_complexity_score"].mean()` and correlate resource seniority with assigned complexity — if seniors systematically get harder cases, raw error comparisons are invalid.

**(e) Shift and time-of-day controls.** Use `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` for hour-of-day, then separate "hours on task" from "clock time" using **overlapping shift windows** (Day and Late both cover 14:00–17:00 but at different `minutes_since_shift_start`).

```python
overlap = df[(df["hour_of_day"].between(14,17)) & (df["shift_id"].isin(["Day","Late"]))]
overlap.groupby("shift_id").agg(err=("error_flag","mean"), svc=("svc_time","mean"),
                                mins=("minutes_since_shift_start","mean"),
                                wip=("wip_at_start","mean"))
```
Same clock hour, different time-on-task → any residual difference is fatigue/circadian rather than the hour itself.

**(f) Batching detection.** Flag events inside batches so their service times can be excluded/modeled separately, and confirm the Junior_H/I Document Review runs and end-of-shift QC bursts.

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(df)  # per activity/resource: simultaneous/seq/batch-at-end
# Also detect suspiciously uniform short svc_time runs by (resource, activity, shift_id)
```
Visual confirmation with `pm4py.view_dotted_chart` (x = timestamp, y = case, color = resource) to see clustered bursts, and `pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph` for events-by-hour/position distributions.

**(g) Resource-experience interactions.** Test `error_flag ~ wip_band * experience_group` and `svc_time ~ position * experience_group`. Expected: significant WIP×experience interaction (juniors most pressure-sensitive), steeper early warm-up slope for juniors.

**(h) Rework / error downstream analysis.** Link errors to later corrections, and attribute automation-induced rework correctly.

```python
rework = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(df)
# Cases passing through Bot_Risk vs human Risk Classification, stratified by WIP:
dfg_perf = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)
# Attribute Rework Correction to the ORIGINATING classification event (bot vs human),
# not to the correcting resource; measure lag (rework within 7 days) with waiting-time features.
```
Also use `filter_case_performance` / `filter_paths_performance` to compare throughput vs. rework on fast-closed cases (do rushed high-WIP cases return as rework more often?), and `cluster_log` to see whether "problem" traces cluster by WIP/shift rather than by worker.

# 4. Intervention options

| # | Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk | How to monitor with event-log indicators |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Strictly cap shift length for all workers** | Limits genuine time-on-task fatigue/circadian degradation if present. | Blunt; the data shows the dominant late driver is **WIP, not hours** (errors flat late under low WIP). Capping hours without capping load may just push backlog to the next shift and worsen handover queues. Reduces capacity when demand peaks late. | Track error/rework at equal WIP before vs after; watch end-of-shift queue length and handover backlog; confirm fatigue component was real via minutes-since-start residuals. |
| 2 | **Move complex cases away from the first hour** | Reduces the hard-carryover pileup at shift start; smooths the early complexity spike (0.72). May improve first-hour error. | If genuine warm-up exists, complex cases are still risky whenever they're done; you may just relocate the risk. Deferring complex cases can breach SLAs on high-priority carryover. | Monitor first-hour complexity share (target from 41% high-priority downward), first-hour error, and whether complex-case error simply moves to a later window. |
| 3 | **Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70** | Directly targets the strongest, best-evidenced driver (the high-WIP error/rework spike, 10.9% at high WIP+complexity). Enables load-shedding/reallocation before quality collapses. | Alert fatigue; if there's no slack capacity, an alert without staffing does nothing. Could encourage even faster rushing to clear the alert. | Count minutes spent above WIP 70 per shift; error/rework rate in >70 windows vs after intervention; time-to-recover below threshold; whether alerts trigger reallocation actions. |
| 4 | **Force random assignment to reduce selection bias** | Makes worker comparisons fairer and removes endogenous case-selection (seniors cherry-picking complex cases), improving measurement validity. | Operationally harmful: sending complex/high-priority cases to juniors will *raise* errors and rework; ignores legitimate skill-based routing. Better as an *analysis* device than a standing policy. | Compare pre/post error by complexity band and experience group; watch junior error on newly-assigned complex cases; SLA breaches on high-priority cases. |
| 5 | **Mandatory micro-breaks in final two hours** | Mitigates fatigue/circadian component if it exists; may reduce corner-cutting. | If the late problem is queue pressure, breaks *reduce* capacity and can *raise* WIP and thus errors — potentially counterproductive. Benefit is conditional on fatigue being real. | A/B by shift: late-shift error/rework at matched WIP with vs without breaks; WIP trajectory during breaks; whether breaks raise end-of-shift backlog. |
| 6 | **Defer low-priority quality checks to next shift** | Reduces end-of-shift QC burst artifacts and last-45-min crunch. | **Do not weaken quality assurance to boost throughput** — deferring QC can let errors escape and inflate downstream rework; only *timing* of low-priority QC should move, never removal. Risk of QC backlog accumulating. | Track escaped-defect / rework-within-7-days rate, QC backlog size across shift boundaries, and correction-burst concentration in last 45 min. |
| 7 | **Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Directly addresses the Day-shift late WIP surge (queue 38→75) and its 7.5% late error; adds slack exactly where the speed–quality trade-off bites; seniors are pressure-robust. | Cost; must be timed to the actual peak; risk of underuse if peak shifts. | Day-shift last-two-hours WIP and error/rework vs baseline; senior utilization; whether the high-WIP window shrinks. Likely the highest-value, lowest-collateral option. |
| 8 | **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | (Ostensibly) deters carelessness. | **Strongly discouraged.** Late-shift raw error is dominated by WIP, case-mix, automation-induced rework, and QC-timing artifacts — not individual negligence. Penalizing raw rates punishes people for management-created queue pressure and for handling harder assignments; incentivizes gaming (hiding errors, avoiding hard cases). Violates the "no punishment on raw late-shift rates" constraint. | If any accountability is used at all, it must be on **adjusted** residuals (within-resource, WIP/complexity-controlled) and treated as coaching signal, not punishment; monitor for gaming (drops in reported errors without drops in downstream rework). |

**Priority read:** interventions 3 and 7 target the dominant (queue-pressure) mechanism with the least collateral damage; 2 is a reasonable case-mix smoother; 1 and 5 are conditional on a fatigue component that the data suggests is secondary; 4 is best used as an analysis technique; 6 is acceptable only as re-timing, never removal; 8 should not be implemented as stated.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias makes raw worker comparisons unfair.** Seniors take complex cases early and juniors get pressure-loaded, easier-but-high-volume work later. Comparing raw error rates across workers therefore compares *different case populations*, not different skill. Any worker-level conclusion must condition on complexity, priority, WIP, and time-of-day, and account for who was *assigned* what.
- **Measurement error and artifacts.** Service times are contaminated by batching (Junior_H/I Document Review runs produce artificially short, uniform times) and by QC being deferred into end-of-shift bursts. "Errors" and "rework" recorded late may reflect *when corrections were logged*, not when or by whom the mistake was made. Attributing these to the closing/correcting worker is a measurement error, not a performance fact.
- **Management-created queue pressure.** The late-shift quality collapse is largely a consequence of rising WIP — a *staffing and demand-management* choice. Holding individual workers responsible for errors caused by understaffing at the demand peak is misattributing an organizational cause to individuals.
- **Automation quality.** Bot_Risk misclassifies more under high WIP, and humans then correct it. Charging that rework to the human corrector (or reading it as human error) both hides the automation defect and unfairly penalizes people cleaning up after the bot. Rework must be attributed to its originating step.
- **Circadian/night unfairness and small samples.** Night staff face intrinsic circadian load and the most complex first hour, on few events (noisy rates). Judging them by the same raw thresholds as day staff is unfair and statistically fragile.
- **Punitive use of error rates backfires.** Penalizing raw late-shift error rates (intervention 8) punishes people for load, case-mix, automation, and logging artifacts they don't control. It incentivizes gaming — under-reporting errors, avoiding hard cases, rushing QC — which *degrades* true quality while improving the measured number. Error metrics should be used for system diagnosis and coaching, on adjusted residuals, never as a raw punitive ranking.
- **Do not degrade quality assurance for throughput.** Faster is not better if it raises downstream rework; QC must be protected, only re-timed, never removed.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

A process-mining dashboard, refreshed near-real-time, with every quality metric shown **adjusted / stratified** (by complexity, WIP, shift, experience) alongside raw values so the raw numbers are never read in isolation.

1. **Adjusted service time.** Mean/median per-event service time (`complete − start`), stratified by WIP band × complexity band × activity_family, with batched events flagged and reported separately (from `discover_batches`). Trend and control limits.
2. **Waiting time / cycle time.** Per-case waiting and service time from `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; time-in-queue before each activity; SLA-breach count for high-priority cases.
3. **WIP / queue pressure.** Live `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start`, minutes-per-shift spent above threshold 70, and a real-time alert flag (ties to intervention 3). Queue-at-shift-start vs queue-at-shift-end.
4. **Rework rate.** Rework-within-7-days and `get_rework_cases_per_activity`, stratified by WIP and by whether the case passed through Bot_Risk vs human Risk Classification (rework attributed to the originating step).
5. **Error rate — raw and adjusted.** Error rate by shift position, minutes-since-start, and WIP band, shown both raw and as within-resource WIP/complexity-adjusted residuals. Raw values labeled "not for individual evaluation."
6. **Complexity / case-mix indicators.** Mean `case_complexity_score`, high-complexity share, and high-priority share by shift position and hour (exposes that early cases are harder; monitors intervention 2).
7. **Batching indicators.** Count and length of detected batches per resource/activity, end-of-shift QC-burst concentration (share of Quality Check / corrections in last 45 min), and a "uniform short-service-time run" flag — so artifacts aren't mistaken for speed.
8. **Handover / queue-ownership changes.** Number of resource handovers per case, cases inherited across shift boundaries (backlog carryover), and queue-ownership transfers — using `discover_performance_dfg` for slow/risky transitions. Highlights management-created pressure at shift boundaries.
9. **Automation-interaction panel.** Bot_Risk classification volume, downstream human-correction rate of bot outputs, and how that correction rate scales with WIP (>70). Surfaces automation quality separately from human error.
10. **Worker-load fairness.** Per-resource distribution of assigned complexity, priority, WIP-at-assignment, event count, and share of night/late work — with a fairness check that no group is systematically loaded with harder cases before any performance metric is interpreted. Time-of-day/circadian panel (hour-of-day error and service time from `extract_temporal_features_dataframe`), and overlapping-window comparisons (Day vs Late, 14:00–17:00) to separate hours-on-task from clock time.
