# 2. Scenario forecasts

**Optimistic**

- **Handover pattern:** Fewer ad hoc cross-team transfers and fewer unnecessary specialist referrals because routing uses consistent criteria. Shared advice returns promptly to a clearly named local case owner.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Pooling smooths the different team demand peaks. The 15% utilization gain comes from reduced idle time and better matching, with enough capacity reserved for urgent and complex work. Legal/Risk capacity is expanded or protected from additional Quality Check work.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Straightforward cases stay stable or improve. East improves most as E_Tech demand is pooled; North and South remain close to baseline. Median and upper percentiles improve or stay within guardrails, including for high-complexity cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standard advice and approvals reduce inconsistent decisions and rework. Local staff still have a route to get early advice and can retain regional context.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** A named local case owner remains accountable for the whole case; specialists advise, and an identified approver owns the decision. Handoffs include reasons and acceptance.
- **Fairness implications:** Comparable cases receive comparable decisions, while routing and service levels still account for customer needs and case complexity. Team differences narrow after case-mix adjustment without one segment bearing longer waits.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue age remains controlled; no rise in repeated referrals, incomplete case notes, reassignment counts, or specialist “bounce backs”; East improves without worsening North/South; customer-segment and complexity gaps remain stable or narrow.

**Central**

- **Handover pattern:** Some nominal transfer reduction occurs in intake and absence cover, but actual specialist and approval transfers rise. The forecast routes more technical and Legal/Risk cases into the shared service, so the number of logged touches may increase.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Average service improves for some pooled work, while shared Technical and Legal/Risk queues become more variable. The management medians already suggest waits rising from 3.2 to 4.0 hours for technical referral, 1.6 to 2.2 hours for advice return, 2.4 to 3.0 hours for approval, and 9.6 to 10.5 hours for Legal/Risk.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Simple cases are stable or somewhat faster; the median may improve slightly, but high-complexity and specialty cases have a slower 90th percentile. East benefits from pooled capacity, while North and South lose some of the speed and context of local advice.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standardization reduces some avoidable variation, but loss of regional knowledge increases rework for a subset of complex or customer-sensitive cases.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Shared queues make work allocation more consistent, but case ownership and approval responsibility are unclear in some cases. Reassignments and waiting for clarification increase.
- **Fairness implications:** Overall results look acceptable, but waits or rework worsen for some locations or customer segments. Aggregate rates conceal these differences.
- **Early warning signs:** Stable average cycle time alongside widening tail latency; increasing queue age, back-and-forth handovers, reassignments, customer-contact attempts, or team/customer-segment gaps.

**Adverse**

- **Handover pattern:** Shared services become a new routing layer. Cases bounce between local teams, technical specialists, Legal/Risk, Quality Check, and approvers; the count of informal consultations falls but formal rework handovers rise.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Arrivals exceed effective capacity, especially when Technical staff also triage Legal/Risk and difficult Quality Check. Utilization reaches nominal targets by eliminating slack, so absences and demand spikes create long queues. Legal/Risk waits worsen from an already high baseline.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Averages may initially look stable because straightforward cases continue flowing, while complex cases and cases for underserved segments develop a long delay tail and more SLA misses.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Lost local context, rushed decisions, and incomplete handoff notes increase rework, repeat contact, overrides, and possibly adverse outcomes.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Local staff assume the shared specialist or approver owns the next step; shared staff assume the local caseworker owns follow-up. Cases sit unowned, and approval accountability is difficult to reconstruct.
- **Fairness implications:** Teams with stronger prior local expertise, customers needing regional or language accommodation, and complex specialty groups receive slower or less suitable service. Allocation rules may distribute cases unevenly or systematically prioritize easier work.
- **Early warning signs:** Growing oldest-case age, 90th/95th percentile waits, queue returns, SLA misses, rework, missing owners, unresolved exceptions, and widening team- or segment-adjusted gaps; deterioration during absences or peaks.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Use per-case and per-eligible-case denominators as well as per-100-case rates. Compare like-for-like periods and adjust or stratify for priority, complexity, specialty, customer segment, location, and arrival volume.

| Timing | Indicator | Log measure and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Leading: days to weeks | Queue pressure and capacity | Queue entry-to-service-start time, oldest open request, arrivals/completions by specialty and week, active workload per specialist, utilization, and wait percentiles. Log queue-entry and service-start events; utilization is not a substitute for queue delay |
| Leading | Routing and handovers | Handover-of-work edges and counts per 100 cases; resource/team changes per case; referrals, returns, cross-team transfers, and handover reasons. Separate necessary referrals from avoidable bounce backs |
| Leading | Ownership and role clarity | Time to named case owner and approver, unassigned time, reassignments, repeated assignment changes, advice awaiting acknowledgment, and approval returns. Add explicit case-owner, decision-owner, and queue-state fields if absent |
| Leading | Handoff quality | Missing or inconsistent notes, incomplete required fields at transfer, request clarification events, and time from specialist response to local action |
| Leading | Distribution and fairness | Wait and routing rates by originating team, location, complexity, specialty, priority, and customer segment. Review both raw and case-mix-adjusted gaps |
| Leading | Resilience | Queue age and completion during planned/unplanned absences, peak arrivals, and staff coverage changes; number of qualified resources able to handle each specialty |
| Leading | Learning and skill mix | Local staff activity mix, repeat requests for the same advice, technical assessment participation, local expertise flag, and changes in local work after central routing |
| Lagging: after outcomes mature | End-to-end performance | Median, 90th, and 95th percentile cycle time and SLA miss rate by cohort and segment; report open-case age to avoid excluding unfinished slow cases |
| Lagging | Quality and outcomes | rework_flag, rework cases per activity, repeat quality checks, correction/override rates, and final outcome, stratified by specialty, complexity, team, and customer segment |
| Lagging | Customer experience | Customer satisfaction and repeat customer contact by segment and location, including cases with accommodation or language needs if those attributes are captured |
| Lagging | Decision consistency | Variation in approval and outcome for comparable cases, controlling for complexity, priority, specialty, and customer segment; consistency alone is not evidence that decisions are correct |
| Lagging | Workforce sustainability | Persistent utilization, absence cover, unresolved backlog, turnover or skill coverage, and local technical capability over several periods |

At minimum, add or validate timestamps for queue request, queue acceptance, service start, specialist completion, advice received, approval request, and approval decision. A single event timestamp cannot reliably separate waiting from service time. Capture the originating team, a stable case owner, decision owner, routing reason, and any accommodation needs. Without these fields, role clarity, fairness, and wait measures will be weak proxies.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** A stable mean or median can hide a sharply increasing 90th/95th percentile, the oldest open cases, and cases repeatedly returned to queues. Include open cases in age reporting and use survival or cohort views so slow unfinished cases are not dropped.
2. **High-complexity cases:** More straightforward work can improve the average while high-complexity cases wait longer for central specialists or accumulate extra review steps. Compare within complexity and specialty, and check interaction effects such as complex Legal/Risk cases.
3. **Local knowledge loss:** Fewer visible local-specialist loops may remove early consultations that prevent an unnecessary referral or catch a regional convention. Later rework can rise even if the first-pass average is unchanged. Track local_expertise_flag, reasons for referrals, repeat technical queries, outcomes, and rework by location.
4. **Fairness:** Aggregate results can hide slower service or worse outcomes for a customer segment, location, or team with a different case mix. Compare both raw and adjusted gaps and capture relevant accommodation/language needs; customer segment alone may not explain these needs.
5. **Learning and skill decay:** Routing hard cases away can reduce local staff exposure and their ability to handle future exceptions or provide informed early advice. Watch local activity mix and skill coverage over time, and collect staff feedback about confidence and access to coaching.
6. **Accountability:** An advice queue and a shared approval queue can obscure who must act next and who owns the decision. Cases may wait without an obvious failed handover. Log owner changes, acceptance, decision authority, and time in unassigned states.
7. **Exception handling and resilience:** Standard routing may fail for urgent, unusual, or incorrectly classified cases. A pooled queue can be more resilient to one person’s absence but less resilient to a central outage, demand spike, or loss of a rare specialty. Measure exception paths, coverage depth, backlog during absences, and recovery time.
8. **Misleading utilization:** Raising utilization by 15% can mean better matching, or it can mean less spare capacity. Near-saturated Legal/Risk specialists may have longer waits as broader demand arrives. Interpret utilization alongside arrivals, queue age, wait distributions, quality, and absence coverage.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Pilot scope:** Use a bounded, reversible pilot for Technical Shared Service first, with explicit capacity and queue ownership. Include eligible high-complexity work from East and a sample from North and South so the pilot tests both East’s capacity relief and the potential loss of North/South local expertise. Keep Legal/Risk expansion and shared approval as separately staged changes where possible; changing all queues at once would make causes of delay or quality changes difficult to identify. If operationally feasible, pilot shared approval on a separate matched cohort after the technical routing has stabilized.
- **Comparison group:** For eligible cases, use a randomized or alternating assignment to shared versus existing local technical review within team × complexity × specialty × priority strata, where safe and operationally acceptable. If individual assignment would contaminate capacity or practice, use a stepped rollout across matched team/specialty cohorts and compare against not-yet-transitioned cohorts. Record exposure and actual routing; analyze by assigned route as well as route received.
- **Observation window:** Establish an 8–12 week pre-pilot baseline, run the pilot for at least 8–12 weeks, then allow 4–8 weeks for outcome and rework follow-up. Extend the window if volumes are low or seasonality, staffing absences, or open cases make cohorts incomparable. Compare the same calendar periods where possible.
- **Segmentation:** Report results by originating team (North, South, East), location, case complexity, required specialty, priority, customer segment, and relevant combinations such as complexity × specialty and team × complexity. Standardize overall comparisons to the baseline case mix. Include an “unknown/missing” category rather than silently dropping incomplete attributes.
- **Guardrails:** Predefine tolerances against baseline/control for 90th/95th percentile cycle time, oldest open case, specialty queue wait, SLA misses, rework, final outcome, customer satisfaction, reassignments, and team/customer-segment gaps. Track both overall performance and the high-risk strata. Set queue-capacity and utilization limits that preserve coverage slack, particularly for Legal/Risk.
- **Staff feedback:** Collect brief weekly feedback from local caseworkers, shared specialists, supervisors, and intake staff on case ownership, referral quality, access to informal advice, workload, note quality, exception handling, and regional/customer context. Review examples of delayed or returned cases with staff, not only survey scores.
- **Rollback or adjustment triggers:** Pause new pilot intake or revert the affected specialty/cohort if queue age or upper-percentile waits breach pre-agreed limits for two consecutive review periods; if SLA misses, rework, adverse outcomes, or adjusted fairness gaps materially worsen against the comparison group; if cases remain unowned or exceptions cannot be safely routed; or if absence coverage fails. First adjust staffing, routing thresholds, local liaison access, or decision ownership where the issue is narrow, then resume only after the guardrail recovers. Keep a documented route for urgent cases throughout.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, in stages.** The structure could help East and improve consistency and cover, but the estimates predict more technical, approval, and Legal/Risk referrals and longer median waits after several handovers. Legal/Risk are already near capacity, and the case mix routed to specialists will change. A 15% utilization target by itself does not establish improved flow or quality.

Retain a named local case owner and explicit decision owner, preserve a fast path for early local technical advice, and define reserved capacity and escalation rules for complex, urgent, and accommodation-sensitive cases. Start with a bounded technical pilot that includes all three teams; assess Legal/Risk and approval centralization separately using the same guardrails. Expand only if case-mix-adjusted quality, tail latency, SLA performance, and fairness remain acceptable while East benefits and North/South do not incur material harm.

Management should not infer success from early averages, lower cross-team handover counts, higher utilization, or a short run of faster simple cases. Those can coexist with worse complex-case tails, more hidden queue waiting, rework, skill loss, and unclear accountability. Decide from mature, segmented outcomes and queue measures against a comparison group.

For genuine post-handover waiting time, the event log needs a timestamp when a request enters a queue and another when work starts (or an explicit queue activity). time:timestamp alone often supports only an inter-event time proxy.
