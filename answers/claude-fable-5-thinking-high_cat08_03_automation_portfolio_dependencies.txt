# 2. Dependency and prerequisite map

**Upstream data quality dependencies.**
- A8 (committee packs) is the most dependent module: Committee packaging explicitly waits for KYC status, normalized financial statements, collateral valuation when required, and credit-analysis notes. It therefore has hard prerequisites in A6, A3/A4, and A7. Automating A8 first would assemble inconsistent artifacts faster.
- A9 (contract generation) depends on clean KYC data and structured Committee-decision data ("depends on clean KYC and decision data"). If A3/A4 have not stabilized KYC outputs, A9's rule checks will fire constantly and legal ops will drown in exceptions — but note the asymmetry: A9 failing "closed" (blocking) is a control improvement, whereas A8 or A11 failing "open" is a control breach. That is why A9 can precede full KYC automation while A11 cannot.
- A6 depends on A5: extraction quality collapses on incomplete or low-quality documents, and A5 is what raises inbound document completeness (the 52%-repeated request loop is the source of fragmentary submissions).
- A2 sits upstream of everything: its 8% misrouting corrupts the control path (KYC depth, committee cadence) for all downstream modules, so any wave that accelerates flow should include or quickly follow with classification hardening.

**Legal/compliance controls.**
- KYC clearance must precede Customer signing. This binds A10 and A11: neither may go live without a hard KYC gate. A9 is the natural place to encode that gate (contracts released to signing only on cleared KYC), which is why A9 doubles as the compliance-strengthening module.
- Contract terms must match the Committee decision (8.5% remediation, 14% for Credit-line renewal). A9's rule checks address this directly; A12 structures whatever residue remains.
- Four-eyes on Credit analysis vs Committee packaging in High-exposure cases (5.8% month-end violations). Any automation touching packaging (A8) or analysis inflow (A6) must carry an identity-segregation check, and A6 matters indirectly: month-end rush is when violations occur, and smoothing analyst load reduces the rush.
- All model-assisted modules (A2, A3, A4, A6) must log suggestion, human override, and final decision per the audit-trail requirement.

**Human escalation capacity.**
- Compliance specialists are 3 FTE with a handover network concentrated on two seniors. A3 generates escalations into exactly this pool; A3 without A4 (which makes each mismatch resolution faster) risks swapping a queue of routine hits for a queue of escalations at the same choke point. A4 is therefore the enabling module and A3 the amplifier, in that order or together.
- Credit analysts (9 FTE) are the stated overload risk: "automation that increases clean inflow to analysts may overload them unless financial statement normalization is improved." This is the direct dependency of A1/A5 on A6 — any module that accelerates or cleans inflow must be paired with A6.
- Legal ops (4 FTE) is underutilized when KYC is slow and overloaded at month end; A9 smooths this by removing drafting labor, but the exception-handling workload it creates (rule-check failures) must be staffed at month end.

**External valuation dependency.**
- Wait for external valuation (58h, 19% late) is outside direct control and synchronizes Committee packaging. A7 can only triage and parallelize — e.g., fire the valuation request immediately after Credit analysis flags collateral (DFG: Credit analysis → Collateral valuation request 2,400) rather than sequencing it, and route by valuer SLA. Every downstream module's cycle-time benefit in Equipment finance (71% require valuation) is capped by this constraint; portfolios should not promise Equipment-finance cycle-time wins that A7 alone can deliver.

**Core-banking integration constraints.**
- Only two deep integrations are possible in the first six months. The deep-integration candidates are A1 (registration into core), A9 (contract/decision data), A11 (activation), and partially A3 (KYC systems). A5 (portal-side), A6 (document layer), A4 (case-management workbench), A10 (e-sign vendor), and A7 (partner interface) are shallow or mid-layer. A rational first-year plan spends the two deep slots on A9 and one of {A3, A1}, and explicitly not on A11.

# 3. Portfolio alternatives

**Portfolio Q — Quick-win (frequency-driven): A1, A2, A10, A9.**
Optimizes: visible labor savings and political capital. A1 automates the highest-frequency task (11,880 registrations), A2 removes 12-minute classification touches at scale, A10 attacks the 44h signing wait, A9 is included because compliance requires one control module and it is the only high-frequency candidate that qualifies.
Sacrifices: process-level performance. A1 and A2 accelerate inflow into the real constraints — Financial document review (18h wait, 24% rework), Normalize statements (20h), Credit analysis (28h, 9 FTE), KYC/specialists (3 FTE) — with no capacity relief there. Back-office savings are also less valuable than they look because that pool is below 65% utilization after 15:00. End-to-end cycle time barely moves; queues migrate downstream and analyst overtime rises.

**Portfolio B — Bottleneck/synchronization: A5, A6, A4, A7.**
Optimizes: end-to-end cycle time and rework. It attacks the three dominant delay structures: the missing-document loop (A5: 52% repeat, 3,300/2,800 DFG loop), the analyst-side gate (A6: 20h normalization wait feeding a 28h credit-analysis queue), the specialist choke point (A4: 31h wait, 82% critical-path incidence, blocks signing), and the longest queue in the log (A7: 58h external valuation synchronization).
Sacrifices: direct labor savings at high-frequency steps and, critically, it contains no unambiguous control-strengthening module (A4 helps compliance capacity but does not add a control), so as stated it fails the compliance constraint — it needs A9 or A3 swapped in to be admissible. It also delivers less visible "headcount" savings, making it politically harder.

**Portfolio C — Compliance-first: A3, A9, A12, A4.**
Optimizes: control posture and regulatory risk. A3 hardens KYC screening with an auditable escalation trail; A9 enforces contract-decision consistency and the KYC-before-signing gate (attacking the 6.5% provisional-signing finding and the 8.5%/14% remediation loops); A12 structures remediation; A4 expands the effective capacity of the 3-FTE specialist pool that every KYC control depends on.
Sacrifices: cycle-time and labor gains outside the compliance path. The missing-document loop and analyst bottleneck remain untouched, so Financial document review and Credit analysis waits persist and RMs see little customer-experience improvement. Throughput gains are modest in year 1.

**Portfolio R — Recommended hybrid (defined here, argued in Section 7): A5, A6, A9, A4.**
Optimizes: the intersection — it takes the two highest process-level-leverage modules from Portfolio B (A5, A6), the control anchor from Portfolio C (A9), and the specialist de-bottlenecker (A4). It satisfies the compliance constraint via A9, spends only one deep-integration slot (A9; A5/A6/A4 are shallow/mid-layer, leaving one deep slot free for wave-2 preparation of A3 or A1), and avoids increasing customer friction (A5 reduces friction by making document requests one-shot instead of 52%-repeated).
Sacrifices: the headline "we automated the biggest activity" story (A1 deferred) and external-valuation triage (A7 deferred to a cheap SLA-based interim measure).

# 4. Expected process-level effects

**Portfolio Q (quick-win).**
- Cycle time: minimal end-to-end improvement. Registration and classification contribute ~20 min service and ~3.5h wait per case; the dominant waits (18h, 20h, 28h, 31h, 58h, 44h) are untouched except signing (A10) and contracting (A9). A10's benefit is real (44h wait, 21% reminder loop on 7,300 cases) but sits at the end of a slow pipeline.
- Waiting time: falls at Register/Classify, rises at Financial document review, Normalize, Credit analysis and KYC as accelerated inflow hits fixed capacity (the DFG shows Classify → KYC 7,250 and Classify → FDR 3,940 — both funnels into constrained pools). Queues migrate; they do not shrink.
- Rework: A2 may cut the 8% misclassification if the recommender outperforms humans, but a bad model raises wrong-control-path rework, the most expensive kind. The 52% document loop and 38% mismatch rework persist.
- Handovers: largely unchanged; back office hands the same volume to the same pools faster.
- Resource demand: back office demand falls (already under-utilized after 15:00, so savings are partly illusory); credit-analyst and compliance-specialist demand rises. Net human workload shifts from cheap, slack capacity to scarce, expensive capacity.
- Control violations: A9 reduces contract-decision mismatches (8.5% → materially lower) and can gate provisional signing; but faster upstream flow into month-end pressure could worsen the 5.8% four-eyes violations if unaddressed.

**Portfolio B (bottleneck/synchronization).**
- Cycle time: largest reduction. Cutting the document loop (A5) removes an 8h request wait plus a repeated 18h review wait for the ~2,800 looping cases; A6 shortens the 20h normalization gate feeding Credit analysis; A4 cuts most of the 31h mismatch wait on the 82% of affected cases where it is critical-path; A7 trims the 58h valuation tail for Equipment finance.
- Waiting time: falls exactly at the four longest documented queues. Concurrency helps: since KYC and Financial document review actually run in parallel (despite sequential recording), fixing both branches (A4 on the KYC branch, A5/A6 on the document branch) shortens the true critical path rather than just one recorded arc.
- Rework: the 52%-repeat request loop and the 38% mismatch rework fall directly; the 24% FDR exception rate falls because submissions arrive complete; the 16% normalization rework falls with structured extraction.
- Handovers: the FDR ↔ Request-missing-documents ping-pong (3,300/2,800) and KYC ↔ Resolve-mismatch loop (1,750/1,160) shrink, reducing handover count per case and the dependency on the two senior specialists visible in the handover network.
- Resource demand: analyst load per case drops (A6 absorbs the 75-min normalization), offsetting the increased clean inflow — this is precisely the pairing the resource note demands. Specialist load per mismatch drops. Back office loses the request-chasing work.
- Control violations: indirect improvement only (less month-end rush pressure on four-eyes); provisional signing and contract mismatch controls are not strengthened — the portfolio's admissibility gap.

**Portfolio C (compliance-first).**
- Cycle time: moderate improvement concentrated on the KYC branch (4h + 31h waits) and contracting (22h). The document/analysis branch (18h + 20h + 28h) is untouched, and since KYC and FDR are concurrent, cutting only the KYC branch improves the critical path only for cases where KYC is the longer branch — mainly mismatch cases and High-exposure SMEs.
- Waiting time: Resolve entity mismatch (31h) and Contract preparation (22h) fall; Wait-for-valuation and signing waits persist.
- Rework: KYC exception rework (11%) and mismatch rework (38%) fall; contract remediation (8.5%, 14% renewals) falls sharply via A9's rule checks; the 52% document loop persists.
- Handovers: KYC-ops-to-senior-specialist dependency loosens (A3 filters, A4 accelerates); Contract prep → Remediate → Contract prep loop (650/510) shrinks.
- Resource demand: specialist effective capacity rises; legal ops drafting load falls but exception-review load appears; analyst load unchanged; back office unchanged.
- Control violations: the strongest improvement — provisional signing (6.5%) suppressed by the A9 gate, contract-decision mismatch enforced pre-signature, full model/override/decision audit trail introduced, A12 codifies remediation.

**Portfolio R (recommended hybrid)** combines B's cycle-time/rework profile on both concurrent branches (A5/A6 on the document branch, A4 on the KYC branch) with C's contract-and-signing control gains via A9, at the cost of leaving the 58h external-valuation wait to an interim SLA measure and deferring A3's screening-volume relief to year 2.

# 5. Risks and compensating controls

- **Auditability of model-assisted decisions (A2, A3, A4, A6, A9).** Risk: regulators cannot reconstruct why a case was cleared or routed. Control: every module logs model suggestion, confidence, human override, and final decision (explicitly required); logs are immutable and sampled monthly by internal audit.
- **False negatives in KYC-related automation (A3, A4).** Risk: a missed hit or wrong entity match is a severe regulatory breach. Control: launch in decision-support mode (no auto-clear); thresholds set so the model only auto-proposes, never auto-closes; periodic back-testing against known hits; the 3 specialists remain accountable sign-off — automation does not transfer compliance responsibility.
- **False positives / exception flooding (A3, A9).** Risk: over-cautious rules swamp the 3-FTE specialist pool or 4-FTE legal ops, recreating the bottleneck as an escalation queue. Control: monitor escalation rate and queue age per pool with explicit capacity triggers; tune thresholds under a documented change-control process; month-end staffing plan for legal ops.
- **Model and rule drift (A2, A3, A6, A9).** Risk: product mix shifts, new document formats, or policy changes silently degrade accuracy; A9's rule library going stale is a stated legal risk. Control: quarterly revalidation with holdout samples; Legal owns the A9 rule library with versioning and mandatory review on every policy change; drift dashboards (override rate trending up = early warning).
- **Extraction errors on low-quality PDFs (A6).** Risk: wrong figures enter the credit model. Control: confidence-scored fields, low-confidence items routed to human normalization, dual-key verification on High-exposure cases, reconciliation totals checked automatically.
- **Misclassification onto the wrong control path (A2).** Risk: High-exposure case treated as Micro — wrong KYC depth, wrong committee cadence. Control: human confirmation mandatory for any High-exposure/exposure-downgrade recommendation; downstream consistency check (collateral or financials inconsistent with class triggers re-classification).
- **Four-eyes erosion (A6, A8 later).** Risk: automation compresses month-end work but the 5.8% High-exposure violations occur exactly then; auto-assembled packs could obscure who "performed" packaging. Control: system-enforced segregation — the workflow blocks Committee packaging completion by the credit analyst of record on High-exposure cases; violation rate is a gate metric (Section 6).
- **Provisional signing / sequence violations (A9, A10).** Risk: e-signature makes premature signing easier. Control: A9 releases contracts to the signing channel only on system-verified KYC clearance; hard block, with exception path requiring compliance officer approval and automatic A12 remediation ticket.
- **Shifted human workload.** Risk: freed back-office labor is banked as savings while new exception-handling roles (extraction QA, escalation review, rule maintenance) go unstaffed. Control: explicit re-deployment plan — back-office capacity moves to document-quality outreach and extraction QA; workload per pool tracked before/after each go-live.
- **Customer friction (A5, A10).** Risk: rigid checklists and nagging reminders alienate applicants and RMs resist. Control: product-specific dynamic checklists co-designed with RMs; nudge frequency capped; RM override with reason code; friction measured by abandonment rate.

# 6. Recommended phased rollout

First-year plan for Portfolio R (A5, A6, A9, A4), respecting the two-deep-integration limit in months 1–6. Deep slots in H1: **A9** (contract/decision/core data) and **pre-work for A3's KYC-system integration** (build only; go-live in year 2) — A5, A6, A4 are portal/document/workbench-layer and do not consume deep slots.

**Phase 0 (Month 0–1) — Baseline.** Freeze process-mining baselines: end-to-end cycle time by segment and product, waiting times at the six long queues (18h, 20h, 28h, 31h, 44h, 58h), loop frequencies (3,300/2,800 document loop; 1,750/1,160 mismatch loop; 650/510 remediation loop), control-violation rates (5.8% four-eyes, 6.5% provisional signing, 8.5%/14% contract mismatch), and utilization per pool.

**Phase 1 (Months 1–4) — A5 missing-document checklist & nudging.** Shallow integration, high yield, reduces friction (helps RM buy-in).
Metrics: repeat-request rate (52% baseline), first-time-complete submission rate, FDR waiting time (18h), FDR exception rate (24%), customer abandonment.
Gate to Phase 2: repeat-request rate below ~30% and no abandonment increase after 8 weeks.

**Phase 2 (Months 3–7, overlapping) — A6 financial-statement extraction/normalization**, piloted on Micro and standard SME first (judgment-heavy High-exposure last).
Metrics: normalization wait (20h), analyst minutes per case, extraction field accuracy on audit sample, low-confidence escalation rate, Credit-analysis queue age (watch for overload — the resource note's warning).
Gate: extraction accuracy above agreed threshold on two consecutive monthly samples and analyst queue age not rising; else hold rollout scope.

**Phase 3 (Months 4–9) — A9 contract generation with control-rule checks** (deep integration slot 1, started month 1, go-live after decision-data mapping is validated). Includes the KYC-clearance-before-signing hard gate.
Metrics: contract remediation loop rate (8.5% / 14% renewals), provisional-signing incidents (6.5% baseline, target ~0 post-gate), contract-prep wait (22h), rule-check false-positive rate, legal-ops exception queue at month end.
Gate: remediation rate halved and false-positive rate stable for one month-end cycle before extending to Credit-line renewals (the worst segment).

**Phase 4 (Months 7–12) — A4 entity-mismatch resolution assistant.**
Metrics: mismatch resolution time (55 min service, 31h wait), mismatch rework (38%), KYC→specialist escalation queue age, senior-specialist handover concentration (social-network metric), specialist override rate.
Gate for year-2 A3 go-live: mismatch queue cleared to under one day and specialists confirm capacity headroom — only then may A3 (built in H1's second deep slot) switch on, because A3's escalations land in this pool.

**Continuous:** monthly control dashboard (four-eyes violations, sequence violations, audit-trail completeness) reviewed with compliance; any control metric regressing beyond baseline pauses the next phase. Interim, non-module measure from month 1: trigger collateral valuation requests immediately at credit-analysis start and publish valuer SLA league tables (poor-man's A7).

**Year-2 queue (prepared, not launched):** A3 (deep integration pre-built), A10 (once A9's KYC gate is proven), A7 full routing, then A8 and A11 — both explicitly deferred until upstream artifacts and controls are demonstrably reliable, per the constraint against automating downstream bots on unreliable upstream controls.

# 7. Final recommendation

I recommend **Portfolio R: A5 (document checklist/nudging), A6 (statement extraction/normalization), A9 (contract generation with control checks), A4 (entity-mismatch assistant)**, rolled out per Section 6.

Why not automate the highest-frequency activity first? Registration (A1) is the classic frequency trap this dataset is built to expose. Three artifacts show it: first, Register application's queue is short (1.5h wait, 8-minute task, 4% rework) — it is visible, not binding. Second, its resource pool has slack: back office runs below 65% utilization after 15:00, so "labor saved" there releases capacity nobody is waiting for. Third, A1's own risk note says it "may expose classification, KYC, and document-review bottlenecks": the DFG funnels its output straight into pools that are genuinely constrained — 9 credit analysts behind a 20h normalization gate and 28h analysis queue, and 3 compliance specialists behind a 31h mismatch queue with a two-person handover dependency. Automating registration first makes cases arrive faster at queues that cannot absorb them; end-to-end cycle time would barely move while analyst and specialist overload, month-end rush, and therefore four-eyes violations (5.8%) would likely worsen. Flow through a process is governed by its constraints, not by its busiest activity.

Portfolio R instead spends the four module slots where the process-mining evidence locates the damage: the largest rework loop (52% repeated document requests driving an 18h review wait), the gating step before the credit model (20h normalization wait on the scarce analyst pool — fixing this is also the precondition for safely accelerating anything upstream later, including A1), the highest per-case delay and hardest blocker (31h mismatch wait, critical-path in 82% of affected cases, legally blocking signing), and the control anchor (A9), which satisfies the compliance mandate, closes the 6.5% provisional-signing and 8.5–14% contract-mismatch findings, and consumes only one of the two deep-integration slots — the other is invested in building A3's KYC integration for year 2.

The trade-off is explicit: we forgo the politically attractive registration win and the external-valuation module in year 1. The registration win is deferred, not rejected — once A5/A6 have protected analyst capacity and A4 has relieved the specialist pool, A1 becomes cheap, safe, and genuinely beneficial in year 2. The valuation wait (58h) is partially addressed by a zero-module interim measure (early triggering and SLA triage). Management gets a defensible story: the portfolio is chosen for where hours of end-to-end time are actually lost and where control findings are actually generated, with every downstream automation (A8, A10, A11) explicitly gated on the upstream data quality and controls it depends on.
