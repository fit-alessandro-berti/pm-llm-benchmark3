# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

For utilization, summing case durations is valid only under strong assumptions about active effort, resource capacity, and the observation window. The union of intervals measures time with at least one open case, not necessarily labor utilization. Similarly, completion-timestamp throughput can be an administrative submission rate rather than production throughput.

- **G1:** Observed waits are 2–6 minutes and each service interval is three minutes. These are reasonable **if** they represent the actual fixture cycle. Summing four three-minute services over the three-minute cycle gives a naive 400% single-resource utilization; using four item-slots instead gives full slot occupancy during that cycle, a fundamentally different quantity. Completion throughput should be expressed as four items per batch cycle, not as an instantaneous spike at 09:03. A serial-single-server conformance or capacity model would falsely report resource violations, while treating the PLC stamps as independently measured item times could hide a cell-level constraint.

- **G2:** Observed waits to the common start range from 6–18 minutes. They are valid only for a KPI defined as “arrival to group launch.” Logged services are 8, 14, 21, and 27 minutes, totaling 70 minutes over a 27-minute union of intervals—a naive 259% utilization for one worker. If work was actually serial after group setup, later items’ logged services include time spent waiting inside the group, their active services are overstated, and their waits to actual item start are understated. If work was concurrent or multitasked, elapsed intervals still need not equal active effort. The four staggered completions may support completion throughput, but WIP and bottleneck calculations based on four simultaneous active services would be inflated.

- **G3:** End batching does **not by itself** bias start-based waiting: observed waits are 18–22 minutes and remain provisional if the starts are genuine review starts. It directly biases service and completion-based flow time. Durations total 67 minutes over a 28-minute union, giving a naive 239% single-worker utilization. Early reviews may have finished well before 11:30, so their logged services include time awaiting folder submission. Throughput appears as zero followed by four completions at 11:30, and WIP remains artificially open until then. A bottleneck can consequently be inferred from impossible utilization while actual review effort is unknown. If starts were also retrospectively estimated, even the waiting times lose their operational interpretation.

- **G4:** If lifecycle semantics are valid, there is little timestamp-induced bias: waits are 7, 10, 13, 16, and 19 minutes, services are four minutes each, and the resource is continuously occupied during the 20-minute drain. Observed drain throughput is five items per 20 minutes. These figures describe this window, not shift utilization or steady-state capacity. If starts were auto-chained, pauses could be absorbed into service, active utilization overstated, and waits assigned to the wrong state. Rising waits and a full drain support local congestion, but not a chronic bottleneck without demand and observation-window data.

- **G5:** Shared identity does not itself invalidate the observed 15–16-minute pool-level waits or 10–25-minute elapsed case durations. It does prevent worker-level attribution. Duration sum is 70 minutes over a 30-minute union: treating the pool as one worker yields a naive 233%, while dividing by three analysts yields an illustrative 78%, but neither is defensible active utilization without availability and effort data. Pool completion counts may be usable; per-analyst throughput is not. Treating the pool as one resource can manufacture a bottleneck, whereas automatically dividing by three can hide one.

- **G6:** The seven observed waits range from 35 to 175 minutes, while every observed service duration is zero. Waiting and service therefore point in opposite directions: enormous apparent congestion but no measured work. Completion throughput becomes a seven-item spike at 17:00, utilization based on summed service becomes approximately zero, and WIP can remain artificially open for hours. Bottleneck algorithms may alternatively report severe queueing or an idle, non-bottleneck resource, depending on which fields they emphasize. SLA and timestamp-based conformance results can be either falsely late or falsely on time, depending on the deadline and the actual unobserved work time.

  The G6 paradox follows directly from assigning one administrative placeholder `T` to both lifecycle fields: waiting becomes `T - queue_arrival`, which includes queueing plus any unobserved work and delay until entry, while service becomes `T - T = 0`. Long waiting and instantaneous service are therefore artifacts of the same substitution, not evidence of highly efficient work.

- **G7:** Observed waits range from 11 to +2 minutes and all services equal 15 minutes. Neither should be used as an exact KPI. Rounding can inflate or deflate either quantity, and the negative waits demonstrate a semantic, rounding, or clock inconsistency rather than a queue that should simply be truncated at zero. Five 15-minute intervals over a 15-minute union produce a naive 500% single-resource utilization; using their union instead produces a fully busy interval, but neither measures manual effort. Five completions at 14:15 create an artificial throughput burst. Queue build-up, bottleneck analysis, and timestamp conformance should be excluded or treated with explicit interval uncertainty.

- **G8:** Observed waits are 38, 43, 48, 54, and 58 minutes; services are consistently seven minutes. The arithmetic services and serial-drain throughput are provisionally informative, but the waits are dominated by time before the first start. Utilization is 100% during the 35-minute drain if the timestamps are trusted, yet only about 48% over 15:00–16:13 if the entire arrival-to-last-completion window is used as the denominator. Including unavailable time can therefore make the resource look idle, while excluding it can make it look saturated. Attributing all waiting to inefficient triage would mislocate the bottleneck if capacity loss is validated. Conversely, the different safety-system clock prevents an exact outage-to-restart calculation.

Because each case contains only one supplied activity, these fragments cannot support control-flow conformance conclusions. When joined to complete logs, common timestamps should not be ordered using `sequence_no`: that would turn insertion order into fabricated execution order. G1, G2, and G5 can create false parallelism or resource-constraint violations; G3, G6, and G7 can distort timestamp alignments and SLA checks; G4 and G8 can hide pauses through auto-adjacency; and G8’s safety relationship can be falsely ordered across unsynchronized clocks.

def max_tie(series):
    counts = series.dropna().value_counts()
    return int(counts.iloc[0]) if len(counts) else 0

def compact_range(series):
    x = pd.to_numeric(series, errors="coerce").dropna()
    if x.empty:
        return "NA"
    lo, hi = float(x.min()), float(x.max())
    return f"{lo:g}" if lo == hi else f"{lo:g}..{hi:g}"

def overlap_counts(g):
    # Computed per cluster/resource; no deduplication by resource or timestamp.
    z = g[["start", "complete"]].dropna()
    intervals = list(z.itertuples(index=False, name=None))
    overlapping = 0
    for (s1, c1), (s2, c2) in combinations(intervals, 2):
        if max(s1, s2) < min(c1, c2):
            overlapping += 1
    total = len(intervals) * (len(intervals) - 1) // 2
    return overlapping, total

def queue_sweep(g):
    # Arrivals add one and observed starts remove one. Events at the same
    # timestamp are netted. Negative backlog is retained as an inconsistency.
    events = {}
    for r in g.itertuples(index=False):
        if pd.notna(r.queue_arrival):
            events.setdefault(r.queue_arrival, [0, 0])[0] += 1
        if pd.notna(r.start):

        backlog += arrivals - starts
        max_backlog = max(max_backlog, backlog)
        min_backlog = min(min_backlog, backlog)
    return max_backlog, min_backlog

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

**Resource identity sensitivity.** If non-pool resource IDs are trusted, G2 and G3 should not be read as four dedicated workers executing in parallel; group logging, serial work with deferred events, or multitasking becomes more important. Trusted single-capacity identities also strengthen the serial readings of G4 and G8. If, purely as a sensitivity assumption, those accounts were shared, overlapping G2/G3 intervals could belong to different people and serial adjacency could no longer be assigned to one worker’s utilization. There is no supplied evidence to apply that assumption to T-B, T-C, T-D, or T-H. G5 is different: sharing is observed context, not a sensitivity invention. Resource trust does not repair G6’s lifecycle semantics or G7’s rounding, and RBT-7 must in any case be modeled as a capacitated cell rather than a conventional single worker.

**Exact-time sensitivity.** Treating the displayed values as exact yields the ties, adjacency, and overlap counts computed above. This is a defensible description of the log, but not of physical execution. Exact adjacency in G4/G8 supports near-serial behavior; it does not establish that no unrecorded pause occurred. Strictly zero pairwise overlap in G6 is similarly meaningless because all seven intervals have zero length.

**Interval-censoring sensitivity.** As an optional sensitivity model—not an observation—one could assume nearest rounding with error at most half the stated precision. Under that model:

- G1’s second-scale ties remain strong evidence of a common PLC cycle, though still not proof of item-level physical simultaneity.
- G4/G8 adjacency could conceal very small gaps or overlaps, but the several-minute serial structure remains robust.
- G2’s common starts and G3’s common ends need only be equal at minute-level logging resolution. Their broader start-versus-end patterns remain intact.
- G5’s shortest pairwise positive overlap is approximately eight minutes, so open-interval overlap is robust to modest one-minute uncertainty. Active concurrency and worker identity remain unresolved.
- G6’s one-minute resolution cannot turn its fields into credible execution lifecycles. The semantic problem dominates the small numerical uncertainty.

For G7, an optional independent nearest-quarter-hour model would make a displayed 15-minute duration consistent with roughly 0–30 minutes before applying additional lifecycle constraints. That is an illustration, not a defensible correction: the actual rule is undocumented, errors may be correlated, and true accuracy is unknown. Under that particular nearest-rounding model, M05’s start would still be no later than approximately 14:07:30, before its 14:11 arrival; thus negative waiting would additionally require another rounding rule, different queue-arrival semantics, or a clock offset. A smaller PM4Py `merge_distance` does not perform this uncertainty analysis and cannot recover the unrounded timestamps.

Several temporal patterns have multiple defensible physical readings:

1. **G1 equal starts and ends:** a real four-item fixture cycle; one machine cycle with item records stamped from the cycle; or synchronized administrative output. Fixture capacity makes the first reading likely but not logically necessary.
2. **G2 common start and staggered ends:** simultaneous start of parallel/multitasked work; group setup followed by serial inspections; or merely a UI group-start event with item starts unobserved. Separate completion buttons make the ends more informative than the common start.
3. **G3 staggered starts and common end:** concurrent reviews; serial reviews with deferred completion; multitasking with suspended intervals; or a genuine common deadline. The folder-submission context favors deferred completion but does not identify the execution order.
4. **G4/G8 exact adjacency:** uninterrupted serial work; automated next-start assignment; ordinary queue draining; or a deliberate sequential operational batch. In G8, all of these can coexist with prior capacity loss.
5. **G5 overlapping intervals:** several analysts working concurrently; fewer analysts multitasking; or open VDI sessions whose elapsed durations contain idle time.
6. **G7 equal coarse intervals:** a genuine common measurement cycle, several serial measurements collapsed onto one grid, or a mixed pattern distorted by undocumented rounding.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

1. **Highest priority—separate execution from administration.** Store immutable item-level `execution_start` and `execution_complete`, plus distinct `recorded_at`, `submitted_at`, `form_opened_at`, and `corrected_at` where applicable. G2 needs separate group- and item-start events; G3 needs review completion separate from folder submission; G6 should either collect the real lifecycle or be explicitly modeled as an administrative-close activity only. Never replace raw values with reconstructed estimates.

2. **Recover or preserve fine execution timestamps.** Export unrounded PLC/scanner/MES values and retain source sequence. For G7, obtain the pre-rounded timestamps and document the rounding rule. If only coarse values can be retained, store explicit lower/upper bounds or a rounding code rather than presenting a point timestamp as exact.

3. **Synchronize and monitor clocks.** Use UTC internally, record time zone and clock source, synchronize upstream, PLC, scanner, VDI, and safety systems where feasible, and log measured clock offset/drift. `recorded_at` should be generated by the receiving database at high precision. This is necessary before using G8’s safety interval to explain the repair queue or before accepting cross-system waiting times.

4. **Separate worker, account, equipment, and session identity.** Collect pseudonymized badge-level worker ID, equipment/resource ID, login/session ID, and account-sharing status. For `analyst_pool`, preserve pool membership and badge identity without treating the shared login as a worker. Resource calendars should identify capacity and availability.

5. **Instrument batches and capacity explicitly.** Add group/batch/fixture-cycle IDs, item membership, workstation capacity, fixture slots, setup start/end, and whether processing is simultaneous, sequential, or merely logged together. For G1, capacity four and a cycle ID would distinguish cell cycle time from independent item services.

6. **Record state changes rather than inferring them from interval endpoints.** Add pause/resume, setup, waiting-for-item, waiting-for-review, downtime, capacity-loss/restoration, and safety-check events. Where multitasking is allowed, collect active-effort intervals or task-switch events. This prevents open elapsed intervals from being interpreted as continuous labor.

7. **Make precision and derivation metadata field-specific.** For every timestamp—including `queue_arrival`—store resolution, rounding/truncation rule, estimated-versus-measured status, derivation/default rule, and known accuracy or clock offset. Source-level “1 min” or “15 min” metadata alone is insufficient.

8. **Validate in order of KPI risk.** First block execution KPIs from G6/G7 and obtain replacement data; then validate G2/G3 lifecycle semantics and G5 identity; next validate G8’s outage relationship and G4’s possible auto-chaining; finally confirm G1’s item-versus-cycle semantics. All KPI calculations should retain quality flags and observation-window/capacity denominators.

# 7. Final adjudication

- **High-confidence temporal or data-semantics diagnoses:** G1 is very likely a genuine fixture-level simultaneous batch; G2 is start batched; G3 is end batched/bulk submitted; G4 is serial/adjacent; G6 is a bulk administrative lifecycle; and G7 is severely coarsened. For G5, high confidence applies only to unreliable worker attribution, not to a particular concurrency mechanism. For G8, serial draining is high confidence, while capacity loss as its cause is only moderate confidence.

- **Provisionally usable, with narrowly defined KPIs:** G1 can support cell-level cycle and batch-throughput analysis if capacity and cycle semantics are respected. G4 can support observed-window serial service and waiting analysis after checking that starts are measured. G8 can support serial-drain service analysis, with waiting segmented or annotated for possible downtime and no exact safety-clock comparison. G2 supports waiting to **group launch**, not necessarily to item service. G3’s start-based waits and G5’s pool-level waits are provisional and require comparable clocks and valid start semantics.

- **Exclude or interval-censor in queue/service analysis:** Exclude G6 from execution waiting, service, utilization, and bottleneck calculations; its administrative submission process can still be studied separately. Prefer excluding G7 until raw timestamps are obtained; otherwise use explicit interval-censoring plus clock/rounding sensitivity and retain negative waits as quality failures. Do not use G2/G3 logged intervals as active service without validation, and do not use G5 for per-worker utilization or throughput.

- **Operational validation is required before performance or conformance conclusions:** G1 needs item-versus-cycle confirmation; G2 needs UI/group semantics; G3 needs actual completion and start provenance; G4 needs auto-chaining and pause checks; G5 needs badge/session identity and effort semantics; and G8 needs outage causality and clock alignment. G6 and G7 require new or recovered data, not merely analytical relabeling.

No cluster alone justifies a shift-level utilization, sustained-throughput, bottleneck, or control-flow conformance verdict. Those conclusions additionally require demand, capacity calendars, observation windows, complete case histories, and validated lifecycle semantics.
