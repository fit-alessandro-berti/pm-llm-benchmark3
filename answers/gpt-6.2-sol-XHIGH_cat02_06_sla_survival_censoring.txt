# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Start the elapsed-time clock at `Submit`. A `Resolve` at or before 72 hours meets the deadline. Assume each case is observed from submission until its first recorded terminal event or the extract cutoff, and that a recorded terminal event ends the internal episode. Let $T$ be the time to that event and $J$ its type. `Transfer` ends internal ownership. Its interpretation changes if the SLA follows the case across organizations.

| Estimand | Definition and operational use |
|---|---|
| **Internal resolution SLA miss** | $1-F_R(72)$, where $F_R(t)=P(T\le t,J=\mathrm{Resolve})$. The denominator is all submissions. Use this for governance when the obligation is successful internal resolution. |
| **Still internally active at 72 hours** | $S_T(72)=P(T>72)$, with all four terminal types included. Use this for the overdue queue and internal staffing. At population level, the miss probability equals this active probability plus the incidences of withdrawal, rejection, and transfer by 72. |
| **Late internal resolution within the next day** | $F_R(96)-F_R(72)=P(72<T\le96,J=\mathrm{Resolve})$, per submission. The illustrative 96-hour horizon gives service recovery a fixed window and avoids extrapolating to lifetime outcomes. Dividing by $S_T(72)$, when positive, gives the recovery probability for cases still internally active at the deadline. |
| **End-to-end resolution SLA miss** | Probability of no resolution by 72 hours, counting external resolution after transfer as success if timely. Use this for a customer-facing SLA that follows the case. For this sensitivity estimand, assume withdrawal and rejection end eligibility for resolution and external resolution can occur only after handoff. |

| Case | Internal SLA miss | Active internally at 72 | Internal resolution in (72, 96] h | End-to-end SLA miss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76 h | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| S09, open at 92 h | Yes, already observed | Yes, already observed | Unknown for the remaining four hours | Yes, already observed |
| S06, withdrawn at 27 h | Yes | No | No | Yes under the stated terminal-eligibility assumption |
| S08, transferred at 68 h | Yes | No | No | Unknown, with four hours remaining at handoff |

For an open case, censor follow-up at its age at the common cutoff. An open case observed through 72 hours has a known deadline outcome and a censored later event time. An open case younger than 72 has an unknown deadline outcome. At a tied elapsed time, count events before removing cases censored at that time, assuming those cases were observed through that age.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use elapsed hours to each observed event or censoring time. The resolution and any-terminal indicators below describe observed events at that time. A zero resolution indicator on an open row leaves future resolution possible.

| `observed_status` | Observed resolution / any internal terminal | Primary competing-risk code | Primary handling and sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `resolved` | 1 / 1 | 1 | Resolution event at the observed duration. Count it as timely at or before 72, late in the fixed recovery window if after 72 and at or before 96. |
| `withdrawn` | 0 / 1 | 2 | Competing withdrawal at the observed duration. Count it toward any disposition, or censor there in a hypothetical net-resolution analysis. |
| `rejected` | 0 / 1 | 3 | Competing rejection at the observed duration. Count it toward any disposition, or censor there in a hypothetical net-resolution analysis. |
| `transferred` | 0 / 1 | 4 | **Internal absorbing event.** Count the handoff as a competing event and end internal follow-up. **Conditionally independent tracking loss.** For an end-to-end model, recode as 0 at handoff if comparable cases with the same observed history can represent follow-up after handoff. **Unrestricted external outcome.** For the end-to-end deadline, bound the outcome by allowing any fraction of early transfers to resolve externally by 72. A handoff after the deadline cannot create an on-time success. |
| `open` | 0 / 0 | 0 | Right-censor at the observed age. Assess follow-up by cohort, priority, and entry period. Vary the unknown deadline outcomes of cases younger than 72 when independence is doubtful. |

The transfer-as-censoring scenario needs exchangeability of subsequent resolution and competing-event risks given the information at handoff, and comparable cases that stay observed. Transfer can select unusual cases and the external organization can change their resolution times. The bounds scenario permits both mechanisms.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

- The timely fraction among cases recorded as resolved is 4/6 in A and 5/7 in B. These denominators select cases that reached resolution before the cutoff and exclude the rest of the submissions. S09 and S26 are already beyond the deadline and would disappear. S13 contributes a known miss to the resolved-only calculation because its 110-hour resolution was observed.
- S06 and S23 had observed competing events. Removing them from the denominator inflates the apparent internal success rate. Treating them as ordinary Kaplan–Meier censoring would redistribute their probability to outcomes among cases still at risk. S08 and S21 left internal ownership before 72, so their internal misses are known even though their end-to-end outcomes are unknown.
- S10 was open at 47 hours and S24 at 67. Both may still meet the deadline. S09 at 92 and S25 at 79 have already missed it and still contribute observed active time up to their respective censoring ages. All four belong in the risk sets before their observed ages.
- The common cutoff creates administrative censoring with follow-up length determined by entry date. If case mix, workload, or rollout changes with entry date, pooling follow-up can be informative. All three transfers, S08, S21 and S32, are escalated P1 cases. S26 has a 72-hour recorded inactivity gap at age 104, and S11 a 55-hour gap at age 81. The gaps are consistent with stalled work or delayed recording. If recording lags depend on workflow, recorded active status can also differ from actual status.
- A has P1/P2/P3 counts of 3/7/5, while B has 5/7/5. B also began later, under a rollout with incomplete compliance. Priority, calendar trends, and unequal follow-up can affect a crude comparison. In the observed prefixes, `AutoCheck` immediately follows `Submit` in 0/15 A cases and 12/17 B cases. That is a routing difference in the extract, with individual policy exposure and the rollout effect still unknown.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

Use **Aalen–Johansen (AJ)** for the primary analysis. At each elapsed event time, use all cases still observed and internally active just before that time. For cause $k$, add $S_T(t-)d_k(t)/Y(t)$ to its cumulative incidence, where $d_k(t)$ is the number of events of that cause and $Y(t)$ the size of the risk set. Update $S_T$ with the total number of terminal events. Estimate all four incidences, then derive the deadline and recovery quantities in section 1. Unadjusted AJ requires independent administrative censoring within the population being estimated. If independence is only plausible within measured strata, estimate within them and standardize, given adequate follow-up and sample size. AJ accommodates competing events without assuming independent latent failure times.

| Descriptive quantity | A (n = 15) | B (n = 17) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Known timely internal resolutions | 4 | 5 |
| Known internal SLA misses | 9 | 9 |
| Open and observed for less than 72 h | 2 | 3 |
| Internally active and observed just before 72 h | 4 | 5 |
| AJ internal SLA miss | 70.8% | 68.4% |
| AJ still internally active at 72 h | 33.9% | 41.6% |
| AJ late internal resolution in (72, 96] h | 8.5% | 19.4% |
| Restricted mean active time through 72 h | 49.6 h | 51.1 h |

Directly standardizing the cohort-by-priority AJ deadline misses to the pooled priority distribution (8/32 P1, 14/32 P2, 10/32 P3) gives 68.8% in A and 67.5% in B. Each stratum has only three to seven submissions. At 96 hours just one A case and two B cases are observed and at risk immediately before that time. Report counts alongside estimates, use the finite-extract bounds in section 6, and treat cohort differences and tail estimates as exploratory. Sampling intervals, if the extract is treated as a sample, address a different uncertainty than the missing-outcome bounds.

| Method | Role for these data |
|---|---|
| Kaplan–Meier | With any internal terminal disposition as the event, KM estimates the same $S_T$ as AJ. Censoring all alternative outcomes and taking $1-\widehat S_{\mathrm{KM}}$ gives a net resolution quantity, which generally overstates the observed-world resolution incidence when competitors occur. A causal interpretation as eliminating competitors requires additional assumptions. |
| Restricted mean time | Use $\int_0^{72} S_T(t)\,dt$ as a secondary summary supported by the extract. It measures expected case-hours under internal ownership per submission up to 72 hours, useful for capacity planning. |
| Cause-specific hazards | Among cases currently active, model rates of each type of exit, such as resolution and transfer. The competing exit removes the case from later risk sets. With more data, combine the estimated causes to predict cumulative incidence. This extract supports descriptive comparisons, with too few events for a reliable adjusted hazard fit. |
| Subdistribution hazards | Fine–Gray regression can compare the resolution CIF across baseline groups with an adjusted model. Its weighted risk set includes cases that already had competing events. It is poorly suited to interpreting stage transition rates, and this extract favors transparent stratified AJ. |
| Landmarking | At a chosen hour such as 48, select cases observed and active then, freeze their histories at that time, and estimate deadline outcomes conditional on the landmark. This directly supports alerting and needs timed histories that the extract only partially supplies. |
| Multi-state model | With event times, estimate transitions between live stages and to absorbing dispositions. Allow exit rates to depend on elapsed case age, time in stage, and relevant history. This would support stage-specific workload analysis. The supplied sequences identify observed transitions. Estimating sojourn times requires their timestamps. |

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Represent the latest recorded nonterminal activity as the live state. The following transition list is derived from adjacent activities in the supplied prefixes. `Review` can recur through the `Rework` or `Escalate` loop.

- `Submit` → `Screen`, `AutoCheck`
- `Screen` → `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`
- `AutoCheck` → `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`
- `Review` → `Rework`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`
- `Escalate` → `Review`, `Resolve`, `Transfer`
- `Rework` → `Review`, `Resolve`
- `Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`, `Transfer` are absorbing in the internal model.

An ordered prefix supports descriptive features such as review visit count, presence of a rework loop, first activity after submission, and the relative order of screening and checks. S07 returns to Review after Rework, while S27 goes directly from Rework to Resolve. S29 is in Review at cutoff and has already escalated, illustrating why current state and prior history are both useful. With timestamped events, create start–stop intervals and update the current state, cumulative visits and rework, escalation history, and inactivity using events already recorded at each prediction time. Evaluate, for example, exits from Review as functions of case age and time in Review, with prior Review visits carried as history.

Cohort and priority are candidate baseline covariates, assuming priority refers to intake priority. If priority can change, use the intake value and timestamp later upgrades. The observed routing, rework, and escalation indicators are post-submission and can be downstream of the policy. Coding `ever escalated` at baseline assigns earlier time to the escalated group even though a case had to remain active until escalation to join that group. It also leaks future information into alerting. Use a time-updated indicator or a landmark history for prediction. For estimation of a total policy effect, conditioning on these downstream variables can remove mediated effects or introduce selection bias.

Compute inactivity at the prediction time from the last event known then. S26's last observed activity was at elapsed hour 32 (104 minus 72), so its recorded gap at a 48-hour landmark was 16 hours if that activity was available in the system then. The gap of 72 hours is a cutoff feature. Terminal rows have zero gap in this extract, so using the extracted gap across statuses for earlier scoring also leaks information. The arithmetic for the last recorded event in an open case leaves all preceding activity times unknown.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

1. **Transfer and follow-up assumptions.** The primary analysis treats transfer as an internal competing event. Reclassifying transfer as censoring with withdrawal and rejection still competing gives a model-based deadline miss of 70.8% in A and 66.8% in B. A's transfer at 68 occurs after its final observed timely resolution. B's transfer at 26 occurs before additional timely resolutions and changes their estimated probability weights. S32 transfers at 96 and changes neither cohort's 72-hour result. For end-to-end performance with unrestricted external outcomes, allow S08 and S21 either to resolve externally by 72 or to miss the deadline. S32 has already missed it.
2. **Withdrawal and rejection.** For a disposition-speed SLA, count both as timely dispositions when they occur by 72. In the primary AJ analysis, B ranks better on internal resolution misses and worse on timely disposition. As a diagnostic, censor withdrawal, rejection, and transfer to compute the net resolution quantity. Its complement is about 63.9% in A and 63.8% in B. This attenuated difference is based on the cumulative resolution hazard among cases that are still at risk.
3. **Informative censoring.** Let $r_g$ be known timely resolutions, $u_g$ open cases younger than 72, $x_g$ transfers before 72, and $n_g$ submissions in cohort $g$. For the end-to-end endpoint, vary the on-time fractions $q_{g,O}$ and $q_{g,X}$ among the unobserved outcomes. The resulting miss fraction is $1-(r_g+u_gq_{g,O}+x_gq_{g,X})/n_g$. Set $q_{g,X}=0$ for the internal endpoint. Letting the fractions range from 0 to 1 gives the following extract-level bounds, without any assumptions about independent censoring.

   | Deadline miss bounds | A | B |
   |---|---:|---:|
   | Internal | 9/15–11/15 (60.0–73.3%) | 9/17–12/17 (52.9–70.6%) |
   | End-to-end | 8/15–11/15 (53.3–73.3%) | 8/17–12/17 (47.1–70.6%) |

   If both young open A cases resolve on time and none of the three in B do, B has the higher internal miss fraction. The reverse assignments favor B. Applying the same earliest and latest outcome logic, the active-at-72 fraction is bounded by 4/15–6/15 in A and 5/17–8/17 in B. The late internal resolution fraction by 96 is bounded by 1/15–5/15 in A and 2/17–6/17 in B. For the late window, S26 is already observed through 96, whereas S09 could still resolve between 92 and 96. Either cohort could have the higher late-resolution fraction. With richer histories, stratify or model follow-up using entry dates, stage, and lagged inactivity, then compare conditional estimates or inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted estimates where follow-up has support. Use outcome scenarios for strata with little or no support and for possible unrecorded outcomes.
4. **Cohort and rollout.** Compare crude, priority-specific, and priority-standardized risks. The estimated crude difference favoring B shrinks from about 2.4 to 1.3 percentage points after standardization. The P1 point estimates go in the opposite direction (66.7% missed in A and 80.0% in B). Calendar maturity restrictions can be assessed once submission dates are available, with the resulting change in the target population made explicit. Verify exposure and compliance using policy records. Cohort mixes calendar and rollout, and comparisons based on observed routing or escalation may reflect selection into those activities. Either direction of a policy effect is consistent with this extract.

# 7. Operational interpretation

At a 48-hour checkpoint, score cases still internally active using their recorded histories up to that hour. Estimate the probability of missing internal resolution by 72 and, separately, the probability of remaining internally active then. A high predicted probability of staying active can trigger queue review, reassignment, or a check for a stalled handoff. A case likely to exit through rejection or withdrawal may need a decision or communication check. Use response capacity and the relative cost of missed versus unnecessary alerts to choose thresholds. Put cases already open beyond 72 in a recovery queue. Use stage occupancy and case-hours to plan staffing and route transferred cases to a handoff follow-up queue if the SLA follows the customer.

Evaluate calibration on subsequent submission cohorts with mature deadline follow-up, or use censoring-aware calibration and scoring when follow-up remains incomplete and the censoring assumptions are credible. Compare predicted with observed deadline risks by priority and cohort, including intersections where there is sufficient data. Report group sizes, follow-up coverage, alert rates, and missed and unnecessary alerts at the chosen threshold. Investigate disparities in logging, transfer, and case mix before changing how alerts are applied. Recheck calibration after staffing or escalation changes. A risk score prioritizes review, while the effect of changing case handling requires a separate evaluation.

Publish the all-submission denominator, terminal-event rules, cutoff date, unresolved deadline counts, and end-to-end uncertainty alongside the headline SLA rate. These distinguish service speed, internal success, and missing external outcomes.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

This uses pandas and PM4Py. The survival calculations implement the risk-set recursions directly and require no survival library. The PM4Py log contains ordered activities without synthetic timestamps.

```python

import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.log.obj import Event, EventLog, Trace

raw = """case|cohort|priority|duration_or_age_h|observed_status|prefix|escalated|rework_count|last_event_gap_h

S25|B|P1|79|open|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Escalate|yes|0|48

S27|B|P2|39|resolved|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Review>Rework>Resolve|no|1|0
S28|B|P2|88|resolved|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Review>Escalate>Resolve|yes|0|0

cases = pd.read_csv(StringIO(raw), sep="|")
deadline, recovery_horizon = 72, 96
causes = {1: "resolve", 2: "withdraw", 3: "reject", 4: "transfer"}
status_code = {"open": 0, "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2,
               "rejected": 3, "transferred": 4}
cases["internal"] = cases["observed_status"].map(status_code)
cases["transfer_censored"] = cases["internal"].replace({4: 0})
cases["net_resolution"] = cases["internal"].replace({2: 0, 3: 0, 4: 0})


def aj_at(rows, code_column, horizons):
    """Return right-continuous estimates at requested elapsed hours."""
    times = rows["duration_or_age_h"]
    exits = pd.crosstab(times, rows[code_column]).reindex(
        columns=[0, *causes], fill_value=0
    )
    grid = exits.index.union(pd.Index(horizons)).sort_values()
    exits = exits.reindex(grid, fill_value=0)
    at_risk, active, area, previous_time = len(rows), 1.0, 0.0, 0
    cif = dict.fromkeys(causes.values(), 0.0)
    results = []
    for time, counts in exits.iterrows():
        area += (time - previous_time) * active
        risk_before = at_risk
        if risk_before:
            for code, cause in causes.items():
                cif[cause] += active * counts[code] / risk_before
            active *= 1 - counts[list(causes)].sum() / risk_before
            at_risk -= counts.sum()  # Include tied censorings in the pre-event risk set.
        if time in horizons:
            supported = risk_before > 0 or active <= 1e-12
            results.append({
                "hour": time, "risk_before": risk_before,
                "active": active if supported else float("nan"),
                "rm_active_h": area if supported else float("nan"),
                **{cause: value if supported else float("nan")
                   for cause, value in cif.items()},
            })
        previous_time = time
    return pd.DataFrame(results).set_index("hour")


def deadline_bounds(rows, allow_external_success):
    times = rows["duration_or_age_h"]
    codes = rows["internal"]
    timely = (codes.eq(1) & times.le(deadline)).sum()
    unknown_open = (codes.eq(0) & times.lt(deadline)).sum()
    unknown_transfer = (codes.eq(4) & times.lt(deadline)).sum()
    # Third transfer scenario: vary external successes between zero
    # and all pre-deadline transfers. No external times are imputed.
    unknown = unknown_open + allow_external_success * unknown_transfer
    lower = (len(rows) - timely - unknown) / len(rows)
    upper = (len(rows) - timely) / len(rows)
    return lower, upper


summary = []
for cohort, group in cases.groupby("cohort"):
    for scenario in ["internal", "transfer_censored", "net_resolution"]:
        curve = aj_at(group, scenario, [deadline, recovery_horizon])
        at_deadline = curve.loc[deadline]
        summary.append({
            "cohort": cohort,
            "scenario": scenario,
            "n": len(group),
            "risk_at_72": int(at_deadline["risk_before"]),
            "one_minus_resolve_cif_72": 1 - at_deadline["resolve"],
            "active_72": at_deadline["active"],
            "resolve_cif_increment_72_to_96":
                curve.loc[recovery_horizon, "resolve"] - at_deadline["resolve"],
            "restricted_active_hours_72": at_deadline["rm_active_h"],
        })
    for label, allow_external in [("internal", False), ("end-to-end", True)]:
        low, high = deadline_bounds(group, allow_external)
        print(f"Cohort {cohort}, {label} SLA miss bounds: {low:.1%} to {high:.1%}")

print("\nAJ curves at reporting horizons, with scenarios using different event codes")
print(pd.DataFrame(summary).round(4).to_string(index=False))
print("Internal active time and active risk use the internal scenario.")

strata = []
for (cohort, priority), group in cases.groupby(["cohort", "priority"]):
    at_deadline = aj_at(group, "internal", [deadline]).loc[deadline]
    strata.append({
        "cohort": cohort,
        "priority": priority,
        "n": len(group),
        "risk_at_72": int(at_deadline["risk_before"]),
        "internal_miss_72": 1 - at_deadline["resolve"],
        "internally_active_72": at_deadline["active"],
    })
strata = pd.DataFrame(strata)
print("\nInternal AJ by cohort and priority")
print(strata.round(4).to_string(index=False))

weights = cases["priority"].value_counts(normalize=True)
strata["weighted_miss"] = strata["internal_miss_72"] * strata["priority"].map(weights)
standardized = strata.groupby("cohort")["weighted_miss"].sum(min_count=len(weights))
print("\nInternal 72 h miss standardized to pooled priority mix")
print(standardized.round(4).to_string())

log = EventLog()
for row in cases.itertuples(index=False):
    trace = Trace(attributes={"concept:name": row.case})
    for activity in row.prefix.split(">"):
        trace.append(Event({"concept:name": activity}))
    log.append(trace)

variant_traces = pm4py.get_variants(log)
variant_by_case = {}
for traces in variant_traces.values():
    for trace in traces:
        variant_by_case[trace.attributes["concept:name"]] = tuple(
            event["concept:name"] for event in trace
        )
cases["observed_variant"] = cases["case"].map(variant_by_case)
cases["review_visits_observed"] = cases["observed_variant"].map(
    lambda path: path.count("Review")
)
cases["autocheck_immediately_after_submit"] = cases["observed_variant"].map(
    lambda path: path[:2] == ("Submit", "AutoCheck")
)
print("\nMost frequent observed sequences, including incomplete prefixes")
print(cases["observed_variant"].value_counts().head().to_string())
print("\nObserved routing and repeated Review by cohort")
print(cases.assign(repeated_review=cases["review_visits_observed"].ge(2))
      .groupby("cohort")[["autocheck_immediately_after_submit", "repeated_review"]]
      .sum().to_string())
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

1. **Exact event and recording timestamps, submission dates, and cutoff.** Reconstruct feature availability, time in stage, queue intervals, and landmark eligibility. Verify complete intake capture, distinguish event occurrence from logging, and check whether administrative follow-up depends on calendar trends. Confirm the terminal-event, reopening, and deadline boundary rules used by operations.
2. **Linked post-transfer outcomes and handoff details.** Obtain external outcome type and time, comparable case identifiers, observation coverage, and transfer reason or destination. These identify external completions and help distinguish loss of visibility from a change in the pathway.
3. **Time-varying workload and baseline case mix.** Capture queues, staffing, case complexity, and timestamped priority changes to study stage hazards, distinguish inactivity from recording delays, and improve prediction and follow-up adjustment.
4. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance.** Collect policy versions and activation dates, eligibility rules, actual automation and escalation exposure, overrides, and assignment by team or time. Establish whether comparable cases had different policy exposure and account for concurrent changes before attempting causal evaluation.
5. **More cases and mature follow-up across priorities.** Obtain enough cause-specific exits to estimate stage and transition effects, quantify tail uncertainty, and validate alert calibration prospectively. The 32-case extract supports descriptive estimates and outcome bounds.
