# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive leadership, Fiber Operations
**From:** Process analytics
**Subject:** Redesign R: scale selectively, pause two components, measure before wider rollout

**Decision requested.** Approve Option E, reinforced with the controls from C and the experiments from D. Scale Redesign R now for simple, low-risk houses only. Pause post-billing activation everywhere, and pause survey skipping for apartments and old buildings. Keep coordinator ownership in the pilot regions with capacity and backup safeguards. Fund a 12-week measurement programme that decides the rest. Do not scale nationally (A) and do not roll back (B).

**Why not a simple yes or no.** R is four bundled changes: coordinator ownership, merged validation/scheduling, predictive survey skipping, and activation checks deferred until after billing. The pilot shows they do not stand or fall together.

- **What worked:** median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%, and visible cost fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161. For simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume) everything improved: median -6 days, p90 -8 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints -1pp.
- **What broke:** p95 cycle time rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, second visits rose from 11% to 18%, manual activation rose from 7% to 15%, and 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%. Estimated cost including rework rose from EUR 198 to EUR 207, and the gaps in that estimate probably understate the pilot figure.
- **Where it broke:** apartments (p90 +12 days), permit-sensitive old buildings (p90 +25 days, first-time-right -11pp) and business customers (billing disputes). Conformance checking shows the mechanisms: billing started before a successful activation test, installations repeated without a survey, and permit checks missing for older apartment buildings.

**Our reading.** The typical case got faster because front-end steps were removed. A minority got much slower because those steps were catching real problems. The savings are booked early; the costs appear later as second visits, manual activations and complaints after billing. Billing customers before service is verified is a customer-trust and potentially regulatory exposure, and stopping it needs no further data.

**What we do not know.** The pilot was not a controlled experiment. Pilot regions had more apartments and old buildings than the baseline, segment attributes are missing for the first two pilot weeks, complaint costs are missing after week 4, and the log holds completion timestamps only. Part of the aggregate deterioration may therefore be case mix rather than R. The segment results and alignment deviations suggest most of it is real and tied to two specific components. We cannot yet say whether coordinator ownership helps or hurts complex cases: it was never tested without the other changes, and it ran at 93% utilisation in two regions.

**The trade-off.** We give up some headline speed and visible savings on the two-thirds of pilot volume that is not simple houses. In exchange we remove the failure modes that drive the tail, rework and complaints. National scaling would lock in a faster median while exporting a slower, costlier and less compliant tail to every region, on coordinator and second-visit capacity that is already saturated. Rollback would discard a demonstrated gain on a third of volume and the accountability benefit of ownership.

**What happens next.**
1. **Now:** gate billing start on a logged successful activation test; reinstate mandatory permit checks and field surveys for apartments and old buildings; review pilot customers billed before activation.
2. **Scale:** merged validation/scheduling plus coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses, with weekly guardrail monitoring.
3. **Stabilise:** cap coordinator load near 85%, name backups, and redeploy idle survey capacity (utilisation fell from 78% to 52%).
4. **Measure:** validate the risk rule, capture 60-day downstream cost, add reason codes, then run a randomised skip test only where the rule looks safe.

**Rollout gate.** We will recommend broader rollout segment by segment. A segment qualifies when, against a concurrent control, first-time-right, complaints and p90 are no worse than baseline, full 60-day cost is lower, and billing-before-activation and missing-permit deviations are near zero. We expect a decision point in about 12 weeks.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

- **What the measures mean.** Fitness asks whether the model can replay what happened. Precision asks whether the model forbids what did not happen.
- **Why fitness is high.** The pilot model was discovered from the pilot log, so 0.94 measures self-consistency, not conformance to the intended design. The coordinator path, including the new Combined validate/schedule edge at 38%, gives a simple backbone that replays most traces.
- **Why precision is low.** Skipped survey, manual activation, second visit and billing start now occur in many orders and combinations. A model that replays them all must make them optional, concurrent or repeatable, so it permits far more behaviour than was observed. Precision falls to 0.51.
- **Why generalization fell.** Many of those paths are supported by few cases in six weeks, so the model is a weak predictor of the next case (0.56 against 0.70).
- **What it means.** The front end became standardised and the back end lost its ordering constraints. Low precision is a symptom of weaker control, and 0.94 fitness should not be quoted as proof that R works. The alignment results are the meaningful conformance evidence, and they show billing before activation, installation without survey, and missing permit checks.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

- **The distribution became a mixture.** Merging validation and scheduling and skipping surveys removes days from the majority of cases, which moves the median by 3.4 days.
- **The minority pays more than before.** When a skipped survey or permit check would have found a problem, it is now found at the installation visit, the most expensive place to find it. The case then enters failed visit, second visit and manual activation loops.
- **Those loops run through saturated resources.** The second-visit team is at 92% and coordinators at 93%. Waiting time grows non-linearly near full utilisation: a simple single-server approximation puts queueing at 93% at roughly three times that at 80%. The temporal profile confirms this, with longer Failed visit -> Second visit delays where coordinator utilisation is high.
- **Two measurement effects may flatter the median.**
  - If cycle time ends at Billing start, moving activation checks after billing shortens the measured cycle without the customer getting a working service sooner. The new Billing start -> Complaint escalation long-tail relation lies outside the measured cycle.
  - If cases still open at extraction were excluded, the pilot median is biased down and the tail understated.
- **What it means.** Median and p95 are both correct; they describe different customers. Scale decisions should be judged on the tail and on "time to verified working service".

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

- **Handover counts measure transfers, not robustness.** Fewer transfers mean fewer queue points and clearer accountability, which is a real gain for routine cases.
- **Handovers also carried hidden redundancy.** Each transfer forced context to be written down and seen by a second team. Under R that context sits with one coordinator.
- **The networks show the change.** The handover-of-work network is simpler, but the working-together network is centralised on coordinators and activation specialists. That is a hub-and-spoke structure with single points of failure, operating at 93% load with no slack to absorb absence.
- **Ownership has a gap at the back end.** Activation specialists report unclear ownership after billing start. Some handovers were replaced by informal, unlogged ones rather than removed.
- **Part of the drop is mechanical.** Merging two activities into one removes a recorded handover by definition.
- **What it means.** Ownership is worth keeping, but only with shared case notes, named backups and a load cap.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

- **The arithmetic.** Visible cost fell by EUR 23 per case (184 -> 161). The estimate including rework rose by EUR 9 (198 -> 207). The implied downstream component therefore rose from about EUR 14 to about EUR 46 per case.
- **The cost moved in time and across budgets.** Savings appear in survey and scheduling. Costs appear later in second visits (+7pp), manual activation (+8pp), complaint handling (+3.7pp) and billing disputes.
- **Scale of the shift.** If baseline rates had applied to the 9,200 pilot cases, there would have been roughly 640 fewer second visits and 340 fewer complaints. This is indicative only, given the mix difference.
- **The pilot figure is probably a lower bound.** Complaint-handling cost is missing after week 4, late-pilot complaints may not yet have arrived, and credits, churn and reputational effects are not costed.
- **The survey saving is partly notional.** The survey team is at 52% utilisation, and the saving is not cash unless that capacity is reduced or redeployed.
- **What it means.** EUR 161 is a local optimum for one cost centre. The decision metric must be full cost per case at 60 days (M4).

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

- **R's common mechanism is removing or deferring checks.** The value of a check depends on how often it finds something.
- **Simple low-risk houses.** Surveys and permit checks rarely change the outcome, so removing them is nearly pure gain: median -6 days, p90 -8 days, first-time-right +3pp.
- **Apartments and old buildings.** Riser access, in-building wiring, landlord consent and permits are common problems. Removing the check turns cheap early detection into expensive late detection: p90 +12 and +25 days, first-time-right -8pp and -11pp.
- **The skip rule targets the hardest stratum.** It was applied to medium-risk addresses, where prediction is least reliable.
- **Mix explains only part of the aggregate decline.** Weighting the segment changes by pilot share gives about -4.2pp first-time-right and +2.9pp complaints, against observed aggregate changes of -6pp and +3.7pp. This rough check suggests that roughly 70-80% of the deterioration is within-segment and that the remainder could be case mix or early-pilot data gaps.
- **What it means.** The redesign should be assigned by segment, and the aggregate should not be used to judge it.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Reasonable when | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures the median and handover gains everywhere. One operating model. Largest visible saving (EUR 23 per case). | Exports +17 days at p95, -6pp first-time-right and +3.7pp complaints. Institutionalises billing before activation and missing permit checks. Coordinators and second-visit teams are already at 92-93%. Full cost is probably higher. | The national mix is overwhelmingly simple houses, the billing and permit gates are fixed first, and full cost is shown to be lower. | **Reject now.** The tail, rework and compliance-like issues are unresolved, and scaling would amplify capacity saturation. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Restores known tail, rework and conformance levels. Removes compliance exposure quickly. Simple to communicate. | Discards a clear gain for 34% of pilot volume. Returns to 34% of cases with 3+ handovers and "waiting for appointment" complaints. Learns nothing about which component failed. | The billing and permit controls cannot be fixed quickly, or coordinator capacity cannot be stabilised and harm is ongoing. | **Reject as a whole.** Roll back two components, not the redesign. Keep it as the fallback if guardrails fail. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skipping and post-billing activation** | Removes the two components most directly tied to the alignment deviations. Keeps accountability gains. | Ownership has not been shown to be safe for complex segments: 93% utilisation, absence stalls, and the rural rescheduling problem. It may not address the missing permit check. It drops the merged step that drives the simple-house gain. | Ownership is shown to be resilient under absence and load (M5) and the permit gate is enforced. | **Partly adopt.** Take its two suspensions; do not scale ownership for complex segments on the current evidence. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Gives causal, component-level evidence. Fixes confounding by mix and region. | Used alone it delays a demonstrated benefit for simple houses and leaves billing-before-activation running while the experiment is prepared. | Harm is contained and there is no segment with a clear result. Neither holds here. | **Adopt as the learning component**, not as the whole decision. |
| **E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses, controls for apartments and old buildings, measurement before wider scale** | Banks the gain where evidence is consistent across every metric. Contains harm where it is concentrated. Keeps the option to expand. | Running two process variants depends on classifying cases correctly, and risk attributes are known to be incomplete. Simple-house results rest on about four weeks of data in four regions. Less headline saving in the short term. | Segment effects are strongly heterogeneous and the harmful mechanisms are identifiable. Both are true. | **Recommend**, with C's suspensions and D's experiments built in. |

No option dominates. A wins on median speed and visible cost. B wins on tail, quality and conformance. E gives up part of each to get most of both, and it is the only option that also generates the evidence needed to decide the remainder.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

**Ranking principle.** A measurement ranks high when its result could plausibly go either way, when it would change a decision affecting a large share of volume, and when it is cheap and fast.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty it resolves | How it could change the decision | Cost and speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M6. Validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes** | Whether the skip rule is wrong everywhere or only in some strata, and whether its false-skip rate is below break-even. | A poor result in all strata retires survey skipping permanently. A good result in specific strata lets skipping return there and defines where M1 is worth running. | Low cost and fast. It is retrospective, using surveyed cases from the baseline (about 31% of 24,000) and the pilot. Caveat: outcomes exist only for cases that were surveyed. |
| 2 | **M4. Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start** | Whether R saves or loses money in total, by segment. Today the difference (EUR 198 against 207) is small relative to the known gaps. | If full cost is lower even in apartments, the case for a controlled re-introduction strengthens. If the gap widens, as we expect, the business case outside simple houses is closed without further experiments. | Medium cost. Can be backfilled for pilot cases, then needs a 60-day wait. |
| 3 | **M7. Conformance monitoring for billing before activation and permit absence** | Whether the two critical deviations are real or logging artefacts, how frequent they are, whether the pause holds, and how strongly they link to complaints. | A strong link to complaints confirms that these two controls recover most of the damage. A weak link means the cause lies elsewhere, in ownership or capacity, and shifts weight towards option C or B. | Very low cost; uses the existing log. It is deployed as a control regardless; this rank reflects its information value. |
| 4 | **M3. Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit** | Attribution: which share of rework comes from skipped surveys, missing permits, coordinator overload, customer causes or network faults. | Unbundles R. It could show that ownership is harmless and rework is entirely skip- or permit-driven, which would support wider ownership rollout, or the opposite. | Low cost. Needs four to six weeks of data and coding discipline. |
| 5 | **M1. Randomised survey-skipping pilot by building-risk stratum** | The causal effect of skipping on first-time-right, second visits and tail, free of regional and mix confounding. | Provides the definitive evidence to re-enable skipping in a stratum or close the question. | Highest rigour but slow and costly, and it exposes customers to risk. Run only in strata that pass M6. About 1,000 cases per arm detects a 5pp first-time-right difference; about 2,800 are needed for 3pp. |
| 6 | **M5. Coordinator ownership against shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether lower resilience is inherent to single ownership or fixable with backups and load caps. | Decides whether ownership extends to complex segments and new regions, which is the largest remaining scale question after survey skipping. | Medium cost. Partly observable from existing absence records before a formal test. |
| 7 | **M2. Start timestamps for installation visit, activation test, manual activation** | Whether tail time is waiting (capacity) or working (complexity), especially Installation visit -> Activation test for apartments. | Directs the remedy towards staffing or towards process design. It refines the fix rather than the scale decision. | Medium cost; requires a field-tool change. Valuable for long-term monitoring. |
| 8 | **M8. Separate customer experience for speed to appointment and working service** | How customers trade speed against reliability. | Would sharpen the threshold weights. It is unlikely to flip the decision, because the complaint shift and NPS drop already indicate the direction. | Low cost. Worth doing but not decision-critical. |

**Sequencing.**
- **Weeks 0-2:** switch on M7, run M6, backfill M4, launch M3.
- **Weeks 3-12:** run M1 in strata that pass M6, run M5 in the pilot regions, start M2 and M8.
- **Week 12:** hold the decision review, once M4 has a full 60-day window.

**Not worth waiting for.** No measurement is needed before stopping billing ahead of activation or before scaling for simple houses. No plausible result would reverse either decision.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision.** Adopt Option E, with the suspensions from C and the experimental programme from D. Scale R selectively, roll back two components, and treat the remaining rollout as conditional on evidence.

## Immediate action (within two weeks)

- Make a logged successful activation test, automated or manual, a hard precondition for Billing start in all segments and regions.
- Review pilot cases billed before successful activation and correct or credit them proactively, starting with business customers.
- Reinstate the Permit check as a mandatory gate before installation scheduling for old and permit-sensitive buildings.
- Assign named ownership for post-billing exceptions, covering both coordinator and activation-specialist responsibilities.
- Turn on conformance monitoring (M7) with weekly reporting to operations leadership.

## What to pause

- **Post-billing activation checks:** everywhere, including simple houses. The speed benefit is partly a measurement artefact and the exposure is not segment-specific.
- **Predictive survey skipping:** for medium-risk apartments, old buildings and business customers, until M6 and M1 report. The survey team at 52% utilisation can absorb the restored surveys.
- **Further expansion of coordinator ownership to complex segments in new regions:** until M5 reports and utilisation is under control.

## What to scale

- **For simple, low-risk houses:** merged validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership, rolled out region by region with the guardrails below. This is the 34% of pilot volume where every metric improved.
- **In the existing pilot regions:** keep coordinator ownership for other segments, but with a utilisation ceiling of about 85%, a named backup per case, shared case notes, and extra second-visit capacity redeployed from the survey team.
- **Stop rule for the scaled segment:** halt the rollout if first-time-right falls below baseline, or complaints exceed baseline by more than 1pp, in two consecutive four-week windows.

## What to measure next

- **First wave:** M6, M4, M7 and M3.
- **Second wave:** M1 in strata that pass M6, and M5.
- **Supporting:** M2 and M8.
- **Reporting changes:**
  - Report "time to verified working service" alongside time to billing start.
  - Report p90 and p95 alongside the median.
  - Report every metric by segment.
  - Include open cases in duration statistics.

## Thresholds that would justify broader rollout

These are proposed values for management to ratify. They apply per segment and are measured against a concurrent control in the same regions, not the historical baseline.

| Gate | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Compliance | Billing before successful activation in under 0.5% of cases; permit check present in at least 99% of permit-sensitive cases; both sustained for four consecutive weeks. |
| Quality | First-time-right no more than 1pp below control; 30-day complaints no more than 0.5pp above; second-visit rate no more than 1pp above. |
| Tail | p90 no worse than control and p95 within +2 days, with median improved by at least one day. |
| Economics | Full 60-day cost per case lower than control with 90% confidence. |
| Skip rule | False-skip rate demonstrably below break-even, defined as survey cost divided by the cost of a failed visit plus second visit plus expected complaint handling. Confirmed by the randomised M1 result, not by the rule's own score. |
| Capacity and resilience | Coordinator and second-visit utilisation at or below 85% at planned volume; cases affected by coordinator absence delayed by no more than one day. |
| Process control | Fitness against the designed (normative) R model of at least 0.90, with the precision of the discovered model recovering towards the baseline level (about 0.70 or higher). |

**How the gates are applied.**
- If a segment passes all gates, extend R to it.
- If it fails on quality or tail but passes on compliance, keep that segment on the baseline path with coordinator ownership only.
- If compliance gates fail anywhere, revert that region to baseline until fixed.
