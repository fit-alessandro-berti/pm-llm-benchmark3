## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Framework: interventional policy contrasts and separable/interventional analogues of mediation effects (not natural direct/indirect effects). Natural effects require cross-world counterfactuals and no-interference assumptions that fail here: shared review/escalation queues create interference, the flag is a policy bundle (priority + routing), exposure-induced confounding and treatment-confounder feedback are plausible, and “set flag = 0” is not the same intervention as “suppress display while leaving risk_score and human information unchanged.” All estimands are therefore defined with respect to explicitly specified policies P0–P4 or stochastic interventions on mediators under fixed components of the policy.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|-------------|-----------------------|---------------------|---------|--------------------------------------------------|---------------|------------------------------|
| E1 | P0 (current flag system) vs P1 (suppress flag display, retain score visibility) | average over arriving cases under observed complexity/load mix | total cycle time | total interventional policy effect | yes (if information set under P1 fully specified) | no pre-flag historical data; residual confounding by risk_score distribution; weak positivity |
| E2 | P0 vs P1 | same population | arrival-to-triage_done | interventional effect on triage completion | yes | behavioral response of triage staff when flag is hidden; residual score visibility |
| E3 | P0 vs P2 (priority lane retained, automatic mandatory manual review removed) | all cases or would-be-flagged cases | total cycle time | total interventional; isolates pathway through manual-review occurrence | yes under explicit routing rule | voluntary review selection still depends on score/complexity; remaining interference |
| E4 | Under P0, stochastic intervention setting manual-review occurrence = 0 (or reduced probability) for flagged cases while holding priority fixed | cases that would be flagged under current rule | cycle time | interventional indirect effect via manual-review occurrence | partially | post-treatment confounders (triage findings) affect both mediator and later outcomes; positivity for forced non-review |
| E5 | Under P0, shift the manual-queue-wait distribution for flagged cases (via capacity change) while holding occurrence fixed | cases that enter manual review | post-triage time / cycle time | interventional analogue of mediation via waiting time | yes if capacity rule is explicit | cross-case interference and spillovers onto unflagged cases |
| E6 | P0 vs P4 (altered escalation criteria or added senior capacity) | cases reaching manual review | escalation occurrence + cycle time | interventional effect via escalation pathway | yes | scarce senior-resource dynamics; selection into escalation |
| E7 | P0 vs P1 or P2 | all cases | decision_outcome (Approve/Reject) as proxy for decision quality | total interventional effect on observed decision | weakly (observed decision ≠ true quality relative to U_underlying_risk) | U_underlying_risk unobserved; no gold-standard labels; selection into paths |
| E8 | P3 (protected manual-review capacity for flagged) vs P0 | all cases (flagged + spillover on unflagged) | cycle time of flagged and of unflagged | total interventional + interference-aware effect | yes | implementation details of “protected”; unobserved capacity cost |

Interference arises because review and escalation queues are shared: one flagged case’s mandatory review occupies limited M-resource capacity and lengthens manual_queue_wait for concurrent cases (including unflagged). This violates SUTVA/no-interference. Exposure-induced confounding occurs when the flag (or priority) affects post-triage variables (reviewer assignment, perceived urgency) that confound the mediator–outcome relation for later stages. Treatment-confounder feedback can appear if early waits or service times update perceived load or risk and influence subsequent routing. The flag is a policy bundle, not an atomic treatment; “set flag = 0” confounds display suppression with routing changes. Unobserved U_underlying_risk and U_reviewer_judgment create residual confounding for both cycle time and decision quality. Dynamic queue states evolve with the treatment of other units, inducing further dependence.

Occurrence rates: flag=1 enters manual review 100 % and escalation 60 %; flag=0 enters manual 40 % and escalation 20 %. Residual-to-close is stably ~1 h after the last service stage (likely administrative close) and is not a major driver.

Observed contrasts show clear acceleration at triage for flag=1 (median wait 0.2 h vs 2.5 h; service 0.6 h vs 0.95 h), consistent with the priority-lane policy. Downstream, every flagged case enters manual review with substantially longer queue waits (6.75 h vs 1.75 h among the 40 % of unflagged that enter), and 60 % escalate with additional ~7 h waits plus 2 h service. Total cycle roughly doubles (16.45 h vs 5.75 h). High complexity and high arrival load amplify the pattern; flagged cases that escape escalation have totals comparable to many unflagged cases. These are descriptive associations, not causal contrasts: flag is entangled with higher risk_score and complexity (confounding), mandatory routing induces selection into longer paths, shared queues create interference, and residual U_underlying_risk may drive both flagging and slower true processing needs. Absent stages cannot be treated as zero-length without bias; occurrence itself is a mediator. Thus the observed differences mix selection, policy routing, capacity effects, and case-mix differences.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---------|--------------------------|---------------------|----------------------|-----------------------|-------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| flag → priority_lane → triage_wait | Priority lane accelerates triage start for flagged | Flag1 arr-to-tri med 0.2 h vs Flag0 2.5 h; T1/T2 resources used for flagged | Priority is the operative difference; no strong unobserved urgency selection | Higher-risk cases arrive when triage is less loaded, or different staff | P1 (suppress flag, lose priority) | High-risk cases wait longer in triage; possible loss of early risk signals |
| flag → priority → triage_service | Priority may also shorten or alter service intensity | Flag1 triage service 0.6 h vs 0.95 h | Priority affects handling intensity or staffing | Selection: simpler or better-prepared cases reach priority lane | P2 (retain priority) | Service quality may change if staff feel rushed |
| flag → mandatory manual-review occurrence | Flag routes nearly all cases into manual review | 100 % of flag1 have manual_start vs 40 % of flag0 | Policy rule is binding; flag is the dominant routing cue | Complexity/risk independently drive both flag and human decision to review | P2 (remove automatic mandatory MR) | Some true high-risk cases may skip needed review; quality risk; queue relief |
| flag → longer manual queue wait | Flagged volume floods the shared MR queue | Flag1 tri-to-man 6.75 h vs 1.75 h (among those entering) | Shared finite capacity; no differential prioritization inside the queue | Higher complexity requires more preparation time before review starts | P3 (protected capacity for flagged) | Unflagged waits may lengthen or stay similar; capacity cost |
| flag / review → escalation occurrence + long esc wait | Reviewed flagged cases are escalated more often into scarce senior queue | 60 % flag1 escalate vs 20 % flag0; +~7 h wait | Escalation criteria use flag or findings correlated with flag | True higher underlying risk legitimately needs senior review | P4 (change criteria or capacity) | Under- or over-escalation relative to true risk; senior overload or missed cases |
| complexity / risk confounding | High complexity drives flag, longer service, escalation and cycle | High-complexity total med ~19.5 h; all Low-complexity are flag0 and short | Complexity is assigned independently of the flag mechanism | Complexity is a collider or part of the causal pathway | Stratify or adjust for complexity (and risk_score) | Over-adjustment can remove true mediated pathways |
| shared-queue interference | One case’s MR or escalation occupies capacity and delays others | Flagged high-load cases show longer waits (7 h vs 4.5 h); clustered morning arrivals | Capacity is limited and shared across flag status | Pure case-mix differences without capacity constraint | P3 (protected or added capacity) or P2 (lower volume) | System-wide spillover on unflagged cycle times |
| reviewer / resource assignment | Flagged cases are systematically assigned particular triage/review resources | Flag1 mostly T1/T2 and M2; flag0 mostly T3/T4 | Assignment is driven by the flag | Resource skill or experience differences drive outcomes independently | Randomize assignment or protect capacity | Learning, skill, or bias effects may change under new assignment rules |
| decision-quality tradeoff | Extra review/escalation improves accuracy relative to U_underlying_risk at the cost of time | All Rejects are High-complexity (often escalated); overall Approve/Reject rates similar across flag | Observed decision proxies true quality | Rejects are simply correlated with complexity, not improved by the longer process | Any policy that reduces review or escalation volume | Higher error rate on borderline cases; possible later rework or appeals |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|----------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------|-------------|---------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Within-risk-score / local-threshold design | Stratify or match within narrow risk_score bands (e.g. 0.55–0.75); local linear regression or RD if a sharp flag threshold exists | risk_score, complexity, arrival_load | Conditional exchangeability within band; continuity of potential outcomes; limited score manipulation | Density continuity of risk_score; covariate balance within band; placebo on pre-policy outcomes if available | Density jump or severe imbalance on complexity → flag not as-if random given score (positivity already weak: max flag0 = 0.61, min flag1 = 0.64) |
| Queue-state-aware analysis | Regress stage waits on concurrent # cases in MR/esc queue or arrival_load + time-of-day; or simulate counterfactual loads | arrival_load, time window, concurrent volume | Observed load sufficiently proxies interference; no simultaneous unmeasured demand shocks | Strong positive association of wait with concurrent flagged volume after covariates | Null association after rich load control → interference weaker than hypothesized |
| Policy-change or randomized encouragement | Cluster-randomize (day/shift) flag display or mandatory-MR rule; or individual encouragement to reviewers | baseline case-mix per cluster | Approximate SUTVA at cluster level; exclusion restriction; adequate compliance | Strong first-stage (routing change); balance across arms; limited cross-cluster spillover | Weak compliance or large quality drop in encouragement arm → pathway not cleanly isolated |
| Mediation-oriented (interventional) | Estimate effects under stochastic intervention on MR probability or on wait quantiles, holding priority fixed (g-computation / MSM style) | pre-treatment covariates only (score, complexity, load) | Sequential exchangeability for the chosen intervention distribution; positivity for mediator interventions | Vary the intervention distribution; compare occurrence-only vs wait-only interventions | Effect primarily through occurrence vs primarily through wait changes interpretation of the bottleneck |
| Negative-control outcome or exposure | Residual-to-close time (should be largely unaffected) as negative-control outcome; low-score cases far from threshold as negative-control exposure | same adjustment set as main analysis | Shares confounding structure but has no causal path from the flag policy | Significant apparent “effect” on residual or on far-from-threshold cases | Significant effect → residual confounding or timestamp artifact |
| Sensitivity analysis for unobserved risk | Rosenbaum-style bounds or parametric simulation of how strong U_underlying_risk must be to explain away the cycle-time (or wait) gap given observed score + complexity | observed risk_score + complexity | Functional form of residual confounding | Tipping-point analysis: Γ or partial-R² needed to nullify the observed difference | If only implausibly strong U tips the result → more credible; otherwise down-weight causal claims |
| Interference-aware design | Two-stage or shift-level randomization of the policy fraction; or calibrate a simple queueing model (empirical or M/M/c) to observed waits and re-simulate under P2/P3 | shift-level covariates + capacity parameters | Most interference is within shift/cluster; model captures capacity sharing | Individual-level vs cluster-level estimates diverge; simulated waits match held-out data | Divergence or poor calibration → interference is first-order; pure unit-level mediation invalid |

These designs identify interventional or policy effects under the stated assumptions; they do not recover natural direct or indirect effects.

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------|-------------------------|--------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| P0 (current) | Fast triage for flagged (priority lane) | High mandatory MR volume, long waits (~6.5 h), frequent escalation | Flagged ~2× longer (med 16.5 h vs 5.8 h) | Low (full current review) | Congestion on shared MR/senior resources for all cases | Descriptive strong; causal moderate | Status-quo baseline for any pilot |
| P1 (suppress flag display) | Lose priority → slower triage for high-score cases (~+2 h) | Less automatic MR volume → shorter queues | Mixed: triage penalty offset by downstream relief; net shorter for many former-flagged | Medium–high: humans may under-review high-risk without the flag cue | Positive (lower forced volume) | Weak (behavioral response unknown; no pre-period) | Not preferred first pilot; high quality risk |
| P2 (retain priority, remove automatic mandatory MR) | Keep fast triage | Reduced MR volume and waits; residual voluntary review by judgment | Substantial reduction for previously mandatory cases (remove 6 h+ wait + possible escalation); some still reviewed | Medium: depends on voluntary uptake of true high-risk cases | Positive spillover (less congestion for unflagged) | Medium–strong (occurrence is the dominant volume driver in the data) | Preferred for limited pilot |
| P3 (retain routing + protected MR capacity for flagged) | Keep fast triage | Flagged waits drop; unflagged may improve or stay neutral if capacity is net-new | Shorter flagged cycle; system benefit depends on net capacity | Low (reviews retained) | Depends on whether capacity is added or reallocated; higher cost | Moderate (queue theory + load contrasts) | Attractive if capacity can be added; tests interference directly |
| P4 (retain MR, change escalation criteria or capacity) | Unaffected | Less or more escalation volume; shorter/longer senior queues | Mainly reduces long-tail cycles for the escalated subset | Medium: criteria change may miss true high-risk or overload seniors | Direct pressure on senior team | Weaker on the main cycle driver (MR wait is larger) | Secondary; useful after MR pathway is better understood |

**Limited pilot of P2.**  
Eligibility: incoming cases with risk_score ∈ [0.55, 0.85] and complexity Medium or High (borderline region with residual positivity and learning value).  
Assignment: cluster-randomize by day or half-day to “priority retained + automatic mandatory MR off” versus P0; reviewers still see the risk_score and may elect voluntary review.  
Queue-capacity guardrail: if overall MR occupancy exceeds a pre-specified threshold, temporarily re-enable mandatory routing for new flagged cases until the queue drains; continuously monitor unflagged waits.  
Primary outcomes: total cycle time (eligible cases and system-wide), MR occurrence rate, MR waiting time.  
Secondary outcomes: escalation rate, decision_outcome distribution, voluntary-review rate and recorded reasons, any available quality proxy (later appeals/rework).  
Minimum process data: all current timestamps, risk_score, complexity, arrival_load, resources, plus concurrent queue-length snapshots at stage entry.  
Stopping conditions: after approximately 150–200 eligible cases or 3–4 weeks; or if system-wide cycle time worsens >10 %, if a quality signal (e.g., reject rate or later rework) degrades beyond a pre-set bound, or if voluntary review of high-score cases falls below a safety floor. The design isolates the mandatory-MR pathway at modest cost while preserving priority for higher-risk cases and generating direct evidence on interference and quality trade-offs.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk**: Within narrow risk_score strata (or after rich adjustment for score + complexity) the cycle-time and manual-wait gaps essentially disappear → the observed differences are largely confounding by unobserved or imperfectly measured underlying risk rather than the flag policy itself.  
2. **Queue interference**: After conditioning on precise concurrent queue length / arrival_load, the association between flag and manual (or escalation) waiting time vanishes → interference is weak and the long waits are driven by other factors (e.g., case complexity or resource assignment).  
3. **Decision quality**: Under a pilot that reduces mandatory review (P2) the rate of later adverse outcomes, appeals, or a validated quality proxy rises sharply relative to the control arm → the time savings are dominated by a clinically or operationally unacceptable quality loss.  
4. **Timestamp semantics**: Investigation shows that many “start” or “done” timestamps are batch-recorded, system-generated, or systematically delayed rather than reflecting real activity times → waiting-time estimates (especially the long manual queues) are artifacts and the acceleration-at-triage / delay-downstream pattern is unreliable.  
5. **Positivity / lack of overlap**: Even after expanding the risk_score window or using matching, there remains essentially no overlap between flagged and unflagged cases on the joint distribution of score, complexity and load → local designs and most observational contrasts lack support and cannot identify policy effects.  
6. **Behavior change by reviewers**: When the flag is suppressed or mandatory review is removed, reviewers systematically alter their voluntary-review or escalation thresholds (e.g., become more risk-averse or more lenient) in ways not captured by the intended policy contrast → the estimated effect of “removing mandatory review” no longer corresponds to a pure routing change and external validity collapses.

## 9. Final synthesis

What is directly observed is a clear descriptive pattern: cases carrying the high-risk flag reach triage far faster (median wait 0.2 h versus 2.5 h) yet finish roughly twice as late (median cycle 16.5 h versus 5.8 h). Every flagged case is routed into manual review and experiences long subsequent waits; 60 % also escalate into a scarce senior queue. Unflagged cases enter these stages far less often. Residual close time is short and stable. Risk scores almost completely separate the two groups, and high complexity co-occurs with both flagging and long paths.

A plausible mechanism is that the flag functions as a policy bundle: it grants priority triage (direct acceleration) while simultaneously triggering mandatory manual review and elevating the probability of escalation. The resulting volume loads shared queues, producing long waits that more than offset the triage gain. Complexity and residual underlying risk confound the contrast, and interference implies that one case’s treatment alters others’ waiting times.

What remains unidentified is the relative size of the pure priority effect, the pure mandatory-review effect, the pure capacity effect, and residual confounding by unobserved risk. Natural direct and indirect effects are not identified under interference and exposure-induced confounding. Decision quality relative to true risk is essentially unobserved.

The intervention that produces the most useful next evidence is a limited, interference-aware pilot of P2 (retain priority triage, remove automatic mandatory manual review). It isolates the dominant volume-and-wait pathway at modest cost, preserves rapid triage for higher-risk cases, generates direct data on voluntary-review behavior and quality trade-offs, and can be guarded by queue-occupancy and quality stopping rules.
