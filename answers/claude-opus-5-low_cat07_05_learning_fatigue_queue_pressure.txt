# 2. Integrated explanation

No single mechanism fits the data, and the most tempting story — "workers get tired, so they make mistakes" — is contradicted by the direction of the service-time trend.

**The shift has at least three regimes, not one gradient.**

*Regime 1 — the opening (positions 1–15).* Events are slow (18.8 min) and moderately error-prone (3.4%). Both are largely explained by what is being worked on, not by who is working. Mean complexity is 0.72, high-priority share is 41%, and the queue is already 46 because the previous shift handed over a backlog. Early and Night shifts start with 48% and 56% high-complexity cases in the first hour, and seniors deliberately self-select the hard carryover. The subsequent improvement to 13.6 min at position 16–30 is therefore *at most partly* warm-up: complexity falls 0.72 → 0.54 over exactly the same interval. The one piece of evidence that something beyond mix is happening is that error and rework fall *together* with service time — a pure mix explanation would predict errors fall too (easier cases), so this is not decisive either. Honest conclusion: warm-up is plausible but its magnitude is unidentified until complexity is controlled, and the raw "learning curve" in the table is an upper bound.

*Regime 2 — the productive middle (positions 16–50).* Complexity, queue, and priority are all near their minima; service time and error rate are both near their best. This is the only stretch where the system is not being distorted by either backlog or surge.

*Regime 3 — the loaded tail (positions 51–75, 76+).* Here service time keeps falling (11.4 → 10.8 min) while error rate nearly triples (2.5% → 7.9%) and 7-day rework nearly triples (4.8% → 13.7%). **Speed and quality decouple.** Fatigue alone cannot produce this: fatigued people are slower. The mechanism that does produce exactly this signature is queue pressure, and the second table confirms it in a design that is independent of shift position — holding complexity band fixed, moving from low to high WIP cuts service time by ~26% and roughly triples error rate. Under load, workers (and the system routing them) shed effort per case: shortened document review, skipped verification, batch-processing, deferred quality checks. That is a rational local response to an unmanageable queue and a management-created condition, not a personal failing.

**Why the errors rise on the *easiest* cases.** At position 76+ mean complexity is 0.43, the lowest in the shift. Cases are getting easier while errors get worse. This rules out case-mix as the driver of the tail and is the strongest evidence that a real behavioural mechanism — pressure, fatigue, or both — operates there.

**Why fatigue is not thereby eliminated, only demoted.** Position and elapsed time and WIP are near-collinear in these aggregates: position 76+ means 459 minutes elapsed *and* WIP 88. Nothing in the tables separates them. Fatigue almost certainly contributes something — the last two hours are the worst hours in all four shifts, including Early, whose queue is *falling* (61 → 42) — and that is a useful hint, because Early's late-shift error rate of 4.2% arises without a rising queue. But fatigue's most likely role is as a **moderator**: tired workers have less reserve capacity, so the same queue spike costs them more quality. The experience table hints at the same interaction structure from a different angle: under low WIP, later-shift error rates are essentially flat for everyone (Seniors 2.2%, Mid 2.9%, Juniors 4.4%) — i.e. *time in shift alone does almost nothing*. Under high WIP the same groups go to 4.9%, 6.8%, and 12.4%. Late-shift degradation is overwhelmingly a load phenomenon, and its severity is graded by experience.

**Circadian effects are asserted but not demonstrated.** Night's slow service times and exception-heavy mix are fully confounded with two-person staffing and with a 56% high-complexity first hour. The four staggered shifts do provide overlapping clock hours that could identify a genuine time-of-day effect, but that comparison has not been made.

**Measurement is not neutral.** Part of the late-shift "speed-up" is not behaviour at all. Batched Document Review compresses recorded service times for 9–18 consecutive events; Bot_Risk contributes instantaneous events; and Quality Check deferred to the last 45 minutes both creates artificial end-of-shift bursts and *relocates in time* the detection of errors that were actually committed hours earlier. Error rate measured at position 76+ therefore partly reflects when checking happens, not when mistakes happen. Any analysis that does not timestamp errors to the *originating* event will over-attribute error to the end of the shift.

**Synthesis.** Service time falls across the shift for three additive reasons — declining case complexity (mix), possible genuine warm-up, and effort-shedding under load — of which only the third also degrades quality. Errors rise in a specific *conjunction*: high WIP × lower experience × late shift × deferred verification. That conjunction, not shift length per se, is the intervention target.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Design principle.** Every comparison should be *within resource*, *within activity family*, and *stratified by complexity and WIP*. Aggregates across resources and activities are uninterpretable here because assignment is non-random by construction.

### 3.0 Preparation

```python
import pm4py, pandas as pd, numpy as np
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

df = pd.read_csv("triage_log.csv", parse_dates=["time:timestamp", "start_timestamp", "shift_start_time"])
log = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id="case:concept:name",
                             activity_key="concept:name",
                             timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
                             start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")

# service time per event, in minutes (do NOT trust a single aggregate mean)
log["svc_min"] = (log["time:timestamp"] - log["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Bot events must never enter human fatigue/learning statistics
log["is_bot"] = log["org:resource"].eq("Bot_Risk")
hum = log[~log["is_bot"]].copy()

hum["log_svc"] = np.log(hum["svc_min"].clip(lower=0.5))
hum["cx_band"] = pd.qcut(hum["case_complexity_score"], 4, labels=["c1","c2","c3","c4"])
hum["wip_band"] = pd.cut(hum["wip_at_start"], [-1, 30, 70, np.inf], labels=["low","med","high"])
hum["hour"] = hum["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
hum["shift_type"] = hum["shift_id"].str.extract(r"(Early|Day|Late|Night)")
hum["exp_group"] = pd.cut(hum["resource_experience_months"], [-1, 12, 48, np.inf],
                          labels=["junior","mid","senior"])
```

### 3.1 Within-resource comparisons

Resource fixed effects absorb permanent skill, permanent case-assignment tendencies, and permanent recording habits. Anything left is within-person variation over the shift.

```python
m_svc = smf.ols(
    "log_svc ~ sequence_position_within_shift + minutes_since_shift_start"
    " + case_complexity_score + C(priority) + C(activity_family)"
    " + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start"
    " + C(shift_type) + C(hour) + C(org:resource)".replace("org:resource", "res"),
    data=hum.rename(columns={"org:resource": "res"})
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": hum["shift_id"]})
```

Build the model **incrementally** and report the position coefficient at each step: (a) position only; (b) + complexity/priority/activity; (c) + WIP/queue; (d) + resource FE; (e) + hour FE. The *decay* of the position coefficient across these steps is the actual finding. If it dies at step (b), "learning" was case-mix; if it survives (b) but dies at (c), it was load; if it survives everything, warm-up/fatigue is real.

Do the same with a binomial model for `error_flag` and for `rework_flag`.

### 3.2 Within-shift position effects (non-parametric, not linear)

```python
prof = (hum.groupby(["exp_group", pd.cut(hum["sequence_position_within_shift"],
                                          [0,5,15,30,50,75,np.inf])])
          .agg(svc=("svc_min","median"), err=("error_flag","mean"),
               cx=("case_complexity_score","mean"), wip=("wip_at_start","mean"),
               n=("svc_min","size")))
```

Always print `cx` and `wip` next to `svc` and `err`. A position profile shown without its complexity and queue profile is the core error this dataset is designed to provoke. Repeat the profile *within the low-WIP stratum only* — that is the closest thing available to a fatigue-only curve.

Visual check with the dotted chart and the events distribution:

pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(hum, "dotted_by_resource.png",

pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(hum, "dist_dayweek.png", distr_type="days_week")

### 3.3 WIP / queue stratification and the endogeneity problem

```python
cell = (hum.groupby(["wip_band","cx_band","exp_group"])
          .agg(svc=("svc_min","median"), err=("error_flag","mean"),
               rw=("rework_flag","mean"), n=("svc_min","size")))
```

WIP is endogenous: a worker who is slow inflates their own queue. Two mitigations:

1. Use **arrival-side** load rather than own-backlog load.
```python
arr = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(log)   # case arrival / finish rate
srv = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)  # per-case service & waiting time
```
Aggregate arrival rate into 30-minute windows and use *that* as the pressure regressor for events in the following window — arrivals are outside the individual worker's control.
2. Use **lagged / leave-one-out** WIP: system WIP excluding the focal worker's own open cases.

### 3.4 Complexity adjustment beyond regression

Coarsened exact matching gives a transparent, assumption-light contrast:

```python
key = ["activity_family","priority","cx_band","exp_group"]
early = hum[hum.sequence_position_within_shift <= 15]
late  = hum[hum.sequence_position_within_shift >= 51]
matched = early.merge(late, on=key, suffixes=("_e","_l"))
matched.eval("d_svc = svc_min_l - svc_min_e").groupby(key)["d_svc"].mean()
```

Also treat `case_complexity_score` as measured with error: re-run key models with complexity replaced by (i) its quartile dummies, (ii) an empirical proxy — the median service time of that activity/priority combination computed on *low-WIP, mid-shift* events only. If conclusions move, residual confounding is material and should be stated as a limitation rather than hidden.

### 3.5 Shift and time-of-day controls

The staggered shifts are the identification asset. Build the crosstab of clock hour × shift position and analyse only cells with support in more than one shift type:

```python
support = pd.crosstab(hum["hour"], pd.cut(hum["sequence_position_within_shift"],[0,15,50,np.inf]))
```

Then fit hour dummies and position together, restricted to resources observed in ≥2 shift types (within-person circadian identification). Use `pm4py.filter_time_range` for shift-window slices and `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` for per-shift subsets:

```python
night = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "shift_id", night_ids, level="event", retain=True)
window = pm4py.filter_time_range(log, "2024-03-01 22:00:00", "2024-03-02 06:00:00", mode="events")
```

### 3.6 Batching detection

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)  # (activity, resource) -> batch types & sizes
```

Flag every event that belongs to a discovered batch, then:
- re-estimate §3.1 excluding batched events (removes the measurement artefact from the speed curve);
- compare `rework_flag` and downstream Quality Check failure for batched vs unbatched Document Review by the *same* resource (Junior_H, Junior_I are the named suspects — but check everyone, since naming two juniors is itself a selection risk);
- test whether batch incidence is predicted by `wip_at_start` — batching as a pressure *symptom* rather than a habit.

Also detect the Quality Check deferral explicitly: distribution of (Quality Check timestamp − preceding activity timestamp) against minutes remaining in shift, and the last-45-minutes burst.

```python
pm4py.save_vis_performance_dfg(*pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log), "perf_dfg.png")
slow = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(log, ("Document Review","Quality Check"),
                                      min_perf=3600*4, max_perf=3600*24)
```

### 3.7 Resource-experience interactions

The key coefficient is the **interaction**, not the main effect:

```python
m_int = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ wip_at_start * C(exp_group) + minutes_since_shift_start * C(exp_group)"
    " + case_complexity_score + C(activity_family) + C(priority) + C(hour) + C(res)",
    data=hum.rename(columns={"org:resource":"res"})
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": hum["shift_id"]})
```

Expectation from the tables: `wip × junior` is large and positive, `minutes × exp_group` much smaller. That distinction decides whether the fix is *load management* or *shift length*. Report marginal effects with confidence intervals, and report per-resource event counts — Night_L and Night_M alone carry the entire "night staff" row, so those estimates are two-person estimates and must be labelled as such.

Optionally use `pm4py.cluster_log` to check whether resources group into behavioural profiles that do *not* line up with the administrative Senior/Mid/Junior labels.

### 3.8 Rework and downstream error analysis

Errors detected late may originate early. Attribute each rework to its **source** event:

```python
rw = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
srv_t = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")

# link Rework Correction back to the last preceding event of the same case/family
log = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name","time:timestamp"])
log["prev_res"] = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(1)
log["prev_pos"] = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["sequence_position_within_shift"].shift(1)
log["prev_wip"] = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["wip_at_start"].shift(1)
origin = log[log["concept:name"].eq("Rework Correction")]
```

Then model rework probability as a function of the *originating* event's WIP, position, complexity, and resource. This is the only way to avoid crediting end-of-shift Quality Check bursts to end-of-shift work. Also compute a **detection-adjusted** error rate: error rates are only comparable across periods if verification intensity is comparable, so condition on whether a Quality Check actually occurred and estimate P(error | checked).

Finally, temporal features for any ML-style screening:

```python
feat, cols = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)
```

| # | Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk | Event-log monitoring |

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Selection bias makes raw worker comparisons close to meaningless.** Assignment is explicitly non-random: seniors take complex carryover early, night staff get urgent exceptions, and complexity falls monotonically over the shift. A junior's 12.4% high-WIP error rate reflects *what they were given and under what conditions*, not only how well they work. Any per-worker number must be risk-adjusted for complexity, priority, activity family, WIP, and shift, and must be published with a confidence interval — Night_L and Night_M each carry a whole demographic row here, and two-person estimates are extremely noisy.

**Measurement error runs in the direction that harms workers.** Recorded service time is compressed by batching and by any bulk status update, so "fast" can mean "recorded in bulk" rather than "rushed" or "skilled". `error_flag` depends on someone having *looked*: Quality Check is deferred to the last 45 minutes, so errors are detected in bursts unrelated to when they were committed, and periods with more checking will look worse than periods with less. Comparing error rates across periods with different verification intensity is invalid without conditioning on whether a check occurred. `case_complexity_score` is itself a model output with its own error, so complexity adjustment is partial and residual confounding should be stated, not assumed away.

**Much of the measured "pressure" is created by management.** Queue length, WIP, staffing levels, shift boundaries, and backlog handover policy are organisational choices. The high-WIP error zone is a designed operating point. Attributing its consequences to individual carelessness moves accountability from the people who set staffing to the people absorbing the consequences.

**Automation cannot be treated as a fixed quality baseline.** Bot_Risk's classifications are corrected more often at high queue length, and the causes are not separable from this data: the bot may be worse on surge case-mix, or humans under pressure may be checking differently. Human resources should not be scored on their correction rate for bot output until bot quality is independently monitored on a held-out labelled sample. Bot events also contribute zero-duration records that will silently bias any aggregate service-time or fatigue statistic downward unless excluded.

**Punitive use of error rates is self-defeating as well as unfair.** It creates strong incentives to under-report errors, to skip or defer Quality Check, to avoid complex and high-priority cases, and to game recorded timestamps — all of which degrade both real quality and the integrity of the event log that this monitoring depends on. The correct uses of these analyses are workload design, staffing, routing, alerting, and targeted coaching or training.

**Process mining on people is surveillance.** Event-level analysis of individual pace and idle time carries real privacy weight and may be subject to works-council or data-protection constraints. Prefer aggregated and role-level reporting; restrict individual views to explicitly agreed purposes; involve worker representatives in defining the metrics; and be transparent about what is measured and how it will and will not be used.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Design rules that apply to every indicator: (a) always show the confounder alongside the metric — never service time without complexity and WIP; (b) segment by activity family and shift, never a single global number; (c) exclude Bot_Risk from human performance metrics and monitor it separately; (d) use medians and interquartile ranges for durations, which are right-skewed; (e) show uncertainty and denominators; (f) default to team/role level, with individual detail access-controlled.

| # | Indicator | Definition | Source | Alert / read |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Risk-adjusted service time** | Median `svc_min` per activity family, plus the residual from a model controlling complexity, priority, WIP, resource | `get_service_time`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, regression residuals | Falling raw time with flat adjusted time = case-mix, not improvement. Falling *adjusted* time with rising error = effort shedding |
| 2 | **Waiting time and queue ageing** | Median and p90 waiting time before each activity; age of oldest waiting case; share of high-priority cases waiting > SLA | `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `filter_case_performance` | p90 and oldest-case age matter more than the mean; catches quiet starvation of low-priority work |
| 3 | **WIP / queue pressure profile** | `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` distribution over the day; minutes per shift above 70; a graded pressure index rather than a single cliff | Raw attributes, 30-min buckets | The primary operational lever. Alert at threshold; trend the *time spent* above it, not just peaks |
| 4 | **Error rate, detection-adjusted** | `error_flag` rate attributed to the **originating** event, split by WIP band × complexity band × experience group; alongside P(error \| Quality Check performed) and Quality Check coverage % | `error_flag`, reverse-linked from Rework Correction | Never display raw error rate alone.
