## 1. What is observed

## 3. Identification strategy

Primary strategy:

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Branch-specific synthetic control / comparative interrupted time series | Event time relative to availability month; separate Alpha/Beta April cohort and Gamma July cohort; months-since-treatment and ramp/dose modeled explicitly | Weighted combination of never-treated Delta/Epsilon; pre-adoption Gamma may inform Alpha/Beta pre-fit but not post-adoption counterfactual after July | Pre-outcome lags, arrivals, FTE, `high_complexity_pct`, escalation/rework baselines, month indicators, known shock indicators for fraud desk/product change/routing where measurable | Branch-specific dynamic ATT of availability on median, p90, rework, escalation, CSAT | Synthetic counterfactual reproduces the branch’s no-automation path; no unobserved branch-time shocks; no unmodeled interference; stable treatment version and measurement | Pre-period fit/RMSPE, placebo on controls, leave-one-donor-out, covariate balance, residual trend checks, scenario bounds excluding May shock | Unobserved local practice changes or fraud-desk spillovers coincident with adoption; too few donors/common shocks causing overfit or biased counterfactual |

The primary strategy is a branch-specific synthetic-control/comparative ITS rather than a pooled TWFE estimate. The rollout was explicitly targeted, the treated branches started from different baselines, and the post period contains several simultaneous shocks: case-mix deterioration, routing changes, staffing moves, and a central fraud desk. A pooled DiD would force a common counterfactual and common dynamic effect on Alpha, Beta, and Gamma, despite their different ramp speeds and local staffing changes. Synthetic control makes the counterfactual explicit for each branch, uses the pre-adoption path to fit the comparison, and can show whether the treated branch diverges only after availability. It also accommodates the ramp by modelling months since availability and, where useful, the realized fast-path share as a separate dose. The main cost is that only two never-treated donors are available and all branches experienced common shocks, so placebo tests and scenario bounds are essential. I would still report a staggered DiD event study as a robustness check, but the primary analysis should avoid a single naïve two-way fixed-effects coefficient because heterogeneous timing and dynamic learning can bias such estimates. The treatment-intensity model is useful for process interpretation, not primary causal identification, because the fast-path share is chosen by the organization and is entangled with learning, staffing, and case mix.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

`expand with an embedded evaluation`

Do not treat Delta and Epsilon as a homogeneous immediate rollout. Epsilon should be first if operational need dominates because it has the highest late-pre complexity, elevated p90, and no automation. Delta should be delayed by 6–8 weeks to serve as a contemporaneous comparison, unless leadership requires both immediately. If both must start, randomize the start date or phase-in by week within branches using a stepped-wedge design, and keep a documented holdout period. This preserves operational learning while creating a credible counterfactual.

Minimum measurement: at least six pre-rollout months and four post-rollout months, with weekly branch-level updates. Collect case-level event logs: registration timestamp, triage engine recommendation, confidence score, manual override, route to specialist/fraud desk, queue entry/exit timestamps, rework loops, resolution/close timestamps, case risk class, arrival channel, and FTE capacity. Do not rely on branch-month medians alone; compute case-level median/p90, queue waiting times, specialist queue length, fraud-desk waiting, and rework by path.

Spillover monitoring: instrument the centralized fraud desk and specialist review queues. Track cross-branch routing, borderline-case referral rates, and waiting times by triage decision. If automation increases fraud-desk referrals, attribute delays to the shared queue rather than branch automation.

Guardrails: predefine pause rules, e.g., p90 cycle time or specialist queue wait >10% above synthetic-control forecast for two consecutive weeks, rework +1 percentage point, complaint-rate increase, or CSAT drop >0.1 after adjusting for response composition. Require manual review for high-complexity cases and allow instant rollback.

Decision rule: expand to the next branch only if, after three months, actual `automated_fast_path_pct` exceeds 30%, median cycle time is not worse than the counterfactual, p90 is not materially worse, rework is flat or lower, escalation-related waiting is understood, and CSAT is stable. If benefits appear only in median while p90 worsens, redesign escalation routing before wider rollout.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automation availability coincided with a large rise in automated-fast-path share, a consistent modest increase in escalation, mixed raw median changes in Alpha/Beta, a clear raw improvement in Gamma, and elevated p90 in early Alpha/Beta post months.
- Suggestive: Compared with untreated branch trends, automation availability may have reduced median cycle time by roughly 4–5 hours and rework by about 2 percentage points, while keeping CSAT stable or slightly higher.
- Conditionally causal: If synthetic-control or staggered-DiD assumptions hold—especially no unmeasured time-varying confounding and no fraud-desk spillovers—availability likely reduced median cycle time by about 5–10% relative to the no-automation counterfactual and increased escalation by about 0.5–1.5 percentage points, but the p90 effect remains unidentified.
- Not supported: A single leadership-facing statement such as “automation reduces cycle time by X%” for all branches, or immediate expansion without embedded evaluation, is not defensible from the supplied data.
