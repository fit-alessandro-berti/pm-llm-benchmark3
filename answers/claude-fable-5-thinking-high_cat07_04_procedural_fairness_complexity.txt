# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this case-management process must be assessed on several distinct dimensions. They can diverge: a process can achieve near-parity on one dimension while imposing clearly unequal burdens on another.

**Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates range from 78% (Group A) to 58% (Group D), with Groups B and C in between. Raw parity is the weakest fairness notion here: the groups differ substantially in case mix (risk band, documentation completeness, channel, accommodation needs), so raw gaps are expected even under a perfectly fair procedure. Raw disparity is a *trigger for investigation*, not evidence of discrimination.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, residual gaps remain: −5.5 pp approval for Group B, −2.2 pp for Group C, −3.1 pp for Group D. These residuals are more informative than raw gaps, but they are only as trustworthy as the covariates. If risk_score_band or documentation_completeness are themselves contaminated by group membership (e.g., language barriers causing "incomplete" documentation), the adjusted estimates can either under- or over-state unfairness. Adjusted disparity is therefore evidence of *unexplained* difference, not yet proof of biased treatment.

**Procedural burden.** How many steps, document requests, reviews, and handovers a case must survive before decision. Group B faces +9.5 pp probability of Request Missing Documents and +8.0 pp probability of Supervisor Review *after adjustment*; Group C faces +4.2 pp and +5.5 pp respectively. Extra procedural steps are burdensome even when the final outcome is identical: each step is an opportunity for delay, error, abandonment, and demand on the applicant's time. This is a core procedural-fairness dimension that outcome parity completely misses.

**Timeliness.** Median cycle times of 8.1 / 13.7 / 15.9 / 12.1 days and 90th-percentile times of 21.4 / 34.2 / 38.8 / 29.5 days. Adjusted differences remain (+2.6 days for B, +4.1 days for C, +0.9 days for D). For benefits applicants, delay is itself a harm (delayed income support, housing, care). The tail (90th percentile) matters as much as the median: Groups B and C have long tails driven by identifiable queues (bilingual staff, accommodation specialists).

**Consistency of routing.** Whether cases with the same observable characteristics take the same paths. Legitimate policy routing exists (high risk → Fraud Review; accommodation request → Accommodation Review; minority language → Language Support Contact). The fairness question is whether routing beyond these rules is consistent: the adjusted +8.0 pp Supervisor Review probability for Group B is not explained by any stated routing rule and suggests discretionary escalation that correlates with group.

**Quality/accessibility of support.** The rights-preserving steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review) exist to make the process accessible, but they are staffed by 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists respectively, with waits of 3.8 and 4.7 days at those queues and no service-level targets. Support that is nominally provided but chronically slow converts a right into a penalty. The log cannot measure quality of the support interaction itself (comprehension, dignity), only its presence and delay.

**Appeal/rework burden.** Appeal rates: 6% (A), 13% (B), 10% (C), 16% (D); reopen/rework rates: 11% / 22% / 24% / 20%. High appeal and rework rates for B, C, and D indicate either lower first-pass decision quality for these groups, poorer explanations that force applicants to appeal to understand decisions, or over-strict initial decisions later reversed. Rework is a burden the applicant pays for organizational error. Adjusted appeal probability remains elevated for B (+4.4 pp) and D (+5.7 pp).

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers; Group B is concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers plus 3 overloaded regional officers; Group C on 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom double as supervisors (a role-conflict and capacity issue). Concentration creates queueing delay, single-points-of-failure, and potentially idiosyncratic decision styles applied disproportionately to one group. Group D's concentration in the fraud queue is rule-driven but inherits any bias embedded in risk_score_band.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to partition observed disparity into: (a) legitimate, policy-mandated routing; (b) case-mix composition effects; (c) residual, unexplained disparity that may indicate discrimination or structural unfairness; and (d) uncertainty that the data cannot resolve.

**Matching or stratification variables.** Stratify or match on the *legitimate* complexity drivers: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and submission month (seasonality/backlog). Use exact stratification on risk_score_band and documentation_completeness (they drive mandatory routing) and coarsened matching or propensity weighting on the rest. Critically, run the analysis both *with and without* risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as controls, because both are plausibly contaminated mediators (see sensitivity analysis below). Compare like with like: e.g., low-risk, complete-documentation, online applicants of Group A vs. Group B should show near-identical paths and times if the process is fair.

**Path/variant comparison.** Within each stratum, compare variant distributions across groups (chi-square or standardized differences per variant). Key contrasts:
- Among cases with *complete* documentation, does Group B still enter Request Missing Documents (V2) more often? If yes, document requests are not driven by documentation state alone.
- Among cases in the *same* risk band, does Supervisor Review probability differ by group? Policy only mandates Fraud Review for high risk; Supervisor Review differences within risk band indicate discretionary escalation.
- Compare V1 (straight-through) attainment rates within strata: the straight-through path is the "privileged" route, and unequal access to it within identical case mix is a strong procedural signal.

**Resource-assignment comparison.** For each activity, compute the distribution of org:resource by group within strata. Measure: (i) concentration (effective number of resources handling each group's cases, e.g., inverse Herfindahl); (ii) workload of assigned resources (cases per resource per day); (iii) outcome variation by resource (do the 7 bilingual caseworkers or 3 overloaded officers have systematically different approval/escalation rates than the general pool for comparable cases?). Use handover-of-work and working-together networks per group to quantify handover counts and identify structural ping-pong patterns (general → specialist → general).

**Waiting-time decomposition.** Decompose cycle-time gaps into per-edge waiting and service components using inserted service/waiting times. For each group, attribute the total cycle-time gap to specific edges: e.g., how much of Group B's +5.6-day raw median gap is explained by the 3.8-day Language Support → Eligibility wait times the fraction of B cases traversing it, versus the +1.2-day Completeness→Request-Docs wait, versus supervisor backlog in two regions? This converts an abstract disparity into an actionable queue-level diagnosis and separates *queue-capacity* unfairness from *decision-behavior* unfairness.

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.** Treat risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as potentially biased mediators, not neutral confounders:
- Re-estimate adjusted disparities excluding each in turn; report the range of estimates rather than a single number.
- Test whether risk_score_band is differentially calibrated: within each band, compare downstream fraud-confirmation rates (or appeal-reversal rates as a proxy) by group. If Group D high-band cases are confirmed as problematic less often than Group A high-band cases, the score over-flags Group D.
- Test whether "incomplete documentation" predicts eventual document sufficiency equally across groups; if Group B "incomplete" cases are resolved with one trivial resubmission at higher rates, the flag partly measures language barrier, not applicant deficiency.
- Bound residual confounding with a quantitative bias analysis (e.g., E-value style reasoning: how strong would an unmeasured legitimate complexity factor need to be to explain the −5.5 pp Group B gap?).

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.** The notes flag backlogs in two regions and longer in-person waits. Estimate all adjusted disparities separately by region and channel. If Group B's disadvantage concentrates in the two backlogged regions, the mechanism is regional capacity, not group-targeted behavior — a different (though still fixable) fairness problem. Also check channel-within-group: do online Group B applicants approach Group A performance? If yes, the in-person/language pipeline is the locus of the disparity. Interaction terms (group × region, group × channel) should be reported, not just main effects.

**Uncertainty handling.** Report confidence intervals for all adjusted contrasts; with 820–1,250 cases per minority group, subgroup cells (group × region × risk band) get small quickly. Pre-register which contrasts are confirmatory versus exploratory to avoid multiplicity-driven false alarms.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Largely justified by case mix or policy:**

- *Group D's concentration in V3 (Risk Screening → Fraud Review → Supervisor Review, 28% vs. 9% for A).* Group D has 51% high risk_score_band and policy mandates Fraud Review for high risk. The routing itself is rule-consistent, and the Risk Screening → Fraud Review wait is similar across groups (2.0–2.8 days). The *routing* is defensible **conditional on the score being valid** — which is exactly the caveat that must be tested, since the score may encode historical disparities (Section 5). Group D's adjusted approval gap (−3.1 pp) and high adjusted appeal probability (+5.7 pp) suggest the score or the fraud pipeline deserves calibration auditing even if the routing rule is legitimate.
- *Group B's presence in V4 (Language Support) and Group C's in V5 (Accommodation Review) per se.* These steps are rights-preserving and mandated by expressed need. Their existence is fair; their *performance* is the problem (below).
- *Part of Group B's V2 share (Request Missing Documents).* Group B has 46% incomplete documentation, so more document requests are expected. However, the *adjusted* +9.5 pp probability of Request Missing Documents — i.e., after controlling for documentation_completeness — is **not** explained by case mix and remains concerning.

**Remaining concerning:**

1. **The support-step penalty.** The two rights-preserving steps carry the largest single waits in the process: 3.8 days at Language Support Contact → Eligibility (only 7 bilingual caseworkers) and 4.7 days at Accommodation Review → Eligibility (small specialist team). V4 and V5 have the longest non-appeal median cycle times (19.2 and 21.6 days vs. 5.8 for V1). Exercising a legal right currently costs roughly 2–4× the straight-through cycle time. The absence of service-level targets for these steps means the penalty is unmonitored and unmanaged. This is the clearest structural procedural-fairness deficit in the evidence.
2. **Unexplained escalation and document requests.** Adjusted +8.0 pp Supervisor Review for Group B and +5.5 pp for Group C, and adjusted +9.5 pp document requests for Group B, persist after controlling for the very variables that should mandate those steps. This pattern is consistent with discretionary over-scrutiny of minority-language and accommodation cases (though other mechanisms are possible; see Section 4).
3. **Resource concentration and handover ping-pong.** Group B funneled to 7 bilingual staff plus 3 *overloaded* officers; Group C to 5 specialists, two of whom also perform Supervisor Review — meaning Group C cases may be effectively reviewed by the same small circle that processed them, weakening the independence of review and adding queueing. The general → specialist → general handover loops in two regions add coordination delay and information loss disproportionately for B and C.
4. **Wait asymmetries at shared steps.** Even at steps everyone traverses, Group B waits longer: Completeness → Request Docs (1.6d vs. 0.4d for A), Supervisor Review → Decision Draft (1.9d vs. 1.0d), Submit → Identity Verification (1.4d vs. 0.8d). Some of this is channel (in-person) and regional backlog, but it compounds with the support-step penalty.
5. **Appeal/rework asymmetry.** Groups B and C rework rates (22%, 24%) are double Group A's (11%). Combined with elevated adjusted appeal probability for B and D, this suggests first-decision quality or explanation quality is worse for these groups — the process makes them pay twice.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Legitimate / structural (not intentional discrimination, but possibly still unfair in effect):**

1. **Capacity mismatch at mandated support steps.** Policy mandates Language Support and Accommodation Review but staffs them thinly (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 specialists) with no timeliness targets. Queueing theory alone then produces long, right-skewed waits for exactly the groups the steps are meant to protect. This mechanism fully explains the V4/V5 wait pattern without any biased decision-making — yet it is a fairness problem because the burden of under-resourcing falls on protected-need groups.
2. **Genuine complexity correlated with group.** Higher incomplete-documentation rates (B), accommodation coordination needs (C), and risk-mandated fraud review (D) legitimately generate extra steps and time. This explains a large share of the *raw* gaps — the case-mix table shows the groups are truly different populations — but by construction cannot explain the *adjusted* residuals.
3. **Regional and channel infrastructure effects.** Backlogs in two regions and slower in-person intake hit groups unevenly because groups are unevenly distributed across regions and channels (38% in-person for B vs. 9% for A). The unfairness is geographic/operational, but it has disparate impact by group.

**Concerning:**

4. **Discretionary over-scrutiny (statistical or implicit bias).** Caseworkers may escalate minority-language or accommodation cases to Supervisor Review, or issue extra document requests, "to be safe" — treating communication difficulty or atypicality as risk. This would produce exactly the adjusted +8.0/+5.5 pp supervisor-review and +9.5 pp document-request residuals.
5. **Feedback-loop bias in risk scoring.** If risk_score_band is trained on historical fraud referrals or prior-case counts, and past enforcement scrutinized some groups more, the score reproduces that history: Group D gets flagged more, reviewed more, generating more recorded "risk events," reinforcing the score. Routing is then rule-consistent but the rule's input is biased.
6. **Documentation flags encoding language barriers.** "Incomplete documentation" may partly measure the applicant's ability to understand instructions in the majority language rather than actual eligibility deficiency. Group B's 46% incomplete share would then be inflated, and controlling for it would *absorb* part of the discrimination into a "legitimate" covariate — making adjusted estimates *understate* unfairness.
7. **Overloaded-specialist decision degradation.** The 3 overloaded regional officers and dual-role accommodation specialists may make rushed or inconsistent decisions, producing higher rework/appeal rates for B and C — poor quality masquerading as applicant-side complexity.
8. **Notification/explanation quality gaps.** If decisions are communicated without adequate translation or accessible formats, applicants appeal to obtain an explanation, inflating B and D appeal rates independent of decision correctness.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in covariates.** risk_score_band and documentation_completeness are treatment-relevant covariates that are plausibly *caused in part by group membership and by the institution's own past behavior*. Conditioning on a contaminated mediator can mask discrimination (attributing biased scrutiny to "risk") or, conversely, induce collider-style distortions. The adjusted table should therefore be read as a range, not a point estimate, and the −5.5 pp / −2.2 pp / −3.1 pp residuals could each be materially larger or smaller under different assumptions about covariate validity.

**Missing applicant needs and burdens.** The log records that Language Support Contact occurred, not whether interpretation was adequate; that Accommodation Review completed, not whether the accommodation was actually delivered. Applicant-side costs — repeated trips for in-person applicants, time off work, comprehension of notices, stress — are entirely unobserved. Cases *abandoned* due to burden may appear as denials or closures, biasing approval-rate comparisons in unknown directions.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity. Within-day load, multitasking, and the dual roles of accommodation specialists (specialist + supervisor) are invisible, so "long wait" cannot be cleanly attributed to queue length versus prioritization behavior versus deliberate deprioritization. The handover network shows who touched a case, not how long each actually worked on it or why it sat.

**Appeal quality and outcome quality.** case_outcome does not capture whether decisions were correct, well-explained, or delivered with dignity. Appeal rates conflate decision error, explanation failure, applicant sophistication, and access to advocacy — Group A's low appeal rate could even reflect *better* self-advocacy earlier in the process rather than better decisions. Appeal *reversal* rates by group would help but are not provided.

**Historical bias in risk scores.** prior_case_count and risk_score_band likely embed prior institutional contact patterns. If some groups were historically surveilled or referred more, these attributes are proxies for past disparate treatment. Treating them as neutral eligibility-relevant facts launders history into present routing. Without access to the score's construction and a group-wise calibration audit (do equal bands imply equal true risk?), no strong conclusion about the fairness of Group D's fraud routing is possible.

**Statistical limits.** With 820–1,250 cases in the minority groups, three-way subgroup cells (group × region × risk band) are small; several adjusted contrasts (e.g., Group C's −2.2 pp) may not be distinguishable from noise. Multiple comparisons across many metrics inflate false-positive risk.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Consistent with the constraints: none of these remove rights-preserving steps; the goal is to make the rights cheap to exercise.

1. **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** (more bilingual caseworkers; expand and de-conflict the specialist team; separate specialist and supervisor roles). Directly attacks the 3.8d/4.7d queues, the single largest identified structural penalty. *Side effects:* cost; hiring qualified bilingual/specialist staff is slow; new staff may initially decide less consistently; if capacity is added by cross-training generalists, support quality may dilute; risk of creating a parallel "second-tier" track if specialist cases remain segregated.
2. **Service-level targets (SLAs) for support steps** with published timeliness thresholds and escalation when breached. Cheap, makes the currently unmonitored penalty visible and managed. *Side effects:* SLA pressure can cause quality shortcuts (perfunctory language contacts to stop the clock), gaming (reclassifying cases to avoid the clocked step), or displacement of delay to unmeasured edges. SLAs must be paired with quality checks on the support interaction itself.
3. **Routing redesign to reduce handovers** — e.g., bilingual or accommodation-trained caseworkers own the case end-to-end instead of general → specialist → general loops; parallelize Language Support Contact with Completeness Check where legally possible instead of sequencing it. *Side effects:* end-to-end ownership by a small specialist pool worsens concentration and queueing unless capacity grows first; parallelization risks doing eligibility work before communication needs are met, which could undermine the right the step protects; redesign has transition costs and retraining needs.
4. **Decision-explanation improvements**: translated, plain-language, accessible-format notifications; reason codes; a pre-appeal clarification contact. Targets the elevated appeal/rework burden for B and D at low cost. *Side effects:* adds a step (slight cycle-time increase); poorly implemented clarification calls could pressure applicants out of legitimate appeals; translation quality itself needs auditing.
5. **Monitoring adjusted disparities** as an ongoing dashboard: per-group adjusted approval, cycle time, escalation probability, document-request probability, support-step waits, appeal and rework rates, with alerting. *Side effects:* Goodhart risk — managers may optimize the monitored metric (e.g., suppress supervisor reviews for Group B to hit parity, potentially harming decision quality); dashboards conditioned on biased covariates can certify a biased status quo; requires governance on which model specification defines "adjusted."
6. **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**: group-wise calibration of the risk score against downstream confirmed outcomes and appeal reversals; re-derivation of documentation flags with language-access controls; documentation of score inputs and lineage. *Side effects:* recalibrating a score used for fraud control may raise fraud-risk exposure during transition and faces legal/political resistance; if the audit reduces flagging of some groups, fraud teams may compensate with informal discretionary referrals (shifting bias off-ledger); audits require outcome labels ("true" fraud) that are themselves products of past biased scrutiny — the audit design must confront that circularity, e.g., via random audit samples.
7. *(Supporting measure)* **Random-order or blinded assignment for discretionary escalation**: require documented reason codes for any Supervisor Review not mandated by rule, enabling review of the discretionary +8.0 pp residual. *Side effect:* documentation burden on caseworkers; reason codes can become boilerplate.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence does not support a finding of intentional discrimination, but it also does not support a finding that the process is procedurally fair. The most defensible characterization is: **the process exhibits at least one well-evidenced structural procedural unfairness, plus residual disparities that are concerning but not conclusively attributable to biased treatment.**

What is well supported: the rights-preserving support steps function as penalties in practice. Language Support and Accommodation Review carry the longest queue waits in the process (3.8 and 4.7 days), are staffed by very small teams, have no timeliness targets, and drive the longest non-appeal variants (19–22 days vs. 5.8 for the straight-through path). Applicants exercising legal rights systematically pay for it in time, handovers, and rework. This conclusion is robust because it depends on directly observed waits and staffing, not on contested covariates.

What is suggestive but not conclusive: the adjusted residuals — −5.5 pp approval and +8.0 pp supervisor review and +9.5 pp document requests for Group B, and elevated adjusted appeal rates for B and D — persist after controlling for documented complexity and are consistent with discretionary over-scrutiny. However, these estimates rest on covariates (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness) that may themselves encode group-correlated bias, in directions that could make the true disparity either larger or smaller. Group D's fraud routing is rule-consistent on its face, but the rule's fairness is only as good as the score's group-wise calibration, which is unverified.

What is indeterminate: whether decisions differ in *correctness* or dignity across groups (the log lacks quality labels), how much of the disparity is regional capacity versus group-linked behavior (requires the subgroup analyses in Section 2), and the experience of applicants who abandoned the process.

Calibrated conclusion: the process is **procedurally unfair in its treatment of support-step users on the timeliness and burden dimensions; indeterminate-but-warranting-investigation on decision-level discrimination**. Priority actions are capacity and SLAs for the support steps, separation of specialist/supervisor roles, an audit of risk-score calibration and documentation flags, and ongoing monitoring of adjusted disparities — none of which require, or would justify, weakening the rights-preserving steps themselves.
