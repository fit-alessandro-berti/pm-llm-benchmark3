# 1. Mechanism map

## 1.1 First, check the arithmetic in management's own estimate

Before building any causal story, it helps to put the two handover tables side by side. The management estimate does not actually show fewer handovers.

| Measure (per 100 cases) | Current | Management post-change estimate | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total logged handovers | 343 | 359 | **+16 (+4.7%)** |
| Specialist-related handovers (tech out + tech back + legal/risk) | 76 | 89 | +13 (+17%) |
| Approval-related handovers (to approver + approver → implementation) | 81 | 92 | +11 (+14%) |
| Cross-team handovers | 6 | 3 | −3 (−50%) |
| "Handover-wait exposure" (Σ frequency × median wait), hours | 588 h | 722 h | **+134 h (+23%)** |
| – of which specialist-related | 251 h | 369 h | +47% |
| – of which approval-related | 159 h | 224 h | +41% |
| – of which cross-team | 53 h | 18 h | −66% |

(Medians do not add up exactly, so the exposure index is only a rough guide. The direction is still clear.)

What this implies:

- The "fewer local-specialist handover loops" claim holds only for *cross-team* and *intake→caseworker* transfers. Every specialist and approval loop gets **more** frequent and **slower**, even on management's own numbers.
- A lower handover count in a future event log could therefore be a **measurement artifact**, not a real reduction. That would happen if `org:resource` is logged as a pool or queue ID (e.g., "TSS", "ApprovalQueue") instead of the individual specialist, or if the "pull advice from shared queue" step produces no event at all. Transfers inside the shared service would then disappear from the handover-of-work network while real coordination grows.
- **Legal/Risk demand:** referrals go from 9 to 12 per 100 cases, which is 189 → 252 per month at 2,100 cases/month (+33%). These specialists already run at 91–93% utilization. If service times stay the same, implied utilization is about **121–124%**, which is an unstable queue. Management's 10.5 h median wait (vs 9.6 h today) is not consistent with queueing behavior at that load. On top of that, "security" is a new routing category with no named security specialist in the organization.
- **Approvals:** volume goes from 42 to 49 per 100 cases (882 → 1,029 per month), but there will be 2 approvers instead of 3. Per-approver load rises by about **75%** (294 → ~515 approvals each per month). The estimated increase in median wait (2.4 h → 3.0 h) assumes current supervisors have a lot of slack.
- **Technical pool:** tech referrals rise from 36 to 41 per 100 (756 → 861 per month, +14%). Difficult Quality Check and the legal/risk triage that local techs currently do informally also move into the pool. Average tech utilization today is (71+68+82)/3 ≈ 74%. The +14% referral increase alone takes it to about 84%. Adding difficult QC and triage plausibly takes it to **88–95%**.

## 1.2 Why pooling can help, and why the benefit is fragile

Queueing theory says pooling identical servers reduces waiting at the same load. Rough M/M/c figures, with waiting time expressed in multiples of the mean service time:

| Configuration | Utilization | Expected queue wait |
|---|---:|---:|
| E_Tech alone (today) | 82% | ~4.6 × |
| N_Tech alone (today) | 71% | ~2.4 × |
| S_Tech alone (today) | 68% | ~2.1 × |
| Pooled 3 techs, same load as today | 74% | **~0.7 ×** |
| Pooled, +15% utilization (management target) | ~85% | ~1.6 × |
| Pooled, with QC and triage added | ~92% | ~3.5 × |
| Pooled, adverse | ~95% | ~6 × |

So pooling is a real advantage, and especially so for East. But the advantage **disappears as extra work is loaded onto the pool**, and it assumes specialists are interchangeable. Regional conventions break that assumption. The current log shows what happens when work leaves its home region: cross-team handovers carry **18% rework**, versus 8–9% in local tech loops. If pooled specialists are assigned at random, about two-thirds of tech cases would be handled out of region. Service-time variance also grows, and in Kingman's approximation waiting grows with (c_a² + c_s²).

"15% higher specialist utilization" is therefore not a benefit in itself. Near saturation, higher utilization **predicts** longer and more variable waiting.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

The probabilities are subjective and depend on design choices (listed after the scenarios). They are not predictions of the unmodified proposal.

## 2.1 Optimistic scenario (≈20–25% under the proposal as written; higher if modified)

**Conditions.** TSS stays at 80–85% utilization. Difficult QC gets reserved capacity or stays partly local. Specialists keep "home region" affinity (for example, 60–70% of their cases from one region). A shared case-note standard is introduced. Legal/Risk capacity is added before routing is broadened. Advice returns to the named originating caseworker.

- **Handover pattern.** Total handovers per case are flat to slightly up (+0–5%). Cross-team handovers halve. The tech loop returns to the same caseworker at least 85% of the time (today 31/36 ≈ 86%). Ping-pong variants are rare. The handover network becomes hub-and-spoke but stays shallow: there are few multi-step paths through the hub.
- **Queue/waiting.** Median tech wait is about 3–3.5 h overall, and East falls sharply (its current 82% single-server overload goes away). Legal/Risk median wait is about 10 h, with the P90 no worse than baseline, because capacity was added. Approval median wait is 2.5–3 h.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** East median 8.6 → ~7.3–7.8 d and P90 22.8 → ~17–19 d. North/South median roughly unchanged (±5%) and P90 unchanged or slightly better. The gap between teams narrows.
- **Rework/quality.** East rework 11.5% → ~9.5–10%. N/S rework flat. Standardized QC reduces variation in decisions between teams.
- **Role clarity/accountability.** Discovered roles match formal roles (clear TSS, caseworker and approver clusters). There is a clear owner rule for each case.
- **Fairness.** The disparity ratio of SLA miss between teams falls (East 9.8% moves toward N/S levels). No segment's P90 worsens by more than 5%.
- **Early warning signs** (that this scenario is holding): weekly TSS arrival and completion rates stay roughly equal. Queue WIP age is stable. Referral rate for low/medium complexity stays at baseline. Staff pulse shows stable "I know who owns this case" scores.

## 2.2 Central scenario (≈45–50%)

**Conditions.** The proposal is implemented largely as written. Some affinity emerges informally. No extra Legal/Risk capacity. The supervisor is removed at go-live.

- **Handover pattern.** Total handovers per case are +5–10%, which is more than management estimates, because low/medium complexity referrals creep up. Cross-team handovers fall to about 3 per 100. Pool-internal transfers appear inside TSS (specialist A starts, specialist B finishes). The same-owner return ratio falls from ~86% to 65–75% because advice is pulled by whichever caseworker is free. `reassignment_count` rises moderately.
- **Queue/waiting.** Median tech wait is about 4–5 h, East better and N/S worse. Legal/Risk median wait is 14–20 h, and the P90 roughly doubles. Approval median wait is 3.5–5 h, with spikes at month-end and during absences.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** The organization-wide *average* is roughly flat (−3% to +3%), mainly because East improves (median −5–10%). North/South medians are +3–8% and P90s +10–20%. High-complexity cases have P90 +15–30%. The distribution is **bimodal**: simple cases get faster through local intake plus automation, and complex cases get slower.
- **Rework/quality.** East rework improves by 0.5–1.5 pp. N/S rework rises by 1–2 pp among complex cases and cases with regional conventions (`local_expertise_flag`=1). Organization-wide rework is flat, which hides both shifts.
- **Role clarity/accountability.** TSS resources show mixed activity profiles (tech + triage + QC). Role discovery produces blurred clusters. Staff report ambiguity about who closes open questions. "Waiting for response" and "unclear owner" become more common handover reasons.
- **Fairness.** Across teams, East improves while N/S degrade slightly, and the averages converge. The tail burden shifts onto high-complexity, legal/risk and language/regional customer segments. Decision consistency across teams improves.
- **Early warning signs:** TSS WIP age trends upward in weeks 3–8. The share of TSS reviews that end without a substantive change rises (a sign of unnecessary referrals). Informal side channels appear, such as caseworkers contacting former local techs by phone (a drop in logged referrals without a drop in rework, or staff reports). Legal/Risk queue length grows week over week.

## 2.3 Adverse scenario (≈25–30%)

**Conditions.** TSS runs above 90% utilization once QC and triage are included. Legal/Risk is effectively at ρ ≥ 1. Deskilling and loss of informal advice start a referral-inflation loop. An approver or specialist is absent during rollout.

- **Handover pattern.** Handovers per case rise by 15% or more. Ping-pong loops appear (caseworker → TSS → caseworker → TSS, and approval returned for more information). Subcontracting patterns multiply. Cross-team handovers reappear under new labels (for example, "absence cover" inside TSS). Shadow handovers that the log does not capture appear: informal advice with no event.
- **Queue/waiting.** Tech median wait is 6–10 h with a heavy tail. The Legal/Risk queue grows without bound: WIP rises each week and the oldest items exceed the SLA. The approval queue backs up whenever one of the two approvers is absent.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** The average may look stable for 4–8 weeks, because long cases have not yet closed (right censoring) and pre-change cases are still draining. After that, the median rises 10–20%, the P90 rises 30–60%, and high-complexity P90 can double.
- **Rework/quality.** Rework rises 3–5 pp in N/S complex cases. Difficult QC is delayed, so rework is found later and costs more. Rework after handover approaches the cross-team level (about 18%).
- **Role clarity/accountability.** No one owns stalled cases. Supervisors and caseworkers disagree about approval authority. Specialist burnout indicators appear: after-hours events, and shorter service times on hard cases, which is a quality risk.
- **Fairness.** East's early gain erodes as N/S volume dominates the shared first-come-first-served queue. Language/regional customers face extra handovers and lower satisfaction. High-complexity cases, which may concentrate in some segments, carry most of the delay. Staff trust drops.
- **Early warning signs:** weekly TSS arrivals exceed completions for 2 or more consecutive weeks. Legal/Risk P90 wait is more than 2× baseline within 3 weeks. Referral rate for medium complexity rises by 5 pp or more. The same-owner return ratio falls below 60%. After-hours activity by specialists increases. Staff report workarounds.

## 2.4 What shifts probability toward the optimistic case

- Reserve capacity or keep difficult QC local.
- Add Legal/Risk capacity before broadening routing to legal/risk/security.
- Route to TSS by assessed need, not by the blanket rule "all high-complexity".
- Use affinity routing and a named owner for returned advice.
- Keep the supervisor position until the approval queue proves stable.
- Standardize case notes before pooling.
- Cap TSS utilization at around 85% rather than targeting "+15%".

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency.** Pooling shortens the queue for the typical case but makes it worse for the tail once utilization passes about 85–90%, because waiting grows as ρ/(1−ρ). A stable mean can hide a P95 that doubles. The current P90/median ratio is already 2.4–2.65; watch whether that ratio rises. *Detection:* per-cohort P90/P95 and the P90/median ratio, cycle-time histograms, performance DFG edges reported as P90 rather than mean.

2. **High-complexity cases carry the queue cost.** The rule "all high-complexity cases go to TSS", combined with simple cases getting faster locally, produces a bimodal distribution. Because high-complexity cases are a minority, the mean can improve while they get much slower. That is a composition effect (Simpson's-paradox risk). *Detection:* stratify every KPI by `case_complexity` and compare within strata, not pooled.

3. **Local knowledge loss.** Regional conventions currently prevent rework in complex cases. The 18% rework on cross-team handovers vs 8–9% in local tech loops shows what happens when work leaves the region. Knowledge loss appears slowly, through rework found later, and through customer contacts that the cycle-time metric does not count. *Detection:* rework by `local_expertise_flag` × team, rework after tech handover, repeated customer contacts.

4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments.** Average improvement can come entirely from East (overload relief) while N/S get worse, or from simple cases while language/regional customers face more handovers. A shared first-come-first-served queue serves North/South's 75% of volume by default. East may gain at first and then be crowded out. *Detection:* disparity ratios for P90, SLA miss, handovers per case and satisfaction by team and `customer_segment`, with difference-in-differences against a control.

5. **Learning and deskilling.** Once hard cases are routed away, caseworkers lose practice. Over months they refer more borderline cases. This raises TSS load, which raises waits, which (if average cycle time is the only goal) creates pressure to route even more. This is a slow feedback loop invisible in the short-term mean. *Detection:* trend of the medium-complexity referral rate, activity diversity of caseworkers, share of "no-change" specialist reviews.

6. **Accountability and ownership.** With a shared approval queue and open pull of advice, cases can sit between owners. The average stays acceptable because most cases have an obvious owner, but orphaned cases pile up in the tail. *Detection:* same-owner return ratio, idle gaps with no active resource, ping-pong variants, "unclear owner" handover reasons, `reassignment_count` P90.

7. **Exception handling.** Absence cover, urgent priority cases, cases needing several specialties (tech + legal), and escalations all compete in one mixed queue. Exceptions are rare, so their delay barely moves the mean, but they are where the business and legal exposure sits. *Detection:* filter by `priority`=high, multiple `required_specialty` values and absence weeks, and check their tail wait.

8. **Resilience loss hidden by utilization gains.** Higher utilization looks efficient until an absence occurs. At 85% utilization on 3 pooled specialists, one absence produces 128% load. With 2 approvers, one absence halves approval capacity. Legal and Risk remain single points of failure. Averages measured over a quiet period will overstate robustness. *Detection:* stress-period analysis (weeks with an absent key resource), recovery time, WIP peaks.

9. **Invisible work and logging artifacts.** A drop in logged handovers can reflect pool-level resource IDs, pull events that are not logged, or informal phone calls to former local techs (shadow work). Cycle time may improve because work moved off the log, not because it disappeared. *Detection:* pool-ID share, events per case, staff interviews, comparing referral decline with rework change.

10. **Measurement artifacts in early post-change data.** Right censoring (long cases not yet closed), pre-change cases still draining, novelty and Hawthorne effects, and regression to the mean for East (its bad baseline may partly revert anyway) all make early averages look better than the steady state. *Detection:* cohort by start date, survival analysis, exclude transition cases, compare to a control group.

# 5. Pilot design

## 5.1 Principle: split the bundle

The proposal bundles six changes with different risk profiles. If they are rolled out together, it will be impossible to tell which one caused an effect. Pilot them in stages:

| Component | Risk | Pilot treatment |
|---|---|---|
| A. Technical pooling (N/S/E_Tech → TSS) | Moderate. Clear pooling benefit, knowledge risk. | Pilot first, with affinity routing and a named-owner return rule |
| B. Difficult QC into TSS | High (extra load in the same queue) | Postpone. Keep local, or give TSS reserved capacity. Add only if TSS utilization stays below 80%. |
| C. Legal/Risk/Security routing broadened | High (ρ already 0.91–0.93) | Do **not** broaden until capacity is added or triage is proven to filter referrals. "Security" needs a named owner. |
| D. Shared approval queue + supervisor removal | High (accountability, −1/3 capacity) | Run the shared queue with all 3 supervisors first. Remove the position only after the queue meets guardrails for a full quarter. |
| E. Rule "all high-complexity → TSS" | Moderate/high (referral inflation) | Replace with a need-based checklist, and pilot that against the blanket rule |
| F. Case-note standardization | Low, and a precondition for B/C/D | Introduce before pooling, in all teams |

## 5.2 Pilot scope

- **Phase 0 (4–6 weeks, all teams):** make logging fit for the analysis. Record individual resource IDs (never pool IDs). Add queue enter/exit events, or a start timestamp per activity. Add an "advice pulled by" event. Add an optional "informal consult" event so invisible work becomes measurable. Introduce the shared case-note template. Collect baseline staff pulse data.
- **Phase 1 (12–16 weeks): Technical pooling for East + South.** E_Tech and S_Tech form a virtual pool. Each specialist keeps about 60–70% of cases from their home region, and the rest is load-balanced. Advice returns to the originating caseworker. Legal/Risk routing, approvals and QC stay unchanged. East is included because it is the most likely to benefit and the most overloaded. South is included so the result is not East-only and the knowledge-loss risk for a strong team can be tested.
- **Phase 2 (stepped wedge, 12 weeks):** North joins the pool (tests the full 3-person pool). In parallel, the shared approval queue runs with **all three supervisors** retained. Optionally, test need-based vs blanket routing for high-complexity cases by randomizing at week level or caseworker level.
- **Phase 3 (conditional):** only if earlier guardrails hold and Legal/Risk capacity has been added: broaden legal/risk/security routing, consider moving difficult QC, and then consider removing the supervisor position.

## 5.3 Comparison group

- **Phase 1:** North serves as the concurrent control. Use difference-in-differences: (post − pre)_pilot minus (post − pre)_North. This removes seasonality and shocks that affect the whole organization.
- **Phase 2:** stepped-wedge design. Each team acts as its own control before it switches, and the others serve as concurrent controls.
- **Supporting checks:** synthetic control (weighted combination of teams on pre-period trends), and matched comparisons of cases on complexity, specialty, segment and priority.
- Check **parallel pre-trends** (12+ weeks) before trusting difference-in-differences.
- Handle East's poor baseline (regression to the mean) by using long pre-periods, not just the last month.

## 5.4 Pre/post observation window

- **Pre-period:** at least 26 weeks, ideally 52, to capture seasonality. Use the most recent 12 weeks for the pre-trend check.
- **Transition exclusion:** cases started within 2 weeks before go-live that are still open at go-live are analyzed separately (as "straddling cases").
- **Post-period:** at least 12–16 weeks of case starts, then an extra follow-up of about 8 weeks so long cases can close and rework can show up.
- **Assign cohorts by case start date**, not end date, so that fast early closures do not bias the comparison. Use survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier, and Cox regression with team × period interaction) for right-censored cases.
- **Minimum sample:** aim for about 200 or more cases per stratum for rework and SLA comparisons. East is about 520 cases/month, so high-complexity strata may need to be pooled across months or reported with wide intervals.

## 5.5 Segmentation (mandatory for every KPI)

- **Team:** North, South, East, with East reported separately and never merged into an average.
- **Complexity:** low, medium, high.
- **Required specialty:** tech, legal, risk, security, none, and multiple.
- **Customer segment:** including language/regional accommodation segments.
- **Priority.**
- **`local_expertise_flag`.**
- **Handover type.**
- **Absence weeks vs normal weeks.**

## 5.6 Guardrail metrics

| Guardrail | Threshold (relative to difference-in-differences baseline) |
|---|---|
| P90 cycle time, any team | Not worse than +10% |
| P90 cycle time, high complexity | Not worse than +15% |
| SLA miss, any team, priority or customer segment | Not worse than +1.5 pp |
| Rework, any team × complexity cell | Not worse than +1.5 pp |
| TSS utilization | ≤ 85% weekly average; no more than 2 weeks above 90% |
| TSS queue | Arrivals ≤ completions on average. P90 WIP age ≤ 1.5× baseline tech wait. |
| Legal/Risk wait P90 | ≤ 1.5× baseline |
| Same-owner return ratio | ≥ 80% |
| Handovers per case (individual level) | ≤ +10% |
| Segment disparity ratio (P90, SLA miss) | Must not rise by > 20% |
| Customer satisfaction, any segment | Not worse than −0.2 |
| Staff role-clarity pulse | Not worse than −0.5 on a 5-point scale |

## 5.7 Staff feedback collection

- **Biweekly pulse survey** (5 items): role clarity, ownership, access to quick technical advice, workload, perceived fairness of the queue.
- **Structured interviews** at weeks 4, 8 and 12 with caseworkers, specialists and supervisors from pilot and control teams.
- **Workaround log:** staff can record informal consultations or shortcuts without blame. This measures shadow work directly.
- **Specialist logs:** case types handled out of region, and cases where regional knowledge was missing.
- **Customer-facing staff:** report language or regional accommodation problems.
- **Triangulation:** compare survey signals with the event log (for example, if "hard to get advice" rises together with "no-change" reviews and referral inflation, the loss of informal advice is confirmed).

## 5.8 Rollback or adjustment triggers

Triggers are graded: **adjust first, then pause, then roll back.**

| Trigger | Response |
|---|---|
| Any single guardrail breached for 2 consecutive weeks | **Adjust:** raise affinity share, add temporary specialist capacity, remove QC or triage from the TSS queue, add priority lanes |
| TSS arrivals > completions for 3 consecutive weeks, or P90 WIP age > 2× baseline | **Pause expansion.** Do not start the next phase. Add capacity. |
| Legal/Risk P90 wait > 2× baseline | **Freeze** any broadened routing to Legal/Risk and revert to the previous routing rules |
| SLA miss +3 pp in any team or segment, or high-complexity P90 +30% | **Roll back** that team to embedded specialists while keeping the case-note standard |
| Same-owner return < 65%, or staff role-clarity drop ≥ 1 point | Re-establish named case ownership. Stop open advice pull. |
| East improves but N/S degrade beyond guardrails | Hybrid: keep a pool for East overflow and restore embedded specialists for N/S |
| Rework in `local_expertise_flag` cases rises > 2 pp | Restore regional affinity or embedded specialists for regional/complex cases |

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: modify, then pilot first. Do not proceed with a full rollout, and do not reject outright.**

**Why not reject.** East has a real, structural problem: a single specialist at 82% utilization, the longest cycle times (median 8.6 d, P90 22.8 d), the highest rework (11.5%) and the highest SLA miss (9.8%). Pooling is a sound, theory-backed remedy for that. Standardized decisions may also improve consistency between teams, which is a fairness gain. Poor cross-team cover (8.9 h wait, 18% rework) shows the current structure is also fragile.

**Why not proceed as proposed.**

- Management's own estimates imply **more** handovers (+4.7%) and about **23% more handover-wait exposure**, not fewer.
- Legal/Risk demand +33% on top of 91–93% utilization is not sustainable.
- Removing a supervisor while approval volume rises gives about +75% load per approver.
- The utilization target removes the slack that makes pooling work.
- North and South may lose regional expertise that currently keeps their rework low.

**Modifications to make before and during the pilot:**

1. **Hub-and-spoke tech pool.** Pool specialists with regional affinity and named-owner return. Give East priority overflow access rather than pure first-come-first-served.
2. **Utilization target.** Replace "+15% utilization" with "TSS ≤ 85% utilization and P90 wait ≤ baseline".
3. **Keep difficult QC out of the TSS queue**, or give it reserved capacity.
4. **Legal/Risk capacity first.** Add capacity, or prove that triage filters referrals, before broadening routing. Assign a named owner for "security".
5. **Need-based routing.** Replace "all high-complexity → TSS" with a need-based checklist, and keep a channel for informal local consultation, for example specialist office hours per region.
6. **Supervisor position.** Keep all three supervisors during the pilot. Decide on removal only after one stable quarter of the shared approval queue.
7. **Learning mechanisms.** Rotate caseworkers through TSS shadowing and hold case reviews, so local technical skill does not erode.
8. **Logging.** Fix logging before go-live: individual resource IDs, queue events and start timestamps.

**Conditions for scaling beyond the pilot.** All of the following must hold, compared with North as control:

- Difference-in-differences shows East improves.
- North/South stay within guardrails, especially P90 and rework for complex and `local_expertise_flag` cases.
- TSS queue stability holds for 12+ weeks, including at least one absence episode.
- Disparity ratios across teams and customer segments do not worsen.
- The staff role-clarity pulse is stable.

**What management should NOT infer from early averages:**

- **"Average cycle time improved, so it works."** Early post-change cohorts are right-censored: slow, complex cases have not closed yet. Pre-change cases are still draining, and novelty effects inflate early performance. Use start-date cohorts and survival estimates, and wait at least 12–16 weeks plus follow-up.
- **"Fewer handovers in the log."** This may be a logging artifact (pool IDs, unlogged pulls, informal side channels). Only individual-level handover counts are valid.
- **"Utilization went up 15%."** This is a cost input, not an outcome. Near saturation it predicts longer and more variable waits.
- **"Organization-wide rework is flat."** This can hide East improving and North/South complex cases getting worse.
- **"East improved."** Part of this may be regression to the mean from a bad baseline, or overload relief that any capacity increase would deliver. Compare against a control and a "just add capacity to East" counterfactual.
- **"No problems in the first month."** Deskilling, loss of informal advice, referral inflation and ownership decay are slow feedback loops that show up after months. Resilience is only tested when an absence happens.
- **"Average satisfaction held."** Language/regional segments can get worse while the average holds.

def sna_to_graph(sna, directed=True):
    g = nx.DiGraph() if directed else nx.Graph()
    for (a, b), w in sna.connections.items():
        if w > 0 and a != b:
            g.add_edge(a, b, weight=w)
    return g

def network_summary(g, hub_nodes):
    bc = nx.betweenness_centrality(g, weight=None)
    deg = dict(g.degree())
    n = g.number_of_nodes()
    max_deg = max(deg.values()) if deg else 0
    centralization = sum(max_deg - d for d in deg.values()) / ((n - 1) * (n - 2)) if n > 2 else 0
    return {"nodes": n, "edges": g.number_of_edges(), "density": nx.density(g),
            "degree_centralization": centralization,
            "hub_betweenness": {h: round(bc.get(h, 0), 3) for h in hub_nodes}}

def queue_wip(d, target_groups, freq="D"):
    """Daily WIP and oldest-item age for cases waiting at a queue (prev completion -> target completion)."""
    d = d.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    d["prev_ts"] = d.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift()
    q = d[d.res_group.isin(target_groups) & d.prev_ts.notna()][["prev_ts", TS]]
    days = pd.date_range(q.prev_ts.min().floor("D"), q[TS].max().ceil("D"), freq=freq, tz="UTC")
    rows = []
    for t in days:
        waiting = q[(q.prev_ts <= t) & (q[TS] > t)]
        age = (t - waiting.prev_ts).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
        rows.append({"day": t, "wip": len(waiting),
                     "p90_age_h": age.quantile(.9) if len(age) else 0.0})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("day")

def role_analysis(d, label):
    sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(d, activity_key=ACT, resource_key=RES,
                                                            timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)
    within, across = [], []
    for (a, b), s in sim.connections.items():
        if a == b:
            continue
        (within if formal_role.get(a) == formal_role.get(b) else across).append(s)
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(d, activity_key=ACT, resource_key=RES,
                                                timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)
    purity = []
    for role in roles:
        members = list(role.originator_importance.keys())
        fr = pd.Series([formal_role.get(m) for m in members]).value_counts(normalize=True)
        purity.append(fr.iloc[0] if len(fr) else np.nan)
        print(label, "role:", sorted(role.activities), "members:", members)
    # activity entropy per resource (role breadth / blur)
    p = d.groupby([RES, ACT]).size().unstack(fill_value=0)
    p = p.div(p.sum(axis=1), axis=0)
    entropy = -(p * np.log2(p.where(p > 0, 1))).sum(axis=1)
    return {"sim_within_formal": np.mean(within), "sim_across_formal": np.mean(across),
            "n_roles": len(roles), "mean_role_purity": np.nanmean(purity),
            "entropy_specialists": entropy[entropy.index.isin(SPECIALISTS)].mean(),
            "entropy_caseworkers": entropy[entropy.index.str.contains("_Case")].mean()}

        "n": n, "closed_share": case_df.closed.mean(),

        "referral_rate": d[d.res_group == "tech"][CASE].nunique() / max(n, 1),

# Difference-in-differences (pilot vs control) with bootstrap CI
def did(metric_fn, n_boot=500, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    base = cases[cases.cohort.isin(["pre", "post"])]
    def est(df):
        m = df.groupby(["arm", "cohort"]).apply(metric_fn)
        return (m["pilot", "post"] - m["pilot", "pre"]) - (m["control", "post"] - m["control", "pre"])
    point = est(base)
    boots = [est(base.sample(frac=1, replace=True, random_state=rng.integers(1e9))) for _ in range(n_boot)]
    return point, np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])

print("DiD SLA miss:", did(lambda g: g.sla_miss.mean()))
print("DiD rework:", did(lambda g: g.rework.mean()))

**Notes on interpreting the analysis:**

- **Always report at the individual-resource level and the resource-group level side by side.** A drop in handovers that shows up only at the individual level, or only in a log containing pool IDs, is not evidence of a real reduction.
- **Completion-only timestamps.** The "wait after handover" then mixes waiting and service time. Add start timestamps or queue-entry events before go-live so that `insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `get_service_time` measure true queueing.
- **Report every KPI by team × complexity × specialty × customer segment, with confidence intervals.** East should be reported separately and never absorbed into an organization-wide average.
- **Judge the post cohort by start date and with survival estimates.** Early closed cases are biased toward simple ones. The `closed_share` column shows how much of each cohort is still unresolved.
