# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is not the result of one mechanism. It is a mixture of learning, case selection, queue pressure, batching, automation effects, and possibly fatigue or circadian influences. The key is that service time and error rate do not move together in a simple way. A falling service time can mean improvement, easier work, pressure-induced rushing, batching, or automation. A rising error rate can mean fatigue, pressure, poor case fit, automation failure, or delayed detection.

## Early shift: complex backlog and warm-up dominate

In the first sequence positions, service time is high and errors are moderately elevated. This should not be interpreted as fatigue. Workers are starting with inherited backlog, complex carryover cases, and a high share of high-priority work. The mean complexity is 0.72 in positions 1–5, compared with 0.43 in positions 76+. Seniors also appear to take complex cases early, especially after night or weekend backlog. Therefore, early slow performance is largely a case-mix and selection effect, combined with warm-up.

Errors in the first bin are not extremely high, likely because complex cases may receive more careful handling, senior resources may be involved, and workers have not yet entered high-pressure queue conditions. However, the early period is still risky because complex cases are being handled while resources are reorienting to the shift.

## Middle shift: apparent learning and stabilization

From positions 6–50, service time falls and error rates reach their lowest levels. This is the part of the curve most consistent with learning or warm-up. Workers are familiar with the queue, tools, and case flow. The case mix also becomes easier: complexity falls from 0.61 to 0.50, queue length is lower, and high-priority share declines.

This does not prove pure learning, because the cases are not randomly assigned. But the fact that error rates fall while service time falls is compatible with genuine stabilization. Seniors show this most clearly: they become faster later in the shift while maintaining low error rates under low WIP.

## Late shift: queue pressure and speed-quality tradeoff dominate

The most dangerous interpretation would be to say that late-shift workers are simply more skilled because their service time is lowest. The data do not support that simple story. In positions 51–75 and 76+, queue length rises sharply, service time continues to fall, and errors and rework increase substantially. This is the classic signature of queue pressure: workers speed up because the queue is high, but quality suffers.

The WIP/complexity table is especially important. Under high WIP, low-complexity cases are processed faster but with more errors. High-complexity cases under high WIP have the highest error rate: 10.9%. This means late-shift speed is not just learning. It is partly a response to pressure. Workers may be triaging faster, spending less time on verification, batching similar cases, relying more on automation, or deferring checks.

Fatigue may contribute, but the evidence is not strong enough to treat it as the primary cause. If fatigue were the dominant mechanism, we would expect late-shift degradation even when WIP and complexity are low. Instead, later low-WIP error rates are relatively stable, especially for seniors. Fatigue may still amplify pressure, particularly for juniors or during long stretches without breaks, but it is not sufficient alone.

## Circadian effects are plausible but confounded

Night shift performance differs from other shifts, but the difference is not clean evidence of circadian fatigue. Night staff handle fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, with high complexity share. The Night shift also starts with lower queue length but ends with higher queue length, suggesting that unresolved work accumulates. Time-of-day effects may exist, but they are entangled with case mix, staffing, and demand patterns.

## Batching and automation explain part of the late-speed pattern

The batching clues are important. Junior_H and Junior_I show consecutive Document Review events with very short service times. Quality Check is delayed to the end of the shift, producing correction bursts. These behaviors can make service time look better while increasing downstream error and rework.

Automation also changes the interpretation. Bot_Risk performs instant classification, but humans correct it more often when queues are high. If bot-assisted cases are processed quickly but require later correction, then measured service time improves while quality worsens. This is not simply worker carelessness; it is a system-level human-automation interaction.

## Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions

Service time falls for several different reasons:

1. Learning and familiarization.
2. Lower case complexity.
3. Selection of easier cases.
4. Pressure to clear high queues.
5. Batching of similar tasks.
6. Automation performing part of the work.

Errors rise only when the risk factors outweigh the benefits of speed. The highest-risk condition is high WIP combined with high complexity. Juniors are especially vulnerable under high pressure. Seniors are more resilient but still show elevated errors under high WIP. Therefore, the correct interpretation is conditional:

- Late speed with low WIP and stable errors is likely learning or efficient routine.
- Late speed with high WIP and rising errors is likely pressure, batching, or unsafe simplification.
- Early slowness with moderate errors is likely complex case mix and warm-up, not fatigue.
- Night-shift slowness may reflect complexity and urgency, not only circadian effects.

log["wip_bin"] = pd.cut(
    log["wip_at_start"],

)

    log["case_complexity_score"],
    bins=[-0.01, 0.5, 1.01],
    labels=["Low", "High"],
)

    log["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, 10_000],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
)

# Approximate late-shift flag for an 8-hour shift: last two hours
log["late_in_shift"] = log["minutes_since_shift_start"] >= 360

# Bot exposure flag, if Bot_Risk performed Risk Classification on the case
bot_cases = (
    log.loc[log["concept:name"] == "Risk Classification"]
    .loc[log["org:resource"] == "Bot_Risk", "case:concept:name"]
    .unique()
)

# 4. Intervention options

| Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk | How to monitor impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May reduce fatigue exposure and limit very long continuous work periods. Could reduce late-shift degradation if fatigue is present. | May increase handovers, inherited backlog, and waiting time. If queue pressure is unchanged, errors may move to the next shift. Can reduce continuity and increase staffing cost. | Error and rework rate in final hours before and after cap; queue length at shift end; inherited backlog at next shift; case waiting time; handover count; overtime; completion rate; WIP after cap. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift | May reduce early cognitive overload and allow workers to warm up before handling difficult cases. Could reduce early errors and long service times. | May delay important complex decisions, increase cycle time, and push complex work into later high-pressure periods. May harm customers if complex cases are urgent. May remove beneficial senior early ownership. | First-hour complexity share; first-hour service time and error rate; complex-case waiting time; complex-case SLA compliance; later-shift error rate; rework within 7 days; shift-end queue; customer escalation rate. |

Directly targets the strongest observed risk condition. | Alert fatigue if too frequent. Workers may feel monitored or pressured. If no capacity is added, alert alone may not improve outcomes. | 4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias | Reduces cherry-picking or unobserved selection. Makes performance comparisons fairer. Balances complexity across resources. Can reveal true resource and pressure effects. | Random assignment may put complex cases with underprepared resources. Can reduce speed and increase errors if not skill-constrained. May conflict with priority rules or customer commitments. | Complexity distribution by resource and experience; error rate by resource and complexity; service time; priority SLA compliance; rework; fairness metrics; exception rate; manual override rate; customer waiting time. |
| 5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours | May reduce fatigue and improve attention during the highest-risk period. # 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions are risky because the event log records outcomes of a system, not isolated individual behavior. Several issues make raw worker comparisons unfair.

## Selection bias

Cases are not randomly assigned. Early shifts inherit complex backlog. Seniors may take complex cases after night or weekend backlog. Night staff handle more urgent exceptions. If one resource receives harder cases, their raw error rate will look worse even if their skill is adequate. Any worker-level evaluation must adjust for complexity, priority, case age, activity family, WIP, and queue pressure.

## Queue pressure created by management

High WIP is often a system-level condition, not an individual failure. Staffing levels, shift design, arrival patterns, automation quality, delayed Quality Check, and backlog inheritance all create pressure. If workers are evaluated on speed and error rates simultaneously while the system places them under high WIP, they face a speed-quality tradeoff that management has designed.

## Measurement error

Service time can be misleading. Very short service times may reflect batching, automation, incomplete logging, or work performed outside the system. Error flags may capture detected errors, not all errors. If Quality Check is delayed, errors may appear late even if they occurred earlier. Rework within 7 days may be influenced by case complexity, customer behavior, and downstream resources.

## Automation quality

Bot_Risk can make human performance look better or worse depending on how errors are attributed. If the bot misclassifies risk and a human corrects it, the correction may appear as human rework. If workers rely on the bot under pressure, errors may rise without being caused by worker negligence. Automation should be evaluated as part of the process, not as a neutral background tool.

## Fatigue is not the only explanation

Fatigue may matter, but the data show that late-shift speed increases and error increases are strongly associated with queue pressure and complexity. Assuming fatigue alone can lead to wrong interventions, such as reducing shift length while leaving demand, staffing, and queue design unchanged.

## Punitive use of error rates

Punishing workers based on raw late-shift error rates is dangerous. It can encourage underreporting, hide systemic problems, damage trust, and increase turnover. Error rates should be used diagnostically, not punitively, unless there is strong evidence of intentional misconduct after fair adjustment for context.

## Recommended stance

Use performance analysis to improve the system: adjust WIP, balance case mix, improve automation, protect quality checks, provide breaks and senior support, and make queue pressure visible. Individual coaching should be based on adjusted, longitudinal, context-aware metrics and should include worker feedback and appeal mechanisms.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should monitor system health, not create a simplistic worker leaderboard. It should be viewable by shift, hour, activity, resource group, WIP band, complexity band, and priority.

| Indicator | Definition | Why it matters | Suggested alert or view |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Adjusted service time | Median and mean event service time by activity, shift, and sequence position, adjusted for complexity and WIP. | Raw service time can hide pressure, batching, or easier case mix. | Trend by position bin; alert if adjusted service time falls while error rate rises. |
| 2. Waiting time and case cycle time | Case waiting time, service time, and total cycle time using `insert_case_service_waiting_time`. | Long waiting time may indicate backlog, handover delays, or deferred checks. | Alert if waiting time increases after shift caps, QC deferral, or staffing changes. |
| 3. WIP and queue pressure | Mean WIP at start, queue length at start, share of events with WIP > 70, and duration above threshold. | High WIP is a key risk condition for errors and rework. | Alert when WIP > 70 for more than a defined duration; show queue trajectory by shift. |
| 4. Error rate and rework rate | Error flag rate and rework within 7 days, standardized by complexity, WIP, priority, and activity. | Raw error rates are confounded; adjusted rates are fairer. | Control chart with expected range; alert if adjusted error rate rises in high-WIP periods. |
| 5. Complexity mix | Mean complexity, high-complexity share, and high-priority share by hour, shift position, and resource group. | Early and night shifts may receive harder cases; complexity strongly affects time and errors. | Alert if first-hour complexity exceeds capacity or if juniors receive disproportionate high-complexity high-WIP work. |
| 6. Batching indicator | Share of events in detected batches, average batch size, short-service bursts, and batch-related rework rate. | Batching can reduce measured service time while increasing downstream errors. | Alert for large batches with elevated rework; view Junior_H/Junior_I Document Review pattern. |
| 7. Quality Check timing | Median time from prior activity to Quality Check, share of QC in last 45 minutes, and correction burst rate. | Delayed QC can create end-of-shift correction bursts and hide earlier errors. | Alert if QC backlog grows or last-45-minute correction rate rises. |
| 8. Handover and queue ownership changes | Number of case handovers across resources or shifts, inherited backlog ratio, and shift-end unresolved queue. | Handovers can delay work, lose context, and shift errors to later periods. | Alert if inherited backlog at shift start is high or if handover count rises after shift-length caps. |
| 9. Worker-load fairness | Distribution of high-complexity cases, high-priority cases, high-WIP exposure, and bot-correction work across resources; Gini index or variance. | Unequal exposure makes worker comparisons unfair and can overload some staff. | Alert if one group consistently receives harder or more pressured work. |
| 10. Automation correction rate | Share of Bot_Risk classifications later corrected by humans, by queue pressure and resource group. | Automation may reduce time but increase downstream correction. | Alert if bot correction rate rises when queue length > 70. |
| 11. Arrival-finish capacity gap | Arrival rate versus finish rate by shift and hour using `insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`. | Shows whether queues are structurally likely to grow. | Alert when arrival rate exceeds finish rate for multiple consecutive hours. |
| 12. Downstream rework paths | Performance DFG and path metrics from Risk Classification, Document Review, and Quality Check to Rework Correction. | Identifies where errors are generated and where they are detected. | Highlight paths with high rework probability, especially Bot_Risk-to-Rework paths. |

Dashboard operating rules:

1. Always show adjusted metrics next to raw metrics.
2. Do not use raw late-shift error rates for individual discipline.
3. Investigate alerts at the shift or system level before attributing issues to individuals.
4. Include worker context: experience group, shift, WIP, complexity, and automation exposure.
5. Track intervention effects over at least several weeks to avoid reacting to noise.
