# 2. Integrated explanation

The pattern is not a single learning curve, a single fatigue curve, or a single circadian curve. It is a changing mixture of mechanisms as the shift progresses, with feedback loops between them.

At shift start, the dominant forces are **case-mix, selection and warm-up**. The queue is inherited, not chosen: Early starts with mean queue 61, Late with 70, and the first positions have mean complexity 0.72 and 41% high priority. Seniors disproportionately receive complex carryover, especially after night or weekend backlog. First-hour high-complexity share is 48% on Early and 56% on Night. Those cases legitimately take longer (high complexity at low WIP already averages 22.4 min versus 12.7 min for low complexity) and have higher baseline error risk. On top of that there may be genuine re-familiarisation: after initial cases, service time falls and errors dip to 2.5% at positions 16-30. But this apparent learning is inseparable from stabilisation: as the shift proceeds to positions 31-50, complexity falls to 0.50, queue falls to 31, and priority falls to 25%. Easier cases, fewer interruptions, and a clearer queue would produce faster, more accurate work even with no individual learning.

In the middle of the shift, the system is in its most favourable regime: moderate complexity, moderate queue, warmed-up staff, and still intact checking behaviour. That is why errors are lowest there, not at the very start and not at the end.

Later, the mechanism changes rather than simply continuing. Demand peaks late on Day (queue ends at 75 compared with 38 at start), Late inherits Day backlog (70 to 66), and Night ends with a larger queue than it started (29 to 55). Queue length rises to 62 at positions 51-75 and 88 at 76+. At that point service time keeps falling, to 11.4 and then 10.8 min, but now this is mainly **pressure-driven compression**, not learning: WIP >70 halves the margin for checking, produces a speed-quality trade-off even for low-complexity cases (9.4 min, 5.8% error), and becomes a strong risk zone for high-complexity cases (16.8 min, 10.9% error). Fatigue and circadian strain plausibly lower the threshold at which workers rush, batch, defer Quality Check, or accept Bot_Risk output, but they operate through and with queue pressure. That is why seniors, who may have more margin, better triage, or easier early handling, remain comparatively stable (4.9% error under high WIP) while juniors reach 12.4% and mid-level staff 6.8%.

Batching and automation distort the measurement of this late regime. Document Review batches of 9-18 consecutive very short events make throughput look excellent while quality costs are deferred. Quality Check delayed to the last 45 minutes means error_flag at the original event may be recorded late or not at all, while rework spikes downstream. Bot_Risk adds instant classifications that humans under queue pressure correct more often, suggesting either poorer Bot performance under changed case mix or heavier human correction burden that is invisible in Bot service time.

Night shift shows that clock and case type matter independently: fewer events, more urgent exceptions, slower late service (15.2 min) and moderate errors (5.1%). That is inconsistent with a simple "late equals fast and careless" story and consistent with circadian slowing plus exception-driven complexity.

In short: early slowness is largely complex carryover plus warm-up; mid-shift improvement is stabilisation plus easier mix plus some learning; late speed is largely queue compression, batching, deferred checking and automation acceptance, amplified for some workers by fatigue and time-of-day effects. Errors rise only in some conditions because the dangerous combination is specific: high WIP, high complexity, late position, less experience, batching or deferred checking, and possibly night or demand-peak timing. Under low WIP, seniors and even juniors late in a shift retain low error rates, which a pure fatigue account cannot explain.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Within-resource comparisons

Estimate within-person change, not between-person league tables. For each resource, compare first 20 events versus later events, and early versus late position bands, separately by activity family and WIP band. Use resource fixed effects or random intercepts so a senior who receives complex carryover is not compared directly with a junior who receives routine cases. Report distributions, not only means, because batches create a left tail of implausibly short service times.

## Within-shift position effects

Treat sequence_position_within_shift and minutes_since_shift_start as non-linear exposures using binned position, splines, or piecewise terms. Test for a U-shaped error curve (high early due to complexity, low in middle, high late) and a monotonic service-time decline with different slopes early versus late. Include interaction terms position x WIP and position x complexity, because a late effect under low WIP should be interpreted differently from a late effect under WIP >70.

## WIP / queue stratification

Cross-tabulate service time, error and rework by WIP bands (<30, 30-70, >70), queue_length_at_start bands, complexity bands, and position bands. Use finer WIP thresholds to test whether 70 is a real breakpoint or a managerial convention. Examine lagged WIP: WIP at start predicting current error, and current error or rework predicting later WIP, to assess feedback and reverse causality.

## Complexity adjustment

Fit mixed models clustered by case, resource and shift-day for log service time and for error or rework. Core covariates: case_complexity_score, priority, activity_family, concept:name, wip_at_start, queue_length_at_start, sequence position, minutes since shift start, clock hour, shift_id, resource_experience_months, and interactions experience x WIP, experience x position, complexity x WIP. Also produce directly standardised rates: reweight each position band, shift and resource group to a common complexity-priority-activity distribution. Check propensity overlap before claiming adjusted comparisons are identified.

## Shift and time-of-day controls

Exploit overlaps between shifts. Compare Early and Day during 09:00-14:00, Day and Late during 14:00-17:00, and Late and Night after 22:00. Fit models with both clock hour and minutes since shift start, plus shift fixed effects, and estimate whether position effects survive within shift type. Analyse Night separately because its case mix is exception-driven and its late service time is slower, not faster.

## Batching detection

Flag runs of consecutive events by the same resource and activity, service times below an activity-specific percentile, and events sharing queue batch or arriving together. Compare in-batch versus singleton events for service time, error_flag, subsequent Quality Check delay and rework. Examine autocorrelation of service times within a resource sequence: batching produces strong positive autocorrelation of very short times followed by a burst of corrections.

## Resource-experience interactions

Estimate experience-group by WIP, experience-group by complexity, and experience-group by late-position interactions. The key hypothesis is not that juniors are uniformly worse, but that junior error risk is much more sensitive to high WIP and early complex assignment. Seniors should be benchmarked on the complexity they absorb, not rewarded for a favourable mix or penalised for a difficult one.

## Rework / error downstream analysis

Distinguish error_flag at the event from rework_flag and Rework Correction within 7 days, Quality Check timing, and case-level outcomes. Cases censored near the end of observation cannot have full 7-day follow-up. Use case performance filtering to compare fast-error cases, slow-accurate cases, and batched cases. Examine time from Risk Classification or Eligibility Decision to correction, and whether Bot corrections cluster at high queue.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions are fragile because the log records the outcome of an allocation system, not a controlled experiment. Seniors appear stable partly because they receive complex cases early, may control their queue, and may have discretion to batch or defer work. Juniors appear error-prone partly because they receive early complexity, late high WIP, and batching opportunities or pressures. Night staff handle fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, so their raw rates are not comparable with Day staff. Comparing raw late-shift error rates without adjustment would systematically penalise those assigned the hardest end of the system.

Measurement error is substantial. error_flag depends on detection, and detection depends on Quality Check timing. If Quality Check is deferred to the last 45 minutes, errors are undercounted early and overcounted in bursts. Service time depends on start_timestamp quality; batched Document Review events have implausibly short times that reflect workflow organisation, not individual speed. Bot_Risk service time is instant by design and must not be averaged with human work. Rework within 7 days is censored for recent cases and may reflect downstream capacity as much as upstream quality.

Queue pressure is partly created by management design: staffing levels, shift start times, demand peaks, assignment rules, the decision to let Day queue build to 75, and tolerance of WIP >70. It is ethically problematic to create or tolerate a high-pressure regime and then attribute the resulting errors solely to worker fatigue or diligence. Automation quality raises a related issue: if Bot_Risk classifications require more human correction when queue >70, penalising the correcting humans mislocates responsibility. Workers may also face automation bias, where accepting the Bot output under pressure is the expected behaviour, yet they are blamed when it is wrong.

Punitive use of error rates creates predictable harms: avoidance of complex cases, reluctance to do Quality Check or Rework Correction, underreporting, speed-up and batching to meet targets, and distrust of monitoring. Small numbers, especially for Night staff, make rankings statistically unstable. Fair monitoring should use adjusted expected errors, exposure time, case mix, WIP exposure, uncertainty intervals, and qualitative review of cases before any individual judgement. Worker representatives should be involved in defining thresholds, breaks, and alert responses. Health and circadian effects also require care: night and early shifts cannot be judged by the same raw standards as day work, and shift-length policies should consider recovery time, commuting, and caring responsibilities, not only event counts.

The dashboard should have a top tile row for WIP, complexity mix, waiting time, adjusted error and rework; a dotted chart for batching and end-of-shift bursts; performance directly-follows graphs for low versus high WIP; and drill-downs by shift, activity, resource group and clock hour. Threshold breaches should prompt a system response (capacity, reassignment, senior review, protected break, or pause on non-urgent work) and should be reviewed with worker representatives. No indicator should automatically trigger individual penalties.
