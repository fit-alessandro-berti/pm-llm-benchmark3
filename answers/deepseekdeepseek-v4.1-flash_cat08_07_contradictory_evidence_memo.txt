# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures how much observed behavior the discovered model can replay. Pilot fitness rose because many cases follow a simplified coordinator path, so the model explains observed traces well. Precision measures whether the model allows too much unrelated behavior. Pilot precision fell to 0.51 because the model permits many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start. Thus high fitness means “the observed mess is well described,” not “the process is standardized and controlled.”

**Improved median but worse tail duration.** Median improved because many simple cases benefited from combined validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership. The tail worsened because complex exceptions became slower: medium-risk apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, failed visits, second visits, manual activation, and billing-before-activation. High coordinator utilization and second-visit utilization created queues when exceptions occurred. The process became faster for routine cases but slower and more fragile for exceptions.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handover count fell because one coordinator owned each case after validation. That improved accountability for simple cases. But knowledge became concentrated: the working-together network centralized around coordinators and activation specialists, field-survey usage fell, and second-visit workload rose. When a coordinator was absent, context was not shared, so cases stalled. Fewer handovers improved coordination only while the coordinator was available.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.** Visible cost before complaint handling fell from €184 to €161, probably because routine cases moved faster. But manual activation, second visits, complaints, and billing disputes create downstream work. The estimate including complaint/revisit handling worsened to €207, and it is incomplete after week 4. The redesign likely shifted cost from front-end coordination to back-end rework and complaint handling.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings.** Simple low-risk houses have predictable installations, so skipping survey and merging tasks worked. Medium-risk apartments and old permit-sensitive buildings have hidden constraints. The predictive rule was often wrong, permit checks were missed, and activation-after-billing created disputes. Aggregate median improvement masks segment harm: apartments and old buildings saw p90, first-time-right, and complaints worsen materially.
