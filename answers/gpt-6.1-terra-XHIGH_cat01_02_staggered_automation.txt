## 1. What is observed

Alpha and Beta had the system available from April, Gamma from July, and Delta and Epsilon remained unexposed to branch availability through August. Availability is a branch-month policy indicator, not a case-level assignment. The recorded automated fast-path share rose from 4% to 25–48% in Alpha, 6% to 27–50% in Beta, and 4% to 25–32% in Gamma; a fast-path share is neither the proportion ever receiving automated triage nor an independently assigned dose. Nonzero pre-availability values require clarification of measurement, pilot use, or coding.

The first adopters were selected for high volume and historically poor cycle times; Gamma followed a readiness pilot. From April, routing increased incoming demand at Alpha and Beta, and it increased at Gamma after July. Product changes altered complexity; staffing changed at four branches; a shared fraud desk began in March; and managers could change work practices. Automation also routed somewhat more borderline cases to the desk. These changes coincide with adoption and learning.

The data contain monthly branch summaries, not case-level paths, timestamps, queue states, or survey response counts. Monthly medians and p90s cannot be pooled into overall case-level quantiles, and CSAT means may reflect changing respondent composition. Complexity is measured at registration, but its comparability and sufficiency for risk adjustment are unknown. The shared desk makes branch outcomes potentially dependent on other branches’ traffic.

## 2. Causal estimands

Effects below are differences between potential outcomes under specified policies, not estimates established by the table. A policy version must specify routing, training, staffing, and the other branches’ automation assignments. With interference, outcomes depend on the whole assignment vector; contrasts must hold others’ assignments fixed or specify a deployment regime.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: cohort-specific difference-in-differences / event study | April and July cohorts; estimate cohort-by-relative-month contrasts, then transparently average; no already-treated controls | Delta and Epsilon throughout; Gamma before July only for April-cohort comparisons where anticipation is excluded | Branch and calendar time by design; pre-rollout baseline complexity, volume and staffing if overlap permits; do not automatically condition on post-policy mediators | Cohort- and event-time availability effects, or bundled rollout effects if co-interventions are inseparable | Conditional parallel untreated trends; no anticipation; stable treatment versions; no differential concurrent shocks; no relevant interference; consistent outcome definitions | Plot pre-period gaps and event times, cohort/control-specific comparisons, placebo dates, overlap, and sensitivity to controls and windows; inspect desk traffic | Too few branches and pre-months to validate assumptions; routing, staff changes and shared queues make counterfactual trends implausible |
| Prospective randomized phased rollout | Randomly allocate first and later start dates, with specified exposure windows and standardized operational policies; preferably randomize more comparable units or desk-separated clusters | Delayed-start units during their untreated period | Prespecified baseline workload, risk mix and staffing for precision; measure co-interventions and network exposure | ITT-like effect of offered availability under the trial regime | Randomization and adherence to assignment; complete outcome observation; known spillover structure or isolated clusters | Balance, assignment integrity, uptake, spillovers, missingness and randomization inference; prespecified subgroup checks | Two remaining branches cannot support reliable branch-level inference alone; shared desk can contaminate controls |
| Controlled interrupted time series / comparative event study | Separate level and slope changes at each start, incorporating ramp-up | Delta and Epsilon time series, supplemented with historical data if obtainable | Calendar seasonality, pre-period trends and predetermined covariates; prespecified changes for common product/desk events | Local discontinuity or trend change in availability/bundle at adoption | Stable counterfactual trends and no other discontinuity at the start; sufficient observations; manageable serial correlation | Plot residuals, alternative break dates, placebo cutoffs, pre-fit and control series | Co-occurring April routing and July demand changes and only 8 months make a break non-specific |
| Matched case-level design, if new data available | Actual eligibility and assignment at triage; estimate dynamic effects by case cohort | Contemporaneous eligible manual cases within comparable branch, time and risk strata | Registration-time risk, demand, staffing and backlog, other pre-assignment features; no downstream variables | Effect of assigned automated versus manual triage among eligible cases | Conditional exchangeability, positivity, consistency and no unmeasured selection; specified network exposure | Check overlap, balance, assignment reasons and unmeasured-confounding sensitivity; follow cases to closure | Agents may select cases on unrecorded complexity; completed fast path is post-treatment selection |

The primary approach makes the staggered timing explicit and compares each cohort with branches still untreated at the relevant time. It avoids using already-treated branches as controls and gives separate results for the April and July cohorts and for exposure duration. This matters because uptake rises over time and early adopters were chosen for weak performance. It also displays how much the result changes when Delta and Epsilon are used separately. The resulting contrasts are transparent descriptive evidence, not automatically causal estimates.

The key counterfactual is how each adopter would have changed without availability under the specified routing, staffing and other-branch policies. January–March gives only three pre-months for Alpha and Beta, and March introduced a shared desk; Gamma’s readiness pilot may also make its pre-period partly exposed. Even apparently parallel pre-trends would not establish post-period parallel trends. April routing, staffing changes and the product change complicate comparison; their causal role must be specified before adjustment. If routing or staffing responds to the rollout, controlling for it can remove part of the total effect or introduce bias; if independently assigned, it may confound the comparison. Aggregate complexity is an incomplete proxy for case mix. With five branches, clustered standard errors and event-study leads have little reliability, and shared desk interference can alter both treated and control outcomes. A prospective randomized rollout and richer historical and case-level data are needed for a more credible policy effect.

## 5. Quantitative evidence

The table uses equal weights for branch-months: Alpha and Beta pre = January–March, post = April–August; Gamma pre = April–June, post = July–August. Each cell is the change in the mean of monthly summaries, post minus pre; rework, escalation and fast path are percentage points, CSAT is scale points, and cycle times are hours. These are descriptive comparisons of different case mixes, not pooled case-level quantiles or causal effects.

| adopter | median Δ h | p90 Δ h | rework Δ pp | escalation Δ pp | CSAT Δ | fast-path Δ pp |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alpha | -0.40 | +9.65 | +0.54 | +2.91 | +0.021 | +34.0 |
| Beta | +1.86 | +9.81 | -0.56 | +2.53 | -0.016 | +34.0 |
| Gamma | -6.47 | -9.27 | -1.23 | +1.17 | +0.140 | +24.5 |

For the same early windows, the equal-weight Delta/Epsilon changes are +5.22 h median, +16.05 h p90, +1.92 pp rework, +1.82 pp escalation and -0.084 CSAT points. Subtracting these from each adopter’s change gives Alpha -5.62 h median and -6.39 h p90, and Beta -3.36 h median and -6.24 h p90. For Gamma’s windows, Delta/Epsilon change by -2.63 h median and -2.22 h p90; Gamma’s corresponding contrasts are -3.84 h and -7.05 h. These are differences in changes, not estimated percent effects: the counterfactual levels and trends are unknown.

As an intensity description, Alpha’s fast-path share increased 23 pp from April to August while its median fell 5.0 h and p90 rose 3.9 h; Beta’s share increased 23 pp, median fell 2.3 h and p90 rose 3.6 h. Gamma’s share rose 7 pp from July to August, with median down 2.4 h and p90 down 4.4 h. Calendar time, demand, risk and staffing also changed. Fast-path completion selects cases and excludes automated escalations, so neither these changes nor an intensity regression identifies a dose response or the effect of assigning an individual case to automation.

Control choice gives a limited sensitivity exercise, not a confidence or causal bound. Using Delta alone versus Epsilon alone, the difference-in-changes ranges are: Alpha median -6.39 to -4.84 h and p90 -7.86 to -4.93 h; Beta median -4.13 to -2.58 h and p90 -7.71 to -4.77 h; Gamma median -4.45 to -3.23 h and p90 -8.22 to -5.88 h. These ranges omit uncertainty and shared biases. A different window changes the picture: Alpha April–August raw median change is -5.0 h, but p90 is +3.9 h; Beta is -2.3 h and +3.6 h. Thus the sign of a raw tail change depends on the comparison. There is no data-derived finite causal bound from these exercises.

Pre-trends are short and uneven: January–March median changes were -1.0 h Alpha, +1.0 Beta, +3.2 Gamma, +1.8 Delta and -1.2 Epsilon. The March desk launch complicates even that check. Early branches were selected for poor historical performance, which can produce regression to the mean if their selection reflected transient delays; their later relative improvement can therefore occur without automation. The table alone cannot distinguish that possibility from a policy effect or other operational changes.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**expand with an embedded evaluation**

Make Delta and Epsilon the next two candidates, but randomly select one to start first and schedule the other for a delayed start. Keep the initial period operationally supported, with a prespecified treatment version, staff training and escalation rules. Randomize additional comparable service teams or eligible cases where feasible, ideally within desk-isolated clusters; two branch assignments alone cannot deliver a precise or robust branch-level estimate. If the shared desk cannot be isolated, record exposures from every branch and assess the entire network as well as each branch. Do not silently change routing, staffing or risk thresholds at the same time; document unavoidable changes and analyze the actual policy bundle. Preserve capacity to revert or slow exposure if guardrails trigger.

Collect at least three months of stable pre-rollout data for both branches, preferably 6–12 months including seasonal history, and at least three months after the first start before the second branch begins. Continue measuring each branch for at least six months after its own start, including case follow-up to capture delayed rework and satisfaction. These are minimum observation windows, not a guarantee of statistical power; use historical variation, eligible-case volumes and the randomization design to assess precision before fixing the scale-up threshold.

Capture case identifiers, registration risk and source, eligibility, assigned and actual triage, timestamps for each activity, referrals, overrides, repeats, closure, reopenings and audit results. Measure daily incoming and completed work, productive staffing by skill, queue lengths, age, wait and service time at the branch and fraud desk, plus referral origin and destination. Track survey invitations, responses and respondent characteristics. Monitor outcomes in already-treated and delayed branches to detect spillovers, and compare desk workload and waits under each phase.

Prespecify guardrails for p90 and severe-wait rates, error or inappropriate closure, reopens, complaints and representative customer feedback, with weekly review and a named owner. Set numerical thresholds from baseline variability, service commitments and capacity before randomization; investigate and pause or reverse exposure when a threshold is breached, especially for high-risk groups. Scale up only if the prespecified randomized availability comparison shows a practically meaningful median or throughput benefit without exceeding any tail, quality or customer-harm guardrail, network capacity remains adequate, and uncertainty is narrow enough to exclude a consequential loss. Otherwise extend measurement or redesign the workflow.

```python
    print("\n", adopter, "pre/post changes and difference in changes")
    print(pd.DataFrame({"adopter_change": treated, "control_change": control,
                        "difference_in_changes": treated - control}).round(3).to_string())

# First adoption date and cohort-relative time, measured in calendar months.
first = df.loc[df.automation.eq(1)].groupby("branch")["month"].min()
df["adoption_month"] = df["branch"].map(first)
df["relative_month"] = (
    (df["month"].dt.year - df["adoption_month"].dt.year) * 12
    + df["month"].dt.month - df["adoption_month"].dt.month
).astype("Int64")
for k in range(-3, 5):
    label = f"lead_{-k}" if k < 0 else f"event_{k}"
    df[label] = df["relative_month"].eq(k).fillna(False).astype(int)
print("\nRelative time by branch and month (NA = never treated):")
print(df.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values="relative_month").to_string())

# Assumptions needed for causal interpretation include exchangeable untreated
# trends, no anticipation, no confounding co-interventions, consistent treatment
# versions, and no relevant interference. They are not established here.
# A simple TWFE coefficient can combine heterogeneous staggered effects with
# problematic weights, so the model below is descriptive and not an event study.
# Post-availability workload, risk and staffing may be mediators or confounders;
# they are not automatically adjusted for without a causal model.
df["month_label"] = df["month"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m")
for outcome in outcomes:
    for name, rhs, term in [
        ("descriptive binary TWFE", "automation", "automation"),
        ("descriptive intensity association per 1 pp", "automated_fast_path_pct", "automated_fast_path_pct"),
    ]:
        model = smf.ols(f"{outcome} ~ {rhs} + C(branch) + C(month_label)", data=df).fit(
            cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"]})
        print(f"{name}: {outcome}: coefficient={model.params[term]:.4f}, "
              f"cluster_SE={model.bse[term]:.4f}, n={int(model.nobs)}")
```

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Fast-path shares increased after availability in the three adopters; early adopters’ raw p90 times rose across the specified pre/post windows.
- Suggestive: Adopters’ median and p90 changes were lower than those of the never-adopting branches in the stated comparisons, though the outcome trajectories and operational changes differ.
- Conditionally causal: A cohort-specific comparison could estimate a specified availability effect if untreated trends were parallel, co-interventions and anticipation absent or appropriately handled, and shared-desk interference controlled; these conditions are not demonstrated.
- Not supported: “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a general, precise causal claim or a guarantee that expansion improves customer outcomes.
