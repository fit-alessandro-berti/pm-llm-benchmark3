# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed curve contains at least two different kinds of speed-up.

**Early in a shift, slower service is not necessarily poor performance.** Workers inherit complex carryover, high-priority work, and a queue from the previous shift. Seniors also selectively take difficult cases. At the same time, workers may be warming up, and juniors may still be acquiring durable skills. As the backlog stabilizes, complexity and queue length fall, and workers become more fluent with the current work. These changes plausibly explain why service time and errors initially improve together. The aggregate pattern cannot tell how much of that improvement is warm-up, durable learning, easier cases, changing activity mix, or reduced queue pressure.

**The middle of the shift resembles a comparatively stable operating region.** By positions 16–50, service time is around 13 minutes, complexity and queue length are lower, and error rates remain near 2.5–2.7%. The small difference between those bins should not be overinterpreted without confidence intervals and denominators.

**Late in the shift, speed increasingly coincides with pressure.** At positions 51–75 and 76+, mean queue length rises to **62 and 88**, while service time falls to **11.4 and 10.8 minutes**. Errors rise to **4.6% and 7.9%**, and seven-day rework to **8.2% and 13.7%**. Because complexity continues to fall, “late cases are simply harder” is not an adequate explanation. The WIP-by-complexity results show the same basic speed–quality pattern within both broad complexity bands.

This late speed can combine genuine fluency with less desirable mechanisms: rushing, parallel task switching, batch completion, deferred review, shorter documentation, or skipping discretionary checks. Some of those mechanisms reduce recorded service time without reducing total work. Delayed Quality Check can also move error detection into the final shift segment. Thus, the late error increase should be investigated both as a real quality problem and as a possible detection-timing artifact. The increase in downstream seven-day rework makes it unsafe to dismiss the pattern as measurement error alone.

**Experience and workload help explain why errors rise only in some conditions.** Later low-WIP error rates do not show a general late-shift explosion. Under high WIP, however, every group deteriorates, with juniors showing the largest absolute increase: from **4.4% to 12.4%**. Seniors are comparatively resilient but not immune. This supports a workload-by-capability interaction rather than a universal fatigue curve.

Shift patterns reinforce that interpretation. Early clears its queue from 61 to 42 and ends with relatively slow service and a 4.2% error rate. Day’s queue grows to 75, and its final hours are fast with a 7.5% error rate. Late inherits a large Day backlog and shows a similar pattern. Night is slower and has fewer, more exceptional cases. Fatigue and circadian effects may still contribute—especially by reducing resilience under pressure—but neither explains the full pattern by itself.

The most defensible explanation is therefore a **time-varying mixture of warm-up, possible durable learning, changing case and activity mix, queue-driven speed–quality tradeoffs, experience-dependent resilience, batching and detection practices, and residual time-of-day or fatigue effects**. A short service time is not, by itself, evidence of improved productivity; it must be evaluated together with waiting, errors, rework, and quality-check coverage.

- observed service time and error/rework rates;
- rates standardized to a common complexity, priority, activity, and resource mix;
- rates additionally standardized to a common WIP and clock-time distribution.

A decomposition or standardized-prediction analysis can then show how much of the early-to-late service change is associated with case-mix/resource composition, how much with WIP, and how much remains in the elapsed-time component. The residual should not automatically be labeled fatigue.

**Shift and time-of-day controls**

- Include shift type, unique shift instance, clock hour, day of week, weekend/night backlog, and previous-shift ending queue where available.
- Compare Early and Day workers during overlapping morning hours, and Day and Late workers during overlapping afternoon hours, while accounting for different elapsed positions.
- Use same-worker rotations where possible.
- Recognize that Night clock-time effects are only weakly separable from Night staffing and case mix without additional schedule overlap or a suitable cross-over design.

**Learning, fatigue, and experience interactions**

- **Durable learning:** test whether performance improves with cumulative prior cases and experience months, persists across shifts, and is activity-specific.
- **Warm-up:** test whether the first-events penalty resets at each shift start even for experienced workers, after adjustment.
- **Fatigue:** derive rest since the previous shift, consecutive workdays, overtime, and actual shift length. Activity gaps should not be assumed to be breaks without verification.
- **Pressure vulnerability:** test WIP-by-experience and elapsed-time-by-WIP interactions. A worker can be experienced and still make more errors when assigned an unsafe workload.

**Batching and workarounds**

- Summarize discovered batches by activity, resource, shift, position, and WIP. Specifically test large late-Day Document Review batches and end-of-shift Quality Check bursts.
- Re-run service-time results excluding batched events, then with batch indicators, and finally using batch-level total effort where timestamps permit.
- Compare waiting time before batched work with service-time savings. A batch can look efficient locally while delaying cases.
- Separate error origination from detection. If Quality Check detects an earlier Document Review error, attribute it to the originating activity where traceability allows.

**Downstream error and rework analysis**

- Trace Rework Correction back to the case, originating activity, responsible operating conditions, and detection activity.
- Compare rework loops, Customer Callbacks, Quality Check delays, total case duration, and total service plus waiting—not just initial service time.
- Use `get_rework_cases_per_activity` descriptively, but implement the exact seven-day definition separately.
- Examine whether high-WIP cases have more downstream corrections even after case-mix adjustment, and whether end-of-shift Quality Check merely delays detection or increases downstream harm.
- Use arrival/finish rates and shift-start/shift-end queues to check whether local speed improvements merely transfer backlog to another shift.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Main risks | Event-log monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers** | May reduce overtime-related fatigue and extreme exposures; could improve predictability and rest. | Late problems already occur within ordinary shifts, so a cap alone does not address WIP. A uniform cap may reduce Night or peak coverage, increase handovers, and transfer work to other staff. | Shift-length distribution and overtime; rest between shifts; high-WIP exposure; handovers and post-handover waiting; adjusted errors/rework; queue at shift end. Compare capped and uncovered periods with demand controls. |
| **2. Move complex cases away from the first hour** | Could reduce early congestion if complex cases are being started without appropriate capacity. | Early work is already relatively low-error, and seniors may deliberately clear difficult backlog. Blanket deferral can increase case age, delay urgent cases, and push complexity into later high-WIP hours. | Complexity- and priority-adjusted waiting; backlog age; first-hour service/errors; complex-case completion; later WIP; downstream rework. Do not judge success by faster early service alone. |
| **3. Add a queue-pressure alert at WIP > 70** | Directly targets the observed high-risk zone, especially high-complexity work and junior staff. It can trigger load balancing, senior assistance, or temporary redirection of new work. | An alert without capacity relief changes little. Other risks include alert fatigue, gaming WIP, deferring work to create hidden waiting, and treating 70 as a universal cliff. | Time-weighted and event-level WIP exposure; percentage of work above 70; alert acknowledgment and response time; staffing/load action taken; errors and rework by WIP, complexity, and experience; waiting and backlog-age guardrails. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases** | Randomization **within clinically/operationally eligible skill and priority strata** could improve causal evaluation and reduce selective assignment in a limited pilot. | Blanket randomization can create unsafe skill mismatches, ignore urgency and specialization, overload juniors, and worsen service. It does not remove queue pressure, worker self-paced selection, or all measurement problems. | Assignment probabilities and overlap; skill–case mismatches; complexity/priority by worker; waiting, errors, rework, escalations, and workload fairness. Prefer logging assignment reasons and a restricted pilot over blanket randomization. |
| **5. Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours** | A plausible, relatively low-cost test of recovery and reduced cognitive overload, particularly if breaks are staggered and coverage is maintained. | If breaks simply remove capacity, WIP may rise and offset any benefit. Adherence and timing matter; the current data do not establish fatigue as the dominant mechanism. | Verified break timing; within-worker before/after comparisons; WIP during and after breaks; adjusted errors/rework; service and waiting; overtime. Use a stepped-wedge or otherwise phased evaluation rather than a raw pre/post comparison. |
| **6. Defer low-priority Quality Checks to the next shift** | Carefully governed deferral of genuinely low-risk checks might smooth end-of-shift correction bursts if the next shift has reserved capacity. | It can delay detection, increase customer harm and rework, create a new backlog, or artificially lower late error counts. Low priority does not necessarily mean low quality risk. It must not become a reduction in Quality Check coverage. | Required-QC coverage must remain complete; QC age and SLA compliance; next-shift queue and ownership; delayed detection; downstream rework and customer impact; last-45-minute correction bursts. Do not remove checks merely to improve throughput. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during Day demand peaks** | Well targeted to the observed system problem. Could reduce high-WIP exposure, support juniors, review complex/automated exceptions, and reduce the backlog inherited by Late. | Cost, senior-review bottlenecks, and changed case selection. Seniors also have higher errors under high WIP, so senior staffing is not an unlimited safeguard. Day-only support may not address Late or Night risks. | Day WIP trajectory and ending queue; senior review utilization and waiting; junior high-WIP errors; complexity-adjusted rework; Bot_Risk correction audit; handovers and Late inherited backlog. Pilot at predicted peaks with predefined capacity triggers. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher raw late-shift error rates** | No defensible quality benefit has been established. Any apparent improvement could simply reflect changed reporting or case selection. | Strong risk of unfairly penalizing juniors, Night staff, and workers assigned high-WIP or complex work. It encourages underreporting, cherry-picking, rushed closure, Quality Check deferral, and avoidance of difficult cases. | Do not implement raw-rate penalties. If supportive review is needed, monitor data completeness, case avoidance, QC coverage, detection rates, rework, workload exposure, and adjusted system-level outcomes. Raw error rankings should not be disciplinary evidence. |

The best-supported initial operational test is **an alert linked to real load relief, combined with targeted senior capacity during Day peaks**, while guarding against increased waiting and backlog displacement. Micro-breaks and shift-length policies can be tested separately, but should not be credited with improvements caused by simultaneous staffing changes.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Worker-level raw comparisons are vulnerable to selection bias.** Seniors appear slow partly because they take complex early backlog cases. Juniors may receive different activities and experience much greater high-WIP exposure. Night staff have smaller samples and more urgent exceptions. These are not like-for-like comparisons.
- **Aggregation can reverse or manufacture apparent effects.** Activity mix, resource composition, case complexity, and queue conditions all change with position. Even adjusted estimates can retain unobserved-confounding and assignment bias. Position itself is partly a consequence of worker speed and batching.
- **Measurement error is operational, not merely statistical.** Start and completion timestamps may represent administrative booking rather than continuous effort. Overlapping tasks and bulk completion can understate service time. Error flags may measure detection intensity rather than error generation. Rework may reflect new information or policy changes, and cases without full seven-day follow-up require special handling.
- **Queue pressure is substantially created by the system.** Staffing decisions, arrival demand, assignment rules, backlog carryover, automation exceptions, and delayed Quality Check policies are management-controlled conditions. It is inappropriate to convert exposure to those conditions into an individual character or effort judgment.
- **Automation quality needs independent scrutiny.** Instant bot service is not automatically productive or correct. Human corrections may be desirable safeguards, and penalizing overrides could increase inappropriate automation acceptance. Conversely, a high correction rate should not automatically be blamed on reviewers without assessing model drift, input quality, and case mix.
- **Punitive use of error rates changes behavior and corrupts the metric.** Workers may avoid complex cases, conceal errors, delay Quality Check, or close work prematurely. Small denominators and regression to the mean make individual rankings especially unstable. Risk-adjusted rates can still be unfair and should not become automatic disciplinary scores.
- **Quality measurement must be protected.** Interventions should preserve required Quality Check coverage and monitor downstream harm. A lower detected-error rate accompanied by less checking is not an improvement.
- **Evaluation should be transparent and participatory.** Workers should know what is logged and how it is used. Restrict access to identifiable data, suppress small cells, consult worker representatives on monitoring and interventions, and evaluate system-level staffing and queue policies alongside individual support needs.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should show observed and case-mix-standardized results, numerators and denominators, confidence intervals where appropriate, and data-completeness warnings. Every speed indicator should be paired with waiting and quality indicators.

| Indicator | Definition and recommended cuts | Purpose and guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Service time** | Median and p90 active minutes from `start_timestamp` to `time:timestamp`; retain mean for comparability. Cut by activity family, shift, elapsed time/position, WIP × complexity, and experience group. Show observed and standardized values; flag batches and automation separately. | Detects warm-up and speed-up without treating all speed as beneficial. Prevent comparisons across unlike activities or inclusion of Bot_Risk’s zero durations in human averages. |
| **2. Waiting time and end-to-end flow** | Waiting before each activity, case waiting total, case completion time, Quality Check delay, and p90 backlog age. Cross-check with `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; inspect performance DFGs for complete cases. | Reveals whether short service or batching merely shifts delay elsewhere. Include open-case backlog age so completed-case metrics do not hide long-running cases. |
| **3. WIP and queue pressure** | Current WIP/queue, time-weighted mean and p90, percentage of work and worker-minutes above WIP 70, queue at shift start/end, and high-WIP high-complexity volume. | Supports live load management and retrospective adjustment. Show alert response and waiting/backlog guardrails to prevent gaming by deferral. |
| **4. Error rate and detection quality** | Errors per eligible event or case, separately reporting originating-activity errors, Quality Check detections, and human automation overrides. Provide WIP-, complexity-, activity-, and experience-stratified rates with intervals. | Distinguishes error generation from detection. Avoid individual league tables and suppress unstable small cells. |
| **5. Seven-day rework and downstream burden** | Cases with Rework Correction within the defined seven-day window divided by cases with full follow-up; time to rework, number of rework loops, rework service hours, and subsequent Customer Callbacks. | Tests whether faster service creates downstream cost. Display results by originating conditions, not only by the worker who performs the correction. |
| **6. Complexity and priority mix** | Mean and distribution of complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, activity mix, carryover share, and urgent-exception share. Show first-hour and late-shift mix by shift and resource group. | Makes selection visible and supplies the standardization basis. Also report overlap so unsupported adjusted comparisons are flagged. |
| **7. Batching and workaround indicators** | Number and size of discovered batches; percentage of events in batches of at least nine; near-zero-service share; overlapping-start and synchronized-completion rates; Document Review batch runs; Quality Checks and corrections in the final 45 minutes. | Separates potentially efficient batching from timestamp artifacts and risky deferral. Pair batch size with waiting, errors, and rework rather than banning batches automatically. |
| **8. Handover and queue-ownership churn** | Cross-shift case continuations, repeated work after resource reassignment, ownership changes within the same work item, distinct resources per case, and waiting after handover. Exclude designed activity-to-activity handoffs where possible; add an explicit owner field if necessary. | Detects costs of shift caps, deferral, and load balancing. Monitor post-handover errors and duplicate work, not just handover counts. |
| **9. Worker-load fairness** | Within comparable roles and shifts: complexity/activity-standardized workload per available hour, high-WIP exposure, complex-case share, overtime, Night/weekend work, rest intervals, and handovers. Summarize dispersion and extreme loads rather than ranking raw case counts. | Identifies whether some workers systematically receive harder or more pressured work. Use for staffing and support, not raw productivity discipline. |
| **10. Demand, capacity, and backlog transfer** | Arrival rate, finish rate, staffed hours, open cases, queue age, and queue change from shift start to end, using arrival/finish-rate enrichment where helpful. | Distinguishes processing improvement from demand reduction and prevents one shift from appearing successful by transferring backlog to the next. |
| **11. Quality Check coverage and timeliness** | Percentage of required checks completed, median/p90 time to Quality Check, deferred checks completed within SLA, next-shift QC backlog, and detection yield. | Ensures throughput interventions do not silently remove or indefinitely delay quality control. |
| **12. Automation interaction** | Bot_Risk volume, correction/override rate, independently audited disagreement rate, input completeness, model version, reviewer, WIP, complexity, and downstream rework. Separate inappropriate acceptance from inappropriate override. | Monitors automation drift and human oversight without treating every correction as a worker or bot failure. |

Recommended cadence is near-real-time for WIP, demand, and staffing; end-of-shift review for service, waiting, batching, handovers, and Quality Check timeliness; and rolling daily or weekly review for matured error and seven-day-rework outcomes. PM4Py dotted charts, event-distribution graphs, batch discovery, and performance DFGs can provide drill-downs, while statistical standardization and uncertainty estimation should sit alongside them. The dashboard should prioritize system conditions and workload fairness over identifiable worker rankings.
