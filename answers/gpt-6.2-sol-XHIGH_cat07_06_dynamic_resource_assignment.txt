# 1. Objectives and constraints

Get cases to the right person in time, with as little avoidable waiting, rework, and transfer of ownership as possible. Judge throughput by **completed, correct work**, not by how quickly the queue is assigned. Also protect workers' capacity, create supervised learning opportunities, and keep enough qualified coverage to handle a surge or an absence.

- **Safety and authorization are non-negotiable.** Skill 0 means no execution, even with a mentor. Never assign a high or urgent case below level 2 without approved supervision; this policy also requires supervision for level 1 on routine work. Security requires level 3, or level 1–2 with explicit supervision by Gita or Hugo. For C001, treat the stated specialist requirement as requiring direct level-3 execution. Final Approval must be finalized by an authorized level-2 or level-3 worker; a level-1 worker may prepare material but may not finalize, even under supervision.
- **Operational guardrails.** Count at most four distinct work items per worker in the batch, including retained cases and booked supervision or trainee work. Check actual availability for all participants, not just the number of open cases. Quality Check can be batched only for normal/low cases that still have more than 24 hours at release; dispatch individually if waiting for the batch would put a case at risk. Avoid adding work to anyone flagged high overtime risk, including work on cases they already own. The only exception is an urgent SLA at risk with no safer feasible option; document the exception without waiving authorization, the batch cap, or working-time limits.
- **Preferences, not absolutes.** Favor a qualified owner when a complex case is expensive to transfer, and require a clear reason to move a case already reassigned twice or more. Aim for at least 20% of normal/low work items to include real work aligned with a documented training priority, provided the trainee and mentor can do it without unacceptable SLA risk. Share effort fairly and prefer not to book both a scarce specialist and their backup at the same time. A lower open-case count alone is not a reason to transfer a case.

The reported 2.7-hour handover wait and 3.3-hour weak-skill service penalty are historical medians for different effects, not constants to add to every case. Likewise, the 1.8× rework and 1.4× appeal rates beyond two reassignments justify extra scrutiny, not a conclusion that the transfers caused all the extra rework or appeals.

# 2. Assignment policy

1. **Build eligible options.** For each ready item, consider retaining the owner, moving to another authorized worker, and a supervised pair where permitted. Record who owns the item, who performs it, who supervises, and who signs off. Eliminate options that violate skill, sign-off, supervision, or batch rules. Calculate each person's usable time from their shift calendar and remaining hours, subtracting existing commitments not already reflected in that figure. Book real time for the transfer, execution, mentoring and sign-off, and do not double-book anyone. Do not treat an overnight gap as working time.
2. **Score urgency and feasibility together.** For each option, estimate a conservative finish time from its first feasible slot, context-transfer delay, and the time needed to do and review the work. Include remaining downstream steps if the deadline is for the full case. Calculate `slack = SLA deadline − conservative finish`; where data permit, estimate miss probability and expected lateness as well. Schedule urgent/high work first where the risk warrants it, emphasizing least slack and greatest avoidable lateness rather than sorting on priority alone. Between cases at comparable risk, use ready time to keep older work from starving. With sparse data, use conservative ranges and confirm slots with workers rather than inventing precise ETAs.
3. **Rank schedules that protect deadlines.** First minimize avoidable urgent/high breaches and preserve acceptable buffers on other work. If the training target can be met within those buffers, require it of the shortlisted plans. For the remaining choices, use a weighted sum of `flow + fit + continuity − load + development + coverage` (each component scaled to 0–1, with its own weight). The following scoring rubric makes the direction and basis of each component explicit. Version the weights and buffers, agree on them with workers and service leads, and test how rankings change when they are varied.

   | Component | What changes the score |
   |---|---|
   | SLA and flow | Score lower expected time to finish correctly higher among safe options. Keep the urgency and buffer checks above separate: a training option that uses another case's buffer is not safe just because the training case itself has slack. |
   | Skill match | Score an adequate routine match as fully satisfactory; on difficult or repeat work, rate a capable specialist above an otherwise equivalent competent worker. For a pair, rate the permitted division of work and actual oversight, not the trainee's score in isolation. |
   | Continuity | Score qualified retention highest; decrease the score for a transfer as handover band, complexity and past transfers rise, with an extra penalty when the new count would exceed two. Protect high-cost, high-complexity ownership particularly strongly. If transfer is necessary, use an acknowledged handoff with the history, next action, and a named customer contact. |
   | Fairness and overtime | Increase the load penalty with projected effort relative to staffed capacity, including carryover cases, complexity and mentoring, and with overtime risk and concentrated high-strain work. Exclude high-risk workers unless the urgent exception applies. |
   | Development | Credit a normal/low item once if it gives a worker documented practice with a named mentor, time for feedback, and defined limits on what the trainee may do. Log why a seat could not be offered if the target cannot safely be met. |
   | Resilience | Lower the coverage score when a plan consumes the last viable backup or fills a scarce skill's calendar with work that can go elsewhere. Check coverage by time window and test whether near-term Security and Approval work can still be handled if a key worker is absent. Use reserved capacity for genuine urgency rather than holding it idle through a preventable miss. |

4. **Commit and replan.** Compare options across the batch before fixing the next slots: one assignment can remove the mentor or backup another case needs. For otherwise tied choices, prefer the qualified current owner, then the lower projected workload relative to capacity, then a published rotation with a stable ID as the last fallback. Record the selected option, reason, meaningful alternative rejected, and any exception. On an absence, late handoff, blocked dependency, or lost SLA buffer, replan unstarted items first rather than automatically transferring work already in progress. If no legal timely option exists, escalate immediately for qualified coverage, reprioritization or an SLA/customer decision; do not bypass a rule to make the dashboard look green.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

Compare the literal baselines first for rule violations, then compare deployable versions using the same authorization and capacity rules, case arrivals, calendars, and starting backlog. First-available as stated can pick a level-1 approver or a non-specialist for Security without supervision. For example, Jonas starts with the fewest open cases and has level-1 Approval; if he still has the lowest eligible-by-that-rule count and enough time when C002 is considered, the literal rule can choose him. Specialist-only avoids that particular mismatch but does not by itself enforce the cap or manage overtime and handovers. The table gives expected tendencies, not measured effects.

| Dimension | First-available | Specialist-only | Dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Throughput | Quick dispatch and potentially short waits; extra service time and rework can erode completed throughput. | Often fast once difficult work starts, but queues at scarce skills can offset that gain. | Uses competent workers for routine work and specialists where they matter most; handoffs, mentoring and reserves consume some short-term capacity. |
| SLA risk | A low open count says little about a feasible finish or remaining work. | Good execution on hard cases, but urgent cases can get stuck behind routine work for the same specialist. | Protects slots based on slack and sign-off needs; relies on fit-for-purpose estimates and active replanning. |
| Error/rework risk | Highest mismatch concern in the literal rule; fast dispatch can lead to repeat work. | Likely fewer immediate skill errors, but transfers and queues can still contribute to rework. | Combines competence and supervision with continuity; still needs to monitor difficult level-2 work and trainee outcomes. |
| Workload fairness | Spreads counts more readily than effort; can repeatedly pick the same apparently free worker. | Concentrates difficult and high-strain work on specialists even with the open-count tie-break. | Accounts for complexity, carried work, mentoring and paid time; requires workers to be able to correct inaccurate snapshots. |
| Development | A trainee may get work by chance without a learning plan. | Few opportunities to build qualified substitutes. | Books protected practice and feedback where safe, and records when coverage prevents it. |
| Ownership continuity | Often moves a case simply because someone else has fewer open cases. | Can move a case away from an adequate owner to get a higher skill score. | Retains owners such as Celine on C007 and Eva on C016; makes necessary transfers explicit. |
| Resilience | Appears flexible, but availability is not the same as qualified coverage. | Vulnerable to absence or a spike at scarce skills; Farid is the only level-3 approver. | Preserves backup windows and develops coverage over time, at the cost of not filling every immediate slot. |

First-available can be sensible for interchangeable routine work once restricted to legal options; specialist-first can be sensible for an unusually costly error. Check the trade-offs in a historical dispatch replay or a monitored pilot: compare correct completions per staffed hour, missed deadlines, transfers, overload and learning opportunities together. Stress-test a Security surge and an absence in Approval. Do not infer which policy wins from a single queue snapshot.

# 5. Explanation to workers

We first check who is authorized to do the work and who has time to finish it safely. We try to keep a case with the person who knows it, especially when the customer would have to repeat their story or the case has already changed hands. Sometimes a deadline, a specialist requirement, or a full schedule means we need someone else. If that happens, we'll explain why and make time to pass on the case history.

We count the work behind the cases, including complex work and mentoring, not just the number of cases beside someone's name. A workload flag is a reason to protect your time, not a judgment of your performance. Training comes with a named person to help and time for feedback. If we need to defer it, we'll tell you why and look for another opportunity. You can flag a bad estimate, an outdated skill record, or an assignment that doesn't fit your capacity. A dispatcher will review it with you; we'll keep a record of any change and its reason.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Keep a decision record for each ready work item, including items deferred or escalated. Join it to lifecycle events with a case ID and work-item ID so repeats of the same activity are distinguishable. Store the decision timestamp and policy version, the skill/authorization and capacity snapshots, owners before and after, performer/mentor/approver roles, and the reason for a selection, deferral or override. Add ready, assignment, start, completion and case-close timestamps; record final SLA terms, training evidence and rework/appeal outcomes. The supplied columns alone do not establish who supervised, exactly when work started, or what alternatives were available.

| Indicator | Audit definition |
|---|---|
| Handover frequency | Count acknowledged changes of accountable owner per 100 cases; exclude first ownership, duplicate events and mentor participation. Break down by activity, handover band and team pair, and track post-transfer waiting. Resource-to-resource activity transitions are a separate process measure. |
| Reassignment count | Report the count distribution and share of cases with more than two changes. Rebuild counts from ordered ownership events and reconcile them with `reassignment_count`. |
| Skill mismatch | Join the skill matrix as of the decision and execution times. Report below-target fit separately from violations: skill 0 execution, insufficient unsupervised skill, invalid Security oversight, and unauthorized final approval. Missing supervision evidence is not proof of compliance. |
| Workload distribution | Track bookings and cap breaches, projected and actual work/mentoring minutes relative to staffed capacity, and their spread by worker and team. Compare effort and high-strain work, not just open counts. |
| Overtime-risk exposure | Report the share of items and booked hours assigned to high-risk workers at decision time, how many had a valid urgent exception, and actual overtime separately. |
| SLA misses | For cases due in the period, count completion after deadline and cases still open past deadline; do not count open cases not yet due as successful. Break down miss rate and lateness by priority, skill and handover band. |
| Rework and appeals | Track repeat diagnosis, returns to correction and appeals separately after assignment, with a fixed follow-up window. Compare by match, training status and reassignment band within comparable case mixes; an association is not proof of causality. |
| Training share | Divide normal/low work items released with matched substantive training and documented supervision by all normal/low items released; count each item once. Track planned, actually started and completed practice separately, including why opportunities were deferred and who gets access to them. |
| Specialist bottlenecks | For Security and Approval, track queue depth, upper-percentile ready-to-start wait, specialist utilization, share handled by the busiest resource, and time windows with no qualified backup. Check batch-related waiting separately for Quality. |
| Policy deviations | Reconstruct eligibility and ranking from decision-time snapshots. Report hard violations, permitted exceptions, departures from the recommended choice, and decisions that cannot be reconstructed. Capture reason and author; review repeated patterns without treating every justified override as a failure. |
| Throughput | Track correct completions per staffed hour alongside end-to-end time and backlog age; do not count repeat work as extra output. |

Compare equivalent periods or cohorts by incoming volume, priority, complexity, activity mix and staffing. Use aggregate indicators for policy review; restrict identifiable logs to those who need them for review or correction.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

Use two views: a completion trace for activity flow and resource networks, and a decision/lifecycle table for ownership, queueing, supervision and compliance. Parse clocks to a consistent timezone. Pair start and completion by work-item ID; maintain actual performer on the completion row. Keep rows without a verified start for completion-based flow analysis, but do not use them to infer service duration.

The urgent filter retains whole traces with an urgent event; for priority or complexity **at assignment**, select work-item decisions using the frozen attributes and join back to full traces. The elapsed-time filter spots long cases; it does not establish an SLA miss. Compare each actual outcome to its own deadline using the applicable SLA clock. Compute ready-to-start and transfer-to-next-start waits from the lifecycle records; completion-to-completion DFG gaps do not separate waiting from service. Validate case service/waiting aggregates on complete timed traces and handle overlapping work explicitly. Use discovered batches to investigate observed grouping, then check permission and remaining slack at actual release. Networks, variants and observed roles can flag handoff paths, repeat loops and potential backup coverage, but they do not prove ownership, supervision, or authorization. Fit conservative time estimates on earlier, comparable work, account for sparse and incomplete observations, and validate on a later period before using those estimates for dispatch.
