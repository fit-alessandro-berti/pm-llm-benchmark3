## 1. Current-process diagnosis

**The dominant path is locally efficient, but not reliably efficient end to end.** The frequency DFG shows the main highway clearly: 3,600 `Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen` transitions, 2,550 `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval`, 2,300 `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement`, and 2,320 `Dispatch replacement -> Quality/compliance close`. The performance DFG explains its attraction: the selected edges into auto screening, fast approval, dispatch, and close have means of 0.8, 1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 hours respectively. These edge times should not be summed into an exact case-level cycle time, but they show why the path appears attractive.

That advantage is substantially eroded downstream:

- Fast-approval cases have a **14.1% complaint/reopen rate**, versus **3.3%** for cases using `Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device`; the overall rate is 9.2%.
- `Fast approval -> Customer complaint/reopen` has a 72-hour mean and 150-hour p90. `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` is worse: 96-hour mean and 180-hour p90. A reopened case then adds, on average, another 40 hours before manual review or 54 hours before the escalation board.
- Fast approval is **31% cheaper before rework but still 9% cheaper after** complaints, reshipments, and service credits. Thus, the evidence does **not** show that fast processing is generally more expensive; it shows that its aggregate cost advantage becomes much smaller. For some high-value cases, a EUR 410 wrong-replacement exposure could change that balance, but the aggregate data do not prove exactly where net cost reverses.
- The fast-versus-repair comparison is observational. Difficult defects may be selected into repair, so the 3.3% repair-path complaint rate cannot be interpreted as the causal effect of merely making a claim slower.

**Evidence completion and customer waiting are a separate, major problem.** `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` has a 34-hour mean and 88-hour p90, while 26% of cases entering evidence completion experience at least two evidence requests. The DFG also contains 320 `Request missing evidence -> Claim abandoned` transitions. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort represents 22% of cases, has a 140-hour median cycle time, and has the highest abandonment. This is not just a staffing problem: repeated requests, customer response, and handoffs back into review create the delay.

The back end has additional long tails. `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote` averages 22 hours and is often too slow for Premium cases; part-price confirmation, rather than simply a shortage of technicians, is a stated cause of quote delay. **The correct trade-off differs sharply by segment.**

- For Premium low-risk accessories, fast processing takes 8 hours median versus 46 hours manually. Its complaint rate is worse—6% versus 3%—but forcing the manual path would expose many customers to service credits beyond 24 hours.
- For Standard low-risk accessories, fast processing takes 13 hours versus 52 hours manually, with complaints of 8% versus 4%. These customers tolerate up to 72 hours when status visibility is good, so communication can preserve experience without making every claim manual.
- For Premium high-value devices, fast processing has a 22% complaint rate versus 5% on the reviewed/repair path; for Standard high-value devices the figures are 18% versus 6%. Here the quality gap is much larger and coincides with high compliance and wrong-replacement exposure. However, automatically sending every such claim through the full 96- or 120-hour reviewed/repair path is not justified either.

Finally, capacity constrains the available solutions. Seven reviewers provide 252 productive hours per week, with queues growing sharply above 82% utilization; the proposed auto-screen change could increase manual inflow by 15–35%. The two fraud/risk specialists already bottleneck on Mondays and after campaigns. The escalation board has a strong satisfaction effect but only one 45-minute daily slot. Conversely, quality inspectors are underused outside repair campaigns, creating a possible—but limited—opportunity for targeted inspection.

The three redesigns below therefore use different primary levers. All treat R1–R3 as executable workflow guards; none uses the low-precision discovered model as its policy engine.

## 2. Redesign A: Segment- and risk-gated straight-through processing

**Redesigned process flow**

1. Replace the existing auto-screen decision table with segment-, value-, defect-, and evidence-aware rules.
2. Preserve straight-through processing for clear low-risk claims:
   - **Premium low-risk accessory:** retain auto screening, fast approval, and rapid dispatch. This is the only segment eligible for R2’s evidence-completeness exception, and that exception should be limited to genuinely low-ambiguity claims.
   - **Standard low-risk accessory:** retain fast approval where evidence completeness is confirmed and the defect/part decision is unambiguous.
3. Prevent direct auto approval for higher-risk claims:
   - Claims above EUR 400 must pass either manual eligibility review or fraud/risk review before fast approval, satisfying R1.
   - High-value or otherwise risk-flagged devices receive evidence validation and one appropriate review even when a particular claim is not above EUR 400. Manual review handles eligibility/technical ambiguity; fraud/risk review is reserved for risk signals so that all high-value demand is not dumped onto two specialists.
   - After clearance, a claim may still receive fast approval and replacement. It enters `Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device -> Quality check` only when repair is actually appropriate.
4. Put an evidence-completeness guard immediately before dispatch, except for the permitted Premium low-risk accessory case.
5. Route every reopen through manual eligibility review before final close or denial. Escalation may follow that review for genuinely cross-functional exceptions; it cannot bypass R3.

**Segments, paths, and activities targeted**

This primarily changes the 2,550-case `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval` decision edge and the high-value dispatch deviations. It protects the fast lane used by Premium and Standard low-risk accessories while targeting the much larger high-value complaint gaps and R1/R2 exposure.

**Mechanism of improvement**

The design removes the decision errors that occur because value, segment, and risk are invisible to outdated rules. It avoids both extremes: low-risk customers do not inherit a 46- or 52-hour manual path, while high-value approval is no longer confused with low-risk straight-through processing. Allowing replacement after clearance also avoids automatically imposing the full reviewed/repair path’s 96- or 120-hour median.

The segment outcomes support testing this design, but they do not prove that review alone will reproduce the reviewed/repair path’s 5–6% complaint rates.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues**

- High-value decision speed and review labor are deliberately traded for better compliance and potentially fewer downstream failures.
- The low-risk fast lane retains its higher observed complaint rates—6% versus 3% for Premium and 8% versus 4% for Standard—because eliminating that gap through universal manual review would destroy valuable speed.
- The 140-hour evidence-completion problem, part-price confirmation delays, and incorrect replacement-part selection are not directly redesigned.
- Updating rules is deployable in two weeks, but the associated 15–35% possible manual-inflow increase makes capacity gating essential. This option does not assume that existing reviewers can absorb it.

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-once with assisted customer orchestration

**Redesigned process flow**

1. Keep clear, complete claims on their existing compliant decision and fulfillment paths; portal adoption must not become a prerequisite for Premium low-risk fast service.
2. When evidence is needed, replace repeated generic requests with a defect-specific evidence bundle:
   `Initial eligibility/defect triage -> Evidence completion state -> One consolidated request with automatic presence/readability checks -> Eligibility, risk, or technical decision`.
3. Keep the case in evidence completion rather than repeatedly returning it to manual eligibility review after each deficiency. Eligibility analysis or part-price confirmation may proceed in parallel, but approval and dispatch remain subject to R1 and R2.
4. Give customers a portal showing the exact outstanding item, next step, and current status. Offer an agent-assisted telephone/upload channel from the outset, not only after digital failure. Use reminders and assisted contact before abandonment.
5. For repair claims, start part-price confirmation while evidence is being completed. The technician then handles the technical quote rather than repeatedly chasing routine evidence, and the customer receives one visible quote/approval step through either digital or assisted channels.
6. Standard low-risk customers receive proactive status even when no action is required. Premium evidence-heavy cases receive prioritized assisted contact. Reopens still follow the R3 manual-review guard.

**Segments, paths, and activities targeted**

This targets the 22% unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort, the 26% repeated-evidence-request pattern, the 34-hour mean return from evidence request to manual review, and evidence-related abandonment. It also addresses quote preparation and the visibility requirement for Standard low-risk customers without changing the basic risk-routing philosophy in Redesign A.

**Mechanism of improvement**

The main gain comes from preventing work and handoffs rather than accelerating every activity. Better first-request completeness can reduce repeated customer effort, reviewer re-reading, and the 88-hour p90 evidence-to-review tail. Starting part-price confirmation earlier attacks the stated quote-waiting cause. Status visibility distinguishes a case that is progressing from one that appears lost.

The portal’s expected **30–45% reduction applies to repeated evidence requests**, not to total cycle time or total cost. Any cycle-time improvement must be measured separately.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues**

- The organization spends on portal integration, defect-specific checklists, and assisted support. The assisted channel is essential; a digital-only design would knowingly risk higher abandonment among customers with low digital literacy.
- A strict complete-bundle rule may add some up-front waiting for customers who previously received an early, incomplete decision. It must not delay clear claims merely to make the process administratively tidy.
- Reducing abandonment can increase downstream repair, replacement, and review volume; that is a desirable customer outcome but not a free capacity saving.
- This option does not directly solve wrong replacement-part selection, high-value repair rework, or all high-value fast-path complaints.

## 4. Redesign C: Risk-based fulfillment quality cells and exception pull

**Redesigned process flow**

1. Leave intake, evidence collection, and technical decision routing unchanged apart from the common R1–R3 guards. The redesign begins when fulfillment is selected.
2. Create two replacement paths:
   - **High-value or low-confidence replacement decisions:** `Technical approval -> Pre-dispatch compatibility gate -> Dispatch replacement -> Quality/compliance close`. The gate checks model/serial/defect evidence against the selected replacement part and records the reason for any hold.
   - **Clear low-risk accessories:** retain rapid dispatch, holding only detected mismatch exceptions. Premium low-risk accessories do not receive a universal manual gate that could push them beyond 24 hours.
3. Roster and cross-train quality inspectors for the high-value gate outside repair-campaign peaks, with explicit queue limits. “Underused” is not treated as unlimited free capacity.
4. Retain `Repair device -> Quality check`, but change its content rather than adding a second universal inspection:
   - High-value repairs receive defect-specific functional tests and focused inspector attention.
   - Low-risk repairs retain the standard check.
   - Rework and wrong-part findings feed back to technicians and dispatch selection rules.
5. After a reopen and the required manual review, simple errors are corrected by the fulfillment cell. Only complex, high-impact cases are pulled into the daily escalation board, preserving its strong satisfaction effect without making it the default complaint queue.

**Segments, paths, and activities targeted**

This targets `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen`, with its 96-hour mean and 180-hour p90 and known link to incorrect part selection. It also targets high-value repair rework of 12.5%, the EUR 410 high-value wrong-replacement exposure, and the scalability weakness of the escalation board.

**Mechanism of improvement**

A 6–18-hour gate is much easier to justify where a wrong replacement is expensive and creates a long complaint tail than where the expected error cost is EUR 95 and rapid service is the main source of value. The design therefore spends time and inspection effort selectively. It seeks to reduce **time to final resolution**, even if time to first dispatch increases. Risk-based repair testing similarly concentrates additional quality effort where rework is most prevalent.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues**

- High-value first-dispatch speed is deliberately sacrificed by 6–18 hours for gated cases, and inspection touch cost increases.
- The benefit depends on the gate’s actual catch rate and the quality of model/part data; neither is provided, so net savings cannot be calculated in advance.
- Some low-risk wrong replacements remain possible because a universal gate is intentionally rejected.
- Evidence-request loops, auto-screen demand, reviewer/fraud capacity, and part-price confirmation delays remain unresolved.
- Moving inspectors into dispatch verification could weaken repair checking during campaigns unless staffing is explicitly rebalanced.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A**

- **Failure 1 — inaccurate segmentation:** stale value, defect, or customer attributes can route a high-value claim around necessary scrutiny or over-route simple accessories. A manual review performed merely to satisfy the rule could create nominal R1 conformance without better decisions.
- **Failure 2 — review overload:** the rule update produces the upper part of the 15–35% inflow range, utilization passes the queue-danger region, and high-value p90 cycle time grows without a corresponding complaint reduction.
- **Failure 3 — local compliance, recurring failure:** evidence and review boxes are checked, but incorrect part selection still produces complaints after dispatch or after close.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** from the auto screen to manual eligibility review; secondary migration to fraud/risk review on Mondays and after campaigns if too many high-value cases are sent there.

**Redesign B**

- **Failure 1 — digital exclusion:** repeated portal requests fall partly because customers abandon. Aggregate abandonment could look stable while customers with low digital literacy become substantially worse off.
- **Failure 2 — completeness becomes a new queue:** the consolidated checklist asks for unnecessary items, validation rejects usable evidence, or clear Premium claims are held behind portal processing, increasing service-credit exposure.
- **Failure 3 — work is moved rather than removed:** assisted staff, evidence validators, or technicians inherit the chasing work; earlier part-price checks may also produce stale quotes if prices or parts change.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** from repeated `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` loops to evidence validation/assisted support, and subsequently to technical decision or fulfillment if fewer customers abandon.

**Redesign C**

- **Failure 1 — the gate becomes a universal 18-hour wait:** inspector queues grow, Premium expectations deteriorate, and first-time-right performance does not improve enough to offset the delay.
- **Failure 2 — inspection checks paperwork rather than compatibility:** the gate creates false assurance while incorrect parts still arrive, now after additional delay.
- **Failure 3 — campaign contention and exception overload:** inspectors are simultaneously needed for repair campaigns and dispatch; technicians inherit holds, or too many cases are classified as complex and still wait for the 45-minute escalation slot.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** from dispatch and downstream complaint handling to pre-dispatch quality inspection, with further migration to technicians/part availability or manual reopen review during peaks.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0 — establish a case-level measurement baseline.**  
Before comparing designs, connect segment, value, defect type, evidence completeness, review history, selected/correct replacement part, first close, final close, reopen, abandonment, and downstream cost. Measure both first-dispatch time and time to final resolution. Retain open, denied, and abandoned cases rather than reporting only successfully closed cases. Establish separate baselines for Mondays, marketing campaigns, and repair campaigns.

The common scorecard should include:

- **Cycle time:** segment-specific median and p90 to decision, first fulfillment, and final close; evidence-customer waiting shown separately from organizational queue time.
- **Service guardrails:** Premium low-risk completions and credits around the 24-hour exposure point; Standard low-risk completion and status experience against the stated 72-hour tolerance.
- **Quality/CX:** first-time-right replacement, complaints/reopens within a fixed post-close observation window, repair rework, repeated evidence requests, abandonment, reshipments, service credits, status inquiries, and escalation waiting.
- **Cost:** activity labor plus complaints, reshipments, credits, wrong replacements, and rework, reported per started claim and per successfully resolved claim.
- **Compliance:** case-level R1/R2/R3 tests and alignment-deviation counts. Fitness and fitting-trace percentage remain secondary diagnostics, not substitutes for rule-level compliance.
- **Resources:** reviewer utilization and queue length against the 82% danger region, fraud-review waits by day/campaign, part-price waiting, inspector/gate queues, and escalation-board demand.

**Stage 1 — first pilot: Redesign A, shadow mode followed by a bounded high-value live pilot.**

1. Run the new rules in shadow first to compare proposed and actual routings and to convert the expected additional review inflow into hours using observed review effort and weekly arrivals. Those necessary capacity inputs are absent from the current aggregate data.
2. Keep the low-risk fast lane unchanged during the initial live pilot. Pilot selective routing on high-value claims, stratified by Premium/Standard and defect type.
3. Use a **compliant reviewed-path control**, not a deliberately noncompliant direct-dispatch control. Historical fast-path outcomes can provide context but not a clean causal comparison.

**Go/scale signals:** systematic R1–R3 breaches are eliminated; high-value complaints/reopens and final-resolution p90 improve relative to the compliant control; any additional decision time is not producing worse total cost or customer outcomes; reviewer queues remain stable; and low-risk speed/credit metrics are unaffected.

**Stop or pause scale-up:** avoidable compliance breaches persist; review inflow creates sustained utilization above the queue-danger region; fraud p90 waiting worsens materially on Mondays/campaigns; or complaints simply move later in the case. If capacity is the binding constraint, reduce evidence-generated review work through Stage 2 or add capacity before scaling A—do not relax R1–R3 to protect queue speed.

**Stage 2 — pilot Redesign B within the unclear/missing-evidence cohort.**

Randomize or step the rollout within defect and customer subgroups where feasible, explicitly including customers likely to need assisted service. Keep risk-routing and fulfillment constant during this pilot so its effect is distinguishable from A.

**Go signals:** repeated evidence requests fall materially, plausibly toward the expected 30–45% reduction; evidence-cohort median/p90 and repeated manual work improve; and there is no meaningful offsetting increase in abandonment overall or in digitally vulnerable subgroups.

**Stop or redesign signals:** requests decline mainly because customers abandon; assisted workloads become unbounded; or total final-resolution time fails to improve despite better portal activity metrics. A digital-only version should not be scaled merely because its portal adoption cost is lower.

Running B before broad A expansion can relieve some evidence-related reviewer demand and provide cleaner evidence for subsequent fulfillment decisions.

**Stage 3 — pilot Redesign C on high-value replacements, then test the repair-quality changes separately.**

Start with high-value or explicitly low-confidence replacement decisions, not low-risk accessories. Compare gated and non-gated but otherwise compliant fulfillment within similar defect/part groups. Include campaign and non-campaign periods so inspector contention is visible.

**Go signals:** incorrect selections, dispatch-related complaints, and final-resolution cost/p90 improve enough to justify the observed 6–18-hour addition; inspector queues remain controlled; and Premium low-risk service is untouched.

**Stop or narrow the gate:** the upper end of the delay range becomes typical, first-time-right performance barely changes, errors reappear as later reopens, or repair quality deteriorates because inspectors were diverted. Only after the replacement gate is understood should the high-value repair-test protocol and revised escalation triage be scaled.

**How to establish that improvements are real rather than displaced**

- Compare like with like within segment, value, and defect type; do not use aggregate fast-versus-repair outcomes as causal estimates. Use randomized or stepped-wedge rollout where operational constraints permit, with uncertainty intervals for complaint rates.
- Follow mature cohorts through a fixed post-close window. A faster first dispatch is not an improvement if `Customer complaint/reopen after close`, reshipment, or escalation subsequently rises.
- Evaluate total cost and time to **final** resolution, including downstream labor and service credits. A design that remains slightly more expensive before rework may still be preferable, but only observed cohort results can establish that.
- Inspect all likely receiving bottlenecks after every pilot. Fewer evidence requests accompanied by longer assisted-support queues, or fewer complaints accompanied by an inspector backlog, are displaced—not eliminated—problems.
- Scale and combine A, B, and C only after their separate effects are understood. The preferred sequence is compliant risk routing first, evidence-demand reduction second, and targeted fulfillment-quality investment third, with capacity evidence governing the pace of expansion.
