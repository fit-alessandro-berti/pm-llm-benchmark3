# 2. Expected temporal signatures

# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

The numerical waits below are simple `start − queue_arrival` differences from the supplied fields. They are apparent waits, not validated queue measures. Service is `complete − start`; utilization depends on whether an interval is worker effort, elapsed ownership, machine occupancy, or a copied group interval. These definitions must not be mixed.

- **G1:** Apparent waits are 6, 5, 4, and 2 minutes. If PLC starts are item-valid, they are reasonable item waits; if the timestamp means fixture-cycle start, report a batch wait as well as member waits. Each case has a three-minute service interval, but summing to 12 item-minutes would overstate one cell's occupied time: the fixture was occupied for about three minutes and held four items. Per-item throughput is four per cycle, while resource utilization should count the cell's three-minute occupancy once. Treating the four equal completions as four independent finishes can create a false burst, but the repeated cycle can be a real capacity batch rather than a bad bottleneck signal.
- **G2:** Apparent waits to the shared 10:00 start are 18, 14, 11, and 6 minutes. These are waits to group begin, not necessarily each item's work start. The computed services (8, 14, 21, 27 minutes) grow because every item is measured from the same group start; they may include waiting within the group or serial work rather than hands-on inspection. Summing them gives 70 minutes over a 27-minute group window (about 259% of one resource), which is impossible as exclusive work by one T-B worker. Using interval union instead gives nearly continuous occupancy from 10:00–10:27, but that too assumes these are valid work intervals. Misreading this can make T-B appear both highly utilized and slow, and can move the apparent bottleneck from group capacity to inspection service. Throughput should use individual completions only if the separate buttons record operational completion.
- **G3:** If the starts are real, apparent waits are 22, 18, 19, and 19 minutes. The 28-, 20-, 13-, and 6-minute “services” end at a shared folder submission, so per-case service is likely overstated for early-finished reviews and incomparable across cases. It may still be true that T-C was occupied through much of 11:02–11:30, but overlapping case durations cannot be summed as worker utilization. A single 11:30 completion creates a false throughput spike and hides completion order. A bottleneck analysis based on shared ends may blame review work or form submission interchangeably; capture the two separately.
- **G4:** Apparent waits rise from 7 to 19 minutes. Four-minute service intervals are mutually adjacent, so the recorded single resource is fully occupied for the 20-minute span; summed service and interval union agree here. Arrivals are about one minute apart while service takes four minutes, so the queue can grow and waits can rise even though service timing is credible. The station is a plausible bottleneck during this sample and output is serial (roughly one case per four minutes in this window), but five cases do not establish a stable long-run rate. There is no reason to infer simultaneous execution from a detector grouping adjacent cases.
- **G5:** Apparent waits to the listed starts are 15, 15, 16, and 15 minutes. Durations of 25, 15, 20, and 10 minutes overlap; their 70 summed case-minutes exceed the 30-minute elapsed span. That is not a valid utilization estimate for a single person. With three analysts, utilization must be calculated by badge/person or as pool occupancy with a clear denominator; the shared login cannot show either. If these are broad case ownership intervals, even person-level overlap may overstate effort. Four completions between 13:20 and 13:30 are not a reliable throughput rate. A pooled login can falsely mark `analyst_pool` as the bottleneck while hiding idle or overloaded individuals.
- **G6:** Using the fields literally gives arrival-to-start waits of 175, 148, 122, 110, 76, 58, and 35 minutes, followed by zero service for every case. This combination is possible if tickets genuinely waited for a 17:00 bulk close that takes no item-level work, but it is also the signature of delayed administrative entry standing in for missing work times. In the latter interpretation, waiting is inflated because recorded start is much later than actual work, while service collapses to zero because start and completion were assigned the same submission time. This produces both inflated waiting and near-zero service at once. Utilization is then missed or placed at 17:00 as a fictitious burst, and throughput appears as seven simultaneous completions. Keep arrival-to-close aging only if that is the intended KPI; exclude these lifecycle values from operational queue-to-start and service estimates until semantics are recovered.
- **G7:** Literal waits range from 2 to −11 minutes and all five services appear to be 15 minutes. Negative waiting is a direct warning that rounded starts are not event-level starts (or that clocks differ). Neither the common 15-minute duration nor the simultaneous starts support a batch-service estimate. Utilization can be arbitrarily distorted by the undocumented rounding rule; cases may actually have run serially, overlapped, or as one group. Throughput timing is concentrated at the same boundaries, so any fine-grained bottleneck or conformance conclusion is unsafe. Use interval-censored/bounded analyses or exclude these lifecycle fields from point estimates.
- **G8:** Apparent waits are 38, 43, 48, 54, and 58 minutes. The first case has a large delay; subsequently arrivals come faster (roughly every 1–3 minutes) than the seven-minute serial service cycle, so the queue continues to grow. Once work begins, T-H appears fully occupied from 15:38 to 16:13, with one case every seven minutes. That supports a local capacity bottleneck during this short run. The 15:00–15:35 safety event is a plausible reason for the initial backlog, but clock mismatch prevents attributing the delay to it. If queue and scanner clocks are offset, all wait estimates and the claimed safety-to-repair relationship shift; validate the clocks before making a downtime or staffing conclusion.

            if isinstance(result, pd.DataFrame):
                out = result.copy()
            elif isinstance(result, dict):

                        pieces.append(part)
                out = pd.concat(pieces, ignore_index=True, sort=False) if pieces else pd.DataFrame()
            else:
                try:
                    out = pd.DataFrame(result)
                except Exception:
                    out = pd.DataFrame()
            case_col = next((c for c in ["case:concept:name", "case", "case_id"] if c in out.columns), None)
            type_col = next((c for c in out.columns if "type" in str(c).lower() or "category" in str(c).lower()), None)
            if type_col is None and "_returned_category" in out.columns:

            elif type_col:
                cats = sorted(out[type_col].dropna().astype(str).unique())
                pm_results.append((label, "all", ",".join(cats) if cats else "no category rows"))

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

**If resource IDs are trusted as individual workers:** G2 and G3 have overlapping intervals on T-B/T-C. A single worker cannot perform all those case intervals as exclusive hands-on work, so at least one lifecycle boundary is a group marker, a submission time, or a broad case-ownership interval. G5 would be internally contradictory if `analyst_pool` were treated as one person. G4 and G8 support serial station utilization because their intervals do not overlap. G1's resource is an automated cell, so worker utilization is the wrong measure; measure cell occupancy and fixture capacity.

**If IDs are pools, stations, or shared accounts:** G5's overlaps can reflect distinct analysts, but the stated maximum of three analysts still does not explain four-way overlap if every interval is exact, exclusive work. T-B/T-C could also hide multiple operators, although the prompt only specifically establishes shared identity for `analyst_pool`. A station ID identifies where work happened, not necessarily who did it. Utilization then needs either individual identities or a clearly defined pool-capacity denominator; dividing pooled occupied time by one worker's shift is invalid.

**If timestamps are exact:** G1's equal three-minute cycle, G4's four-minute serial cycle, and G8's seven-minute serial cycle are strong temporal evidence. G2's common start and G3's common end are also exact patterns, but timestamp exactness still does not make a group-start or folder-submit event an item-level lifecycle boundary. G6's exact 17:00 values could mark a true bulk close, while still not describing the earlier work. G7 cannot be treated as exact despite minute-formatted values because its known legacy transformation dominates the display.

**If timestamps are interval-censored at the stated precision:** Treat boundaries as ranges consistent with the documented granularity and source rule; do not assume nearest-minute or ±7.5-minute error without evidence. Minute-level differences of 6–8 minutes in G2/G3 and four/seven-minute scanner cycles are more robust than sub-minute ordering, but precision is not accuracy, and event meaning remains the larger issue. For G7, the rounding rule is undocumented and may be asymmetric; use rule-dependent bounds broad enough to include its possible rounded source times. Do not calculate point durations or utilization from 14:00 and 14:15 as though they were actual events.

Several identical or near-identical patterns have multiple physical explanations:

- **Shared starts (G1, G2, G7):** a true fixture batch; one group setup/start copied to member cases; or many actual starts collapsed by timestamp coarsening.
- **Shared ends (G1, G3, G6):** true simultaneous release from a machine; a folder or bulk-form submission after separate work; or a logical action that closes several cases at once.
- **Overlapping intervals (G2, G3, G5):** real parallel people/equipment; a single group start or common end creating artificial intervals; shared-account work by different analysts; or broad task ownership intervals that include pauses.
- **Adjacent intervals (G4, G8):** one worker's serial tasks; machine-paced cycles; or sequential work after a queue/downtime period. Adjacency identifies order, not why that order occurred.
- **A common timestamp under a common resource (G6/G7):** an actual synchronized event, a shared submitter, or a low-resolution/rounded clock. A smaller PM4Py tolerance cannot distinguish these.

For queue analysis, G1/G4 are the most usable if queue and lifecycle fields share a clock and the lifecycle semantics hold. G2 can support waiting-to-group-start, but not item-level waiting-to-work without individual starts. G3's start-based wait is provisional while its end-based cycle time is not. G5's case waiting may be retained provisionally, but resource utilization must not be attributed to one analyst. G6 should be excluded from queue-to-start and service point estimates; arrival-to-close aging is usable only if explicitly defined as an administrative SLA. G7 should be interval-censored or excluded from point estimates. G8 wait estimates are provisional pending clock alignment; its serial service sequence is stronger than the safety-event causal claim.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

Prioritize these changes in order:

1. **Record operational lifecycle events at the level where work happens.** Capture item-level work start and finish separately from group start, setup, release, review-folder submission, ticket close, and database insertion. Define whether each event means hands-on work, machine occupancy, decision completion, or administrative recording.
2. **Keep event time and recording time as separate immutable fields.** Store source event time, database `recorded_at`, source event ID, and sequence number. Do not backfill a submission timestamp into start/complete. For delayed entry, retain both the best-known event time and the later entry time, with provenance.
3. **Synchronize and document source clocks.** Record timezone, clock source, offset/drift checks, and correction history for upstream queues, PLCs, tablets, scanners, safety systems, and MES. Preserve raw timestamps. Reconcile the T-H safety and repair clocks before joining events or attributing downtime.
4. **Capture person and system identity distinctly.** Record badge/person ID for execution, login/submitter ID for data entry, and station/machine/service-account ID in separate fields. Mark shared accounts explicitly; retain a pool ID rather than treating it as one worker.
5. **Add explicit batch and group structure.** Give each fixture cycle, group begin, folder, bulk submission, and multi-case action a batch/group ID; link member cases and record group-level versus item-level event scope. For G1 include fixture/capacity; for G2 include the begin-group action; for G3/G6 link submission/close transactions to cases.
6. **Measure capacity and interruptions.** Capture station capacity, number of fixture slots, staffed capacity, availability/down state, safety holds, queue release, and reason-coded pause/resume events. Keep separate “waiting for resource,” “blocked,” and “work paused” states so elapsed case time is not mistaken for labor effort.
7. **Make precision metadata operational.** Store source resolution, actual rounding/truncation rule, uncertainty bounds, and whether timestamps are synchronized/estimated. For legacy MES, recover its transform or preserve raw source values and emit interval bounds; never label rounded quarter-hour points as exact starts and finishes.
8. **Validate with targeted trace audits.** For representative G1–G8 cases, compare raw PLC/scanner/form actions with worker or supervisor reconstruction. Use the audit to establish event semantics and clock offsets before publishing queue, service, or utilization KPIs.

# 7. Final adjudication

High-confidence interpretations are G4 as serial four-minute work, G8 as serial seven-minute work after a substantial delay, G1 as a likely four-item fixture cycle, and G7 as coarsened lifecycle data. G2 is confidently a shared group-start pattern, but its item-level execution model is unresolved. G3 is strongly a shared folder-submit/end pattern. G5 is clearly not a trustworthy one-person resource trace; concurrency among up to three analysts is plausible, with interval semantics still needing review. G6 is clearly a common manual bulk/entry event, not seven established item-level zero-duration services.

Use G1, G4, and G8 for provisional performance analysis only after checking clock comparability and event scope. Use G2 for wait-to-group-start, not item service, until separate starts are available. For G3, preserve validated starts if confirmed but do not use the common end to calculate per-case service or completion throughput. G5 needs badge-level identity before person utilization or analyst bottleneck conclusions. Exclude G6 lifecycle start/complete from operational queue and service point estimates; retain queue-arrival-to-close only as a clearly named aging measure if the bulk close is confirmed. Interval-censor or exclude G7 lifecycle values from point queue/service/utilization analysis. Validate the G8 safety-clock relationship before attributing its initial queue delay to that event.

No PM4Py batch label alone establishes that cases were physically processed simultaneously. The defensible conclusion comes from combining interval patterns with source semantics, precision, insertion lag, identity quality, queue arrivals, and operational validation.
