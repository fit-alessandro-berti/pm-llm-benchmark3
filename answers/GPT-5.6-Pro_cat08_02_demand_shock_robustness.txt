# 1. Stress scenarios

The stress model should be based on **work content and resource demand by case type**, not merely order count. Normal throughput is about 2,300 orders per week, versus 3,300 during the peak, an increase of roughly 43%. However, Routine volume is almost unchanged—about 1,403 versus 1,386 orders per week—while Urgent volume increases by approximately 131%, Complex-panel volume by 121%, and Repeat-after-failure volume by 61%. Because Complex-panel orders contain multiple assays, their true machine workload rises even more than the case count suggests.

The tails are already fragile before the peak: the normal-period Urgent p90 is 19 hours against an 18-hour contractual target, while the Routine p90 of 68 hours leaves only four hours of margin against its 72-hour target. During the peak, the combined share of pre-analytical exits to recollection or manual correction rises from approximately 7.0% to 12.3%, and the Repeat-assay share among the listed Assay-run exits rises from approximately 6.7% to 14.8%. All calculations and evidence below use the supplied order-level log; because it is flattened by order rather than tube and assay, event counts should be treated as workload signals rather than exact physical work units. 

### Scenario A — winter mix surge toward Urgent and Complex-panel orders

This scenario resembles the observed peak:

* Urgent and Complex-panel demand more than doubles on a weekly basis.
* Urgent orders require short lead times and often bypass batching.
* Complex panels create multiple assay operations, are 3.1 times more likely to require Medical validation, and are 2.4 times more likely to enter Specialist review.
* Repeat-after-failure work also increases, consuming scarce assay capacity without creating a new completed order.

This is not a homogeneous volume increase. It disproportionately stresses assay sequencing, Technical validation, Medical validation, and Specialist review.

### Scenario B — assay-machine degradation or outage

Two variants should be tested:

1. **M2 degradation:** M2 remains available but its failure rate increases, especially after night shifts or at high utilization.
2. **M1 outage:** only the faster but less reliable M2 remains available for a material portion of a shift.

The observed peak already has 98% machine utilization, an 18-hour median assay queue, and a Repeat-assay transition rate more than twice the normal value. A degradation scenario can therefore become unstable through repeat amplification even without another increase in order arrivals.

### Scenario C — Medical-validator or Specialist-review absence

This includes:

* an unplanned Medical-validator absence during a high-complexity day;
* the known every-second-Friday Specialist-review absence;
* an evening or weekend coverage gap after a large assay completion wave.

Medical-validator utilization rises from 58% to 93%, with its median queue increasing from 2 to 22 hours. Specialist review rises from 43% to 87%, while its queue increases from 5 to 36 hours. The performance edge from Hold for specialist review to Medical validation reaches 48 hours during the peak. A single absence can therefore make validation—not assay execution—the binding constraint.

### Scenario D — courier delay or compressed arrival wave

A late route may combine the usual 10:00 and 17:00 peaks into a narrower arrival window. Alternatively, several routes may arrive on time but with unusually large winter-wave loads.

The first pressure appears at Courier intake, Registration, and Pre-analytical check. Registration overtime is especially risky because error rates rise after nine-hour shifts. Those errors then create Manual accession correction, Request recollection, physician-contact work, and another Receive-sample cycle.

### Scenario E — compound disruption

A plausible severe case combines:

* an Urgent/Complex mix surge;
* degraded M2 performance;
* one absent specialist or Medical validator;
* compressed courier arrivals.

Under this scenario, the bottleneck may switch several times within the same day. For example, intake may be limiting in the morning, assay capacity at midday, and Medical validation in the evening. A design that maximizes one station’s local utilization is particularly dangerous here.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### Scenario A — mix surge

**Likely migration:**

`Pre-analytical check → Accession correction → Preparation/Assay → Technical validation → Medical/Specialist validation`

Pre-analytical utilization reaches 96%, and its median queue reaches 9.5 hours. The combined correction and recollection exits are also materially more frequent. Preparation then operates at 89% utilization with an 11-hour queue, followed by the assay machines at 98% utilization and an 18-hour queue.

Once samples complete their assays, Technical validation becomes a hard downstream constraint at 101% utilization and a 16-hour queue. The heavier Complex-panel mix then raises demand for Medical validation and Specialist review. This is reflected in:

* Assay run → Technical validation increasing from 5 to 15 hours;
* Technical validation → Medical validation increasing from 8 to 24 hours;
* Hold for specialist review → Medical validation increasing from 18 to 48 hours.

A strict Urgent-first rule at preparation and assay can lower the waiting time of some Urgent cases, but it has three spillovers:

1. It fragments machine schedules, reducing effective assay throughput.
2. It can starve aging Routine cases.
3. It may delay Complex panels even though those panels need the same scarce downstream Medical-validation capacity.

Accelerating assay output without adding or protecting Technical- and Medical-validation capacity merely moves the queue downstream. Because Technical validation is already above 100% peak utilization, this migration would occur quickly.

### Scenario B — machine degradation or outage

**Likely migration:**

`Preparation/Batch wait → Assay and Repeat-assay loop → Technical validation burst → Medical validation`

During active degradation, assay throughput falls and the preparation and Batch-wait queues expand. The Repeat assay → Assay run median rises from 10 to 26 hours in the observed peak, so a failed run does not merely consume a second runtime; it also spends substantial time waiting to regain machine access.

A fixed implementation of I3—M1 for Urgent and Complex panels and M2 for all Routine work—would make the system less robust:

* M2 would receive a large, concentrated Routine workload.
* M2’s higher failure rate could generate more repeat work.
* Repeat work would compete with both new Routine work and critical reruns.
* M1 could occasionally have unused capacity while the M2 queue and repeat loop remain overloaded.

During an outage, downstream validators may temporarily appear underutilized because assays are not completing. That is a deceptive lull. Once the machine recovers, the accumulated assay queue produces a completion wave. Technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization, then becomes the next bottleneck. If the recovery wave contains many Complex panels, Medical validation and Specialist review follow.

Therefore, “machine recovered” is not equivalent to “process recovered.” Recovery should continue until the validation queues and total work in process also normalize.

### Scenario C — validator or specialist absence

**Likely migration:**

`Medical/Specialist validation → Technical-validation holding queue → Assay/preparation WIP → upstream admission`

The first visible queue forms after Technical validation or at Hold for specialist review. Continuing to maximize assay utilization during the absence creates technically completed but unreleasable work.

Complex panels are especially exposed because of their higher likelihood of both Medical validation and Specialist review. Urgent orders are also vulnerable because their 18-hour target leaves little tolerance for a 24-hour Technical-to-Medical wait.

Adding an evening Medical-validation shift through I5 relieves this constraint, but the next bottleneck may be:

* Release-result processing if a large block is validated simultaneously;
* handover and reconciliation if evening validators inherit partially reviewed cases;
* Specialist review if Medical-validation capacity is added without corresponding specialist availability.

Conditional outsourcing through I10 can remove some Complex-panel work from the internal Specialist-review queue. Its 12-hour transport delay, however, means that it should not be treated as a generic Urgent-case solution. It also creates a new path through partner receipt, traceability, result reconciliation, and final release.

### Scenario D — courier compression or delay

**Likely migration:**

`Courier intake → Registration → Pre-analytical check → Correction/Recollection → Preparation and Assay`

Courier intake reaches an 88% peak utilization and a 7.5-hour median queue. The compressed arrival wave then reaches Registration at 85% utilization. Extending clerk shifts may temporarily clear registration, but errors after nine-hour shifts can increase the correction and recollection workload.

I8, automated registration, can clear the first visible queue. It does not remove the physical sample checks. Without paced release, automated orders reach Pre-analytical check faster, where utilization is already 96%. A few hours later, the same wave reaches the assay machines.

I6, early defect triage, reduces the amount of defective work that proceeds into preparation and assay. However, it may initially increase the visible number of early rejections and the immediate workload for recollection communication. Since Request recollection → Receive sample takes 60 hours at peak, early identification improves decision timing but does not itself eliminate the long external loop.

I7, courier smoothing, is valuable because it reduces arrival variability rather than simply adding internal capacity. It can nevertheless move waiting to physician sites or courier routes, so Urgent and specimen-stability exceptions must remain available.

### Scenario E — compound disruption

**Likely migration:**

`Compressed intake → Pre-analytical exceptions → constrained assay/repeat loop → Technical validation → Medical/Specialist validation`

In this scenario, there is no useful single-bottleneck diagnosis. Several reinforcing mechanisms are active:

* compressed arrivals create a large synchronized cohort;
* pre-analytical errors add recollection and correction work;
* M2 failures amplify assay demand;
* Urgent priority fragments the remaining machine schedule;
* Complex panels generate disproportionate validation work;
* validator absence prevents completed assays from leaving the system.

Strict Urgent-first dispatch may improve the first few Urgent cases while making later Urgent cases worse: fragmentation lowers total throughput, Routine and Complex work accumulates, and repeat work returns to the machine queue. Similarly, accelerating registration or preparation may only increase the number of cases trapped before assay or validation.

The correct response is therefore controlled degradation: protect truly time-critical work, limit work released toward unavailable downstream resources, and explicitly accept upstream waiting for lower-slack-priority cases rather than allowing uncontrolled work-in-process growth.

# 3. Robust redesign package

### A. Replace push scheduling and strict Urgent-first dispatch with finite-WIP, slack-based control

I1 should not be implemented as an unconditional Urgent-first rule. Instead, dispatch should combine:

1. **Remaining SLA slack:** contractual deadline minus the conservative estimate of remaining processing time.
2. **Downstream readiness:** whether assay, Technical-validation, and—where applicable—Medical-validation capacity will be available.
3. **Aging protection:** a nonzero service floor for aging Routine and Complex-panel work.
4. **Batch efficiency:** used as a tie-breaker rather than the primary objective.

Three controlled release gates are appropriate:

* Pre-analytical check → Test preparation;
* Test preparation/Batch wait → Assay run;
* assay start for Complex-panel work → expected Medical/Specialist-validation capacity.

The system should issue capacity tokens in **assay-equivalent work and expected validator minutes**, not simple case counts. For example, one Complex-panel order should not consume the same planning unit as one single-assay Routine order.

**Why this is robust:** It prevents front-end improvements from flooding a constrained downstream station and protects against starvation caused by a single-priority rule.

**Assumptions:** Order type, due date, planned assays, and likely validation route are known sufficiently early. Remaining-time estimates must be stratified by case type, machine, shift, and current queue state.

**Where it can fail:** Upstream waiting may be unsafe for specimens with short stability windows. Such orders require an override. Poor remaining-time estimates can also allocate tokens incorrectly.

### B. Use adaptive batching and reliability-aware machine routing

I2 should become a conditional 12-to-24 Routine batching policy rather than a permanent increase to 24:

* Use larger Routine batches when enough Routine work is ready, the oldest Routine case retains adequate target margin, and protected Urgent/Complex slots are available.
* Return to smaller batches during machine degradation, outage recovery, high Urgent risk, or rising Routine age.
* Group compatible Urgent samples into small planned runs where possible rather than allowing every Urgent order to interrupt immediately.

I3 should be replaced with dynamic machine reservations:

* Reserve—but do not permanently dedicate—part of M1 for low-slack Urgent and Complex-panel cases.
* Permit Routine work on M1 when protected demand is absent.
* Cap M2 loading when its repeat rate or night-shift failure signal rises.
* Avoid assigning all Routine work to M2; distribute exposure so that one unreliable machine does not create a correlated repeat cohort.
* Treat reruns as a separate dispatch class: they need prompt service, but they should not automatically displace every new Urgent order.

**Why this is robust:** It balances setup efficiency, reliability, and deadline risk across both demand and machine scenarios.

**Assumptions:** Machine-level failure and repeat data are available promptly. Assays are technically routable between M1 and M2 where the policy proposes switching.

**Where it can fail:** Larger batches may amplify the effect of a bad run. Improved machine throughput can immediately overload Technical validation unless the WIP tokens are enforced.

### C. Combine early defect containment, guarded automation, and arrival smoothing

I6, I7, and I8 form a coherent front-end package:

* Use I6 to identify unusable samples within two hours and assign standardized reason codes.
* Feed defect reasons back by physician site, courier route, sample type, and time of collection so that the intervention reduces future defects rather than merely rejecting them sooner.
* Use I8 only for orders whose required digital metadata passes completeness and consistency checks.
* Send automated orders into a controlled Pre-analytical queue rather than directly releasing every order as fast as possible.
* Use courier pre-advice to communicate expected tube count, case mix, and arrival time before the physical route arrives.
* Apply I7 to smooth ordinary collections while preserving exceptions for Urgent or stability-sensitive samples.

The combined package addresses a peak pre-analytical exception share of about 12.3%, compared with roughly 7.0% normally.

**Why this is robust:** It reduces variability and prevents defective work from consuming assay and validation capacity. Arrival smoothing works across mix surges, courier disruptions, and some staffing disruptions.

**Assumptions:** Digital metadata is trustworthy, couriers and submitting sites can change schedules, and defect reasons can be traced to their sources.

**Where it can fail:** Incorrect but apparently complete metadata may pass automation and create more difficult downstream errors. Faster registration will expose Pre-analytical check and Assay as bottlenecks unless flow is paced.

### D. Create a qualified flex pool, but move staff only under dual-queue rules

I4 is useful as a resilience capability, not as a permanent peak-period assignment. Two Technical validators can be trained for accession correction, but deployment should require both:

* an accession-correction backlog above its trigger; and
* adequate remaining Technical-validation capacity.

At the observed peak, Technical validation is already at 101% utilization. Moving validators away under those conditions would exchange a 14-hour correction queue for a 16-hour—and likely growing—Technical-validation queue.

I5 should be forecast-activated for expected mix peaks and known specialist-coverage gaps. The evening shift should be synchronized with assay and Technical-validation completion forecasts rather than staffed on every evening regardless of need.

**Why this is robust:** Cross-training creates options without committing capacity to the wrong station. Evening Medical validation directly addresses the disproportionate downstream work generated by Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

**Assumptions:** Staff are credentialed for each task and shift handovers can preserve review quality.

**Where it can fail:** Evening validation can create fatigue, incomplete handovers, or a late release-result wave. Cross-trained staff cannot resolve simultaneous accession and Technical-validation overload.

### E. Prepare I10 as an overflow circuit breaker, not a default route

The partner-lab route should be operationally ready before winter, including:

* eligibility rules;
* chain-of-custody requirements;
* electronic result reconciliation;
* ownership of follow-up questions and repeats;
* explicit end-to-end turnaround measurement.

A Complex-panel order should be outsourced only when:

`predicted internal remaining time > predicted partner remaining time + transport/integration risk margin`

The 12-hour transport delay makes the route most defensible when the internal Specialist-review queue is already long enough to outweigh that penalty. Urgent orders should be presumptively excluded because the supplied evidence does not show that the partner can complete transport, assay, validation, integration, and release within 18 hours.

**Why this is robust:** It supplies optional capacity for Specialist-review, machine, and compound shocks without permanently restructuring ordinary flow.

**Assumptions:** Partner quality, assay compatibility, data exchange, and traceability are validated.

**Where it can fail:** Transport disruption, partner congestion, ambiguous responsibility, or result-integration delays can make outsourcing slower than internal processing.

### F. Exclude three unmodified proposals from the base design

* **Unmodified I1:** strict Urgent-first scheduling risks Routine and Complex starvation and fragments machine schedules.
* **Unmodified I3:** static machine dedication concentrates Routine work and repeat exposure on M2.
* **I9:** holding Routine releases creates deliberate waiting at a station that is not currently evidenced as the bottleneck. Medical validation → Release result is only five hours at peak, substantially below the major upstream waits. I9 should be reconsidered only if a separate release-workload measurement demonstrates a real constraint.

The robust base is therefore: finite-WIP/slack control, adaptive batching, reliability-aware routing, early quality containment, guarded automation, courier smoothing, and forecasted downstream coverage. Cross-trained reassignment and outsourcing remain conditional options.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

### External recollection time remains largely uncontrolled

Request recollection → Receive sample takes 60 hours at peak. Early triage can identify the problem sooner, but the laboratory cannot guarantee when the submitting physician and courier will supply a replacement. Recollection also consumes clerk time for explanations and can end in cancellation.

### Normal operations already lack sufficient Urgent tail margin

The normal Urgent p90 is 19 hours against an 18-hour target. The process is therefore not truly robust in the normal period; it is merely acceptable near the center of the distribution. Small disruptions can immediately create contractual breaches.

### Specialist review remains a concentrated dependency

The every-second-Friday absence is predictable, but the queue reaches 36 hours during peaks and the corresponding performance edge reaches 48 hours. Evening Medical-validation capacity alone cannot remove this dependency.

### Machine reliability and demand are coupled

M2 is faster but less reliable, and Repeat-after-failure work is clustered after M2 usage and night shifts. Routing more work to M2 can increase total machine demand rather than simply redistribute it.

### The flattened log can misidentify the bottleneck

A Complex-panel order with several tubes and assays is counted as one case, as is a single-assay Routine order. Order-level utilization and DFG frequencies may therefore understate:

* machine visits per Complex order;
* shared tubes or reruns;
* partial panel completion;
* the amount of validation work generated by one order.

The redesign should log Order, Tube, Assay, Machine run, and Validation task as linked objects. Until then, workload estimates remain uncertain.

### Redesign-induced vulnerability: upstream waiting

Finite-WIP control intentionally moves waiting upstream. This is preferable to invisible downstream congestion, but it can threaten sample stability or make the intake area operationally crowded. Stability-sensitive specimens must bypass the normal WIP cap.

### Redesign-induced vulnerability: larger correlated failures

Adaptive 24-sample batches improve setup efficiency, but a faulty preparation or M2 run can affect more samples at once, creating a large repeat cohort.

### Redesign-induced vulnerability: late validation and release waves

An evening Medical-validation shift may release many results in a short interval. This can overload result reconciliation, reporting, communication of critical values, or the following shift’s handover even though Release result is not presently the main bottleneck.

### Redesign-induced vulnerability: outsourcing complexity

I10 introduces a 12-hour transport leg, additional custody transfers, partner-specific quality risks, and ambiguity over ownership of repeats. It removes internal work only if the partner path is measured end to end rather than stopping the clock when the sample leaves the regional laboratory.

### Compound shocks may still require explicit service degradation

No internal scheduling policy can guarantee every Urgent target, every Routine target, full quality control, and unrestricted Complex-panel intake during simultaneous machine loss and specialist absence. The organization needs a pre-authorized escalation rule stating which lower-risk work can wait, who approves outsourcing, and how affected customers are informed.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

The dashboard should be stratified by **case type, machine, shift, courier route, and submitting site**. An overall average would conceal both the changing case mix and the concentration of repeats around M2 and night shifts. Signals should be refreshed intraday and reviewed formally each day.

| **SLA breach rate and p90 cycle time by case type**                         | Urgent p90 at or below **18h**; Routine p90 at or below **72h**                            | Verifies that local queue improvements translate into contractual performance. Median-only reporting is insufficient.     |
| **Percentage of cases with any queue over 12h**                             | Warning above **30%**; severe above **45%**                                                | The observed values move from 22% normally to 54% at peak. This captures propagation across stations.                     |
| **Overall rework plus assay-repeat rate by machine/shift**                  | Overall rework warning above **9%**, severe near or above **11%**                          | Normal overall rework is 7.5%; peak is 11.8%. Segmentation distinguishes mix effects from machine or shift deterioration. |
| **Recollection completion and cancellation outcomes**                       | Falling 60h return time and falling cancellation share                                     | Early triage is not successful if samples are rejected faster but replacements still return too late or are cancelled.    |
| **Cycle-time p50 and p90 for Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure cases** | Both percentiles stable or decreasing; p90 must not be sacrificed to improve Urgent median | Detects starvation and the hidden cost of Urgent priority rules.                                                          |
| **Internal versus partner-lab end-to-end turnaround and exception rate**    | Outsourcing should produce a positive net time benefit after transport and integration     | Prevents I10 from appearing successful merely because work leaves the internal queue.                                     |
