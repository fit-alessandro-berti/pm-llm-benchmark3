## 1. Stress scenarios

**Diagnostic baseline (derived from the logs).** Normal volume is about 2,300 orders per week (18,400 over 8 weeks). Peak volume is about 3,300 per week (9,900 over 3 weeks), a 43% increase. The increase is not spread evenly across types:

| Case type | Normal orders/wk | Peak orders/wk | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Routine | ~1,400 | ~1,390 | flat |
| Urgent | ~410 | ~960 | ×2.3 |
| Complex-panel | ~300 | ~660 | ×2.2 |
| Repeat-after-failure | ~185 | ~300 | ×1.6 |

Routine volume is flat. The stress comes from Urgent, Complex, and Repeat work, which compete for the same assay, validation, and medical resources.

Flow counts show the load shifts downstream:
- **Medical validation inflow** (technical validation  medical validation, plus holds and repeat-to-MV edges) is about 530/wk normally and about 1,370/wk at peak, roughly ×2.6.
- **Assay-run inflow** is about 2,280/wk normally and about 3,420/wk at peak, roughly ×1.5.
- **Repeat share at assay** rises from about 6.7% to about 14.8%.
- **Recollection share** rises from about 4.2% to about 8.0%.
- **Specialist hold share** of technical validation output rises from about 1.8% to about 7.5%.
- **Batch-wait share** of preparation output rises from about 4.4% to about 12.7%.
- **Urgent batch bypass** falls from 74% to 58%. Urgent cases are being batched more at exactly the moment they need priority.

Contract exposure is already marginal at normal load. Normal Urgent p90 is 19h against the 18h penalty threshold. At peak, the Urgent median is 17h and the p90 is 33h, so roughly half of Urgent cases are likely near or over the threshold. This is an inference from median and p90, not a directly reported breach rate.

**Scenarios.** Each scenario varies by case type and disruption type, not just volume.

**S1 – Demand mix shift (winter peak, no equipment fault).** Intake is about 3,300/wk with Urgent near 29% and Complex near 20%. Routine is flat. Pre-analytical (96%), assay (98%), technical validators (101%), and medical validators (93%) are all near or above capacity. Specialist review is at 87%.

**S2 – Assay-machine degradation (M2 failing, M1 partly out).** M2 fails more often and runs faster, so repeat-after-failure cases rise and cluster after night shifts and M2 use. Run S2 with normal mix and again with S1 mix to test whether it compounds.

**S3 – Medical validator or specialist-review absence.** One medical validator is out, and the specialist is unavailable on alternate Fridays. Complex cases (3.1× MV need, 2.4× hold probability) and Urgent cases queue at MV, which already has a 22h median queue at peak. HoldMV is 48h at peak.

**S4 – Courier arrival compression.** Pickups cluster at 10:00 and 17:00 and courier utilization is already 88% at peak. Arrivals hit registration (queue 2.8h at peak) and pre-analytical checks (queue 9.5h). Clerks working beyond 9h make more errors, and recollection loops consume intake capacity.

**S5 – Courier or transport delay.** Transport from collection to the lab slips by about 12h, for example from a route disruption or a partner-lab transfer. This consumes most of the 18h Urgent budget before testing starts.

**S6 – Compound winter wave.** S1 mix, M2 degraded (S2), one medical validator absent (S3), and recollection rising to about 12%. This is a stress test, not a forecast. It checks whether the redesign degrades gracefully.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The DFG and queue data show a chain of dependencies: Assay  Technical validation  Medical validation  Specialist review. Upstream, there is a second chain: Courier  Registration  Pre-analytical  Recollection loop  Accession correction  Preparation batching. Relieving one link tends to push load into the next.

**S1 – Peak mix shift.**
- Sequence: **Assay (98%, queue 18h)  Technical validation (101%, queue 16h, output 42% to MV)  Medical validation (93%, queue 22h)  Specialist review (87%, queue 36h, HoldMV 48h).**
- Also: Batch wait (entry 14h median, 22h to assay) grows for Routine work, while Urgent cases bypass batching less often at peak.
- If I1 (strict Urgent-first) is applied at assay, Urgent cases reach technical validators sooner. Technical validators are already over capacity, so the queue moves to validation and then to medical validation. Routine cases are starved at assay and pile into batch wait. The result is a larger total WIP, not a faster system.
- If I2 (batch size 24) is applied, preparation efficiency improves but the Batch wait  Assay edge (already 9h normal, 22h peak) doubles or worse. Urgent bypass becomes more frequent, which fragments the machine schedule and adds setup time at assay, the most loaded station.

**S2 – M2 degradation.**
- Sequence: **M2 failure  Repeat assay loop (repeatassay 10h normal, 26h peak)  Assay (M1 saturated)  Technical validation bursts  Medical validation.**
- Repeats consume assay capacity twice. Each failed M2 run re-enters the assay queue, and repeat cases are clustered after M2-heavy shifts.
- If I3 (dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex, route all Routine to M2) is applied, M2 receives all Routine work during high-load periods, which raises its failure exposure. Failed Routine runs re-enter the queue. Repeat share rises, and repeats then compete with Urgent cases on M1. M1 has the least slack at peak (98%).
- Repeats also create bursty arrivals at technical validation, which is already over capacity.

**S3 – Medical validator or specialist absence.**
- Sequence: **Medical validation (queue 22h, 93%)  Specialist review (queue 36h, 87%)  HoldMV (48h)  Release.** Upstream, technical validators hold completed results that cannot be released until MV clears them.
- Urgent cases breach the 18h contract. Complex cases accumulate in hold.
- Inferred: managers often respond by pulling technical validators into medical or hold work. That reduces technical validation capacity, which is already at 101%, so assay output then blocks at technical validation.
- If I5 (evening MV shift) is added, the shift covers medical validation but still depends on technical validators to supply cleared results. Without evening technical validation coverage, the shift starves.
- I10 (outsourcing Complex overflow) relieves specialist review but adds at least 12h of transport. Complex median is already 111h at peak. The transport delay and traceability burden are most damaging when the disruption is short-term, because the overflow route is slower than waiting out a single absence.

**S4 – Courier arrival compression.**
- Sequence: **Courier intake (60%  88% utilization, queue 7.5h)  Registration (queue 2.8h)  Pre-analytical (queue 9.5h, 96%)  Recollection loop (42h normal, 60h peak)  Accession correction (queue 14h, 91%)  Test preparation batching.**
- Compressed arrivals create batches of samples that enter preparation together, which lengthens batch wait.
- Clerks past 9h have a higher error rate, which raises pre-analytical defects, which raises recollection loops, which consumes more intake capacity. This is a reinforcing loop.
- If I8 (automated registration for complete digital metadata) is applied, registration load falls but pre-analytical checks become the first visible bottleneck. Pre-analytical is already at 96%, so the result is more queue, not less.
- If I7 (courier slot smoothing) is applied without aligning registration staffing, the arrivals are spread out but registration still gets bursts when rebooked slots coincide.

**S5 – Transport delay.**
- Sequence: **Transport delay (lost time)  Urgent 18h budget compressed  Assay and validation surge later in the day  Medical validation queue spikes.**
- Urgent cases lose budget before testing starts. Priority rules then push a surge of Urgent work into assay and validation in a short window, creating a second wave of queues.
- I10 is not appropriate for Urgent cases because the transport delay is roughly 12h.

**S6 – Compound.**
- Sequence: **Pre-analytical defects  recollection loops  accession correction and intake clerks  fewer usable samples at assay; M2 failures  repeat loops  technical validation bursts; MV absence  hold accumulation and forced re-prioritization.**
- The critical point: several bottlenecks are active at once, so no single intervention can keep Urgent within 18h. The system needs admission control and case-type limits, not only faster processing.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The following interventions were chosen because they stay useful across several scenarios, not because they lower the historical average.

**R1. Reserved capacity with aging-based priority (modified I1).**
- Reserve a fixed share of M1 slots and technical-validation time for Urgent and Complex work. Routine cases are promoted to Urgent priority once their age exceeds a limit, for example 24h.
- *Why robust:* bounds Urgent queue without starving Routine indefinitely, and the aging rule bounds Routine p90, which is already 120h at peak.
- *Overloads:* technical validation, because Urgent work arrives faster. Mitigation: cap technical-validation intake for Urgent to the reserved share.
- *Assumptions:* Urgent share stays below about 35%. Reserved capacity is actually idle when Urgent demand is low, which is a cost in normal periods.
- *Failure modes:* if Urgent exceeds the reserve, the reserve becomes a queue of its own. Also a single-point failure if M1 is down.

**R2. Early pre-analytical defect triage at intake (I6, moved upstream).**
- Apply rule-based screening at registration and courier receipt, with a 2h decision SLA. Route defects to recollection before they enter preparation.
- *Why robust:* defects and recollection loops feed almost every stress scenario. Recollection loops take 42h normal and 60h peak, and they consume intake and accession capacity.
- *Overloads:* the pre-analytical check at 96% utilization. To avoid this, screening must be rules-based and performed at intake, not as additional manual review at pre-analytical.
- *Assumptions:* defect rules are clinically validated and specimen-quality criteria are documented.
- *Failure modes:* over-rejection creates recollection loops that are longer than the original delay. Under-rejection moves defects into assay repeats.

**R3. Courier slot smoothing and clerk shift staggering (I7 plus shift design).**
- Shift pickup slots from the 10:00 and 17:00 peaks to a wider set of windows. Stagger clerk shifts so no clerk exceeds 9h at a time.
- *Why robust:* it reduces the arrival spike that drives S4 and reduces errors from fatigue, at low implementation cost.
- *Overloads:* registration in the shifted windows, if the new windows coincide with clerk shift changes. Must be checked against registration capacity.
- *Failure modes:* clinic resistance and uneven routing. Only a partial fix for courier delays (S5).

**R4. Tiered medical validation with rule-based release for clean Routine and Urgent results (new).**
- Results that pass QC, delta checks, and reference-range checks go directly to release. Complex cases and flagged results keep full medical validation.
- *Why robust:* medical validation is the strongest bottleneck across S1, S3, and S6. At peak, 42% of technical-validation output goes to MV, and much of that is routine.
- *Overloads:* technical validation, which becomes the sole gate for auto-released results.
- *Assumptions:* clinical governance accepts the rule set; the flags are reliable.
- *Failure modes:* silent errors and reduced sensitivity to unusual patients. Regulatory and clinical sign-off is required.

**R5. Variable batch size with an age cap (modified I2).**
- Keep batches at 12 by default. Allow 24 only when Urgent share is below a threshold and assay queue is below its trigger. Release partial batches once the oldest Routine sample reaches a maximum age, for example 8h.
- *Why robust:* it captures setup efficiency without forcing the Urgent bypass or lengthening Batch wait Assay indefinitely.
- *Overloads:* assay machine schedules, which fragment when Urgent bypass is frequent. Limit bypass by using a single priority rule.
- *Failure modes:* more partial batches reduce setup efficiency during moderate load. The age-cap threshold needs monitoring.

**R6. Cross-train preparation bench staff for accession correction (modified I4).**
- Preparation benches are at 69% normal and 89% peak utilization, so they have more slack than technical validators. Cross-training them relieves the two-person accession team (91% at peak) without drawing on technical validation.
- *Why robust:* it protects the technical validation bottleneck, which is already over capacity (101%).
- *Assumptions:* four weeks of training lead time. Accession correction skills are teachable to bench staff.
- *Failure modes:* lead time means the benefit comes after the next wave. Bench staff pulled into corrections can slow batching at peak.

**R7. Scenario-triggered mode switching (Section 4).**
- A single fixed configuration cannot cover all scenarios. Policies must switch on and off based on leading indicators.

**Why I3 is modified.** I3 as proposed sends all Routine work to M2 during peak, which raises repeat risk (S2). Instead, use soft reservation: M1 is reserved for Urgent and Complex, Routine uses M2 by default, and Routine spills to M1 when M1's queue is short and M2 is failing.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Each policy has a trigger to switch on and a separate condition to switch off. Off conditions are stricter than on conditions (hysteresis) to avoid flapping.

**P1 – Peak Mix Mode (S1).**
- *On:* Urgent plus Complex share of the trailing 4h intake is above 35%, or weekly volume is above 1.3× baseline for two consecutive days.
- *Actions:* enable R1 reserved capacity; cap Routine batch size at 12 when the Urgent queue exceeds 4h; start Complex preparation first in the morning; add an evening medical-validation shift (I5) limited to 16:00–22:00, paired with technical-validation coverage so cleared results are available to the shift.
- *Off:* Urgent plus Complex share below 25% for 24h.

**P2 – M2 Degradation Mode (S2).**
- *On:* M2 downtime exceeds 2h in 24h, or M2 repeat rate exceeds twice the M1 repeat rate over the last 50 runs.
- *Actions:* move Routine to M1 spill when the M1 queue is below 4h; route repeats to M1 with a QC check before re-run; flag M2 cases for re-run review; open a maintenance window.
- *Off:* M2 stable (downtime under 1h and repeat rate within 1.2× M1) for 48h.

**P3 – Medical Validation or Specialist Absence Mode (S3).**
- *On:* medical validation queue exceeds 12h for two consecutive hours, or the specialist is absent on a scheduled day.
- *Actions:* Urgent results go to medical validation first. Activate R4 tiered release for clean Routine results. Use a senior cover rota. Consider I10 overflow for Complex only when the hold age exceeds 24h and expected MV turnaround exceeds 36h, and only with traceability controls in place.
- *Off:* hold queue below 8h for 12h.

**P4 – Courier Compression Mode (S4).**
- *On:* more than 35% of the day's pickups fall within one 60-minute window, or registration queue exceeds 2h.
- *Actions:* stagger pickups (R3); extend registration coverage in the peak window; use pre-registration from digital metadata (I8) only for orders with complete metadata, and only once pre-analytical capacity has been checked.
- *Off:* courier queue below 2h for 24h.

**P5 – Transport Delay Mode (S5).**
- *On:* transport lag exceeds 6h for Urgent samples.
- *Actions:* pre-book Urgent pickups; use local testing priority for Urgent; do not route Urgent to the partner lab.
- *Off:* transport lag returns to normal for 24h.

**P6 – Pre-analytical Defect Surge (S6 component).**
- *On:* recollection rate exceeds 8% over 24h.
- *Actions:* increase triage staffing at intake; prioritize accession correction over new preparation; feedback to ordering clinics on common defects.
- *Off:* recollection rate below 5% for 48h.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Medical validation and specialist review remain thin.** Even with tiered release and evening coverage, one specialist and a Friday absence pattern create a single-point failure. Complex cases will still breach during extended absence.
- **Technical validation is over capacity at peak (101%).** Most redesign options push more work through it. It is the least-protected bottleneck in the redesign.
- **M2 failure exposure.** Soft reservation mitigates but does not remove repeat clustering after M2-heavy shifts.
- **Urgent 18h contract under S5.** A 12h transport delay consumes most of the budget. No intervention inside the lab fully protects Urgent in that case. Pre-booking and contract renegotiation are the only real fixes.
- **Redesign-induced vulnerabilities:**
  - *Reserved capacity* (R1) is idle at normal load. It is also a single point of failure if M1 is down, and it may be underused if Urgent demand falls quickly.
  - *Tiered medical validation* (R4) reduces human review, so errors that pass rule checks become harder to detect. Clinical sign-off and periodic audits are needed.
  - *Upstream triage* (R2) can increase recollection loops if rejection thresholds are too strict. Each loop costs about 42–60h.
  - *Mode switching* can oscillate. Hysteresis helps, but staff may spend time reconfiguring instead of processing.
  - *Partner overflow* (I10) adds traceability and chain-of-custody obligations that the current logs do not measure.
  - *Cross-training* (R6) takes four weeks. Benefits arrive after the next peak unless training starts now.
- **Data-consistency check.** Medical-validator utilization rises from 58% to 93%, while the flow counts imply weekly medical-validation inflow up about 2.6×. Either staffing or overtime changed during peak, or validation effort per case fell. Confirm which before setting capacity targets.

**Lagging outcomes (reviewed daily and weekly).**
- Urgent released within 18h: target at least 90% in peak; currently about half of peak Urgent cases are near the threshold.
- Routine released within 72h: target at least 90%.
- Complex median and p90 by week.
- Rework rate (peak baseline 11.8%) and repeat rate (peak baseline 14.8% at assay).
- Recollection-loop cycle time (peak baseline 60h).
- Count of Urgent contract breaches and penalty exposure.

Leading indicators should trigger the policies in Section 4 before lagging outcomes deteriorate. Lagging outcomes confirm whether the policies worked and whether any intervention shifted load to another station.
