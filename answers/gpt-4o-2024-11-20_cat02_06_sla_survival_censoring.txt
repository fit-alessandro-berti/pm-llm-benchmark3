# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** The clock starts at the `Submit` event for each case.  
**Time horizon:** The SLA is breached if 72 elapsed hours have passed without resolution (`Resolve`).  
**Event(s):**  
- Primary event of interest: `Resolve` (case resolution).  
- Competing events: `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`.  
- Open cases reflect censoring at the observation cutoff.

**Estimands and contributions:**
1. **Estimand 1 - Probability of not being resolved by 72 hours:** Measures the risk of SLA breach. For example:
   - Case S03 (`resolved` at 76 hours): Contributes as an SLA breach.
   - Case S09 (`open` at 92 hours): Contributes as a censored SLA breach since it is still unresolved past the SLA.
   - Case S06 (`withdrawn` at 27 hours): Removed from the risk set at 27 hours due to a competing event.
   - Case S08 (`transferred` at 68 hours): Censored or competing event based on assumptions about transfers (detailed in Section 2).

2. **Estimand 2 - Cause-specific cumulative incidence of resolution after 72 hours:** Focuses on when and if resolution occurs specifically after the SLA window.  
   - Case S03: Contributes as a late resolution (after 72 hours).
   - Case S09: Censored at 92 hours for non-resolution by observation cutoff.
   - Case S06: Removed from resolution modeling due to an earlier withdrawal.
   - Case S08: Excluded or treated as competing (based on transfer handling).

### Treatment of censoring
Right censoring applies directly to `open` cases (whose status beyond the cutoff is unknown). Competing risks (withdraw/reject/transfer) must be distinguished as terminal outcomes versus purely censored data. Transfers especially introduce uncertainty about eventual resolution.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| Observed Status | Event Indicator  | Competing Risk Code | Censoring Rule                      | Sensitivity Alternatives                                   |
|------------------|------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| `resolved`       | 1 (resolution)  | 0                    | Not censored                        | Not applicable                                            |
| `withdrawn`      | 0               | 1 (withdrawn)        | Censored for resolution estimation  | Treated as terminal for some analyses                     |
| `rejected`       | 0               | 2 (rejected)         | Censored for resolution estimation  | Treated as terminal for some analyses                     |
| `transferred`    | 0               | 3 (transferred)      | Ambiguous censoring—depends on external resolution assumptions | Alternative 1: treated as terminal; 2: censored; 3: excluded entirely |
| `open`           | 0               | None                 | Right censored at observation cutoff | Not applicable                                            |

**Assigning transfers (3 plausible options):**
1. **As terminal competing events:** Transfers result in loss of internal visibility and are assumed to end the case internally.  
2. **As censored:** Transfers reflect end of internal observation but allow for potential future resolution.  
3. **Excluded:** Transfers are removed from SLA modeling due to unverifiable outcomes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis based on completed `resolved` cases would exclude:
1. **Survivorship bias:** Resolving cases (e.g., S01, S02) differ from cases that are open (e.g., S09 or S26), transferred (e.g., S08 or S21), or withdrawn (e.g., S06). Ignoring these cases skews risks and patterns.  
2. **Informative censoring:** Transfers like S08 and S21 (potential terminal status) likely depend on process factors (e.g., escalation). Cases unresolved past the cutoff often exhibit long `last_event_gap_h` (e.g., S26).  
3. **Competing risks:** Ignoring cases ended in `Withdraw` or `Reject` mistakenly attributes all unresolved cases to `open` status.
4. **Cohort imbalance:** Rollout irregularities between A and B cohorts (e.g., automation differences) affect SLA times but would be masked by unresolved trends.

**Example bias:** Cases above 72 hours but unresolved (S09: open at 92 hours, S10: escalated open at 47 hours) are excluded from naive analysis, understating tail risk.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Proposed approaches:**
1. **Primary:** Cause-specific cumulative incidence using the Aalen-Johansen method. This accounts for competing risks (resolution, withdrawal, rejection, and transfer). It provides actionable insights into breach risks under competing events.  
2. **Secondary:** Multi-state modeling representing activity stages (`Submit`, `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, etc.) as transitions. This incorporates process dynamics like rework or escalation.  

**Method comparison:**
- **Kaplan-Meier:** Overestimates SLA compliance since it cannot account for competing risks.  
- **Cumulative incidence:** Accurately disentangles competing events.  
- **Cause-specific hazard model:** Models risk drivers but focuses on instantaneous hazards rather than cumulative outcomes.  
- **Subdistribution hazards:** Directly models cumulative incidence curves, though less intuitive for non-specialists.  
- **Multi-state models:** Captures sequential process patterns and stage-specific risks, critical for SLA governance.  

**Recommended:** Start with cumulative incidence and cause-specific hazards as primary methods. Combine with multi-state modeling for process-aware fine-grained insights.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Proposed process-derived features:**
1. **Current stage:** Last event in the prefix (e.g., `Review`, `Escalate`).  
2. **Path patterns:** Categorize prefixes (e.g., Escalate > Transfer).  
3. **Rework counts:** Instances of `Rework`.  
4. **Escalation timing:** First occurrence of `Escalate`.  
5. **Inactivity gaps:** `last_event_gap_h`.

**Feature types:**
- **Baseline:** Priority, cohort.  
- **Time-varying:** Escalation timing, inactivity gap.  
- **Post-treatment:** Rework count (after initial `Review`).

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

1. **Transfer treatment:** Compare with transfers (a) as terminal events, (b) as censored, and (c) excluded. Observed SLA breach rates could differ substantially.  
2. **Withdrawal/rejection:** Treating these as terminal disproportionately affects short duration cases (e.g., S05 and S06).  
3. **Cohort-policy effects:** Automation in cohort B could bias time-to-resolution and escalation rates. Models must stratify by cohort.

**Potential reversals:**  
- If transfers contribute substantial SLA breaches, tail risks may be higher.  
- If withdrawals/rejections are treated as terminal, resolution rates drop.

---

# 7. Operational interpretation

To implement survival outputs:
- **Alerts:** Identify high-open cases near SLA (e.g., S15, S25) for immediate attention.  
- **Workload actions:** Adjust task assignments for escalating cases.  
- **SLA governance:** Track unresolved tail risks by priority (`P1`, etc.).  

**Fairness:** Check resolution rates across cohorts (e.g., B may benefit unfairly from automation). Ensure prioritization aligns with P1 versus P2/P3 urgency.

**Calibration:** Validate models against known resolved/withdrawn rates to ensure realistic SLA breach probabilities.
