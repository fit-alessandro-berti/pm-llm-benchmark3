# 2. Integrated explanation

The first part of the shift is consistent with warm-up, but it also contains a difficult carryover workload: mean complexity and high-priority share are highest in positions 1–5, and seniors preferentially take complex backlog. The fall in average service time and errors through position 30 could therefore reflect both genuine familiarization and a change to easier cases, activities or workers. Experience differences may also reflect assignment and training rather than skill alone.

Later, mean service time continues to fall while errors and seven-day rework rise sharply, especially in positions 51 onward as the queue grows. The WIP table shows the same speed-quality association within both broad complexity bands. This is consistent with people accelerating or using batches under pressure, potentially reducing review time, but those associations do not establish the cause. Delayed quality checks and late correction bursts can shift when errors are observed; short recorded times may also reflect batching or incomplete start-time capture. Fatigue could contribute, but these data do not distinguish it from pressure and other time-varying factors. In contrast, later low-WIP quality is relatively stable for seniors and mid-level workers, and improves for juniors, so late work does not uniformly produce more errors.

The shifts combine different demand, backlog, urgency and staffing conditions. Day-shift queues rise strongly and its last-two-hour error rate is highest; night work has fewer events and more urgent exceptions. Clock time, elapsed time on shift and workload move together, so no observed shift difference alone establishes a circadian effect. Automation and its human review add another layer: more bot corrections at high queue may indicate output quality, case mix, review selection or changes in logging. The operational concern is faster apparent throughput accompanied by quality losses and downstream work, not a proven single explanation.

# 3. Proposed analyses

Define the unit for each outcome before modeling: an activity execution for service time and event errors, a case or originating decision for downstream rework, and a shift for staffing and workload. Clarify whether error_flag denotes an error in the current activity, a later discovery, or both. Link correction and quality-check events to the originating work using case identifiers and, where needed, explicit decision or work-item IDs. Use a seven-day observation window for rework, exclude or separately report originating events without complete follow-up, and distinguish the time of an error from its detection. Report counts, denominators and uncertainty, including sparse worker and shift cells.

Validate timestamps, time zones and overnight shift boundaries; ensure start precedes completion, identify missing or zero durations and concurrent work, and distinguish elapsed activity duration from active labor time. Verify whether WIP and queue are center-wide, team-level or worker-level snapshots, include the focal case or not, and are captured before assignment and task start. Reconstruct them from lifecycle transitions only if the log is sufficiently complete. Bot events should be analyzed separately from human work. Verify the complexity scale, priority definitions, batching identifiers and stable shift IDs. A late sequence position is observed only for shifts with enough completed work, so compare like-for-like workers and shifts and account for differing shift lengths, volume, activity and selection into high positions.

- **Within-resource and within-shift comparisons:** Plot and model early and later events for the same worker, with worker and shift effects or appropriate hierarchical random effects. Use activity-specific service-time distributions, medians and upper quantiles as well as means. Separate elapsed minutes, sequence position and repeated activity count; they are correlated, so assess overlap and collinearity rather than interpreting each coefficient as an independent effect automatically.
- **WIP, mix and assignment:** Stratify WIP and queue by measured thresholds, including the exact boundaries at 30 and 70, and inspect continuous nonlinear relationships. Adjust for pre-task complexity, priority, activity, backlog age and known assignment rules; include WIP-by-complexity and WIP-by-experience interactions. Standardize predicted outcomes to a common case mix and show the distribution of assignments and regions without comparable observations. Do not control for variables that occur after the task starts when estimating the total effect of WIP.
- **Time and experience:** Include shift type, local clock time, day of week, time since shift start, actual shift duration and worker experience; collect prior sleep, breaks, overtime and rotation where feasible. Compare overlapping clock times across shifts and different positions at comparable WIP. Flexible time terms and sensitivity analyses are preferable to assuming linear effects. Confounding and lack of overlap can prevent separate identification of fatigue and circadian effects.
- **Quality and downstream work:** Model error probability and seven-day rework with appropriate denominators, a worker/shift cluster structure and interactions; link downstream corrections to the originating event and bot decision. Compare detection coverage and delay, plus case completion and total rework time. Avoid treating a correction event's timestamp as the time the original error occurred. Check whether later apparent errors arise from deferred checks. Assess repeat cases and uncertainty with clustered intervals or a hierarchical model.
- **Batching and flow:** Detect temporal batches by resource and activity, validate them against consecutive runs and any actual queue-batch ID, and examine start/completion overlap and exceptionally short durations. Compare downstream quality for batches and comparable non-batches. Use dotted charts and time distributions to inspect bursts, handovers and delayed checks. Examine performance DFG edges and case paths for queues and rework, recognizing that aggregated paths do not prove individual causation.
- **Causal follow-up:** Pre-specify outcomes and compare eligible, comparable periods or teams. Randomized or phased assignment of extra senior coverage, alerts or breaks can help estimate effects, including effects mediated through queue changes. Workload and routing spill across workers and shifts, so randomize at an appropriate team/shift level and measure spillovers. Observational adjustment alone cannot remove unmeasured assignment, demand or quality-detection bias.

Pilot breaks or bounded assignment randomization separately or in a design that can distinguish their effects. Use safety and service guardrails and review whether improvements persist through downstream rework rather than relying on faster activity completion.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Workers receive different cases, shifts, resources and opportunities for review. Seniors' early complex backlog, juniors' late batches and night staff's urgent exceptions make raw rankings especially misleading. Workers who report errors or handle cases that receive more checks can appear worse than workers whose errors are less often detected. Small counts and delayed outcomes make rates unstable; show denominators and uncertainty, suppress unreliable individual comparisons, and invite workers to explain process context.

Recorded timestamps may capture system entry instead of actual start or finish, and elapsed service time may include interruptions or parallel work. Error and rework flags can be incomplete, inconsistent or discovered later. Complexity, queue ownership and assignment history may also be missing or inaccurate. Audit measurement and changes in logging before interpreting trends, and use a consistent follow-up window.

Queue pressure can be created by staffing, demand, scheduling, routing and management targets outside a worker's control. Assess those system conditions before attributing outcomes to individuals. Bot errors and human correction coverage need separate measurement; a human correction is not automatically a human error, and fewer corrections need not mean better automation. Avoid punitive use of raw late-shift error rates, protect individual data, involve staff in interpreting results, and preserve a route for correcting inaccurate records.
