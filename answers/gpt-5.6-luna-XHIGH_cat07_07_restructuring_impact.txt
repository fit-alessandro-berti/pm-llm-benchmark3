# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring changes both the routing topology and the ownership model. It is therefore likely to reduce some visible local transfers while increasing contention at shared queues. The management estimates should be treated as hypotheses to test, not as guaranteed effects.

| Restructuring action | Mechanism | Expected observable process-mining effect | Main qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech into one Technical Shared Service | Specialist capacity is pooled and cases are routed to a common queue | Fewer location-to-location or ad hoc transfers; more caseworker-to-specialist transfers; higher resource concentration and higher working-together density around the shared service | The proposed frequency already rises from 36 to 41 per 100 for caseworker-to-technical work, and the return transfer rises from 31 to 36. A nominally simpler organization can therefore create more recorded queue-mediated interactions |
| Route all high-complexity work centrally | A common specialist queue receives a larger and more variable workload | Higher arrival-rate variability, longer queue waits, more queue episodes per complex case, and a wider cycle-time distribution for high-complexity cases | The average may improve for East while the tail worsens for everyone when the queue is busy |
| Put technical, legal/risk, and difficult quality work in the same shared-service area | Several demand streams compete for partly overlapping scarce expertise | More resource-sharing and subcontracting-like patterns; resource utilization and queue occupancy rise; waiting time becomes sensitive to mix and priority rules | Legal and Risk already run at 93% and 91% utilization, so a reported 15% utilization increase is not a safe blanket target without capacity or triage changes |
| Keep Intake and simple Eligibility local | The front of the process remains geographically close to customers and local teams | Bot-to-intake and intake-to-caseworker handovers should remain near their current level; local early-stage performance may look stable | Loss of informal technical advice can cause later specialist referral, clarification, or rework even when early activities are unchanged |
| Shared routing rules and a shared advice queue | Standard rules should reduce ad hoc routing and make expertise searchable | Lower cross-team handovers (management estimate: 6 to 3 per 100), more standardized activity sequences, and potentially fewer variant types | If notes or queue states are ambiguous, cases may be pulled repeatedly, producing repeated resource changes that are not captured as simple cross-team handovers |
| Remove one local supervisor and create a shared approval queue | Approval is pooled and decisions may become more consistent | Local caseworker-to-approval handovers rise from 42 to 49 per 100; approval waiting time rises from 2.4h to an estimated 3.0h; approval-to-implementation rises from 39 to 43 | Fewer named local approvers can reduce accountability, increase clarification loops, and make an approval appear complete while ownership remains unresolved |
| Standardize specialist and approval decisions | More common rules and peer review can reduce variation | Lower activity/resource variant entropy, more similar decision paths, and possibly lower rework for routine cases | A lower number of variants is not by itself evidence of better quality; it can indicate over-routing or suppressed legitimate exceptions |
| Pool people across locations | Absence in one location can be covered by other specialists | Less absence-driven cross-team transfer and potentially less variance between teams; the modeled cross-team wait falls from 8.9h to 6.0h | Resilience improves only if there is spare capacity, cross-training, good case notes, and a clear fallback. A single central queue is also a common point of failure |
| Remove local technical exposure | Difficult cases no longer routinely pass through local technical staff | Falling local-expertise flags, fewer local caseworker–technical loops, reduced activity/resource similarity within teams, and weaker growth in local technical capability | The same reduction in local loops could be a positive efficiency effect or a negative learning effect; it must be read alongside rework, escalation, and staff capability measures |
| Apply one queue policy to all teams and segments | Central rules allocate work across demand pools | More equal median waits are possible, but priority, language, geography, customer segment, and case mix can produce unequal wait distributions | Compare distributions within comparable strata, not just team averages. A queue can be formally uniform but substantively unfair |

In the short run, the likely topology is a hub-and-spoke pattern: local intake and casework feed a small number of shared resources, and the shared resources feed multiple local teams. The hub should be visible in the handover-of-work, working-together, and resource-similarity networks. The key distinction is between fewer *organizational boundaries* and fewer *case-level transfers*. The former may fall while the latter, waiting episodes, and reassignments rise.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

The scenarios below are conditional on the proposed routing and on whether management supplies enough capacity, queue discipline, and ownership controls.

### Optimistic scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Cross-team and absence-cover transfers fall as estimated. Routine cases stay local, while genuinely complex cases make one well-formed transfer to the shared technical service and one clear return. The handover network becomes more standardized, with fewer unexplained resource changes and fewer repeated pulls from the advice queue.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Pooling smooths East’s peaks and uses North/South capacity during their slack periods. Median technical and approval waits stay close to, or below, current levels despite higher routing frequency. Legal/Risk receives better triage and does not materially exceed its already high utilization. Queue wait is separated from active service time and remains stable across shifts.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median cycle time improves, especially for East and routine cases. The 90th and 95th percentiles improve or remain stable, rather than merely being hidden by a better mean. High-complexity cases may still be slower than simple cases but do not develop a new extreme tail.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standard decisions and access to broader specialist knowledge reduce avoidable rework, clarification loops, and post-approval corrections. Local expertise is preserved through consultation, templates, and feedback. Rework falls within complexity and specialty strata, not only in the aggregate.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Each case retains a named local owner; the shared service owns its advice or decision within a defined service-level contract; the supervisor or approval queue has an explicit decision owner. Handover reasons and acceptance/rejection states are recorded rather than inferred from resource changes.
- **Fairness implications:** Comparable cases receive comparable waits and outcomes across North, South, and East and across customer segments. Regional or language accommodations remain available. East catches up without North/South being disadvantaged by the pooled queue.
- **Early warning signs:** Technical queue occupancy has headroom; the wait-to-service ratio is stable; reassignment counts do not rise; local-expertise flags do not collapse; local staff report that they can obtain timely advice; and 90th/95th percentile waits do not diverge by team, specialty, or segment.

### Central scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Cross-team handovers decline, but transfers into and out of shared services increase roughly as management predicts. Cases have more central queue events and more resources touching them. The visible local loop is replaced by a shared-service loop; repeat advice requests and reassignment counts rise modestly for complex cases.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Routine work is acceptable, while high-complexity, legal, risk, security, and difficult quality work compete for attention. Median waits rise moderately toward the estimates of 4.0h for technical work, 3.0h for approval, and 10.5h for Legal/Risk. The tail is more volatile than the median, particularly after demand spikes or absences.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Overall median cycle time is flat or modestly better because local work is pooled and East benefits. The 90th percentile is flat or somewhat worse. The distribution becomes more separated by complexity and specialty: simple cases improve, but high-complexity cases carry more queue time.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Routine rework is flat or slightly lower. Complex-case rework, post-specialist clarification, and quality-check rework are flat or higher because local context is less available. Cases with missing or inconsistent notes have more loops. Any aggregate quality gain is concentrated in low-complexity work.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** The formal routing policy is clear, but practical ownership is ambiguous at queue boundaries. Caseworkers may wait for “the queue” rather than a named person, and shared specialists may regard an advice response as their endpoint even when implementation or customer explanation remains unresolved.
- **Fairness implications:** Team medians may converge, but high-complexity or language-sensitive customer segments wait longer. A FIFO queue may disadvantage urgent cases unless priority is explicitly audited. North and South may lose local responsiveness while East’s average improves.
- **Early warning signs:** More `handover_reason` values indicate clarification or missing context; technical and approval queue waits rise while service time does not; repeated resources appear on the same activity; high-complexity cases have a growing 90th percentile; and local staff report declining confidence or capability even before customer outcomes move.

### Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern:** The number of named cross-team transfers falls, creating a misleading appearance of simplification, but central queue-mediated handovers, reassignments, rejected referrals, and repeated advice requests increase. A specialist may touch a case, return it for missing context, and receive it again. Handover counts based only on team changes understate the actual coordination burden.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** The shared technical hub becomes a bottleneck. Technical, legal/risk, and quality work compete, and near-capacity Legal/Risk queues become unstable. Queue waits and abandonment/reprioritization rise sharply after peaks or absences. A pooled resource without effective spare capacity amplifies rather than absorbs variability.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** The mean can remain stable because routine cases are fast and some East work improves, while high-complexity cases and disadvantaged segments accumulate very long waits. The 90th/95th percentile, maximum, and deadline-miss rate worsen; cycle-time multimodality appears by team, specialty, priority, or customer segment.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Missing regional context, generic decisions, and delayed clarification increase rework, failed quality checks, repeat customer contacts, and post-implementation corrections. Local technical skills atrophy, increasing future referral volume and creating a self-reinforcing load on the hub.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** No one clearly owns a case while it is queued. Approval decisions are standardized but difficult to explain or challenge, exceptions are bounced between the caseworker, shared specialist, and queue coordinator, and accountability for SLA misses is disputed.
- **Fairness implications:** Customers requiring local language, regional accommodation, or additional explanation experience more transfers and longer waits. Teams with complex portfolios, especially East if its mix differs, are compared unfairly with teams carrying simpler work. Priority rules may systematically favor high-volume or easy cases, and resource allocation may disadvantage a location or segment without an explicit intent to do so.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue occupancy approaches saturation; the wait-to-service ratio and queue age rise; reassignments and `handover_reason` values for missing information increase; local-expertise flags and local technical participation decline; repeated quality checks and rework cases rise; and tail SLA misses grow before the overall mean moves.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Leading indicators should be reviewed daily or weekly during the pilot. Lagging indicators require enough completed cases and outcomes to avoid reacting to noise. Every comparison should be stratified by team, case complexity, required specialty, priority, and customer segment, and should report counts and confidence intervals where feasible.

| Timing | Indicator | How to measure from the log | What it would signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leading: days | Handover rate by type | Consecutive-event resource/team changes per case, classified by `handover_reason`; report per 100 cases and per completed case | Whether local loops truly fall or are being replaced by repeat queue interactions |
| Leading: days | Queue-entry and queue-exit wait | Time from local handoff or queue entry to specialist/approver acceptance; use event timestamps and a queue-state convention | Direct pressure on Technical, approval, Legal/Risk, and Quality queues |
| Leading: days | Wait-to-service ratio | Waiting time divided by active service time by activity, resource, and segment | Bottleneck formation that an end-to-end average can hide |
| Leading: days | Queue age and backlog | Open cases by queue, age percentiles, oldest case, and arrivals/completions per day | Whether capacity is keeping up and whether backlog is becoming stale |
| Leading: days | Resource concentration and utilization | Volume, active time, concurrent assignments, and work share by `org:resource`; compare against capacity and the claimed 15% utilization gain | Whether utilization rises productively or simply approaches saturation |
| Leading: days | Reassignment and referral churn | `reassignment_count`, repeated activity-resource pairs, rejected referrals, and number of shared-queue visits per case | Hidden coordination cost and unclear routing |
| Leading: days | Case ownership continuity | Number of distinct resources/teams per case, time without an owner, and missing owner at each queue state | Accountability loss even when formal handover counts fall |
| Leading: days | Local expertise exposure | `local_expertise_flag`, local technical participation before referral, and requests for informal advice | Early loss of local knowledge and a possible future rework mechanism |
| Leading: days/weeks | Variant and path change | Variant frequencies, number of variants, activity/resource variant entropy, and new loops around advice, approval, or quality | Whether the process is standardizing or generating new exception paths |
| Leading: weeks | Fairness of waiting | Median, p90, p95, maximum, and SLA-risk rate within comparable team/segment/complexity strata; include language or regional accommodation where recorded | Differential burden that an overall mean conceals |
| Leading: weeks | Staff learning and confidence | Short pulse survey, observed technical escalations, advice requests, training completion, and local staff ability to resolve routine exceptions | Whether centralization is reducing capability or improving access to expertise |
| Lagging: weeks/months | End-to-end cycle time distribution | Case close minus intake, with median, p75, p90, p95, maximum, and mixture by segment | Whether any apparent gain is robust in the tail |
| Lagging: weeks/months | Outcome quality and rework | `outcome`, `rework_flag`, repeat activities, `get_rework_cases_per_activity`, post-implementation corrections, and customer recontacts | Whether speed was purchased with lower quality |
| Lagging: weeks/months | SLA performance | Close-to-deadline duration and misses by priority, complexity, team, specialty, and segment | Customer and contractual impact, especially on tails |
| Lagging: weeks/months | Customer experience | Local customer satisfaction, complaint/recontact rate, accommodation success, and explanation/appeal rate | Whether standardization harms local responsiveness |
| Lagging: weeks/months | Decision consistency and fairness | Equivalent-case decision agreement, override/appeal rates, outcome and wait gaps after case-mix adjustment | Whether standardization is consistent without disadvantaging a group |
| Lagging: months | Resilience | Recovery time after absence or demand spike, backlog decay, failed handovers, and variance in performance across disruption windows | Whether pooling provides real redundancy rather than a central single point of failure |
| Lagging: months | Capability and role health | Skill coverage, cross-training, local expertise retention, voluntary escalation, staff turnover, and role-clarity survey results | Long-term learning, accountability, and retention effects |

The central operational dashboard should show both a mean and a distribution. For example, the claim that cycle time improved should not be accepted unless p90/p95, high-complexity cases, and SLA misses are at least non-inferior. A useful decomposition is:

`end-to-end time = active service time + queue/waiting time + rework-loop time + unobserved/ownership time`.

The last term should be made observable with explicit queue-entry, acceptance, and owner events; otherwise it will be misclassified as an unexplained process delay.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency and deadline risk.** A faster routine majority can offset a small number of very late cases in the mean. Central queues are especially likely to create long waits during peaks. Monitor p90/p95, maximum, queue-age percentiles, and SLA misses by priority and segment.

2. **High-complexity cases.** Routing all difficult cases centrally changes the case mix at the hub. Their active work may be appropriate but their queue time, clarification loops, and probability of escalation can grow. Aggregate cycle time can improve while the cases most in need of expertise deteriorate.

3. **Local knowledge loss.** Reduced contact with embedded specialists can remove knowledge of regional customer conventions and informal preventive advice. This may first appear as lower `local_expertise_flag`, fewer local technical touches, more missing-context handovers, or less confident staff, and only later as rework, complaints, or poor outcomes.

4. **Fairness and unequal burden.** A common queue does not guarantee equal treatment. Different teams and customer segments may have different complexity, language, urgency, or accommodation needs. Equal average wait can coexist with a much worse p95 for one segment, or with a lower-quality outcome for customers requiring local context.

5. **Learning and capability erosion.** If all hard cases leave local teams, local caseworkers have fewer opportunities to learn. Short-term utilization may rise while the organization becomes more dependent on the shared service, referral volume increases, and future capacity becomes less resilient.

6. **Accountability and role ambiguity.** A case can have many participants but no clear owner. The queue may own throughput, the specialist may own an opinion, the caseworker may own customer contact, and the approver may own a decision without anyone owning the end-to-end result. This can be invisible when timestamps show that each activity completed on time.

7. **Exception handling and hidden loops.** Standard paths may become shorter while exceptions are bounced between queues, returned for context, or handled through unlogged conversations. A lower count of cross-team transfers can coexist with more reassignments and repeat activities.

8. **Near-capacity instability.** The Legal/Risk specialists are already at 93% and 91% utilization. Small demand or absence shocks can cause disproportionate waits. Averages over a month smooth this instability and conceal the operational risk of a single bad week.

9. **Decision explainability and appeals.** Standardized approval can reduce visible variation while making exceptions harder to explain. Appeals, overrides, repeat customer contacts, and supervisor escalations may rise without changing the basic activity sequence.

10. **Resilience and common-mode failure.** Pooling gives nominal redundancy for individual absences but creates dependency on one queue, one routing rule, one knowledge base, or one coordinator. Failure of that common component can affect all locations simultaneously; average performance during normal days will not reveal it.

11. **Measurement gaming or semantic drift.** If teams classify work differently after the change, fewer recorded handovers or a shorter “service” interval may reflect changed event definitions rather than real improvement. Baseline and post-change activity, queue, and ownership semantics must be versioned and reconciled.

12. **Customer-contact quality.** A case may close faster after a standardized decision while the customer has had more confusing contacts or less locally appropriate explanation. Closure time and `outcome` should be paired with satisfaction, complaints, recontacts, and accommodation outcomes.

# 5. Pilot design

### Pilot scope

Run a controlled, reversible pilot for one part of the routing model first. A suitable design is to pilot the Technical Shared Service for a defined subset of high-complexity cases and one specialty stream, while keeping Legal/Risk and difficult Quality Check under their current governance until the technical queue is understood. Include East because it may benefit from relieving an 82%-utilized local specialist, and include a representative North or South slice so that the pilot tests both overload relief and loss of local expertise. Do not remove the local supervisor during the initial pilot; approval centralization should be a separate stage or a separately randomized treatment.

Before activation, define event semantics for queue entry, queue acceptance, advice returned, reassignment, owner, and final decision. Preserve the old team and resource fields even when work is performed centrally. Record routing-rule version, pilot flag, capacity/absence events, and reasons for referral or return.

### Comparison and timing

Use a stepped-wedge or matched comparison design if operational constraints permit. Start with one treatment slice and retain matched North/South/East non-pilot slices as controls. Match or adjust for historical case mix using complexity, required specialty, priority, customer segment, intake date, and expected workload. A difference-in-differences comparison is preferable to a simple before/after comparison because demand and seasonal effects can change.

Collect at least 4–8 weeks of pre-change baseline and 8–12 weeks after stabilization. Keep an initial implementation period separate from the steady-state analysis, since training and routing defects may temporarily distort waits. Extend the observation window if high-complexity or rare specialty cases are too sparse for reliable estimates.

### Required segmentation

Report each primary metric overall and within:

- North, South, and East, with a separate view of cases previously handled by E_Tech;
- low, medium, and high `case_complexity`;
- each `required_specialty`, including legal, risk, and security;
- priority bands and deadline proximity;
- customer segment, location, language, and accommodation requirement where available;
- local-expertise versus non-local-expertise cases;
- absence, demand-spike, and normal operating windows.

Use case-mix-adjusted comparisons as well as raw results. Do not let a fall in East’s average, caused by routing easier work into the pool, be interpreted as proof that the shared service helped all teams.

### Guardrails and success criteria

Pre-register a small set of primary outcomes: end-to-end median and p90/p95 cycle time, queue wait by service, rework rate, SLA miss rate, and customer satisfaction. Add guardrails that must not materially worsen:

- p95 and maximum queue wait for high-complexity, legal/risk, and accommodation-sensitive cases;
- rework, failed quality checks, repeat contacts, and adverse outcomes;
- ownerless time, reassignment count, and clarification/missing-context handovers;
- performance gaps between North, South, and East and between customer segments after case-mix adjustment;
- local expertise exposure, staff role-clarity/confidence, and capability indicators;
- Legal/Risk utilization and queue age, with an explicit headroom threshold;
- resilience metrics during planned leave or a controlled workload spike.

Define success as improvement or non-inferiority on tail and quality guardrails, not merely a 15% rise in utilization or a lower overall mean. Set minimum sample sizes and review weekly, with a daily operational view for queue safety.

### Staff and customer feedback

Collect a short weekly survey from local caseworkers, shared specialists, supervisors, and queue coordinators covering ownership clarity, ease of obtaining context, decision confidence, workload fairness, and learning opportunities. Hold structured retrospectives on a sample of completed and delayed cases. Code free-text feedback by handover reason and compare it with the event log.

For customers, sample high-complexity, specialty, and region/language-sensitive cases for recontact quality and explanation adequacy. Review complaints and appeals, not just satisfaction scores. Where possible, have independent reviewers assess whether equivalent decisions were adequately explained and whether regional accommodations were met.

### Rollback and adjustment triggers

Pause expansion or restore local routing for a slice if any of the following persists beyond a pre-specified number of review periods: p95 or SLA misses materially worsen; high-complexity or a protected/customer segment has a material wait or outcome gap; rework or repeat contacts rise; queue age breaches its limit; Legal/Risk loses capacity headroom; ownerless time or reassignment churn increases; or staff identify an unresolved accountability or safety issue. Adjust capacity, routing thresholds, priority rules, documentation, or local consultation before re-testing. Rollback should preserve the event history and allow affected cases to be returned to a named local owner.

# 6. Recommendation

Pilot first, with a modified scope and explicit guardrails. The pooling logic is plausible: it could relieve East, reduce ad hoc absence transfers, improve access to scarce expertise, and standardize routine decisions. But the stated routing also increases technical and approval handovers and places more work into shared queues, while Legal/Risk already has very little spare capacity. The benefits and risks are therefore both structurally credible.

Management should not infer from an early improvement in average cycle time that the restructuring is successful. Early averages may be dominated by simple cases, temporary implementation effort, changed event definitions, or faster local steps while the high-complexity tail, rework, local learning, and fairness gaps worsen. Nor should a 15% utilization increase be treated as a benefit unless service quality and queue headroom remain safe; utilization near saturation can reduce resilience.

Proceed to broader rollout only if the pilot shows a stable or improved p90/p95 and SLA performance, no material quality or fairness degradation, clear named ownership, preserved local expertise mechanisms, and resilience under absence or demand spikes. Keep a local consultation path and postpone removal of the local supervisor until the shared approval queue has separately demonstrated accountable decisions and acceptable tail performance.

The following is a PM4Py-style sketch. Exact network return types and parameter names vary slightly by PM4Py version, so the wrapper functions should be adapted to the installed version. The important design points are to preserve event attributes, compare like-for-like strata, and distinguish active service from waiting.

# df contains both periods and all fields listed in the question.  Make the

For the handover comparison, define a handover consistently—such as a change in `org:resource`, a change in `team`, or an explicit queue acceptance—rather than mixing definitions across periods. Analyze all three views: resource-to-resource transfers reveal coordination, team-to-team transfers reveal organizational boundaries, and queue-entry-to-acceptance reveals waiting.
