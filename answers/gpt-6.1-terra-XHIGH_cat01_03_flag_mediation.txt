## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

A flag is a signal generated from a score and other intake information; it is not a change in underlying risk. Define policies at the level of a whole operating system over a prespecified horizon, starting from the same distribution of cases, staff, and queue states. P0 is current practice. Under P1, the explicit flag indicator is hidden from people while the score and other information remain available; automated routing and capacity remain as under P0. Staff may infer flag status from routing, so this is display suppression, not blinding. Under P2, priority triage remains, but the flag no longer automatically requires review; staff may request it under a specified, audited clinical or operational rule, and other routing and capacity remain fixed. P3 retains P0 routing and adds protected review capacity with a stated staffing schedule, eligibility, and allocation rule. P4 retains review and uses a specified revised escalation rule or adds senior capacity; these are different versions that need separate evaluation. Outcomes should include all cases, not just flagged cases, over a defined horizon with enough follow-up to close cases.

Let Y be arrival-to-close hours; contrasts below are intervention minus comparator. For subgroup estimands, membership is defined by the baseline flag that the existing algorithm would produce, not by a post-intervention display. The overall policy effect averages over all arrivals and includes spillovers. The triage outcome is elapsed time from arrival to triage completion. Decision quality requires an independently adjudicated reference, not merely approval rate.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P2 versus P0, both applied to whole system | All eligible arrivals in a fixed horizon | Mean Y | Total policy effect, including spillovers | Yes, once review discretion, implementation and horizon are specified | No policy variation here; queue interference, changing arrivals and staff behavior |
| E2 | P2 versus P0 | Baseline-flagged arrivals | Arrival-to-triage completion | Total policy effect on early timing | Yes, given policy and observation window | P2 changes downstream work and potentially staff availability; flagged cases differ at baseline |
| E3 | P3 versus P0 | All arrivals and separately baseline-flagged arrivals | Mean Y | Total policy effect on cycle time | Yes, with protected capacity and counterfactual staffing specified | Whether staff are added or redeployed determines spillovers; insufficient observed variation |
| E4 | Under P0, replace review-occurrence assignment with a feasible stochastic assignment modeled on P2, versus P0 assignment | Baseline-flagged arrivals within a system-level rollout | Mean Y | Interventional indirect analogue through review occurrence, letting downstream processes evolve | Conceptually, if the joint allocation rule and eligible histories are fully specified | Review decisions depend on latent risk and triage findings; system capacity, positivity and shared queues |
| E5 | Under P0 routing, implement an explicit review scheduling/capacity protocol versus P0 scheduling, preserving review eligibility | Reviewed cases under each system policy, and preferably all baseline-flagged arrivals as the primary population | Y and manual queue wait | Queue-targeted interventional analogue, not a natural indirect effect | Yes for a concrete feasible queue-level protocol; conditioning on reviewed cases changes the population if eligibility changes | Queue waits cannot be independently assigned to each case; queue state and service needs confound the relation |
| E6 | Under P0 review, replace escalation assignment by an explicit P4 rule versus current rule | All baseline-flagged arrivals, with all-case spillovers reported | Mean Y | Escalation-path intervention analogue including resulting queues | Yes once criteria, staffing and handling of non-escalated cases are defined | Review findings affect both escalation and outcome; unobserved severity and shared senior capacity |
| E7 | P1 versus P0 | All arrivals, separately baseline-flagged | Mean Y | Total effect of suppressing display while keeping automation | Yes if display, automatic routing, and visibility of lane are specified | Staff can infer the flag; behavior changes and underlying score may still drive decisions |
| E8 | P2 versus P0 | All arrivals with independently assessed outcomes | Error rate, harm, or compliance against a prespecified reference standard | Total effect on decision quality | Yes if reference standard and follow-up are defined | True outcomes may be delayed, selectively observed or changed by the decision itself |
| E9 | A specified triage-lane protocol versus usual lane, with review and escalation governed by explicit common rules | All arrivals, with baseline-flagged stratum | Arrival-to-triage completion and Y | Component intervention, not a natural direct effect | Only if lane assignment, competing priorities, and downstream rules are operationally feasible | Lane changes other cases' waits and later information; component cannot be isolated merely by conditioning on mediators |

The occurrence, waiting, and escalation interventions have different targets and can overlap: their contrasts do not necessarily add up to E1. I use total system-policy effects and explicit interventional analogues, not natural direct/indirect effects or separable effects. Natural effects require cross-world counterfactual relations and, in common formulations, no mediator–outcome confounding caused by the exposure; triage findings and queue evolution violate the simple story. A controlled direct effect that forces every case into or out of review would be a separate, potentially unsafe and infeasible intervention. Even stochastic analogues require consistency, exchangeability, overlap, and a coherent queue-level intervention; they are not identified by this table.

One case's lane and routing affect other cases' queues and may feed back to its own later wait; a time-indexed system model is needed to represent this without cycles. Triage findings can be affected by priority and can affect both review and the final outcome, making them exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounders. Review findings similarly precede and affect escalation and outcome. Queue state changes over time as cases and staff move, and both affects and is affected by earlier routing. Arrival load is only a coarse proxy for that history. Reviewer assignment may also respond to risk and congestion. Conditioning on review or escalation can select cases with different unobserved risk.

## 3. Stage decomposition

Entries are arithmetic means in hours, rounded to two decimals; `n` is the group size. Each optional-stage mean uses only cases actually reaching that stage. Review and escalation support counts are shown alongside the group name as `review/escalation`; NA means no case in that subgroup reached that stage. Residual-to-close runs from the last completed recorded stage to close; it is not assumed to be pure service. For non-reviewed cases the residual starts at triage completion, for reviewed but non-escalated cases at review completion, and for escalated cases at escalation completion. Because optional-stage means have different denominators, adding columns does not generally yield the total mean. All values are elapsed local-clock hours, not business hours.

Flagged cases start triage after 0.24 hours on average versus 2.48 for unflagged cases, and their mean triage service is also shorter. Yet their total cycle averages 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All 10 flagged cases have review versus four unflagged; six versus two reach escalation. Among those reviewed, the respective waits from triage completion to review start average 6.50 and 1.88 hours. Among escalated cases, waits to senior service average 7.00 and 6.50 hours, with only six and two observations. These patterns fit an early priority benefit followed by additional routing and downstream waiting; they do not establish that any flag component caused those differences. Complexity, hidden risk, service needs, time of day, reviewer assignment, and dynamic congestion differ. Conditioning on review or escalation also selects different populations. The overnight intervals may include off-shift time, and timestamps do not distinguish eligible queue time from other delay.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is assignment near a score threshold associated with timing? | If a real cutoff exists, compare just above and below it with a prespecified local regression discontinuity; estimate the local effect of eligibility or, with a valid instrument, the local effect of flag receipt | Narrow score bandwidth; baseline complexity, arrival time and load for precision; check flag compliance | Known stable threshold, no precise manipulation, continuity of potential outcomes; no concurrent cutoff rule and limited interference | Plot flag probability, density and baseline covariates around cutoff; vary bandwidth | No flag jump, manipulated scores or another policy changing at cutoff rules out a clean local estimate; no timing jump weakens a threshold-driven explanation |
| Does congestion explain the review delay? | Use time-resolved queue snapshots and longitudinal models for entry-to-service wait; compare similar case and queue histories and simulate a capacity change | Arrival and review-entry time, queue length, workload, staff availability, priority, risk, complexity and prior findings | Accurate queue entry and availability, sufficient support, measured confounding; appropriate handling of feedback | Compare predictions with held-out waits by shift and workload; check pre-entry queue balance | Delay persists at matched queue states or vanishes after queue and shift adjustment, respectively weakening or supporting congestion as an explanation |
| What does changing routing cause? | Randomize P2 vs P0 at queue or shift level, with prespecified independent review criteria and enough follow-up; alternatively use a staggered policy change with a credible comparison | Baseline risk, complexity, calendar time, initial queue state; account for cluster and period | Random assignment and adherence, or parallel trends and no concurrent changes for observational rollout | Check pre-policy trends, assignment balance, adherence and timing of changes | No cycle-time benefit or deterioration in independently assessed quality would weaken P2's appeal |
| What portion is targeted through review occurrence or waiting? | Specify a joint, history-dependent stochastic routing intervention or feasible capacity/scheduling protocol; use longitudinal g-computation or randomized policy comparisons, then simulate outcomes and all-case spillovers | Baseline factors, triage findings, time-varying queue and staffing history, reviewer and prior treatments | Consistency, sequential exchangeability, positivity and a correct system model; no unmodeled interference across randomized systems | Inspect overlap and weights, calibrate predicted review and wait distributions, validate out of sample | Extreme weights, impossible schedules or poor prediction make the proposed decomposition unreliable |
| Are apparent effects artifacts of case mix or time? | Test a prespecified pre-arrival outcome, such as a documented historical risk measure, against future randomized policy assignment; use a negative-control exposure such as a later administrative label that cannot affect earlier wait | Randomization strata or baseline risk, complexity and calendar time | The control is unaffected by policy and shares relevant bias pathways; no anticipation or recording change | Look for an assignment difference in the pre-arrival measure or a future-label association with earlier waits | A substantial association suggests imbalance, leakage or measurement bias; a null result cannot prove exchangeability |
| Could hidden risk explain the contrast? | Vary the prevalence and association of an unmeasured severity factor with routing and outcomes in bias formulas or a simulation; compare with an independently audited severity subset | Recorded score, complexity, arrivals, and measured intake facts | Sensitivity model and plausible parameter ranges; audit selection understood | Report the strength of hidden-risk associations required to remove or reverse the effect | A modest plausible unmeasured-risk association sufficient to explain the difference would weaken causal confidence |
| How large are spillovers? | Randomize entire independent queues or suitably long queue-time blocks to a policy; measure all cases and track carryover, with washout where feasible | Cluster, time, baseline queue, staffing and arrival mix; report flagged and unflagged strata | Limited between-cluster interference; block carryover measured or modeled; enough independent units | Compare unflagged waits and queue lengths, test carryover into subsequent blocks | Large unflagged delays or persistent carryover changes the all-case recommendation even if flagged cases improve |

A score cutoff is not supplied, and the observed scores are nearly separated by flag, so the threshold design is conditional on verifying a real assignment rule and collecting substantially more data. These 20 cases do not identify the proposed effects.

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current | Flagged priority remains | Review and senior bottlenecks may persist | Observed mean 16.51 hours for flagged; no causal estimate | Review may protect quality, but delays can harm | Existing queues affect all cases | Descriptive only | Retain as comparator while collecting richer data |
| P1: hide display, retain automation | Usually little change, unless staff behavior changes | Automated demand remains; human routing may change | Uncertain | Hidden signal could impair judgment; score remains available | Human behavior may alter shared queues | Speculative | Test only with clear display and inference rules; not a substitute for changing mandatory routing |
| P2: retain priority, remove mandatory review | Priority remains; indirect staffing effects possible | Potentially fewer reviews and shorter queues | Could fall, but depends on discretionary review and escalation | Missed risks or mandatory-compliance breaches | Capacity released for other cases; staff may substitute work | Plausible, not identified | Consider a tightly guarded trial after compliance and quality criteria are established |
| P3: current routing plus protected review capacity | Priority remains | Flagged review wait may decrease; faster throughput may increase senior demand | Likely lower if review is capacity-constrained; size uncertain | Review retained, but reviewer quality and downstream overload matter | Added staff can help; redeployment can hurt unflagged cases | Plausible, based on observed waits only | Preferred limited pilot with truly additional trained capacity and all-case monitoring |
| P4: retain review, change escalation criteria or capacity | No intended change | Revised criteria may reduce senior demand; extra capacity may reduce its wait | Could reduce cycle time for escalated cases; uncertain for all cases | Criteria changes could miss severe cases; capacity changes have staffing and cost risks | Senior capacity or staffing may shift delays to other work | Plausible but sparse escalation support | Evaluate rule change and added capacity separately after recording criteria and staffing |

Pilot P3 in operationally independent review queues with sufficient flagged demand and trained capacity. Randomly assign comparable queues, or queue-time blocks with measured carryover, to current staffing or a prespecified amount of genuinely additional protected review capacity; include all arrivals in analysis and report the baseline-flagged subgroup. Do not divert existing unflagged capacity. Record baseline queue state, staff hours and service eligibility, and monitor waits for both flag groups and the senior queue against prespecified limits. Primary outcome: mean arrival-to-close time for all arrivals, with complete follow-up and cluster-aware analysis. Secondary outcomes: flagged and unflagged cycle-time distributions, review and escalation waits, utilization, independent blinded decision-quality audit, compliance, and staffing cost. Collect event start/end and actual queue-entry/eligibility times, arrival times, score, baseline flag, complexity, triage and review findings, routing decisions and reasons, staff availability, queue state, shifts, outcomes and adjudicated quality. Stop or pause for a prespecified material rise in adjudicated harm or compliance failures, excessive unflagged waiting or senior backlog, unsafe staffing, or implementation failure. Set thresholds and analysis horizon before randomization.

events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events, case_id="case:concept:name", activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp", start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)

warnings.warn("DFG performance differences are descriptive, not causal; "
              "case mix, queues, timestamps and interference matter.")
for flag in (0, 1):
    subset = events.loc[events["flag"] == flag].copy()
    n_cases = subset["case:concept:name"].nunique()
    if n_cases < 30:
        warnings.warn(f"Flag {flag} has only {n_cases} cases; subgroup estimates are unstable.")
    frequency_dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(subset)
    performance_dfg, perf_starts, perf_ends = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(subset)
    print(f"\nFLAG {flag}: {n_cases} cases")
    print("Frequency DFG:", frequency_dfg, "starts:", starts, "ends:", ends)
    print("Performance DFG (seconds):", performance_dfg,
          "starts:", perf_starts, "ends:", perf_ends)

# NaT arithmetic preserves structurally absent stages as NaN, never zero.
def hours(later, earlier):
    return (later - earlier).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0

df["triage_wait_h"] = hours(df["triage_start"], df["arrival"])
df["triage_service_h"] = hours(df["triage_done"], df["triage_start"])
df["manual_wait_h"] = hours(df["manual_start"], df["triage_done"])
df["manual_service_h"] = hours(df["manual_done"], df["manual_start"])
df["escalation_wait_h"] = hours(df["escalation_start"], df["manual_done"])
df["senior_service_h"] = hours(df["escalation_done"], df["escalation_start"])
last_done = df["escalation_done"].combine_first(df["manual_done"]).combine_first(df["triage_done"])
df["residual_to_close_h"] = hours(df["close"], last_done)
df["cycle_h"] = hours(df["close"], df["arrival"])
metrics = ["triage_wait_h", "triage_service_h", "manual_wait_h", "manual_service_h",
           "escalation_wait_h", "senior_service_h", "residual_to_close_h", "cycle_h"]

groups = [("flag 0", df[df.flag == 0]), ("flag 1", df[df.flag == 1])]
groups += [(f"complexity {level}", df[df.complexity == level])
           for level in ("Low", "Medium", "High")]
groups += [(f"flag 1 load {level}", df[(df.flag == 1) & (df.arrival_load == level)])
           for level in ("High", "Normal")]
groups += [("flag 1 escalated", df[(df.flag == 1) & df.escalation_start.notna()]),
           ("flag 1 not escalated", df[(df.flag == 1) & df.escalation_start.isna()])]
for label, group in groups:
    if len(group) < 30:
        warnings.warn(f"Small subgroup: {label}, n={len(group)}")
    print(f"\n{label}: n={len(group)}, review={group.manual_start.notna().sum()}, "
          f"escalation={group.escalation_start.notna().sum()}")
    print(group[metrics].agg(["count", "mean"]).round(2).to_string())

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. An independent severity audit finds that hidden risk explains most of the flagged cases' longer downstream work and review is already appropriately targeted; the flag-routing explanation would weaken.
2. A queue-level trial shows protected review capacity materially increases unflagged waits or senior backlog enough to worsen all-case outcomes; P3 would no longer be preferred.
3. Independent follow-up shows that changed capacity or routing increases errors, harm, or compliance failures beyond prespecified limits; the pilot should stop and the recommendation change.
4. Timestamp validation shows that recorded review-start gaps mostly represent intentional eligibility holds or off-shift periods rather than waiting for review capacity; a staffing intervention may not address the delay.
5. There is inadequate overlap in risk, complexity, queue states, or feasible treatment assignments, so the estimated policy effects depend on extrapolation; the proposed mediation interpretation must be narrowed or abandoned.
6. Reviewers respond to faster access or hidden flags by changing scrutiny, referrals, or workarounds enough to erase the benefit or degrade decisions; the mechanism and policy need revision.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed:** In these 20 cases, flagged cases start triage sooner on average: 0.24 versus 2.48 hours after arrival. Their average cycle time is nevertheless 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All ten flagged cases received manual review and six were escalated, compared with four and two of the ten unflagged cases. Among reviewed cases the average interval from triage completion to review start was 6.50 hours for flagged cases and 1.88 hours for unflagged cases. These are small, selected groups, and the clock intervals can include nonworking time.

**Plausible mechanism:** Priority can accelerate the first stage while routing more cases into manual review and a scarce senior queue adds work and waiting. Complex cases and concentrated arrivals may intensify those waits. Faster review might also move demand downstream, and changing capacity for one group can affect everyone sharing a queue.

**Unidentified:** The flag is correlated with risk and complexity, the recorded score is imperfect, and triage and review findings are missing. The observed associations cannot distinguish the effects of display, priority, review occurrence, queueing, escalation, and case mix. Natural mediation effects would require strong cross-world assumptions; shared queues and treatment-induced findings further complicate identification. Approval or rejection alone does not establish decision quality.

**Most useful next evidence:** Randomize genuinely additional protected review capacity across suitable queues or time blocks, retain existing routing, and monitor all cases, senior backlog, independent decision quality, and compliance. This directly tests whether feasible review capacity improves overall outcomes while measuring spillovers and downstream bottlenecks.
