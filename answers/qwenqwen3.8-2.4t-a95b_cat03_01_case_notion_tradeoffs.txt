## 1. Analytical assumptions

I interpret a **case notion** as the object type whose instances become case identifiers when the OCEL is flattened. If flattening is performed on `Order`, each order (`O1`–`O4`) becomes a case; if flattening is performed on `Package`, each package (`P1`–`P4`) becomes a case, and so on. I use only the supplied direct event-to-object relations unless I explicitly describe an analytical transformation.

I interpret **convergence** as the collapsing of distinct business operations, object lifecycles, or object involvements into the same flattened trace or the same DFG path. For example, flattening on `Item` makes all six items converge to the same four-activity sequence: `Add Item`, `Pack Package`, `Dispatch`, `Confirm Delivery`.

I interpret **divergence** as the situation where one case notion contains multiple parallel or repeated object lifecycles, producing branching, repeated activities, or apparently looping behavior. For example, `O1` diverges because it is split across `P1` and `P2`, producing two `Pack Package`, two `Dispatch`, and two `Confirm Delivery` events. `O3` diverges even more because it participates in `P3`, `P4`, `D2`, `D3`, and invoice `V2`.

I treat **copied shared events** as both evidence and duplication. They are evidence that a given case object was involved in a business event. For example, `e16` is legitimate evidence that `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` were all affected by dispatching delivery `D2`. However, for frequency-based DFGs, throughput counts, or activity costing, the same physical event is duplicated across cases and can inflate counts. Therefore, shared events should be annotated with the number of related cases and, where appropriate, weighted.

I would permit the following analytical transformations, but only explicitly:

- Propagation of relations through O2O links, such as `Order contains Item`, `Package packs Item`, `Delivery carries Package`, `Invoice bills Order`, and `Payment settles Invoice`.
- Splitting or enriching activity labels using object-object qualifiers, especially distinguishing `M2 partially_settles V2` from `M3 completes_settlement V2`.
- Weighting copied events by inverse case multiplicity, for example giving `e16` weight `1/3` in each of `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` when counting order-level dispatch occurrences.
- Removing shared events for sensitivity analysis, provided this is declared and not treated as the default semantic view.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Cross-order package `P3` duplicated into order cases**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Event `e13` (`Pack Package`) is related to `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`. When flattened on `Order`, the same physical packing event is copied into both `O2` and `O3`. The DFG edge into and out of `Pack Package` is therefore counted twice for one operation. This can inflate packing frequency and make it appear as though each order independently triggered a pack operation. The effect is harmful for capacity or operation-count analysis, but informative for understanding that both orders reached the packing stage.

2. **Cross-order package `P4` duplicated into item cases**  
   Affected flattening: `Item`. Event `e14` (`Pack Package`) is related to `O3`, `O4`, `I32`, `I41`, and `P4`. In an item flattening, `e14` is copied into both `I32` and `I41`. The DFG makes it appear as though two independent item-level packing activities occurred, while in fact one package was packed containing items from two different orders. This is harmful if the analyst counts item-level packing effort, but context-dependent if the goal is simply to know when each item became packed.

3. **Consolidated delivery `D2` appears deceptively simple in delivery flattening**  
   Affected flattening: `Delivery`. `D2` has only `e16` (`Dispatch`) and `e24` (`Confirm Delivery`). The case looks identical in structure to `D1`, which has `e15` and `e23`. However, `D2` carries both `P2` and `P3`, thereby involving `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`. The delivery DFG hides consolidation complexity. Performance comparison is biased: `D2` took 4 hours 55 minutes from dispatch to confirmation, but this duration is not comparable to `D1`’s 3 hours unless package count, order count, and route complexity are considered. This distortion is harmful for operational benchmarking but informative for pure transport duration.

4. **Consolidated delivery `D2` creates simultaneous order milestones**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Events `e16` and `e24` are copied into `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`. Thus three order cases receive the same `Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery` timestamps. In an order DFG, this can inflate the frequency of `Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery` and can make it appear that three orders were independently dispatched and confirmed at the same time. The effect is harmful for counting independent logistics operations, but informative for order-level customer experience because all three orders were affected by `D2`.

5. **Consolidated invoice `V2` double-counts billing and payment exposure in order cases**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Invoice `V2` bills both `O2` and `O3`. Events `e19` (`Issue Invoice`), `e22` (`Record Payment` for `M2`), and `e25` (`Record Payment` for `M3`) are copied into both `O2` and `O3`. If one aggregates `Record Payment` events per order, the two instalments of `V2` appear once for `O2` and once for `O3`, suggesting four order-level payment events rather than two unique payment events. This is harmful for financial reconciliation or order-level payment frequency, but informative for understanding that both orders share billing risk.

6. **Partial payments on `V2` can look like a payment loop or duplicate payment**  
   Affected flattening: `Invoice`. Invoice `V2` has `e19` (`Issue Invoice`), `e22` (`Record Payment` for `M2`), and `e25` (`Record Payment` for `M3`). If both payment events retain the same activity label `Record Payment`, the invoice DFG may show `Record Payment`  `Record Payment`. This can be interpreted as duplicate payment, rework, or an exception. In reality, `M2` partially settles `V2`, and `M3` completes settlement. The effect is informative if the qualifiers `partially_settles` and `completes_settlement` are preserved as activity or event attributes; otherwise it is harmful.

7. **Payment flattening produces near-singleton lifecycles**  
   Affected flattening: `Payment`. Each payment object has exactly one event: `M1` has `e21`, `M2` has `e22`, `M3` has `e25`, and `M4` has `e26`. The resulting cases are singleton cases. A DFG contains no meaningful edges, and log skeleton discovery will find almost no temporal constraints. This is a severe lifecycle truncation. It hides the fact that `M2` and `M3` are temporally and financially related through `V2`. This flattening is harmful for process discovery but useful as a payment ledger view.

8. **Item and package flattenings truncate the end-to-end order-to-cash lifecycle**  
   Affected flattenings: `Item` and `Package`. Item `I11` begins at `e05` (`Add Item`) and ends at `e23` (`Confirm Delivery`), missing order creation `e01`, invoicing `e18`, payment `e21`, and order closure `e28`. Package `P1` begins even later at `e11` (`Pack Package`) and ends at `e23`. If these logs are used to measure end-to-end cycle time, the result will be systematically shorter than the true order lifecycle. This distortion is harmful for end-to-end performance analysis, but informative for local operational stages such as item fulfilment or package handling.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would still create flattened logs, but each should be used only for questions that match its semantic granularity.

The **Order flattening** should be created because it is the only direct flattened view that covers creation, item addition, packing, dispatch, delivery, invoicing, payment, and closure for `O1`–`O4`. It can safely answer questions such as: How long does an order remain open? Which orders have incomplete delivery or payment? Which orders are affected by shared packages, deliveries, or invoices? It should not be used without qualification to count unique physical operations or unique payments.

The **Item flattening** should be created for item-level operational analysis. It can safely answer: How long after `Add Item` is each item packed? Which items are confirmed together in the same delivery? It cannot safely answer order-to-cash questions because items do not directly contain `Create Order`, `Issue Invoice`, `Record Payment`, or `Close Order` events.

The **Package flattening** should be created for packing and logistics analysis. It can safely answer: How long from packing to delivery confirmation for `P1`–`P4`? Which packages share the same dispatch? It should not be used to infer order-level financial completion.

The **Delivery flattening** should be created only for transport analysis. It can safely answer: What is the dispatch-to-confirmation duration for `D1`, `D2`, and `D3`? It cannot safely answer questions about order complexity or package workload unless the O2O relations `D2 carries P2/P3` are explicitly joined.

The **Invoice flattening** should be created for billing and settlement analysis. It can safely answer: How long from invoice issuance to payment? Does `V2` have multiple payment events? It should not be used to infer physical delivery performance.

The **Payment flattening** can be created as an audit or fact view, but not as a process log. It can answer: Which payments exist and when were they recorded? It cannot answer control-flow questions.

Object-centric views are required for questions where copying shared events would distort the semantics. An **OC-DFG** is needed to see activities as event nodes connected to multiple object types, so that `e13` is one packing event involving `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`, rather than two independent order-level packing events. An **OTG** is needed to expose structural consolidation: `P3` packs items from two orders, `D2` carries two packages, and `V2` bills two orders. An **ET–OT graph** is useful to see which activities touch which object types, for example that `Dispatch` touches orders, items, packages, and deliveries simultaneously. An **object interactions summary** can quantify interactions such as order-package, package-delivery, and invoice-payment relations. **Equivalent-execution clustering** can help identify whether packages, deliveries, invoices, or orders have similar lifecycle patterns, but clusters must be interpreted with the O2O structure.

When flattened and object-centric views contradict, the reconciliation rule should be explicit. For control-flow frequency, prefer unique event counts or weighted shared-event counts. For stakeholder-specific experience, use the relevant flattened case notion. For structural dependency, use OTG/OC-DFG. For financial settlement, use invoice and payment objects rather than order-copied payment events.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**Position B: Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

I would use **Order** as the primary lead object, but not as the sole semantic lens.

### Decision criteria

The lead object should:

1. Represent the business entity for which end-to-end accountability exists.
2. Cover the widest meaningful lifecycle without requiring silent propagation.
3. Be connectable to all other relevant object types.
4. Allow stakeholder questions about fulfilment, delivery, billing, and closure.
5. Have distortions that can be explicitly managed through weighting or secondary views.

### Strongest supporting evidence

Orders are the natural end-to-end anchors in this snapshot. Each order has a clear beginning in `Create Order` (`e01`–`e04`) and a clear end in `Close Order` (`e28`–`e31`). All other object types are connected to orders either through events or O2O relations: items are contained in orders, packages pack items, deliveries carry packages, invoices bill orders, and payments settle invoices. The order flattening is the only direct flattening that includes both physical fulfilment and financial settlement.

### Strongest counterargument

The strongest counterargument is that orders are not independent physical units. `P3` combines items from `O2` and `O3`; `P4` combines items from `O3` and `O4`; `D2` consolidates `P2` and `P3`; `V2` bills both `O2` and `O3`. Therefore, order flattening copies shared events and can double-count operations. If the analysis goal is strict operational counting, `Package`, `Delivery`, or `Invoice` may be less distorted.

### Residual risk

Even with `Order` as primary lead, there is residual risk that analysts will treat copied events as independent order activities. For example, `e22` and `e25` may be counted twice if analyzed per order, although they are only two payment events for `V2`. Similarly, `e16` and `e24` may be interpreted as three independent dispatch/confirmation operations, although they are one delivery dispatch and one delivery confirmation.

### Conditions for changing the decision

I would change the lead object if the analytical question changes. If the question is transport performance, `Delivery` should lead. If the question is settlement compliance, `Invoice` should lead. If the question is packing efficiency, `Package` should lead. If the organization refuses to accept weighted or annotated shared events, then I would reject a single lead object and rely primarily on object-centric graphs.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

First, I would test the effect of **removing or down-weighting copied shared events**. In the order flattening, events such as `e13`, `e14`, `e16`, `e17`, `e19`, `e22`, `e24`, `e25`, and `e27` are copied into multiple order cases. I would compute three versions of the order DFG: one with raw copied events, one where each event is weighted by `1 / number_of_related_order_cases`, and one where events related to more than one order are excluded. If edges such as `Pack Package`  `Dispatch` collapse or weaken substantially in the weighted version, the original frequency is driven by consolidation rather than independent order processing.

Second, I would compare **direct E2O relations with an explicitly propagated-relation variant**. The default analysis would use only the supplied E2O relations. The propagated variant would traverse O2O relations: `Order contains Item`, `Package packs Item`, `Delivery carries Package`, `Invoice bills Order`, and `Payment settles Invoice`. For example, propagation could attach invoice and payment events to items or packages. This would lengthen item/package lifecycles, but only because of an analytical assumption. Comparing the two variants shows whether conclusions about lifecycle length and stage waiting depend on the propagation policy.

Third, I would **change the lead object** and compare the resulting logs. The order flattening shows `O2` and `O3` with two `Record Payment` events because of `V2`. The invoice flattening shows `V2` with two payment events but no order-level double counting. The payment flattening shows `M2` and `M3` as singleton cases. The package flattening shows `P3` and `P4` as simple three-event lifecycles. If one lead object suggests a loop or exception while another shows a normal consolidation pattern, the discrepancy should be resolved by returning to the O2O/E2O graph rather than by choosing the most convenient flattened log.

Fourth, I would compare **frequency metrics based on events, unique objects, and total object involvements** in the OC-DFG. For example, `e13` is one unique event, one package (`P3`), two orders (`O2`, `O3`), and two items (`I21`, `I31`). Similarly, `e16` is one unique dispatch event, one delivery (`D2`), two packages (`P2`, `P3`), and three orders (`O1`, `O2`, `O3`). A DFG edge frequency based on copied order cases will differ from a frequency based on unique events or unique packages. Reporting the metric definition alongside every frequency is essential.

A final validation step would be to check **temporal plausibility across object types**. For example, `O3` has confirmation events `e24` and `e27`, payment events `e22` and `e25`, and closure `e30`. The order-level sequence includes `Confirm Delivery` before the final `Record Payment` for `D2`, but also a final `Record Payment` before the final `Confirm Delivery` for `D3`. This is not necessarily an anomaly; it reflects consolidated invoicing and staggered deliveries. Validating such patterns requires the object-centric graph, not only the flattened order trace.
