# 1. Mechanism map

The proposed change replaces a **local, team-embedded structure** with a **hub-and-spoke shared-service structure**. In process-mining terms, the key question is not only whether average cycle time improves, but where the following move:

- waiting time,
- handovers,
- rework,
- ownership,
- specialist capacity,
- fairness across teams and customer segments.

A useful baseline observation is that the supplied handover summary does **not** clearly show a reduction in total recorded handovers. Summing the listed frequencies:

- current listed handovers: **343 per 100 cases**
- proposed listed handovers: **359 per 100 cases**

So the restructuring may reduce some **local informal loops** while increasing **formal queue-based handovers**. The process-mining analysis should therefore distinguish between:

- fewer unplanned local interruptions,
- more formal specialist queue transitions,
- more waiting time hidden inside central queues,
- more or fewer rework loops.

| Restructuring action | Mechanism | Expected process-mining effect | Observable PM measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech into Technical Shared Service | Local specialist loops are replaced by central queue handovers | Within-team handover density falls; cross-team/local-to-central handovers rise; total handovers may not fall | `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network`, `pm4py.discover_working_together_network`, handovers per case, edge weights between local caseworkers and central resources |
| Centralize Specialist Review, Legal Review, Risk Review, difficult Quality Check | Scarce specialist capacity becomes pooled and queue-managed | Waiting time after handover becomes more sensitive to utilization; queue length and aging become central performance drivers | `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `pm4py.get_service_time`, performance DFG edge durations, waiting-time distributions, queue WIP |
| Route all high-complexity cases to Technical Shared Service | Routing becomes rule-based rather than locally negotiated | More consistent specialist referral, but possible over-referral or misrouting; local caseworkers may lose informal triage support | Filter by `case_complexity`, handover reason distribution, reassignment_count, referral rate from local caseworker to TSS |
| Route legal/risk/security cases to shared specialist queue | Broader detection of specialist need | More cases enter already scarce legal/risk queues; waits may increase non-linearly because Legal/Risk are already near capacity | Filter by `required_specialty`, waiting time after handover, legal/risk throughput, rework after specialist review |
| Create shared approval queue and remove one local supervisor | Approval becomes standardized but less locally owned | Approval waiting may rise; accountability may become diffused; approval rework may change | Handover edge `local caseworker  shared approval queue`, waiting time after approval handover, reassignment_count, number of resources per case |
| Expected 15% higher specialist utilization | Higher resource efficiency target | If utilization rises from already high levels, queueing delay can increase disproportionately, especially above ~85–90% utilization | Service time per resource, throughput, waiting time, utilization proxies from event timestamps |
| Loss of embedded local technical expertise | Informal early advice disappears | More formal referrals, more clarification handovers, possible increase in rework for complex or region-specific cases | `local_expertise_flag`, local technical activity share, `handover_reason`, rework_flag by complexity/team/customer_segment |
| Shared queues and pooled roles | Role boundaries become less explicit | Role clarity may fall; cases may wait unowned; reassignments may increase | `reassignment_count`, distinct resources per case, `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`, `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` |
| Centralization as resilience strategy | Pooled resources can cover absence, but central queue can become single point of failure | Resilience may improve for ordinary absences but worsen during demand spikes or queue saturation | Cycle-time variance during absence/peak periods, cross-team handover frequency, queue aging, throughput stability |
| Fairness effects | Routing may be formally equal but outcome effects may differ by team, complexity, priority, language, region, or customer value | East may improve because E_Tech is overloaded; North/South complex cases may worsen; vulnerable or low-priority segments may receive slower service | Stratified metrics by `team`, `location`, `customer_segment`, `priority`, `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, SLA miss rate, p90 waiting time |

Important structural interpretation:

- **Current structure**: dense local working-together networks, stable local handover loops, local accountability, but uneven specialist load.
- **Proposed structure**: stronger central nodes, more formal handovers, potentially higher specialist utilization, but higher risk of central queue delay, role ambiguity, and fairness problems.

The process-mining evidence should therefore test whether the change produces:

1. genuine reduction in unnecessary handovers, or merely a shift of handovers into central queues;
2. genuine service-time improvement, or merely more waiting hidden inside specialist queues;
3. standardization without loss of local context;
4. fair outcomes across teams, complexities, priorities, and customer segments.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic scenario

This scenario assumes that the shared-service model is implemented with enough capacity, good routing rules, clear queue ownership, and effective knowledge transfer from local specialists to the central team.

### Handover pattern

- Local caseworker-to-embedded-tech loops decrease.
- Cross-team absence-cover handovers fall from about 6 to near 3 per 100 cases.
- Formal handovers to Technical Shared Service and shared approval increase, but are clean and non-repetitive.
- Reassignment_count falls or remains stable.
- Rework loops after Quality Check and Specialist Review decrease because decisions are standardized.

### Queue/waiting-time pattern

- Technical Shared Service median wait stays near management estimate: around 3.5–4.5 hours.
- Legal/Risk shared queue median wait remains near 10–12 hours.
- Shared approval median wait remains near 2.5–3.5 hours.
- Queue aging is limited; 90th percentile waiting does not explode.
- Specialist utilization rises but remains below dangerous saturation, for example below 85–88% for most shared queues.

### Cycle-time distribution

- Median cycle time improves modestly or remains stable.
- East improves because E_Tech was overloaded.
- North and South do not materially worsen.
- High-complexity cases do not experience severe tail degradation.
- 90th percentile cycle time remains stable or improves for East.

### Rework and quality pattern

- Overall rework rate falls slightly due to standardized decisions.
- Quality Check rework falls because difficult checks are centralized.
- Complex regional cases do not show a rework increase.
- Outcome quality remains stable or improves.

### Role clarity and accountability

- Queue ownership is explicit.
- Shared approval has a named accountable role.
- Handover_reason values show fewer “unclear owner” or “clarification needed” entries.
- Reassignment_count remains low.

### Fairness implications

- East benefits from reduced specialist overload.
- North/South do not lose materially.
- Customer segments with language or regional accommodation needs do not show higher handover rates or longer waits.
- Priority rules prevent high-value cases from systematically crowding out vulnerable or low-priority cases.

### Early warning signs

Even in the optimistic scenario, monitor:

- waiting time after handover to Technical Shared Service;
- p90 cycle time for high-complexity cases;
- rework after central specialist advice;
- local_expertise_flag decline;
- fairness gaps by team and customer_segment.

- Rework after Technical Shared Service or Legal/Risk review becomes a key diagnostic.
- Quality Check may improve for difficult checks but worsen where local customer conventions matter.

### Role clarity and accountability

### Fairness implications

## Adverse scenario

This scenario occurs if central capacity is insufficient, routing is too aggressive, local knowledge is not transferred, and shared approval lacks clear accountability.

### Handover pattern

- Formal handovers increase substantially.
- Central service returns cases to local teams for missing information.
- Rework loops increase.
- Cross-team cover may reappear despite the intended reduction.
- Reassignment_count rises sharply.
- Handover_reason shows many corrections, clarifications, and ownership disputes.

### Queue/waiting-time pattern

- Shared approval waits rise to 6–12 hours.

### Fairness implications

- High-complexity cases are penalized.

- Fairness gaps by team, location, priority, and customer_segment widen.

- Queue aging alerts.

- Reassignment_count increases by 50% or more.
- Rework after central specialist advice rises sharply.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope

Use a **phased, partial rollout** rather than an immediate full restructuring.

Recommended pilot:

1. **Phase 0: Baseline and capacity stress test**
   - Establish baseline metrics for at least 12 weeks.
   - Model Technical Shared Service, Legal/Risk, and shared approval capacity.
   - Confirm whether a 15% utilization increase is feasible without excessive queueing.

2. **Phase 1: East-first Technical Shared Service pilot**
   - Centralize technical specialist handling for East cases.
   - Apply the high-complexity routing rule to East.
   - Keep North and South embedded technical specialists as controls.
   - Do not fully remove North/South local expertise yet.
   - Keep Legal/Risk shared as today, but monitor added triage burden carefully.

3. **Phase 2: Optional shared approval pilot**
   - If Phase 1 guardrails are met, pilot shared approval for East medium/high-value cases.
   - Do not combine shared approval and full technical centralization in one step unless capacity is proven.

4. **Phase 3: Controlled expansion**
   - Expand to South only if East pilot guardrails are met.
   - Expand to North last because North currently has strong local expertise and stable loops.

This design recognizes that East may benefit because E_Tech is already overloaded, while North and South may lose more from local expertise removal.

## Comparison group

Use both:

- **Pre/post comparison** within East.
- **Concurrent control groups**: North and South continue the current model during the pilot.

Preferred analytical approach:

- difference-in-differences comparing East against North/South before and after the pilot;
- matched-case comparison by `case_complexity`, `priority`, `required_specialty`, and `customer_segment`;
- case-mix adjustment because East, North, and South may have different volumes and complexity profiles.

## Pre/post observation window

Recommended windows:

- **Baseline window**: 12 weeks before pilot.
- **Ramp/exclusion window**: first 2 weeks of pilot excluded from formal evaluation, but monitored closely.
- **Pilot window**: 12 weeks after ramp.
- **Post-pilot review window**: 4 weeks to observe delayed rework, complaints, and outcome effects.

If the process has strong seasonality, use a longer window or compare against the same season from the previous year.

## Segmentation

All pilot metrics should be segmented by:

- `team`
- `location`
- `case_complexity`
- `required_specialty`
- `priority`
- `customer_segment`
- `handover_reason`
- `reassignment_count`
- `local_expertise_flag`
- language or regional accommodation need, if available
- simple versus high-value cases
- absence or peak periods

Special attention should be given to:

- East versus North/South;
- high-complexity cases;
- legal/risk/security cases;
- customer segments needing language or regional accommodation;
- low-priority cases that may be crowded out.

## Guardrail metrics

| Metric | Amber threshold | Red threshold |
|---|---:|---:|
| Technical Shared Service median wait after handover | > 5 hours | > 7 hours for 2 consecutive weeks |
| Technical Shared Service p90 wait | > 12 hours | > 20 hours |
| Legal/Risk median wait | > 12 hours | > 16 hours |
| Shared approval median wait | > 4 hours | > 6 hours |
| High-complexity p90 cycle time | +10% versus baseline/control | +20% versus baseline/control |
| Overall SLA miss rate | +1.5 percentage points | +3 percentage points |
| SLA miss rate for any protected segment | +2 percentage points | +4 percentage points |
| Rework rate after central specialist advice | +1.5 percentage points | +3 percentage points |
| Reassignment_count | +20% | +40% |
| Handover_reason “missing info” or “routing correction” | +25% | +50% |
| local_expertise_flag decline | > 20% without quality gain | > 40% without quality gain |
| Cross-team emergency cover | Increase above baseline | Sustained increase above baseline |
| Staff role-clarity survey | Moderate decline | Severe decline |

## Staff feedback collection

Process mining should be combined with direct staff feedback:

- weekly pulse survey for local caseworkers, central specialists, supervisors, and approval staff;
- role clarity score: “I know who owns the case at each stage”;
- psychological safety score for escalating complex cases;
- qualitative review of 20–30 difficult cases per week;
- structured interviews with East, North, and South teams;
- customer feedback by segment;
- review of case notes for consistency and completeness;
- escalation logs and queue-override logs.

Staff feedback is essential because some harms, such as loss of informal advice or accountability confusion, may not appear immediately in timestamps.

## Rollback or adjustment triggers

### Immediate rollback triggers

Rollback or pause the pilot if any of the following occur:

- central queue aging exceeds SLA for more than 3 consecutive days;
- high-complexity p90 cycle time worsens by more than 20%;
- SLA miss rate worsens by more than 3 percentage points overall;
- rework after central specialist advice rises by more than 3 percentage points;
- fairness gap widens materially for any protected or vulnerable segment;
- reassignment_count rises by more than 40%;
- staff report severe loss of ownership or customer harm.

### Adjustment triggers

If amber signals appear, adjust before rolling back:

- add central capacity;
- reintroduce a local technical liaison;
- tighten routing rules;
- improve case-note templates;
- add priority protection for vulnerable or complex cases;
- create explicit queue ownership;
- introduce escalation path for region-specific cases.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: modify and pilot first. Do not proceed with full rollout immediately.**

The proposed restructuring has plausible benefits:

- better specialist pooling;
- reduced overload in East;
- more standardized decisions;
- fewer ad hoc cross-team transfers;
- potentially better use of scarce legal/risk expertise.

However, the risks are substantial:

- Legal/Risk specialists are already near capacity.
- East’s high specialist utilization suggests that centralization may worsen queueing unless capacity is increased.
- North and South currently benefit from stable local expertise.
- Shared approval may reduce accountability.
- Total recorded handovers may increase even if local loops decrease.
- Average cycle time can hide tail delay, fairness harm, rework, and learning erosion.

The restructuring should be modified as follows:

1. **Do not assume total handovers will fall.**
   - Measure handovers per case, handover type, rework loops, and reassignment_count.
   - Distinguish planned specialist handovers from clarification or correction handovers.

2. **Do not centralize without queue capacity.**
   - Model Technical Shared Service, Legal/Risk, and shared approval capacity.
   - If expected utilization exceeds roughly 85–90%, add capacity or retain local triage.

3. **Preserve some local technical capability.**
   - Keep local technical liaisons in North and South during the pilot.
   - Use them for regional context, early triage, and complex customer accommodation.

4. **Make accountability explicit.**
   - Every shared queue needs a named owner.
   - Shared approval needs clear decision rights and escalation rules.

5. **Protect fairness.**
   - Monitor waiting time and SLA misses by team, complexity, priority, location, and customer_segment.
   - Prevent high-value or urgent cases from systematically crowding out vulnerable or lower-priority cases.

6. **Use a phased rollout.**
   - Start with East because it may benefit most.
   - Use North and South as concurrent controls.
   - Expand only if guardrail metrics are met.

Management should **not infer success from early average cycle time alone**. Early averages may improve because:

- simple cases remain local;
- complex cases are still in queues and have not yet completed;
- rework has not yet accumulated;
- fairness effects are hidden in aggregate numbers;
- local skill erosion has not yet affected outcomes;
- resilience problems appear only during absence or demand spikes.

The correct inference from early results should be based on:

- waiting time after handover,
- p90/p99 cycle time,
- rework after central specialist advice,
- fairness by segment,
- reassignment_count,
- handover_reason,
- local expertise usage,
- staff role-clarity feedback.

Proceed only if the pilot shows not only stable or improved median cycle time, but also controlled queue waiting, stable rework, clear accountability, and fair outcomes across teams and customer segments.

def outcome_metrics(log_df):
    out = log_df.groupby("period").agg(
        cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean")
    )

    if "sla_miss" in log_df.columns:
        out["sla_miss_rate"] = log_df.groupby("period")["sla_miss"].mean()

        out["bad_outcome_rate"] = (
            log_df.groupby("period")["outcome"]
                  .apply(lambda x: x.isin(["failed", "reopened", "sla_miss"]).mean())
        )

    return out
