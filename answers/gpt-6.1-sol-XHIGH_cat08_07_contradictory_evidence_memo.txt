## 1. Executive memo

**Decision: choose E—a controlled, segment-specific rollout—with C’s safeguards and D’s experimental discipline.** Retain the simplified path for verified simple, low-risk houses. Restore surveys and applicable permit checks for riskier addresses, require successful activation before billing, and test the revised operating model before expanding it nationally.

The pilot contains a valuable improvement, but the full redesign has not earned a national rollout. Median duration fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%. Simple, low-risk houses improved on duration, first-time-right installation, and complaints. Rolling back every component would discard those benefits.

The downside is material: p95 duration rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right installation fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and SLA breaches increased. Visible cost fell by EUR 23 per order, but estimated full cost rose by EUR 9. The latter estimate is incomplete; neither figure establishes profitability. Faster billing is also an inadequate success measure when activation checks can occur afterward.

The evidence suggests two different operating realities. Straightforward orders benefit from merged validation/scheduling and fewer transfers. Complex orders encounter incorrect survey skips, missing or unrecorded permit checks, activation exceptions, and congested recovery work. Coordinator utilization of 93% in two regions and second-visit team utilization of 92% make capacity and absence coverage plausible contributors to the worsening tail. These are explanations to test, not proven causal effects.

High model fitness does not override these outcomes. Pilot fitness of 0.94 means the model accommodates observed traces; precision of 0.51 indicates excessive permitted behavior. Alignment deviations around billing, activation, repeat visits, and permits require comparison with explicit operating requirements, including verification that missing events represent missing work. They are not cleared by a favorable aggregate fitness score.

Immediately enforce verified activation before billing and documented checks where permits are required. Review affected open cases and customers already billed without verified working service, resolving activation, appointments, and billing corrections as applicable. Suspend the predictive survey-skipping rule for medium-risk, permit-sensitive, and unclassified addresses. Preserve coordinator accountability, but introduce shared case records, named backup coverage, and capacity limits before extending ownership. Redirect suitable survey capacity toward prevention and recovery, subject to skills and travel constraints.

Maintain a capped expansion for reliably classified low-risk houses under these controls. Other segments should use the restored controls while retaining useful coordination improvements where staffing supports them. Business orders need explicit ownership through activation and billing; rural orders need protected rescheduling capacity.

The pilot’s different building mix, missing early attributes, incomplete downstream costs, and uncertain outcome maturity prevent confident attribution. A six-week pilot reporting a 62-day p95 also requires reconciliation of enrollment dates and follow-up. These limitations justify better comparisons, not inaction on the observed service failures.

Fund control monitoring, risk-rule validation, full 60-day downstream costing, and structured exception data now. Run concurrent comparisons within reliable risk strata and test absence resilience. Expand only where meaningful speed gains survive measurement to working service, tail and quality guardrails pass, full economics are acceptable, and required activation and permit controls operate reliably. Management should accept some added preventive work to avoid expensive failures, while preserving demonstrated segment-specific opportunities.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness and low precision.** Fitness measures how well observed traces can be reproduced by a model. Precision penalizes behavior that the model permits but the observations do not support. A model that allows many combinations of survey omission, manual activation, repeat visits, and billing can therefore attain high fitness while being too permissive to guide operations. Neither score measures customer service quality or compliance with an independent operating policy.

The alignment findings are compatible with high aggregate fitness: consequential deviations can be concentrated in particular activities or segments while most events fit. Some deviations may also reflect an outdated reference model or changed activity labels. Assess conformance against explicit mandatory controls and a versioned reference model, then validate disputed events against operational records. The lower generalization score is an additional warning under the chosen estimator, not proof that future cases will fail. Compare discovery configurations and evaluate models on held-out, comparable traces.

**Improved median and worse tail.** Shortening common, straightforward paths can move the median downward while a minority of complicated orders experience much longer delays. Reduced surveys, failed visits, manual activation, and congested revisits offer plausible mechanisms. High resource utilization increases sensitivity to variability and absences, although the exact queueing effect is unmeasured.

Quantiles cannot be reconstructed by averaging segment changes. Nor can the aggregate change be attributed to R without controlling for building mix and region. Earlier billing may shift unresolved work outside the measured cycle. Track order-to-verified-working-service alongside order-to-billing, include unresolved orders, and reconcile cohort dates and follow-up before interpreting the 62-day p95. A completed-case-only sample can understate delays by excluding stalled orders.

**Fewer handovers and lower resilience.** A single owner can reduce transfers and improve customer accountability while becoming a dependency for decisions and case knowledge. The handover network describes transfers; the working-together network reveals continuing collaboration and centralized workload. Its concentration, together with observed absence stalls, supports a resilience concern. Shared records, backup authority, and specialist ownership of exceptions can preserve accountability while making work transferable.

**Lower visible cost and higher downstream cost.** Avoiding a survey or transfer can save money immediately while increasing failed visits, activation work, travel, complaints, and billing corrections later. The displayed totals imply an estimated downstream increment of EUR 46 in the pilot versus EUR 14 at baseline—a EUR 32 difference that would exceed the EUR 23 visible saving. This is arithmetic from incomplete estimates, not a verified cost decomposition. Freed survey capacity also does not automatically become a cash saving. Evaluate full cost per enrolled order, including unresolved orders, under consistent accounting and follow-up.

**Simple-house improvement and apartment/old-building deterioration.** Simplification is more likely to work where access, technical conditions, and permissions are predictable. Apartments and older buildings can require preparation that the skip rule fails to recognize. Reported segment outcomes, missing permit events, and apartment activation delays support that explanation; they do not isolate its causal contribution. Business billing disputes and rural rescheduling delays show that building risk alone is insufficient segmentation.

DFG changes reinforce these hypotheses but require careful normalization: merged activities change graph structure even without changing work, and repeat events can inflate edge frequency. Together, the artifacts locate where to investigate; they do not replace outcome measurement or controlled comparisons.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Conditions under which it would be reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Rapidly captures simplification and coordination benefits; establishes one operating model. | Exports worsened tails, rework, billing problems, and coordinator dependency. Existing evidence does not establish safe economics or segment transferability. | Only after representative, controlled comparisons pass segment-specific service, cost, capacity, and mandatory-control gates. The current unmodified bundle does not qualify. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Restores familiar survey, activation, and routing controls; reduces exposure if the revised process cannot be operated reliably. | Discards low-risk-house gains and useful ownership changes; baseline also has material delays and failures. | Appropriate if harmful behavior cannot be contained, risk classification cannot support safe routing, or even the safeguarded low-risk path fails comparison. Targeted restoration is justified now; wholesale rollback is not supported solely by aggregate deterioration. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation** | Preserves accountability while removing two plausible sources of harm. Easier to manage than the full bundle. | Ownership alone is not causally validated and may retain bottlenecks or absence stalls. Removing other components may reduce apparent savings. | Reasonable with staffing headroom, shared records, backup coverage, clear specialist responsibilities, and evidence that ownership improves outcomes under absence. Scale incrementally rather than assuming nationwide suitability. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Separates treatment, case-mix, and capacity effects; estimates segment-specific economics and uncertainty. | Delays expansion and requires operational discipline. Repeating known harmful configurations would impose avoidable customer failures. | Appropriate under restored activation/permit safeguards, reliable risk labels, concurrent controls, and predefined stopping rules. Randomize only components with genuine uncertainty. |
| **E. Low-risk deployment, controls elsewhere, measurement before wider scale** | Retains the strongest reported benefits and contains the most concerning pathways. Produces evidence while continuing useful service improvements. | Requires reliable classification and multiple routing rules; mistaken low-risk assignment or shared-resource congestion could undermine gains. | Best current choice if unknown-risk orders receive conservative routing, expansion remains capacity-limited, and each additional segment must pass its own rollout gates. |

E should be implemented with C’s protective changes and D’s evaluation. This is a policy for staged learning and deployment, not a claim that the hybrid will improve every metric.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

The ranking reflects **marginal decision value now**: ability to change deployment or safeguards, severity of avoidable loss, speed, and feasibility. It is qualitative because intervention costs, loss valuations, and prior probabilities are unavailable. Measurement collection can run concurrently; a lower-ranked experiment may become more valuable after its prerequisites are met.

| Rank | Measurement or experiment | Uncertainty resolved | How the result could change the decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **M7: Conformance monitoring for early billing and missing permits** | Whether warning traces represent actual control failures, inapplicable requirements, or incomplete recording; whether restored gates work. | Confirmed failures require containment and case remediation. Verified control execution allows continued restricted deployment; logging-only problems require instrumentation repair rather than an unsupported operational conclusion. Validate against source records and explicit requirements. |
| **2** | **M6: Validate the building-risk rule against survey outcomes** | Whether risk classification and skip eligibility distinguish addresses needing preparation, especially apartments and older buildings. | Poor accuracy or coverage supports continued survey requirements and revised classification. A reliable narrow subgroup could qualify for a guarded skip experiment. Use representative sampling, including would-be skipped addresses, rather than only historically surveyed cases. |
| **3** | **M4: Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing** | Whether visible savings survive revisits, complaints, activation labor, and billing corrections. | Mature net savings could support expansion of a safe segment; persistent excess cost favors narrower deployment or redesigned recovery. Include costs before billing and costs for unresolved orders so non-billed failures do not disappear. |
| **4** | **M3: Structured exception reason codes** | Whether failures arise from survey omission, permits, customer access, technical activation, capacity, or ownership. | A concentrated, correctable cause supports targeted controls. Diverse causes or widespread recurrence support a larger redesign or segment rollback. Require auditable codes and periodically review their accuracy. |
| **5** | **M5: Coordinator versus shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether reduced transfers require dependence on one person, and whether backups preserve the speed benefit. | Successful shared coverage supports broader ownership deployment. Continued stalls favor team ownership, additional capacity, or narrower coordinator scope. Hold technical controls comparable and use absence drills or ethically managed natural absences. |
| **6** | **M1: Randomized survey skipping by risk stratum** | The causal effect of omitting surveys on speed, tails, quality, and cost. | Safe noninferiority with meaningful savings could reopen skipping for validated strata. Harm supports permanent exclusion of those strata. Defer until M6 and exception data establish genuine uncertainty; exclude required-permit or clearly unsafe cases and stop early for harm. Its eventual decision value could be high. |
| **7** | **M2: Start timestamps for installation and activation work** | Whether long inter-event intervals reflect queueing, execution, or ownership delays. | Queueing points toward capacity and routing changes; longer execution points toward technical preparation or specialist support. It helps choose a remedy, but does not remove the already observed quality problems. Start inexpensive instrumentation immediately. |
| **8** | **M8: Separate experience of appointment speed and working service** | Whether faster scheduling compensates customers for later unreliability, and which service dimensions drive dissatisfaction. | Strong reliability concerns justify tighter quality guardrails and customer recovery; sustained gains across both dimensions strengthen the low-risk business case. Survey at comparable exposure times and examine nonresponse. |

**Evaluation design and sequencing.** First repair risk/segment labels, case identifiers, activity mappings, and outcome definitions. Keep unknown risk as an explicit category with conservative routing; analyze reliably labeled cohorts separately and test sensitivity to earlier missing data. Reconcile the six-week enrollment window, reported duration quantiles, and complaint follow-up. Record region, staffing, absences, and treatment actually received.

Start M7, M6, M3, M2, and prospective M4 collection together. Review controls and backlog within two weeks, but do not claim mature economics then. Final costing needs the specified 60-day follow-up after billing, with continued tracking of unresolved orders.

Compare the safeguarded hybrid with a concurrent comparator within region and risk stratum, keeping mandatory controls in every arm. Randomize eligible orders where feasible; if shared staff create substantial spillovers, use enough team/time blocks and analyze the resulting clustering. Four regions alone are not a strong basis for a region-level randomized trial. Standardize aggregate comparisons to a common case mix and report segment results separately.

Predefine endpoints, practical margins, stopping rules, and sample size sufficient for tail and quality estimates. Report uncertainty intervals and account for clustered work. Separate ownership and survey questions through M5 and M1 rather than attributing a bundle comparison to one component. Missing evidence should hold the affected expansion decision open, not be counted as a pass.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Adopt E now: controlled expansion for verified simple, low-risk houses; restored safeguards elsewhere; targeted experiments before wider rollout.**

- **Immediate action:** Gate billing on verified successful activation, verify applicable permits, and review affected open and already billed cases. Assign one accountable owner through working service and resolution of billing exceptions. Add shared case records, named backups, and recovery capacity.
- **Pause:** Suspend predictive survey skipping for medium-risk, permit-sensitive, and unknown-risk orders, and suspend deferred post-billing activation checks across all segments. Pause national rollout of the unmodified R bundle.
- **Scale:** Retain combined validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership for reliably identified low-risk houses, using capped regional expansion. Extend ownership elsewhere only with backup coverage and sufficient capacity; retain required preparation and activation controls. Do not infer low risk merely from missing attributes.
- **Measure next:** Prioritize M7, M6, and M4, supported immediately by M3 and M2. Test absence resilience with M5. Run M1 only in validated strata with genuine uncertainty; use M8 to verify customer value beyond appointment speed.

**Broader-rollout gates.** The following are proposed business acceptance criteria, not observed results or existing contractual SLAs. Fix them before evaluating the next experiment. Compare each candidate segment with its concurrent, matched comparator; the pooled baseline is a planning reference, not a valid benchmark for every segment. Let Δ denote candidate minus comparator.

| Dimension | Proposed evidence required before expansion |
|---|---|
| Useful speed | Order-to-verified-working-service median improves by at least two days, with a one-sided 95% upper confidence bound for Δ below zero. Report order-to-billing separately. |
| Tail duration | p90 and p95 point estimates are no worse than the comparator. One-sided 95% upper bounds exclude deterioration exceeding two days at p90 and three days at p95. Include unfinished orders using appropriate time-to-event analysis. |
| Installation quality | First-time-right point estimate is no worse, and the one-sided 95% lower bound excludes a decline exceeding 1 percentage point. Track manual activation and repeat visits as explanatory outcomes. |
| Complaints and SLA | Point estimates are no worse; one-sided 95% upper bounds exclude increases exceeding 0.5 percentage points for each. Use mature 30-day complaint observation and identical SLA definitions. |
| Full economics | Consistent, mature costing demonstrates no higher mean full cost: the one-sided 95% upper bound for the cost difference is at or below zero. Include recovery, complaint handling, billing corrections, and unresolved-case costs. |
| Required controls | Verified activation gates and applicable permit controls operate on every relevant release; no unresolved confirmed bypasses. Missing logs must be reconciled before declaring success. A confirmed prohibited release pauses the affected pathway. |
| Capacity and resilience | Demonstrated absence coverage and transferable case context. Use a proposed sustained utilization target of at most 85% for coordinator and second-visit capacity, then calibrate headroom to demand variability; utilization alone does not prove resilience. |

These gates permit small, explicitly bounded uncertainty around quality and tails while requiring useful speed and acceptable economics. They do not assume that one design dominates all outcomes. If a trade-off exceeds the agreed limits, management must explicitly reassess that segment rather than relaxing thresholds after seeing results.

Expand in stages only for segments that pass, with continuing control monitoring and capacity checks. A passing low-risk segment does not authorize apartments, old buildings, business customers, or rural installations. If safeguards cannot be enforced or the protected low-risk path fails, roll back the affected pathway; use a full rollback only if failures are systemic and cannot be contained.
