## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I use **interventional (stochastic) direct and indirect effects** as the primary framework, with controlled direct effects for specific contrasts. Reasons: (a) `flag` is a deterministic threshold function of `risk_score`, so natural effects requiring within-unit crossing of the mediator distribution are not well-defined; (b) mediators are sequential and share queues, violating the cross-world independence needed for natural effects; (c) interference through shared review/escalation queues violates SUTVA. Interventions are defined as *policy changes to the organisation’s response to the flag*, not as "switching the flag bit while everything else stays equal" — the latter is ill-defined because `flag` is downstream of `risk_score`, which reviewers may still see.

**Interference, exposure-induced confounding, treatment-confounder feedback (180 words):** Interference arises because `manual_queue_wait` and `escalation_queue_wait` are functions of *all* cases currently occupying shared reviewers/seniors; flagged cases shift the queue for everyone (E9). Exposure-induced confounding appears because `flag` induces `manual_review`, which then becomes a confounder for the `escalation -> cycle_time` relationship — one cannot condition on review occurrence without also conditioning on a downstream of treatment. Treatment-confounder feedback: `flag -> manual_review -> escalation`, where the manual-review state mediates and simultaneously confounds later mediators. Cross-world assumptions (e.g., nested counterfactual independence) are not credible when queues couple cases. Therefore natural direct/indirect effects are not identified; only interventional analogues with explicitly specified distributions of downstream mediators are defensible. Priority-lane acceleration of triage cannot be assumed to be the "direct effect" of the flag because the same flag also increases mandatory review volume, which lengthens the *downstream* path through the same queue.

**Observed contrasts and why they are not automatically causal (150 words):** Flagged cases wait ~14 min vs ~149 min to triage start (consistent with priority-lane acceleration), then have ~0 min to manual start vs ~113 min for unflagged reviewed cases (consistent with queue skipping). Yet flagged cases spend ~391 min in manual service vs ~165 min and escalate much more often (6/10 vs 2/10), with escalation service times ~420 min vs ~120 min. The downstream delays dwarf the triage gain. These contrasts are not causal because: (i) `flag` is triggered by high `risk_score`, which itself correlates with `complexity` and likely with `U_underlying_risk` that drives reviewer workload; (ii) `arrival_load` differs across the two groups and modulates queues; (iii) the high-load flagged subgroup has both the most senior scarcity and the longest service — a composition effect, not a flag effect. The data are also single-day, small (n=20), and may not span a stable equilibrium.

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 (current) | flagged faster (~14m) | flagged dominate M2 and senior queue; unflagged reviewed cases queue | flagged ~2× longer despite faster triage | high risk detected; some over-treatment | high on shared queues | observational only | keep as benchmark |
| P1 (suppress flag display) | priority lane lost  flagged triage waits revert to ~unflagged | review volume may drop if reviewers rely on flag; risk_score still seen so partial persistence | flagged cycle drops if reviewers do less; little change if reviewers infer flag from score | risk that high-risk cases skip review if reviewers under-react to score | low | weak (depends on reviewer behaviour) | not a clean pilot; useful only as a falsification arm |
| P2 (priority + no mandatory review) | flagged triage still fast | review volume falls; queue clears faster for unflagged | flagged cycle drops substantially (review is the long stage); risk that some flagged skip review | medium-high; depends on whether human judgment catches high-risk without prompt | positive for unflagged | medium if randomised | strong pilot candidate with quality guardrail |
| P3 (priority + protected review capacity) | flagged triage fast; review queue for flagged isolated | unflagged queue improves; flagged review waits stable | flagged cycle drops if protected capacity  arrival rate; otherwise marginal | low; review still mandatory | positive for unflagged, neutral for flagged | medium; needs capacity data | best candidate if capacity can be added |
| P4 (priority + review + change escalation criteria/capacity) | flagged triage fast | escalation queue shift depends on criteria tightening vs capacity addition | flagged cycle drops if escalation criteria tightened *or* senior capacity added; could rise if criteria loosen without capacity | depends: tighter criteria may miss some true escalations; added capacity reduces risk | senior capacity addition positive; criteria tightening ambiguous | low (escalation trigger endogenous) | useful as a second-stage pilot after P2/P3 |

**Preferred pilot — P3 (priority triage + protected review capacity for flagged cases), with a P2 arm for comparison:**

- *Eligibility:* cases with `flag=1` and `complexity  {High, Medium}` arriving within the pilot window; unflagged cases are the spillover study population.
- *Randomisation/assignment rule:* 50% of flagged cases randomly assigned to protected review capacity (P3), 50% remain on the shared queue (P0). Randomisation stratified by `complexity` and `arrival_load` band. Unflagged cases are not randomised but tracked.
- *Queue-capacity guardrail:* protected reviewer slot is reserved only if shared review backlog > N cases; if protected reviewer idle for > 30 min, allow shared-queue pull to avoid idle capacity.
- *Primary outcome:* total cycle time (close  arrival) for flagged cases. *Secondary outcomes:* manual-queue wait for unflagged cases (interference), reject rate and post-close audit flags (quality proxy), escalation rate.
- *Minimum process data:* per-case timestamps for all stages, reviewer IDs, queue-depth snapshots at triage_done and manual_start, risk_score, complexity, arrival_load, post-close audit outcome.
- *Stopping conditions:* (i) post-close audit shows excess adverse outcomes in either arm above pre-specified threshold; (ii) unflagged cycle time worsens by > X% relative to pre-pilot baseline (interference harm); (iii) < 30 flagged cases enrolled within 4 weeks (power failure).

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** If an unobserved `U_underlying_risk` both raises the probability of being flagged and independently lengthens manual service and escalation (e.g., because harder cases simply take longer), the apparent flagcycle-time effect could be substantially or entirely confounded, reversing the conclusion that the policy bundle causes the delay.
2. **Queue interference dominance:** If the longer flagged cycle is primarily caused by other flagged cases occupying the same M2 reviewer and senior queue — i.e., each flagged case delays the others — then adding protected capacity (P3) might not shorten individual flagged cycle time, and the policy recommendation could shift toward throttling flag volume rather than routing.
3. **Decision quality:** If post-close audits show that flagged cases’ extra review/escalation detects material high-risk issues that unflagged cases miss at non-trivial rates, then the longer cycle is justified and suppressing mandatory review (P2) would be harmful despite the cycle-time saving.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** If "triage_done" is recorded when an *entry* is made rather than when triage is clinically complete (or if `escalation_start` is recorded at decision time, not at queue entry), then "waiting" durations are mis-measured and the decomposition could reverse: what appears as "manual service" may actually be queue time, or vice versa.
5. **Positivity / lack of overlap:** If at high `risk_score` essentially all cases are flagged and at low `risk_score` none are, then no unflagged comparable group exists for high-risk cases and the interventional analogues E2–E8 are not identified near the threshold; conclusions would have to be limited to local-RD estimates and could not support a system-wide policy claim.
6. **Reviewer behaviour change:** If reviewers have learned to treat *any* high `risk_score` as equivalent to the flag (e.g., they always send risk_score  0.7 to manual review regardless of the flag display), then P1 (suppress display) would have no operational effect, the apparent "flag" effect would actually be a "risk_score" effect, and the relevant intervention is to change reviewer guidelines or the risk score itself, not the flag display.

## 9. Final synthesis

What is directly observed: flagged cases reach triage start ~10× faster than unflagged cases (~14 vs ~149 min), enter manual review with ~0 min queue wait, but spend ~2.4× as long in manual service (~391 vs ~165 min) and escalate ~3× more often (6/10 vs 2/10). Their total cycle time is ~2.1× longer (~1051 vs ~490 min). The downstream delays — manual service, manual-to-escalation wait, escalation service — dwarf the triage acceleration. These are correlations within an n=20 single-day sample.

What is a plausible mechanism: the flag is a policy bundle — priority triage plus mandatory manual review plus (via review findings) higher escalation. The triage gain is real but small; the mandatory review routes flagged cases into a long, shared, scarce-resource pipeline dominated by reviewer M2 and a small senior team. The same flag that accelerates triage also *creates* the load that lengthens the queue for everyone, including other flagged cases. The flag is therefore not "causing delay" in a simple sense — the organisation's response to the flag is.

What remains unidentified: the counterfactual "would this case have been long without the flag?" is unidentified because (i) `flag` is a deterministic function of `risk_score`, which itself correlates with `complexity` and `U_underlying_risk`; (ii) queue interference couples cases; (iii) reviewers may infer flag status from the visible risk_score, so P1 may not equal "no flag." Natural direct/indirect effects are not identifiable; only interventional analogues with explicit mediator distributions are defensible.

Most useful next evidence: a randomised pilot of P3 (protected review capacity for flagged cases) against P0 within flag=1, with P2 (no mandatory review) as a secondary arm, tracking cycle time, unflagged spillover, and post-close audit quality. This separates queue-mediated delay from occurrence-mediated delay and produces policy-actionable rather than purely descriptive evidence.
