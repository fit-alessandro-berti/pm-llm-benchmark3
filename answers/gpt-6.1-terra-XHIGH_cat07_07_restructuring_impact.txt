Report case counts, missingness and uncertainty for each slice. A handover is a recorded change in resource, not necessarily a change in accountability or an informal conversation.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

- **Tail latency:** A few very old cases can be masked by many quick closures. Track p90/p95, oldest queue age, overdue open cases and SLA misses.
- **High-complexity cases:** Simple cases may improve while complex cases wait longer for scarce expertise. Compare distributions within complexity and specialty, including open work.
- **Local knowledge loss:** Regional conventions and informal early advice may disappear before recorded errors rise. Track clarification loops and rework, and ask staff and customers about missed context.
- **Fairness:** Team, language, accommodation or customer-segment gaps can widen while the aggregate improves. Compare waits, outcomes and rework for comparable cases, with adequate denominators and privacy protections.
- **Learning:** Routing all difficult work away can erode local capability and future independent resolution. Use competency assessments, supervised participation and cross-training measures over time.
- **Accountability:** A fast average can coexist with cases stranded between queues or repeatedly reassigned. Measure unassigned age, ownership changes and unresolved exceptions, and audit decisions.
- **Exception handling:** Rare legal, risk, security or difficult quality cases may sit behind routine work or be routed incorrectly. Track specialty-specific backlog, escalation time, reroutes and aged exceptions.
- **Resilience:** A shared queue may work on ordinary days but fail when a specialist is absent or demand spikes. Measure backup coverage, peak backlog and recovery time.
- **Quality and selection:** Quick closure can precede later rework or appeals; excluding unfinished cases can make current performance look better. Follow cohorts long enough to capture later outcomes and monitor open-case ages.

# 5. Pilot design

Pilot the shared technical service and approval queue in a limited, reversible phase, with explicit capacity reserved for legal/risk/security and difficult quality checks. Include East and a comparable subset of North/South cases so pooled capacity's unequal effects are visible; retain a named local case owner, local expert access for regional exceptions, and an escalation route. Test the proposed routing rules and document any temporary exceptions. Define security expertise and capacity before routing security cases; do not assume existing staff have that skill.

Randomly assign eligible cases within each team, complexity and specialty stratum to pilot or the existing model where operationally feasible. Otherwise use comparable units or time-matched cohorts and adjust for measured case mix. Prevent spillover: ring-fence staffing and queue capacity for both arms, record shared resources and absences, and avoid diverting control work into the pilot. If that is infeasible, use a phased team-level rollout and treat comparison as observational. Agree on the primary outcomes, data definitions and thresholds before launch.

Observe at least 8–12 weeks before and 8–12 weeks during the pilot, longer if volumes, seasonality or the cycle-time tail require it. Follow each intake cohort through closure and a defined rework window; report open-case age at each cutoff. Compare concurrent groups and pre/post changes, with uncertainty and case counts, segmented by origin team, complexity, specialty and customer segment, and further by priority and accommodation needs where available and lawful. Preserve origin team even when work moves to the shared service.

Set guardrails against the concurrent control and baseline: p90/p95 cycle time, queue age and backlog, SLA misses, rework/quality, outcomes and satisfaction, segment gaps, ownership gaps, workload and absence coverage. For illustration, pre-agree escalation if a queue's backlog and oldest age rise for two consecutive weeks, or if p90, SLA misses or a segment gap materially worsen relative to control with sufficient observations. Agree numeric tolerances with process owners using baseline variability and service commitments, rather than treating an arbitrary threshold as evidence of harm. For a severe quality, accommodation or accountability failure, pause the affected route immediately. Investigate early signals, add capacity, change priorities or routing, restore local advice, or roll back the affected queue if deterioration persists. Record decisions and reassess after adjustments.

Collect short weekly anonymous staff feedback and structured case reviews from local and shared staff, including failed referrals, informal advice, ambiguity, learning opportunities and customer accommodations. Include customer feedback and audit a sample of both fast and delayed cases. A go/no-go decision should require stable queues, acceptable quality and equity guardrails, evidence of workable ownership and coverage, and enough follow-up to observe delayed outcomes.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with modifications.** Pooling could relieve East and improve access to scarce expertise, but the proposed combined workload and already high Legal/Risk utilization make capacity and queue design decisive. Preserve local advice and a named accountable owner, provide skill-specific capacity and backup, define accommodation and exception paths, and maintain supervised local learning. Expand only if the pilot meets the agreed distributional, quality, fairness and resilience guardrails across teams and relevant segments; adjust or stop affected routes if it does not.

Management should not infer from an early fall in average completed-case cycle time that handovers, total work, tail delays or inequities have fallen, or that learning and resilience are safe. Early results may reflect case mix, unfinished slow cases, a temporary capacity buffer or shifting work into queues. Interpret them alongside open-case age, capacity, segmented distributions and later outcomes.
