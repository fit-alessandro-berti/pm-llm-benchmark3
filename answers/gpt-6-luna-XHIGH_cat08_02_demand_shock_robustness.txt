# 1. Stress scenarios

1. **Peak mix shift:** Total weekly volume rises from about 2,300 to 3,300 orders, while Urgent share rises from 18% to 29% and Complex-panel share from 13% to 20%. This is the observed peak pattern, not a uniform volume increase. Urgent penalties apply after 18 hours; Routine target is 72 hours.
2. **Assay degradation during the peak:** M2 becomes unreliable or unavailable during a high-demand week, with repeats concentrated after night shifts and M2 use. The scenario is especially adverse if Urgent and Complex-panel cases have already been assigned to M1 and Routine work depends on M2.
3. **Medical-validation and specialist absence:** A medical validator is absent during a Complex-panel-heavy peak, overlapping the known every-second-Friday specialist absence. The 2.4× higher specialist-hold propensity and 3.1× higher medical-validation propensity for Complex-panel orders compound the loss.
4. **Courier compression and delay:** Deliveries bunch at the 10:00 and 17:00 routes, or one route arrives late. Intake arrivals become lumpy while downstream benches and machines are already highly loaded.
5. **Peak with elevated sample defects:** A winter surge brings poor samples and more accession exceptions alongside the higher Urgent/Complex mix. Recollection and correction loops compete for intake and specialist capacity while the original order remains unresolved.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

- **Peak mix shift:** Registration can absorb some work with overtime, but its error rate rises beyond 9-hour shifts. The first hard constraint is likely pre-analytical check (96% utilization, 9.5h median queue); its flows to recollection and correction total 1,200 of 9,730 receipts at peak. Errors and rework then consume intake capacity: recollection-to-receive takes a median 60h, and clerks spend time explaining errors. Bypassing batch wait for Urgent cases fragments machine schedules (only 58% bypass at peak), pushing the 98%-utilized machines and 101%-utilized technical validators further. At peak, test-preparation-to-batch-wait is 14h and batch-wait-to-assay is 22h. Relieving machine queues alone moves the backlog into technical validation (16h queue; assay-to-technical validation edge 15h) and then medical validation (22h queue; technical-to-medical edge 24h). The 610 peak holds versus 290 normal, and hold-to-medical edge rises from 18h to 48h. A strict Urgent-first rule can protect some 18h contracts but starve Routine and Complex-panel work and increase total WIP.
- **Assay degradation:** Capacity falls at a station already at 98% peak utilization and 18h median queue. If M1 is reserved for Urgent and Complex-panel while M2 fails, Routine work accumulates; if cases are shifted to M1 without sequencing, critical work does too. Repeat assays consume the machine a second time: 1,260 peak transitions enter Repeat assay, 1,080 return to Assay run, and the logs associate repeats with night shifts and M2. Machine recovery can therefore expose technical validation as the next constraint (101% utilization), followed by medical validation and specialist review. Broad batch enlargement might reduce setup work but worsens waits for Urgent work and delays the point at which repeat failures are detected.
- **Validator/specialist absence:** The 22h medical queue, 93% peak utilization, and 24h technical-to-medical edge leave little slack. Specialist review is more fragile: 36h median queue and a 48h hold-to-medical edge at peak, even at 87% utilization, with one specialist predictably unavailable every second Friday. A shift or qualified backup can shorten that queue, but may simply push cases into medical validation unless the shift covers that step too. Cross-training technical validators to correct accession errors can relieve the 14h correction queue, but drawing them away from technical validation worsens its already overloaded 16h queue.
- **Courier compression/delay:** Bunched arrivals first raise courier intake (7.5h peak queue), then sample receipt-to-pre-analytical wait (9h edge) and pre-analytical queue (9.5h). A late route creates idle periods followed by a pulse that can exceed bench, assay, and validation capacity. Smoothing deliveries helps these queues only if receiving and downstream work can absorb arrivals. Faster digital registration (I8) likewise shifts work downstream; it does not create pre-analytical or machine capacity.
- **Defect-heavy peak:** A two-hour defect decision can stop bad samples from occupying later capacity, but immediate rejection may multiply recollection traffic. Pre-analytical check already sends 780 peak orders to recollection, and the return loop takes 60h; adding poorly coordinated recollections risks overloading courier intake and clerks. Conversely, leaving defects undetected sends avoidable repeats toward machines and technical validation. Defect triage must therefore include a rapid, explicit recollection path and track defect categories.

# 3. Robust redesign package

- **Use a controlled, class-aware release policy with WIP limits at pre-analytical, assay, and both validation steps.** Reserve a small, reviewed share of near-term machine and validator capacity for Urgent work, while giving Complex-panel orders protected access to medical and specialist capacity. Sequence remaining work by age and due time, with a cap on how much Urgent work can jump the queue. Do not release more work into a downstream queue that is already above its limit. This addresses the observed queue migration and avoids treating all work as equally interchangeable. It depends on reliable case labels, timestamps, and queue visibility; misclassification or excessive WIP caps can leave capacity idle or miss contracts.
- **Smooth courier arrivals and coordinate receiving with downstream slots (I7).** Spread pickups around the 10:00/17:00 peaks and use appointment/receiving windows where possible. This reduces arrival pulses rather than merely moving them. It depends on courier and clinic cooperation; missed or weather-delayed routes remain possible. A smooth arrival profile can still overload pre-analytical or machines if total or weighted demand exceeds capacity.
- **Automate complete-metadata registration (I8), but gate release to work queues.** This should reduce the 2.8h registration queue without sending an uncontrolled surge to pre-analytical check. Monitor completeness and error rates; incomplete digital orders must enter a staffed exception path. Automation fails as a capacity fix if pre-analytical check is the binding constraint.
- **Introduce early defect triage (I6) with a two-hour decision target, defect codes, and a staffed recollection coordination path.** Correctable documentation issues should go to correction; unsuitable specimens should be identified promptly with collection instructions. Track repeat/recollection reasons so the team distinguishes preventable defects from assay failures. This can reduce downstream waste, but it depends on rapid clinician response and could worsen the 60h recollection loop if re-collection logistics are not available.
- **Cross-train for bounded flex coverage, not permanent reassignment (I4).** Accession specialists remain the primary correction pool; trained technical validators can cover correction only when the correction queue breaches a trigger and technical-validation WIP is below its own limit. The reverse is not assumed unless competency is validated. This can shorten the 14h correction queue, but the technical pool is already at 101% utilization, so unbounded diversion is unsafe.
- **Use smaller, timed Routine batches with urgent exceptions rather than doubling all batches (I2).** Batch Routine work in planned windows (testing 12 as a starting cap), with a maximum wait before dispatch and protected urgent slots. This retains setup efficiency while containing the 14h preparation-to-batch-wait and 22h batch-wait-to-assay peak edges. It depends on knowing assay compatibility and machine setup costs; too-small batches waste capacity, while too-large batches recreate long waits.
- **Treat machine assignment as dynamic and failure-aware, not a permanent M1/M2 split (I3).** Protect suitable M1 capacity for Urgent and Complex-panel work when it is available, while limiting Routine loading of M2 to its demonstrated reliability and repeat rate. Keep a fallback plan for M2 failure and cap work released to either machine. This depends on real-time equipment status and assay compatibility; dedicating M1 too rigidly can strand capacity or overload it, and shifting all Routine work to M2 can increase repeats.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

- **Peak mix policy:** Turn on when Urgent share approaches 29% or Complex-panel share approaches 20%, or when pre-analytical utilization stays above 90% for a shift or the median queue exceeds 8h. Use due-time bands and the WIP release gate; reserve Urgent slots but limit consecutive overrides. Turn off after shares and queues remain below their trigger bands for two consecutive operating days. This prevents one temporary surge from permanently penalizing Routine work.
- **Machine outage/degradation policy:** Turn on at any confirmed machine outage, or when a machine’s repeat rate rises above its rolling baseline and its queue exceeds 12h. Stop loading the affected machine, prioritize repeat-risk checks by assay and shift, transfer only compatible work to the available machine, and use the emergency partner/approved referral path for cases that cannot meet their deadline. Turn off after engineering clears the machine and repeat rate/queue return to baseline for two shifts. Do not respond by sending all work to M1 or enlarging batches.
- **Validator absence policy:** Activate the evening medical-validation shift for forecast absences or when medical queue exceeds 12h, or technical-to-medical median wait exceeds 18h. Schedule qualified specialist backup or selective Complex-panel outsourcing when specialist queue exceeds 24h or absence is confirmed. Keep a clinician-led triage for cases whose result can safely wait; Urgent and clinically time-sensitive work gets the protected slots. Deactivate surge staffing/referral when queues remain below 8h (medical) and 16h (specialist) for two shifts and staffing returns. The 12h transport delay in I10 means only suitable Complex-panel work should be referred, with chain-of-custody and result reconciliation confirmed; never use this as an Urgent shortcut unless end-to-end timing and accountability are assured.
- **Courier disruption policy:** Turn on for a missed/late route or when courier intake queue exceeds 4h, or sample arrival is materially concentrated into a single route window. Rebook overflow pickups, stagger receiving, and temporarily lower downstream release caps to the demonstrated capacity of the next constrained station. Turn off when arrivals return to planned windows and intake queue falls below 2h for two route cycles.
- **Defect/rework policy:** Turn on if rework rises above the 11.8% peak reference or the pre-analytical-to-recollection share increases across two daily reviews. Add a defect-triage huddle and clinician contact coverage; investigate M2/night-shift repeat clusters separately from collection defects. Turn off the extra triage coverage once rework is below the normal 7.5% reference for a week and recollection waits are stable. Treat these figures as warning references, not proof that any one cause has changed.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- A simultaneous surge in Urgent and Complex-panel share can exceed the protected slots; prioritization still requires clinical/due-time rules and some cases will miss targets.
- The process remains sensitive to long recollection loops (60h peak) and specialist holds (48h hold-to-medical edge). Faster detection cannot guarantee a replacement sample or specialist availability.
- A machine outage plus high repeat rate can consume the remaining machine slack even with dynamic assignment. Assay compatibility may prevent transferring work freely.
- The redesign itself can create **fragmented machine schedules and stranded capacity** if Urgent reservations are too large, WIP caps are set too low, or M1 is held idle for work that does not arrive. It can also shift labor from technical validation to accession correction at the wrong time. Review reservation and cap settings against observed arrivals and queue ages at each shift handoff.
- Flattening by order hides the number of tubes and assays per order. Complex-panel workload may be undercounted if the team uses orders alone to plan machine slots; track tube/assay workload as well as order counts.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

**Leading indicators (review each shift during peaks):**

- New arrivals by case type and by route window; flag Urgent share near/above 29% or Complex-panel share near/above 20%, and any sharp concentration at 10:00 or 17:00.
- Queue age and WIP by station and case type: warning at pre-analytical queue above 8h, correction above 12h, assay above 12h, technical validation above 12h, medical validation above 12h, or specialist review above 24h. Escalate earlier if queue age is rising over consecutive checks.
- Utilization and available capacity by machine, technical validators, medical validators, and specialist review; warning above 90% for a shift, with immediate outage status separate from utilization.
- Batch wait by class and urgent bypass share; investigate if Urgent bypass falls below the observed 58% peak level or Routine wait grows while batches are enlarged.
- Defect, recollection, and repeat entries by cause, machine, and shift. Use 11.8% peak rework as a warning reference; separate M2/night-shift assay repeats from pre-analytical defects.
- Age of open recollection loops and cases awaiting a specialist, plus confirmed absences and courier route misses, so queues can be forecast before they breach service targets.

**Lagging outcomes (review daily and by cohort):**

- Urgent percentage released within 18h and count/age of cases forecast to breach; Routine percentage within 72h.
- Median and p90 cycle time by case type, especially Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure, compared with normal and peak references.
- Overall rework/repeat rate and percentage of cases with any queue over 12h. Use these to judge whether the leading indicators and activated policies are improving resilience rather than merely moving the backlog.
