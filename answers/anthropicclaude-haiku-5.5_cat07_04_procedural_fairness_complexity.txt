# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness here means whether applicants in different groups face comparable procedures, burdens, delays, and quality of support once legitimate differences in complexity are accounted for. Outcome parity is one dimension among several.

**Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates range from 58% (D) to 78% (A). Raw gaps are not evidence of discrimination, because groups differ in risk, documentation, language need, and accommodation requests. Raw outcome parity is also not a fairness standard in itself, since a lower approval rate can be correct if the applicants are less eligible.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** The preliminary model gives approval gaps of -5.5pp (B), -2.2pp (C), and -3.1pp (D). These are much smaller than the raw gaps, especially for D (raw gap about -20pp). The adjustment is only as credible as its covariates. Some adjusted covariates may be affected by group membership (see Section 5), so adjusted gaps are a partial picture.

**Procedural burden.** This covers extra steps, information requests, loops, handovers, and escalations the applicant does not need. The adjusted results show higher probabilities of Request Missing Documents (+9.5pp for B, +4.2pp for C) and Supervisor Review (+8.0pp for B, +5.5pp for C). Supervisor Review is not on the list of legitimate routing rules, so its excess is harder to justify.

**Timeliness.** Adjusted cycle-time gaps are +2.6 days (B), +4.1 days (C), and +0.9 days (D). Medians are reported in the raw table and adjusted gaps are means from a different model, so these figures should not be read as directly comparable. Tail performance also matters: the 90th percentile for C is 38.8 days, against 21.4 days for A.

**Consistency of routing.** Routing should follow stated rules: high risk leads to Fraud Review, accommodation requests lead to Accommodation Review, and language needs lead to Language Support Contact. The question is whether the same rule is applied the same way across groups, and whether extra steps occur without a triggering rule. The variant data suggest that Supervisor Review and handovers occur beyond the stated rules for some groups.

**Quality and accessibility of support.** Rights-preserving steps are only fair if they are reachable and effective. Waiting for bilingual staff (3.8 days for B) and for accommodation specialists (4.7 days for C) may mean the support arrives too late to be useful. Outcome data cannot show whether the support was of good quality.

**Appeal and rework burden.** Adjusted appeal probabilities are +4.4pp (B) and +5.7pp (D), with D's appeal path (V6) taking a median of 30.7 days. Higher rework for B (22%) and C (24%) suggests errors or information gaps early in the process. Appeal rates are an imperfect signal because they could reflect unclear decisions, perceived unfairness, or genuine error.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group B is concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. Group C is concentrated on 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. Distributing scarce, rights-relevant capacity unevenly is a fairness concern even when routing is correct.

**Transparency and dignity (partially unobservable).** Explanation quality, communication tone, and whether applicants understood the process are not captured. These dimensions may matter most to applicants and cannot be assessed from this log.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should separate four things that the raw data conflate:

- **Disparity:** any difference between groups in outcomes or burden.
- **Discrimination:** differential treatment that is not explained by legitimate factors and is attributable to the process, staff, or rules.
- **Legitimate routing:** differences that follow policy-mandated steps for specific needs.
- **Uncertainty:** differences whose source cannot be identified with the available data.

**Step 1: Define the complexity signature and causal structure.** Before adjusting, specify which variables are legitimate determinants of procedure and which may be mediators or post-treatment:

- Legitimate complexity: accommodation request, language need, documented incompleteness (if not caused by language barriers), household size, region, month.
- Contested: risk_score_band (may encode historical enforcement), prior_case_count (may proxy for prior system contact), documentation_completeness (may be a mediator of language barriers).
- Likely post-treatment: Supervisor Review, Request Missing Documents, handovers. These should not be controls when estimating the total effect of group on cycle time or appeals.

**Step 2: Matching and stratification.** Use exact or coarsened exact matching on accommodation request, language need, risk band, household size, region, channel, and month. Run two specifications: one with documentation_completeness and prior_case_count, and one without. If the adjusted gap shrinks substantially when documentation is added, consider whether that is mediation (language barriers causing incompleteness) rather than confounding.

**Step 3: Path and variant comparison within strata.** Within each complexity stratum, compare:

- Variant frequencies (e.g., V1 vs V2 vs V3 share by group).
- Count of "excess" steps: steps not required by the policy rules for that case (e.g., Supervisor Review without a trigger, Request Missing Documents beyond the first request).
- Number of handovers and loops.

If a group takes more excess steps within the same stratum, that is evidence of procedural burden unrelated to stated routing.

**Step 4: Resource-assignment comparison.** For each activity, compare:

- Caseworker type and assignment concentration (share of group cases handled by top-k workers; Herfindahl index).
- Caseworker random effects in a mixed model, to separate individual worker speed from group effects.
- Queue length and workload at the day level when available, to test whether waiting reflects capacity or priority rules.

**Step 5: Waiting-time decomposition.** Decompose the cycle-time gap (log scale, Blinder-Oaxaca style) into:

- Explained by complexity (more steps required).
- Explained by extra steps (excess routing).
- Explained by waiting at specific handoffs (language, accommodation, supervisor, fraud).
- Unexplained residual.

Use quantile regression to examine whether the 90th-percentile gap differs from the median gap. Tail waits are often where procedural unfairness is concentrated.

**Step 6: Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.**

- Re-estimate adjusted gaps with and without risk_score_band; report how much of D's approval and appeal pattern depends on it.
- Re-estimate with and without documentation_completeness; report the mediated share.
- Use bounding or partial identification (e.g., Manski-style bounds) where the direction of bias is uncertain.
- Compute E-values or equivalent to estimate how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away the adjusted gaps.
- Replace risk_score_band with a re-estimated band based on less historically contaminated inputs, if feasible, and check whether fraud routing and approval gaps move.

**Step 7: Subgroup checks.** Repeat key comparisons by region (especially the two regions with backlogs and handover patterns) and by channel (in-person vs online). Check whether the B and C disparities concentrate in specific regions or channels. Concentration would point to local capacity or practice rather than group-level rules.

**Interpretation rules.**
- An adjusted gap that persists within matched strata and is not explained by policy-required steps is a disparity requiring explanation.
- A gap that is only explained by a variable that may itself be biased (e.g., risk band) is "explained but not justified" until the variable is audited.
- Sample sizes for small cells (e.g., 7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists) limit inference; report confidence intervals and avoid over-precise attribution.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Ranked by importance:

**(a) Language Support path and bilingual capacity (Group B). Partly justified, partly concerning.**
- V4 (language support path) accounts for 24% of B cases vs 1–3% for others. This routing is legitimate by policy.
- Median cycle time on V4 is 19.2 days. The language-support-to-eligibility wait of 3.8 days, with only 7 bilingual caseworkers, reflects a capacity constraint.
- Justified: the path itself. Applicants with language needs should receive this step.
- Concerning: the waiting time, the lack of a timeliness target for the step, and the concentration of B cases on a small pool. Applicants are delayed because of the state's staffing, not their own complexity. The policy's rights-preserving status makes this a clear fairness issue.

**(b) Missing-document loop for Group B. Mostly concerning, with uncertainty.**
- Adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5pp, and the completeness-to-request wait is 1.6 days vs 0.4 days for A.
- B has 46% incomplete documentation vs 21% for A, and 57% language need.
- This may reflect language barriers in understanding document requirements, in-person submission issues, or requests issued in a language the applicant cannot easily act on. Each is a procedural burden that the documentation covariate partly absorbs and thereby hides.
- Uncertainty: whether incompleteness is a true complexity signal or a product of how requests are communicated.

**(c) Accommodation path for Group C. Concerning.**
- V5 accounts for 31% of C cases (vs 2–3% for others), with a median cycle time of 21.6 days.
- Adjusted cycle time is +4.1 days, the largest adjusted gap in the table.
- The accommodation-to-eligibility wait of 4.7 days is a specialized-queue problem.
- Two of the five accommodation specialists also perform Supervisor Review. This creates a bottleneck and a possible independence problem. The same person who assessed accommodation needs also reviews the case.
- Justified: the Accommodation Review step itself, which is required by policy.
- Concerning: the queue length, the role overlap, and the absence of timeliness targets.

**(d) Unexplained Supervisor Review for Groups B and C. Concerning.**
- Adjusted probability of Supervisor Review: +8.0pp (B), +5.5pp (C). Supervisor review is not listed as a legitimate routing rule.
- Supervisor review to decision wait is 1.9 days (B), 1.6 days (C), vs 1.0 days (A), with backlog in two regions.
- Possible explanations include escalation after language or accommodation issues, defensive escalation, or regional practice. Without a stated rule, this extra step is a procedural burden that needs justification.

**(e) Fraud Review routing for Group D. Justified in form, uncertain in basis.**
- V3 accounts for 28% of D cases (vs 8–11% for others), with a fraud-review wait of 2.8 days (highest among groups).
- The routing follows the stated risk-based rule, so the step itself is legitimate.
- The concern is that risk_score_band is 51% high for D, and may encode historical enforcement patterns. If the risk score is biased, the routing is legitimate in form but unfair in source.

**(f) Appeal path for Group D. Concerning, with uncertainty.**
- V6 (appeal path) accounts for 13% of D cases vs 4–8% for others, with a median of 30.7 days.
- Adjusted appeal probability is +5.7pp.
- Approval gap is smaller than appeal gap, which suggests that the decision explanation or the process (including fraud review) may be driving contestation, not just the decision itself. Appeal quality is not observed.

**(g) Lower-concern items.**
- Decision-to-notification waits differ little (0.5–0.8 days). Not a priority.
- Submit-to-verification wait is slightly higher for B (1.4 days), consistent with in-person processing. Minor, but worth monitoring given in-person channel effects.
- V1 (fast path) share is lower for B, C, and D. Differences in V1 share are largely explained by complexity, but a within-stratum comparison is needed to confirm.

**Note on the variant table:** V4 is shown ending at Decision without Notify or Case Closure, which may indicate a truncated path summary or a real deviation. Check before drawing conclusions.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Legitimate mechanisms:**
1. **Genuine complexity.** Applicants with language needs, accommodation requests, or high risk scores require additional steps. Policy-mandated routing explains much of the path differences.
2. **Documentation requirements.** Some applicants legitimately need more documents, and requests are a necessary step.
3. **Regional variation in workload.** Two regions have backlogs. Regional differences are not group-specific, but group distribution across regions may produce group-level patterns.

**Concerning mechanisms:**
4. **Capacity mismatch.** Only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists serve rights-relevant needs. Queues create waits that are unrelated to applicant complexity. Groups with higher need bear the cost.
5. **Mandatory steps without timeliness targets.** Rights-preserving steps lack service-level targets, so they are not managed for speed or quality. Delays accumulate without alarm.
6. **Handover loss and bouncing.** Repeated general  specialist  general handovers add delay, risk information loss, and create opportunities for error. These are concentrated in two regions.
7. **Role conflict in review.** Accommodation specialists who also perform Supervisor Review may create bottlenecks and reduce independence of review.
8. **Language barrier as a cause of incompleteness.** Applicants who struggle with language may submit incomplete documents, triggering requests that a native-language applicant would not need. Documentation completeness then absorbs part of the effect.
9. **Risk score as encoded history.** If risk scores reflect past enforcement or prior-case history, routing to Fraud Review and resulting outcomes may reproduce historical disparity.
10. **Defensive escalation.** Caseworkers may escalate to supervisors more often for cases with language or accommodation complexity, reflecting uncertainty or fear of error rather than need.
11. **Explanation quality and appeals.** Unclear or untranslated decision notices may increase appeals and rework, which the process then records as procedural burden on the affected group.
12. **Priority rules.** If queues are first-in-first-out, support-step cases may wait behind high-volume general cases; if priority is set by risk, D cases may be expedited through fraud review but delayed elsewhere.
13. **Data-capture artifacts.** Language need may be recorded inconsistently with language preference, and missing-document requests may be logged as multiple events when they are one request.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in documentation_completeness.** Completeness may be lower for applicants who face language barriers, have disabilities affecting paperwork, or use in-person channels with less support. Controlling for it may absorb the very disparity under study, understating procedural burden. It should be treated as a possible mediator, not as a neutral confounder.

**Historical bias in risk_score_band.** The risk score may be trained on historical fraud findings, which depend on past enforcement. Group D's 51% high-risk share could reflect real risk, but could also reflect over-policing or scoring features correlated with group membership (e.g., prior-case history). Risk routing is legitimate by policy, but the score's neutrality is unverified.

**Prior_case_count as a proxy.** A high prior-case count may reflect long-term system contact, possibly including earlier unfair outcomes. Using it as a control assumes it is an independent legitimate factor.

**Missing applicant needs.** Accommodation requests are recorded only when made. Applicants who need accommodation but do not request it (because of process burden, stigma, or lack of awareness) are invisible. Language need is recorded separately from preference, so mismatches are unobserved.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity and not per caseworker. Queue waits may reflect staff absence, shift patterns, or caseload that the log does not capture. Caseworker identity may be partly anonymized or reassigned, limiting individual-level analysis.

**Single-timestamp events.** With one timestamp per event, "waiting time" is the gap between events and conflates queue time, service time, and idle time. Waiting is not directly observed.

**Appeal quality.** The log records whether an appeal occurred, not its grounds, quality, or outcome reasons. Appeals may reflect unclear decisions, errors, or dissatisfaction with support. Appeal outcomes (overturned or upheld) are not described.

**Explanation and dignity.** Case outcome does not capture the clarity of decision explanations, translation quality, or the manner of communication. These are central to procedural fairness and are missing.

**Withdrawal and attrition.** Applicants who abandon cases during long waits may not appear as complete cases. If attrition differs by group, outcome comparisons are biased toward those who persisted.

**Intersectional overlap.** Groups overlap (e.g., some Group B applicants may also have accommodation needs or high risk scores). Group-level summaries may hide heterogeneity within groups.

**Small cells.** Support-staff pools are small, so individual-caseworker effects may be large and unstable.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.**
- Benefit: directly reduces the 3.8-day and 4.7-day queue waits; addresses the root of the timeliness gap.
- Side effects: recruitment lead times, cost, and risk of burnout among a small specialist pool if demand surges. Hiring bilingual staff may draw from other needed roles. Capacity added to queues without shared triage may not reduce variability.

**Service-level targets for support steps.**
- Benefit: makes rights-preserving steps visible and managed; sets accountability for timeliness.
- Side effects: targets can create pressure to close steps quickly, reducing quality or depth of language and accommodation support. Targets should be paired with quality measures (e.g., whether the need was actually met) and with clock-stopping rules for applicant-caused delays.

**Routing redesign to reduce handovers.**
- Options: assign a single case owner who has language or accommodation competence; or create pooled "support-enabled" generalist teams; or embed specialists in regional teams.
- Benefit: fewer handovers reduce delay and information loss.
- Side effects: training cost; generalists may be less expert in complex accommodation or language needs; specialist knowledge may be diluted; new ownership structures may create their own accountability gaps.

**Decision-explanation improvements.**
- Options: plain-language and translated notices; explicit statement of which documents are missing and why; reasons for supervisor escalation.
- Benefit: may reduce appeals and rework (B, C, D), and improve dignity and perceived fairness.
- Side effects: longer drafting time (may increase cycle time at the decision step); may reveal previously opaque risk-based decisions, which could trigger more appeals before the review is fixed; translation quality must be verified.

**Monitoring adjusted disparities.**
- Options: continuous dashboards of matched and stratified gaps in waiting time, excess steps, appeals, and rework; alerts when gaps exceed thresholds.
- Benefit: detects drift and verifies that mitigations work.
- Side effects: metric fixation (Goodhart's law), gaming of recorded routing; noisy small-sample alerts; privacy considerations for group-level reporting; risk of treating adjusted gaps as settled facts.

**Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.**
- Options: test whether risk scores predict fraud findings equally across groups (calibration by group); audit whether incompleteness is driven by language or channel; re-examine which inputs feed the score.
- Benefit: addresses potential bias at the source; could identify whether the Fraud Review routing (D) and document requests (B) are fair.
- Side effects: removing or reweighting risk features may reduce fraud detection and increase false negatives; changing documentation standards may increase workload or reduce integrity checks. Any change needs legal and policy review.

**Other options (smaller, targeted):**
- Proactive outreach in the applicant's preferred language before issuing missing-document requests (reduces the +9.5pp burden), with a clear checklist.
- Separate accommodation reviewers from supervisor-review roles (addresses independence and bottleneck; side effect: requires additional supervisory capacity).
- Explicit escalation criteria for supervisor review, so unexplained escalation is reduced; side effect: may reduce caseworker discretion in complex cases where escalation is appropriate.
- Priority or triage rules for support-step cases, so that they are not delayed behind general work; side effect: may lengthen waits for other applicants and needs to be monitored for equity across groups.

**What not to do.** Removing or shortening language support or accommodation steps to improve cycle time would reduce rights protection and shift burden to applicants. The fix is capacity and routing, not elimination.

```python
              "disability_accommodation_requested", "prior_case_count",
              "region", "case_outcome", "appeal_flag"]

    lambda s: (s.max() - s.min()).total_seconds() / 86400)
case_df["log_cycle"] = np.log1p(case_df["cycle_days"])  # import numpy as np
for name, act in {"fraud": "Fraud Review", "access": "Accommodation Review",

                  "reqdoc": "Request Missing Documents", "appeal": "Appeal Intake"}.items():
    case_df[f"has_{name}"] = acts.map(lambda s, a=act: a in s)

logs = {g: pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "applicant_group", [g],
                                               level="case", retain=True)
        for g in GROUPS}

# Variant shares within complexity strata (e.g., same routing requirements)
case_df["stratum"] = (case_df["req_fraud"].astype(str) + "|" +

case_df["variant"] = var_sig
within = (case_df.groupby(["stratum", "applicant_group", "variant"]).size()

perf = {}

# Filter by performance (tail focus)

# ---- 6. Procedural constraints (log skeleton / DECLARE) -----------------

# Compare the group coefficients: a large change indicates mediation by documentation

mm = smf.mixedlm(spec_base, case_df, groups=case_df["caseworker_id"]).fit()

# Tail: quantile regression for the 90th percentile of cycle time
from statsmodels.regression.quantile_regression import QuantReg

# Appeal and approval (logit with cluster-robust SEs)
m_appeal = smf.logit("has_appeal ~ C(applicant_group) + C(region) + C(channel)"

```

**Notes on the plan.**
- Model 1 (total effect) excludes Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents as controls, since they are plausibly on the causal path from group to cycle time. Model 2 can include them to estimate direct effects, but the gap should be interpreted as "conditional on procedure" rather than "total."
- Matching or coarsened exact matching (e.g., the `cem` package) can replace regression for the within-stratum path comparison.
- Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition can be implemented on the fitted model coefficients (group vs reference) to split the cycle-time gap into complexity, excess steps, and residual.
- Sensitivity runs: drop risk_score_band, drop documentation_completeness, drop prior_case_count, and report how group coefficients move.
- Subgroup runs: repeat the rule-rate, variant, and cycle-time analysis by region and channel.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence is **indeterminate as to overall procedural fairness, with specific, credible procedural concerns that warrant targeted investigation.**

What the evidence supports:
- After adjustment, Groups B and C still face higher probabilities of extra steps (missing-document requests, Supervisor Review) and longer cycle times (+2.6 and +4.1 days). Group D's adjusted gaps are small on approval and cycle time, but its appeal burden remains elevated (+5.7pp).
- Some of the observed burden is directly linked to rights-preserving steps (language support, accommodation review) that face capacity constraints without timeliness targets. This is a fairness concern regardless of intent.
- Supervisor Review for B and C is not covered by stated legitimate routing rules, and it is not clear it is needed. This is the most actionable unexplained difference.
- Resource concentration (bilingual staff, accommodation specialists with overlapping review roles) is a structural concern that the outcome data cannot fully explain.

What the evidence does not support:
- It does not establish discrimination. The data show disparities and unexplained burdens, not intent or differential standards applied by individual decision-makers.
- It does not establish that the risk score is biased, or that the Fraud Review routing is unjustified. Those are questions requiring audit.
- It does not show whether the appeal and rework differences reflect decision quality, explanation quality, or error.
- Adjusted estimates depend on covariates that may be mediators or biased, so the magnitude of "unexplained" disparity is uncertain. Small support-staff pools also limit precision.

A calibrated conclusion: the process is not demonstrably fair on procedural dimensions for Groups B and C, given burdens that persist after measured complexity and that trace to capacity and routing practices. Whether it is unfair in the sense of discriminatory treatment cannot be determined from this data. The next steps are the audits and within-stratum analyses described above, plus capacity and timeliness fixes for support steps that do not reduce the support itself.
