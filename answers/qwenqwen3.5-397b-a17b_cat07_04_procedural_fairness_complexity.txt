# 1. Fairness dimensions

To assess procedural fairness beyond raw outcome parity, we must evaluate how the process structure itself impacts different groups. Five critical dimensions emerge from the data:

1.  **Outcome Parity:** Raw approval rates differ significantly (Group A: 78% vs. Group D: 58%). However, parity alone is insufficient evidence of unfairness because case mix (risk, documentation) differs. Fairness requires that equally eligible applicants receive equal outcomes, not that all groups have identical approval rates regardless of eligibility.
2.  **Adjusted Outcome Disparity:** After controlling for case mix (risk, documentation, channel), Group B still faces a -5.5 percentage point approval disadvantage and Group D a +5.7 percentage point higher appeal probability. This residual disparity suggests that factors beyond documented complexity influence decisions or that the controls (e.g., risk scores) themselves may be biased.
3.  **Procedural Burden:** Fairness involves the effort required from the applicant. Group B and C cases traverse variants with more steps (V4, V5) and higher probabilities of "Request Missing Documents." While some requests are legitimate, a +9.5 percentage point higher probability of document requests for Group B suggests a higher administrative burden placed on minority-language applicants, potentially due to communication barriers rather than actual missing data.
4.  **Timeliness:** Group B and C experience significantly longer cycle times (+2.6 and +4.1 days adjusted). Crucially, waiting times are concentrated at rights-preserving steps (Language Support: +3.8d wait; Accommodation Review: +4.7d wait). When steps designed to ensure access become bottlenecks, the process penalizes applicants for exercising their rights to support.
5.  **Resource-Allocation Fairness:** Group B and C cases are disproportionately assigned to specialized, scarce resources (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists). This creates a "specialist queue" effect where accessing necessary support inherently slows down the process compared to Group A, who are spread across 52 general caseworkers. This structural imbalance creates inequity in service speed based on protected characteristics.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish between legitimate complexity, discrimination, and structural bias, the following analysis strategy is required:

*   **Matching or Stratification Variables:** Perform propensity score matching or stratification on `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `household_size_band`, and `region`. This creates comparable cohorts where Group A and Group B/C/D cases have identical observed complexity profiles.
*   **Path/Variant Comparison:** Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to isolate specific process paths (e.g., V1 vs. V4). Compare approval rates and cycle times *within* the same variant across groups. If Group B takes longer than Group A on Variant V1 (the standard path), this indicates disparity not explained by process routing.
*   **Resource-Assignment Comparison:** Analyze `org:resource` utilization rates. Compare the caseload per resource for bilingual specialists vs. general caseworkers. High utilization on specialized resources indicates a capacity mismatch rather than individual bias.
*   **Waiting-Time Decomposition:** Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to separate "working time" (activity duration) from "waiting time" (queue time). Compare waiting times specifically at `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` against general steps like `Eligibility Assessment`.
*   **Sensitivity Analysis for Possibly Biased Covariates:** Treat `risk_score_band` as a sensitive variable. Run the adjustment model twice: once including risk scores and once excluding them. If disparities widen significantly when risk scores are excluded, the risk model may be masking discrimination by encoding historical bias.
*   **Subgroup Checks by Region and Channel:** Stratify by `region` and `channel`. The data notes backlog in two regions affecting Group B. Disaggregating ensures that local resource shortages are not generalized as system-wide discrimination, nor vice versa.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The variant and waiting-time data reveal specific procedural disparities:

*   **Variant Distribution:** Group A predominantly follows V1 (42%, 5.8 days), the straight-through path. Group B is shifted toward V4 (24%, 19.2 days), which includes `Language Support Contact`. Group C is shifted toward V5 (31%, 21.6 days), including `Accommodation Review`.
    *   *Justification:* These variants are triggered by legitimate needs (language, disability).
    *   *Concern:* The cycle time penalty for V4/V5 is extreme (3-4x longer than V1). This suggests the process treats support needs as exceptions rather than integrated standard work.
*   **Waiting-Time Bottlenecks:** The wait between `Language Support Contact`  `Eligibility Assessment` is 3.8 days for Group B. The wait between `Accommodation Review`  `Eligibility Assessment` is 4.7 days for Group C.
    *   *Concern:* These waits exceed the `Submit`  `Identity Verification` wait for all groups. Rights-preserving steps are the slowest parts of the process, creating a "support penalty."
*   **Handovers and Resource Concentration:** Group B cases experience more handovers (general  bilingual  general). Group C cases hand over to accommodation specialists who also perform supervisor reviews.
    *   *Concern:* High handover rates increase error risk and loss of context. The dual-role of accommodation specialists (review + supervision) creates a conflict of interest or bottleneck where the same scarce resource controls both access and oversight.
*   **Risk Routing:** Group D is routed to `Fraud Review` (V3) 28% of the time vs. 9% for Group A.
    *   *Justification:* Driven by `risk_score_band`.
    *   *Concern:* If the risk score is biased (see Section 5), this routing systematically subjects Group D to more intrusive scrutiny (Fraud Review) based on potentially flawed inputs.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Four mechanisms could generate the observed patterns:

1.  **Resource Scarcity (Legitimate but Unfair):** There are only 7 bilingual caseworkers for a significant volume of Group B cases. This is not necessarily discriminatory intent, but it creates a structural inequity where accessing a legal right (language support) results in slower service due to under-resourcing.
2.  **Process Design Rigidity (Concerning):** The process treats support steps as sequential gates (e.g., Language Support *then* Eligibility) rather than parallel activities. This design choice inherently adds latency for protected groups without adding value to the decision quality.
3.  **Bias in Risk Scoring (Concerning):** The `risk_score_band` drives routing to Fraud Review (V3). If historical data used to train the risk model reflects past biased enforcement against Group D, the model perpetuates this by routing them to slower, more scrutinizing paths, creating a feedback loop of disparity.
4.  **Documentation Barriers (Mixed):** Group B has higher incomplete documentation (46%). This could be due to genuine missing info, or it could be due to forms not being accessible in minority languages or caseworkers marking docs as "incomplete" due to communication difficulties rather than actual absence. This triggers Variant V2 (Request Docs), adding cycle time.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The current event log attributes are insufficient for a definitive fairness ruling:

*   **Measurement Bias in Risk Scores:** `risk_score_band` is treated as an objective control variable, but it may encode historical bias. Without knowing the model features, we cannot trust it as a neutral comparator for complexity.
*   **Documentation Completeness Ambiguity:** `documentation_completeness` is binary. It does not capture *why* docs were incomplete. For Group B, this may reflect language barriers in form instructions rather than applicant failure.
*   **Unobserved Resource Workload:** We see `org:resource` IDs but only daily granularity. We do not know if the 7 bilingual caseworkers were on leave, overloaded, or working part-time during the log period.
*   **Appeal Quality:** `appeal_flag` indicates an appeal occurred, but not the outcome. A high appeal rate for Group D could mean unjust denials (fairness issue) or merely that Group D is more likely to appeal regardless of merit (behavioral difference).
*   **Missing Applicant Needs:** There is no attribute for "applicant satisfaction" or "dignity of treatment." A case can be approved quickly but involve rude or confusing interactions, which is a fairness failure not captured in the log.
*   **Historical Bias:** The log reflects the process as it *was*, not as it *should be*. If past policies discouraged Group B from applying unless certain of approval, the current log reflects a selected population, skewing outcome analysis.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process appears **procedurally unequal** rather than definitively discriminatory, though the distinction carries significant risk. There is no evidence of intent to deny benefits based on group membership; indeed, support steps (Language, Accommodation) are explicitly designed to protect rights. However, the **structure of these protections creates a secondary penalty**. Applicants requiring rights-preserving support (Groups B and C) face systematically longer waits, more handovers, and higher administrative burden than those who do not (Group A), even after controlling for case complexity.

Furthermore, the reliance on `risk_score_band` for routing Group D introduces uncertainty; if the risk model encodes historical bias, the process automates disparity under the guise of legitimacy. The residual adjusted disparities in approval rates (-5.5% for Group B) and appeal rates (+5.7% for Group D) cannot be fully explained by the observed case mix. Therefore, while the process is not overtly unfair in outcome intent, it is **unfair in operational design**, as it fails to resource and integrate support steps efficiently, effectively punishing applicants for their protected needs. Immediate action is required to decouple support access from service delays.
