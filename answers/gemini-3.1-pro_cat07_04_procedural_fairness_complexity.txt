# 1. Fairness dimensions

When assessing procedural fairness in this public-benefits case-management process, we must look beyond simple approval rates and evaluate multiple dimensions of the applicant experience:

*   **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates show significant disparities, with Group A at 78% and Groups B, C, and D at 63%, 69%, and 58%, respectively.
*   **Adjusted outcome disparity:** Even after controlling for case-mix variables (risk, completeness, channel, etc.), negative disparities persist for Groups B (-5.5 pp), C (-2.2 pp), and D (-3.1 pp), indicating that case complexity alone does not explain the gap.
*   **Procedural burden:** Groups B and C face more complex paths (Variants 4 and 5) with additional handovers between general caseworkers and specialized staff. Group D faces higher rates of Fraud Review (Variant 3).
*   **Timeliness:** Cycle times are drastically longer for minority and accommodated groups. Group A's median cycle time is 8.1 days, while Group B (13.7 days) and Group C (15.9 days) experience severe delays.
*   **Consistency of routing:** While routing applicants to specialized queues (Language Support, Accommodation, Fraud Review) is policy-mandated, the consistency and efficiency of how these routes are executed vary wildly, heavily penalizing those who need support.
*   **Quality/accessibility of support:** Rights-preserving steps exist but are structurally inaccessible due to severe under-resourcing, turning supportive policies into procedural hurdles.
*   **Appeal/rework burden:** Groups B, C, and D experience significantly higher appeal rates (13%, 10%, 16%) and reopen/rework rates compared to Group A (6% and 11%), suggesting lower initial decision quality or poor communication of outcomes.
*   **Resource-allocation fairness:** There is a stark imbalance in resource allocation. Group A is served by 52 caseworkers, while Group B relies heavily on just 7 bilingual caseworkers, and Group C relies on 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom are split with supervisor duties).

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish between true discrimination, legitimate routing based on complexity, and uncertainty, the analysis strategy should include:

*   **Matching or stratification variables:** Stratify cases using exact matching or propensity score matching on `channel`, `household_size_band`, `region`, and `month`. 
*   **Path/variant comparison:** Compare cycle times and outcomes *within* specific process variants. For example, compare Group A and Group B cases that both follow Variant 2 (Submit → Verify → Completeness → Request Docs → Eligibility → Decision → Notify) to isolate delays not caused by language support routing.
*   **Resource-assignment comparison:** Analyze the workload distribution. Compare the active caseloads of the 52 general caseworkers against the 7 bilingual and 5 accommodation specialists to quantify the resource bottleneck.
*   **Waiting-time decomposition:** Use event timestamps to separate active processing time from queue waiting time. Specifically, isolate the wait times immediately preceding "Language Support Contact" and "Accommodation Review."
*   **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates:** Run the adjusted models twice: once including `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`, and once excluding them. If the disparity widens significantly when excluded, it suggests these variables may be absorbing systemic biases.
*   **Subgroup checks by region and channel:** Analyze outcomes specifically in the two regions with known backlogs to see if regional resource constraints are driving the overall disparities for Groups B and C. Compare in-person vs. online applications within Group B to isolate channel effects.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The provided data reveals critical procedural disparities that blend legitimate complexity with concerning inequities:

*   **Concerning Resource Bottlenecks:** The most glaring disparity is the wait time for specialized support. The 3.8-day wait for Language Support (Group B) and 4.7-day wait for Accommodation Review (Group C) are highly concerning. While the *steps* are justified by case needs, the *delays* are driven by under-resourcing (7 bilingual staff, 5 accommodation staff).
*   **Concerning Handover Penalties:** In two regions, Group B and C applicants experience "ping-pong" handovers (general → specialist → general/supervisor). This structural inefficiency adds queue time at each transition and is a concerning procedural penalty for requesting rights-preserving support.
*   **Justified but Monitored Routing:** Group D's heavy routing through Variant 3 (Fraud Review) adds cycle time (18.4 days). If the `risk_score_band` is highly accurate and neutral, this routing is justified by the complexity of risk assessment.
*   **Justified Documentation Delays:** Group B has a 46% incomplete documentation share, leading to high usage of Variant 2 and a 1.6-day wait for Request Missing Documents. This delay is procedurally justified by the missing information, though the root cause of the missing info remains a concern.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms could generate these observed patterns:

1.  **Legitimate - Specialized Need:** Group C's high rate of disability accommodation requests inherently requires a more thorough, specialized review (Variant 5), legitimately adding an extra step to the process compared to Group A.
2.  **Concerning - Resource Starvation:** The agency has failed to allocate staffing proportional to case mix. The severe lack of bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists creates artificial bottlenecks, penalizing Groups B and C with excessive queue times.
3.  **Concerning - Biased Risk Scoring:** The `risk_score_band` may heavily weight `prior_case_count`. If Group D historically faced systemic barriers or over-policing in benefits applications, the risk score will unfairly route them to Fraud Review, depressing their approval rates and inflating cycle times.
4.  **Concerning - Language-Driven Documentation Failures:** Group B's high rate of incomplete documentation (46%) may not be applicant error, but rather a failure of the agency to provide application materials or instructions in minority languages, artificially inflating their procedural burden.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event log attributes are insufficient to capture the full picture of fairness:

*   **Measurement bias:** `documentation_completeness` is treated as an objective fact, but it may actually measure the accessibility of the application form.
*   **Missing applicant needs:** The data lacks granularity on the *type* of disability accommodation or the *specific* minority language required, making it impossible to assess if the assigned specialist was actually equipped to help.
*   **Unobserved resource workload:** Because resource workload is only observed at a daily granularity, we cannot see intra-day queue buildup, task-switching penalties, or the actual hours spent per case. A specialist might handle 5 cases a day that take 1.5 hours each, while a generalist handles 15 cases taking 15 minutes each.
*   **Appeal quality and outcomes:** The `appeal_flag` shows that appeals happen, but not their success rate. If Group B's appeals are overwhelmingly successful, it indicates the initial denials were flawed.
*   **Historical bias in risk scores:** `risk_score_band` is a black box. If it is trained on historical data where certain demographics were disproportionately investigated for fraud, the score itself is a mechanism of structural bias, not a neutral control variable.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

*   **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review:**
    *   *Side effects:* Increases payroll costs; requires specialized hiring and training; may lead to temporary underutilization of these staff if case mix shifts.
*   **Service-level targets for support steps (e.g., max 24h wait for Language Support):**
    *   *Side effects:* Could incentivize specialists to rush reviews, potentially degrading the quality of the accommodation or translation provided to meet the metric.
*   **Routing redesign to reduce handovers (end-to-end specialized caseworkers):**
    *   *Side effects:* Requires cross-training bilingual/accommodation staff to handle general eligibility and fraud review; reduces the flexibility of the general caseworker pool.
*   **Decision-explanation improvements (especially for minority languages):**
    *   *Side effects:* Increases the active service time for the "Decision Draft" and "Applicant Notification" steps, potentially slightly increasing overall cycle time in exchange for lower appeal rates.
*   **Monitoring adjusted disparities:**
    *   *Side effects:* Requires dedicated data science resources; risks creating a compliance mindset where managers "game" the metrics rather than improving the actual applicant experience.
*   **Auditing `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`:**
    *   *Side effects:* May require temporarily suspending automated risk routing, leading to a short-term increase in manual review burden and longer cycle times for all groups.
