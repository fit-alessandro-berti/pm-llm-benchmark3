## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience** in this setting means preserving safe, traceable service under disruption while absorbing additional cost, delay, or constrained capacity. It is not synonymous with maximizing cases shipped. A process can be resilient even if cycle time doubles, provided essential controls remain intact and the organization knows what deferred work and risks remain.

**Concealed noncompliance** occurs when throughput or service is maintained by removing, weakening, or postponing a required control, while standard performance measures do not expose the resulting gap. The deviation may be visible in the event log, as C and G were, but its compliance consequence can still be concealed in downstream rework, cancellations, audit queues, or missing evidence.

### Clearest resilience signals

- **B, manual authorization**, is the strongest example: it accepted scarce compliance capacity and slower processing in order to retain the authorization gate.
- **Targeted I**, deferral of genuinely low-urgency bulk work, protected hospital capacity and reduced load on the improvised system. The concept is resilient; the observed implementation was too broad because pharmacy service deteriorated.
- **J, backlog prioritization**, is resilient when criticality and product-risk rules are explicit, measurable, and fair. The 30% backlog cancellation ratio shows why governance matters.
- **K and L** are restorative resilience mechanisms. They reduced the risk of permanently inconsistent inventory and missing evidence, but their workload and limitations must remain visible.
- **D, E, F, and H** can be short-term resilience measures only when they are time-bounded, contemporaneous, risk-tiered, and independently verified.

### Clear risky shortcuts

- **C, skip system authorization**, traded a mandatory gate for throughput. It was especially unsafe for new customers, changed product scope, recall-sensitive ambient products, and any case where standing authorization had not been revalidated.
- **G, dispatch without scan**, traded custody and traceability for speed. For controlled products, it was not merely weak evidence; it disrupted the chain needed to establish authorized handling and secure transfer.
- **F’s sampled release approach** and **H’s phone-only POD** also became shortcuts when applied to cold-chain or controlled orders, because the evidence did not satisfy the product’s risk needs.

### Ambiguous adaptations

- **A** is resilient when it changes sequence only; it becomes concealed noncompliance when it changes control requirements.
- **D** was reasonable in the first hours of T2 but unsafe once the snapshot became stale.
- **E** maintained physical flow but moved traceability work downstream; reconstructed scans should never be presented as equivalent to contemporaneous scans.
- **I and J** are resilient only under transparent urgency and fairness rules.
- **K and L** restore state but do not convert a noncompliant original event into a compliant one. After-the-fact remediation is not equivalent to real-time compliance.

Risk appetite should determine how much service to protect, whether to defer, and where to concentrate scarce staff. It should **not** determine whether mandatory authorization, temperature, security, release, or custody controls may disappear. If equivalent controls cannot be maintained, the acceptable response is to narrow or stop the affected shipment scope—not to redefine compliance as optional.

## 4. Recovery process model

### A. Operating states and explicit triggers

The redesign should separate operational continuity from compliance closure.

| State | Entry trigger | Required behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal** | Core systems and controls operating within agreed thresholds. | Standard process with automated reconciliation and routine exception management. |
| **Emergency Service Mode** | Any of: WMS, authorization, inventory, scanning, or dispatch control unavailable for more than **30 minutes**; expected restoration more than **2 hours**; inventory data older than **30 minutes**; inventory mismatch above **0.5%** or any high-risk SKU discrepancy; mandatory temperature/security event-capture failure; projected backlog consuming more than one shift of protected capacity; or credible data-integrity compromise. | Use the controlled manual process below. Scope shipment by risk. No unauthorized, unreconciled, or unevidenced high-risk dispatch. |
| **Recovery and Reconciliation Mode** | Core systems return but manual cases, inventory differences, or evidence gaps remain. | Dual-run only where necessary; reconcile inventory and cases; continue priority fulfillment; keep remediation visible and separately staffed. |
| **Compliance Closed** | All emergency cases are reconciled, required evidence is complete or deviations formally recorded, high-risk audit items are closed, and no critical discrepancy remains. | Only then may the incident be declared fully closed, even if cycle-time metrics had already recovered. |

Recommended initial exit criteria from Emergency Service Mode are three consecutive operating shifts or 24 hours of stable service, at least **99.9%** authorization/inventory synchronization, full mandatory-control capture for high-risk orders, no unresolved critical stock discrepancy, and every manual case entered in the reconciliation register. These thresholds should be validated against volume, criticality, and applicable requirements.

### B. Intake and two-axis triage

Every order should enter a single offline incident register with a unique case ID, even if fulfillment is deferred.

Use two separate classifications:

1. **Fulfillment priority**
   - Priority 1: verified hospital or pharmacy order with urgent clinical need.
   - Priority 2: standard customer with a near-term promised date or short-dated stock.
   - Priority 3: other standard orders.
   - Priority 4: low-urgency wholesale/bulk work without downstream clinical urgency.
   - Pharmacy orders are not automatically noncritical; customer-provided urgency and product need should be requested.

2. **Control tier**
   - Tier 1: controlled substances.
   - Tier 2: cold-chain and recall-sensitive products.
   - Tier 3: ordinary ambient products.
   - Controlled, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive orders may be sequenced later, but their controls are never relaxed.

Deferral should be based on a transparent score covering clinical impact, promise-date risk, age, expiry, and downstream customer impact. A minimum capacity floor should protect pharmacy-standard service. Deferral or cancellation must be recorded with a reason and communicated; silent backlog cancellation should not be an allowable recovery action.

### C. Minimum controls by customer and product risk

The product column overrides customer urgency. Every accepted order still receives a per-order quality/release check; sampling can supplement but not replace that check.

| Customer class | Ambient | Cold-chain | Controlled-substance | Recall-sensitive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hospital-critical** | Expedite only after valid authorization, fresh inventory confirmation, case-level traceability, full release, dispatch evidence, and proof of delivery. | Hospital controls plus calibrated continuous temperature capture, excursion review and disposition, linked temperature record, and custody confirmation. | Hospital controls plus independent dual verification at pick/release/dispatch, lot/serial capture, tamper-evident custody, secure handoff, and signed authorized-recipient receipt. The ordinary fast lane cannot waive these controls. | Hospital controls plus lot/serial/expiry capture, recall-status clearance, segregation, and documented release before dispatch. |
| **Pharmacy-standard** | Same authorization, release, traceability, and POD controls as normal operation; it may be sequenced later but not exempt. | Same cold-chain controls as Hospital-critical; service priority does not reduce temperature obligations. | Same controlled-substance controls as Hospital-critical, including dual control and secure custody. | Same lot/serial, recall-clearance, segregation, and release controls. |
| **Wholesale-bulk** | May be deferred first when there is no urgent downstream need; once accepted, the complete ambient-control chain applies. | Same cold-chain controls; defer rather than release without temperature evidence. | Same controlled-substance controls; product risk does not justify a bulk-order exception. | Same recall and lot/serial controls; no “known customer” shortcut. |

Hard stops should apply when:

- Customer or product authorization cannot be validated.
- Inventory ownership, lot, serial, or expiry cannot be established.
- A required cold-chain temperature record is absent.
- Controlled dual control or secure custody cannot be performed.
- Recall status cannot be checked for a recall-sensitive item.
- Dispatch and custody evidence cannot be captured equivalently.

The appropriate response to a hard stop is to hold or defer the affected order or segment, not to ship it with a reconstructed record.

### D. Manual evidence capture

Each manual transaction should create or update a unique case record containing:

- Order/customer/product identifiers and authorized scope.
- Event time, operator, approver, reason code, and incident reference.
- The version and issue time of every offline authorization or inventory source used.
- Sequential paper-form and barcode identifiers where electronic scanning is unavailable.
- Lot/serial/expiry, quantity, location, and inventory transaction reference.
- Quality/release checklist, temperature logger file, excursion decision, and controlled dual-control signatures.
- Dispatch handoff, carrier/courier identity, recipient identity and authority, custody times, and proof of delivery.
- A reconciliation status and exception owner.

Required controls should include:

- **Authorization:** signed offline source with expiry; no indefinite reliance on customer history; callback to an independently obtained authorized number.
- **Inventory:** snapshot age visibly displayed and automatically invalidated; frequent refresh from the transaction journal; physical verification for controlled and cold-chain lots.
- **Picking and packing:** unique case ID on paper labels; independent pack check; no overwriting of the original event chronology.
- **Reconstruction:** reconstructed scans or events clearly marked as reconstructed, linked to original evidence, time-stamped at reconstruction, and independently reviewed.
- **Controlled products:** sequential paper custody record, dual signatures, secure carrier handoff, and signed authorized-recipient receipt.
- **Cold-chain:** logger identifier, calibration status, continuous data file, excursion assessment, and quarantine outcome.
- **POD:** authenticated e-signature or signed paper receipt; phone confirmation alone for low-risk ambient orders only, with identity verification and later independent confirmation.
- **Invoicing:** a spreadsheet may be a temporary exception register, but it must be version-controlled, access-controlled, reconciled to source cases, and visible in the incident workload. Final invoice close should not conceal an unreconciled shipment.

### E. Backlog prioritization

The backlog should be a controlled work queue, not an overflow folder. Each case should show:

- Clinical urgency and promised delivery time.
- Current age and projected SLA breach.
- Product control tier and short-dated/expiry risk.
- Complete, partial, or missing evidence state.
- Inventory availability and safety-hold status.
- Customer communications and cancellation history.

Rules should include:

- Re-score the queue at least each operating shift, not only once at recovery.
- Age cases so an old low-risk order is not indefinitely starved.
- Reserve capacity to prevent pharmacy-standard starvation while protecting hospital-critical demand.
- Prioritize controlled/cold-chain/recall reconciliation even if the customer segment is lower priority.
- Cancel only for unsafe stock, unfulfillable demand after alternatives, or confirmed customer withdrawal. Record the reason and make cancellation visible.
- Report deferral and cancellation rates by customer class, product mix, and urgency so a 61% pharmacy fill rate or a 30% backlog cancellation ratio cannot be hidden inside aggregate throughput.

### F. Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

At system cutover, freeze new manual reservations and execute the following flow:

`Manual case  match order and authorization  match inventory/lot/serial  match pick/pack  match quality/release and temperature/security evidence  match dispatch/POD  match invoice  independent QA decision`

Decision branches:

1. **Complete and matched:** close the case after normal reconciliation.
2. **Corroborated but reconstructed:** attach the reconstruction with its true event time, source evidence, reason, and reviewer approval. Do not represent it as an original contemporaneous event.
3. **Irrecoverable gap:** quarantine the affected inventory or receivable, record a deviation/nonconformance, open corrective action, and prevent final invoice close where required evidence is absent.

Reconciliation order should be:

1. Controlled, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, and unauthorized cases.
2. Hospital-critical cases.
3. Other pharmacy-standard cases.
4. Wholesale-bulk cases.
5. Lower-risk documentation cleanup.

A proposed remediation SLA is **24 hours for high-risk cases, 48 hours for hospital cases, five business days for all remaining cases**, and no item over ten business days without executive exception. This SLA should appear in workload and management reporting; the T4 queue cannot again become an invisible tail.

Operational recovery and compliance closure should be reported separately:

- **Operationally recovered:** systems and normal flow restored.
- **Inventory reconciled:** physical, manual, and system stock agree or discrepancies are contained.
- **Compliance closed:** high-risk evidence and deviations are resolved or formally accepted with corrective action.

| **Rework and correction** | First-pass yield; pre-dispatch mismatch rate; inventory reconciliation discrepancy rate; percentage of cases requiring repack before release. | Rework/correction rate; repack and recheck frequency; correction effort per case; audit findings caused by recurring defects. | Return toward the **3.5%** baseline, with a separate target for recurring-cause reduction. T2’s 12.5% and T3’s 9.0% show sustained recovery risk. |
| **Fairness across customer groups** | Capacity allocation and deferral rates by customer class, adjusted for product mix and urgency; backlog age gaps; cancellation rate ratios; notification completion. | Fill, on-time service, cancellation, complaint, credit, and lost-sales differences across comparable customer groups. | After adjusting for risk and urgency, no customer group should have unexplained service or cancellation gaps above an agreed threshold—for example, five percentage points or 1.25 times the portfolio rate without documented cause. |
| **Audit remediation and hidden workload** | Open queue age; daily burn-down; number of cases approaching SLA; high-risk cases unresolved; staff hours still needed to close the tail. | Time to remediate, percent closed within SLA, open items beyond SLA, repeat findings, and proportion of invoice closes entering remediation. | Zero open controlled, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, or authorization cases beyond SLA. The T4 rate of approximately **8.3%** of invoice-close instances entering audit remediation should be visible and declining, not hidden in an “invoices closed” metric. |

For future incidents, management should review leading indicators every operating shift. A trigger should be based on control and service risk, not on a generic outage label—for example, two consecutive readings of rising unmatched inventory, any missing high-risk evidence, or a forecast breach of protected capacity.

## 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Issue a risk-tiered emergency operating standard with explicit hard stops.** Prohibit C and G for controlled, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive products; require equivalent authorization, release, custody, and POD evidence; define when deferral is mandatory. | **Low to medium; 2–4 weeks.** | Immediately closes the highest-risk deviations seen in T2 and prevents service priority from becoming control bypass. | Throughput may fall initially, and managers may pressure teams to override hard stops. Independent compliance oversight is needed. |
| **2** | **Create one offline incident case and evidence register.** Combine a signed authorization cache, short-lived inventory snapshots, manual pick/pack forms, temperature files, controlled custody records, POD attestations, and a reason-coded reconciliation status. | **Medium to high; 4–8 weeks.** | Converts improvisation into auditable compensating controls and reduces downstream reconstruction. It addresses B, D, E, F, and H without pretending they are normal-system substitutes. | Paper can be lost or altered; offline sources can be stale; data-entry burden can be significant. Dual verification and expiry controls are required. |
| **3** | **Institutionalize reconciliation and a separate compliance-closure queue.** Use risk-ranked case matching, explicit SLAs, independent QA, visible workload reporting, and final-close gates for high-risk cases. | **Medium; 4–6 weeks.** | Prevents the T4 pattern in which normal processing appeared recovered while approximately 8.3% of invoice closes entered remediation. It makes nonrecoverable gaps visible instead of backfilling them. | Some contemporaneous gaps can never be repaired. A large historical backlog may still require temporary surge capacity. |
| **4** | **Adopt an objective backlog, service, and fairness policy.** Require explicit urgency scoring, pharmacy capacity floors, age-based reprioritization, customer notification, and reason-coded cancellation. | **Low; 2–4 weeks.** | Protects hospital-critical service without repeating the 61% pharmacy fill rate or 30% backlog cancellation ratio. It also makes service trade-offs auditable. | More capacity may need to be reserved for lower-volume or higher-cost service, reducing maximum throughput. |
| **5** | **Improve resilient capture at the point of work:** offline-capable barcode devices, electronic signatures, local temperature-logger download, controlled custody scanning, and electronic POD. | **High; 3–9 months.** | Reduces dependence on morning snapshots and reconstructed scans, particularly for controlled and cold-chain products. It addresses the root cause rather than only the workaround. | Capital cost, device management, cybersecurity, connectivity failure, and training burden remain. Redundant channels should be tested. |
| **6** | **Build a live incident control tower.** Combine system health, offline-source age, authorization coverage, control completeness, scan/POD capture, backlog age, customer fairness, and audit burn-down into one dashboard with named thresholds and escalation. | **Medium; 6–12 weeks.** | Detects degradation before the T2-level cycle-time and exception spikes. It also exposes hidden work rather than reporting only cases shipped. | Dashboard data can be as incomplete as the source systems, and excessive alerts can create fatigue. Alert ownership and data-quality checks are essential. |
| **7** | **Exercise the model under realistic constraints.** Run tabletop and technical exercises involving scanner loss, stale inventory, temperature-logger failure, cyber uncertainty, pharmacy demand, and audit staff shortages. Include manual-capture and reconciliation work. | **Medium; quarterly.** | Tests whether authority, evidence, capacity, and IT recovery work together. It would likely have exposed the missing quality-release, stale-snapshot, and no-scan paths before T2. | Exercises can become procedural compliance rather than behavioral readiness. Include failed-evidence scenarios and after-action remediation of the playbook. |

A practical sequencing is to implement recommendations 1, 4, and 6 immediately; 2 and 3 within 60–90 days; and 5 and 7 as part of the longer-term cyber-resilience and site-continuity program.

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower cycle times, additional compliance staffing, a visible backlog, and selective deferral when these costs preserve authorization, temperature, security, release, custody, and proof controls. It should not accept skipped authorization, unscanned controlled-product dispatch, missing temperature or dual-control evidence, stale inventory decisions, silent pharmacy harm, or the description of an incident as “recovered” while high-risk reconciliation and audit work remains open. The recommended operating scope would change if evidence demonstrates 100% contemporaneous authorization and mandatory control completion, reliable inventory reconciliation, acceptable custody/POD records, and closure of high-risk audit cases within SLA. If equivalent evidence cannot be produced, the recommendation shifts from broad shipment to a narrower, risk-tiered scope with deferral of the affected products—not relaxation of the controls.
