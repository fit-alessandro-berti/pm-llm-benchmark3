# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Observation, definitions.** Time origin is `Submit` for every case. The horizon is $\tau=72$ hours from `Submit`. `duration_or_age_h` is interpreted exactly as supplied: time to the observed terminal outcome for `resolved`, `rejected`, `withdrawn`, and `transferred` rows, and age at the single frozen observation cutoff for `open` rows. No start dates, cutoff date, or within-case event timestamps are supplied, so elapsed duration is observed but event times for intermediate activities are not.

Terminal internal dispositions are `Resolve`, `Reject`, and `Withdraw`. `Transfer` is a terminal observation in the internal system but not a known terminal outcome for the case: after transfer the internal system stops observing it. `Open` means no disposition had occurred by cutoff.

Let $T_R$ be time to resolution, $T_D$ time to first internal disposition of any type, and $J$ the type of first disposition. With competing dispositions, $T_R$ is not observed after another disposition intervenes.

At least four different quantities can legitimately be called “probability of breaching the SLA.” They answer different decisions and must not be interchanged.

**Estimand E1 — Not resolved by 72 hours, $1-F_R(72)$.** 
$$E1 = P(\text{no Resolve by }72)=1-P(T_R\le 72,\ J=\text{Resolve}).$$
This is one minus the cumulative incidence of resolution by 72 hours in the presence of competing dispositions. It matches a customer- or regulator-facing question: “By the deadline, what proportion of submitted cases have actually been resolved?” A case withdrawn at 27 hours counts in E1 as not resolved by 72, because operational success did not occur by the deadline. A transferred case is ambiguous for the eventual external outcome, but under an internal-accountability version it also counts as not internally resolved by 72; see Section 2 for alternatives.

**Estimand E2 — Cumulative incidence of late resolution, $P(72<T_R,\ J=\text{Resolve})$.** 
This is the probability that a case is eventually resolved, but only after 72 hours. It matches recovery and rework questions: “How much late success are we producing after the deadline has already been missed?” It is not the same as E1. E1 includes cases never resolved internally, whereas E2 isolates late resolution. With a frozen extract, E2 is only partially identified because open cases may resolve later and transferred cases may resolve externally.

**Estimand E3 — No terminal disposition by 72 hours, $P(T_D>72)$.** 
This is the probability of still having no internal terminal disposition at 72 hours, regardless of whether the case later resolves, withdraws, rejects, or transfers. It matches backlog and workload questions: “How many cases are still live and undisposed at the deadline?” This is an event-free survival probability for the composite endpoint “any internal disposition,” with `Transfer` normally included as an internal disposition because the internal queue no longer owns the case, unless the governance question is about final external closure.

**Estimand E4 — Cause-specific cumulative incidences by 72 hours, $F_k(72)=P(T_D\le72,J=k)$.** 
Here $k\in\{\text{Resolve, Reject, Withdraw, Transfer}\}$. This matches diagnostic governance: is non-resolution by 72 driven mainly by late handling, early rejection, withdrawal, transfer, or cases still open? E1 equals $1-F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)$ when all disposition types are retained as competing events and transfer is treated as a competing internal disposition.

**Recommended primary estimand.** For SLA accountability, E1 with transfer treated as a competing internal disposition is the most defensible primary estimand, reported jointly with $F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)$, $F_{\text{Reject}}(72)$, $F_{\text{Withdraw}}(72)$, $F_{\text{Transfer}}(72)$, and E3. This avoids pretending that withdrawal is success and avoids deleting unresolved cases. E2 and the transfer-external-outcome variants belong in sensitivity analysis because the extract is frozen and post-transfer outcomes are unknown.

**Already-observed breaches versus censoring.** An open case aged over 72 hours, such as S09 at 92 hours, S11 at 81, S25 at 79, and S26 at 104, is not “censored before the SLA question is answered” for E1 or E3 at 72 hours. Its 72-hour status is known adversely. It is right-censored only for events after its age. By contrast, an open case aged under 72 hours, such as S31 at 23 hours, S15 at 35, S10 at 47, S29 at 51, and S24 at 67, is right-censored before the horizon: its 72-hour status is unknown. This distinction is central and is destroyed if open cases are dropped or if all open cases are labelled simply “breached” or simply “censored” without reference to age.

**Crude identified bounds from the supplied table.** These are observations/bounds, not model estimates. Nine cases are known resolved by 72 hours. Eighteen cases are known not resolved by 72: four resolved after 72, four open beyond 72, and ten with a non-resolution internal disposition at or before 72. Five open cases are censored before 72. Therefore, without assumptions about those five,
$$P(\text{not resolved by }72)\in[18/32,\ 23/32]=[56.3\%,\ 71.9\%].$$
Similarly, eight cases are known to have had no disposition by 72, namely the four late resolutions plus the four open beyond 72, and five are unknown, so
$$P(\text{no disposition by }72)\in[8/32,\ 13/32]=[25.0\%,\ 40.6\%].$$
Aalen-Johansen estimation in Section 4 uses the timing and risk sets rather than only these bounds, but the bounds show why a single percentage without a censoring statement is fragile.

**Last-event gap.** `last_event_gap_h` is 0 for all terminal rows as supplied and positive for open rows. It should not be converted into a censoring time different from `duration_or_age_h`. It is a marker of inactivity at cutoff: S26 has gap 72, S11 gap 55, S25 gap 48, S09 gap 31, S10 gap 19. A long gap may indicate a stalled case, a case effectively abandoned but not administratively closed, delayed recording, or a process waiting on an external party. That makes ordinary non-informative censoring for open cases questionable, especially for post-cutoff resolution.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Naive completed-resolved analysis.** If only the 13 resolved cases are analysed, four were resolved after 72 hours: S03 at 76, S13 at 110, S20 at 74, and S28 at 88. The naive late proportion among resolved cases is $4/13=30.8\%$. That number answers “among cases observed to resolve, what fraction resolved late?” It does not answer “what fraction of submitted cases were resolved by 72?” The denominator excludes nine open cases, four withdrawals, three rejections, and three transfers. It conditions on a future outcome, successful resolution, which is precisely the event whose timing is under study.

**Survivor and selection bias.** Resolved-only analysis selects cases that survived competing exits and remained observable until resolution. S06 withdrawn at 27, S05 rejected at 9, and S08 transferred at 68 are removed even though each is informative that internal resolution by 72 did not occur under the primary estimand. Long-running unresolved cases are also removed: S09 is open at 92 and S26 open at 104. The resolved-only sample therefore over-represents pathways that reach `Resolve` and under-represents stalled, escalated-transfer, withdrawn, and rejected pathways.

**Competing risks are not censoring.** Treating withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as ordinary censoring in a Kaplan-Meier analysis of resolution estimates a hypothetical net probability of resolution in a world where those exits are prevented but the resolution hazard otherwise stays the same. That is not the observed SLA. For example, censoring S06 at withdrawal at 27 hours would keep alive, in a hypothetical sense, a case that in reality can no longer be internally resolved after withdrawal. In cumulative-incidence terms, withdrawal removes probability mass from future resolution. The same applies to S23 rejected at 11 hours and, under the primary internal estimand, S21 transferred at 26 hours.

**Informative censoring through openness and inactivity.** Open cases are right-censored at cutoff, but censoring may be related to prognosis. S26 is open at 104 hours with a 72-hour last-event gap; S11 is open at 81 with a 55-hour gap; S25 is open at 79 with a 48-hour gap. These look different from S31, open at 23 hours with gap 3, or S15, open at 35 with gap 2. If long-gap open cases are effectively stalled, then censoring them as if they had the same future resolution hazard as recently active cases overstates future resolution. Conversely, if a long gap reflects waiting for a scheduled external response that often succeeds, censoring could understate it. The extract cannot decide that; it motivates sensitivity analysis rather than a silent independence assumption.

Transfer censoring is also plausibly informative. All three transferred cases, S08, S21, and S32, contain `Escalate` immediately before `Transfer` in the supplied prefixes: `...Escalate>Transfer`. Transfer is therefore associated with an escalation pathway, not a random administrative disappearance. Censoring transfers without discussion would remove a selected, escalated subgroup from the resolution risk set.

**Open below versus above 72 hours.** Open cases below 72, S31 23h, S15 35h, S10 47h, S29 51h, S24 67h, are censored before the SLA status is known. Open cases above 72, S09 92h, S11 81h, S25 79h, S26 104h, are known not to have been resolved or otherwise internally disposed by 72. Lumping both groups as “open, exclude” deletes known breaches. Lumping both as “breached” fabricates breaches for S31 and S15, which may still resolve by 72. The correct handling is time-based: status at 72 is known for the second group and censored for the first.

**Cohort imbalance and non-random rollout.** Cohort A has 15 cases and cohort B has 17. Known resolved by 72: A has S01, S02, S04, S07, four cases; B has S16, S17, S18, S19, S27, five cases. Known not resolved by 72: A has nine cases; B has nine cases. Censored open before 72: A has two, S10 and S15; B has three, S24, S29, S31. Thus the crude bounds for not resolved by 72 are A $[9/15,11/15]=[60.0\%,73.3\%]$ and B $[9/17,12/17]=[52.9\%,70.6\%]$. They overlap widely. A resolved-only comparison gives late resolution A $2/6=33.3\%$ versus B $2/7=28.6\%$, a small descriptive difference that is highly selected and not evidence of a policy effect.

Cohort B began after automated-screening and escalation-policy change, but assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete. The prefixes themselves show incomplete or heterogeneous uptake: many B cases begin `Submit>AutoCheck>...`, for example S16–S19, S21, S22, S24, S25, S27–S29, S31, S32, while others such as S20, S23, S26, S30 do not show early `AutoCheck`. Cohort is therefore an intention-to-treat-like label at best, not proof that the automated pathway was received. Priority mix also matters because P1 is highest priority and escalation is plausibly tied to priority and complexity. Any cohort contrast is associational unless the assignment mechanism is modelled credibly, which this small extract cannot support on its own.

**Escalation as a post-baseline selector.** Ten cases have `escalated=yes`: S03, S04, S08, S10, S11, S13, S18, S21, S25, S28, S29, S32 — in fact twelve by the table: A S03, S04, S08, S10, S11, S13 and B S18, S21, S25, S28, S29, S32. Escalated cases include fast resolutions such as S18 at 12 hours and S04 at 29, late resolution S13 at 110 and S28 at 88, transfers S08, S21, S32, and long open cases S11 and S25. Comparing “escalated versus not” from baseline would mix cases that escalate immediately with cases that only become escalated after surviving and remaining active long enough to reach escalation. That is a time-dependent exposure problem, addressed in Section 5.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Design stance for these data.** The sample is tiny: 32 cases, 13 resolutions, 10 non-transfer/non-resolution dispositions including transfer, and nine open cases. Event times for intermediate activities are absent. The primary analysis should therefore be descriptive and estimand-led: nonparametric cumulative incidence with explicit risk sets, bounds, and sensitivity analyses. Multivariable survival regression with several covariates would be overfit and should not be the primary evidence.

| Method | What it estimates | Fit for these data | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier for composite any disposition | $P(T_D>t)$, probability of no internal disposition yet; transfers included as disposition in primary version | Appropriate for E3 if open cases are censored at age and censoring is treated as conditionally independent given strata. Simple and uses all cases. | Secondary/supporting: backlog survival. Also useful as KM for a clearly hypothetical endpoint only if labelled as such. |
| Kaplan-Meier for resolution only, censoring competing events | Net resolution probability in a hypothetical world without withdrawal/rejection/transfer | Not the observed SLA. It will generally exceed cumulative incidence of resolution because competing events are removed rather than absorbing. | Do not use as primary. May appear only as a clearly labelled contrast showing why competing-risks methods are needed. |
| Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence functions | $F_k(t)$ for Resolve, Reject, Withdraw, Transfer with right-censoring of open cases | Directly matches E1 and E4. Handles the fact that S06 cannot later contribute internal resolution after withdrawal. Nonparametric and transparent for $n=32$. | **Primary.** Report stacked CIFs and values at 24, 48, 72, and possibly 96 hours, with risk-set counts. No smoothing over-interpretation. |
| Cause-specific hazards | Instantaneous rate of each disposition among cases still undisposed | Useful diagnostically: is low resolution incidence due to low resolution hazard, high withdrawal hazard, or transfer hazard? With few events, estimate only coarse/stratified or smoothed descriptive hazards, if at all. | Secondary diagnostic. Avoid cause-specific Cox with many covariates. |
| Subdistribution hazards, Fine-Gray | Hazard associated directly with cumulative incidence of one cause, keeping competing-event cases in a weighted risk set | Targets CIF covariate effects, but with 13 resolutions and multiple causes it is unstable here. Interpretation is subtle for operational audiences. | Not primary. At most exploratory for one pre-specified contrast, with wide intervals and no causal language. |
| Restricted mean time | For composite disposition, restricted mean time without disposition up to $\tau$; for resolution, restricted mean time lost or time spent undisposed | Useful operational translation: average days within first 72 hours spent undisposed. Less dependent on a single 72h binary cut. Still needs censoring assumptions. | Secondary operational summary, e.g. mean undisposed time in first 72h derived from KM for any disposition; and mean time before resolution can be framed via CIF integrals if defined carefully. |
| Landmarking | Among cases still open at a landmark such as 24 or 48 hours, predict subsequent resolution/disposition using information available by the landmark | Fits alerting: at 48 hours, which still-open cases are unlikely to resolve by 72? Avoids using future escalation after the landmark. Needs enough cases at risk; here landmarks leave very small denominators, so use descriptively. | Secondary for dynamic prediction design; not confirmatory with current $n$. |
| Multi-state model | Transition hazards among states such as Submitted/Screening, Review, Escalated, Rework, Absorbing dispositions | Best process representation conceptually, but exact transition times are unavailable. Only terminal duration and activity order are known, so transition-specific hazards and sojourn times are not identifiable from the extract alone. | Use as representation and data-collection blueprint now; fit only when event timestamps are supplied. |

**Primary approach.** Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence for first internal disposition with four competing types, `Transfer` as a competing internal disposition in the main analysis, open cases right-censored at age. Report $F_R(72)$, $1-F_R(72)$ for E1, $F_{\text{Reject}}(72)$, $F_{\text{Withdraw}}(72)$, $F_{\text{Transfer}}(72)$, and the undisposed probability $1-\sum_k F_k(72)$ for E3. Stratify descriptively by cohort and priority, but present counts and risk sets alongside percentages because strata become very small: for example P1 has S04, S08, S11, S18, S21, S25, S29, S32, only eight cases.

**Secondary approaches.** Kaplan-Meier for any internal disposition to summarize backlog persistence; restricted mean undisposed time over 0–72 hours; landmark descriptions at 24 and 48 hours among still-open cases; cause-specific hazard discussion in coarse time bands if risk sets permit. Multi-state modelling is specified as the target analysis once timestamps exist.

**Uncertainty.** With $n=32$, confidence intervals will be wide. Use nonparametric intervals where available and exact or bootstrap intervals cautiously; bootstrap resampling must resample cases and preserve the estimand encoding. More important than a single interval is reporting the partial-identification bounds for the five cases censored before 72 and the transfer sensitivity range. Statistical significance testing for cohort effect is not recommended as the decision standard here; the design is non-randomized and the sample is small.

**Assumptions to state with estimates.** Assumption A1: open-case censoring at cutoff is independent of future disposition given observed stratum, for model-based CIF/KM point estimates. This is needed because five cases are censored before 72 and all open cases are censored after their observed age. It is questionable for long-gap cases, hence bounds and sensitivity analyses. Assumption A2: in the primary internal estimand, transfer ends internal resolution opportunity. This is an accountability definition, not a claim that external resolution never happens. Assumption A3: cohort comparisons are descriptive unless assignment and compliance are further modelled.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Baseline features, known at or immediately after Submit.** Cohort A/B; priority P1/P2/P3; initial route only if it is determined at intake before outcomes evolve. The first post-Submit activity may be close to baseline but is still post-origin: A cases uniformly show `Submit>Screen>...`, whereas B cases may show `Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>...` or `Submit>Screen>AutoCheck>...`. Because cohort B rollout compliance was incomplete, presence of `AutoCheck` is better interpreted as received-pathway information than as the policy assignment itself. Label this **Assumption A4**: `AutoCheck` in the prefix is a proxy for exposure to automated screening, but timing and whether it was mandatory, optional, or triggered by case features are unknown.

**Time-varying or stage features, valid only with time-aware use.** Current stage derived from the latest observed activity for open cases and from the path history for terminal cases; whether the case has entered `Review`; number of `Review` occurrences so far; whether `Rework` has occurred so far; whether `Escalate` has occurred so far; time since last event, `last_event_gap_h`, for open cases at cutoff; path pattern such as repeated `Review`, `Review>Rework>Review`, or `Escalate>Transfer`. These are legitimate in a landmark or multi-state model when measured only up to the prediction time. They are leakage if used as fixed baseline predictors of the final outcome.

Examples from prefixes: repeated review appears in S03 `Review>Escalate>Review`, S07 `Review>Rework>Review`, S13 `Review>Rework>Review>Escalate`. Rework is flagged by `rework_count=1` for S07, S13, S27. Escalation followed by transfer characterizes S08, S21, S32. S27 has `Review>Rework>Resolve`, showing rework does not mechanically imply breach: duration 39. S13 has rework plus escalation and resolves at 110, showing the combination can also mark a long path. The feature is prognostic/process-descriptive, not causal by itself.

**Post-treatment and leakage risks.** `Escalate` occurs after case start. A case must remain undisposed long enough to be escalated. Treating eventual escalation as a baseline covariate creates immortal-time bias: before escalation occurs, the case is guaranteed not to have had a terminal event that would have precluded escalation, yet a naive baseline model credits that survival time to the “escalated” group from time zero. It can also be reverse causation: cases escalate because they are stuck, urgent, or complex. Similarly, total `rework_count` over the whole case is known only at the end; using final rework count to predict breach leaks the future. `last_event_gap_h` for terminal cases is 0 by construction in the extract and must not be used as a predictor of terminal status; for open cases it is a cutoff-time inactivity measure only.

Safe designs: baseline model uses cohort, priority, and intake features only; landmark model at 24/48 hours uses prefix up to that landmark, requiring event timestamps to construct honestly; time-dependent covariate model uses escalation as a time-varying indicator switching from 0 to 1 at the escalation time, again requiring timestamps; multi-state model treats escalation as a state entered at a known transition time.

**Proposed state-transition representation in text.**

State S0 Submitted: every case starts at Submit.
S0 -> ScreeningOrAutoCheck: observed first activities are Screen or AutoCheck, order varies especially in cohort B.
ScreeningOrAutoCheck -> Review: most cases enter Review; some resolve or exit before Review.
ScreeningOrAutoCheck -> Resolve: fast path examples S01, S16, S18 after escalation for S18.
ScreeningOrAutoCheck -> Reject: S05, S23 `Submit>Screen>Reject`.
ScreeningOrAutoCheck -> Withdraw: S12 `Submit>Screen>Withdraw`.
Review -> Rework -> Review: loop observed in S07 and S13; S27 has Review>Rework>Resolve without a second Review shown.
Review -> Escalate: escalation from review or screening; S03, S08, S13, S21, S28, S32 patterns.
Escalate -> Review: S03 and S29 show return to review/activity after escalation.
Escalate -> Resolve: S04, S18.
Escalate -> Transfer: S08, S21, S32, a distinct absorbing internal exit.
Review -> Resolve, Review -> Reject, Review -> Withdraw, Review -> Transfer in principle, though observed transfers here follow Escalate.
Absorbing internal states: Resolved, Rejected, Withdrawn, Transferred-out.
Open is not a process state; it is censoring of a case currently in a transient state at cutoff, with last observed prefix giving its current stage.

Transition hazards of scientific interest are stage-specific: screening-to-review progression, review-to-resolution versus review-to-rework, escalation-to-resolution versus escalation-to-transfer, and inactivity or administrative censoring while in review. The present extract identifies the order of states and the total case duration, but not the time spent in each state; therefore stage hazards cannot be estimated credibly yet.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

**Sensitivity S1 — Transfer treatment.** Compare T1 transfer as competing internal disposition; T2 transfer censored at transfer; T3 transfer counted as external success at transfer; T4 transfer counted as not resolved by 72. Only T1 and T4 give the same E1 classification for S08, S21, S32 at 72 because all transfers occur before 72 and none is an observed internal Resolve. T3 is the optimistic bound for eventual resolution. T2 changes risk sets and estimates a hypothetical retained-case process. **Conclusion that could reverse:** cohort or priority ranking by “resolution” can reverse if transferred escalated cases are counted as successes, because transfers are concentrated in escalated paths and include two B cases, S21 and S32, versus one A case, S08.

**Sensitivity S2 — Withdrawal and rejection as competing events versus censoring.** Primary: competing events. Alternative: censor at withdrawal/rejection to approximate a resolution-only process if those exits were eliminated. This alternative will increase the estimated resolution probability because cases such as S05 rejected at 9 and S12 withdrawn at 15 no longer absorb probability mass. **Conclusion that could reverse:** a pathway with many early withdrawals could look acceptable under censoring, because its failures vanish from the denominator experience, while under cumulative incidence it clearly produces low resolution by 72. The censoring version should never be headline SLA performance.

**Sensitivity S3 — Informative censoring of open cases.** Compare: standard independent censoring; worst-case for resolution where all five open cases censored before 72 fail to resolve by 72; best-case where all five resolve by 72; and a gap-informed scenario where long-gap cases are treated as less likely to resolve. The identified bound for E1 overall is $[56.3\%,71.9\%]$ before modelling. Cohort bounds overlap: A $[60.0\%,73.3\%]$, B $[52.9\%,70.6\%]$. **Conclusion that could reverse:** “B has lower SLA breach than A” can reverse within the bounds if A’s two early-censored cases resolve by 72 while B’s three early-censored cases do not. A point estimate that hides this is overconfident.

**Sensitivity S4 — Cohort policy interpretation.** Compare cohort as assigned-policy label, an intention-to-treat-style descriptive contrast; versus received-pathway grouping by observed early `AutoCheck`; versus excluding or separately flagging B cases without early AutoCheck as possible non-compliance. The received-pathway contrast is vulnerable to selection because AutoCheck may be triggered by case type, and to post-baseline conditioning. **Conclusion that could reverse:** B’s advantage, if any, may reflect case mix, priority, incomplete rollout, or censoring rather than the automated-screening/escalation policy. No causal policy claim is supported by cohort alone.

**Sensitivity S5 — Priority and case-mix adjustment, descriptively standardized.** Compare crude cohort CIFs with priority-stratified CIFs and, only as an illustrative standardization, cohort CIFs reweighted to a common priority distribution. With eight P1, fifteen P2? Count check: P1 cases are S04, S08, S11, S18, S21, S25, S29, S32, eight cases; P2 cases are S01, S03, S06, S07, S10, S13, S15, S16, S19, S22, S24, S27, S28, S31, fourteen cases; P3 cases are S02, S05, S09, S12, S14, S17, S20, S23, S26, S30, ten cases. Strata are thin. **Conclusion that could reverse:** if B has more high-priority fast-tracked cases or fewer stalled P3 cases, crude cohort differences may shrink or change sign after stratification; conversely stratification can reveal that an apparent overall benefit is confined to one priority band.

S20 resolves at 74, just after the deadline: it is a breach for E1 at 72 but nearly a success operationally, whereas S13 at 110 and S26 open at 104 are deeper tail cases. **Conclusion that could reverse:** ranking pathways by median time can differ from ranking by 72h tail risk; governance should focus explicitly on the tail if the SLA is a deadline commitment.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**From survival output to alerts.** Use E3 and stage information for live-case alerting: a case still undisposed has rising operational urgency as it approaches 72 hours, especially if it is in `Review`, has repeated review/rework, has escalated without resolution, or shows a growing inactivity gap. Because exact event timestamps are absent in this extract, the current data can support only age-based alert rules, for example review at 48 hours if still open, mandatory senior review at 60 hours, and breach review after 72. With timestamps, landmark models could estimate, among cases still open at 48 hours, the probability of no resolution by 72 conditional on stage and activity up to 48. That is a prediction for triage, not proof that an intervention on a feature will cause resolution.

**Workload actions.** Convert $F_k(72)$ and undisposed survival into staffing language: expected number of cases still undisposed at 72 per 100 submissions; expected number resolved late; expected transfers needing external follow-up; and restricted mean undisposed time in the first 72 hours as an approximate workload burden measure. Escalation-to-transfer concentration in S08, S21, S32 suggests a specific operational question: after escalation, is transfer the intended pathway for certain case types, and if so, who owns external follow-up and customer communication? Withdrawal incidence should trigger a different action, such as clearer intake communication, while rejection incidence points to screening criteria and upstream submission quality.

**SLA governance.** Report a small dashboard rather than one breach percentage: resolved by 72, resolved after 72 so far, rejected by 72, withdrawn by 72, transferred by 72, still undisposed at 72, and censored before 72 with unknown 72h status. That accounting identity prevents gaming. If transfer counted as success, teams could improve the metric by transferring difficult escalated cases. If withdrawals were censored, teams could improve apparent resolution by encouraging withdrawal. The primary governance metric should therefore keep competing dispositions visible.

**Causal caution.** A high predicted breach risk for escalated cases does not mean escalation causes delay; escalation may be a response to complexity or impending delay. A low breach rate after `AutoCheck` does not prove automation caused improvement; cohort B differs by period, compliance, and possibly case mix. Risk scores should trigger help, review, or additional capacity, not automatic blame or automatic deprioritization. Any intervention claim needs a design such as randomized rollout, stepped-wedge implementation, or credible adjustment for assignment and compliance.

**Calibration and fairness checks.** By priority and cohort, check calibration-in-the-large and calibration by risk band: among cases predicted to have, say, 60% probability of non-resolution by 72, does the cumulative-incidence estimate in that group approach 60%? With only 32 cases, calibration can only be coarse; report counts, not just percentages. Check false-alert burden and missed-breach rates by priority: P1 cases may be escalated earlier by design, so equal breach rates across priorities are not automatically the fairness target. The relevant checks are whether similarly situated cases receive similar alerts and whether a priority group is systematically under-alerted relative to its observed undisposed-at-72 rate. Also check cohort fairness after the policy change: if B improves average time but worsens the P3 tail, aggregate success can hide tail harm. S26, a B P3 case open at 104 with a 72-hour gap, and S20, a B P3 case resolved at 74, illustrate why tail-by-priority reporting matters.

**Decision thresholds.** Tie thresholds to costs: the cost of a false alert is reviewer time; the cost of a missed breach is SLA penalty, customer harm, or rework. A 60-hour mandatory review may be rational even with modest predictive accuracy if breach cost is high. Do not set thresholds from the same 32 cases and then present them as validated; treat them as pilot rules requiring prospective monitoring.

H = 72.0
df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(CSV))
df["t"] = df["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)

STATUS_TO_CAUSE = {
    "resolved": "Resolve",
    "rejected": "Reject",
    "withdrawn": "Withdraw",
    "transferred": "Transfer",
}

def encode(df, transfer_scenario="competing"):
    """
    Returns KM/AJ-ready columns:
      time, event_any (1=any internal disposition, 0=censored),
      cause in {Resolve, Reject, Withdraw, Transfer, ExternalResolveAssumed, None}
    Estimands encoded downstream:
      E1 = 1 - CIF_Resolve(72); E3 = KM survival for any disposition at 72.
    Transfer scenarios:
      competing: Transfer is a competing internal disposition.
      censored: Transfer right-censors the internal process at transfer time.
      success_bound: Transfer counted as resolution at transfer time (optimistic bound only).
    """
    out = df.copy()
    out["cause"] = out["observed_status"].map(STATUS_TO_CAUSE).fillna("None")
    out["event_any"] = (out["observed_status"] != "open").astype(int)
    out["time"] = out["t"]
    if transfer_scenario == "censored":
        m = out["observed_status"] == "transferred"
        out.loc[m, "event_any"] = 0
        out.loc[m, "cause"] = "None"
    elif transfer_scenario == "success_bound":
        m = out["observed_status"] == "transferred"
        out.loc[m, "cause"] = "ExternalResolveAssumed"
    elif transfer_scenario != "competing":
        raise ValueError(transfer_scenario)
    return out

def aalen_johansen(data, horizon=H):
    """Nonparametric CIF for first disposition; open/transfer-censored rows are censored."""
    d = data.sort_values("time").copy()
    times = sorted(d.loc[d["event_any"] == 1, "time"].unique())
    causes = ["Resolve", "Reject", "Withdraw", "Transfer", "ExternalResolveAssumed"]
    cif = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    surv_any = 1.0
    rows = []
    for tt in times:
        at_risk = (d["time"] >= tt).sum()
        if at_risk == 0:
            continue
        events = d[(d["time"] == tt) & (d["event_any"] == 1)]
        d_all = len(events)
        surv_before = surv_any
        for c in causes:
            dc = (events["cause"] == c).sum()
            if dc:
                cif[c] += surv_before * dc / at_risk
        surv_any *= (1.0 - d_all / at_risk)
        rows.append({"time": tt, "at_risk": at_risk, "S_no_disposition": surv_any, **{f"CIF_{k}": v for k, v in cif.items()}})
        if tt >= horizon and False:
            pass
    # Value at horizon: CIF is a step function using events <= horizon; survival just after horizon events.
    at_h = [r for r in rows if r["time"] <= horizon]
    if not at_h:
        return {"S_no_disposition_72": 1.0, **{f"CIF_{c}_72": 0.0 for c in causes}}, pd.DataFrame(rows)
    last = at_h[-1]
    res = {"S_no_disposition_72": last["S_no_disposition"]}
    for c in causes:
        res[f"CIF_{c}_72"] = last[f"CIF_{c}"]
    return res, pd.DataFrame(rows)

def binary_bounds(data, horizon=H):
    """Partial-identification bounds for P(not resolved by 72), primary internal definition."""
    known_success = ((data["observed_status"] == "resolved") & (data["t"] <= horizon)).sum()
    late_resolved = ((data["observed_status"] == "resolved") & (data["t"] > horizon)).sum()
    open_over = ((data["observed_status"] == "open") & (data["t"] > horizon)).sum()
    nonresolve_disp = (data["observed_status"].isin(["rejected", "withdrawn", "transferred"])).sum()
    cens_before = ((data["observed_status"] == "open") & (data["t"] <= horizon)).sum()
    known_fail = late_resolved + open_over + nonresolve_disp
    n = len(data)
    return {
        "n": n, "known_resolved_by_72": int(known_success),
        "known_not_resolved_by_72": int(known_fail),
        "censored_before_72": int(cens_before),
        "P_not_resolved_72_lower": known_fail / n,
        "P_not_resolved_72_upper": (known_fail + cens_before) / n,
    }

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Highest priority: exact event timestamps and calendar dates.** The extract gives only total duration or age and activity order. Without timestamps for `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Rework`, `Escalate`, and terminal events, stage durations, time-varying escalation, landmark predictors at 24/48 hours, and transition hazards are not identifiable. Also needed are case start dates and the exact cutoff date to check calendar-period effects, differential follow-up by cohort, and whether cohort B simply had less time to accrue late resolutions.

**External outcomes after transfer.** For S08, S21, and S32 and future transferred cases, record external receipt time, external resolution/withdrawal/rejection, and whether the 72-hour clock continues contractually after transfer. This determines whether transfer is a competing internal disposition, a handoff in a longer end-to-end process, or a successful discharge.

**Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance.** Record why a case entered cohort B, eligibility for automated screening, whether `AutoCheck` was assigned, offered, completed, overridden, or failed, timestamps of the policy change by team/queue, and any manual triage rules. Without this, cohort is not a treatment and `AutoCheck` presence is a selected received-pathway marker. Priority assignment rules and any changes to them are also needed, because P1 may be both a severity marker and a fast-track intervention.

**Case-mix and workload covariates.** Collect case type/complexity, channel, customer-initiated withdrawal reason, rejection reason, staffing/queue length at arrival and over time, handler/team, waiting on customer versus waiting internally, and service-calendar effects such as weekends. Time-varying workload is especially important: a case open during a backlog surge has a different censoring and resolution environment. These variables are needed to judge whether censoring is plausibly conditionally independent and whether priority/cohort contrasts are confounded.

**Event-gap validity and administrative closure rules.** Define `last_event_gap_h` precisely: last system event, last human touch, or last customer-visible event? Determine whether long-gap open cases are stalled, awaiting external input, completed but not closed, or data-quality artefacts. S26 with gap 72 and S11 with gap 55 should be auditable examples. Also record whether open cases can be auto-closed and whether terminal rows can be reopened; the supplied `last_event_gap_h=0` for terminal rows suggests the gap is defined relative to the terminal event and is not a predictor.

**Sample size and follow-up maturity.** Thirty-two cases cannot support rich cause-specific regression, stable landmark prediction, fairness assessment across cohort-by-priority cells, or credible causal policy evaluation. A stronger extract would include more cohorts/periods, longer follow-up so fewer cases are censored before 72, and pre-specified estimands before unblinding outcome comparisons. Until then, the defensible deliverable is the accounting dashboard, Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidences with risk sets, partial-identification bounds, transfer and censoring sensitivity analyses, and a timestamped multi-state design for the next extract.
