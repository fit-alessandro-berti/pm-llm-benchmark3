# 1. Fairness dimensions

**Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, Group D 58%. These are fairness signals, but they are not evidence of discrimination by themselves. The groups differ materially in risk, documentation, channel, language-support needs, accommodation requests, and prior-case count.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** The preliminary adjusted model still shows lower approval probability for Groups B, C, and D: -5.5, -2.2, and -3.1 percentage points. That means case mix explains some but not all of the raw gap. These remaining differences warrant investigation, especially for Group B. They still do not prove discriminatory treatment, because omitted needs or biased covariates may remain.

**Procedural burden.** Groups B and C more often traverse support-heavy paths: Group B has high use of Language Support Contact and Request Missing Documents; Group C has high use of Accommodation Review and Supervisor Review. These steps may be legitimate or rights-preserving, but they impose extra contacts, handovers, waiting, and possible stigma. A fair process should minimize burden even when the step is justified.

**Timeliness.** Adjusted cycle-time differences remain: +2.6 days for Group B, +4.1 days for Group C, +0.9 days for Group D. Raw 90th-percentile times are also much higher for B, C, and D. The waiting-time table identifies plausible bottlenecks: Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment is 3.8 days for Group B; Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment is 4.7 days for Group C. These are central procedural-fairness concerns.

**Consistency of routing.** Some routing appears consistent with policy: Group D is routed to Fraud Review more often and has higher measured risk; Group B is routed to Language Support Contact more often and has higher language-support need; Group C is routed to Accommodation Review more often and has more accommodation requests. However, adjusted Supervisor Review is still higher for B (+8.0 pp) and C (+5.5 pp), suggesting added scrutiny not fully explained by the modelled covariates.

**Quality/accessibility of support.** The log shows that support steps occur, but not whether they are timely, understandable, respectful, or effective. With only 7 bilingual caseworkers and a small accommodation-specialist pool, the presence of a support step may not mean meaningful access. Group B’s multilingual applicants and Group C’s accommodation requesters may receive slower and more fragmented procedures even though those steps exist to preserve rights.

**Appeal/rework burden.** Raw appeal rates are higher for Groups B, C, and D, and adjusted appeal probability remains higher for B (+4.4 pp) and D (+5.7 pp). Reopen/rework rates are roughly twice Group A’s rate for B, C, and D. Appeals may reflect substantively worse decisions, poor explanations, inaccessible communication, or complex cases. The `appeal_flag` alone cannot distinguish these.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A cases are spread across 52 workers, while Group B is concentrated among 7 bilingual workers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and Group C among 5 accommodation specialists. This concentration may be necessary for expertise, but it creates bottleneck risk and unequal exposure to overload. Resource allocation is therefore a procedural fairness issue, not merely an operational detail.

**What can and cannot be inferred.** The evidence supports an inference of differential procedural experience, especially for Groups B and C. It does not by itself establish discriminatory intent, substantively incorrect decisions, or causal discrimination. Raw approval gaps should not be equated with discrimination, but adjusted timeliness, handover, support-queue, and resource-concentration disparities should not be dismissed as legitimate merely because case mix differs.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A sound analysis should separate four things:

- **Disparity:** any observed group difference, whether justified or not.
- **Legitimate routing:** different treatment tied to policy-relevant need, such as high risk, documented accommodation request, or language-support need.
- **Discrimination or inequitable treatment:** an adverse procedural difference that remains after accounting for legitimate need and is linked to group membership or a biased proxy.
- **Uncertainty:** differences that could be explained by omitted needs, measurement error, limited overlap, or sensitive modelling choices.

**Matching or stratification variables.** Compare cases that are as similar as possible on:

- `risk_score_band`
- `documentation_completeness`
- `channel`
- `household_size_band`
- `prior_case_count`
- `region`
- month or policy period
- `language_preference`
- `disability_accommodation_requested`

The first seven match the preliminary model; the last two are needed because language support and accommodation review are policy-relevant routing needs. However, risk and documentation should also be treated cautiously because they may be outcomes of earlier inequities. Use exact matching where feasible, then coarsened exact matching or propensity/optimal matching with overlap checks. Report groups with poor overlap separately rather than forcing comparisons.

**Path/variant comparison.** Use `pm4py.get_variants` to identify the full variant distribution, not only the top paths. Compare outcomes and times:

- within the same variant, e.g., Group A vs Group B cases both in V2;
- across legitimate need-equivalent paths, e.g., applicants with language-support need who follow V4 versus similar applicants whose support is delayed or fragmented;
- by edge-level transitions, e.g., Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents, Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment, Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment, Supervisor Review  Decision Draft;
- by loop counts, e.g., repeat Request Missing Documents, repeat Supervisor Review, appeal loops.

Path fixed effects or sequence-level matching can help distinguish “different cases follow different paths” from “similar cases are delayed more often on the same path.”

**Resource-assignment comparison.** Compare cases handled by the same resource, same team, or same region-month workload stratum. Useful questions include:

- Are Group B cases slower because of the 7 bilingual workers, or because those workers receive harder cases?
- Are Group C cases slower because accommodation specialists are overloaded or because they also perform supervisor review?
- Do handover networks show avoidable general  bilingual  general or general  accommodation specialist  supervisor cycles?
- Within the same resource, do groups still differ after case-mix matching?

Resource fixed effects can reduce confounding by worker, but they cannot identify bias in the initial assignment of cases to workers.

**Waiting-time decomposition.** Decompose cycle time into:

1. time before first substantive activity;
2. wait between required activities;
3. service/processing time;
4. support-step queue time;
5. rework loops;
6. appeal-related time.

The most important decompositions are the support queues: Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment and Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment. If excess time is concentrated in queues rather than decision deliberation, the fairness issue is access and service design rather than legitimate complexity.

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.** Re-run comparisons:

- with and without `risk_score_band`;
- with and without `documentation_completeness`;
- using alternative risk thresholds;
- treating `documentation_completeness` as a mediator affected by language access rather than a neutral control;
- using negative-control edges such as Decision Draft  Applicant Notification, where differences are small;
- bounding the possible effect of unmeasured need;
- auditing whether high-risk status predicts fair downstream processing across regions and workers.

If the estimated group disparity changes substantially when risk or documentation is removed, the adjusted result is fragile and should not be treated as definitive.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.** Repeat the analysis by:

- region, especially the two regions with supervisor-review backlog;
- online vs in-person channel;
- language-support need vs no language-support need;
- accommodation request vs no accommodation request;
- high vs non-high risk band.

This is essential because Group B has high in-person and language-support shares, Group C has high accommodation share, and resource bottlenecks are region-specific.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate needs-based routing.** High-risk cases require Fraud Review; accommodation requests require Accommodation Review; minority-language requests require Language Support Contact. This can explain much of Group D’s V3 path, Group C’s V5 path, and Group B’s V4 path.

2. **Resource-capacity mismatch.** Language-support and accommodation steps are required but not staffed or targeted at the same service level as ordinary steps. Seven bilingual caseworkers and a small accommodation-specialist pool can turn rights-preserving steps into queues. This is concerning even though the step itself is legitimate.

3. **Fragmented service design.** The observed handovers—general  bilingual  general for Group B, and general  accommodation specialist  supervisor for Group C—may create repeated explanation, re-verification, and loss of context. The procedure may be formally compliant but practically burdensome.

4. **Documentation effects of language and channel barriers.** Group B has high incomplete-documentation and in-person shares. Documentation may be recorded as “incomplete” because forms, instructions, or communications are not accessible, not because the applicant is less eligible or less prepared.

5. **Historical bias embedded in risk or prior-case variables.** Group D has high prior-case count and high measured risk. If risk scores reflect prior policing, prior administrative errors, or historical under-investment, then risk-based routing may reproduce past inequity under a neutral label.

6. **Additional scrutiny or risk aversion for B and C.** Adjusted Supervisor Review is higher for B and C even after controls. This could reflect conscientious handling of harder cases, or it could reflect implicit bias, stricter thresholds, or uncertainty when staff interact with applicants whose language or accommodation needs differ.

7. **Poor explanation quality.** Higher appeal rates may arise even when decisions are substantively similar if applicants do not understand the decision, receive inadequate translations, or are not given accessible reasons. The log cannot test this directly.

8. **Regional backlog interaction.** Two regions have supervisor-review backlog. If B and C are concentrated in those regions through language, accommodation, or in-person routing, regional resource problems can become group disparities.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias.** `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities through prior contacts, enforcement patterns, or region. It should not be treated as a neutral complexity control. `documentation_completeness` may reflect applicant behavior, but it may also reflect inaccessible forms, language barriers, disability-related barriers, or inconsistent staff assessment.

**Missing applicant needs.** The log lacks severity and type of language need, specific accommodation type, urgency, digital-access barriers, caregiver constraints, income volatility, and applicant preferences for channel. Without these, apparent “same-need” comparisons may still be unequal.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity. The log does not show real-time queue length, concurrent assignments, worker absences, skill mix, prioritization rules, or whether bilingual/accommodation staff were doing other high-priority work.

**Insufficient service-time detail.** If only completion timestamps are available, it is difficult to separate actual processing time from waiting time. PM4Py waiting-time insertion can help, but it cannot create true start/end timestamps that were never recorded.

**Appeal quality is not captured.** `appeal_flag` does not show appeal grounds, success rate, who assisted the applicant, whether the explanation was accessible, or whether the appeal process itself was fair. A high appeal rate is a burden signal, not proof of bad decisions.

**Outcome quality is narrow.** `case_outcome` does not capture benefit amount, timeliness of activation, correctness of eligibility reasoning, explanation quality, dignity of treatment, or applicant experience. A case can be “approved” yet procedurally unfair.

**Dynamic process details may be lost.** Case-level attributes may change over time, rework loops may not be fully represented in top variants, and support needs may be identified late. The log may not capture when staff first learned of a language or accommodation need.

**No direct evidence of intent or causation.** The data can show patterns consistent with inequitable procedure, but not whether decision-makers intended unequal treatment or whether a specific policy caused the disparity.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation | Expected benefit | Possible side effects or trade-offs |

Do not remove Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review to improve cycle time. The correct response is to make those steps timely, accessible, and less burdensome.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does **not** appear procedurally fair on the available evidence, although a definitive finding of discriminatory intent or causation is **indeterminate**. Raw approval-rate gaps alone should not be labelled discrimination. But after complexity adjustment, Groups B and C still show longer cycle times, more document requests, more supervisor review, and support-heavy paths concentrated in scarce queues. Language-support and accommodation steps are legitimate and rights-preserving, but their delays and handovers create material procedural burden. Group D’s routing is more plausibly explained by measured risk, yet reliance on `risk_score_band` and its elevated adjusted appeal rate require audit.

The most defensible conclusion is: **there are credible procedural-fairness concerns, especially for Groups B and C, arising from support-step bottlenecks, resource concentration, and unequal procedural burden; the causal source and legality of those disparities require further investigation.**
