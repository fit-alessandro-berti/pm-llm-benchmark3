# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Chief Operating Officer  
**From:** Operations Strategy Team  
**Date:** 2025-08-28  
**Subject:** Decision on Redesign R for fiber-installation process  

**Bottom line**  
Do not scale Redesign R nationally. Do not roll it back. The evidence supports a controlled hybrid: keep the high-confidence improvements for simple low-risk houses, pause the mechanisms that create tail risk, rework, and activation-related complaints, and run a short, well-measured experimental phase before any further scale.

**What the evidence shows**  
The pilot improved median cycle time from 14.2 to 10.8 days and reduced cases with 3+ handovers from 34% to 12%. But the same pilot produced a worse tail: p90 cycle time rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days. First-time-right installation fell from 82% to 76%, complaints within 30 days rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation rose from 7% to 15%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. The estimated full downstream cost worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207, though that estimate is incomplete.

These aggregate numbers hide the most important pattern: the redesign works well for simple low-risk houses and poorly for complex cases. Simple low-risk houses saw median duration improve by 6 days, p90 improve by 8 days, first-time-right improve by 3 points, and complaints fall by 1 point. Apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural long-distance installs saw worse tails, more rework, and higher complaint rates. Pilot regions also had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline average, so aggregate comparisons are partly a mix effect.

**Why this is not a straightforward scale decision**  
Two design choices are especially suspect: skipping field surveys for medium-risk addresses and moving some activation checks after billing start. Both correlate with process-mining signs of trouble: the pilot model has high fitness but low precision, meaning it permits too many combinations and does not represent a disciplined process. The frequency of billing before activation, installation visits without prior field survey, missing permit checks for older apartments, and complaint escalation after billing all point to the same failure mechanisms.

Scaling R nationally would export these problems to a much larger population. Rolling back entirely would abandon documented gains in the largest, simplest segment and the real reduction in handovers. Neither all-or-nothing path is defensible.

**Recommended action**  
Adopt a hybrid, staged approach:

- **Keep** coordinator ownership and merged address-validation/appointment-scheduling for simple low-risk houses.
- **Suspend** field-survey skipping and post-billing activation everywhere until they can be validated in a controlled setting.
- **Restore** baseline controls for apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural long-distance installs: field survey, permit checks, and activation testing before billing.
- **Run** better measurement now: validate the risk rule, collect structured reason codes, instrument start timestamps, measure full downstream cost, and monitor conformance for billing-before-activation and missing permit checks.
- **Do not scale nationally** until the thresholds described in Section 6 are met.

This recommendation accepts the complexity of running two disciplined flows. That complexity is smaller than the cost of a national rollout built on an overfit process model, or a rollback that throws away a clear segment-level win.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision**  
Fitness asks how much observed behavior the model can replay. Precision asks how much of the model’s allowed behavior is actually observed. The pilot model has high fitness because it is permissive: it includes the simplified coordinator path, many optional branches, and few hard guards. Low precision means it also allows many paths that are not observed or should not happen. The alignment diagnostics are the practical symptoms: billing before activation, repeated installation without a survey, missing permit checks, and complaints after billing. The model fits the log by making undesirable events legal, not by discovering a disciplined process.

**Improved median but worse tail duration**  
The median responds to the common path. The redesign shortened address validation, scheduling, and handover steps for simple houses. The p90 and p95 respond to the worst failing cases. When a survey is skipped or activation is moved late, a case can cascade into a failed visit, second visit, manual activation, and complaint. A process can become faster on average and worse at the upper end because it trades reliability for speed on some cases. The distribution shifted, not the entire process uniformly.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience**  
Handover count and resilience are different properties. Coordinator ownership reduces the number of formal handovers but concentrates case knowledge in one person. At 93% utilization, coordinator availability becomes a bottleneck. When a coordinator is absent, cases stall because context is not shared. The handover network became simpler, but the working-together network became more centralized and fragile. A simpler network is not necessarily a more robust network.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework and downstream cost**  
The visible cost metric stops before complaint handling and some downstream activities. In the pilot, many failures occur after billing start, so their cost is excluded or lagging. The reduction from EUR 184 to EUR 161 is a front-loaded cost improvement. Second visits, manual activation, complaint handling, and revenue disputes appear later. The incomplete full-cost estimate worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207, suggesting the apparent savings are offset or exceeded. Without full-cost measurement, the cost improvement is not trustworthy.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings**  
The same intervention can have opposite effects depending on case complexity. Simple low-risk houses do not need field surveys or permit checks, so merged scheduling and reduced handovers remove waste. Apartments and old buildings require survey and permit verification. When the skip rule bypasses those prerequisites, installation quality drops. The result is more manual activation, second visits, complaints, and tail duration. The aggregate curve hides this interaction; segment-level analysis is essential.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally**  
- Advantages: Immediately captures median speed and handover reductions; standardizes one process; momentum from pilot success.  
- Risks: Scales known tail, rework, complaint, and cost problems to the whole country; exposes the company to billing-before-activation complaints and missing permit checks; the low-precision process model suggests many unmanaged paths; coordinator and second-visit teams would be severely overloaded.  
- Reasonable only if: future controlled evidence shows complex segments can be protected, full costs are not worse, and conformance violations are eliminated. That evidence does not exist today.

**B. Roll back to baseline**  
- Advantages: Removes pilot harms; restores a familiar, more constrained process; easy to communicate.  
- Risks: Surrenders meaningful simple-house gains (6 days median, 8 days p90, +3pp first-time-right, 1pp complaints) and the large reduction in handovers; returns to the original inefficiencies; treats a mixed result as failure.  
- Reasonable only if: compliance or safety risks are judged unacceptable and segments cannot be reliably identified. The current evidence does not justify discarding the segment-level wins.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**  
- Advantages: Retains accountability and handover benefits; removes the most likely failure mechanisms; is a cautious single-process option.  
- Risks: Leaves potential simple-house speed gains from merged validation/scheduling unused; does not address coordinator overload and absence risk; may still require additional controls for permit-sensitive old buildings.  
- Reasonable only if: management wants the safest possible step and does not trust segment/risk labels enough for a hybrid process. This is a good fallback.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**  
- Advantages: Produces causal evidence; avoids scaling a confounded pilot; can quantify segment-specific effects; fills data gaps.  
- Risks: Delays action; costs time and resources; if run without any interim deployment, it forgoes already clear simple-house benefits.  
- Reasonable only if: uncertainty is the dominant factor and management can accept a period without further rollout. It is best combined with a safe interim hybrid.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, run measurement before wider scale**  
- Advantages: Matches process design to segment evidence; harvests the proven wins; contains damage in complex segments; creates a learning loop for later decisions.  
- Risks: Increases operating complexity; depends on reliable segment and building-risk classification; early pilot attributes are incomplete before week 3.  
- Reasonable only if: classification can be improved with better data and manual review. This is the recommended path, with the additional guardrail of suspending post-billing activation until it is measured.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Expected decision value is highest for measurements that most reduce the risk of scaling something harmful or forgoing something beneficial.

1. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.**  
   This directly addresses the survey-skip mechanism. If the rule is accurate, skipping can be applied only to safely identified cases. If it is not, field survey should remain mandatory for non-trivial risk. This could change the decision from “never skip” to “skip only validated low-risk cases” or “keep skipping suspended.”

2. **M1 — Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.**  
   This is the strongest causal follow-up to M6. It would tell us whether survey skipping is safe in each risk stratum. It could change the decision if it shows skip is safe in segments where we currently plan to require survey, or harmful even in simple houses. M6 should come first because it is faster and helps avoid designing an experiment around a poor rule.

3. **M3 — Add structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activations, complaints, and second visits.**  
   Without reason codes, we cannot separate failures caused by the skip rule, post-billing activation, coordinator overload, or customer behavior. These codes would make targeted fixes possible and directly support segment thresholds. They could change the decision by revealing whether pausing certain mechanisms is sufficient or whether other fixes are needed.

4. **M4 — Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start.**  
   This resolves the economic contradiction. If full cost is materially worse, even hybrid expansion must be reconsidered. If full cost is neutral or better, the argument for scaling selected components becomes stronger. This measurement could change the decision from “pause and investigate” to “continue in segments.”

5. **M2 — Instrument start timestamps for installation visit, activation test, and manual activation.**  
   This separates waiting time from execution time and localizes where the tail is created. It can adjust resource allocation and show whether p90 degradation is caused by backlog or execution failure. It is valuable, but it informs optimization more than the core go/no-go decision.

6. **M7 — Run conformance monitoring for billing before activation and missing permit checks.**  
   This is a guardrail. It verifies that unwanted paths do not recur after we impose controls. It can trigger a halt if violations appear, but it is less likely to change the initial strategic recommendation.

7. **M8 — Collect customer experience separately for speed to appointment and working service after installation.**  
   This explains the NPS change and complaint-type shift. It is useful for design priorities, but it is less decision-critical than operational and cost measures.

8. **M5 — Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership under coordinator absence.**  
   This addresses resilience and staffing. It is important, but it can be managed with backups, caseload caps, and shared context. It does not tell us which process components to scale or suspend.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: adopt a controlled hybrid, Alternative E, with immediate safety closures. Do not scale R nationally. Do not roll back.**

**Immediate action**  
- Convert pilot regions to a stratified protocol.  
- For simple low-risk houses: keep coordinator ownership and merged address-validation/appointment-scheduling. Keep activation testing before billing. Do not skip field survey unless the validated rule supports it.  
- For apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural long-distance installs: restore the baseline sequence, including field surveys, permit checks, and activation testing before billing. Coordinator ownership may be tested, but only with capped caseload and shared case context.  

**What to pause**  
- National rollout of Redesign R.  
- Field-survey skipping for medium-risk addresses until M6/M1 validate the rule.  
- Moving activation checks after billing start until M4/M7 show no net downstream cost or complaint harm.  
- Scaling coordinator ownership without addressing the 93% utilization and absence-resilience problem.  

**What to scale**  
- Scale only the proven components for the proven segment: coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple low-risk houses.  
- Add capacity or backup so coordinator utilization stays below roughly 85% and case context is shared across the team.  

**What to measure next**  
- Run M6 first, then M1 if the risk rule is salvageable.  
- Add M2, M3, M4, and M7 in parallel.  
- Monitor resource utilization, second-visit team load, and the working-together network as part of the implementation.  

**Thresholds that would justify broader rollout**  
For any segment or component to move from hybrid to wider rollout, require at 95% confidence:  
- First-time-right no more than 1 percentage point below baseline.  
- 30-day complaint rate no more than 0.5 percentage point above baseline.  
- p90 cycle time no more than 2 days above baseline.  
- Estimated full 60-day downstream cost no higher than baseline.  
- Zero conformance violations for billing-before-activation and zero missing permit checks in permit-sensitive cases.  
- Coordinator utilization below 85% or an effective backup/shared-context mechanism in place.  

If these thresholds are met over an additional 6–10 weeks, expand region-by-region. If they are not met, keep the suspended components suspended and redesign them before any further scale.
