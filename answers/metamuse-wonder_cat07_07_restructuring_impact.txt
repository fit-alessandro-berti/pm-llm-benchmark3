# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring does not simply "remove handovers." It changes *who* work moves between, *how* it waits, *who learns from it*, and *who is accountable for it*. Nominal handover counts can fall while effective coordination, queueing, reassignment, and rework rise.

| Restructuring action | Mechanism | Expected process-mining effect | Observable measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech into Technical Shared Service | Pooling replaces named local relationships with a queue and any-available-specialist pickup | **Nominal handover reduction** locally, but handovers become less personal and less continuous. Continuity of the same caseworker-specialist pair weakens | Handover-of-work network: fewer local caseworker  named tech edges, one high-degree Technical Shared Service node; drop in reciprocity of local loops; rise in `reassignment_count`; more distinct resources per case |
| Centralize Specialist Review, Legal/Risk Review, difficult Quality Check in same queue | Different work types compete for the same scarce capacity; triage quality becomes decisive | **Central queue formation**. Work that was parallel across 3 local techs plus legal/risk becomes serialized through a hub. Hidden bottleneck if difficult Quality Check and legal/risk triage are absorbed informally | Performance DFG waiting time into Specialist Review / Quality Check; service vs waiting time per case; waiting-time p50/p90/p95 by `required_specialty`; WIP and queue age; variants containing repeated Specialist Review |
| Route all high-complexity and legal/risk/security cases to shared queue | Referral threshold lowers; early informal local advice is bypassed | **Specialist utilization** rises, possibly beyond sustainable level. Management expects +15%, but Legal is already at 93% and Risk at 91% utilization — there is almost no headroom. Pooling helps only if total demand and rework do not rise | Resource utilization proxy from service time; cases per specialist per week; share of cases with `required_specialty` in {legal, risk, security}; referral rate per 100 cases by `case_complexity`; queue wait nonlinear above ~85-90% utilization |
| More cases routed to specialists because local advice is unavailable | Loss of early, informal prevention: local techs currently stop unnecessary referrals and catch errors early | **Rework** may rise even as formal referral quality improves. Standard advice returned through a queue may miss regional conventions and case context | `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity`; `rework_flag` rate after specialist handover vs before; Rework activity frequency per 100 cases; variants with Technical Assessment  Specialist Review  Technical Assessment loops |
| Remove embedded specialists from North/South/East | Local caseworkers lose proximity, tacit knowledge, and regional customer conventions | **Local expertise loss**, uneven: East may gain relief because E_Tech is at 82% and East cycle times are worst; North/South, with stable loops and higher satisfaction, have more to lose | Performance split by `team` and `local_expertise_flag`; rework and cycle time for cases previously handled with local expertise; activity-based resource similarity showing local caseworkers doing less Technical Assessment over time; declining share of complex cases completed locally |
| Shared approval queue and removal of one local supervisor | Approval is standardized but detached from case ownership | **Role clarity** risk: standardized decisions improve, but accountability diffuses — "the queue approved it." Supervisor approval loop becomes anonymous | Organizational role discovery: roles blur, more resources perform Supervisor Approval / Specialist Review across teams; working-together network becomes less team-clustered; cases with many approvers per case; handover_reason codes for "ownership unclear," "awaiting decision" |
| Single shared queue for all teams | Pooled capacity absorbs local absence better in theory; but the hub is a single point of failure | **Resilience to absence**: improves for local team absence (any specialist can pick up), worsens for hub absence, sickness, or attrition — all teams stall simultaneously instead of one | Handover network centrality: betweenness/degree concentration on shared service; cycle-time degradation on days with specialist absence; cross-team handover frequency; variance of waiting time by day/week; subcontracting network showing work bouncing between locals and hub |
| Uniform routing rules for all teams/segments | Same queue, same priority rules applied to heterogeneous demand | **Fairness across teams/customer segments**: pooling can equalize East's disadvantage, or high-volume North/South and high-`priority` cases can crowd out East, low-priority, and customers needing regional/language accommodation | Waiting time, SLA miss, rework, and number of handovers filtered by `team`, `customer_segment`, `priority`, `case_complexity`; share of cases exceeding SLA by segment; reassignment_count by segment; outcome distribution by segment |

Key distinction to monitor: **nominal handovers** (counted activity-to-activity/resource-to-resource transfers in the log) versus **effective handovers** (queue pickups, reassignments, repeat reviews, advice-seeking outside the log that later appears as delay or rework). Management's table assumes cross-team handovers fall 6  3 per 100 cases and local loops shrink, but it also *increases* local caseworker  shared service from 36 (embedded tech) to 41 per 100 cases, the return loop from 31 to 36, and legal/risk referrals from 9 to 12. Even on management's own numbers, specialist-facing handovers rise; only their *location* changes.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Baselines for reference: median wait after local caseworker  embedded tech is 3.2h with 9% rework;  legal/risk is 9.6h with 14% rework; cross-team is 8.9h with 18% rework. Team medians: North 6.8d, South 7.4d, East 8.6d; p90 up to 22.8d in East. Legal/Risk utilization is already 91-93%.

## Optimistic scenario — pooling works, triage is accurate, capacity is protected

- **Handover pattern:** Local specialist loops genuinely decline; Technical Shared Service edges are high-volume but stable, with the same specialist often returning advice to the originating caseworker. Cross-team handovers fall toward 3 per 100 cases. `reassignment_count` stays flat.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Pooled capacity smooths peaks; median wait to Technical Shared Service stays near management's 4.0h estimate, p90 does not deteriorate versus the 3.2h embedded baseline by more than a modest margin. Legal/Risk waits stay near 9.6-10.5h because routing detects cases earlier, not just more of them.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East improves most: median toward 7.5d, p90 falls from 22.8d as E_Tech overload is relieved. North/South medians roughly unchanged. Overall p90 improves, not just the mean.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework after specialist handover stays at or below 9%; difficult Quality Check in the hub catches errors earlier, so Rework activity frequency falls, especially in East from 11.5% toward North's 8.2%.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Routing rules are explicit, so organizational roles remain discoverable and distinct: locals own Intake/Eligibility/case ownership, hub owns defined review types. Working-together network shows hub collaboration *in addition to* intact local clusters.
- **Fairness implications:** East's SLA miss rate (9.8%) converges toward North/South. No customer segment sees systematically longer waits; regional accommodation is preserved through case notes and named-caseworker continuity.
- **Early warning signs (positive confirmation):** Stable referral rate per 100 cases, flat reassignment_count, waiting-time p90 stable within 2-4 weeks, rework after specialist advice not rising, specialist service time per case not inflating.

## Central scenario — average improves slightly, tail and complexity worsen, benefits concentrate in East

- **Handover pattern:** Nominal local handovers fall as forecast, but effective handovers rise: cases are picked up, returned for clarification, and re-queued. Local caseworker  Technical Shared Service rises above 41 per 100 cases as the referral threshold drops without local informal advice. Return loop exceeds 36 per 100.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Median wait to shared service lands above the 4.0h estimate (plausibly 6-10h) with a long tail; Legal/Risk waits exceed 10.5h because 93%/91% utilization leaves no slack for the rise from 9 to 12 referrals per 100 cases. Shared approval queue adds a second wait that partially offsets gains, near or above the 3.0h estimate at p90.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Overall median improves slightly, driven by simple cases and East relief, but p90/p95 worsen for high-complexity cases. North/South medians flat-to-slightly-worse; East median improves while its complex-case tail does not.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework after specialist handover rises from 9% toward 12-15% for cases where local context mattered; standardized advice increases repeat Technical Assessment and Quality Check failures in regionally specific cases. Simple-case quality improves through standardization.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Role discovery shows blurring: many resources touch Specialist Review and Approval; caseworkers report unclear ownership when the queue returns partial advice. Handover_reason shifts toward "awaiting specialist," "clarification needed," "ownership unclear."
- **Fairness implications:** High-`priority` and high-volume team demand is served faster; low-priority, East-originated, and accommodation-needing segments wait longer in the same queue. Average fairness looks acceptable, segment-level fairness does not.
- **Early warning signs:** Rising share of cases with 2 specialist touches; reassignment_count trending up within weeks; waiting-time p90 rising while p50 is flat; rework after specialist return rising in North/South specifically; local caseworkers' Technical Assessment service time falling (deskilling signal) alongside rising referral rates.

## Adverse scenario — hub becomes the system bottleneck and local capability atrophies

- **Handover pattern:** The shared queue becomes the dominant hub in the handover network; nearly all complex work funnels through a few specialists. Subcontracting network shows bouncing: local  hub  local  hub. Cross-team handovers reappear informally as staff seek workarounds outside the queue, so logged cross-team falls while real coordination fragments.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Utilization pushes past sustainable levels; waiting time grows nonlinearly. Median specialist wait multiples of the 4.0h estimate; Legal/Risk waits far exceed 10.5h; approval queue and specialist queue compound, so cases wait twice. Absence of one key specialist stalls all three teams simultaneously.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median may still look acceptable because simple local cases dominate volume, but p90/p95 and high-complexity cycle times deteriorate sharply; SLA misses concentrate in complex, legal/risk, and accommodation-needing cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework rate after specialist handover approaches or exceeds the current cross-team level (18%); difficult Quality Check queued in the hub is rushed or rubber-stamped under load, so Rework is discovered later by customers rather than internally. Outcomes worsen for complex cases.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Organizational roles collapse into an undifferentiated reviewer pool; no named owner for specialist decisions; supervisor accountability removed locally is not replaced. Staff feedback reports diffusion of responsibility; log shows many distinct resources per case with no continuity.
- **Fairness implications:** Systematic disadvantage for East (smaller voice in a shared queue), low-priority cases, and customer segments needing regional/language handling, who accumulate extra handovers and reassignments. Satisfaction gaps widen even if overall satisfaction is flat.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue age and WIP rising week-on-week; specialist overtime/service-time inflation; `handover_reason` dominated by capacity/awaiting codes; spikes in reassignment_count; temporal features showing work bunching at queue-release times; local specialists/former techs exiting or being bypassed; rising variants with repeated Specialist Review  Rework loops.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

Average cycle time can improve while the process gets worse, because simple, high-volume local cases dominate the mean. At least these risks hide underneath a stable or improving average:

1. **Tail latency.** The median and mean can improve through faster simple cases while p90/p95 explode in the shared queue. East already shows the pattern to watch: median 8.6d but p90 22.8d. A pooled queue near capacity produces exactly this — acceptable typical waits, extreme waits for the unlucky. Always report p50/p90/p95 and SLA misses, not the mean.
2. **High-complexity cases.** All high-complexity cases are forcibly routed to the hub, so they absorb 100% of queue risk while contributing little to volume-weighted averages. Complexity-segmented cycle time can deteriorate sharply while the headline number improves. This is also where rework and customer harm concentrate.
3. **Local knowledge loss.** Regional conventions currently prevent rework invisibly — the prevented error never appears in any log. Removing embedded techs removes a benefit that is only measurable by its absence: rising rework, clarification loops, and repeat customer contact months later, after the early averages looked fine. North and South, with satisfaction 4.3 and 4.1 built on local expertise, carry this risk; East's lower baseline makes its loss harder to see.
4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments.** A single queue with priority rules systematically advantages high-`priority`, high-volume, and easy-to-serve cases. East (520 cases/month vs North's 820), low-priority cases, and customers needing language/regional accommodation can each wait longer and accumulate more reassignments while the overall average improves. Averages aggregate away exactly the groups fairness analysis must separate.
5. **Learning and deskilling.** If every hard case leaves the local team, local caseworkers stop developing technical judgment. The log signature — falling local Technical Assessment on complex cases, rising referral rate, shrinking local role profiles — appears long before the outcome: a workforce that can no longer triage, making the hub permanently indispensable and permanently overloaded. This is a slow, compounding risk invisible in any single quarter's cycle time.
6. **Accountability and role clarity.** A shared approval queue can standardize decisions while dissolving ownership: when a queued, anonymous approval fails, no one is accountable, and cases bounce for clarification. Cycle time may not move at all while decision quality, auditability, and staff confidence degrade. Watch distinct approvers per case, `handover_reason` = ownership/clarification, and role-discovery blurring.
7. **Exception handling and resilience.** Standardized queues handle standard cases well and exceptions badly. The current cross-team cover already fails (18% rework) because case notes are inconsistent — centralization multiplies dependence on those same notes. Exceptions will either wait longest in the queue or escape into unlogged workarounds, so the log can look cleaner while real exception handling worsens. Similarly, resilience concentrates: one hub absence now stalls all teams, a correlated failure that averages across healthy weeks will hide until it happens.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope
Pilot the Technical Shared Service and shared approval queue with **one specialty stream and East plus a partial North/South cohort**, rather than only East — East is the most likely beneficiary (E_Tech at 82%, worst cycle times), so an East-only pilot would overstate benefits. A defensible scope: route East's technical cases and a randomized/rotating subset of North and South high-complexity technical cases to the shared service for the pilot; keep Legal/Risk routing rules unchanged initially (they are already at 91-93% utilization and cannot absorb experimental load), and add them only in a second phase if phase one passes guardrails. Keep Intake and simple Eligibility local everywhere, as proposed.

## Comparison group
North/South cases not routed to the pilot, matched on `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, `priority`, and `customer_segment`, serve as the concurrent comparison group. Compare pilot vs comparison *and* each group against its own pre-period baseline, segment by segment — an East-only before/after comparison confounds the restructuring with East's regression to the mean.

## Pre/post observation window
Use at least **8-12 weeks pre** (the existing log) and **8-12 weeks post**, excluding a 2-week transition/wash-in period from headline results but monitoring it daily for guardrails. This covers monthly volume cycles (520-820 cases/team/month) and gives enough complex and legal/risk cases per segment for p90 estimates.

## Segmentation
Pre-register analysis cuts by: `team` (North/South/East separately — pooled results are not a decision basis), `case_complexity` (especially high-complexity, which is 100% routed), `required_specialty` (technical vs legal/risk/security), `customer_segment` (including segments needing regional/language accommodation), and `priority`. Report every primary metric overall *and* per segment.

## Guardrail metrics
Reviewed weekly during the pilot: waiting time p50/p90 into Specialist Review and shared Approval vs baselines (3.2h embedded tech, 9.6h legal/risk, 2.4h local supervisor); referral rate per 100 cases; `reassignment_count`; rework after specialist handover (baselines 9% tech, 14% legal/risk); specialist service-time load vs sustainable ceiling; queue WIP and oldest-case age; segment-level SLA trajectory. Guardrails apply per segment, not only in aggregate.

## Staff feedback collection
Weekly short pulse surveys for caseworkers, specialists, and supervisors on role clarity, ownership, advice usefulness, and workaround use; structured coding of `handover_reason` plus free-text review; fortnightly interviews deliberately sampling North/South staff (expertise-loss risk) and East staff (expected beneficiaries) separately; a log of informal/off-system advice-seeking so unlogged coordination is visible rather than assumed away.

## Rollback or adjustment triggers
Pre-commit to triggers such as: specialist waiting p90 exceeds 2× the embedded baseline for 3 consecutive weeks; rework after specialist handover rises more than 3-4 percentage points in any team; high-complexity p90 cycle time worsens while overall median improves; any customer segment's SLA miss rate worsens by more than 2 points vs its baseline; Legal/Risk load exceeds sustainable utilization once added. Responses in order: tighten referral/triage criteria, restore named-specialist continuity for complex cases, add hub capacity or reserve local tech hours for informal advice, reintroduce a local approval step — and full rollback to embedded specialists if triggers persist after adjustment. Rollback criteria are decided *before* seeing results.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: pilot first, with modifications — do not proceed directly to full rollout, and do not reject outright.**

East has a genuine problem the proposal plausibly addresses: E_Tech at 82% utilization, 11.5% rework, 9.8% SLA misses, p90 of 22.8 days. Pooling could relieve it. But the same proposal removes the mechanism that makes North and South perform well, and it routes *more* work into Legal/Risk capacity that is already at 91-93% utilization, where management's expected +15% utilization gain is arithmetically close to impossible without queue explosion or hidden overtime. The expected benefit (fewer nominal handovers) is partly an artifact of relabeling: specialist-facing handovers rise in management's own forecast.

Conditions for proceeding beyond the pilot: (1) add or protect specialist capacity and explicit triage criteria before widening referral rules; (2) preserve a local informal-advice channel and named-specialist continuity for high-complexity cases, so local expertise and learning do not atrophy; (3) keep a named accountable approver even within a shared approval queue; (4) scale only if guardrails pass *per segment* — East improvement bought with North/South or minority-segment deterioration is not success; (5) treat Legal/Risk centralization as a separate, later decision requiring capacity first.

What management should **not** infer from early averages: an improving mean or median cycle time in the first weeks does not show the restructuring works. Early averages will be dominated by simple local cases, by East's relief effect, and by wash-in behavior (staff working around the queue informally). They cannot reveal tail latency, complex-case harm, fairness gaps, deskilling, or resilience loss, all of which accumulate over months. Conversely, a small early median increase during transition does not prove failure. Decisions should rest on the pre-registered segmented indicators — waiting-time tails, rework after handover, reassignment, segment-level SLA, and role/learning signals — sustained over the full pilot window.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The sketch below compares a pre-restructuring log and a post/pilot log. It assumes the columns listed in the scenario and uses the PM4Py functions named there; exact parameter names vary slightly by PM4Py version, so treat it as analysis pseudocode structured around real calls.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"

def load(path):
    df = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=[TS])
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS)
    return pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

pre_log = load("log_before.csv")
post_log = load("log_after.csv")

def networks(log, label):
    how = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
    wt = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
    sub = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log)
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
    sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
    print(label, "handover edges:", len(how[0]) if isinstance(how, tuple) else how)
    return {"handover": how, "working_together": wt,
            "subcontracting": sub, "roles": roles, "similarity": sim}

net_pre = networks(pre_log, "PRE")
net_post = networks(post_log, "POST")

def process_performance(log, label):
    dfg, start, end = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    perf_dfg, p_start, p_end = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    top = pm4py.filter_variants(log, list(variants)[:20])
    rework = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
    print(label, "variants:", len(variants), "rework per activity:", rework)
    return perf_dfg, variants

perf_pre, var_pre = process_performance(pre_log, "PRE")
perf_post, var_post = process_performance(post_log, "POST")

def waiting_and_service(df, label):
    dfw = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df.copy())
    svc = pm4py.get_service_time(dfw) if hasattr(pm4py, "get_service_time") else None
    hub = dfw[dfw[ACT].isin(["Specialist Review", "Supervisor Approval", "Quality Check"])]
    summary = hub.groupby(ACT)["@@waiting_time"].quantile([0.5, 0.9, 0.95]) \
        if "@@waiting_time" in hub else hub.describe()
    print(label, summary)
    return dfw

pre_df = pm4py.convert_to_dataframe(pre_log)
post_df = pm4py.convert_to_dataframe(post_log)
pre_w = waiting_and_service(pre_df, "PRE")
post_w = waiting_and_service(post_df, "POST")

def segment(log, attr, values, label):
    sub = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, attr, values, level="event")
    return sub

cohorts = {}
for name, attr, vals in [
    ("East", "team", ["East"]),
    ("North", "team", ["North"]),
    ("South", "team", ["South"]),
    ("HighComplexity", "case_complexity", ["high"]),
    ("LegalRisk", "required_specialty", ["legal", "risk", "security"]),
]:
    cohorts[("pre", name)] = segment(pre_log, attr, vals, name)
    cohorts[("post", name)] = segment(post_log, attr, vals, name)
    process_performance(cohorts[("post", name)], f"POST {name}")

def outcome_table(df, label):
    case = df.sort_values(TS).groupby(CASE).agg(
        team=("team", "first"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "first"),
        segment=("customer_segment", "first"),
        specialty=("required_specialty", "first"),
        reassignments=("reassignment_count", "max"),
        rework=("rework_flag", "max"),
        outcome=("outcome", "last"),
        start=(TS, "min"), end=(TS, "max"))
    case["cycle_days"] = (case["end"] - case["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    print(label)
    print(case.groupby(["team", "complexity"])["cycle_days"]
          .quantile([0.5, 0.9, 0.95]).unstack())
    print(case.groupby("segment")[["rework", "reassignments"]].mean())
    print("SLA/outcome by segment:", case.groupby("segment")["outcome"].value_counts(normalize=True))
    return case

pre_cases = outcome_table(pre_w, "PRE")
post_cases = outcome_table(post_w, "POST")

for name, log in [("pre", pre_log), ("post", post_log)]:
    feats = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
        pm4py.convert_to_dataframe(log),
        activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)
    print(name, "temporal feature columns:", list(feats.columns)[:10])
```

How to read the comparison: handover-network centralization on the shared service and loss of local reciprocity (Section 1 mechanisms); performance-DFG waiting into Specialist Review/Approval at p90/p95, not mean; variant growth in specialist-loop and rework variants; role discovery and resource similarity showing whether local roles blur; and the segmented case table testing whether East gains, North/South losses, high-complexity tails, and customer-segment fairness move in opposite directions beneath a stable overall average.
