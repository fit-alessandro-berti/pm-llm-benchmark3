## 1. Decision rule

Use a **stage-gated, value-and-effect-aware portfolio rule**, not a probability threshold.

### A. Eligibility gate

A case is eligible for a swarm only if all are true:

1. **Process eligibility:** current stage is before `Diagnosis Complete`.
2. **Timing feasibility:** enough time remains for a 45-minute swarm plus follow-up action; ordinarily at least 75 minutes remaining. Cases with 1.5–2 hours left may still qualify, but require an explicit “can swarm alter diagnosis in time?” check.
3. **Causal plausibility:** there is a plausible diagnostic bottleneck or missing-information hypothesis that the swarm can address. A high predicted breach probability alone is insufficient.
4. **Incremental-alert rule:** either:
   - first eligible alert, or
   - a materially new event occurred, or
   - a prior response/triage identified unresolved diagnostic uncertainty that the swarm could now address.
5. **Portfolio externality test:** estimated robust direct value must exceed swarm cost, false-alarm burden, and an allocated estimate of queue spillover harm.
6. **Capacity rule:** reserve one of four slots until 15:00 unless a candidate has a high robust net benefit or the incoming regulatory incident is ruled out.

`Diagnosis Complete` is a hard exclusion for this intervention. Such cases can receive a separate late-stage escalation, but not a diagnostic swarm.

### B. Robustified risk and effect inputs

Treat `p_now` as an observational risk signal, not as treated risk. Use an uncertainty-adjusted risk interval:

- **SME:** add roughly 0.04–0.09 to the model estimate, capped at 1.0. For prioritization use a central adjustment of about +0.065, but retain a wide interval.
- **Strategic Enterprise:** subtract roughly 0.05–0.12, central adjustment about -0.085, because the model currently overpredicts after workflow redesign.
- **Public Sector:** retain the estimate as central, but use moderate uncertainty bands due to sample size.
- **Non-Native-Language Support:** retain a wider interval around both risk and treatment effect; do not mechanically penalize the group for uncertainty. Require human review and use conservative portfolio exposure limits rather than dismissing it.

For intervention effect, use the supplied range as a **prior range**, not a known causal effect. The operational score is based on a robust low-to-midpoint effect, discounted for:

- weak timing feasibility;
- missing/imputed data;
- uncertain alert mechanism;
- segment-specific causal uncertainty;
- risk that the diagnostic hypothesis is wrong.

A practical score is:

\[
\text{Robust priority value}
=
(\underline{\Delta p}_{adj}\times V)
-450
-\text{false-alarm burden}
-\text{allocated spillover harm}
+\text{non-monetary obligation adjustment}.
\]

For an ordinary selected slot, allocate a base spillover charge of approximately:

\[
6 \times 0.02 \times \bar V_{\text{untreated diagnosis queue}},
\]

then stress-test at 0.01 and 0.03. This is not a literal invoice to one case; it is a portfolio guardrail.

### C. Alert persistence and minimum evidence change

An alert is not a new intervention opportunity merely because a model refreshes.

- **First alert:** assess normally.
- **Repeated alert without new evidence:** suppress for one operational cycle or until a material state change; no repeat swarm absent a new event.
- **Material evidence change:** re-enter review if there is a new customer log, new diagnostic result, assignment failure, queue/staffing change, material deadline compression, or a calibrated risk movement exceeding roughly 0.10 absolute probability.
- **No automatic escalation from repeated alerts:** persistence creates an operational review task, not repeated swarm entitlement.
- **After a swarm:** no second swarm unless a materially new diagnostic event occurs and the case owner documents why the first swarm could not address it.

### D. Priority tiers

- **Tier A — deploy now:** robust positive value under base assumptions, high diagnostic plausibility, and acceptable externality.
- **Tier B — human review / deploy if capacity opens:** positive in optimistic/base conditions but sensitive to calibration, data quality, timing, or spillovers.
- **Tier C — watch or use a non-swarm action:** potentially important, but not a justified use of scarce swarm capacity now.
- **Tier D — ineligible for swarm:** stage-ineligible or repeated alert without incremental evidence.

Non-monetary obligations can elevate a case one tier—for example, public-service continuity, regulatory duties, severe customer safety impact, or contractual governance—but should be documented separately from monetary value.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

### A. Cool-down and post-intervention rule

- After a swarm, impose a **case-level cool-down until a material event occurs**, normally at least one prefix cycle and generally 2–4 hours for the same diagnostic state.
- A second swarm requires:
  1. documented material new evidence;
  2. continuing pre-`Diagnosis Complete` eligibility;
  3. a different or newly exposed diagnostic bottleneck;
  4. approval by the swarm lead or duty manager.
- Repeated prediction refreshes alone do not satisfy the condition.

### B. Suppression logic

Suppress repeated alerts when all are true:

- no material event since the previous alert;
- no major stage change;
- no substantial new deadline compression;
- prediction movement is small, for example less than 0.10 absolute risk;
- no new operational evidence indicates the prior action failed because of a different mechanism.

Suppression should still leave a visible case-monitoring record. “No new swarm alert” must not mean “no owner is monitoring the case.”

### C. Escalation logic

Escalate, rather than repeatedly swarm, when:

- the case is nearing `Diagnosis Complete`, where swarm effectiveness falls;
- the case needs customer approval, executive coordination, commercial exception, or staffing rather than diagnosis;
- the predictive signal persists but no causal diagnostic mechanism is identified;
- the model signal conflicts with operational facts, as in a missing feature or suspected imputation artifact.

Escalation actions may include an owner check, data repair, customer-information request, queue reprioritization subject to fairness controls, or a late-stage resolution escalation distinct from the swarm.

### D. Re-entry logic

A suppressed case re-enters the swarm queue if one of the following occurs:

- new customer logs, telemetry, test results, or diagnostic evidence arrive;
- a new diagnostic hypothesis emerges;
- assignment changes, queue degradation, or staff loss materially affects feasibility;
- time-to-deadline crosses a predefined urgency band;
- a calibrated, data-quality-verified risk movement exceeds the materiality threshold;
- a prior intervention uncovered a new independent bottleneck.

### E. I-05 versus I-07

**I-05:** Do not deploy now. Its third alert is generated by repeated 30-minute scoring, but no new event occurred. The risk has barely changed from 0.55 to 0.57. Even though SME risk is likely underpredicted and the stated swarm effect is favorable, repeatedly reacting to unchanged predictions would create alert fatigue, duplicate customer burden, and unjustified capacity consumption. Keep it in suppressed monitoring; re-enter only upon new evidence, meaningful state change, or verified deadline deterioration.

**I-07:** Treat differently. It is also a third alert, but a **new customer diagnostic log** is a material information event. That makes the repeated alert potentially actionable despite `p_now` declining from 0.75 to 0.72. The log may enable the swarm to identify or redirect a diagnostic path. Its short deadline means the case needs immediate mechanism review and, if the log is actionable, immediate deployment. This illustrates that alert novelty is about information and decision relevance, not merely an increase in model score.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

### A. Value-based prioritization can starve lower-value cases

A pure expected-euro rule will repeatedly prefer high-value enterprise cases, particularly I-04 or I-08, over lower-value SME or public cases. This may be commercially rational in a narrow sense but can create systematically worse service for lower-revenue customers and weaken contractual equity.

**Safeguards:**

- Maintain a **protected service floor**: eligible lower-value cases meeting a high clinical/operational urgency criterion cannot be indefinitely displaced solely by account value.
- Use a two-part score: economic impact plus a documented **service-obligation component** for public service, vulnerable customers, safety, regulatory, or contractual severity.
- Track intervention rates, time-to-assistance, and untreated breach rates by value band and segment.
- Use periodic capacity allocation constraints, for example a review trigger if one segment’s eligible high-risk cases receive materially lower swarm access after adjustment for stage, deadline, and actionable evidence.
- Do not impose rigid equal allocation per day if it would ignore true severity; use fairness review and guardrails rather than simplistic quotas.

### B. Non-Native-Language Support uncertainty

I-09 has sparse calibration data and uncertain intervention effects. Treating uncertainty as low priority would produce a self-reinforcing disadvantage: fewer interventions, less outcome data, and continued uncertainty.

**Safeguards:**

- Route such cases to a **human-reviewed pathway** with language-appropriate support, rather than denying deployment because the estimate is imprecise.
- Record language-support needs separately from performance outcomes and audit whether data quality, feature availability, or documentation practices differ.
- Reserve a small, ethically governed learning allocation or randomized encouragement design for eligible uncertain segments, subject to service safeguards, to estimate effect rather than perpetually assuming uncertainty.
- Use wider confidence intervals in decisions, but avoid automatically assigning the lower bound only to this group. Apply comparable uncertainty penalties to all sparse-data contexts.

### C. Differential calibration across segments

SME underprediction can cause missed interventions; Strategic Enterprise overprediction can consume scarce slots and impose unnecessary disruption. These are both fairness and operational problems.

**Safeguards:**

- Use segment-specific calibration models or post-processing adjustments with uncertainty intervals.
- Publish and review calibration curves, Brier score, reliability slope/intercept, false-negative rates, and false-positive rates by segment, stage, and time-to-deadline band.
- Require recalibration review when segment-level calibration errors exceed predefined tolerances, for example persistent absolute calibration error above 0.03–0.05 in decision-relevant probability bands.
- Do not convert calibration adjustments into hidden hard thresholds; retain human review for cases near allocation boundaries.

### D. Selective intervention changes future training labels

Swarmed cases may have fewer breaches, but that outcome reflects both baseline difficulty and intervention. If the post-intervention outcome is used as if it were an untreated label, the model can learn that cases like those selected are lower risk than they actually are. Conversely, untreated cases harmed by congestion may appear intrinsically riskier.

**Safeguards:**

- Store treatment assignment, start/end time, swarm contents, staff, rationale, queue conditions, and post-treatment process events.
- Separate models for:
  - untreated baseline breach risk;
  - intervention propensity;
  - estimated conditional treatment effect;
  - queue/spillover effects.
- Exclude or properly adjust treated outcomes when retraining an untreated-risk model; use causal methods rather than naive supervised learning.
- Preserve pre-intervention snapshots and prediction timestamps so labels can be linked to the decision context.
- Audit whether model features become proxies for prior intervention access.

### E. Spillover harms may fall on untreated groups

Senior specialists leave ordinary diagnosis work during swarms. The burden may fall disproportionately on cases already lower in the priority queue, lower-value customers, SMEs, language-support users, or customers whose issues are less visible to senior staff.

**Safeguards:**

- Log the six-or-more cases exposed to each swarm-related queue delay, including segment, value band, stage, deadline proximity, and outcome.
- Monitor queue-delay and incremental-breach exposure by group, not only intervention recipients.
- Set a **congestion stop rule**: pause additional swarms when real-time queue delay or predicted untreated harm exceeds the estimated incremental direct benefit.
- Protect near-deadline untreated cases through a queue safety rule, such as no swarm start if it would delay an untreated diagnosis-stage case already within a defined critical time buffer.
- Report “benefit received” and “harm imposed” jointly by segment; fairness cannot be assessed from recipient counts alone.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

### A. Operational monitoring

Monitor in real time and daily:

- number of swarm slots used, held, and released;
- response time from alert to swarm;
- actual swarm duration and specialist interruption time;
- stage at deployment and stage at swarm completion;
- number and type of diagnostic actions produced;
- time from swarm to `Diagnosis Complete`;
- queue length, waiting time, and aggregate delay for non-intervened diagnosis cases;
- SLA breaches and near-breaches among selected, unselected, and queue-exposed cases;
- customer disruption events and false-alarm burden.

Create a live dashboard with a queue-congestion warning. If observed aggregate delay approaches 15 minutes per slot or high-risk untreated cases are accumulating, suspend optional deployments and preserve capacity.

### B. Predictive monitoring

For each segment, stage, and deadline band, monitor:

- calibration intercept and slope;
- reliability curves;
- Brier score and log loss;
- observed-versus-predicted breach rates;
- false-positive and false-negative patterns;
- missingness rates and imputation rates by feature and segment;
- model-score drift and feature-distribution drift.

Specific triggers:

- **SME:** investigate if adjusted predictions remain systematically low after applying the provisional correction.
- **Strategic Enterprise:** reassess immediately if post-redesign overprediction persists or changes direction.
- **Non-Native-Language Support:** track sample size, missingness, model error intervals, and intervention access; do not declare calibration “acceptable” from sparse data.
- **I-06-type missingness:** if a live feature failure materially changes scores, flag the prediction as degraded and require human validation before high-cost action.

### C. Causal-effect monitoring

The key estimand is not merely “breach rate among swarmed cases.” It is the conditional effect of a swarm before `Diagnosis Complete`, including downstream queue effects.

Collect:

- pre-decision risk, uncertainty interval, stage, time remaining, segment, and alert history;
- exact reasons for alert and the swarm’s diagnostic hypothesis;
- treatment timing, staff, actions taken, and follow-up;
- untreated comparison cases with similar stage, time-to-deadline, value, and evidence state;
- queue state and exposed untreated cases at the time of deployment.

Use a staged evaluation design:

1. **Short term:** matched comparisons, inverse-propensity weighting, doubly robust estimation, and sensitivity analyses for unobserved urgency.
2. **Where ethically acceptable:** randomized encouragement or lottery among near-tied eligible cases when capacity is binding. This improves effect estimation without withholding basic service from clearly critical cases.
3. **For repeat policy:** compare outcomes following new-evidence re-entry versus mechanically repeated alerts to validate the suppression rule.
4. **For spillovers:** estimate queue-level incremental delays and breach changes using time-series/event-study methods around swarm starts, controlling for baseline congestion.

Estimate heterogeneous treatment effects by stage, deadline horizon, segment, data quality, and diagnostic mechanism. Do not generalize a favorable effect from one segment to another without evidence.

### D. Capacity and portfolio monitoring

Track whether the portfolio policy is actually beneficial:

- direct expected benefit versus realized proxy outcomes;
- number of reserved slots that expire unused versus number of urgent arrivals rejected for lack of capacity;
- incremental harm to untreated diagnosis-stage cases;
- marginal benefit of the first, second, third, and fourth daily swarm;
- whether fourth-slot deployments are systematically less valuable due to congestion;
- specialist fatigue and ordinary diagnosis backlog.

Use a **marginal slot rule**: deploy the next slot only when estimated robust incremental benefit exceeds incremental labor, customer burden, and updated queue spillover cost. The fourth slot should be especially scrutinized because externalities are likely nonlinear under congestion.

### E. Fairness monitoring

At least monthly, and more frequently if volume permits, report by segment, value band, language-support status, and public/private service category:

- alert rate;
- eligibility rate;
- swarm allocation rate conditional on eligibility;
- time-to-swarm;
- breach rate among comparable eligible cases;
- false-alarm burden;
- queue-delay exposure among untreated cases;
- calibration quality and uncertainty;
- treatment-effect estimate precision.

Investigate disparities after controlling for stage, deadline, actionable evidence, and congestion. A lower intervention rate alone is not proof of unfairness, but lower intervention coupled with worse outcomes, poorer calibration, or greater spillover exposure is a strong warning signal.

### F. When to change the rule

Review thresholds, reserve policy, calibration adjustments, and allocation weights on a scheduled basis—initially weekly during the workflow redesign and feature-instability period, then monthly when stable. Change the rule when any of the following occurs:

- realized spillover harm exceeds the base assumption;
- the fourth slot is repeatedly net-negative or materially worsens untreated outcomes;
- segment calibration error exceeds the stated tolerance in decision-relevant bands;
- estimated treatment effect is near zero for a stage or alert mechanism;
- an intervention mechanism proves especially effective for a subgroup;
- fairness monitoring shows persistent unequal access or unequal exposure to queue harms;
- the regulatory-arrival reserve is consistently unnecessary or repeatedly insufficient.

The policy should therefore evolve from conservative robust decision-making toward better-calibrated, causal-effect-aware allocation, while retaining hard process eligibility, repeated-alert suppression, and explicit protection against queue externalities.
