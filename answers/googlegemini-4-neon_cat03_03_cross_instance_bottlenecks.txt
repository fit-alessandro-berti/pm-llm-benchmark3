## 4. What flattening misses

Single-object flattening projects a multi-object event log onto one case identifier, discarding cross-object relations and duplicating shared events (convergence) or splitting linked lifecycles (divergence):

- **Job flattening**: *Visible*: per-job control flow, rework frequency (2/6), and job-level medians. *Hidden/duplicated*: `Quarantine Lot`/`Release Lot` events (excluded by source extraction), off-system overrides (`J2`), tool holds (`FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3`), and the fact that `Batch Cure` (12:05) is one shared event duplicated across `J1`–`J3`. *Misleading conclusion*: Because no job exceeds 1.8 SD above the six-job mean and `Pack -> Dispatch` has a 1.1 h median, managers conclude performance is broadly uniform—missing that *every* job is delayed by different coupled bottlenecks, inflating the mean/SD and masking `J1`’s 5 h 55 min post-pack wait.
- **Machine flattening**: *Visible*: utilization (44%–58%), standard sequences (`Setup -> Machine Process -> Release`), and inter-event gaps (39–73 min). *Hidden/duplicated*: waiting jobs queued off-machine (`J4` at `CNC-2`, `J1`–`J2` for `OVEN-1`, `J6` at `TEST-1`), tool unavailability (`FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3`), and lot holds. *Misleading conclusion*: Moderate utilization and short event gaps (e.g., `TEST-1`’s 39-min max gap while `CAL-3` recalibrates for 45 min) imply ample free capacity and no scheduling problem.
- **Batch flattening**: *Visible*: batch execution cadence (`B1` at 12:05, `B2` at 14:50) and machine/rack pairing. *Hidden/duplicated*: individual job readiness timestamps (08:10, 08:25, 13:10), `J6` (unbatched), upstream tool/lot causes of late arrivals (`J3`, `J5`), and downstream shipment waits. *Misleading conclusion*: A clean `Load -> Cure -> Unload` flow with only two batches makes batching look low-frequency and low-impact, hiding 555+ min of job waiting inside `B1` and `B2`.
- **Tool flattening**: *Visible*: acquisition/release intervals (`FIX-A` 09:00–11:40), `RACK-7` quarantine (until 14:35), and `CAL-3` recalibration (16:35–17:20). *Hidden/duplicated*: unlogged demand (`J4` arriving at 09:50, `J6` at 16:05), machine idleness caused by tool outages, and shipment/batch ripple effects. *Misleading conclusion*: Low tool interaction counts (`Batch–Tool` = 2) or routine 45-min calibration look operationally benign because blocked jobs never appear in the tool’s trace.
- **ComponentLot flattening**: *Visible*: `L7`’s 210-min quarantine (10:20–13:50) and component membership (`C2`, `C5`; `L8`’s `C1`, `C3`). *Hidden/duplicated*: `J2`’s off-system override into `B1`, `C4`/`C6` (unassigned to lots), and cross-job delays imposed by `J5` on `J4` (`B2`) and `J6` (`TEST-1`). *Misleading conclusion*: `L7` appears to hold `C2` and `C5` symmetrically for 3.5 h, hiding both the compliance breach on `J2` and the asymmetric operational delay on `J5` and `J4`.
- **Shipment flattening**: *Visible*: `Create Shipment -> Load` (0.8 h median) and `Load -> Dispatch` (0.3 h median) across `S1`–`S3`. *Hidden/duplicated*: when constituent jobs actually finished packing (`J1` at 13:30 vs. `J4` at 19:10), duplicating shipment dispatch across jobs only if rejoined. *Misleading conclusion*: Logistics appears fast (1.1 h total), completely concealing `J1`’s 5 h 55 min consolidation wait.

**Why low machine utilization coexists with long job waiting**: Utilization measures only primary-machine busy time, whereas flow time depends on **multi-resource conjunction and synchronization**. First, *secondary-resource gating* prevents an idle machine from starting a waiting job (`CNC-2` idle 10:05–10:47 without `FIX-A`; `OVEN-1` idle 12:55–14:50 without `RACK-7`; `TEST-1` idle 16:35–17:25 without `CAL-3`). Second, *synchronization starvation* holds early jobs off an idle machine until the slowest partner or lot release clears (`B1`, `B2`, `L7`). Third, *post-machine consolidation* (`S1`) adds nearly 6 hours of job waiting without consuming a single minute of machine capacity.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

### Narrative A: The `J3`–`FIX-A`–`J4`–`B2`–`S1`–`J1` double-hit cascade (5 types: Job, Tool, Machine, Batch, Shipment)
- **Temporal precedence**: `J1` (08:10) and `J2` (08:25) become ready for `B1`. `J3` acquires `FIX-A` (09:00), `J4` reaches `CNC-2` (09:50), `CNC-2` is idle (10:05–10:47), `J3` releases `FIX-A` (11:40), `J4` starts `Machine Process` (11:45), `J3` reaches `B1` readiness (11:55), and `B1` starts (12:05). `J1` packs at 13:30; `J4` reaches `B2` readiness at 13:10, cures in `B2` at 14:50, packs at 19:10, and `S1` (`J1`+`J4`) dispatches at 19:25.
- **Resource feasibility**: `FIX-A` is an exclusive-use fixture required by `J4`’s `Machine Process` on `CNC-2`; `J4` could not legally execute on `CNC-2` during 09:50–11:40 if `J3` physically retained `FIX-A`. `OVEN-1` and `RACK-7` cannot run `B2` simultaneously with `B1`.
- **Policy constraints**: `B1` enforces all-members-ready synchronization (`{J1, J2, J3}`), `B2` synchronizes `{J4, J5}`, and `S1` enforces consolidated dispatch of `{J1, J4}` across batch boundaries.
- **Causal speculation**: It is plausible—but unproven—that `J3`’s long `FIX-A` hold caused *both* `J3`’s late `B1` readiness (delaying `J1` by ~210 min) and `J4`’s `CNC-2` wait (pushing `J4` into `B2` and delaying `J1` again in `S1` by ~340 min). Unlogged rework on `J4` after `B2` (14:5019:10) or another job on `CNC-2` (10:47–11:45) could account for part of `J4`’s lateness.

### Narrative B: The `L7` quarantine split, off-system override, and `RACK-7`/`B2` stall (6 types: ComponentLot, Component, Job, Batch, Tool, Machine)
- **Temporal precedence**: `J2` (`C2`  `L7`) is ready for `B1` at 08:25. `L7` (`C2`, `C5`) is quarantined at 10:20. `J2` enters `B1` on `OVEN-1`/`RACK-7` at 12:05 (off-system override). `OVEN-1` becomes idle at 12:55; `J4` is ready for `B2` at 13:10; `L7` is released at 13:50; `RACK-7` finishes “cleaning/quarantine” at 14:35; `J5` (`C5`  `L7`) is ready at 14:40; `B2` starts at 14:50.
- **Resource feasibility**: `B2` physically requires `OVEN-1` and `RACK-7`; while `RACK-7` is in cleaning/quarantine until 14:35, `B2` cannot load onto idle `OVEN-1`.
- **Policy constraints**: Quality policy halts components in a quarantined lot (`L7`), governing `J5`, whereas an ad-hoc supervisory override bypassed this policy for `J2`. `B2`’s batching rule additionally prohibited curing `J4` ahead of `J5`.
- **Causal speculation**: Processing quarantined `C2` (`J2`) on `RACK-7` during `B1` (12:05–12:55) may have *triggered* `RACK-7`’s subsequent quarantine/cleaning until 14:35, converting a local override into a downstream tool outage for `B2`—alternatively, `RACK-7` cleaning could be routine post-cure maintenance unrelated to `L7`. Similarly, `J5`’s 14:40 readiness may reflect `L7`’s 13:50 release plus 50 min of machining, or an independent upstream delay.

### Narrative C: Upstream `L7`/`B2` lag feeding `TEST-1`/`CAL-3` contention and `S3` (6 types: ComponentLot, Batch, Job, Machine, Tool, Shipment)
- **Temporal precedence**: `L7` releases at 13:50  `J5` ready for `B2` at 14:40  `B2` starts at 14:50  `J5` ready for `Final Test` at 16:00, `J6` at 16:05  `J5` tests on `TEST-1`/`CAL-3` (16:10–16:35)  `CAL-3` recalibrates (16:35–17:20) while `TEST-1` is idle (16:35–17:25)  `J6` tests (17:25–17:50)  `S3` (`J5`+`J6`) dispatches 35 min after its last job packs.
- **Resource feasibility**: `Final Test` requires simultaneous availability of `TEST-1` and `CAL-3`; `J5` and `J6` cannot test concurrently, and `J6` cannot run while `CAL-3` is undergoing recalibration.
- **Policy constraints**: `TEST-1` appears to dispatch FIFO (`J5` at 16:00 ahead of `J6` at 16:05), metrology policy requires `CAL-3` recalibration, and `S3` consolidates `J5` and `J6`.
- **Causal speculation**: Had `L7` not held `J5` until 13:50 (and `B2` until 14:50), `J5` might have cleared `TEST-1` earlier, avoiding the 16:00/16:05 collision with `J6`—or `CAL-3`’s recalibration might still have been triggered by test-count thresholds regardless of arrival order. Because `J6` finishes testing last (17:50), `CAL-3`’s delay likely extended `J5`’s post-pack wait inside `S3` as well.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

1. **Time-boxed dynamic batching for `OVEN-1` (Synchronization intervention)**
   - *Target*: Batch synchronization barriers (`B1`, `B2`).
   - *Expected benefit*: Caps pre-batch wait (e.g., 60-min max wait after first ready job), saving ~150–175 min each for `J1`/`J2` in `B1`.
   - *Helped*: Early-ready jobs (`J1`, `J2`, `J4`) and downstream shipment partners (`S1`, `S2`).
   - *Potentially harmed*: Late jobs (`J3`, `J5`) pushed into extra solo/partial cures; `OVEN-1` energy/capacity if cure runs increase from 2 to 3–4; `RACK-7` contention if cleaning cycles multiply.
   - *New cost/risk*: Higher oven energy cost, increased `OVEN-1` utilization (44%65–75%), and tighter `RACK-7` turnaround.
   - *Perverse metric*: "Minimum batch fill rate" (re-creates 4-hour waits) or "zero pre-batch wait" (triggers single-piece runs that saturate `OVEN-1`).
   - *OCEL evaluation*: Track `Job` pre-batch wait distribution alongside `OVEN-1` utilization, `RACK-7` queue overlap, and `Batch` count per shift via multi-object time-series A/B trials across shifts.

2. **Redundant secondary tooling & hot-swap calibration (`FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3`) (Tool intervention)**
   - *Target*: Shared-tool contention and tool outages.
   - *Expected benefit*: Duplicate `FIX-A` and `RACK-7` plus a hot-swappable `CAL-3b` eliminate secondary-resource blocking while primary machines sit idle (saving up to ~95 min for `J4` on `CNC-2`, ~45 min for `J6` on `TEST-1`, and preventing `RACK-7` from gating `B2`).
   - *Helped*: `J4`, `J6`, `B2`, and indirectly `J1` (`S1`) and `J5` (`S3`).
   - *Potentially harmed*: `TEST-1` and `CNC-2` may experience higher peak arrival bursts once upstream tool gates open, shifting queues onto machines or inspection/rework.
   - *New cost/risk*: Capex and calibration/maintenance overhead; duplicate tooling can mask root causes of long holds (e.g., why `J3` held `FIX-A` 160 min).
   - *Perverse metric*: "Tool utilization %" (encourages hoarding or under-provisioning spares).
   - *OCEL evaluation*: Measure co-occurrence of `[Machine idle  Job ready  Tool busy/down]` states before and after adding spares, checking whether waiting shifts to `Inspect`/`Rework` or `Machine` queues.

3. **Batch-aligned or split-shipment consolidation policy (`S1`–`S3`) (Shipment policy intervention)**
   - *Target*: Shipment consolidation barriers.
   - *Expected benefit*: Aligning shipment grouping with batch membership (or allowing split dispatch past a wait threshold, e.g., 90 min) cuts `J1`’s 355-min post-pack wait by ~250–340 min.
   - *Helped*: Early-packed jobs (`J1`) and customers needing fast partial delivery.
   - *Potentially harmed*: Receiving docks facing multiple deliveries; freight budget; late partners (`J4`) if solo freight costs trigger delayed dispatch.
   - *New cost/risk*: Higher transport cost, extra `Create/Load` admin work, and potential SLA breach if customers require complete-order shipments.
   - *Perverse metric*: "Freight cost per shipment" (drives extreme consolidation) or "On-time first-item dispatch" (fragments every order unnecessarily).
   - *OCEL evaluation*: Join `Job` (`Pack`) and `Shipment` (`Dispatch`) lifecycles to evaluate the Pareto frontier of `Pack->Dispatch` job waiting vs. shipments-per-job and freight cost.

4. **Tiered component-lot triage & governed override workflow (`L7`, `L8`) (Lot-governance intervention)**
   - *Target*: Component-lot release coupling and unlogged overrides.
   - *Expected benefit*: Rapid component-level sub-lot disposition isolates defective items without freezing whole lots for 210 min, while formalizing overrides prevents contaminated parts from triggering `RACK-7` quarantine.
   - *Helped*: `J5` (avoiding late `B2`/`TEST-1` cascade), `J4` (`B2`), `J6` (`TEST-1`), and `RACK-7` availability.
   - *Potentially harmed*: Quality-engineering workload; jobs previously expedited via informal overrides (`J2`) may wait longer while formal checks complete.
   - *New cost/risk*: Faster triage risks false-negative releases (escaped defects) and higher rework downstream.
   - *Perverse metric*: "Average lot release time" (pressures QA to rubber-stamp releases, raising field defects/rework).
   - *OCEL evaluation*: Track `ComponentLot` hold duration jointly with downstream `Job` rework rate, `Tool` quarantine events (`RACK-7`), and override compliance rate.

5. **Unified OCEL telemetry & event-schema upgrade (Logging-only intervention)**
   - *Target*: Data-integration and visibility artifacts.
   - *Expected benefit*: Adds explicit `Ready for Resource`, `Wait for Tool`, `Tool Status Change`, and `Override Approved` events, links `ComponentLot` events to `Job`/`Component` at ingestion, and links `Pack` to `Shipment`. Improves diagnosis without touching physical rules.
   - *Helped*: Production planners, QA auditors, and process analysts diagnosing future delays across all six jobs.
   - *Potentially harmed*: Shop-floor operators and supervisors if manual barcode/terminal scans add friction and slow handoffs.
   - *New cost/risk*: IT integration cost, sensor/RFID investment, and alert fatigue or gaming if operators delay scanning "Ready" to hide wait times.
   - *Perverse metric*: "Logged wait time" (incentivizes delayed `Ready` scans rather than genuine wait reduction).
   - *OCEL evaluation*: Audit timestamp completeness, reduction in unexplained idle-machine/ready-job intervals, and cross-system override reconciliation (`Job–Lot` E2O linkage rate approaching 100%).

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline

The script below inspects object-centric interactions, compares multiple OC-DFG frequency metrics (`event_couples`, `unique_objects`, `total_objects`), contrasts machine-flattened gaps against cross-object readiness and tool/shipment intervals, and extracts object-centric features.

```python
import pandas as pd
import networkx as nx
import pm4py

# Assumes `ocel` is a loaded pm4py OCEL 2.0 object containing types:
# Job, Component, ComponentLot, Machine, Tool, Batch, Shipment

# 1. Cross-object interaction structure & type-level schemas
interactions_df = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
obj_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel, graph_type="object_interaction")
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)          # Object-type interaction graph
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)        # Event-type to object-type relations
ocel_nx = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(ocel)

print("Top object-type pair interactions:")
print(interactions_df.head(10))
print("Object-Type Graph edges:", list(otg.keys()) if isinstance(otg, dict) else otg)
print("Event-Type to Object-Type mapping sample:", list(etot.items())[:8] if isinstance(etot, dict) else etot)

# Identify higher-order events touching >= 3 distinct object types
rel = ocel.relations.copy()
ev_type_diversity = (
    rel.groupby(["ocel:eid", "ocel:activity"])["ocel:type"]
    .nunique()
    .reset_index(name="n_object_types")
)
higher_order_events = ev_type_diversity[ev_type_diversity["n_object_types"] >= 3]
print("Higher-order synchronization events (>=3 object types):")
print(higher_order_events.sort_values("n_object_types", ascending=False).head(10))

# 2. Discover OC-DFG and compare >=2 object-centric frequency metrics
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
edges_block = ocdfg.get("edges", {})
event_couples = edges_block.get("event_couples", {})
unique_objects = edges_block.get("unique_objects", {})
total_objects = edges_block.get("total_objects", {})

freq_rows = []
for ot in ocel.objects["ocel:type"].unique():
    ec_ot = event_couples.get(ot, {})
    uo_ot = unique_objects.get(ot, {})
    to_ot = total_objects.get(ot, {})
    for edge in set(ec_ot) | set(uo_ot) | set(to_ot):
        ec_val = len(ec_ot.get(edge, ())) if hasattr(ec_ot.get(edge, 0), "__len__") else ec_ot.get(edge, 0)
        uo_val = len(uo_ot.get(edge, ())) if hasattr(uo_ot.get(edge, 0), "__len__") else uo_ot.get(edge, 0)
        to_val = len(to_ot.get(edge, ())) if hasattr(to_ot.get(edge, 0), "__len__") else to_ot.get(edge, 0)
        freq_rows.append({
            "object_type": ot,
            "edge": f"{edge[0]} -> {edge[1]}",
            "event_couples": ec_val,
            "unique_objects": uo_val,
            "total_objects": to_val,
            "divergence_ratio": round(to_val / max(uo_val, 1), 2),
        })

freq_comparison = pd.DataFrame(freq_rows).sort_values(
    ["object_type", "event_couples"], ascending=[True, False]
)
print("OC-DFG frequency metric comparison (exposes batch convergence & rework loops):")
print(freq_comparison.head(15))

# 3. Contrast flattened performance DFGs across viewpoints
# IMPORTANT: Do NOT equate machine-event gaps with job waiting.
perf_summaries = {}
for ot in ["Job", "Machine", "Batch", "Tool", "ComponentLot", "Shipment"]:
    flat_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, ot)
    perf_dfg, start_act, end_act = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(flat_log)
    perf_summaries[ot] = {edge: vals.get("median", vals) if isinstance(vals, dict) else vals
                          for edge, vals in perf_dfg.items()}
    print(f"\nFlattened performance DFG ({ot}) sample edges:", list(perf_summaries[ot].items())[:5])

# 4. Cross-object waiting reconstruction (beyond machine-event gaps)
# Example A: Pack -> Dispatch waiting per Job via shared Shipment events
job_rel = rel[rel["ocel:type"] == "Job"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp"]]
ship_rel = rel[rel["ocel:type"] == "Shipment"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp"]]

pack_times = (
    job_rel[job_rel["ocel:activity"] == "Pack"]
    .groupby("ocel:oid")["ocel:timestamp"].max()
    .rename("pack_ts")
)
dispatch_links = (
    job_rel[job_rel["ocel:activity"] == "Dispatch"]
    .merge(ship_rel[["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid"]], on="ocel:eid", suffixes=("_job", "_ship"))
    .groupby("ocel:oid_job")["ocel:timestamp"].min()
    .rename("dispatch_ts")
)
shipment_wait = pd.concat([pack_times, dispatch_links], axis=1).dropna()
shipment_wait["consolidation_wait_min"] = (
    (shipment_wait["dispatch_ts"] - shipment_wait["pack_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
)
print("\nCross-object Job Pack-to-Shipment-Dispatch waits (min):")
print(shipment_wait)

# Example B: Tool-overlap / Machine-idle cross-check using NetworkX ego paths
# Checks whether a job's delay between readiness and Machine Process overlaps another job's Tool hold
tool_rel = rel[rel["ocel:type"] == "Tool"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp"]]
tool_holds = (
    tool_rel.sort_values("ocel:timestamp")
    .groupby("ocel:oid")["ocel:timestamp"]
    .agg(["min", "max", "count"])
)
print("\nTool event span summary (contextualized against job readiness, not machine gaps alone):")
print(tool_holds)

# Shortest multi-type dependency path between coupled jobs (e.g., J3 -> J4 -> J1)
if "J3" in ocel_nx and "J1" in ocel_nx:
    undir_nx = ocel_nx.to_undirected()
    print("Shortest object-event path J3 <-> J1:", nx.shortest_path(undir_nx, source="J3", target="J1"))

# 5. Extract object-centric features for Job-level delay attribution modeling
job_features, job_feature_names = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, "Job")
feat_df = pd.DataFrame(job_features, columns=job_feature_names)
print("\nExtracted Job OCEL feature matrix shape:", feat_df.shape)
print("Sample feature names:", job_feature_names[:10])
```

## 8. Falsification and additional data

### Six observations that would weaken the top-ranked explanation (Batch synchronization)

1. **Inaccurate readiness timestamps**: If `J1` (08:10) and `J2` (08:25) were only *administratively* released for `B1` while physically undergoing rework, kitting, or transport until ~11:45, then their 235-min and 220-min synchronization waits would collapse.
2. **Pre-12:05 `OVEN-1` or `RACK-7` unavailability**: If `OVEN-1` was running an unlogged maintenance/warm-up cycle or an unrecorded job (`J0`) from 08:00 to 12:00, `B1` could not have started earlier even if `J1` and `J2` had run solo.
3. **Mandatory minimum-load thermal physics**: If curing fewer than three jobs in `OVEN-1` causes thermal-profile failure (making solo or 2-job runs physically infeasible), then `B1`’s wait is a process-physics requirement rather than an avoidable batching policy bottleneck.
4. **Fixed-time carrier cutoff for `S1`**: If `S1`’s carrier only departs once daily at 19:25 regardless of when `J1` or `J4` finishes, then `B1`’s delay on `J1` had zero effect on final customer delivery, shifting priority away from batch synchronization toward carrier scheduling.
