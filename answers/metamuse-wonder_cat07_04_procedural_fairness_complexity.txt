# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness is not a single metric. Raw outcome differences are a starting point for investigation, not a diagnosis.

## Outcome parity
Raw approval rates differ markedly: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, Group D 58% — gaps of -15, -9, and -20 percentage points vs. A. Median cycle times are 8.1 days for A vs. 13.7 (B), 15.9 (C), and 12.1 (D), with 90th percentiles of 21.4 vs. 34.2, 38.8, and 29.5 days. Appeal rates are 6% vs. 13%, 10%, 16%, and rework 11% vs. 22%, 24%, 20%. Parity alone would flag concern, but groups differ substantially in documented complexity, so parity is neither required for fairness nor sufficient proof of unfairness.

## Adjusted outcome disparity
After controlling for risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior cases, region, and month, gaps shrink but do not disappear: approval -5.5pp for B, -2.2pp for C, -3.1pp for D. This attenuation shows case mix explains much of the raw gap. The residual is a *disparity conditional on measured covariates*, not yet proven discrimination — it could reflect unmeasured complexity, measurement bias in the controls themselves, or differential treatment.

## Procedural burden
Beyond outcomes: how many steps, requests, handoffs, and re-contacts must an applicant endure? Group B has +9.5pp adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents and Group C +4.2pp; B and C are far less likely to follow the short variant V1 (19% and 22% vs. 42% for A). Burden includes applicant effort — resubmitting documents, repeating information after handovers — which the log records only partially.

## Timeliness
Timeliness is both total cycle time and *where* waiting occurs. Adjusted cycle time remains +2.6 days (B), +4.1 days (C), +0.9 days (D). Waiting concentrates at support and review steps: Language Support Contact  Eligibility 3.8 days for B; Accommodation Review  Eligibility 4.7 days for C; Completeness  Request Missing Documents 1.6 vs. 0.4 days.

## Consistency of routing
Are like cases routed alike? Group B has +8.0pp and C +5.5pp adjusted probability of Supervisor Review, and D is heavily routed to Fraud Review (28% in V3 vs. 9% for A). Some routing is policy-mandated, but supervisor review inflation for B/C after adjustment questions whether routing rules are applied consistently or whether uncertainty about unfamiliar cases triggers defensive escalation.

## Quality/accessibility of support
Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review are rights-preserving: they exist to make the process accessible. Fairness asks whether they are *effective* — timely, competent, dignity-preserving — not merely present. A support step that adds 3.8–4.7 days of waiting and extra handovers may formally satisfy a right while substantively penalizing its exercise.

## Appeal/rework burden
Appeals are both a remedy and a burden. Adjusted appeal probability is +4.4pp (B) and +5.7pp (D), and V6 (appeal path) covers 8% of B and 13% of D vs. 4% of A. Higher appeals could mean worse initial decisions, poorer explanations, or greater applicant capacity to appeal — direction is ambiguous without appeal *outcomes*.

## Resource-allocation fairness
Group A is spread across 52 caseworkers; B concentrates on 7 bilingual workers plus 3 overloaded regional officers; C on 5 accommodation specialists; waits for Supervisor Review differ by group (1.0d A vs. 1.9d B). Even with neutral per-case decisions, under-resourcing the queues that specific groups must use is a structural allocation choice with disparate impact.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate four claims: (i) observed disparity, (ii) disparity explained by legitimate need, (iii) disparity consistent with differential treatment, and (iv) residual uncertainty.

## Matching or stratification variables
Stratify/match cases on pre-routing, plausibly legitimate need variables: `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, month, and requested support (language, accommodation). Compare groups *within* strata (e.g., online, complete documentation, medium risk, same region) using standardized rates or propensity/overlap weighting. Report common support: if few Group A cases need language support, that contrast is extrapolation, not evidence.

Crucially, run three model tiers: (1) demographics/context only; (2) plus channel and region; (3) plus risk and documentation. If a gap appears only after adding tier-3 variables, those variables may be mediating or masking disparity rather than explaining it.

## Path/variant comparison
Do not compare only endpoints. Compare variant shares and within-variant outcomes: for cases on the *same* variant (e.g., V2), do approval, waiting, and appeal still differ by group? If B and A on V1 have similar times and approvals, disparity is mainly *routing into* longer paths; if they differ within V1, disparity also operates *inside* the path. Both matter, but remedies differ.

## Resource-assignment comparison
Compare distribution of `org:resource` per group: concentration (share handled by top-k workers), handover counts, and whether assignment predicts waiting conditional on activity and region. Test whether B/C cases assigned to overloaded specialists wait longer than comparable cases assigned elsewhere.

## Waiting-time decomposition
Decompose cycle time into service time vs. waiting time per transition. Attribute the adjusted total gap (+2.6d B, +4.1d C) to specific edges: e.g., how much of C's gap is Accommodation Review  Eligibility (4.7d) vs. Supervisor Review  Decision (1.6 vs. 1.0d)? This distinguishes "longer because more steps" from "slower at the same steps."

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates
`risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may encode historical disparities or language barriers. Repeat adjustment: excluding them; including them; and treating them as *mediators* (report total effect without them vs. direct effect with them). If B's approval gap widens when documentation is excluded, documentation is on the causal pathway — controlling for it understates unfairness. Also simulate an unmeasured confounder: how strong would it need to be to erase -5.5pp?

## Subgroup checks by region and channel
Estimate group gaps within region × channel cells. The notes flag handovers and supervisor backlogs in two regions and longer waits for in-person applications. If B's disadvantage is concentrated in two regions or the in-person channel, it is a local capacity/routing problem; if uniform, it is systemic. Check interactions, not just main effects.

Throughout, report uncertainty (confidence intervals, cell sizes) and pre-register which differences would count as materially unfair, to avoid post-hoc rationalization in either direction.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## What the variants show
The simple path V1 covers 42% of A but only 19% (B), 22% (C), 18% (D). B is channeled into V4 (language support, 24% vs. 1% for A, 19.2 days) and V2 (document requests, 34%, 14.9 days). C is channeled into V5 (accommodation review, 31% vs. 2%, 21.6 days — the slowest non-appeal variant). D is channeled into V3 (fraud review, 28% vs. 9%, 18.4 days) and V6 (appeals, 13%, 30.7 days).

## Potentially justified by complexity
D's fraud-review routing aligns with its 51% high-risk share (vs. 18% for A), and Risk Screening  Fraud Review waits are similar across groups (2.0–2.8d), suggesting the queue itself treats routed cases comparably. C's accommodation routing aligns with 62% requesting accommodation; B's language routing with 57% needing support. *Existence* of these steps is legitimate and should not be counted against the process per se.

## Remaining concerning
1. **Waiting inside rights-preserving steps.** B waits 3.8 days after Language Support Contact and C 4.7 days after Accommodation Review, with only 7 bilingual and 5 accommodation staff. The burden falls precisely on those exercising a right.
2. **Document-request loop for B.** Completeness  Request Missing Documents wait is 1.6d vs. 0.4d for A, B's V2 share is 34%, and adjusted request probability is +9.5pp — larger than B's incomplete-documentation share alone would predict, and documentation may itself be harder to complete across a language barrier.
3. **Supervisor Review inflation.** +8.0pp (B) and +5.5pp (C) after adjustment, with longer Supervisor  Decision waits (1.9d, 1.6d vs. 1.0d). Legitimate routing rules do not obviously require this.
4. **Handover fragmentation.** B cases bounce general  bilingual  general; C cases general  specialist  supervisor (with two specialists doubling as supervisors, a possible bottleneck and role conflict). Handovers impose repetition and delay not captured in outcome rates.
5. **Appeal concentration.** V6 is 13% of D and 8% of B; adjusted appeal gaps persist (+5.7pp D, +4.4pp B), implying initial decisions or explanations serve these groups worse.

In short: *which* extra steps occur is mostly explainable; *how slow, fragmented, and escalation-prone* those steps are is not yet explained.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity routing.** Higher measured risk legitimately triggers Fraud Review for D; accommodation and language needs legitimately trigger specialist steps for C and B. This explains variant composition and part of cycle time, consistent with D's small adjusted cycle-time gap (+0.9d).

2. **Capacity scarcity in specialized queues.** Seven bilingual workers and five accommodation specialists serve entire groups. Queuing, not animus, produces the 3.8d and 4.7d waits and concentration on 3 overloaded regional officers. This is structural disadvantage: neutral-looking staffing decisions allocate delay by group.

3. **Defensive escalation and unfamiliarity.** Generalist caseworkers facing language barriers, accommodation needs, or communication uncertainty may request documents or supervisor review more often to avoid error — explaining B's +9.5pp document requests and +8.0pp supervisor review after adjustment. Individually cautious, collectively burdensome.

4. **Biased or noisy measurement upstream.** If risk scores are trained on historically skewed enforcement, or completeness is judged against majority-language norms, then "legitimate" routing variables transmit past disparity into current routing: D gets more fraud scrutiny; B gets more document requests — each step procedurally regular given the score, unfair given the score's provenance.

5. **Handover and coordination failure.** Specialist involvement designed as a single consultation becomes sequential ping-pong (general  bilingual  general), restarting queues each time and losing context, inflating waits and rework (22–24% for B/C).

6. **Communication and explanation gaps.** If notifications and requests are poorly translated or inaccessible, applicants submit wrong/incomplete documents and appeal more because they cannot understand or comply — generating B's rework and D/B's appeal patterns without any decision-maker intending harm.

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive; capacity (2) and measurement (4) can compound.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in controls.** `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities in who was investigated or reported; adjusting for it can launder bias into "complexity." `documentation_completeness` may measure fit with majority-language administrative norms, not applicant diligence.
- **Missing applicant needs.** The log lacks severity/type of disability, actual language proficiency, literacy/digital access, income volatility, housing instability, and case substance. Adjusted gaps (e.g., -5.5pp approval for B) may reflect these unobservables — or conceal them.
- **Mediator vs. confounder ambiguity.** Channel (in-person) and documentation may be *consequences* of group circumstances (no internet access, language barriers). Controlling for mediators understates total disparity; not controlling overstates decision bias. The data alone cannot settle which.
- **Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only daily. Caseworker caseload mix, absence, queue priority rules, and part-time status are unseen, so waiting-time attribution to "resource fairness" vs. "case difficulty" is incomplete.
- **Appeal quality unknown.** `appeal_flag` records occurrence, not grounds, representation, success rate, or time-to-resolution. Higher appeals could signal worse first decisions *or* better access to appeal rights.
- **Outcome narrowness.** `case_outcome` omits explanation quality, dignity, correctness of the approval amount, and downstream hardship. A process can be outcome-equal and still procedurally degrading, or slower yet more accurate.
- **Historical/ selection effects.** Who applies, who persists after a document request, and whose prior cases exist (`prior_case_count`) are themselves shaped by past treatment; the log only sees survivors of earlier friction.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** (more bilingual caseworkers, more specialists, cross-training generalists). *Side effects:* cost; hiring lags; risk of diluting expertise if cross-training is shallow; capacity may induce demand as access improves (a desirable but budget-relevant effect).

- **Service-level targets for support steps** (e.g., maximum wait after Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review, monitored like other steps). *Side effects:* queue-jumping may displace other cases; targets can be gamed (cursory contact to stop the clock); targets without capacity create pressure, not speed.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers** (specialist consults embedded in the generalist's case rather than transferring ownership; warm handovers; single case owner). *Side effects:* specialists become bottlenecks in a new form; generalists may under-consult; accountability for support quality can blur.

- **Decision-explanation improvements** (translated, plain-language, accessible notices stating what was missing and why, with a named contact). *Side effects:* drafting/review time per case; translation quality risks; more precise explanations may initially *raise* appeals by making grounds clearer.

- **Monitoring adjusted disparities** (routine dashboards of adjusted approval, waiting-by-transition, routing, and appeal gaps by group × region × channel). *Side effects:* metric fixation; small-cell privacy risks; adjusting for biased covariates in the dashboard can institutionalize a too-comforting baseline.

- **Auditing `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`** (validate against independent outcomes, test error rates by group, review completeness criteria with language-access staff). *Side effects:* recalibration may shift many routings and approvals; removing a predictor can reduce accuracy for genuinely high-risk cases; audit findings may create legal exposure requiring careful governance.

Explicitly not recommended: removing or discouraging Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review to cut cycle time. That would improve a metric by withdrawing a right.

import pm4py

# per group: rerun handover on groups[g]; measure concentration,
# generalist->specialist->generalist ping-pong, specialist load

Analysis workflow:
1. Build case-level table; verify each case has consistent group/region/channel.
2. For each stratum (region × channel × risk × completeness), compute group rates for approval, Supervisor Review, Request Missing Documents, appeal, and median/p90 duration; pool with standardization and report cell sizes.
3. Use performance DFGs to locate where pooled waiting gaps arise; cross-check with `insert_case_service_waiting_time` so slow *handoffs* are not confused with slow *work*.
4. Use handover/roles outputs per group to quantify specialist concentration and ping-pong patterns, joined with resource load.
5. Run log-skeleton/Declare checks per group: if mandated support-before-decision ordering is violated or extra loops occur more for B/C, that is direct procedural evidence.
6. Repeat steps 2–5 (a) without risk/documentation controls and (b) within-channel and within-region, as sensitivity analyses.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does not appear procedurally neutral, but the evidence does not support a conclusion of proven intentional discrimination.

Raw gaps overstate unfairness because case mix genuinely differs, and much routing — fraud review for D, language support for B, accommodation review for C — follows stated, rights-preserving policy. However, disparities persist after adjustment (notably B: -5.5pp approval, +9.5pp document requests, +8.0pp supervisor review; C: +4.1 days), and the burden concentrates exactly where groups must use scarce support services: 3.8-day and 4.7-day waits, concentrated queues of 7 bilingual and 5 specialist staff, extra handovers, and higher appeal/rework loads. That pattern is consistent with structural procedural disadvantage — formally equal rules operating through unequally resourced pathways — compounded possibly by biased risk/documentation measures and defensive escalation, which the current attributes cannot rule in or out.

Calibrated verdict: **indeterminate as to discriminatory intent or individual decision bias; concerning and partially evidenced as to procedural unfairness in timeliness, burden, and resource allocation for Groups B and C, with Group D's treatment contingent on the validity of the risk score — which itself requires audit before D's routing can be called legitimate.**
