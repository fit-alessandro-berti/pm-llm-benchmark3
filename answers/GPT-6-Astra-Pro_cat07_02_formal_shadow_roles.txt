# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

I would distinguish **workload centrality**, **role-defining responsibility**, and **decision authority**. They need not coincide. Two complementary measures are useful:

$$
\text{Within-resource activity share}_{r,a}
=\frac{n_{r,a}}{\sum_b n_{r,b}},
\qquad
\text{Resource contribution to activity}_{r,a}
=\frac{n_{r,a}}{\sum_s n_{s,a}}.
$$

The first identifies what occupies a resource’s recorded work; the second identifies concentration of a capability. Neither establishes authority, and neither accounts for task duration or complexity.

**Automated intake.** A1 and A2 are Nora’s entire recorded scope. Nevertheless, “Validate Entitlement” should not be assumed to have the same meaning for Nora and a human: the roster calls Nora’s activity a precheck, and one fragment includes human validation after automated registration. The proposed core is therefore automated screening and intake, not general entitlement accountability.  

**Experienced front office.** Registration, validation, standard response and closure form the routine core. Diagnosis, negotiation and specialist interaction are peripheral capabilities for Amira and Ben; Giorgio’s commercial activity is substantial enough to warrant secondary membership rather than being dismissed as occasional noise. Their shared front-office role does not imply equal exposure to difficult cases.  

**Bounded-scope routine service.** Kai and Lina’s core resembles front-office work at the label level, but their observed case mix and specialist involvement are much narrower. This supports a scoped execution role, not a conclusion that temporary workers are inherently less capable. The incomplete entitlement notes suggest a need to validate documentation practice and supervision, rather than simply counting completed requests.   

**Technical delivery.** A3, A5 and A10 define the shared core. Calculated from the supplied case table, high/critical cases represent **222/527 ≈ 42.1%** of Chen’s handled cases versus **91/491 ≈ 18.5%** of Dina’s. This supports a complexity-sensitive assignment distinction, but not an established competence ranking: their usual shifts differ, and the prompt provides no matched-case performance comparison. Security-adjacent activity remains peripheral unless its responsibilities and authorization are verified.  

**Security assurance.** Erik contributes only **170 of 10,534 recorded events, approximately 1.6%**, but performs **96 of 146 security reviews, approximately 65.8%**. That contrast illustrates why event volume is a poor measure of organizational importance. His other activities plausibly support security resolution, rather than establishing a general technical-delivery role. These percentages are calculations from the displayed activity matrix. 

**Commercial exception resolution.** Pricing review and negotiation are Fatima’s core and Giorgio’s secondary specialization. A9 participation should remain separate from supervisory authority: a commercial endorsement, preliminary approval and final supervisory decision may share a coarse activity label. The source does not specify which interpretation applies to Fatima’s or Giorgio’s A9 events. 

**Supervisory oversight.** Hana’s core is decision oversight, not merely producing A9 records. Her subsequent review of Mateo’s preliminary approvals supports an important distinction in decision finality. It does not establish that every Hana approval is final or that every other performer lacks delegated authority; the authorization policy is absent. 

**Quality and knowledge stewardship.** Quality checks and knowledge updates form a coherent dual core: checking current work and maintaining reusable knowledge. Implementation and configuration participation may reflect troubleshooting or corrective feedback. Whether a quality check must be independent of implementation on the same case is a policy question, not something established by activity counts. 

**Escalation coordination.** Mateo’s core is defined more by **where and why he participates** than by his highest activity count. Closing, validating or reviewing a case can be an interface action within escalation recovery. Reopening and specialist-routing fragments make a coordination interpretation more plausible than treating every activity he touches as a separate specialist qualification.  

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

### Giorgio: a commercial shadow role that probably extends beyond absence cover

The explicit substitution episode demonstrates operational coverage: during Fatima’s six-day absence, Giorgio performed 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations. **Assuming those episode counts are included in the ten-week matrix**, that leaves **41 pricing reviews and 70 negotiations outside the stated absence episode**. His commercial involvement therefore appears broader than a single temporary replacement, consistent with the note that he shadows pricing on complex front-office cases. It does not establish that all remaining events occurred under ordinary conditions.   

The benefit is continuity and reduced dependence on a single commercial specialist. The risks include uncertain decision limits and competing front-office responsibilities. The reported median pricing duration increased from 38 to 74 minutes—approximately 95%—but this is **not a causal estimate of Giorgio’s relative efficiency**. Case selection, workload, absence-related congestion and the meaning of “duration” are unresolved. I would formalize the useful overlap through verified competence, explicit delegation and protected coverage capacity, rather than either suppressing it or assuming full interchangeability. 

### Mateo: an escalation broker with a provisional approval function

Mateo bridges reopened cases, specialists and supervisory decisions. This is evidence of a coordination function, but the supplied fragments do not establish a measured centrality score or a formal command relationship. 

During Hana’s workshop, **13 of 21 preliminary approvals were rechecked, approximately 61.9%**. That is a recheck proportion, **not an error or reversal rate**. Rechecking could reflect an intended provisional-approval arrangement, quality assurance or concern about particular cases; the summary does not distinguish these explanations. 

His security-referral filtering also has mixed implications: fewer false-escalation complaints accompanied two critical cases waiting more than two days. The evidence supports investigating the triage mechanism, not concluding that filtering is either categorically beneficial or the proven cause of the delays. I would preserve coordination while adding explicit urgency criteria, escalation timers and a route to qualified security review when triage is uncertain. 

### Erik: a rare specialist embedded in a cross-functional process

Erik is a formal security specialist, so the “shadow” element is not his title; it is the organizational dependency that a frequency-only model may hide. **137 of his 166 listed handled cases are high or critical, approximately 82.5%.** His value is consequently associated with difficult interventions, not throughput volume alone. 

The observed roster suggests a **potential single-qualified-person dependency** because Erik is the only resource explicitly listed with security certification. However, the summary does not prove that no qualified backup exists outside the roster or that every A6 event requires that certification. Chen’s, Dina’s, Ivan’s and Mateo’s A6 participation should trigger a scope review, not automatic promotion into an equivalent security role.  

Two additional boundaries deserve attention: **quality-to-technical feedback**, where checking and implementation support overlap, and **automation-to-human validation**, where identical labels may hide different responsibilities. These are potentially valuable interfaces, not evidence that shadow work is improper.

### Data limitations that affect confidence

**The activity vocabulary is not fully aligned.** “Coordinate” appears in the fragments but is absent from A1–A14 and the activity matrix. It may be an omitted activity, a higher-level description or an alias. I would preserve that uncertainty rather than silently map it to A9 or another activity. Other shortened fragment labels also need an agreed mapping before implementation.  

**Some summary denominators need reconciliation.** The matrix totals 245 events for Hana and 415 for Mateo, whereas their complexity rows total 274 and 429 handled cases. If these are distinct cases evidenced by those same events in the same window, the counts cannot all have that interpretation. Different coverage, definitions or omitted activities could explain the discrepancy, but the source does not say which. Also, resource-level case counts must not be summed into a unique process-wide case total because resources can share cases.  

**The supplied similarity scores need provenance.** Ordinary cosine applied to the displayed activity vectors gives approximately **0.998 for Chen–Dina** and **0.505 for Fatima–Giorgio**, rather than the reported 0.94 and 0.71. Row-wise normalization alone does not explain that difference. I retain the supplied scores as reported summaries, but would request their weighting, preprocessing and window definitions before using numerical thresholds. The proposed roles do not depend on treating those scores as exact or as membership probabilities.  

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The original event-level log is needed to discover actual roles and networks. The summary cannot reconstruct case-level handovers, co-participation or substitution-conditioned behavior. **No raw-log discovery results are claimed here.**

The implementation below assumes that `case_complexity` is a numeric, case-level score; timestamps are UTC or have explicit offsets; and substitution flags describe individual events. It rejects changing within-case complexity rather than silently choosing an aggregation. Missing substitution flags remain unknown.

PM4Py’s role-discovery interface returns role objects with activity sets and resource importance information; it does not supply a formal authorization matrix. The inspected activity-similarity implementation uses **Pearson correlation**, so the example calculates **cosine separately**. Its social-network results expose connections and a directedness indicator. These implementation details were checked against the official source; the organizational conclusions above remain grounded in the supplied file.

```python
from __future__ import annotations

import warnings
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

C = "case:concept:name"
A = "concept:name"
T = "time:timestamp"
R = "org:resource"
K = "case_complexity"
S = "temporary_substitution"

KEYS = dict(
    activity_key=A, resource_key=R,
    timestamp_key=T, case_id_key=C
)
NET_KEYS = {k: v for k, v in KEYS.items() if k != "activity_key"}


def edge_table(network) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # These weights are algorithm scores, not necessarily raw counts.
    return pd.DataFrame(
        [(u, v, w) for (u, v), w in network.connections.items()],
        columns=["source", "target", "weight"],
    ).assign(directed=network.is_directed)


def role_context(events: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(events, **KEYS)

    memberships = pd.DataFrame([
        {
            "mined_role": i,
            R: resource,
            "activities": tuple(role.activities),
            "mined_weight": weight,
        }
        for i, role in enumerate(roles)
        for resource, weight in role.originator_importance.items()
    ])
    if memberships.empty:
        return memberships

    # Match BOTH resource and activity. A person's entire workload must
    # not be attributed to every role associated with that person.
    allocation = (
        memberships[["mined_role", R, "activities"]]
        .explode("activities")
        .rename(columns={"activities": A})
    )
    used = allocation.merge(events, on=[R, A], how="inner")
    group = ["mined_role", R]

    volume = used.groupby(group).agg(
        events=(A, "size"),
        substitution_events=(
            "_sub", lambda s: s.eq("substitution").sum()
        ),
        unknown_substitution_events=(
            "_sub", lambda s: s.eq("unknown").sum()
        ),
    ).reset_index()

    # Complexity is case-weighted, not event-weighted.
    mix = (
        used.drop_duplicates(group + [C])
        .groupby(group)
        .agg(
            cases=(C, "nunique"),
            scored_cases=(K, "count"),
            median_case_complexity=(K, "median"),
        )
        .reset_index()
    )

    report = memberships.merge(volume, on=group).merge(mix, on=group)
    known = report["events"] - report["unknown_substitution_events"]
    report["substitution_share_among_known"] = (
        report["substitution_events"] / known.replace(0, np.nan)
    )
    report["formal_authority"] = (
        "not inferred; verify against policy/delegations"
    )
    return report


def analyze(path: str) -> dict:
    # Preserve identifiers, including any leading zeros.
    df = pd.read_csv(path, dtype={C: "string", A: "string", R: "string"})

    missing = {C, A, T, R, K, S} - set(df.columns)
    if missing:
        raise ValueError(f"Missing columns: {sorted(missing)}")
    if df.empty or df[[C, A, T, R]].isna().any().any():
        raise ValueError("Require nonempty data and complete event keys.")

    # Input contract: UTC/offset-aware timestamps. Localize naive
    # timestamps using the documented source timezone beforehand.
    df[T] = pd.to_datetime(
        df[T], format="mixed", utc=True, errors="raise"
    )
    if df[T].isna().any():
        raise ValueError("Missing or invalid timestamp.")

    df[K] = pd.to_numeric(df[K], errors="raise")
    if df.groupby(C)[K].nunique(dropna=True).gt(1).any():
        raise ValueError(
            "Complexity varies within a case; define a "
            "time-specific analysis first."
        )
    df[K] = df[C].map(df.groupby(C)[K].first())

    flag = df[S].astype("string").str.strip().str.lower()
    values = {
        "true": "substitution", "1": "substitution",
        "1.0": "substitution", "yes": "substitution",
        "false": "routine", "0": "routine",
        "0.0": "routine", "no": "routine",
        "unknown": "unknown", "": "unknown",
    }
    if (flag.notna() & ~flag.isin(values)).any():
        raise ValueError("Unrecognized temporary_substitution value.")
    df["_sub"] = flag.map(values).fillna("unknown")

    tied = bool(df.duplicated([C, T], keep=False).any())
    if tied:
        warnings.warn(
            "Timestamp ties: directed handovers depend on "
            "an unverified tie order."
        )

    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df, case_id=C, activity_key=A, timestamp_key=T
    )

    # Event-level filtering is appropriate for activity profiles.
    # Do NOT use these shortened traces to infer handovers.
    views = {
        "all": df,
        "routine": df.loc[df["_sub"].eq("routine")].copy(),
        "substitution": df.loc[df["_sub"].eq("substitution")].copy(),
        "human_routine": df.loc[
            df["_sub"].eq("routine") & df[R].ne("R14_Nora")
        ].copy(),
    }
    reports = {
        name: role_context(view)
        for name, view in views.items()
        if not view.empty
    }

    native_similarity = (
        pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df, **KEYS)
    )

    # Explicit cosine, distinct from the inspected PM4Py Pearson metric.
    counts = pd.crosstab(df[R], df[A]).astype(float)
    x = counts.to_numpy()
    norms = np.linalg.norm(x, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    unit = np.divide(
        x, norms, out=np.zeros_like(x), where=norms > 0
    )
    cosine = pd.DataFrame(
        unit @ unit.T, index=counts.index, columns=counts.index
    )

    # Keep full observed traces, including bot and substitution events.
    handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
        df, beta=0, **NET_KEYS
    )
    together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df, **NET_KEYS)

    # Compare affected CASES without deleting their other events.
    affected_ids = df.loc[df["_sub"].eq("substitution"), C].unique()
    affected = df.loc[df[C].isin(affected_ids)].copy()
    affected_handover = (
        edge_table(pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
            affected, beta=0, **NET_KEYS
        ))
        if not affected.empty else pd.DataFrame()
    )

    return {
        "pm4py_version": pm4py.__version__,
        "roles": reports,
        "activity_counts": counts,
        "cosine": cosine,
        "pm4py_similarity": edge_table(native_similarity),
        "handover": edge_table(handover),
        "working_together": edge_table(together),
        "substitution_case_handover": affected_handover,
        "timestamp_ties_present": tied,
        "activity_labels": sorted(df[A].unique()),
    }


# Requires the original event-level CSV, not the summarized matrix.
result = analyze("event_log.csv")
print(result["roles"]["all"].to_string(index=False))
print(result["handover"].sort_values("weight", ascending=False).head(20))
```

### How I would interpret and validate the outputs

**Compare role profiles across conditions, not just across people.** Examine Giorgio’s commercial membership during ordinary and substitution work; Mateo’s A9 participation inside and outside the workshop episode; and technical profiles within comparable complexity, product and customer segments. Repeat over time and examine small-sample support. Mined role numbers are local identifiers: “role 2” in one slice need not equal “role 2” in another.

**Use the networks as complementary behavioral evidence.** Handover-of-work concerns ordered resource succession; working-together concerns resources participating in the same case. Neither establishes managerial authority, actual communication or simultaneous collaboration. The supplied fragments suggest relationships to inspect—such as technical-to-quality work and specialist-to-coordinator-to-supervisor escalation—but do not provide their weights.  

**Preserve trace semantics when filtering.** Removing bot or substitution events before discovering a handover network can create apparent direct handovers between resources that were not adjacent in the original trace. The code therefore filters events only for activity-profile discovery, and selects whole observed case histories for the substitution-case network.

**Keep authority and competence outside the clustering output.** Join the resulting resource-role evidence to a separately validated register of certifications, delegation scope, decision finality and approved backup assignments. An observed event is evidence of participation, not proof that the person was authorized or independently competent to make the underlying decision.

Finally, the supplied schema has no start timestamp. I would not infer hands-on service time or pure waiting time by treating gaps between successive timestamps as those quantities. The reported 38- and 74-minute pricing durations remain source-reported measurements until their definition is established.  

# 6. Operational interpretation

**Retain specialization, but formalize useful overlap.** The strongest management response is not to replace the roster with mutually exclusive clusters. Maintain routine-service capacity, technical delivery, commercial expertise, security expertise, quality assurance and supervisory oversight, while explicitly recognizing Giorgio’s commercial overlap and Mateo’s coordination function.

### Staffing and resilience

Build a coverage matrix of **role × complexity × product × shift**, distinguishing independently qualified coverage, supervised backup and unverified participation. This would test whether apparently similar resources can cover the same operational need. Chen and Dina, for example, have different observed risk exposure and usual shifts; Ivan and Julia also work different usual shifts. Erik’s day-shift security role should prompt a review of urgent coverage outside that shift, not an unsupported conclusion that such coverage is absent. 

Two staffing cautions are essential:

* **Similar activity profiles do not establish interchangeability.** Reassignment may change risk exposure, remove local expertise or transfer downstream rework to other teams.
* **Low event volume does not establish spare capacity.** Erik’s specialist concentration and critical-case exposure make event-count-based reductions particularly difficult to justify. Nor can the table determine staffing requirements without task effort, available hours, arrival patterns and response obligations.  

Coverage for Giorgio should also account for what happens to his front-office work when he substitutes commercially. A backup arrangement that preserves one queue while overloading another is not automatically a resilience improvement.

### Training and risk control

Prioritize **entitlement documentation and checked closure practice** for routine-service work. The 17 reopened cases are a concrete investigation target, but not a defensible temporary-worker error rate: the corresponding cohort, exposure denominator and follow-up completeness are not supplied. Review whether the failure involves understanding, note templates, supervision, handoff quality or pressure to close—not just the worker’s contract type.  

For commercial backup, validate Giorgio’s decision scope and outcomes before expanding it. For technical/security overlap, distinguish assisting a review from independently owning it. For Mateo’s approval work, record whether an event is a preliminary assessment, delegated final decision or subsequent confirmation. These measures clarify responsibility without assuming that existing shadow arrangements are improper.

The security-filtering episode warrants particular attention. Preserve the potential benefit of fewer unnecessary referrals while investigating the two delayed critical cases. Examine triage decisions, referral timestamps, specialist availability and queue progression before deciding whether the remedy is revised routing, stronger urgency recognition or additional qualified capacity. 

### Measurement before structural change

Track **critical-case time to qualified review, commercial duration distributions, mature-cohort reopen rates, downstream rework, and approval rechecks separately from overturned decisions**. Compare like-for-like case mixes and distinguish ordinary periods from substitutions and incident surges. Improve the log with decision finality, delegation references, consistent coordination labels, substitution intervals, and queue/service timestamps.

**The recommended decision is to preserve the functional specialties, make the bridging assignments explicit, and resolve authority and coverage gaps before changing headcount or broadly reallocating work.** The evidence supports targeted improvements to security routing, approval provenance and entitlement documentation more strongly than it supports a wholesale reorganization based on activity-frequency clusters.
