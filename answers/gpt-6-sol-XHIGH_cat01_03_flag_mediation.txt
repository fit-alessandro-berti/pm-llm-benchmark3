## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Let a policy apply to an entire arrival block (for example, a shift and its shared queues), and let outcomes include every arrival in that block. This makes spillovers part of a policy effect. Define flag eligibility using the *pre-policy* score and complexity rule. P0 is the current bundle. P1 hides the flag badge from staff but leaves the backend priority lane and automatic review rule, the risk score, and other case information intact. P2 keeps priority triage and lets staff order review by prespecified clinical criteria instead of the flag mandate. P3 adds genuinely incremental protected review capacity while keeping routing. Split P4 into P4a (prespecified escalation criteria, fixed senior capacity) and P4b (incremental senior capacity, current criteria). Also define T0 as P0 with only the priority-lane rule disabled. These definitions require stable staffing and override rules within each contrast.

I use **policy/component effects and interventional mediation analogues**, not natural direct or indirect effects. Natural effects would combine a case's mediator under one policy with its outcome under another, despite post-flag triage findings, changing queues, and interference. “Set flag to zero” is especially ambiguous: it could alter a database field, hide a badge, or disable several routing rules.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Assign whole blocks P2 versus P0 | All arrivals, including unflagged | Mean arrival-to-close time | Total policy effect, including spillovers | Yes, with specified blocks and overrides | No randomized policy variation; carryover between blocks |
| E2 | P0 versus T0 | Baseline flag-eligible arrivals within randomized blocks | Arrival-to-triage-completion time | Priority-component effect | Yes, if lane capacity and triage resources are fixed by protocol | Flag eligibility tracks risk; lane congestion and other cases' assignments |
| E3 | P2 versus P0, priority and staffing fixed | Baseline flag-eligible arrivals | Review probability and cycle time | Interventional analogue targeting review *occurrence*, including its later consequences | Yes as a policy effect; not a pure natural indirect effect | Discretionary review responds to unrecorded findings; queue spillovers |
| E4 | P3 versus P0 with review decisions and service protocol held fixed | Reviewed flag-eligible cases and all queue users | Manual queue wait and cycle time | Waiting-targeted interventional analogue | Policy yes; an isolated waiting effect needs an exclusion assumption | Added capacity can change reviewer assignment, service, and other waits; conditioning on review selects cases |
| E5 | P4a versus P0 | Review-eligible arrivals in blocks | Escalation probability, senior wait, cycle time | Escalation-component interventional analogue | Yes if criteria and overrides are explicit | Escalation is selected by case findings; quality may change |
| E6 | P2 versus P0, assigned by block | Baseline flag-eligible arrivals | Mean/median arrival-to-close time | Subgroup total cycle-time effect | Yes; baseline subgroup only | Small sample, weak score overlap, queue interference |
| E7 | P2 versus P0 | All arrivals, with independent outcome audit | Error, appeal, harm, or compliance rate | Total effect on decision quality | Yes after defining an audit standard and follow-up window | Approve/Reject is a decision, not ground truth; rare harms |
| E8 | P1 versus P0 | All arrivals | Escalation, cycle time, audited quality | Effect of *display* through staff behavior | Yes under the stated backend implementation | Staff can infer risk from score and other information; imperfect blinding |
| E9 | P4b versus P0 | All arrivals, especially escalated cases | Senior queue wait and cycle time | Capacity-targeted interventional analogue | Yes if capacity is incremental | Staffing may change service speed and selection; downstream bottlenecks |

```text
Intake: underlying_risk -> risk_score -> flag; complexity, arrival_load, U_queue_state
   |              flag -> priority_lane -> triage_wait
   v
Register -> [triage_wait] -> Triage [triage_service; U_triage_findings]
   -> {manual_review?} -> [manual_queue_wait] -> Manual Review [manual_service]
   -> {escalation?}    -> [escalation_queue_wait] -> Escalation [senior_service]
   -> Close [cycle_time; decision_outcome versus U_ground_truth]

Other arrivals/routes -> shared review and senior queues -> later waits for this case
This case's route -> shared queues at later times -> waits for other cases
```

The queues create interference: routing one case to review or escalation changes waits for later cases, including unflagged ones. Aggregate arrival load is only a coarse proxy for the evolving queue. Triage findings and reviewer behavior arise after the flag is seen and can affect both later routing and completion time; adjusting for them as if they were baseline confounders can block part of the policy effect or induce bias. Review occurrence also selects which cases have review waiting and service times. A policy can change this selected population, so conditional wait comparisons do not identify a mediated effect. The graph is schematic: its unobserved nodes are not claimed to be measured by this CSV.

## 3. Stage decomposition

All entries below are **mean hours**. A wait or service interval is averaged only over cases that entered that stage; `NA` means no case in that subgroup entered it. `residual-to-close` begins at the last completed stage (triage, manual review, or escalation). Consequently, conditional stage means need not sum to a subgroup's mean total cycle time.

Review supports are 10/10 flagged and 4/10 unflagged; escalation supports are 6/10 and 2/10, respectively. Among low-, medium-, and high-complexity cases, review supports are 0/4, 6/8, and 8/8; escalation supports are 0/4, 1/8, and 7/8.

Flagged cases reach triage start about 2.24 hours sooner and finish triage about 2.59 hours sooner after arrival. Yet their mean total cycle is about 8.34 hours longer. Every flagged case enters review, compared with four unflagged cases, and six flagged cases enter escalation, compared with two unflagged cases. Among reviewed cases, the measured gap before manual start averages 6.50 hours when flagged versus 1.88 when unflagged; manual service itself is similar (2.70 versus 2.75 hours). This is consistent with faster triage followed by longer downstream routing and waiting. It is not a causal decomposition: the groups have different risk, complexity, and review selection; a case arriving later encounters a different queue; and routing one case changes others' waits. In particular, the escalation means condition on a highly selected subset. Local-clock timestamps also need validation across shifts and daylight-saving changes.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flag -> priority -> triage timing | The priority lane advances flagged arrivals | Flagged mean triage wait 0.24 h versus 2.48 h | Comparable arrivals or randomized lane assignment; correct timestamps | T1/T2 staffing, arrival hour, case mix | T0 contrast or protected lane experiment | Delays unflagged triage if capacity is shared |
| Flag -> review occurrence | Mandate sends many flagged cases into extra work | 10/10 flagged reviewed versus 4/10 unflagged | Same discretionary review rule and no hidden severity difference for causal attribution | Flag is a proxy for true risk and appropriate review | P2 | Fewer review checks could harm quality; could free queue capacity |
| Flag -> manual queue | Review inflow plus shared capacity lengthens waiting | Conditional mean 6.50 h flagged versus 1.88 h unflagged | Measured queue state and comparable reviewed cases | High-complexity cases assigned to M2 or released into a busy shift | P3 or queue scheduling rule | Protected slots may delay unflagged reviews if reallocated |
| Flag -> escalation | Review or visible risk prompts senior referral | 6/10 flagged escalated versus 2/10 unflagged | Comparable case findings; explicit escalation criteria | Genuine higher severity drives escalation | P4a | Lower senior load may miss difficult cases |
| Escalation -> senior wait | Scarce senior team adds delay | Escalated flagged cases have 7.00 h mean senior wait and 21.42 h mean cycle | No unmeasured severity/decision effects; stable queue | Escalated cases would have been slow anyway | P4b | More referrals may be induced if capacity becomes easier to access |
| Complexity/risk -> routing and duration | Flag mixes policy with underlying need | High complexity mean cycle 19.77 h; low 4.35 h; score/flag nearly separate | Risk and complexity measured sufficiently | Hidden case facts explain both flag and delay | Better risk measurement; stratified design | Over-adjustment may remove a real policy pathway if using post-triage data |
| Shared-queue interference | Extra reviews for one group delay others | Review and senior queues are explicitly shared | Queue histories capture arrivals, departures, staffing, and carryover | Coarse arrival_load or overnight staffing causes apparent congestion | Block-randomized P2/P3 | Gains for flagged cases can be offset by losses elsewhere |
| Reviewer/resource assignment | M2/senior assignment may shape wait or service | Flagged high-complexity and many escalated cases use M2 | Resource allocation observed and comparable cases within resource | M2 receives the hardest cases; no causal resource effect | Balance reviewer assignment or add matched capacity | Changing assignment may alter expertise and quality |
| Decision-quality tradeoff | Review/escalation can lengthen cycles to improve decisions | Approve/Reject recorded; no audit or ground truth | Independent quality endpoint and sufficient follow-up | More stages are unnecessary bureaucracy | P2 with audit and override, or P4a | Faster closure may increase errors, appeals, or compliance failures |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does flag eligibility cause a discontinuity near a score cutoff? | With many more days, estimate a local regression-discontinuity effect just above versus below the actual flag threshold (apparently near 0.62 here). It identifies the *bundle* locally, not its mediators. | Narrow score window; pre-intake complexity, arrival time/load | Known stable threshold, no score manipulation, continuity of potential outcomes | Density/manipulation test; continuity of complexity and pre-flag covariates; placebo cutoffs | A cycle-time jump without a review-rate jump would weaken occurrence mediation; no triage jump would weaken priority-lane explanation |
| Is downstream delay a queue phenomenon? | Reconstruct each queue's time-varying number waiting/in service, staff schedules, priority rules, and stage-entry times; model stage-specific waits under matched queue states. | Score, complexity, arrival hour, actual queue length/workload, resource availability; do not adjust for post-flag variables when estimating total effects | Complete queue logs and comparable cases within state | Residual wait versus actual queue length; check whether high-load label predicts true backlog | Similar flagged/unflagged waits at equal queue state with no flagged-induced backlog would weaken a congestion mechanism |
| What does changing the mandate do? | Randomize P2 versus P0 by shift/queue block, with washout and fixed staffing; estimate intention-to-treat effects on all arrivals and eligible flagged cases. | Randomization block and preassigned strata; baseline score, complexity for precision | Adherence, limited cross-block carryover, independent audit | Balance, override rates, review-rate first stage, carryover and contamination checks | No review reduction, or worsened quality despite faster closure, would argue against rollout |
| How much is associated with review occurrence versus its wait? | Use sequential g-computation or a longitudinal marginal structural model for feasible stochastic review and scheduling regimes; report interventional analogues and bounds, not natural effects. | Baseline risk proxies, complexity, time; pre-review findings, queue state and staffing at each decision | Sequential exchangeability, positivity, correctly timed covariates, handled interference | Check support for optional review within strata; compare simulated versus observed stage distributions | Lack of overlap or strong sensitivity to hidden findings would preclude a credible decomposition |
| Could a spurious time trend explain the flag contrast? | Use pre-triage features that the current flag cannot change (for example, intake-to-registration data quality or precomputed complexity) as negative-control outcomes; use future block flag prevalence as a negative-control exposure for earlier waits. | Intake time/shift and baseline case mix | Controls share confounding but no policy pathway; future exposure does not affect the past | Test association of flag with pre-flag outcomes and of future prevalence with earlier waits | Strong associations suggest sorting, time trends, or faulty timestamp semantics |
| How large could hidden risk bias be? | Sensitivity analysis varying prevalence and impact of unrecorded high-risk facts on review, escalation, and duration; compare with an independent blinded risk audit. | Recorded score, complexity, intake covariates | Plausible bounds for hidden-risk effects; audit is blinded | Determine bias strength needed to erase the observed 8.34 h cycle gap | A modest plausible hidden-risk factor that erases it weakens policy attribution |
| Do one case's routes affect others? | Randomize P2 by whole queue blocks at different saturation levels; estimate both eligible-case and unflagged bystander effects, including lagged backlog. | Block, staffing, arrivals, pre-block backlog, time of day | Partial interference within blocks or measured cross-block carryover | Exposure-response of unflagged waits to randomized flagged review volume; placebo before block starts | No bystander effect despite substantial review-volume change weakens shared-queue explanation |

The 20 rows alone cannot support these designs: all flagged scores exceed all unflagged scores, there is no pre-policy period, and arrival load is not a queue trace.

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current bundle | Flagged triage remains fast | Review and senior queues remain heavily used | Observed flagged mean 16.51 h; causal baseline unknown | Review may protect quality, but quality unmeasured | Possible delay to unflagged cases | Descriptive only | Maintain as control and safety benchmark |
| P1: hide badge, backend rules unchanged | Little change if lane remains automatic | Mandatory review remains; staff discretion may shift | Likely small/uncertain | Staff may lose useful cue; score remains visible | Small/uncertain | Very weak | Use only if testing display behavior specifically |
| P2: remove mandatory review, retain priority | Little direct change to triage | Fewer reviews if discretion changes; may shorten both queues | Possibly shorter, especially for flagged medium complexity; magnitude unknown | Missed review is the central concern; audit and override required | Can free shared capacity for others | Mechanism plausible, effect unestimated | **Limited randomized pilot** |
| P3: add protected review capacity | Little change to triage | Shorter review wait if capacity is incremental; senior queue may become bottleneck | Likely shorter for reviewed cases, uncertain overall | Lower omission risk; different reviewers may change quality | Benefit if new capacity; harm if merely reallocated | Wait pattern suggests target, no causal estimate | Contingency if P2 fails quality or review remains needed |
| P4a/P4b: revise criteria / add senior capacity | Little change to triage | P4a fewer referrals or P4b shorter senior wait | Potentially shorter for escalated cases | P4a may miss difficult cases; P4b lower omission risk | P4a frees senior slots; P4b costs new capacity | Sparse: eight escalations total | Define and test criteria/capacity separately |

**Pilot P2.** Enroll newly flagged medium-complexity cases for which no independent rule requires review; allow a documented reviewer override. Randomize whole review-queue shifts, stratified by predicted arrival load, to P2 or P0. Assign all eligible cases in a shift to its policy, keep priority triage and staffing fixed, and analyze all arrivals for spillovers. Separate shifts with a prespecified backlog washout or model measured carryover. Stop enrollment in a block if the shared review backlog or oldest unflagged wait exceeds a prespecified operational limit; ensure urgent cases retain immediate access.

Primary outcome: arrival-to-close time for eligible cases, subject to a prespecified noninferiority boundary for blinded, independently adjudicated decision quality. Secondary outcomes: review and escalation rates, stage waits/services, unflagged cycle time, errors/appeals, and staff overrides. Collect exact event start/completion times, flag/score/complexity, triage findings, review reasons, queue arrivals and departures, staffing, resource IDs, overrides, and blinded audit results. Stop or pause for excess serious errors, audit noninferiority failure, persistent queue harm to any group, or breakdown of randomization/adherence. This pilot estimates a policy effect and supplies mediation evidence through measured routing and queues; it does not identify natural indirect effects.

```python
    support = g[metrics].count().to_dict()  # NaN denotes absent stage
    mean = g[metrics].mean().round(2).to_dict()
    print(label, "n=", len(g), "review=", int(g.manual_start.notna().sum()),
          "escalation=", int(g.escalation_start.notna().sum()))
    print("  support:", support)
    print("  conditional stage means (h):", mean)
    if len(g) < 10 or (support and min(support.values()) < 5):
        warnings.warn(f"Small subgroup or stage support in {label}; interpret cautiously.")

warnings.warn(
    "DFG performance contrasts and stage mean differences are descriptive, not causal; "
    "review/escalation are selected stages and queues create interference."
)
```

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** Blinded audit finds that unrecorded risk strongly predicts both flag status and legitimate review/escalation need, and explains the apparent excess cycle time after a credible design adjusts for it.
2. **Queue interference:** A block experiment finds that reducing mandatory review does not reduce review backlog, or instead lengthens unflagged and urgent cases' waits through displaced work; then P2's expected system benefit reverses.
3. **Decision quality:** Independent follow-up shows P2 causes a clinically or legally unacceptable increase in wrong decisions, appeals, or harms, even if closure is faster.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** Clock drift, overnight handoffs, or logging at administrative rather than actual start times account for the measured 6.50-hour manual wait; the queue mechanism would need re-evaluation.
5. **Positivity/overlap:** Near the true score cutoff, cases on the two sides have different complexity or no comparable discretionary-review decisions; the proposed local and mediation analyses cannot identify the claimed effects.
6. **Reviewer behavior:** Under P2, reviewers compensate for the missing mandate by voluntarily reviewing nearly every flagged case or by extending triage, so the predicted occurrence and cycle-time gains disappear.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed:** In these 20 cases, flagged arrivals wait a mean 0.24 hours to start triage, compared with 2.48 hours for unflagged arrivals. Their mean total cycle is nevertheless 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All ten flagged cases receive manual review, versus four of ten unflagged cases; six versus two reach escalation. Among cases that receive review, the interval from triage completion to manual start is 6.50 versus 1.88 hours on average. Manual service times are similar. These are conditional descriptive summaries, and the stage means do not add to the group cycle means because stages are optional.

**Plausible mechanism:** Priority routing advances flagged cases at triage, while the review mandate increases work and exposes them to a shared manual queue; some then enter a scarce senior queue. Complex, genuinely risky cases can also take longer for appropriate reasons. The flag is a score-driven policy cue, not the underlying risk.

**Unidentified:** This one-period sample has no randomized policy contrast, sparse overlap in score and complexity, no queue-state trace, and no independent decision-quality measure. It cannot determine natural direct or indirect effects, nor tell whether hiding the badge, changing the mandate, adding staff, or changing escalation would have equivalent effects. Shared queues mean each policy can affect untreated cases.

**Next evidence:** A guarded, shift-randomized P2 pilot among flagged medium-complexity cases would directly test whether relaxing mandatory review shortens cycles while preserving audited quality and avoiding harm to other queue users. Its review-rate change, stage timestamps, actual queue states, and override reasons would clarify the mechanism without pretending to identify an impossible single-case “flag effect.”
