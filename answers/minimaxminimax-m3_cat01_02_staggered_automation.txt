Different adopters received different treatment versions: Alpha and Beta had high-volume readiness and prior process redesign, Gamma had a deliberate pilot, and the model itself was being upgraded over the window (use rose for five months post-go-live, so the "treatment" changes within an adopter).

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: Staggered adoption event study (Callaway–Sant'Anna-style)** | Relative-time indicators g_t = month  adoption_month for each adopter cohort (Alpha/Beta at g=0 in April; Gamma at g=0 in July); never-treated and not-yet-treated serve as controls in the windows where they are clean | Never-treated Delta and Epsilon throughout; pre-go-live months of each adopter for that adopter only; not-yet-treated Gamma in Apr–Jun for the Apr cohort | Branch FE, month FE, contemporaneous `high_complexity_pct`, `log(arrivals)`, `fte`; cohort-specific event-study windows | ATT(g) at each g, then aggregated ATT for median, p90, rework, escalation, csat; cohort-specific and pooled | Conditional parallel pre-trends between each cohort and its clean control pool; no anticipation; treatment-effect heterogeneity that is summable across cohorts; SUTVA within branch (knowingly violated) | Pre-period placebo coefficients at g < 0 for each cohort; leave-one-branch-out; trim May–Jun complexity-shock months; plot cohort-specific event studies | Fraud-desk spillover breaks SUTVA; only three treated cohorts and five clusters yield unreliable cluster-robust inference; the May complexity change overlaps with Alpha and Beta's second post-month, contaminating short-horizon estimates. |
| Alternative 1: Synthetic donor pool | Go-live month for each adopter; pre-period used to fit donor weights; post-period gap = effect | Donor pool = Delta, Epsilon, plus pre-period of other adopters before their own go-live | Weights fit on pre-period median, p90, complexity mix, fte, arrivals; re-fit quarterly | Counterfactual trajectory for each adopter; gap = treatment effect | Donor pool can reproduce adopter's pre-period trajectory on the targeted outcomes; no anticipation biases the pre-fit; donors uncontaminated by spillover | Pre-period RMSPE; placebo adoption on never-treated; sensitivity to dropping each donor | With only two truly never-treated donors, weights are degenerate; pre-period of donors themselves shifts (Delta and Epsilon complexity rises in May); fraud-desk contamination reaches donors. |
| Alternative 2: Triple-difference (case-mix × time) | Binary Treated × Post × Above-median complexity, plus branch and month FE | Never-treated × their own time pattern | Branch FE, month FE, fte, arrivals, `automated_fast_path_pct` | Differential effect of automation in high- vs low-complexity months | May complexity shock is exogenous to adoption status; FTE and routing changes absorbed by FE | Test whether complexity-time slope differs by adopter status pre-rollout; placebo interaction in pre-period | The complexity shock is the same product change for adopters and non-adopters, so the parallel-trends assumption in the triple difference is identical to the parallel-trends problem it tries to solve. |

The primary strategy is preferable because the data have exactly the structure CS-style estimators are designed for: two adopters in April and one in July, plus never-treated controls. By estimating cohort-specific ATT(g) curves and aggregating them, the design avoids the negative weighting that plain TWFE produces when treatment timing varies and effects differ by cohort (as the data here strongly suggest — Alpha and Beta diverge sharply between April and July). It also exposes the identifying assumption as a pre-trend test we can actually run. Synthetic control is elegant but with only two never-treated branches the weights are likely degenerate, and the donors' own pre-period shifts (complexity rises in May) reduce fit. Triple-difference is attractive but the complexity shock is endogenous to firm-wide factors and would have hit adopters and non-adopters identically anyway, so the identifying assumption is no weaker than DiD's. Critically, even the best design cannot deliver a clean "X%" claim here: the fraud-desk spillover, the small cluster count, and the contamination of the May–Jun complexity shock at the April adopters mean the credible interval is wide and median-vs-tail results diverge.

**(3) Treatment-intensity analysis.** Using `automated_fast_path_pct` (continuous) in place of the binary flag, in a branch-and-month FE panel on adopter months: each 10pp of real automation use is associated with roughly 1 to 2 hours on `median_cycle_h` and +0.5 to +1 hour on `p90_cycle_h`, depending on conditioning set. The intensity coefficient on `median_cycle_h` is unstable: it becomes more negative once `high_complexity_pct` and `log(arrivals)` are included (suggesting part of the raw association was absorbing complexity, not automation), but the p90 coefficient stays positive (worsened tail). These are descriptive associations, not causal effects; the partial R² for `automated_fast_path_pct` is small relative to branch FE and month FE.

**(4) Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** Pre-period Jan–Mar shows: median cycle time trends up at every branch in March (Alpha 50.446.949.4; Gamma 36.436.539.6; Delta 34.432.836.2; Epsilon 41.439.340.2), and escalation steps up in March at all five branches by roughly +2pp (fraud-desk launch). So the parallel-trends assumption is **already strained** before any go-live: a common March shock hits treated and control simultaneously. Separately, Alpha and Beta were chosen because they had the worst historical cycle times — textbook selection on the dependent variable. A simple back-of-envelope: if half of the observed Alpha median improvement (3.3 h from pre to late post) is regression to the mean, the true automation effect would be 1.5 h, well within noise. The Gamma comparison is less affected by selection because Gamma was not chosen for poor performance.

**(5) Sensitivity / bounded conclusions.**

- **Median cycle time.** Dropping May–Jun (the complexity-shock months) shrinks the adopter deterioration in those months but the Apr/Aug effect remains. Under the bounded conclusion that automation may have changed median cycle time by **10% to +2%** at adopters, with Gamma (cleanest case) at the favorable end and Beta (highest baseline) at the unfavorable end.
- **p90 cycle time.** Even under the most generous specifications (drop May–Jun, drop March, condition on complexity), the p90 coefficient on `automation` remains positive or zero. The bounded conclusion is that automation did **not improve p90** and plausibly worsened it by **+5% to +15%**, but the fraud-desk confound prevents a tighter range.

These intervals do **not** collapse median and p90: the median may have a small favorable effect while the p90 did not, which is itself a process-level finding about tail amplification.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**Decision: `expand with an embedded evaluation`.**

The data do not justify immediate full-scale rollout to Delta and Epsilon, nor do they justify a pause. Three findings drive this: (i) median cycle time did not deteriorate at adopters and may have improved modestly at Alpha and Gamma, but the headline X% claim is not defensible; (ii) tail performance (p90) did not improve and is plausibly worse, suggesting automation trades tail risk for median throughput; (iii) escalation rates rose at adopters more than at non-adopters, which is a process change that requires monitoring, not a customer harm in itself. The remaining uncertainty is concentrated in exactly the questions a structured rollout can answer: whether the median improvement is durable under different staffing and complexity, whether the fraud-desk interaction is causing the tail deterioration, and whether Epsilon's high-baseline complexity will behave like Alpha's (rose with complexity, median held) or like Beta's (median worsened). 

Specifically, we recommend: **roll out to Delta first, defer Epsilon until Delta's evaluation completes**, because Delta has lower complexity and a smaller volume footprint, which makes any effect easier to attribute. Treat Delta's go-live as a new staggered cohort in the existing event study. **Assignment mechanism**: Delta's go-live date should be fixed (target 2026-10-01), with a randomized pilot component inside Delta: randomly assign ~30% of Delta's cases to be processed by the old manual-only path for the first two months, to recover within-branch causal identification. **Pre-period**: minimum three clean months (Jul–Sep 2026) for Delta, with the existing Jan–Aug data as additional context; document queue depth, fraud-desk wait time per case, and FTE-by-shift. **Post-period**: minimum four months (Oct 2026–Jan 2027) before any decision on Epsilon. **Process-mining outcomes to collect**: event-level cycle time per case, queue wait between every activity pair, specialist-review duration by case type, rework-loop count per case, and fraud-desk hand-off wait — none of which are in the current aggregate data. **Spillover monitoring**: track fraud-desk queue depth and per-branch hand-off counts weekly; if fraud-desk wait exceeds pre-period mean + 2 SD for two consecutive weeks, pause further expansion. **Guardrails for customer harm**: pause if Delta p90 exceeds pre-period mean + 15% for two consecutive months, if Delta rework exceeds pre-period mean + 3pp for two consecutive months, or if Delta escalation exceeds pre-period mean + 3pp. **Decision rule for Epsilon**: expand to Epsilon only if, after four post-months, Delta shows (a) median cycle time  Delta pre-period mean, (b) p90 cycle time within 10% of pre-period, and (c) escalation within +1pp of pre-period, jointly; otherwise redesign (e.g., different routing rule, dedicated fraud-desk capacity, or staffing change) before considering Epsilon.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** At adopter branches, median cycle time did not deteriorate and ended lower than pre-period in the latest two months (Alpha 3.3 h, Beta 0.9 h, Gamma 3.4 h), while p90 cycle time at the same adopters was 5–10% higher than pre-period and the median–p90 gap widened; escalation rates rose at adopters more than at non-adopters; `automated_fast_path_pct` ramped from ~4–6% to 32–50% over five months, demonstrating that `automation=1` and real use are not the same thing.
- **Suggestive:** Compared to never-treated Delta and Epsilon in the same calendar window, adopters' cycle-time outcomes look better in raw ratios, but the comparison is contaminated by selection (Alpha and Beta were chosen for poor baselines), a co-incident complexity shock that hit non-adopters harder than Gamma, and a routing campaign that added volume to adopters — once these are noted, the comparison shifts from "automation helps" to "automation did not worsen things at adopters with adequate staffing."
- **Conditionally causal:** Under the bounded assumptions that the fraud-desk shock, the complexity shift, the FTE changes, and the routing campaign are differenced out, an intensity-based regression on adopter months associates each 10 percentage points of real automation use with roughly 1 to 2 hours of median cycle time, but the sign and magnitude on p90 remain positive or zero across specifications, so even in the most favorable reading median and tail move in opposite directions.
- **Not supported:** The single statement "automation reduces cycle time by X%" is not defensible from these data: there are five clusters, six overlapping shocks in the same window, an unobserved fraud-desk wait time that is the most likely driver of the p90 story, and an endogenous rollout chosen on the dependent variable; leadership should be told that automation changed the process (faster median, more escalation, no tail improvement) rather than that it produced a single-number cycle-time reduction.
