## 1. Model scope and state variables

The digital twin simulates the municipal building-permit process from portal submission to final permit/rejection decision. It is an event-driven, stochastic discrete-event simulation model with individual case attributes, resource pools, calendars, queues, and selected cross-case dependencies. It does not model the complete city planning ecosystem, construction code compliance, or third-party land-use decisions beyond their effect on permit-process times and outcomes.

### Boundary

- **In scope**: all activities in the supplied process tree, applicant document responses, public-notice waiting, inspection travel/batching, resource calendars, case type, district, architect, missing-documents loops, review loops, legal check, and permit issuance/rejection.
- **Out of scope**: physical construction, financial approval, detailed planning-committee debates, non-permit work performed by environmental/legal resources, and emergency permit cases removed from the log.

### Entities

- **Cases**: building-permit applications with attributes:
  - `case_type`: Simple renovation / New residential / Commercial / Complex variance
  - `district`
  - `architect_id`
  - `application_value`
  - `requires_fire`
  - `requires_environment`
  - `requires_public_notice`
  - latent risk/objection profile
- **Activities**: process-tree tasks, including silent/`tau` branches and rework loops.
- **Resources**: clerks, planners, fire reviewers, environmental reviewers, inspectors, legal officers, system resources, and external parties (applicants, architects, objectors, consultants).
- **Third-party actors**: applicant response processes and neighborhood objections are represented as stochastic delays and event modifiers, not as fully observed municipal tasks.

### State variables

- WIP and queue length per activity and case type.
- Backlog of cases waiting for each resource pool.
- Next available time per resource.
- Resource calendar status: holiday, council meeting week, training, etc.
- Current routing state per case.
- Waiting-for-input flags for applicant documents, inspection no-shows, objections, or third-party review.
- Cross-case group state:
  - Architect activity bursts.
  - Cases sharing the same architect/district that may be batched for inspection.
  - Objection events that affect multiple related public-notice cases.

### Resources and calendars

The model maintains explicit resource agents with calendars:
- Clerks: 5 FTE; Mon–Fri 08:00–16:00; one clerk assigned to portal support on Monday.
- Planners: 8 FTE; Mon–Fri 09:00–17:00; two planners reserved for Simple renovation.
- Fire reviewers: 2 FTE-equivalent; Tue–Thu only.
- Environmental reviewers: 3 FTE; Mon–Fri, but only 60% available for permit work.
- Inspectors: 4 FTE; Tue–Fri field days; travel time varies by district.
- Legal officers: 2 FTE; no routine work during council meeting weeks.
- System tasks: unlimited capacity with negligible service time.

### Cross-case dependencies

- **Architect bursts**: applications from the same architect arrive together or in short windows.
- **District batching**: inspection scheduling can group cases in the same district to reduce travel.
- **Neighborhood objection**: a latent group event may extend multiple public-notice cases in nearby districts/projects.
- **Shared queues**: all activities are subject to resource contention; waiting time emerges from arrival, priority, and capacity.

## 2. Parameter-estimation plan

The event-log summary, illustrative excerpt, and known process model are used to estimate parameters for simulation. PM4Py is used mainly for log preprocessing, model visualization and conformance; the simulation engine is a custom discrete-event implementation that consumes estimated parameters as DataFrames/dictionaries.

### 2.1 Arrivals

- Use the first event timestamp of every case as its arrival timestamp.
- Remove outliers? Emergency/invalid cases already removed.
- Estimate:
  - Average arrival rate per month and per day-of-week.
  - Seasonal multipliers using the historical 18-month log.
  - Monday peak factor: observed 2.2x Wednesday.
  - Burst multiplier for top architects: fit a self-exciting arrival model or a batch-arrival model where a single architect “generates” 1–5 cases in a 3–7 day window.
- Non-stationary demand in simulation:  
  \[
  \lambda(t) = \lambda_0 \times \text{monthFactor}(t) \times \text{dayFactor}(t) \times \text{hourFactor}(t)
  \]
  plus an architect-burst term.

### 2.2 Service times and waiting times

Do **not** collapse service time and waiting time.

- For activities with observed start and completion timestamps:
  - service time = completion time  start time;
  - waiting time = start time  previous activity completion time.
- For activities with only completion timestamps:
  - Estimate total sojourn from previous completion to current completion.
  - Use observed service-time distributions for the same activity/resource/case-type segment to infer a plausible service-time conditional distribution;
  - waiting time = sojourn  simulated/expected service time, truncated at zero.
- Fit parametric distributions:
  - service times: lognormal or Weibull, stratifying by activity, resource pool, case type, district.
  - applicant response times: gamma or lognormal with heavy tails.
  - public-notice duration: mixture distribution:
    - no objection: fixed minimum 14 days + small delay;
    - objection: fixed minimum + lognormal extension, with latent objection probability.
- Use Bayesian hierarchical estimation where missing data is substantial, then propagate uncertainty through simulation sensitivity analysis.

### 2.3 Routing and branching probabilities

- Parse the supplied process tree and align it to the event-log variants using PM4Py conformance checks.
- Estimate transition probabilities from event-log frequencies:
  - missing-document loop probability after Completeness check;
  - probability of Fire-safety review, Environmental review, Public-notice period;
  - Legal check inclusion probability;
  - Reject vs Issue permit probability;
  - rework probabilities after Planning review, Inspection, Legal check, and appeal.
- For attributes with missing routing signals:
  - use logistic regression on available attributes (`case_type`, `district`, `application_value`, `requires_*`);
  - treat missing attribute values as a separate missing-indicator category or use multiple imputation.

### 2.4 Resource calendars and availability

- Reconstruct resource calendars from HR data and timestamp patterns.
- Use PM4Py `format_dataframe` to convert timestamps, then compute event counts by day-of-week/hour/resource.
- Fit availability windows and exceptions:
  - Monday portal support reduces clerk capacity.
  - Legal officers unavailable during council-meeting weeks.
  - Environmental reviewer only has 60% permit-dedicated time.
- Model resources as finite-capacity agents; no parallel processing unless explicitly allowed by process-tree `+` semantics.

### 2.5 Rework and loops

- Count repeated activity occurrences per case, e.g.:
  - `Completeness check` repeated after `Request missing documents` and `Receive missing documents`;
  - `Planning review` repeated after later review failure;
  - `Site inspection` repeated after no-show/defect;
  - `Legal check` repeated after rejection draft or appeal.
- Estimate loop probabilities and the number of additional rework iterations from variant frequency tables and PM4Py alignments.
- Because rework reasons are unstructured, the model uses aggregate rework transition probabilities, not text-mining of reasons.

### 2.6 Cross-case dependencies

- **Architect burst**: estimate the conditional probability that a new application from the same architect arrives within 7 days of another application.
- **Inspection batching**: identify cases with overlapping inspection readiness windows and same district; estimate the probability that they are scheduled as a batch and the resulting travel-time reduction.
- **Objections**: since objections are not directly observed, use public-notice duration as a latent proxy. For each public-notice case, classify the duration as “likely objection” using a mixture model. Treat this latent variable as a shared event for related district/architect cases in the simulation.

## 3. Assumptions

### 3.1 Arrivals

- Arrival process is non-homogeneous Poisson with weekly/seasonal multipliers.
- Architect bursts are captured by latent group arrival processes for top architects.
- Excluded rare emergency cases remain outside the model boundary.

### 3.2 Service times

- Service times are independent of queue length, except for inspection travel time reductions due to batching.
- Service-time distributions are stationary over the simulated horizon, though parameter uncertainty is allowed in sensitivity analysis.
- When start timestamps are missing, service time is inferred and treated as uncertain, not as observed.

### 3.3 Routing

- The supplied process tree is a valid representation of the official routing for the modeled cases.
- Parallel review activities represented by `+` can overlap for applicable case types.
- `tau` branches are silent choices in the simulation.

### 3.4 Resources/calendars

- Resources only work according to their calendars; calendar exceptions are known or sampled from historical patterns.
- A task that starts before a calendar break continues after the break; no preemption.
- Legal check and public-notice period are affected by council-meeting weeks and holidays.

### 3.5 Rework

- Rework probabilities are estimated from repeated activity loops.
- The model does **not** generate new business rules not represented in the process tree.
- Rework can happen after Planning review, Inspection, Legal check, or applicant appeal.

### 3.6 Applicant/third-party response

- Applicant response time is exogenous but can be modified by interventions D1 and D7.
- Third-party objections are latent, correlated across related cases, and only observed through public-notice duration.

### 3.7 Cross-case dependencies

- Same-architect applications may be correlated in arrival and inspection readiness.
- District-batched inspections may delay the first case in the batch but reduce total travel time.
- Objections may affect multiple public-notice cases in the same neighborhood/district.

### 3.8 Non-stationary demand

- Seasonal and daily demand patterns are deterministic inputs calibrated from the log.
- Stress scenarios can override them to test robustness.

### 3.9 Missing data

- Missing start timestamps are not treated as true service times.
- Missing objection events are treated as latent variables.
- The model truth is “reasonable belief”, not exact replication of unobserved events.

## 4. Experiment matrix

The table below defines the baseline, individual interventions D1–D10, a combined policy, and stress scenarios.

| Experiment ID | Policy / factor | Levels / settings | Expected trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BASE** | Current process, current demand | Arrival multipliers from log; current calendars; no new policy | Reference performance; exposes bottlenecks. |
| **D1** | Pre-submission document checker | Missing-doc loop probability reduced to 50% of baseline; applicant response time reduced by 20% for pre-checked cases; added applicant effort not modeled in minutes | Reduces Completeness rework and rework delays; may reduce early applicant burden but could discourage submissions if appointment capacity is low. |
| **D2** | Simple-renovation no-inspection rule | Simple renovation cases with `application_value < 50k` and no prior violation skip Inspection scheduling and Site inspection | Reduces inspection load and cycle time for low-risk cases; creates downside risk of undetected violations not fully captured by log. |
| **D3** | District-batched inspection scheduling | Batch cases by district; max batch size 2–3; travel time reduced by 30% per additional case in batch | Saves inspector travel time and increases throughput; may increase waiting of the first case in a batch and worsen district fairness. |
| **D4** | Temporary peak-month inspector | Add 1 inspector FTE in March–May and Sep–Nov | Reduces inspection queue peaks; increases cost and training overhead; no effect in non-peak months. |
| **D5** | Parallelize legal pre-check | Legal check can start for Commercial/Complex when Planning and Fire/Env results are available, before inspection or public notice | Reduces total legal-path cycle time; may increase legal rework due to incomplete information. |
| **D6** | Reserve one planner daily for aging cases | One planner per day handles cases older than e.g. 30 days | Improves p90/p95 cycle times; may slightly increase median duration of younger cases. |
| **D7** | Architect appointment slots for Monday submissions | Smooth 30% of Monday portal arrivals to Tue–Thu slots | Reduces Monday queue spikes and completeness-check waiting; may increase applicant inconvenience and appointment failure. |
| **D8** | Environmental-review triage | Use district/project attributes to triage cases into fast-track vs full review | Reduces environmental queue delays for low-risk cases; creates misclassification risk if attributes are incomplete. |
| **D9** | Public-notice objection early-warning | Early community notification/outreach before official notice; objection probability/latent extension reduced by 25% | Reduces long-tail public-notice cases; consumes communication staff and may not fully prevent objections. |
| **D10** | Combined low-risk bundle: D1+D2+D3+D4 | All four policies simultaneously | Large expected throughput and cycle-time improvement; interaction effects need simulation because reduced inspection load may alter batching behavior. |
| **S1** | Demand shock | Arrival multiplier = 2.0 for 6 months; Monday peak factor 2.5 | Tests whether interventions remain stable under overload; exposes capacity bottlenecks. |
| **S2** | Resource shortage | One inspector on sick leave; legal officer unavailable for 2 council-meeting weeks | Tests robustness of inspection and legal bottlenecks; D4 and D5 may mitigate. |
| **S3** | Objection wave | Objection probability doubled; public-notice extension tail 2x; appeal rework increased by 50% | Tests robustness of D9 and Complex variance long tail. |
| **S4** | Combined stress | S1 + S2 + S3 simultaneously | Tests full-system robustness and interaction effects. |

## 5. Warm-up, replications, and uncertainty treatment

### Warm-up

- Start the model with an initial WIP snapshot sampled from historical backlog, not empty, to avoid unrealistic initial behavior.
- Use a warm-up period of **180 simulation days**.
- Track mean WIP, queue length, and resource utilization in weekly batches; use Welch’s method or MSER to confirm stabilization.
- For intervention comparison, discard the first 180 days of output.

### Run length

- Simulate **24 months after warm-up** for all baseline and intervention runs.
- This includes at least two seasonal cycles and captures annual calendar effects.

### Replications and seeds

- Use **50 independent replications** for each scenario.
- For high-stress or high-variance scenarios, use **100 replications**.
- Fix random seed for baseline replication `r`; use the same seed for the corresponding intervention replication to enable paired comparisons (Common Random Numbers).

### Confidence/credible intervals

- Report percentile bootstrap 95% intervals for:
  - median cycle time;
  - p90 and p95 cycle times;
  - SLA compliance;
  - resource utilization;
  - rework rate.
- For heavy-tailed applicant response times, use robust quantile estimators and bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap.
- If Bayesian posterior samples are used for parameters, propagate them by sampling parameter vectors across replications, not just stochastic event noise.

### Sensitivity analysis

- Perform one-way sensitivity on parameters with highest uncertainty:
  - arrival multiplier;
  - applicant response tail parameters;
  - objection probability;
  - missing-data imputation assumptions.
- Use a factorial or Sobol design if the parameter space is small enough.
- Report “robustness sets”, not a single optimum.

### Heavy-tailed handling

- Fit mixture distributions for long tails;
- Winsorize only for numerical diagnostics, not for the simulation input;
- Report p90/p95 separately because medians hide tail risk.

## 6. Validation strategy

### Face validation

- Walk through the process tree and event-log excerpt with domain experts.
- Verify that calendars, resource pools, and policy interventions match the real operating environment.
- Confirm that the output metrics are meaningful to permit-office managers.

### Event-log replay validation

- Use PM4Py conformance checking on a holdout event log:
  - token-based replay or alignments;
  - report fitness and precision.
- The objective is to ensure the process-tree model can reproduce the main behavioral paths without impossible loops or deadlocks.

### Distributional validation

- Simulate baseline and compare simulated activity sojourn times, waiting times, cycle times, and variant frequencies to the empirical log.
- Use Q–Q plots, Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and percentile ratio comparisons.
- Agreement should be within tolerance for median and p90, not exact point-by-point fit.

### Resource-utilization validation

- Compare simulated utilization by resource pool to historical utilization inferred from event timestamps.
- Investigate any simulated resource with 100% utilization and no realistic queue, because it may indicate missing capacity or service-time overestimation.

### Bottleneck validation

- Identify bottlenecks from empirical performance DFG and from simulated average queue waiting times.
- Ensure the model’s bottleneck ordering matches observed process behavior: for example, Monday clerks, inspection travel, environmental review, and legal council-meeting lags.

### Out-of-sample validation

- Calibrate on the first 12 months of the event log.
- Simulate the last 6 months using observed initial backlog and demand.
- Compare forecast cycle times, WIP, and activity completion counts against the held-out data.
- Iterate if calibration fails on tail metrics.

## 7. Decision criteria

The final choice should not be based solely on average throughput. Use a multi-criteria decision framework with hard constraints and soft objectives.

### Metrics

- **Median cycle time** by case type.
- **Tail cycle times**: p90 and p95, especially for Commercial and Complex variance.
- **SLA compliance**: percentage of cases completed within regulatory target, e.g.:
  - Simple renovation: 30 days;
  - New residential: 60 days;
  - Commercial: 90 days;
  - Complex variance: 180 days.
- **Fairness**: coefficient of variation or Gini coefficient of median cycle time across districts and top architects; avoid intervention benefits concentrated in one district.
- **Resource utilization**: maintain skilled resources within target operating range, e.g., 50–90% average utilization; over 95% is a risk.
- **Rework rate**: fraction of cases with rework loops; no intervention should sharply increase rework unless caused by tighter legal scrutiny.
- **Backlog and WIP**: maximum weekly backlog and unfinished case count.
- **Robustness**: metric degradation under stress scenarios S1–S4.

### Decision rules

- **Hard constraints**:
  - p90 cycle time for any case type does not worsen by more than 10% vs baseline.
  - Resource utilization never exceeds 95% for any resource pool in more than 5% of weeks.
  - SLA compliance for Complex variance does not fall below current observed level.
- **Selection rule**:
  - Choose the intervention that improves median cycle time by a statistically significant margin, with the 95% CI lower bound above zero, while satisfying all hard constraints.
- If no intervention clearly dominates, present the Pareto frontier of interventions under baseline and stress scenarios, and recommend D10 only if it remains feasible under S1–S4.
