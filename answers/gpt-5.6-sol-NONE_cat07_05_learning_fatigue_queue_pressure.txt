# 1. Competing mechanism table

# 2. Integrated explanation

The pattern is a mixture of **composition, adaptation, operational pressure, and measurement effects**, not a single fatigue curve.

At the start of a shift, events are slower partly because staff receive a difficult inherited workload. Positions 1–5 have the highest observed complexity, priority share, and substantial backlog; senior staff also appear to take complex carryover cases deliberately. The decline from 18.8 to 13.6 minutes through positions 16–30 may contain genuine warm-up—workers reacquire task context and settle into a rhythm—but it also reflects declining complexity and priority. Raw early-versus-late comparisons therefore overstate learning.

Positions 31–50 combine greater familiarity with an easier case mix and relatively moderate queues. This is the period most consistent with efficient steady-state operation: service time is lower, but errors remain near 2.7%. A credible learning effect should look like this—faster processing without a major quality penalty—after adjusting for activity and case mix.

The later period is qualitatively different. At positions 51–75 and 76+, complexity continues to fall, yet queue length rises to 62 and then 88. Service time drops to 11.4 and 10.8 minutes, while errors and downstream rework increase sharply. The WIP table reproduces this pattern within broad complexity bands: high WIP is associated with faster handling and substantially poorer quality for both low- and high-complexity cases. Thus, late speed is unlikely to be learning alone. It is consistent with deadline effects, queue-clearing behavior, reduced review depth, batching, and possibly fatigue-induced compensatory rushing.

Fatigue may contribute, particularly when high cumulative workload, end-of-shift Quality Check bursts, and queue pressure occur together. However, it is not isolated by these aggregates. Later errors under low WIP are nearly stable for seniors, mid-level, and night staff and lower for juniors than in their first 20 events. Quality deteriorates most under high WIP. This points to an interaction: prolonged work may reduce resilience, but queue pressure appears to activate or amplify the quality problem.

Circadian effects are also plausible but confounded. Day-shift performance deteriorates when late-day demand raises the queue, while Early’s final hours are less problematic. Night workers handle fewer but more urgent exceptions and have longer service times. Comparisons across overlapping shifts are needed to distinguish clock-time effects from hours worked, demand, staffing, and case mix.

Batching further complicates recorded service time. Consecutive Document Reviews can create genuine setup efficiencies, rushed processing, or misleadingly short timestamps if work is performed before statuses are updated. End-of-shift Quality Check bursts shift errors and corrections across activities and times. Bot_Risk’s zero-duration events must be excluded from human service-time summaries, while its downstream corrections should be treated as automation–human system outcomes.

Overall, service time falls for at least three different reasons:

1. **Early case mix becomes easier.**
2. **Workers warm up and gain short-run task fluency.**
3. **Under late high WIP, workers or the system accelerate, batch, or abbreviate review.**

Errors rise mainly in the third regime, especially for complex cases and less-experienced workers. That is a conditional speed–quality tradeoff, not evidence that all late work or all faster work is intrinsically unsafe.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Statistical design

Use event-level models with resource-clustered standard errors or hierarchical effects.

### Within-resource and within-shift effects

For service time, fit a log-duration or gamma model:

\[
\log(ServiceTime_{e}) =
f(Position_{e}) + g(MinutesSinceStart_{e}) + h(WIP_{e})
+ Complexity_{e} + Priority_{e} + Activity_{e}
+ ClockHour_{e} + Shift_{e} + Batch_{e}
+ ResourceFE + \epsilon_e
\]

For errors and seven-day rework, use logistic or hierarchical logistic models with the same covariates.

Important specifications include:

- Resource fixed effects to compare each worker with themselves.
- Flexible position bins or splines rather than assuming a linear trend.
- Separate `sequence_position_within_shift` and `minutes_since_shift_start`; event count and elapsed time measure different exposures.
- Activity-family interactions, because fatigue and batching may differ by task.
- WIP × complexity, WIP × experience, and position × WIP interactions.
- Position × shift and position × clock-hour terms.
- Break exposure, cumulative active minutes, previous-shift rest, and consecutive workdays if available.
- Cluster uncertainty by resource and shift, since events in one shift are dependent.

A useful sequence of models is:

1. Position only.
2. Add resource and activity controls.
3. Add complexity and priority.
4. Add WIP, queue, and arrival/finish rates.
5. Add shift and clock time.
6. Add batching and automation interactions.

The change in estimated position effects across models indicates how much of the raw pattern is attributable to composition and pressure. It is not, by itself, a causal decomposition.

### WIP and queue stratification

Report curves separately for:

- WIP `<30`, `30–70`, and `>70`;
- low/high complexity;
- activity family;
- experience group;
- shift and clock-hour band.

Also fit WIP continuously with splines around 70. Test whether the apparent threshold is stable across shifts and activities. Use WIP at event start to reduce reverse timing, but recognize that case difficulty may already have contributed to the queue.

### Complexity and selection adjustment

Use observed complexity, priority, activity, path, inherited-backlog status, day-of-week, and preceding events. Examine assignment explicitly:

- Probability that a senior receives a high-complexity case by shift position.
- Probability that a junior receives a batchable activity late in a shift.
- Complexity distributions before and after handovers.
- Matched comparisons within resource, activity, complexity band, and queue band.

Do not treat the complexity score as complete. Missing documents, unusual paths, customer unavailability, and bot uncertainty may be unmeasured complexity.

### Circadian versus elapsed-work comparisons

Exploit overlapping shifts:

- At 10:00–13:00, compare Early workers late in their shift with Day workers early in theirs.
- At 15:00–17:00, compare Day workers late with Late workers early.
- Compare the same shift position across different clock hours and shifts.

If clock hour predicts performance after controlling for position, WIP, and case mix, that supports a circadian component. If elapsed time predicts outcomes at the same clock hour, that supports work-duration or fatigue mechanisms.

### Batching and timestamp validation

Identify sequential, simultaneous, and concurrent batches. Compare:

- batch versus non-batch service time;
- first, middle, and last event in a batch;
- error/rework rates by batch size;
- timestamp compression and inter-event gaps;
- final-45-minute Quality Check bursts.

Validate a sample against user-interface telemetry or direct observation. Otherwise, a short event duration may reflect delayed clicking rather than short actual effort.

### Resource-experience interactions

Model experience continuously and by group:

\[
Error \sim WIP \times Experience + Position \times Experience
+ Complexity \times Experience + Activity + Resource/Shift\ effects
\]

This can distinguish a general queue effect from a disproportionate impact on juniors. Avoid interpreting experience effects as innate worker quality; experienced workers receive different assignments and may perform more informal supervision.

### Downstream quality analysis

`error_flag` can be incomplete or inconsistently recorded. Link each event to:

- Rework Correction within 7 days;
- later Quality Check failure;
- repeated activity;
- case reopening;
- human override of Bot_Risk;
- path delay and total case duration.

Use survival analysis for time to correction and logistic models for any correction within a fixed window. Attribute quality outcomes to the process chain, not automatically to the last human who touched the case.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Risk or limitation | Event-log monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers** | May reduce extreme cumulative workload and protect recovery where overtime or extended shifts occur. | The observed deterioration happens within ordinary shifts and is tightly associated with WIP. A universal cap may not address demand peaks and could increase handovers or understaffing. It should not be treated as proof that fatigue caused the pattern. | Shift duration, prior rest, events and active minutes per shift, overtime, error/rework by duration and WIP, handover count, waiting time, and unfinished queue at shift end. |
| **2. Move complex cases away from the first hour** | Could reduce startup overload, especially for juniors, and allow routine warm-up. | May delay urgent or inherited complex cases, transfer burden to later high-WIP periods, and remove deliberate senior triage. Complexity should be routed by urgency and capability, not postponed mechanically. | Complexity and priority by position, time to first handling, SLA breaches, queue aging, error/rework, senior load, and whether complex cases collide with later pressure. |
| **3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP >70** | Directly targets the strongest observed risk regime. It could trigger staffing, throttling, second review, or reprioritization before quality deteriorates. | Alerts may be ignored, create alarm fatigue, or induce further rushing unless tied to operational support. The threshold may differ by activity and shift. | Time above threshold, response time, staffing changes, service time, errors, seven-day rework, complexity-stratified outcomes, alert frequency, false alarms, and queue recovery time. Use phased rollout if possible. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases** | Could improve causal evaluation and reduce discretionary cherry-picking in a controlled pilot. | Full randomization is operationally and ethically inappropriate where skills, urgency, continuity, language, or specialization matter. It may increase errors and remove beneficial senior handling. Prefer constrained randomization within eligible risk/skill strata. | Assignment probabilities, balance in complexity and priority, SLA, transfers, reassignments, errors/rework, continuity, worker load, and exceptions to randomization. |
| **5. Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours** | May restore attention and provide evidence about fatigue or recovery effects. Staggered breaks could particularly help during sustained load. | If staffing is unchanged, synchronized breaks may worsen WIP and shift the risk to coworkers. Compliance timestamps may not represent actual recovery. | Break timing/duration, pre/post-break error and speed, WIP during breaks, redistributed workload, overtime, missed breaks, and effects by experience and activity. Pilot with staggered coverage. |
| **6. Defer low-priority Quality Checks to the next shift** | Can smooth end-of-shift bursts and permit fuller review when current pressure is extreme. | Deferral creates handovers, increases waiting and feedback latency, and may allow defects to propagate. It must not become removal of Quality Checks merely to improve throughput. Only genuinely low-risk checks should be deferred with explicit ownership and deadlines. | QC waiting time and age, deferred volume, completion next shift, correction yield, escaped defects, reopenings, handovers, total case duration, and queue ownership changes. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Aligns experienced capacity with the Day queue increase and can support complex cases, junior escalation, and bot override review. This is strongly consistent with the observed pressure pattern. | Seniors may become bottlenecks or be diverted from other shifts. Effects may reflect extra capacity rather than seniority, and demand may shift. | WIP duration above 70, reviewer utilization, junior escalations, bot corrections, errors/rework, service and waiting times, complexity allocation, overtime, and spillover to Early/Late shifts. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | No reliable expected process benefit from raw-rate penalties; at most, it might change behavior superficially. | Unfair and likely counterproductive because late errors reflect WIP, case mix, assignment, batching, automation, and documentation. It encourages under-reporting, avoidance of complex cases, and gaming. Do not implement based on raw rates. | Replace punitive metrics with system-level, risk-adjusted learning measures. Audit reporting completeness, assignment mix, queue exposure, error severity, team outcomes, and psychological-safety indicators. Use individual review only with validated attribution and due process. |

A practical first package would combine a WIP alert with explicit response protocols, peak senior coverage, staggered micro-break pilots, and risk-based redistribution of work. Evaluate it with a phased or difference-in-differences design, monitoring both throughput and downstream quality.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Raw worker rankings are not risk-adjusted.** Seniors take complex carryover work; night staff receive urgent exceptions; juniors perform late Document Review batches. Different observed error rates may reflect assignments rather than competence.
- **Selection operates in both directions.** Managers route difficult work to trusted staff, and workers may select tasks based on expertise or queue conditions. Random assignment is neither currently present nor necessarily desirable.
- **Complexity measurement is incomplete.** A single score may omit poor documentation, customer availability, exceptional policy rules, path history, language requirements, and bot confidence. Residual differences should not automatically be attributed to effort or fatigue.
- **Error attribution may be wrong.** A Quality Check can reveal a defect originating earlier; a Rework Correction timestamp records discovery or repair, not necessarily creation. Case-level causal attribution requires path analysis and manual validation.
- **Recorded service time can be inaccurate.** Batching, delayed status updates, parallel work, interruptions, and automated timestamps can make duration appear unusually short or long.
- **Management creates part of the exposure.** Staffing, scheduling, backlog handovers, performance targets, queue policies, and delayed Quality Checks influence WIP. Workers should not be blamed for errors produced by an understaffed high-pressure system.
- **Automation is part of the sociotechnical process.** Bot_Risk’s instant events should not make human work look slow, and its errors should not be attributed automatically to reviewers. Monitor calibration, confidence, overrides, false negatives, and whether queue pressure reduces human scrutiny.
- **Punitive error metrics encourage gaming.** Penalties can cause under-reporting, avoidance of difficult cases, unnecessary referrals, delayed completion, and reluctance to correct automation. Raw late-shift rates are especially unsuitable for discipline.
- **Small samples require uncertainty.** Night staff and narrow resource/activity strata may have unstable rates. Show confidence or credible intervals and minimum-volume rules.
- **Fairness includes workload, not just outcomes.** Track exposure to high WIP, urgent work, complex cases, undesirable shifts, and correction work. Equal error targets under unequal exposures are not equitable.
- **Monitoring must have governance.** Limit access to identifiable worker data, define retention and appeal processes, document metric definitions, involve workers in interpretation, and prioritize process improvement over surveillance.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should support filters for date, shift, clock hour, activity, resource group, individual resource where authorized, experience band, complexity, priority, WIP band, batch status, and bot involvement. Every rate should show event counts and uncertainty.

| Indicator | Definition and display | Diagnostic purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Human service time** | Median, mean, and 90th percentile from `start_timestamp` to `time:timestamp`, excluding Bot_Risk; trend by position, minutes since shift start, activity, and WIP. | Separates stable efficiency from extreme durations and reveals the late speed-up. |
| **2. Waiting time** | Case/activity waiting time from `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; median and p90 by shift and priority. | Detects whether throughput gains merely move delay elsewhere. |
| **3. WIP and queue exposure** | Current WIP/queue, percentage of event starts above 70, time above threshold, and queue trajectory from shift start to end. | Measures the main operational pressure regime and alert performance. |
| **4. Error rate** | `error_flag` per event, risk-adjusted and raw, stratified by WIP, complexity, activity, position, and shift; include confidence intervals. | Prevents misleading aggregate worker comparisons and reveals pressure interactions. |
| **5. Rework and correction rate** | Rework within 7 days, repeated activities, Rework Correction cases, and time to correction using `get_rework_cases_per_activity`. | Captures delayed quality outcomes that event-level error flags miss. |
| **6. Complexity and priority mix** | Mean complexity, band shares, high-priority share, and inherited-backlog share by shift position and resource group. | Makes early-shift selection and senior case allocation visible. |
| **7. Batching indicators** | Number and share of events in batches, batch size, sequential/concurrent type, within-batch inter-event time, and batch-specific error/rework. | Distinguishes setup efficiency, timestamp compression, and rushed bulk work. |
| **8. Handover and queue ownership changes** | Cases crossing shifts, resource changes, queue reassignment count, age at handover, and unresolved ownership. | Detects quality and delay risks created by deferral or shift boundaries. |
| **9. Worker-load fairness** | Per-resource distribution of event count, active minutes, high-WIP exposure, complex/urgent share, correction work, undesirable shifts, and missed breaks. | Evaluates whether burdens and risky assignments are distributed fairly. |
| **10. Position-based speed–quality panel** | Service time, error, and seven-day rework by sequence-position bin, with adjusted and unadjusted curves. | Shows whether apparent learning remains after accounting for case mix and pressure. |
| **11. Shift and clock-time heat map** | Performance by clock hour and elapsed shift hour, including overlapping-shift comparisons. | Helps separate circadian/time-of-day patterns from time-on-task and demand. |
| **12. Automation interaction** | Bot volume, bot confidence where available, human override/correction rate, review delay, and outcomes by WIP and complexity. | Detects whether automation or reduced human oversight contributes to high-WIP failures. |
| **13. Quality Check pipeline** | QC queue age, checks completed in final 45 minutes, deferrals, correction yield, escaped defects, and next-shift completion. | Prevents throughput improvements from being achieved by suppressing or delaying assurance work. |
| **14. Arrival/finish balance** | Case arrival and finish rates, net queue growth, and staffing by interval using `insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`. | Identifies demand–capacity imbalance before WIP exceeds the risk threshold. |
| **15. Process and path performance** | Performance DFG, high-delay/high-rework paths, and cluster/variant outcomes using `discover_performance_dfg` and `cluster_log`. | Identifies whether specific process routes, rather than workers, create delay or error. |
| **16. Intervention safety panel** | Pre/post or control/treatment trends for WIP, service, waiting, errors, rework, handovers, fairness, and SLA breaches. | Ensures an intervention does not improve throughput by shifting risk, delaying checks, or overloading another shift. |

The dashboard should visually distinguish **descriptive raw metrics** from **risk-adjusted estimates**. It should emphasize system and team diagnostics by default; identifiable worker views should be restricted to supportive coaching, workload balancing, and validated case review rather than punitive ranking.
