## 1. Current-process diagnosis

The process has three different problems that are being blurred into one “fast versus slow” debate: an over-broad fast lane, an unstable evidence-completion loop, and a fragmented post-decision process. The frequency DFG identifies where these problems occur, but its edge counts are not complete case variants and should not be summed as though every edge represented a different case. The listed terminal edges also cover fewer than 5,000 cases, so they cannot support an exact completion-rate calculation without case-level trace analysis. 

**The dominant fast path is a local speed-and-cost optimum, but not necessarily an end-to-end optimum.** The frequency DFG’s main spine is:

`Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement -> Quality/compliance close`

with 3,600 cases entering auto screening, 2,550 auto-to-fast approvals, and 2,300 fast approvals followed by replacement dispatch. The selected performance-DFG medians along that spine are very short: 0.5 hours, 0.4 hours, 1.8 hours, and 3.2 hours respectively. Their sum—about 5.9 hours—is not a valid case-cycle estimate because conditional edge medians cannot simply be added, but it explains why the path looks operationally attractive.

The contradiction is that this path is inexpensive only when measurement stops too early:

* Fast approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits.
* Complaint/reopen incidence is 14.1% on the fast path versus 3.3% on the completed repair path.
* A complaint typically emerges well after the apparent success event: `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` has a 70-hour median and a 180-hour p90.
* Once reopened, another 31 median hours are spent reaching manual review, or 42 median hours reaching the escalation board.

Thus, the current process optimizes time to first dispatch or first close, while externalizing cost and elapsed time into later complaint and recovery cycles.

**The fast lane is not uniformly inappropriate.** Segment outcomes show that a single global routing rule would be a mistake:

* For Premium low-risk accessories, fast processing gives an 8-hour median and a 6% complaint rate. Full manual processing takes 46 hours, reduces complaints to 3%, but crosses the 24-hour service-credit threshold. Keeping a controlled fast lane is defensible.
* For Standard low-risk accessories, fast processing takes 13 hours with 8% complaints; manual processing takes 52 hours with 4% complaints. Because customers tolerate up to 72 hours when status visibility is good, there is room for a modest control without necessarily harming customer experience.
* For Premium high-value devices, the contrast is severe: 20 hours and 22% complaints on the fast path versus 96 hours and 5% complaints on the reviewed/repair path.
* For Standard high-value devices, the equivalent comparison is 28 hours and 18% complaints versus 120 hours and 6% complaints. The expected cost of a wrong high-value replacement is EUR 410, versus EUR 95 for a low-risk accessory.

The reviewed path’s lower complaint rate is observational rather than causal proof; reviewed and fast cases may differ in complexity. Nevertheless, the segment-controlled differences, high wrong-replacement cost, and conformance evidence jointly make unrestricted high-value fast approval difficult to defend.

**The evidence-completion subprocess is a separate bottleneck.** The performance DFG shows a 26-hour median and 88-hour p90 from `Request missing evidence` back to `Manual eligibility review`. Twenty-six percent of cases entering evidence collection do so at least twice. The frequency DFG includes 320 transitions from evidence request to abandonment, while the unclear-defect/missing-evidence segment has a 140-hour median and the highest abandonment. This is not simply a slow manual-review problem: evidence is requested incompletely, returned asynchronously, and then reconsidered through repeated review cycles.

**Conformance results show widespread rule failures despite apparently high aggregate fitness.**

* Token-based replay fitness is 0.91, but only 68% of traces fit. The high average fitness therefore masks deviations distributed across roughly one-third of traces.
* Alignments identify `Fast approval` before evidence completeness, high-value dispatch without risk review, and complaints/reopens after close.
* These deviations directly implicate R1 and R2. R3 is also exposed when reopened cases can progress toward another terminal outcome without an enforced manual review.
* Low discovered-model precision means the mined model overgeneralizes rare mixtures of auto approval, risk review, and repair. That discovered model should not be used as the redesign blueprint merely because it reproduces most observed behavior.
* The 350 direct transitions from `Fast approval` to `Quality/compliance close` require case-level investigation. They could represent legitimate no-fulfillment cases, omitted events, or premature closure; the DFG alone cannot distinguish them.

**Capacity is imbalanced rather than universally insufficient.** Seven manual reviewers provide 252 productive hours per week. Because their queue grows sharply beyond 82% utilization, the practical queue-risk boundary is approximately 207 productive reviewer-hours per week, although case throughput cannot be calculated without review service times. Updating the auto rules could increase manual inflow by 15–35%, so a compliant rule change without capacity controls could simply move the bottleneck.

Fraud/risk review has only two specialists and predictable Monday and campaign peaks. Repair-quote delay is mainly part-price waiting rather than technician work, while the two quality inspectors are often underused. The escalation board has strong customer impact but only one 45-minute daily slot and therefore cannot be the normal recovery mechanism.

The three redesigns below intentionally attack different root causes:

1. Redesign A corrects routing and mandatory controls.
2. Redesign B reconstructs evidence completion and the customer interaction.
3. Redesign C changes ownership and fulfillment for high-value cases.

## 2. Redesign A: Guardrailed segment-and-risk fast lane

**Redesigned process flow.**

`Intake -> segment/value/defect/evidence-aware auto triage -> compliant branch -> technical decision -> fulfillment or repair -> compliance close`

The auto screen becomes a router rather than an unrestricted approval engine:

* **Premium low-risk accessory:** retain the rapid auto-approval and replacement branch, using the explicit R2 exception. Part and product matching should be automated where possible so that a human gate does not jeopardize the 24-hour service expectation.
* **Standard low-risk accessory:** require an explicit `Evidence completeness confirmed` event, then permit fast approval. Add a lightweight pre-dispatch part-selection check. The available 6–18-hour control delay is more tolerable here than for Premium low-risk claims, provided status visibility is good and p90 performance is controlled.
* **Any claim above EUR 400 and all high-value devices:** auto screening may classify and prioritize the case, but it may not give final fast approval. The workflow must require either manual eligibility review or fraud/risk review before approval, satisfying R1. Evidence completeness must be recorded before dispatch, satisfying R2.
* **Risk-indicated cases:** route to the two fraud/risk specialists. High-value cases without a fraud indicator should normally use manual review rather than consuming scarce fraud capacity merely to satisfy R1.
* **Replacement of a high-value device:** require a pre-dispatch part/device match gate. A repair quote remains available when replacement certainty is low.
* **Any reopened claim:** automatically route to manual eligibility review before another close or denial, implementing R3 as a hard workflow constraint rather than a warning.

Gate outcomes, override reasons, device value, evidence state, and selected replacement part should be logged as auditable events or attributes.

**Targets.**

This design primarily targets the 35% of cases that are Premium or Standard high-value devices, the 25% Standard low-risk segment where a modest gate is operationally tolerable, and all reopened cases. It deliberately preserves the fastest treatment for the 18% Premium low-risk segment.

**Mechanism of improvement.**

The design stops using frequency as a proxy for appropriateness. Segment-aware auto rules prevent high-value and incomplete-evidence cases from entering a path that is fast only before downstream failure is counted. The high-value part-selection gate directly addresses the long-tail `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` pattern and the EUR 410 expected wrong-replacement cost.

The existing underused quality inspectors are the most plausible initial resource for the pre-dispatch gate. Their use must be dynamically reduced or supplemented during repair campaigns, when their current workload rises. The auto-rule update is deployable within two weeks, making the routing component relatively reversible and testable.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.**

Redesign A accepts that first-pass cycle time will increase for high-value cases. That is a deliberate trade: a 20- or 28-hour fast-path median is not a desirable outcome when it carries 22% or 18% complaint rates and material compliance exposure.

The design may increase manual-review inflow by 15–35%, potentially pushing reviewer utilization beyond the 82% queue threshold. It also does little to solve repeated evidence requests or part-price waiting. A pre-dispatch gate can catch an incorrect part selection, but it cannot repair poor evidence collection or an incorrect eligibility decision upstream.

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first, assisted-completion journey

**Redesigned process flow.**

`Intake -> lightweight eligibility/evidence triage -> consolidated evidence package if needed -> portal or assisted completion -> Evidence completeness confirmed -> final eligibility/risk decision -> technical decision -> fulfillment or repair -> close`

The key changes are:

1. The first eligibility touch identifies the complete defect-specific evidence package, rather than requesting one document at a time.
2. The customer receives one structured request containing required documents, acceptable alternatives, examples, and a visible completion status.
3. The portal performs basic validation—such as file readability, required fields, serial-number consistency, and document type—before submission.
4. The design is **digital-first, not digital-only**. Customers can select assisted completion, and cases showing difficulty should be offered human support rather than being repeatedly rejected by the portal.
5. Premium customers receive proactive assistance before evidence delay becomes a service failure. Low-risk claims whose required evidence is already held by the organization continue without portal friction.
6. A second evidence request is allowed only when new facts emerge or the first package was objectively deficient. The reason must be logged.
7. Manual eligibility review does not repeatedly restart while the evidence package is still incomplete. Once completeness is confirmed, the case returns to the appropriate auto, manual, or risk decision branch.
8. Standard customers receive event-triggered status updates during customer waiting, internal review, quote preparation, and fulfillment.

**Targets.**

The principal target is the unclear-defect/missing-evidence segment, representing 22% of cases, together with the 26% repeat-request rate among cases that enter evidence collection. It also targets the 320 observed `Request missing evidence -> Claim abandoned` transitions and the 34-hour mean/88-hour p90 return from evidence request to manual review.

**Mechanism of improvement.**

A new evidence portal is estimated to reduce repeated evidence requests by 30–45%. The redesign strengthens that technology with process changes: one consolidated request, an explicit completeness event, assisted access, and restrictions on repeated piecemeal requests.

The expected improvement is not merely shorter upload time. Better evidence at the decision point should reduce unnecessary manual reconsideration, improve part selection, and make R2 enforceable because evidence completeness becomes an observable process state. Status visibility also uses the stated tolerance of Standard low-risk customers: a visible 40–60-hour process may be perceived more favorably than a nominally fast process that goes silent and later reopens.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.**

The portal front-loads effort and may make some simple claims feel more difficult. It could increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy, especially if the assisted channel is slow or stigmatized. Consequently, portal completion cannot be made the only access route.

This redesign does not by itself solve fraud-review scarcity, high-value replacement errors, or the 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate. It can also shift defects downstream: a portal may mark all required files as present even when their substantive quality is insufficient. “Evidence complete” must therefore mean decision-usable, not merely uploaded.

## 4. Redesign C: End-to-end value-stream cells with stable-resolution close

**Redesigned process flow.**

After eligibility and evidence completion, cases are assigned to one of two end-to-end operating cells rather than being passed among generic queues.

**Accessory replacement cell**

`Eligible low-risk claim -> part match -> approval -> dispatch -> delivery confirmation -> quality/compliance close`

This cell preserves rapid processing, especially for Premium low-risk accessories. One case owner remains accountable through dispatch and any immediate customer issue.

**High-value resolution cell**

`Reviewed eligibility/risk decision -> technical diagnosis -> repair-versus-replacement decision -> repair quote/customer approval or validated replacement -> embedded quality check -> provisional resolution -> stable-resolution confirmation -> quality/compliance close`

Within the high-value cell:

* Premium high-value cases receive reserved expedited reviewed capacity, but no mandatory control is bypassed.
* A nonbinding part-price query begins as soon as a likely-repair case is identified, in parallel with remaining review work. The formal repair quote is issued only after eligibility and technical requirements are satisfied.
* Repair is preferred when technically and economically justified, but it is not mandatory. The 12.5% rework rate for repaired high-value devices means an unconditional “repair everything” rule would be unsafe.
* A quality inspector is embedded in the cell for repair quality and high-value replacement matching.
* Dispatch or repair completion produces an operational `Provisional resolution`, not immediate administrative success.
* Final `Quality/compliance close` occurs after required controls are complete and there is no unresolved delivery, part-selection, or repair issue.
* A reopen returns to the same cell and case owner, with mandatory manual eligibility review as required by R3.
* The escalation board is reserved for policy exceptions, disputed remedies, or unusually severe customer failures. It is not the ordinary route for every complaint.

**Targets.**

This redesign targets Premium and Standard high-value devices, high-value repair rework, wrong replacement selection, the long reopen cycle, and the premature-close problem indicated by complaints after close. It is particularly relevant where continuity and first-time-right resolution matter more than minimum time to dispatch.

**Mechanism of improvement.**

The redesign changes organizational ownership rather than just adding decision rules. An end-to-end owner sees eligibility evidence, technical reasoning, part selection, fulfillment, and any complaint, reducing the likelihood that each queue locally optimizes its own task while downstream costs remain invisible.

Early part-price checking attacks the stated source of repair-quote delay. Embedded inspection uses currently underutilized quality capacity and addresses both the 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate and expensive wrong replacements. Separating provisional resolution from final compliance close removes the incentive to declare success before the likely 70–180-hour complaint window has matured.

For Premium high-value customers, the objective is an expedited **reviewed** path, not a return to uncontrolled fast approval.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.**

Dedicated cells reduce resource pooling. A high-value cell may be overloaded after campaigns while the accessory cell is underused, or vice versa. Cross-training and explicit overflow rules would therefore be necessary.

Upfront labor cost will probably rise, and formal time to final close may lengthen because closing is no longer used as an early success marker. The design should consequently report both time to operational resolution and time to final compliance close. It also does not directly repair the missing-evidence journey; unclear cases may still arrive at the cell late unless Redesign B is separately adopted.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Mandatory controls should not be randomized.** R1, R2, and R3 are the minimum compliant process, not optional experimental treatments. The organization should stop knowingly allowing high-value approval without review, dispatch without required evidence completeness, or reopened cases reaching a terminal outcome without manual review. Experiments should compare different compliant ways of delivering the process.

### Stage 0: Establish a measurement contract

Before comparing designs, add or validate the following event-log elements:

* `Evidence completeness confirmed`, including who or what confirmed it.
* Claim value, device class, defect type, customer segment, and route-selection reason.
* Selected replacement part/device, pre-dispatch gate result, and override reason.
* Operational resolution, provisional resolution, final compliance close, and any subsequent reopen linked to the same claim.
* Reviewer, fraud, technician, inspector, and escalation workload at assignment time.
* Direct downstream costs: reshipment, repair rework, service credit, complaint handling, and escalation.

The current “complaint after close” pattern means first close cannot be the sole outcome boundary. Report time to first decision, time to fulfillment, time to stable resolution, and time to final administrative close separately.

### Stage 1: First pilot—Redesign A on Standard high-value devices

Use one sufficiently frequent high-value product or defect family, initially excluding Premium cases so that the organization can learn without exposing its strictest service segment. Enforce R1 and R2 for every eligible case. Use a stepped rollout or randomized comparison only for nonmandatory elements such as the specific pre-dispatch gate design.

The Standard high-value segment is the best first test because its fast-path complaint rate is 18%, its wrong-replacement exposure is high, and it does not carry the stated Premium low-risk 24-hour service-credit constraint.

**Go indicators:**

* An approximately 80–90% or greater reduction in R1/R2 alignment deviations, with no increase in R3 deviations.
* A 25–35% relative reduction in complaint/reopen or wrong-part reshipment among mature cases.
* A reduction in total cost to stable resolution, even if first-dispatch cost increases.
* Gate delay generally within the stated 6–18-hour range.
* Manual-review utilization remaining below the 82% queue-risk threshold.
* Time to stable resolution no worse than matched, currently compliant reviewed cases by more than roughly 10–15%.
* No material deterioration—approximately more than 10%—in nonpilot manual-review p90.

**Stop or redesign indicators:**

* Sustained manual utilization above 82%.
* Gate delays above 18 hours without a meaningful error-detection yield.
* Compliance deviations merely change event labels rather than disappear.
* Complaints fall in the pilot but rise in nonpilot cohorts because reviewer or inspector capacity has been displaced.
* Premium or low-risk performance deteriorates through shared-resource spillover.

### Stage 2: Pilot Redesign B on unclear-defect/missing-evidence cases

Run a digital-first portal cohort against a current-process or stepped-wedge cohort, but make assisted submission available in both. Stratify results by customer segment, defect family, chosen access channel, and need for assistance.

**Go indicators:**

* Repeated evidence requests fall by approximately 30–45%, consistent with the stated potential, or at minimum show a robust reduction of about 25%.
* Median and p90 time in evidence waiting decline by roughly 20–30%.
* Abandonment does not increase by more than about 2–3 percentage points overall.
* No material abandonment or completion-rate gap—roughly 3–5 percentage points—emerges between customers using the digital and assisted channels.
* Manual reviewer touches per completed case fall rather than simply moving to portal-support contacts.
* Evidence marked complete is rarely rejected later as unusable.

**Stop or redesign indicators:**

* Abandonment increases despite shorter processing among completers.
* Portal users appear fast only because difficult or digitally excluded customers leave the process.
* Evidence completeness improves administratively but downstream complaints, fraud referrals, or technical rejections rise.
* Portal support becomes another queue with comparable p90 delay to the former evidence loop.

### Stage 3: Pilot Redesign C for one high-value device family

Proceed only after routing and evidence data are reliable enough to estimate true high-value demand. Form one limited end-to-end cell with manual eligibility capability, technical expertise, access to fraud review when indicated, and embedded quality inspection.

Premium high-value cases may be included in a later pilot wave after the cell demonstrates stable reviewed-cycle performance.

**Go indicators:**

* High-value complaint/reopen declines by roughly 35–50% relative to an appropriately matched baseline.
* Wrong-replacement cost per resolved case declines materially.
* High-value repair rework moves from 12.5% toward a pilot guardrail of approximately 8–10% or lower.
* Median and p90 time to stable resolution improve against the existing 96-hour Premium and 120-hour Standard reviewed-path medians, or at least remain stable while quality improves substantially.
* Escalation-board demand remains exceptional rather than becoming the normal end of the cell.
* Non-cell repair, quality, and quote queues do not experience material p90 deterioration.

**Stop or redesign indicators:**

* First-pass quality improves only because the cell avoids difficult repairs or sends more cases to denial.
* Dedicated resources are idle while non-cell queues grow.
* Repair rework remains near or above 12.5%.
* Provisional-resolution duration grows without reducing later complaints.
* The cell lowers its own cycle time by shifting part-price, inspection, or escalation waiting to other teams.

### Metrics to monitor across all stages

Every pilot should use the same balanced scorecard:

* **Cycle time:** median and p90 to decision, dispatch/repair, stable resolution, and final close—not only the mean.
* **Cost:** total cost to stable resolution, including review labor, reshipments, repair rework, service credits, complaints, and escalation.
* **Quality:** first-time-right fulfillment, wrong replacement, complaint/reopen, repeat repair, and repeated evidence request.
* **Compliance:** case-level R1/R2/R3 violations, alignment moves, percentage fitting traces, and override reasons. The 0.91 token fitness should not be used alone.
* **Customer experience:** abandonment, status-related contacts, service-credit exposure, complaint severity, and recovery outcome.
* **Resources:** reviewer utilization, fraud backlog by weekday/campaign, inspector wait, quote waiting, portal-support wait, and escalation-board demand.
* **Distributional effects:** all metrics by Premium/Standard, value class, defect type, access channel, and pilot/nonpilot status.

### Determining whether improvement is real or merely shifted downstream

Cases must remain under observation beyond the 180-hour p90 for post-dispatch complaints. A fixed minimum maturation window of at least eight days—and preferably a common 14-day or longer business window—should be applied before final complaint and cost comparisons. Incomplete cases should be treated as right-censored rather than silently excluded.

Pre/post process-mining comparisons should specifically test whether:

* `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` becomes less frequent, rather than merely occurring later.
* `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review -> Request missing evidence` loops decline.
* Performance-DFG p90 values improve on targeted edges without worsening neighboring queues.
* R1–R3 alignment deviations genuinely disappear.
* Premature close is replaced by stable resolution, not by a renamed intermediate event.
* Nonpilot cases do not absorb the waiting time or workload removed from pilot cases.

The recommended portfolio direction is therefore not one universal redesign. Apply Redesign A as the immediate control and routing foundation, adopt Redesign B where evidence loops dominate provided accessibility guardrails pass, and reserve Redesign C for high-value product families where the reduction in wrong fulfillment and reopen work justifies dedicated ownership.
