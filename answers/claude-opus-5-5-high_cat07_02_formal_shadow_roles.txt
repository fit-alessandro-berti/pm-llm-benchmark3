# 1. Proposed role structure

The structure below has three layers. It is not a single partition of resources.

1. **Formal roles** come from titles, certifications and home teams. They describe authority.
2. **Observed operational roles** come from activity mix, case complexity and position in the handover network. They describe what people actually do.
3. **Overlays** are shadow, bridging and boundary roles. They explain cross-functional work, substitutions and rare interventions.

A resource can hold a core role and one or more peripheral or overlay roles. Membership is weighted by the share of the resource's work, the complexity of that work, and the authority it carries. It is not decided by the single most frequent activity.

Reference figures derived from the matrix (10 weeks):

- **A1 Register:** 1,668 events in total.
- **A2 Validate:** 2,251 events in total. This is more than A1, so many cases are validated twice: a bot precheck followed by human validation.
- **A6 Security Review:** 146 events. Erik 66%, Chen 16%, Mateo 11%, Dina 4%, Ivan 3%.

- **A9 Supervisor Approval:** 218 events. Hana 63%, Mateo 20%, Giorgio 9%, Fatima 7%.
- **A11 Quality Check:** 684 events. Ivan and Julia 89%.
- **A13 Reopen Request:** 140 events. Mateo 30%, Hana 17%, Chen 11%.

# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

Working definitions:

- **Core** activities carry a large share of the resource's work *and* match the resource's authority and certification.
- **Peripheral** activities are low-share, situational, or substitution-driven. They are also peripheral when they are performed without formal authority.
- Some peripheral activities are **authority-sensitive**: A2 by uncertified staff, A6, A7 and A9. These are listed explicitly even when their counts are small, because low frequency does not mean low risk.

## Automated intake & entitlement precheck (R14_Nora)
- **Core:** A1 and the A2 precheck. Nothing else.
- **Frequency:** 760 A1 and 610 A2 events. About 150 registrations (20%) skip the automated precheck. The size of the bot's decision boundary is one of the most important numbers in the process.
- **Complexity:** 85 medium-complexity cases get an automated precheck. Their human re-validation rate should be measured.
- **Authority:** None in the human sense. The rule owner is accountable. The bot's A2 is a *precheck*, not the entitlement decision, and should be labelled that way in the log (for example "A2a precheck" and "A2b validation").
- **Substitution:** Not applicable. Bot outages are the resilience scenario, and they would move about 45% of intake onto humans.

## Front-office service agents (R01_Amira, R02_Ben)
- **Core:** A1, A2, A4, A12. These are 86% (Amira) and 91% (Ben) of their events. Both are entitlement certified, so their A2 is formally authoritative.
- **Peripheral:** A3 first-level diagnosis (75/60), A8 (42/25), A5 (30/18), A10, A11, A14.
- **Authority-sensitive periphery:** Amira has 18 A7 events and Ben has 5, although neither holds a pricing certification. This could be preparatory logging ("exception requested") or real uncertified review.
- **Complexity:** Amira handles about twice as many high and critical cases as Ben (67 vs 34) and has a higher median (0.34 vs 0.29). She is the senior-track member of the role, not a different role.
- **Substitution and rework:** They absorb rework from the temps' reopenings (fragment 6). That load is hidden in their A2/A4/A13 counts.

## Temporary intake & standard-response agents (R11_Kai, R12_Lina)
- **Core (operationally):** A1, A2, A4, A12, which are 98–99% of their events. Cases are almost all low complexity (310 and 290 low, 0 critical).
- **Core by design:** A1, A4 and A12 for simple requests. A2 is **operationally core but formally unauthorized**, because they hold no entitlement certification. The 17 reopenings traced to incomplete validation notes show the risk.
- **Peripheral, should be eliminated:** A3 Diagnose (8/12) and A8 Negotiate (3/4). These are out of scope for uncertified seasonal staff.
- **Substitution:** The role is itself a capacity substitution, seasonal load support. The fact that they do A2 does not show entitlement competence.
- **Authority:** Closing simple requests only, ideally after a Nora precheck or with a mandatory validation-note template.

## Hybrid front-office / commercial senior agent (R07_Giorgio)
- **Core, two parts:**
  - Front office: about 68% of events (A2 190, A4 150, A12 130, A1 90).
  - Commercial: A8 88 and A7 72. This is 22% of his events, but a large share of the organization's commercial capacity: 31% of A7 and 26% of all A8 (88 of 336 A8 events in total).
- **Substitution-adjusted:** 31 of his 72 A7 events and 18 of his 88 A8 events came from Fatima's 6-day absence. Excluding them leaves 41 A7 and 70 A8 events in normal operation. That is a stable, recurring shadow commercial function, not only emergency cover.
- **Peripheral but authority-sensitive:** 20 A9 Supervisor Approval events by a Senior Service Agent who has no approval certification.
- **Complexity:** Median 0.58 and 36 critical cases. His commercial work concentrates on complex front-office cases (the caveat in the similarity table).
- **Performance under substitution:** The median pricing duration doubled (38 to 74 minutes). He is competent enough to keep the flow moving but not equivalent to the specialist.

## Technical analysts, complex tier (R03_Chen) and standard tier (R04_Dina)
- **Core (both):** A3, A5, A10. These are about 81% of Chen's events and 84% of Dina's. Both are configuration certified.
- **Peripheral (both):** A4, A12, A2, A8, A13, A14, A11.
- **Where they differ:**
  - Chen has 222 high+critical cases (42 critical) against Dina's 91 (9 critical). His median is 0.71 against her 0.48.
  - Chen has 24 A6 security reviews against Dina's 6, and neither holds a security certification.
  - Chen works the late shift, when Erik (day shift) is unavailable. That makes Chen a likely de facto after-hours security fallback.
- **Authority:** Configuration and implementation authority. No security sign-off authority. Chen's A6 events are peripheral but high-risk.
- **Interpretation:** One formal role with two operational tiers. Dina is a development-stage analyst, not a lower-value one.

## Security review specialist (R05_Erik)
- **Core:** A6. That is 96 events, 56% of his own work and 66% of all A6.
- **Peripheral:** A3, A5, A10, A11, A14 on the same security cases, where he follows a case through implementation and KB capture.
- **Complexity:** 83% of his cases are high or critical. He has 55 critical cases.
- **Frequency vs importance:** He has the lowest human event volume (170) but the highest median complexity (0.86).
- **Substitution:** In weeks 7–8, his A6 count fell because Mateo filtered referrals, not because demand fell. A frequency drop there reflects routing, not a change in role.
- **Authority:** Sole certified security authority, which makes him a single point of failure.

## Commercial exception specialist (R06_Fatima)
- **Core:** A7 (122) and A8 (90), 73% of her events.
- **Peripheral:** A2 (22, commercial entitlement check), A12, A3, A10, A4.
- **Authority-sensitive:** 16 A9 events. These could be delegated commercial sign-off within limits, or approvals logged on behalf of the supervisor.
- **Complexity:** Medium to high (median 0.67).
- **Substitution:** Her absence showed that the process depends on her. Throughput held because Giorgio stepped in, but the pricing cycle time doubled.

## Supervisory approval & escalation authority (R08_Hana)
- **Core:** A9 (138, 56% of her own work and 63% of all A9 events).
- **Secondary core:** A13 Reopen (24) and A8 Negotiate (22). These fit a supervisor who handles escalated complaints and negotiations.
- **Peripheral:** A11 (12, supervisory spot checks), A2, A3, A7, A12.
- **Complexity:** 192 of 274 cases are high or critical. She has 62 critical cases.
- **Substitution:** In week 5, Mateo's preliminary approvals were rechecked at a 62% rate (13 of 21). This confirms that final approval authority stayed with Hana even when she was absent.
- **Authority:** The only approval-certified resource. Her authority is out of proportion to her event volume.

## Cross-functional escalation & reopen broker (R13_Mateo)
- **Core, partly invisible:** Coordination and routing, which appears in the fragments but is not one of A1–A14. His logged core is A13 (42, 30% of all reopens) and the reopen and escalation loop (fragment 5).
- **Peripheral:** Everything else at low volume.
  - A9 has 44 events: 21 preliminary approvals in week 5, and 23 in normal weeks that need explaining.
  - A6 has 16 events. These are most plausibly triage, based on weeks 7–8.
  - There are 10 A7 events.
- **Complexity:** Median 0.77 and 76 critical cases, the most of anyone. He sees the organization's hardest cases, but mostly as a router.
- **Authority:** Escalation certified. He has no approval or security authority. His preliminary decisions are advisory unless a policy delegates them.

## Quality gatekeeper & knowledge steward (R09_Ivan, R10_Julia)
- **Core:** A11 (310/298) and A14 (96/104). They perform 89% of quality checks. Julia does slightly more KB updates, and Ivan slightly more complex cases.
- **Peripheral:** A10 (38/35), A5 (26/22), A12, A3, A13. Ivan also has 4 A6 events on the late shift.
- **Complexity:** Medium (medians 0.52 and 0.48).
- **Authority:** Quality certified. Their A10 and A5 events are formally out of role. They also create a segregation-of-duties risk if the same analyst checks a solution they implemented or configured themselves.
- **Substitution:** Split day and late shifts give natural mutual cover. This is the most resilient role.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

None of these roles is improper in itself. Each one exists because the formal structure leaves a gap. Each is listed with its benefits, its risks, and the evidence needed to decide how to formalize it.

## 3.1 R07_Giorgio: commercial shadow role (hybrid front-office / commercial deputy)
- **Evidence:**
  - Pricing certified, although his title is Senior Service Agent.
  - 72 A7 and 88 A8 events. About 41 A7 and 70 A8 fall outside Fatima's absence.
  - Fragment 4: Giorgio often negotiates cases Fatima reviewed. Fatima analyzes, Giorgio deals with the customer.
  - 20 A9 approvals.
  - Similarity 0.71 with Fatima and 0.84 with Amira.
- **Benefits:**
  - Business continuity during Fatima's absence: 31 A7 and 18 A8 events were covered.
  - Customer-facing continuity on complex front-office cases, since the customer negotiates with the agent they already know.
  - Commercial capacity is about 1.5 people rather than 1.
- **Risks:**
  - Doubled pricing cycle time under substitution.
  - Possible inconsistency in pricing decisions.
  - Segregation of duties: in cases where he performs A7, A8 *and* A9, one person would review, negotiate and approve the same exception.
  - The front office loses its most senior agent whenever commercial demand spikes.
  - His expertise is informal, so it is undocumented and unprotected. It disappears if he leaves.
- **Needed to decide:**
  - Whether written delegation limits exist for his A7 and A9 work.
  - Case-level overlap of A7, A8 and A9 by the same resource.
  - Whether his exceptions differ from Fatima's in discount depth, approval rate or reopen rate.

## 3.2 R13_Mateo: cross-functional escalation broker
- **Evidence:**
  - Flat profile across all 14 activities.
  - The coordination step appears in fragments 3 and 5 but has no activity label.
  - 30% of reopens. 76 critical cases.
  - Week 5: preliminary approvals (13 of 21 rechecked).
  - Weeks 7–8: security triage.
  - Likely the highest betweenness in the handover network, linking Front Office, Technical, Risk, Commercial, Management and Quality.
- **Benefits:**
  - Specialists spend less time on work that doesn't need them. False-escalation complaints fell when he filtered security referrals.
  - He is the only routing intelligence for reopened cases, so rework gets to the right expert.
  - He keeps flow going during absences.
- **Risks:**
  - Gatekeeping can delay critical work. Two critical cases waited more than 2 days in weeks 7–8.
  - He acts as a de facto second approver without authority. 62% of his preliminary approvals were rechecked, which suggests low alignment or deliberately conservative review.
  - Single point of failure for coordination.
  - Cognitive overload: he has the most critical cases of anyone and his core work is unlogged.
  - Because his work is invisible, capacity planning will underestimate his load.
- **Needed to decide:**
  - Log coordination as an explicit activity or case attribute.
  - Betweenness and constraint measures in the handover and subcontracting networks.
  - Outcomes of triage decisions: filtered referrals that later proved security-relevant, and time-to-A6 for critical cases.
  - Recheck outcomes: whether Hana overturned his approvals or just re-signed them.

## 3.3 R05_Erik: rare but critical specialist
- **Evidence:**
  - Two-thirds of all security reviews.
  - Median complexity 0.86 and 55 critical cases.
  - The only certified security resource.
  - Lowest human event volume.
  - Weeks 7–8 show that his availability, and whatever routes work to him, sets the risk level for Enterprise Security cases.
- **Benefits:** Deep expertise applied to the cases that need it. Low volume is expected for a specialist who is correctly placed.
- **Risks:**
  - Bus factor of about 1 for security sign-off.
  - Queueing is non-linear: small increases in referrals near capacity cause large waits.
  - A frequency-based role model or utilization report might label him underused and pull him into general technical work, which would damage critical-case response.
  - Uncertified fallback (Chen's 24 and Dina's 6 A6 events, Ivan's 4) may be filling the gap informally.
- **Needed to decide:**
  - Weekly A6 demand vs capacity.
  - Waiting time from Diagnose to A6 by complexity band.
  - Who performs A6 when Erik is off shift, and with what outcome.

## 3.4 R03_Chen: informal late-shift security fallback (bridging Technical and Risk)
- **Evidence:** 24 A6 events without security certification. Late shift, while Erik works days. 42 critical cases. The caveat about "more security-adjacent cases".
- **Benefit:** After-hours coverage for security-adjacent work.
- **Risk:**
  - Uncertified sign-off on security decisions.
  - Chen and Dina look interchangeable in the model, but they are not. Replacing Chen with Dina's profile would remove the fallback without anyone noticing.
- **Needed to decide:** Whether Chen's A6 events are full reviews or pre-screens forwarded to Erik, and whether Erik later confirms them.

## 3.5 R14_Nora: automation boundary role
- **Evidence:** About 46% of registrations and 610 entitlement prechecks. About 150 registrations are not prechecked. Humans re-validate after the bot (fragment 2).
- **Benefit:** Removes a large amount of low-value intake work.
- **Risks:**
  - The bot's precheck rules act as unwritten policy.
  - Double validation wastes capacity.
  - If the precheck accepts a case that should fail, the error goes downstream without a named human reviewer.
  - Bot volumes distort every frequency-based role model.
- **Needed to decide:** Precheck pass/fail rates, human override rate, and reopen rate by precheck outcome.

## 3.6 R11_Kai and R12_Lina: temporary-workforce role with a hidden rework dependency
- **Evidence:**
  - Uncertified A2 work: about 520 events.
  - 17 reopenings traced to incomplete validation notes.
  - Rework flows to Amira, Ben and Mateo (fragment 6).
- **Benefit:** Seasonal capacity for low-complexity, high-volume work.
- **Risk:** Rework is transferred to permanent staff and the broker, so net capacity gained is smaller than their event counts suggest. There is also an entitlement-compliance exposure.
- **Needed to decide:** Reopen rate per closed case by closer and by validator, compared with Amira and Ben on the same complexity band, and the rework hours that land on others.

## 3.7 R09_Ivan and R10_Julia: quality gatekeeping and knowledge stewardship
- **Evidence:** 89% of A11 and 200 of 261 KB updates (77%). They also do some implementation and configuration.
- **Benefit:** A consistent quality gate and a knowledge feedback loop from reopens into the KB.
- **Risk:** Self-checking when the same person implements and checks a case. Once KB work is recognized as its own function, it may get crowded out by quality-check volume.
- **Needed to decide:** Case-level overlap of A10/A5 and A11 by the same resource.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The script below is illustrative but coherent. Function names and core parameters follow the PM4Py simplified interface. Low-level parameter names for threshold sensitivity differ between PM4Py versions, and the script says where.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import networkx as nx
import pm4py

CASE, ACT, TS, RES = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "org:resource"
KEYS = dict(activity_key=ACT, resource_key=RES, timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)
CKEYS = dict(activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)   # functions without a resource key
CX_BANDS = ["low", "medium", "high", "critical"]

#-- 1. Load and format --------------------------------------------------------
raw = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution_events.csv")
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(raw, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS)

df["temporary_substitution"] = (
    df["temporary_substitution"].astype(str).str.lower().isin(["true", "1", "yes", "y"])
)
if pd.api.types.is_numeric_dtype(df["case_complexity"]):
    df["complexity_score"] = df["case_complexity"]
    df["complexity_band"] = pd.cut(df["case_complexity"], [-np.inf, .35, .60, .80, np.inf],
                                   labels=CX_BANDS)

def roles_to_frame(roles):
    rows = []
    for i, role in enumerate(roles):
        acts = role.activities if hasattr(role, "activities") else role[0]
        imp = role.originator_importance if hasattr(role, "originator_importance") else role[1]
        total = sum(imp.values())
        for res, cnt in imp.items():
            rows.append({"role_id": i, "role_activities": tuple(sorted(acts)),
                         RES: res, "events_in_role": cnt, "share_of_role": cnt / total})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

#: Bootstrap co-membership: how often do two resources share a core role?
def bootstrap_comembership(log, n_boot=50, seed=7):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    cases = log[CASE].unique()
    resources = sorted(log[RES].unique())
    co = pd.DataFrame(0.0, index=resources, columns=resources)
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        sample = rng.choice(cases, size=len(cases), replace=True)
        boot = pd.concat([log[log[CASE] == c].assign(**{CASE: f"{c}_{k}"})
                          for k, c in enumerate(sample)])
        m = roles_to_frame(pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(boot, **KEYS))
        m = m[m.share_of_role > 0.05]
        for _, grp in m.groupby("role_id"):
            members = grp[RES].tolist()
            co.loc[members, members] += 1
    return co / n_boot

    p, q = np.asarray(p) + eps, np.asarray(q) + eps
    p, q = p / p.sum(), q / q.sum()
    m = 0.5 * (p + q)
    return 0.5 * (np.sum(p * np.log2(p / m)) + np.sum(q * np.log2(q / m)))
```

## Cautions about changing staffing based only on mined roles
1. **Mined roles describe how work is currently routed, not what people are capable of or allowed to do.** Clusters come from who was *assigned* the work. Giorgio's commercial work, Chen's security reviews and Mateo's approvals might reflect bottlenecks, shift patterns or informal habits, not competence or authority. Formalizing or removing these roles based only on counts risks either legitimizing uncertified decisions or quietly cutting the fallback the process depends on.
2. **Temporary substitution does not prove permanent competence.** Giorgio's substitution doubled pricing cycle times. 62% of Mateo's preliminary approvals were rechecked. His security filtering reduced false escalations but let two critical cases wait more than 2 days. Performing an activity during an absence shows availability, not equivalence.
3. **Similar activity profiles do not imply interchangeable people.** Chen and Dina (0.94) differ in critical-case and security exposure. Temps and front-office agents differ in certification and rework. Staffing substitutions based on cosine similarity would move risk to the wrong place.
4. **Frequency is not importance.** Cutting or redeploying low-volume resources such as Erik, or Hana's approval function, because of low event counts would remove the capacity that handles the most critical cases.
5. **The log is incomplete and has artifacts.** Coordination is unlogged. Bot events inflate intake. Approvals may be logged on someone else's behalf. There are no start timestamps, so durations are sojourn proxies. Staffing decisions need interviews, delegation records and a validation period, not mined output alone.
6. **Using mined roles to evaluate individuals changes behaviour and raises privacy and labour-relations issues.** If role mining becomes a performance or headcount tool, people will log differently: brokers stop recording coordination, and shadow roles go underground. Organizational mining should be framed as process and structure diagnosis, with works-council or privacy review where required.
