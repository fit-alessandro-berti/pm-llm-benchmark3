# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** Set \(t=0\) at `Submit` for every case. The SLA horizon is \(H=72\) hours. For terminal rows, `duration_or_age_h` is the observed time to the terminal event. For `open` rows, it is age at the observation cutoff, i.e. an administrative right-censoring time unless the case has already passed 72 hours.

**Observation labels.** `last_event_gap_h` is inactivity since the last recorded event, not a time origin and not a terminal-event time. In the supplied table all terminal rows have gap 0; open rows have gaps from 2 to 72 hours.

**Core distinction.**

- **Right censoring:** an `open` case is observed only until cutoff. If age at cutoff is below 72, its 72-hour status is unknown. If age at cutoff is above 72, it is still censored for post-cutoff outcomes, but it is already known not to have reached an internal terminal outcome by 72.
- **Competing events:** `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and, under the internal-SLA view, `Transfer` prevent later internal `Resolve`. They are not ordinary non-informative censoring.
- **Already-observed breach:** under the internal success definition, any case that is not internally `Resolve` by 72 — including late resolutions, withdrawals, rejections, transfers, and open cases older than 72 — is already known not to have met the SLA.

**Useful estimands**

| Estimand | Definition | Best operational use |
|---|---|---|
| E1. Probability of no internal resolution by 72 | \(P(T_{\text{Resolve}}>72)\), allowing `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer` as competing terminal outcomes; equivalently report \(1-CIF_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\). | Primary SLA governance: did the internal process deliver `Resolve` in time? |
| E2. Probability of no terminal disposition at 72 | \(P(T_{\text{any terminal}}>72)\), where any of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer` ends internal observation. | Backlog/staffing: how many cases remain active at 72. |
| E3. Cumulative incidence of late resolution | \(P(72 < T_{\text{Resolve}} \le \tau,\ \text{Resolve occurs before competing terminal})\), for a chosen follow-up horizon \(\tau\), e.g. 120 hours here. | Understanding delayed recovery after an SLA miss. |
| E4. Cause-specific cumulative incidence by 72 | \(CIF_{\text{Withdraw}}(72)\), \(CIF_{\text{Reject}}(72)\), \(CIF_{\text{Transfer}}(72)\). | Targeted process improvement: withdrawal/rejection quality issues vs external handoff demand. |

**Contribution examples**

| Case | Observed fact | E1: no Resolve by 72 | E2: no terminal at 72 | E3: late Resolve | E4: competing causes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03 | Resolved at 76 | Counts as not resolved by 72; in AJ analysis remains at risk to 72 and is administratively censored at 72 for \(CIF_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\). | Had no terminal disposition at 72. | Event of interest at 76. | No competing non-resolution event. |

| `transferred` | S08, S21, S32 | Primary: E1 = 1; E2 terminal = 1; E4 transfer event | 4 | Not ordinary censoring in primary analysis. | T1: competing non-success, primary. | `open`, age at/above 72 | S09, S11, S25, S26 | E1 = 1 known by 72; E2 still no terminal at 72; E3 unknown after cutoff | 0 at cutoff | For time-to-event analysis, censor at cutoff, but keep in risk set through 72. | Long gaps, especially S26 gap 72, S11 gap 55, S25 gap 48, suggest censoring may be informative. |

**Transfer assumptions in detail.**

- **T1, primary:** `Transfer` is a competing terminal event. This matches the stated internal success definition because the internal system stops observing the case and it has not reached internal `Resolve`.
- **T2, censoring sensitivity:** `Transfer` is censored at transfer time. This is defensible only if transfer is independent of the unresolved-resolution hazard given covariates. That is doubtful here: transfers S08, S21, and S32 are all P1 and escalated.
- **T3, external-success sensitivity:** assume transfer means eventual external resolution and count it as “success” at transfer time. This changes the question from internal SLA success to externally confirmed operational success; the prompt says eventual resolution is unknown, so this is an assumption, not an observation.
- **T4, informative-transfer sensitivity:** model transfer as informative via weighting, shared frailty/joint modeling, or report bounds where transfers are worst-case failures vs best-case successes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Observation:** there are 13 resolved cases. Among them, S03=76h, S13=110h, S20=74h, and S28=88h are later than 72. A completed-resolved-only “late rate” of 4/13 is a conditional description of resolved cases, not the SLA breach probability.

- **Survivor bias.** Restricting to `resolved` cases excludes cases whose long duration is exactly the problem: S09 open at 92, S11 open at 81, S25 open at 79, S26 open at 104, and transfers such as S08, S21, S32. It also excludes non-success terminal outcomes such as S05, S06, S12, S14, S22, S23, S30. These exclusions remove likely failures from the denominator.

- **Informative censoring.** Open cases are not interchangeable with cases censored at random. S26 has age 104 and gap 72; S11 has age 81 and gap 55; S25 has age 79 and gap 48. Long inactivity plus no terminal disposition may indicate stalled handling. Dropping them or treating post-cutoff outcomes as ignorable can understate tail risk.

- **Competing risks.** Withdrawals and rejections before 72 — S05, S06, S12, S14, S22, S23, S30 — cannot later reach `Resolve`. Treating them as ordinary censoring in a resolve-time Kaplan-Meier curve effectively asks a hypothetical “what if they had remained observable?” question, not the operational SLA question.

- **Transfer removes high-priority risk from observation.** S08, S21, and S32 are all P1 and escalated. S08 transfers at 68 and S21 at 26, before the SLA horizon. Censoring them at transfer would remove informative cases from the risk set exactly when they matter for P1 tail risk.

- **Cohort imbalance and policy confounding.** Cohort A has 15 cases; cohort B has 17. B has more P1 cases, 5 vs 3 in A, and all P1 cases are escalated. AutoCheck appears in 12 of 17 B prefixes but only 2 of 15 A prefixes. Because assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, a crude B-vs-A contrast confounds policy era, priority mix, and compliance.

- **Active open cases below vs above 72.** S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are open but younger than 72; their 72-hour status is unknown. S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open and older than 72; for E1 they are already observed non-resolutions by 72. These two groups must not be pooled as if they carried the same information.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | What it estimates | Fit to this data | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier for time to `Resolve`, censoring non-resolve terminals | Hypothetical resolution probability if competing exits were non-informative | Useful only as a sensitivity analysis | Usually overstates operational success because withdrawal/rejection/transfer prevent later Resolve. |
| Kaplan-Meier for time to any terminal | Probability of remaining active at 72 | Good for E2 backlog measurement if open censoring is approximately independent | Long-gap open cases challenge independent censoring. |
| Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence | Absolute probability of each terminal cause by time \(t\) in the presence of competing risks | **Primary recommendation** for E1 and E4; \(1-CIF_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\) is the internal-SLA failure probability under the chosen transfer scenario | Requires explicit cause coding and transfer assumptions. |
| Cause-specific hazards | Instantaneous rate of Resolve or each competing cause given still active | Good for diagnosing stage/priority effects and alerting mechanisms | Hazard ratios are not absolute breach probabilities. |
| Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards | Direct covariate modeling of cumulative incidence | Reasonable secondary model for adjusted \(P(\text{no Resolve by }72)\) by cohort/priority | Proportional subdistribution hazards may be unrealistic; time-varying process covariates need care. |
| Restricted mean time / restricted mean time in active state | Average observed active or time-to-event experience up to a horizon | Useful complementary workload metric, e.g. mean active time through 72 | Must define how competing exits contribute; not itself a breach probability. |
| Landmarking at 24 or 48 hours | Risk conditional on being active at a landmark, using only information available then | Operationally attractive for triage alerts | Current extract has only final prefixes and total duration, not event timestamps, so true landmarks need richer data. |
| Multi-state model | Transition intensities among Screen/AutoCheck/Review/Escalate/Rework and terminal states | Best long-term process-aware model | With 32 cases and no event timestamps, transition intensities would be sparse; treat as exploratory until timestamped logs are available. |

**Recommendation.**

- **Primary:** Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence with causes `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`; report  
  \[
  \widehat{P}(\text{no internal Resolve by }72)=1-\widehat{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
  \]
  stratified by cohort and priority. Treat `Transfer` as competing in the primary analysis and show transfer scenarios as bounds.

- **Secondary:** Aalen-Johansen/KM for E2, \(P(\text{no terminal disposition at }72)\), to quantify active backlog. Add cause-specific cumulative incidences for withdrawal, rejection, and transfer.

- **Predictive extension:** landmarked cause-specific or subdistribution models using only features observed before the landmark. Do not put final `prefix`, final `escalated`, or final `rework_count` into a baseline model.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

**Assumption labels:** all scenarios below are analytical assumptions, not observed facts, except where the table itself supplies the status.

| Analysis | Transfer treatment | Other choices | Expected direction / possible reversal |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Primary internal SLA | Transfer = competing non-success | Withdraw/Reject competing; open censored at cutoff; report \(1-CIF_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\) | Treats S08 and S21 as SLA failures before 72 and S32 as not internally resolved by 72. This is the strict internal-governance view. |
| B. Transfer as ignorable censoring | Transfer censored at transfer time | Otherwise as A | Removes S08, S21, S32 from risk after transfer. Likely raises estimated resolution/success and lowers estimated breach. Could reverse a conclusion that transfer is an important P1 risk pathway, but relies on a strong and doubtful independence assumption. |
| C. Transfer as best-case external success | Transfer recoded as success at transfer time | Only defensible for an external-resolution SLA, not the stated internal SLA | S08 at 68 and S21 at 26 would become successes by 72; S32 at 96 remains after horizon. Could materially narrow or reverse cohort contrasts because B has two transfers and A has one. |
| D. Transfer as informative / bounded | Report worst-case and best-case bounds; optionally weight by P1/escalation | Include open long-gap cases with pattern-mixture assumptions | Worst case increases breach risk; best case decreases it. If long-gap open cases like S09, S11, S25, S26 are assumed likely unresolved, tail risk rises. |
| E. Withdrawal/rejection reclassified | Treat Withdraw/Reject as censored or neutral | Competing-risk model otherwise | Censoring S05, S06, S12, S14, S22, S23, S30 would increase estimated Resolve probability. Could reverse whether “failure” is driven by process delay vs deliberate exits, but changes the estimand. |
| F. Cohort-policy adjustment | No causal interpretation of cohort B | Crude vs priority-stratified vs adjusted for priority/escalation/AutoCheck compliance | Because B has more P1 cases and AutoCheck rollout is incomplete, a crude apparent B improvement or worsening could reverse after stratification/standardization. Escalation cannot be interpreted causally within P1 because all P1 cases are escalated. |

**Qualitative conclusions most vulnerable to reversal.**

1. “Cohort B improves SLA performance” — vulnerable to priority imbalance and non-random rollout.
2. “Escalation reduces/increases risk” — vulnerable to confounding by priority and timing.
3. “Transfers are benign” — vulnerable to T2/T4 assumptions.
4. “Late resolutions are the main problem” — vulnerable to excluding long-gap open and non-resolve terminal cases.
5. “Observed active cases below 72 will meet SLA” — not supported; those cases are censored before the horizon.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Convert survival outputs into decisions without causal language.**

- **Alerts:** use a landmarked predicted probability such as  
  \[
  \hat{P}(\text{no Resolve by }72 \mid \text{active at }t,\ \text{history up to }t)
  \]
  for triage. At 24 or 48 hours, rank active cases by predicted breach risk and trigger review when the absolute predicted risk exceeds a capacity-based threshold. The threshold should be chosen from alert burden and missed-SLA costs, not from this extract alone.

- **Workload actions:** E2, the probability of still being non-terminal at 72, maps directly to expected backlog. Cause-specific incidences map to different actions: high withdrawal/rejection incidence suggests screening or service-quality investigation; high transfer incidence suggests external-handoff capacity and outcome-tracking needs.

- **SLA governance:** report E1 as \(1-CIF_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\) with the transfer scenario stated. Do not report only completed resolved cases. Show open >72 cases as observed breaches for E1 while also reporting E2 so that late-but-eventually-resolved cases are not conflated with never-resolved cases.

- **Uncertainty:** small strata, especially priority-by-cohort cells, require confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals. Transfer and informative-censoring assumptions should be presented as a scenario band rather than hidden in one point estimate.

- **No causal interpretation:** cohort B and `escalated` are process indicators, not randomized treatments. A lower estimated risk in B, or an association between escalation and delay, may reflect priority mix, severity, or rollout compliance. Use language such as “higher predicted risk,” not “causes breach.”

**Calibration and fairness checks.**

- Assess calibration overall and by `cohort` and `priority`: calibration slope/intercept, reliability plots, Brier score, and observed-vs-predicted breach by risk decile.
- At each alert threshold, compare sensitivity, false-alarm rate, and alert burden by P1/P2/P3 and A/B.
- Check whether P1 cases receive more alerts simply because all P1 cases are escalated; this may be appropriate operationally but must not be interpreted as an escalation effect.
- Monitor AutoCheck compliance within B and any A cases with AutoCheck, because incomplete rollout can make cohort-level calibration unstable.

def admin_truncate(frame, tau):
    out = frame.copy()
    later = out["time"] > tau
    out.loc[later, "time"] = tau
    out.loc[later, "cause"] = 0
    return out

def aj_cif(frame, event_cause, tau):
    f = admin_truncate(frame, tau)
    event_times = sorted(f.loc[f["cause"] == event_cause, "time"].unique())
    surv_before = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = int((f["time"] >= t).sum())
        d_event = int(((f["time"] == t) & (f["cause"] == event_cause)).sum())
        d_all = int(((f["time"] == t) & (f["cause"] != 0)).sum())
        if n == 0:
            continue
        cif += surv_before * (d_event / n)
        if d_all > 0:
            surv_before *= (1.0 - d_all / n)
    return cif, surv_before

def km_survival(frame, tau):
    f = admin_truncate(frame, tau)
    event_times = sorted(f.loc[f["cause"] != 0, "time"].unique())
    surv = 1.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = int((f["time"] >= t).sum())
        d = int(((f["time"] == t) & (f["cause"] != 0)).sum())
        if n > 0 and d > 0:
            surv *= (1.0 - d / n)
    return surv

def summarize_aj(frame, event_cause, tau, by=None):
    groups = [("all", frame)] if by is None else list(frame.groupby(by, sort=False))
    out = []
    for key, sub in groups:
        cif, _ = aj_cif(sub, event_cause, tau)
        if key == "all":

        out.append(dict(group=label, n=len(sub),

# 9. Limitations and additional data

Prioritized data needs for stronger inference:

1. **Exact event timestamps for every prefix activity.** The current data gives only final sequence, total duration/censor age, and final gap. Without event times, escalation timing, rework timing, Review-to-Review loops, true landmarks, and stage-specific hazards cannot be estimated reliably.

2. **External outcomes after transfer.** For S08, S21, and S32, eventual resolution is unknown. Without external follow-up, transfer must remain a competing event or be handled through explicit scenario bounds.

3. **Cutoff date and case start dates.** The freeze cutoff is not dated. Calendar time matters if staffing, holidays, policy intensity, or queue conditions changed.

4. **Time-varying workload and queue state.** Staffing levels, backlog, assignee, team, and queue age at each event would help distinguish risk from workload confounding.

5. **Assignment mechanism to cohort B and rollout compliance.** The prompt states assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete. A causal policy effect requires the rule, eligibility criteria, timing, and compliance process.

6. **Severity at submission and reason codes.** Escalation is almost perfectly tied to P1 and may reflect severity. Without baseline severity, escalation effects cannot be separated from case mix.

7. **Reason for withdrawal/rejection.** These may be deliberate, quality-related, or administrative. Treating them uniformly as failures may be useful for SLA governance but insufficient for process improvement.

8. **Definition of SLA success for transfers.** Governance must decide whether internal `Resolve` is the only success or whether externally confirmed resolution counts. The prompt implies internal resolution is the observable operational success.

9. **More cases.** With 32 cases and 12 cohort-priority combinations, stratified survival estimates are unstable. Confidence intervals or hierarchical/shrinkage models are needed before operational deployment.
