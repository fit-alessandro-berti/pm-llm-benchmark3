## 1. What is observed

Automation availability is the branch-month flag `automation=1`; it means the triage system could be used, not that every case was automated. Actual treatment intensity is better captured by `automated_fast_path_pct`, which was low in all branches before availability and rose gradually to 25–50% in adopting branches. Rollout was not random: Alpha and Beta were chosen in April because of high volume and poor historical cycle times, while Gamma adopted later after a readiness pilot; Delta and Epsilon remained untreated through August. Several operational changes occurred concurrently: a centralized fraud desk began serving all branches in March; routing campaigns increased arrivals to adopters; staffing changed in April–June; and a May product change increased high-complexity work. Outcome measurement is also limited: cycle times are branch-month medians and p90s rather than case-level distributions; fraud-desk waiting times are missing; rework and escalation are aggregate percentages; and CSAT means have variable 20–45% response rates without response counts. These features define what can be described, but they do not by themselves support a causal conclusion.

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |

| Primary: augmented synthetic control / synthetic DiD | Branch-specific event time relative to adoption month; dynamic post-adoption gaps | Delta and Epsilon as never-treated donors; Gamma pre-July may inform Alpha/Beta April–June counterfactual but is excluded from later treated periods | Pre-period outcomes, arrivals, arrivals/FTE, `high_complexity_pct`, `rework_pct`, `escalation_pct`, routing indicators, staffing changes, month effects | Branch-specific dynamic ATT of availability | Conditional parallel trends; synthetic control reproduces counterfactual; no unmeasured branch-specific post shocks; spillovers negligible or shared | Pre-period fit, placebo branches and placebo dates, leave-one-out donor tests, covariate balance, sensitivity to fraud-desk period | Poor donor match or shared fraud-desk/routing shocks affect controls and treated differently |

| Alternative 2: controlled interrupted time series / segmented regression | Level and slope change at each branch’s adoption month | Untreated branches experiencing same calendar shocks | Month effects, `high_complexity_pct`, arrivals/FTE, FTE changes, routing campaign indicators, March fraud-desk indicator | Level/slope change attributable to availability | Correct functional form; no simultaneous branch-specific shocks; covariates capture demand/mix changes | Autocorrelation checks, placebo cutoffs, alternative windows, residual inspection | May complexity/staffing/routing changes coincide with adoption and have nonlinear queue effects |

The primary augmented synthetic-control approach is preferable because the rollout was deliberately targeted and only five branches exist. A pooled two-way fixed-effects event study would impose common dynamics and can be badly biased when treatment effects arrive at different times and evolve with learning; here Alpha/Beta and Gamma adopt in different shock environments. Synthetic control makes each adopter’s counterfactual explicit: it constructs a weighted version of the branch from untreated and not-yet-treated branches, matching pre-period cycle time, tail, rework, escalation, demand, staffing, and complexity. The resulting gaps can be inspected against the May complexity shock, staffing losses, and routing changes, and placebo tests can show whether a gap is unusual. The method also accommodates branch-specific dynamic effects, such as early tail worsening followed by later improvement as `automated_fast_path_pct` rises. Its limits are equally explicit: with only two never-treated branches, the donor pool is thin, and shared fraud-desk spillovers can violate the no-interference assumption. Therefore, the estimate should be reported as a scenario-dependent ATT with placebo bounds, not as a universal percentage reduction.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Start with Delta as the next pilot because it has the lowest complexity, stable staffing gain, and shorter cycles, making it operationally safer and a cleaner test. If leadership insists on serving the higher-need branch sooner, add Epsilon two to four weeks later under a separate evaluation arm; do not treat both as a single unmeasured rollout. Use randomization wherever possible. The best design is case-level randomized triage within each branch: eligible incoming cases are randomized to automated triage or manual triage, with stratification by high-complexity flag and arrival hour. If only branch-level availability is feasible, randomize the phase-in date between Delta and Epsilon and use a waitlist control.

Collect at least 12 weeks of pre-rollout and 12 weeks of post-rollout data at case and queue level, not just branch-month aggregates. Required fields include case ID, branch, arrival timestamp, risk class, triage mode, automated decision, escalation decision, fraud-desk referral, specialist queue entry/exit timestamps, rework loop events, resolution/close timestamps, FTE roster, daily arrivals, routing rule, and survey invitation/response counts. Monitor spillovers directly: fraud-desk queue length and waiting time by referring branch, specialist backlog, cross-branch routing volumes, and control-branch cycle times.

Guardrails: pause or reduce automated routing if p90 cycle time worsens by more than 10% for two consecutive weeks, rework rises more than 2 percentage points, escalation exceeds specialist capacity, fraud-desk waiting time breaches SLA, or CSAT falls by more than 0.1 after adjusting for response propensity. Provide a manual fallback for high-risk classes and customer-complaint cases.

Decision rule: expand only if the embedded evaluation shows median cycle time improvement of at least 5% or noninferior median with no more than 5% p90 worsening, rework/escalation within guardrails, and CSAT noninferiority. If estimates are imprecise, continue the pilot until the credible interval excludes material harm.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automation availability was staggered and targeted; actual automated fast-path use rose from about 4–6% to 25–50%, escalation rose in all branches, and median versus p90 cycle-time behavior differed across branches and periods.
- Suggestive: Adopting branches showed smaller cycle-time deteriorations than untreated branches during some windows, and Gamma/late-Alpha periods improved as fast-path use increased, but these patterns are confounded by complexity, staffing, routing, and systemwide shocks.
- Conditionally causal: If a synthetic-control or randomized phase-in design’s assumptions hold, automation may produce a modest median cycle-time reduction and small relative rework reduction, while the tail effect remains uncertain and escalation likely increases.
- Not supported: A single leadership-facing statement such as “automation reduces cycle time by X%” for immediate expansion to Delta and Epsilon.
