# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

This review distinguishes four intended use cases for discovered process models of the custom-order dossier process:

- **Exploration / discovery communication**: understanding observed behavior, communicating with business stakeholders, and tolerating log incompleteness or noise. Higher fitness and generalization, lower precision, and high simplicity/stakeholder scores are acceptable. Over-permissiveness is tolerable if flagged.
- **Conformance enforcement / compliance monitoring**: rejecting non-compliant cases at runtime or in audits. High precision against a curated negative suite, strict adherence to P1–P7, and soundness are mandatory. Fitness may be sacrificed on edge cases; unseen combinations should not be auto-accepted.
- **Simulation / what-if analysis**: generating realistic compliant traces for capacity planning or stress testing. The model must be sound, generate only policy-plausible behavior, and support controlled variation (e.g., optionality of Supplier Review).
- **Stakeholder communication / training**: explaining the process to process owners, auditors, and operators. Simplicity, absence of label duplication, transparent correction semantics, and high comprehension scores dominate.

The fitness/precision trade-off is use-case dependent. For exploration, Model S’s high fitness/generalization is attractive even with low precision. For enforcement, Model C’s high training precision is attractive only if it does not reject legitimate hold-out behavior and if precision is measured against negatives (it is not). Neither original model is acceptable for enforcement without modification. Metrics are treated as evidence of log fit, not as ground truth of policy correctness. Missing attributes (external/internal, affected-check) make perfect conditional modeling impossible; any model must either approximate or expose the residual uncertainty via post-hoc checks. The 80/20 split, alignment costs, seeds, and preprocessing are undocumented, so all quantitative claims are provisional.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

**Strongest case for Model S.**  
It prioritizes robustness and usability. The combination of near-perfect training and hold-out fitness, high token generalization, high simplicity, negligible runtime, zero duplicate labels, and strong informal stakeholder scores makes it practical for exploration, communication, and analysis of incomplete logs. The parallel composition of independent exclusive choices naturally accommodates any subset and any interleaving without combinatorial explosion of branches. Silent transitions provide a simple mechanism that absorbs missing events (F4). For purely exploratory work the low precision is tolerable (F7). The model is easy to maintain and to explain to non-specialists.

**Strongest case against Model S.**  
The model is semantically misaligned with the agreed policies, not merely tolerant of noise. By placing the two mandatory checks under independent `X(activity, tau)` operators inside a parallel block, it systematically generates policy-violating traces that compliance stakeholders correctly reject (F1). Concrete undesirable or policy-ambiguous traces admitted by the language of Model S (standard process-tree semantics, each activity at most once, free interleaving of the chosen subset):

1. `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` (B3 – both mandatory checks omitted).  
2. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` (B4 – Expedite precedes both mandatories).  
3. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Auto Approval, Release Order` (Technical Review skipped).  
4. `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` (Credit Check skipped).  
5. `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Expedite Review, Auto Approval, Release Order` (both mandatories omitted; only optionals present).  
6. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` (Expedite alone, no checks).  

These are not accidental side-effects of a noise-tolerant design; the silent transitions explicitly optionalize activities that P2 declares mandatory, and the unconstrained partial order places Expedite Review arbitrarily early. The complete absence of `Correct Dossier` means every correction case requires alignment deviations; the still-high fitness figures therefore imply either that corrections are rare in the log or that the alignment costs under-penalize them—masking incompleteness. The permissiveness that produces high fitness and generalization is an accidental semantic flaw relative to P2, P4 and P7, not a justified response to missing events. Model S is unacceptable for enforcement, for simulation of compliant behavior, or for audit.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

**Strongest case for Model C.**  
It stays close to frequent observed variants and therefore delivers high training precision and exact matches for common compliant paths (B1, B7 and similar). None of its eight branches omit the mandatory checks or place Expedite Review before them, so it avoids the gross violations that disqualify Model S. For auditors who deliberately treat every unseen combination as non-compliant until explicitly approved (F8), the model is “audit-ready” on the training distribution. The explicit enumeration makes the supported behavior transparent, even if the resulting Petri net is large.

**Strongest case against Model C.**  
Branch enumeration produces brittleness and severe maintainability problems. With only eight hard-coded sequences the model rejects many combinations that stakeholders regard as valid or at least plausible: alternate check order together with Auto Approval (B2), Supplier Review + Expedite Review + Auto Approval (B5), Correct Dossier followed by selective recheck (B6), Correct together with Supplier or Expedite, multiple successive corrections, and other interleavings of the two optionals. This over-specificity explains the sharp hold-out fitness collapse and the low generalization score: the model memorizes the particular sequences present in the training split rather than capturing the underlying concurrent and optional structure (F2, F4).  

High training precision is therefore largely an artifact of positive-log-only evaluation (F5) plus exhaustive listing of frequent paths; it does not demonstrate discrimination against a curated negative suite. The reported 33 duplicate labels create multiple transitions with identical activity names, complicating impact analysis, ownership assignment and change management (F3). The resulting Petri net is large, slow to align, low in simplicity and low in stakeholder comprehension. Correction handling is both incomplete and opaque: only one branch contains a Correct, and that branch forces both rechecks plus Manager Approval, ignoring selective rechecks, Auto Approval after correction, and repeated corrections (F6). Adding any newly approved variant requires a new branch and risks further label duplication and combinatorial growth. Consequently Model C is difficult to keep synchronized with evolving policy and is unsuitable as a long-term enforcement or simulation artifact.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by probable impact on a governance decision:

1. **Positive-log-only precision (no curated negative suite)** – F5. Both the high precision of Model C and the low precision of Model S are incomplete; the metric cannot distinguish policy-violating extra behavior from harmless unobserved behavior. Highest decision impact because precision is the principal quantitative claim advanced for Model C.
2. **Fitness and generalization rewarded by optionalizing mandatory activities** – Model S’s silent transitions on Credit Check and Technical Review inflate fitness and generalization while systematically violating P2. The pathology is masked by the high numeric scores.
3. **Train/hold-out split sensitivity, missing seeds, preprocessing and possible leakage** – The dramatic hold-out degradation of Model C may be an artifact of the particular undocumented 80/20 split; F4 already notes that hold-out contains combinations absent from training. Claims are unverifiable.
4. **Missing-event bias interacting with silent transitions** – Model S absorbs missing Supplier Review events via tau and therefore records high fitness; Model C penalizes them. The comparison is confounded, especially given F4’s observation that some Supplier Review events may be missing.
5. **Duplicated-label effects** – Model C’s 33 duplicates enlarge the Petri net, inflate runtime, degrade simplicity and complicate ownership (F3). Alignment and precision calculations may treat the multiple transitions differently from a single-label model.
6. **Alignment-cost sensitivity and runtime confounding by model size** – Unknown cost functions and the large net produced by Model C make runtime and residual-cost comparisons partly artifactual.
7. **Generalization metrics that reward excessive permissiveness** – Token-based generalization favors Model S for the same reason its precision is low.
8. **Informal stakeholder-score methodology** – No documented protocol, sampling frame or explanation method; the 4.4 vs 1.9 gap may partly reflect complexity aversion rather than true understandability of policy.
9. **Absence of soundness, reachability and negative-suite results** – Neither submission reports these elementary checks.

Collectively the pathologies imply that the numeric leaderboard cannot be used to select a model for any high-stakes use case.

# 7. Third Alternative

We design `model_third` as a block-structured process tree that enforces the mandatory concurrent checks, a controlled correction loop that always re-executes both checks, concurrent optional Supplier and Expedite reviews placed after all checks, and exclusive choice of the two approvals. Residual policy uncertainty caused by the missing attributes is handled by explicit post-hoc diagnostics rather than by silent optionalization of mandatory behavior.

**1. Compact formal description**

```
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  *( +( 'Credit Check', 'Technical Review' ), 'Correct Dossier' ),
  +( X('Supplier Review', tau), X('Expedite Review', tau) ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

where `*(A, B)` denotes the standard process-tree loop `A (B A)*`, `+` is concurrent, `X` is exclusive choice, and `tau` is the silent transition.

**2. Self-contained PM4Py code**

```python
post_hoc_policy_checks = {
    "P7_naked_repeat_flag": "Scan each trace; if any Credit Check or Technical Review occurs more than once, a Correct Dossier must appear between consecutive occurrences. (Structure already prevents this; diagnostic is defensive.)",
    "external_supplier_when_attr_available": "When the external/internal attribute is recovered: external cases must contain Supplier Review; internal cases must not. Until then the activity remains optional in the control-flow model.",
    "selective_recheck_review": "Any trace containing Correct Dossier followed by only one of the two checks (not both) is flagged for process-owner review. The model forces both; the missing affected-check attribute prevents automated selective logic.",

}
```

**4. Explicit semantics**

- Mandatory checks (P2): both Credit Check and Technical Review occur at least once, concurrently (any order), before any optional review or approval. After every Correct Dossier both are re-executed.
- Optional Supplier Review (P3): realized by `X(Supplier Review, tau)` after the check/correction phase; concurrent with Expedite Review. No data condition is possible.
- Expedite Review (P4): optional, forced after all (re)checks by sequential position, before approval, concurrent with Supplier Review.
- Exactly-one approval (P5): exclusive choice of Manager Approval or Auto Approval immediately before Release Order.
- Correction / recheck (P6, P7): Correct Dossier may appear zero or more times, but only as the redo part of the loop; each occurrence is followed by a full concurrent re-execution of both checks. Naked repeats of a check are impossible. Selective recheck is deliberately disallowed (conservative approximation).

**5. Limitations caused by the missing attributes**

- External/internal attribute absent ⇒ Supplier Review is unconditionally optional. The model may accept an internal order that contains Supplier Review or an external order that lacks it. Mitigation: apply the post-hoc rule when the attribute becomes available, or maintain a small family of attribute-conditioned models.
- Affected-check attribute absent ⇒ selective re-execution of only the affected check cannot be expressed. The model always requires both checks after Correct Dossier. This is safer (avoids under-checking) but may reject some policy-valid selective cases (e.g., B6). The process owner must either accept the force-both rule or supply the missing attribute so that refined models or filters can be built.
- No other data or resource guards are present; the model is pure control-flow.
- Activity labels are assumed unique; the construction introduces no duplicates.

The resulting model is safer than Model S (rejects all mandatory-check skips and early Expedite placements; represents Correct Dossier; enforces P7 by construction) and more generalizable and maintainable than Model C (accepts concurrent check orders, Auto Approval together with both optionals, multiple corrections; single non-duplicated structure; extension requires only local operator changes rather than new branches).

# 9. Validation Experiments

A concrete, reproducible evaluation program must be executed before any model is approved for enforcement or simulation use.

- **Reproducible case splits**: publish the exact preprocessing code, the random seed(s), and the resulting training/hold-out case identifiers. Repeat the 80/20 split with at least three different seeds and report variance.
- **Attribute-stratified hold-out**: when the external/internal attribute (or any proxy) can be recovered or imputed, evaluate fitness and precision separately on external versus internal cases. Likewise stratify by presence/absence of Correct Dossier and of Expedite Review.
- **Curated negative-trace suite**: at minimum the forbidden probes B3, B4, B8 plus systematic variants (skip only Credit Check, skip only Technical Review, Expedite before any check, two approvals, Release before approval, Correct after Release, etc.). A model that accepts any trace from the negative suite is rejected for enforcement use.
- **Missing-event and duplicate-event perturbation tests**: randomly delete Supplier Review (or other) events and re-measure fitness; randomly insert duplicate checks without Correct and verify that the model (or the post-hoc check) flags them.
- **Unseen-combination tests**: explicitly construct the combinations listed in F4 and the adversarial traces of Section 8; measure acceptance and alignment cost.
- **Alignment-cost sensitivity**: re-run fitness and precision under at least two different cost functions (standard unit costs versus higher cost on silent moves or on log moves of mandatory activities).
- **Soundness and reachability**: `pm4py.check_soundness` on the Petri-net conversion; confirm that every mandatory activity is reachable and that dead transitions do not exist.
- **Disaggregated metrics**: report fitness, precision, generalization and simplicity separately on training, hold-out and negative-suite logs; never collapse them into a single scalar.
- **Stakeholder comprehension protocol**: present five representative traces (two happy-path, one correction, one optional-heavy, one near-violation) to at least five process participants; ask them to mark accepted/rejected and to explain the model’s decision in their own words; record inter-rater agreement and average comprehension score under a fixed questionnaire.
- **Decision rule for rejection despite good aggregate metrics**: a model is rejected for enforcement if (a) it accepts any trace from the negative suite, (b) it fails soundness, (c) stratified hold-out fitness on validated external or correction cases falls below a pre-agreed threshold (e.g. 0.85), or (d) the documented stakeholder protocol yields an average score below 3.0. Exploration use may tolerate lower precision provided the negative-suite violations are explicitly listed as known limitations.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

- **Exploration / communication**: Model S may be retained temporarily as a lightweight exploratory artifact, provided every report that uses it explicitly lists the policy violations it admits (the six concrete traces of Section 3 and B3/B4). It must not be used for any compliance decision.
- **Compliance / enforcement / simulation**: both original models should be retired. Model S fails mandatory-check and ordering constraints; Model C is brittle, hard to maintain and rejects legitimate combinations.
- **Third alternative**: `model_third` (or an equivalent POWL realization of the same partial order and loop) is the only candidate suitable for both exploration and enforcement. Before deployment it must pass the validation program of Section 9, in particular:
  - zero acceptance of the negative suite,
  - soundness,
  - stratified hold-out fitness above the agreed threshold on validated cases,
  - stakeholder-protocol score ≥ 3.0,
  - documented process-owner acceptance of the force-both recheck approximation and of the unconditional optionality of Supplier Review.
- Once the missing attributes become available, the model should be refined into a small family of attribute-conditioned variants or augmented with data-aware guards; until then the post-hoc checks remain mandatory companions.

# 11. Dissenting View

The recommendation to retire both originals and to adopt a force-both-recheck process tree still over-constrains the process. P6 explicitly contemplates selective re-execution of only the affected check; by forcing both checks after every Correct Dossier the third model will systematically reject a subset of policy-valid traces (illustrated by B6 and Adv3). Likewise the unconditional optionality of Supplier Review will accept internal orders that contain the activity and external orders that lack it—precisely the residual uncertainty the missing attribute creates. A more faithful approach would keep a simple concurrent backbone (the mandatory checks and the exclusive approval) and express the remaining constraints as DECLARE rules or as post-hoc filters that can be updated the moment the attributes appear, rather than baking a conservative approximation into the control-flow model. Model S’s high fitness and generalization already demonstrate that a permissive skeleton plus external policy checks can achieve practical utility for exploration; the same pattern could be extended to enforcement once a negative suite exists. The reported metrics, while imperfect, still show that Model C’s precision is dramatically higher on the observed data; discarding it entirely may discard useful audit evidence for the frequent paths. Finally, the validation program itself is costly; in a resource-constrained setting the board might rationally prefer to keep Model S for exploration and to treat every deviation from Model C’s eight branches as a manual-review exception until the attributes are recovered.

Additional evidence that would change the decision: (1) a statistically significant frequency of selective-recheck cases that the process owner formally validates as compliant, (2) recovery of the external/internal and affected-check attributes together with a demonstration that attribute-conditioned models materially improve stratified fitness without re-introducing label duplication, or (3) a negative-suite evaluation showing that Model S, once augmented with a lightweight set of DECLARE constraints, rejects every forbidden probe while retaining its simplicity and stakeholder scores.
