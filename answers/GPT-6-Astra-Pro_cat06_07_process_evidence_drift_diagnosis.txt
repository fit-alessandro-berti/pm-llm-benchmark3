## 1. Drift diagnosis table

**The evidence supports several overlapping incidents, not one continuous failure:** April is primarily a feature-pipeline incident; June introduces a genuine routing change; July–September bring worsening parts performance, with a new population added in September; October’s predictive metrics are heavily distorted by selective label availability.

All business observations below come from the supplied scenario. Confidence refers to the proposed explanation, not merely to whether a metric changed.

Let \(X_0\) denote information available immediately after the **correct, normalized diagnosis landmark**. I use *covariate drift* for changes in \(P(X_0)\), and *concept drift* for changes in \(P(Y\mid X_0)\). Pure covariate shift additionally assumes that the conditional outcome relationship remains unchanged. Population and process changes can induce either or both, so these categories are not mutually exclusive. ([arXiv][1])

## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

**January.** Establish a mature reference: AUROC 0.81, Brier 0.132, ECE 0.025, and low missingness. This is a useful operational baseline, not proof that every segment was calibrated equally well.

**February.** Similar performance and data quality strengthen the baseline. The small breach-rate change from 0.18 to 0.19 is not, by itself, evidence of deterioration.

**March.** Predictive performance remains stable. The parts-edge median has nevertheless risen from January’s 28 h to 35 h. This suggests monitoring a gradual operational trend rather than assuming the entire pre-July process was perfectly stationary.

**April.** This is primarily a **schema and prediction-landmark failure**. AUROC falls to 0.67, ECE rises to 0.110, unknown activities reach 17%, and queue missingness reaches 14%. Labels are fully mature, while the parts-edge median remains near March’s level. Wrongly constructed prefixes and missing predictions offer a direct explanation that operational deterioration does not. Evaluation must also account for cases that received **no score**, not only malformed scores that remain measurable.

**May.** The mapping patch produces partial recovery: AUROC reaches 0.78 and ECE falls to 0.050. Recovery is incomplete, consistent with residual feature-cache contamination and inconsistent duplicate treatment. A repaired event label is not equivalent to a repaired feature vector.

**June.** The remote-routing policy changes the business process: V2 rises from 34% to 52%. The observed breach rate falls to 0.15 while mean risk remains 0.20. Reasonable ranking with excessive average risk makes recalibration worth investigating.

However, this is **not automatically pure covariate drift**. Routing occurs after the prediction landmark. A new routing policy could change outcomes for the same \(X_0\), even if outcomes within each eventual variant remain stable. The defensible conclusion is verified process drift with a testable, rather than established, concept-drift component.

**July.** The supplier incident begins on 10 July. Parts waiting reaches 65 h and V3 breach rate reaches 0.46. The aggregate predicted/observed rates, 0.18 versus 0.19, conceal a more concerning V3 change. Exposure should be assigned using parts-order dates and supplier relationships—not merely the case-start month.

**August.** The operational problem becomes severe: the parts-edge median doubles to 130 h, V3 share reaches 35%, and V3 breach rate reaches 0.64. AUROC falls to 0.68 and observed breaches substantially exceed predicted risk. Both a more adverse mix and deterioration within V3 are plausible contributors. **Partner Z cannot explain this month: it has not yet arrived.**

**September.** Three mechanisms overlap: continuing supplier problems, more V3 cases, and Partner Z’s population shift. Higher case volume creates an additional workload hypothesis, but queue saturation is not established by the supplied data. V3’s breach rate falls slightly from 0.64 to 0.61 despite a higher parts median; therefore, neither supplier effects nor variant outcomes should be described as mechanically monotonic. Deadline slack, composition, and maturity may explain that difference.

**October.** The observed breach rate falls to 0.16, but only 55% of labels are available and longer paths are disproportionately missing. Meanwhile, parts waiting reaches 155 h and the variant mix remains broadly adverse. This is not credible evidence of general recovery. The reported AUROC, Brier, and ECE characterize a selected labeled sample; their population-level bias need not have the same direction.

**What label incompleteness can and cannot explain.** Assuming maturity and breach rate use the same cohort denominator, and available labels are correct,

$$
p_{\mathrm{final}}=mr_{\mathrm{observed}}+(1-m)u,\qquad 0\leq u\leq1.
$$

The resulting identification bounds—not confidence intervals—are:

| Month     | Possible final breach rate |
| --------- | -------------------------: |
| July      |              18.62%–20.62% |
| August    |              26.04%–33.04% |
| September |              25.42%–43.42% |
| October   |               8.80%–53.80% |

Thus, under these assumptions, August remains worse than July even under the most favorable assignment of its missing labels. October permits no comparable conclusion.

**A reconciliation issue also matters.** Combining August’s variant shares and variant breach rates gives

$$
0.25(0.18)+0.40(0.11)+0.35(0.64)=0.313,
$$

not the headline 0.28. September similarly gives 0.3412 rather than 0.31. Obtain exact counts, score coverage, cohort definitions, and label vintages before making an exact decomposition.

As an **illustration only**, using the supplied July/August variant tables gives a 4.47-percentage-point mix contribution and a 6.95-point within-variant contribution, using July rates for the mix term and August weights for the within-variant term. These are not an exact decomposition of the headline increase, nor a causal attribution.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

**1. Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting.**
Record prediction time, promised deadline, when the outcome became knowable, and when its label arrived. Plot label availability against age since prediction and deadline, stratified by source, risk band, and observable path history. Include unlabeled cases in the denominator. Preserve frozen predictions and reevaluate them at predeclared ages—for example, 30, 45, 60, and 90 days—plus a sufficiently mature evaluation. Delayed-feedback evaluation must explicitly preserve this timing rather than assume immediate ground truth. ([Springer][2])

Under **API-delay dominance**, October’s ingestion lag increases and its results change materially as labels arrive. Under **real deterioration**, adverse conditional outcomes persist after maturity alignment. A fixed 60-day age is not automatically sufficient merely because median delay is 52 days.

Separate unfinished outcomes from delayed transmission: an authoritatively unresolved case past its promised deadline may already have a knowable breach label without waiting for closure. Do not infer this from a missing event in an incomplete feed.

**2. Controlled pipeline replay.**
Run a factorial replay with the model frozen: original extraction; normalized activities; normalized activities plus identity-based duplicate cleaning; and those repairs plus complete feature-cache rebuilding. Reconstruct the intended landmark and all dependent features, including the count strictly before diagnosis. Compare new versus legacy sources around 20 April and 6 May.

Under **pipeline failure**, feature values, score coverage, and performance improve specifically for repaired records. Under **operational or concept drift**, substantial deterioration remains on repaired, comparable cases. Maintain separate “as served” and “corrected replay” results: newly reconstructed predictions are counterfactual repairs, not predictions actually delivered in April. Unknown activities that cannot be recovered must remain flagged rather than guessed.

**3. Calibration and discrimination by variant, source, and month.**
On aligned cohorts, report sample sizes, positives, maturity, score coverage, AUROC, precision–recall performance, Brier, calibration intercept/slope, reliability plots, and recall at the actual alert capacity. Use consistent calibration bins and uncertainty intervals; ECE alone is insufficient. Post-processing calibration is a distinct task from learning a better ranking. ([Proceedings of Machine Learning Research][3])

Under **mix change**, within-segment calibration and discrimination remain approximately stable while pooled metrics move. Under **conditional deterioration**, V3 or another segment retains worse residuals after alignment. Aggregate AUROC is not a weighted average of segment AUROCs.

Also contextualize Brier: June’s 0.139 exceeds the hindsight constant-prevalence benchmark \(0.15(0.85)=0.1275\). That benchmark is descriptive, not an honestly deployable forecast of an unknown future prevalence.

**4. Canonical variants and DFG/performance-DFG comparisons.**
Compare case-normalized variant shares, transition probabilities, edge support, median/p90 elapsed times, and uncertainty. Use consistent activity normalization and lifecycle semantics. Construct adjacent event pairs before calendar slicing so cross-month parts waits are not lost. PM4Py’s discovery APIs distinguish frequency DFGs from performance DFGs and support median aggregation. ([GitHub][4])

Under **schema corruption**, artificial nodes and duplicate-induced self-loops largely disappear after repair. Under **routing change**, diagnosis-to-remote transition frequency changes without a corresponding remote-duration deterioration. Under **supplier deterioration**, parts waiting remains elevated within comparable sources and suppliers.

Include outstanding-order ages: completed-edge medians omit unfinished waits. The stable remote edge is a useful comparison, but not a randomized control.

**5. Conditional outcome tests within the same path.**
Compare mature V3 cases before and after supplier exposure, balancing diagnosis-time severity, available device/customer attributes, source, and remaining promised-time slack. Fit flexible outcome relationships with period and path-by-period interactions; assess out-of-time residuals rather than relying only on significance tests. Use case- or time-block uncertainty estimates that acknowledge shared supplier shocks.

Under **mix-only change**, period differences disappear after adequate standardization. Under **concept drift relative to \(X_0\)**, comparable cases retain different outcome probabilities.

The same activity sequence does not imply the same case difficulty. Actual subsequent parts waiting is a potential explanatory mediator, not a legitimate diagnosis-time predictor. Controlling for it in the primary test could also remove the very supplier effect being investigated.

**6. Partner Z transport checks.**
First compare Z with non-Z cases from the **same September exposure period**, reducing confounding by the supplier timeline. Reweight or match on comparable diagnosis-time characteristics; separately perform retrospective path-standardized analysis. Report overlap, weight tails, and effective sample size,

$$
n_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{(\sum_i w_i)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2}.
$$

Under **population-mix change with a stable conditional mechanism**, appropriately weighted non-Z outcomes and model errors approximate Z’s results. Under **missing risk factors or source-specific operational differences**, residual gaps remain. Importance weighting requires conditional stability and adequate support; it cannot establish performance in unsupported regions. ([Journal of Machine Learning Research][5])

Historical rural distance/device age should be added to this audit only when trustworthy point-in-time values can be recovered.

**7. Missingness, lineage, and target-definition audits.**
Trace every feature to source fields, ingestion times, transformation versions, cache keys, and backfill runs. Compare missingness within fixed source/variant populations, and compare stored feature vectors against freshly recomputed vectors. Check prediction coverage, repeated landmarks, timestamp ties, and missing starts—not only unknown-activity share.

Under **pipeline failure**, discrepancies align with versions or source transitions and persist within comparable populations. Under **genuine covariate change**, lineage remains consistent while valid values change. Under **population expansion**, an omitted feature may become important despite no increase in the existing queue-feature missingness metric.

Finally, verify the authoritative resolution definition and promised-date revision policy. “No documented outcome-definition change” is reassuring evidence, not proof that no accidental semantic change occurred.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment: restore trustworthy decisions before changing the learner.**
Suspend automatic use of malformed prefixes, not necessarily the entire service. Route affected cases to a documented fallback using verified deadline and operational information. A fallback model is useful only if its feature pipeline is compatible with the current schema. Preserve missing-score cases in coverage reporting.

Freeze retraining triggered solely by October’s provisional metrics. Repair outcome ingestion in parallel. Continue operational escalation for parts-dependent cases based on verified supplier delays and outstanding-order ages; label uncertainty does not invalidate that evidence.

**Feature repair is a prerequisite, not an alternative to recalibration.**
Apply a versioned activity map across sources, use consistent replay identification, rebuild the landmark index, and invalidate every dependent cache. Recompute historical prefix features without using events or field values unavailable at the original decision time. Retain irreversible unknowns and ambiguous event ordering as explicit quality states.

Acceptance criteria should include reproducible feature values, correct landmark timing, restored coverage, and no unexplained source-specific discrepancies—not merely a lower unknown-activity share.

**Short-term recalibration: appropriate where ranking survives.**
After repair and maturity checks, test an intercept correction, then a regularized intercept-and-slope calibration such as

$$
p'=\operatorname{logistic}\!\left(a+b\,\operatorname{logit}(p)\right),\qquad b>0.
$$

A strictly increasing transformation preserves ranking; it cannot repair lost AUROC. June is a plausible recalibration candidate, but a June-derived correction should not be assumed valid during the later supplier crisis. A single global adjustment may also overcorrect V1/V2 while leaving V3 underpredicted.

Use a separate chronological calibration set and a later evaluation set. Reassess decision thresholds against capacity and error costs after calibration.

**Full retraining: warranted when mature, repaired conditional degradation persists.**
Add defensible diagnosis-time predictors: remaining promised-time slack, diagnosed parts requirement or already-recorded routing intention, lagged supplier-health indicators, current queue state, planned travel distance, device age, and relevant source information. Supplier indicators must use information available then, not a future monthly performance summary.

Use chronological, label-availability-aware training and validation. At each simulated training date, only labels already available may enter training. Preserve evaluation coverage for stable variants while ensuring sufficient representation of the adverse recent regime.

**Training eligibility by period.**

| Period               | Decision                                                                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **January–March**    | Suitable baseline data after ordinary checks, but insufficient alone for the changed operating environment.                                                                            |
| **April**            | Unsafe until landmarks, duplicates, coverage, and dependent features are fully repaired. Fully mature labels do not make corrupted features safe.                                      |
| **May**              | Conditionally usable after verifying source-specific repairs and cache lineage; do not assume the deployment date repaired every record.                                               |
| **June**             | Mature and potentially useful after quality checks; represents the new routing regime, not the later supplier regime.                                                                  |
| **July**             | Potentially usable with maturity controls and supplier-exposure dating. Simply taking its currently labeled rows may still introduce selection.                                        |
| **August–September** | Priority data for adaptation once sufficiently mature or supported by a justified censoring-aware method. Unsafe for naïve complete-label-only retraining at the stated cutoff.        |
| **October**          | Quarantine from ordinary supervised retraining and headline performance claims until outcome ingestion/maturity is resolved. Still usable for label-free process and input monitoring. |

**Segment-specific models require a deployment-valid segmentation rule.**
Start with pooled models containing interactions, or partially pooled segment calibrators, rather than immediately fitting an independent Partner Z model. Its effective mature sample size and feature support are not supplied.

V1/V2/V3 are defined by future behavior. They are valid retrospective audit strata but are not automatically available model-selection inputs at diagnosis. Variant-specific deployment is justified only when the relevant route or parts requirement is already known and recorded at that landmark. Otherwise, use prefix-based routing probabilities or a pooled model.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

Maintain an immutable raw-event store, a versioned canonical-event layer, point-in-time feature snapshots, a prediction ledger, and a separate label ledger. Each prediction should record the landmark, model/feature versions, score, eligibility status, and no-score reason. Labels should retain both outcome-effective time and arrival/revision time.

Use three explicit clocks: **event-calendar time** for operational activity, **case-start time** for cohort evolution, and **prediction time** for model evaluation. Attach reporting cutoff and label vintage to every metric.

| Monitor                     | Principal signals and interpretation                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Predictive performance**  | Mature AUROC, precision–recall, Brier skill, capacity-constrained recall, and score coverage, with segment counts and uncertainty.                                                            |
| **Calibration**             | Reliability curves, intercept/slope, ECE with fixed methodology, and mean risk versus outcomes on the **same** population. Show mean risk for all scored cases separately from labeled cases. |
| **Label maturity**          | Availability curves, outcome-to-ingestion lag, API backlog, missing labels by source/path/risk, and successive-vintage metric revisions.                                                      |
| **Data quality**            | Unknown activities, replay/conflict rates, missing landmarks, ambiguous ordering, feature missingness, stale caches, and point-in-time lineage violations.                                    |
| **Process variants**        | Canonical variant shares, routing probabilities, rare/unmapped behavior, and explicit separation of complete traces from ongoing prefixes.                                                    |
| **Performance DFG**         | Edge counts, median/p90 elapsed times, outstanding-order ages, and cross-boundary/censoring diagnostics.                                                                                      |
| **Population**              | Source shares, volume, rurality/device age where available, support/overlap, and segment-specific prediction coverage.                                                                        |
| **Intervention/deployment** | Model, mapper, routing-policy and threshold changes; actions taken, overrides, capacity, source-level access, and downstream outcomes.                                                        |

**Alert dependencies should govern the response.**

A data-quality alarm creates a pipeline incident and invalidates affected model comparisons. A label-delay alarm marks affected predictive metrics provisional and routes investigation to the outcome feed; it does **not** automatically initiate retraining. Neither alarm should suppress independent supplier-performance evidence or valid deterioration found in older mature cohorts.

Input or variant drift alone initiates investigation and shadow evaluation. Persistent mature calibration deterioration with stable ranking supports recalibration. Persistent mature discrimination or conditional-residual deterioration, after data-quality checks, supports retraining. Define effect-size thresholds, minimum segment information, and persistence requirements in advance; critical schema violations should bypass persistence delays.

Record intervention exposure because routing or score-triggered actions can themselves change outcomes. Otherwise, a useful intervention can make a risk model appear to overpredict.

**Monthly process-evidence sketch.**
The code below assumes globally unique case identifiers and milestone timestamps, and uses UTC calendar months. It preserves unidentified duplicate candidates rather than deleting legitimate repeated activities. `ingested_at`, when available, supports as-of filtering; without it, the input must already be an appropriate as-of extract.

The PM4Py calls follow the documented formatting, filtering, discovery, variant, and temporal-feature interfaces. These produce evidence summaries, not an automatic drift verdict. ([GitHub][6])

```python
from pathlib import Path
import hashlib
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

C, A, T = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"
D = "Initial Diagnosis Completed"


def monthly_evidence(path: str = "field_service_events.csv", *, cutoff: str) -> dict:
    """UTC-month, as-of audit; globally unique case IDs; no model fitting."""
    raw = pd.read_csv(path, dtype={C: "string", A: "string",
                                   "source": "string", "event_id": "string"})
    if not {C, A, T}.issubset(raw.columns):
        raise ValueError(f"Required columns: {C}, {A}, {T}")
    raw[T] = pd.to_datetime(raw[T], utc=True, errors="raise")
    if raw[[C, T]].isna().any().any() or raw[C].str.strip().eq("").any():
        raise ValueError("Missing case ID or timestamp; quarantine invalid rows.")
    cut = pd.to_datetime(cutoff, utc=True, errors="raise")
    if pd.isna(cut):
        raise ValueError("A valid extraction cutoff is required.")
    raw["_row"] = range(len(raw))
    raw = raw.loc[raw[T].le(cut)].copy()
    if "ingested_at" in raw:
        arrival = pd.to_datetime(raw["ingested_at"], utc=True, errors="raise")
        if arrival.isna().any():
            raise ValueError("Unknown ingestion time prevents as-of reconstruction.")
        raw = raw.loc[arrival.le(cut)].copy()
    if raw.empty:
        raise ValueError("No events at this cutoff.")
    raw["raw_activity"] = raw[A]
    raw[A] = raw[A].str.strip().replace({
        "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": D, "": "UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY"
    }).fillna("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY")

    # Only immutable, source-scoped event IDs authorize replay removal.
    drop = pd.Series(False, index=raw.index)
    keys = ["source", "event_id"]
    if set(keys).issubset(raw.columns):
        identified = (raw[keys].notna().all(axis=1) &
                      raw[keys].apply(lambda s: s.str.strip().ne("")).all(axis=1))
        payload = [c for c in raw if c not in
                   keys + ["_row", "raw_activity", "ingested_at"]]
        conflicts = raw.loc[identified].groupby(keys)[payload].nunique(dropna=False)
        if conflicts.gt(1).any().any():
            raise ValueError("Conflicting event-ID payloads: quarantine, do not deduplicate.")
        drop = identified & raw.duplicated(keys, keep="first")
    removed = raw.loc[drop].copy()
    clean = raw.loc[~drop].sort_values([C, T, "_row"], kind="stable")
    clean = pm4py.format_dataframe(clean, case_id=C, activity_key=A, timestamp_key=T)
    clean = clean.sort_values([C, T, "_row"], kind="stable").reset_index(drop=True)
    # Timestamp ties are flagged, not claimed to encode a true business order.
    ties = clean.loc[clean.duplicated([C, T], keep=False)].copy()
    clean["events_before_landmark"] = clean.groupby(C).cumcount()
    diagnosis = clean.loc[clean[A].eq(D)].drop_duplicates(C, keep="first")
    prefix_features = diagnosis[[C, T, "events_before_landmark"]].copy()
    starts = clean.loc[clean[A].eq("Case Created")].groupby(C)[T].min()
    unanchored = sorted(set(clean[C]) - set(starts.index))

    # Pair before calendar slicing: retain observed cross-month DFG edges.
    pairs = clean[[C, A, T]].copy()
    pairs["next_activity"] = clean.groupby(C)[A].shift(-1)
    pairs["next_time"] = clean.groupby(C)[T].shift(-1)
    pairs["elapsed_h"] = (pairs["next_time"] - pairs[T]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    pairs["origin_month"] = pairs[T].dt.strftime("%Y-%m")
    edge_calendar = (pairs.dropna(subset=["next_time"])
        .groupby(["origin_month", A, "next_activity"])["elapsed_h"]
        .agg(n="size", median_h="median").reset_index())

    def process_summary(g: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
        if g.empty:
            return {"variants": {}, "dfg": {}, "median_edge_h": {}}
        dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_dfg(g)
        perf, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(g, perf_aggregation_key="median")
        return {"variants": pm4py.get_variants(g), "dfg": dfg,
                "median_edge_h": {edge: seconds / 3600 for edge, seconds in perf.items()}}

    rows, process = [], {}
    first_month = clean[T].min().normalize().replace(day=1)
    for lo in pd.date_range(first_month, cut.normalize().replace(day=1), freq="MS"):
        hi, month = lo + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1), lo.strftime("%Y-%m")
        # Explicit half-open interval avoids counting midnight twice.
        calendar = pm4py.filter_time_range(clean, lo.isoformat(), hi.isoformat(), mode="events")
        calendar = calendar.loc[calendar[T].ge(lo) & calendar[T].lt(hi)].copy()
        ids = starts.index[(starts >= lo) & (starts < hi)]
        cohort = clean.loc[clean[C].isin(ids)].copy()  # All observed events through cut.
        for basis, g in (("event_calendar", calendar), ("case_start", cohort)):
            r = (raw.loc[raw[T].ge(lo) & raw[T].lt(hi)] if basis == "event_calendar"
                 else raw.loc[raw[C].isin(ids)])
            d = diagnosis.loc[diagnosis["_row"].isin(g["_row"])]
            rows.append({"month": month, "basis": basis, "cases": g[C].nunique(),
                "raw_events": len(r), "clean_events": len(g),
                "removed_replays": int(drop.reindex(r.index).sum()),
                "unknown_share": g[A].eq("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY").mean(),
                "diagnosis_landmarks": len(d),
                "missing_queue_at_diagnosis": (d["queue_length"].isna().mean()
                                              if "queue_length" in d else float("nan")),
                "closed_cases_seen": g.loc[g[A].eq("Case Closed"), C].nunique()})
            process[(basis, month)] = process_summary(g)

    tmp = clean.copy()
    if "org:resource" not in tmp:
        tmp["org:resource"] = pd.NA  # Unobserved, not a fabricated resource.
    temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
        tmp, grouper_freq="MS", case_id_key=C, start_timestamp_key=T)
    # Retain counts only: point events do not identify service/waiting durations.
    temporal = temporal[["timestamp", "unique_activities", "num_events", "number_of_cases"]]
    return {"monthly": pd.DataFrame(rows), "process": process,
            "edge_calendar": edge_calendar, "temporal": temporal,
            "prefix_features": prefix_features, "removed_replays": removed,
            "timestamp_ties": ties, "unanchored_cases": unanchored,
            "metadata": {"cutoff": cut.isoformat(), "pm4py_version": pm4py.__version__,
                "normalizer": "diagnosis-alias-v1", "queue_column_present": "queue_length" in raw,
                "sha256": hashlib.sha256(Path(path).read_bytes()).hexdigest()}}
```

Interpret `event_calendar` variants as **trace fragments**, not full business variants. Case-start cohorts retain all observed events through the cutoff, but recent cases still contain unfinished prefixes. `closed_cases_seen` is not label maturity. Likewise, `edge_calendar` retains observed cross-month edges but does not solve censoring of unfinished waits; add a separate outstanding-order analysis before drawing supplier-performance conclusions.

The temporal extractor provides window-level summaries; its service/waiting outputs are deliberately excluded here because milestone timestamps do not establish activity execution intervals. ([GitHub][7])

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

**Retraining on October’s available labels.**
The learner may absorb the selected short-path population and learn systematically understated risk for longer cases. **Mitigation:** retain unlabeled cases in maturity reporting, exclude naïve recent complete-label samples from retraining, and require maturity or defensible censoring assumptions with sensitivity analysis.

**Calibrating around April’s pipeline bug.**
A calibrator may compensate for malformed event counts or misplaced landmarks, then become wrong once features are repaired. It also cannot restore missing predictions. **Mitigation:** repair and replay first; validate score coverage and feature equivalence before calibration.

**Calling August–September only a mix change.**
This would ignore the large V3 outcome increase and parts-delay evidence, potentially leaving high-risk cases underprotected. **Mitigation:** retain within-path, exposure-aligned tests and act on independently verified supplier problems while the statistical attribution is refined.

**Treating June’s routing change as universal model obsolescence.**
Unnecessary full retraining could introduce instability when a smaller calibration or policy-context update would suffice. **Mitigation:** assess within-segment ranking and conditional outcomes, then choose the least disruptive remedy that passes out-of-time validation.

**Attributing Partner Z’s raw outcome gap directly to unfair model behavior—or dismissing fairness because its cases differ.**
Both conclusions exceed the evidence. Selective maturity, V3 exposure, rurality, and device age confound the comparison. **Mitigation:** examine mature errors, calibration, prediction coverage, intervention access, and customer outcomes both overall and within comparable groups. Do not condition away routing decisions that may themselves create unequal access or delay.

**Leaking final variants or future supplier outcomes into the repaired model.**
Offline results may improve dramatically while the proposed features are unavailable at diagnosis. **Mitigation:** enforce point-in-time joins, feature availability timestamps, chronological replay, and a strict distinction between retrospective explanatory variables and deployable predictors.

**Allowing a label-delay alarm to suppress every other warning.**
October’s uncertainty could become an excuse to ignore worsening parts performance or already-established mature deterioration. **Mitigation:** use separate validity gates for predictive, data-quality, and operational evidence. Uncertain model metrics justify withholding a model verdict—not withholding independently justified operational action.
