## 1. Current-process diagnosis

The supplied evidence points to a process that is locally optimized for speed but not consistently optimized for end-to-end value. The fast path is not “bad” in general: it is the dominant route, very fast for low-risk accessories, and cheaper before rework. But it is being used too broadly, especially where evidence, value, defect ambiguity, or compliance rules require more control. The diagnosis below uses the supplied DFG, performance DFG, conformance findings, segment outcomes, rework statistics, and resource information as the evidence base. 

**Main performance problem:** the process has two very different delay regimes. The high-volume auto path is fast: `Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen` has mean 0.8 hours, `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval` mean 1.0 hour, and `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement` mean 3.0 hours. By contrast, the evidence/manual/repair routes introduce long queues: `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` has mean 34 hours and p90 88 hours; `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote` has mean 22 hours and p90 60 hours; `Repair quote -> Customer approval` has mean 30 hours and p90 80 hours. The slow path is not just slower because it is more careful; it also contains operational waiting, especially around missing evidence and part-price confirmation.

**Main quality and rework problem:** the fast path pushes errors downstream. `Customer complaint/reopen` occurs in 9.2% of all cases, but 14.1% of fast-approval cases and only 3.3% of cases following `Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device`. For high-value devices the contrast is sharper: fast path complaint/reopen is 22% for Premium high-value and 18% for Standard high-value, versus 5% and 6% respectively on reviewed/repair paths. Fast approval is therefore a real speed/cost advantage for some claims, but a false economy for high-risk claims.

**Main compliance problem:** the process violates control requirements in exactly the places where the fast path is most tempting. R1 requires claims above EUR 400 to have Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review before Fast approval. R2 prohibits replacement dispatch before evidence completeness is confirmed, except for Premium low-risk accessory claims. R3 requires reopened claims to pass Manual eligibility review before final close or denial. The alignment diagnostics identify frequent deviations: Fast approval before evidence completeness, Customer complaint/reopen after close, and Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices. Token-based replay fitness of 0.91 is acceptable at a broad level, but only 68% fitting traces means the exceptions are operationally material, not noise.

**Main customer-experience problem:** the current design gives the wrong experience to different segments. Premium low-risk accessory customers benefit strongly from speed: median cycle time is 8 hours on the fast path versus 46 hours on the manual path, and service-credit exposure becomes high after 24 hours. In that segment, over-controlling the process would likely harm the customer experience. For Premium high-value devices, however, fast completion is deceptive: median cycle time is 20 hours on the fast path, but complaint/reopen is 22% and compliance risk is high if R1 or R2 is violated. For Standard low-risk accessory customers, a 72-hour completion can be acceptable if visibility is good, so speed alone is not the only customer-experience lever.

**Main resource problem:** the bottlenecks are not evenly distributed. Auto screening is almost free and unlimited, but its rules are outdated and not segment-aware. Manual eligibility review has seven reviewers, but the queue grows sharply above 82% utilization; updating auto-screen rules could increase manual-review inflow by 15–35%. Fraud/risk review has only two specialists and is already a Monday/marketing-campaign bottleneck. Repair quote has three technicians, with delay mainly caused by part-price confirmation. Quality check has only two inspectors, but they are underused except after repair campaigns. This means every redesign must consider displaced bottlenecks, not just better routing logic.

**The core contradiction:** the most frequent path is attractive because it is fast, cheap, and scalable: 3,600 of 5,000 cases go from Intake to Auto eligibility screen, and 2,550 proceed from Auto eligibility screen to Fast approval. But the same pattern produces downstream complaints, reshipments, service credits, and compliance exceptions. Fast approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework, but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits are included. The right conclusion is not “stop using fast approval.” The better conclusion is: preserve fast approval where the risk-adjusted evidence supports it, and remove it from high-value, incomplete-evidence, unclear-defect, and reopened cases.

## 2. Redesign A: Segment-aware risk router with hard compliance gates

* **Redesigned process flow:**
  `Intake claim -> Segment/value/defect/evidence classification -> Segment-aware eligibility route`.

  The auto screen remains the first decision engine, but it no longer directly approves all apparently simple cases. It assigns each claim to one of four routing lanes:

  1. **Premium low-risk accessory lane:** fast approval remains available, including the R2 exception, provided the claim matches the low-risk accessory definition and part selection confidence is high.
  2. **Standard low-risk accessory lane:** auto approval remains available only when evidence completeness is confirmed and the replacement selector is confident; otherwise the case moves to evidence completion or manual review.
  3. **High-value / above-EUR-400 lane:** the system blocks Fast approval until either Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review has occurred, satisfying R1.
  4. **Reopened-claim lane:** every reopened case is automatically routed to Manual eligibility review before any close or denial decision, satisfying R3.

  Dispatch is blocked unless evidence completeness is confirmed, except for the explicit Premium low-risk accessory exception. The redesign should be implemented as a process-control rule, not merely as a dashboard warning.

* **Targets:**
  This redesign targets high-value devices, claims above EUR 400, reopened claims, and cases where Fast approval currently occurs before evidence completeness. It directly addresses the alignment deviations involving Fast approval before Evidence completeness, Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices, and reopened claims bypassing required manual review.

* **Mechanism of improvement:**
  The improvement comes from separating “fast because low risk” from “fast because the system failed to ask the right question.” Premium low-risk accessory claims keep speed. High-value and above-EUR-400 claims receive the required review before approval. Reopened cases are forced through the manual checkpoint. This should reduce compliance risk, wrong approvals, and expensive high-value replacement errors. It also uses auto screening where it is strongest: cheap classification and routing, not unrestricted approval.

  The expected benefit is largest for Premium and Standard high-value devices. In those segments, the current fast path has complaint/reopen rates of 22% and 18%, compared with 5% and 6% on reviewed/repair paths. Even if reviewed paths are slower, the fast path appears to be a poor end-to-end trade-off for these claims.

* **What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**
  This redesign sacrifices some speed for high-value and above-EUR-400 claims. It may increase manual-review inflow by the stated 15–35%, which is dangerous because the manual queue grows sharply above 82% utilization. It also does not solve repeated evidence requests by itself, does not fix repair quote delays, and does not directly improve wrong part selection after a technically valid approval. It is primarily a routing and compliance redesign, not a full customer-experience redesign.

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first intake with assisted completion and status visibility

* **Redesigned process flow:**
  `Intake claim -> Evidence completeness checklist -> Evidence completion support -> Eligibility assessment -> Technical decision -> Fulfillment/repair -> Close`.

  Instead of letting cases bounce repeatedly between Request missing evidence and Manual eligibility review, the process creates a structured evidence-completion stage immediately after intake. The evidence request is consolidated into one defect- and segment-specific checklist. Customers receive a clear status view showing what is missing, why it is needed, and what happens next.

  The new evidence portal is used where appropriate, but it is paired with an assisted route for customers likely to struggle with digital submission. The assisted route could include simplified upload instructions, store/service-desk support, or manual evidence capture by support staff. A second evidence request should require a reason code, so repeated requests are treated as process failures rather than normal behavior.

* **Targets:**
  This redesign targets the “Unclear defect / missing evidence” segment, which is 22% of cases and has median cycle time of 140 hours. It also targets the high-volume evidence loop: `Auto eligibility screen -> Request missing evidence`, `Manual eligibility review -> Request missing evidence`, and `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review`. It directly addresses the fact that Request missing evidence occurs at least twice in 26% of cases that enter it.

* **Mechanism of improvement:**
  The improvement comes from moving evidence quality upstream. The current process often discovers missing evidence after eligibility work has already started, causing repeated loops and long waits. The selected performance DFG shows why this matters: `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` has mean 34 hours and p90 88 hours. The new evidence portal is expected to reduce repeated evidence requests by 30–45%, but the redesign adds assisted completion because the prompt warns that the portal may increase abandonment for customers with low digital literacy.

  This design also improves customer experience for Standard low-risk accessory customers. They tolerate 72-hour completion if status visibility is good, so the process can trade a small amount of immediate speed for fewer surprises, fewer repeated requests, and clearer expectations.

* **What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**
  This redesign may slow the visible start of eligibility assessment because the process pauses to complete evidence properly. It may increase abandonment among low-digital-literacy customers if the assisted route is poorly designed. It also does not fully solve R1 for above-EUR-400 claims unless combined with Redesign A’s routing rules. Nor does it address wrong replacement selection after approval. Its main focus is reducing ambiguity, repeated evidence work, abandonment, and R2-related dispatch-before-completeness problems.

## 4. Redesign C: Right-remedy fulfillment cell with pre-dispatch quality gate

* **Redesigned process flow:**
  `Technical decision -> Remedy confidence check -> Pre-dispatch quality gate when needed -> Dispatch replacement or repair route -> Quality/compliance close`.

  After eligibility and technical decision, the process introduces a targeted “right-remedy” checkpoint before fulfillment. The checkpoint is not applied to every claim. It is applied to high-value devices, low-confidence replacement selections, reopened claims, and defect types historically associated with wrong replacement or repair rework.

  For low-risk accessories with high-confidence part selection, dispatch remains fast. For high-value devices or uncertain part selection, one of the underused quality inspectors verifies the selected replacement or repair route before dispatch. For repair cases, the repair quote process is redesigned so part-price confirmation is pulled forward and tracked with a queue SLA. The daily escalation board is reserved for exceptional customer-saving cases, not routine correction of wrong fulfillment decisions.

* **Targets:**
  This redesign targets downstream quality failures: `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen`, incorrect replacement part selection, high-value replacement cost errors, repair rework, and slow repair quoting. It responds directly to the temporal profile finding that `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` is a long-tail pattern linked to incorrect replacement part selection. It also targets the repair side, where repaired high-value devices have 12.5% rework repair compared with 4.0% for repaired low-risk devices.

* **Mechanism of improvement:**
  The mechanism is to catch wrong remedy decisions before they become expensive customer failures. A wrong replacement costs an expected EUR 95 for low-risk accessories but EUR 410 for high-value devices, so the quality gate is most valuable for high-value claims. The prompt says a lightweight pre-dispatch quality gate could catch wrong replacement selection but adds 6–18 hours depending on design. That delay is not worth applying broadly to Premium low-risk accessories, but it may be worthwhile for high-value devices where complaints, reshipments, and compliance exposure are costly.

  The repair-quote part of the redesign addresses a different bottleneck: `Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote` is slow, and `Repair quote -> Customer approval` has mean 30 hours and p90 80 hours. Since the delay is mostly caused by waiting for part-price confirmation, the redesign treats part-price confirmation as a managed queue rather than an incidental technician dependency.

* **What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**
  This redesign deliberately adds 6–18 hours to selected fulfillment decisions. It may worsen service-credit exposure if applied to Premium low-risk accessory claims, so those should generally be excluded unless part-selection confidence is low. It also does not solve repeated evidence requests upstream and does not by itself prevent Fast approval from violating R1 or R2. Its focus is downstream correctness, not eligibility routing.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A: Segment-aware risk router with hard compliance gates**

* **Failure mode 1:** manual-review inflow rises by the expected 15–35%, pushing reviewer utilization above 82%. The process then replaces compliance exceptions with long manual-review queues.
* **Failure mode 2:** the routing rules are too conservative, sending too many Standard low-risk or Premium low-risk claims into manual review. That would damage cycle time and service-credit exposure without enough quality benefit.
* **Failure mode 3:** high-value claims are routed to Fraud/risk review too often rather than Manual eligibility review, overloading the two fraud/risk specialists, especially on Mondays and after marketing campaigns.
* **Likely bottleneck migration:** from downstream complaints/reopens and compliance exceptions to Manual eligibility review and, if poorly tuned, Fraud/risk review.

**Redesign B: Evidence-first intake with assisted completion and status visibility**

* **Failure mode 1:** the evidence portal reduces repeat requests for digitally confident customers but increases abandonment for customers with low digital literacy. This would improve internal loop metrics while worsening access and fairness.
* **Failure mode 2:** the checklist becomes over-inclusive, asking for evidence that is not needed for simple claims. This could slow low-risk cases and create unnecessary customer effort.
* **Failure mode 3:** evidence is marked “complete” structurally but remains substantively poor, so cases still loop back after manual review. In that case, the process would relabel the problem rather than solve it.
* **Likely bottleneck migration:** from Manual eligibility review and repeated Request missing evidence loops to intake evidence support, evidence validation, and customer-assistance capacity.

**Redesign C: Right-remedy fulfillment cell with pre-dispatch quality gate**

* **Failure mode 1:** the gate is applied too broadly, adding 6–18 hours to claims where the expected wrong-replacement cost is low. This is especially risky for Premium low-risk accessory claims with high service-credit exposure after 24 hours.
* **Failure mode 2:** the quality gate catches obvious errors but misses systemic catalog, defect-code, or part-master-data problems. The organization would add delay without materially reducing `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen`.
* **Failure mode 3:** part-price confirmation remains unmanaged outside the repair team, so the repair quote path still has high p90 delay even after creating the right-remedy cell.
* **Likely bottleneck migration:** from post-dispatch complaints, reshipments, and escalation board corrections to Quality check inspectors and part-price confirmation.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**First pilot:** start with a limited Redesign A pilot for high-value devices and all claims above EUR 400. This is the best first move because the compliance exposure is explicit, the current high-value fast path has very high complaint/reopen rates, and the auto-screen update can be deployed quickly. The pilot should not change Premium low-risk accessory routing except to confirm that those cases are correctly classified and remain within the R2 exception.

**Pilot design:** use a staged rollout by segment and defect type rather than a full-process switch. For the first stage, block Fast approval for high-value and above-EUR-400 claims unless Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review has occurred. Keep low-risk accessory claims as a comparison group, because they are the segment where the fast path is most defensible. For compliance rules, do not use a randomized holdout that knowingly permits violations; use before/after comparison and segment-level difference-in-differences instead.

**Metrics to monitor:**

* R1 violations: above-EUR-400 claims reaching Fast approval without Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review.
* R2 violations: replacement dispatch before evidence completeness, excluding Premium low-risk accessory exceptions.
* R3 violations: reopened claims closing or being denied without Manual eligibility review.
* Complaint/reopen rate by segment, especially Premium high-value and Standard high-value.
* End-to-end cost after rework, including complaints, reshipments, and service credits.
* Manual eligibility review utilization, queue length, median delay, and p90 delay.
* Fraud/risk review utilization, especially Mondays and post-campaign periods.
* Premium cycle-time exposure against the 24-hour expectation for Premium low-risk accessories.
* Downstream reopen timing, including delayed complaints after close.

**Stop/go thresholds for Redesign A:** continue if high-value complaint/reopen rates move materially downward, for example from the current 18–22% fast-path range toward the high single digits or low teens, while R1/R2 deviations fall sharply. Stop or narrow the pilot if manual-review utilization persistently exceeds the 82% danger zone, if p90 manual-review delay rises by more than roughly 20–30%, or if Premium cases are slowed without a corresponding reduction in complaints or compliance risk.

**Second pilot:** run Redesign B for unclear-defect and missing-evidence cases. This should follow or partially overlap with Redesign A, because better evidence completeness will reduce pressure on manual reviewers created by stricter routing. The evidence portal should be tested with an assisted route from the beginning, not as a later patch.

**Stop/go thresholds for Redesign B:** continue if repeated evidence requests fall within or near the expected 30–45% range, if `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` delay falls materially, and if abandonment does not rise among low-digital-literacy or Standard customers. Stop or redesign the customer interface if abandonment rises by more than a small number of percentage points, if evidence “completion” does not reduce later manual loops, or if the portal merely shifts work from reviewers to unsupported customers.

**Third pilot:** introduce Redesign C for high-value devices, low-confidence replacement selections, and repair campaigns. Do not apply the pre-dispatch quality gate broadly at first. It should be targeted where the expected wrong-replacement cost and complaint risk justify the 6–18 hour delay.

**Stop/go thresholds for Redesign C:** continue if `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` falls materially, wrong replacement selections are caught before shipment, and the added gate time stays closer to the low end of the 6–18 hour range for most gated cases. Stop or narrow the gate if service-credit exposure rises for Premium low-risk cases, if inspectors become a new queue, or if complaint/reopen rates do not improve after a full downstream observation window.

**Sequencing logic:** Redesign A removes the most serious policy violations and prevents the fast path from being used where it is structurally unsafe. Redesign B then reduces the upstream ambiguity that would otherwise overload manual review. Redesign C should come after the first two pilots because fulfillment quality is easier to improve once evidence and eligibility are cleaner. Running C too early could make inspectors compensate for upstream errors rather than fix the process.

**How to learn whether improvements are real rather than shifted downstream:** measure cases through a maturity window long enough to capture complaints after close, because complaint/reopen occurs with long delays after Fast approval and Dispatch replacement. Compare end-to-end outcomes by segment, not just average cycle time to first close. Track reopened claims, reshipments, service credits, abandonment, and downstream manual-review load. A redesign should not be counted as successful if it improves first-pass closure while increasing late complaints, abandonment, or workload in another queue.
