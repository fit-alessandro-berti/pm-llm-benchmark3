# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Queueing quantities and units

| Quantity | Symbol | Definition (as used in this dataset) | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrival rate |  | `arrivals` counted during 07:00–19:00 opening window | cases/day |
| Throughput (departure rate) | TH | `completed` counted during 07:00–19:00 | cases/day |
| Median service time | S | Median of active handling durations (excluding wait), sampled from cases completing that day | minutes |
| 90th-percentile service time | S | 90th percentile of same distribution | minutes |
| Mean WIP | L | Mean number of cases in-system from 15-minute snapshots taken during opening hours | cases (time-averaged) |
| Staff capacity | C | Mean concurrently available FTE during opening hours | FTE-hours/hour (concurrent) |
| Complex-case share | f_c | Share of *arrivals* following multi-step variant | % of daily arrivals |
| Outage duration | — | Minutes of logged service interruption during opening hours | minutes |
| Peak concentration | f_peak | Share of daily arrivals arriving in the 08:00–09:59 window | % of daily arrivals |

**Opening-hours convention.** All daily totals span 12 hours (07:00–19:00). Overnight service is zero. Arrivals outside opening hours are not recorded but may queue in an external buffer; the data provide no visibility into this.

**What is *not* supplied:**

- No per-case event log; no activity sequences; no case-level timestamps.
- No opening backlog (B) for each day.
- No per-day overtime or break schedules.
- No queue length at 19:00 (closing backlog).
- No routing information other than a binary complex/simple flag.

## At least five threats to naive inference

1. **Overnight carry-over is invisible.** Completed > arrivals on many days (e.g., Apr 16: 129 completed, 112 arrived) proves that cases arriving on prior days are completed today. A naive arrival-rate-based Little's-Law calculation will mis-attribute carried-over work to today's arrival stream.

2. **Outage distortion.** Apr 15 lost 180 of 720 opening minutes (25 %). The 58 completions represent only ~4 productive hours of throughput. Naively comparing Apr 15 and Apr 16 completions misrepresents both the demand pressure (still 112 arrivals on Apr 16) and the recovery dynamics.

3. **Case-mix non-stationarity.** Complex_pct rises from a median of ~21 % in the first two weeks to ~44 % in the third week. Service times (median 24–26  34–36 min; p90 44–50  80–88 min) roughly double. A pooled model would estimate one service time when two distinct service environments exist.

4. **Intraday burstiness is hidden.** The Apr 21 hourly vector shows 34 arrivals in a single hour (08–09), representing ~30 % of the daily total in one 8.3 % time slice. Daily totals cannot reveal this; a model calibrated on daily  will underestimate peak queue lengths.

5. **Simpson's paradox through regime pooling.** If the staffing intervention on Apr 29 is effective, pooled pre/post averages will blend the high-complexity low-staff regime with the high-complexity adequate-staff regime, potentially masking both the problem and the fix.

6. **Survivorship bias in service-time percentiles.** On days with backlogs, the *completed* cases may be a non-random subset (e.g., shorter cases get cleared first during recovery), making service-time statistics conditional on routing rather than representative of the demand stream.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## Alternative: Two-regime split by case-mix phase only (pre-complexity / post-complexity), treating outage as an anomaly within the post-complexity period

| Regime | Dates | Label |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Apr 6–19 | Simple-mix period |
| A2 | Apr 20 – May 3 | Complex-mix period |

**Operational story:** The case-mix shift is the dominant structural change. The outage is a one-off perturbation, not a regime. The staffing intervention on Apr 29 is a second-order adjustment to a system that was already reconfigured by case mix. Under this view, we ask: *does the system behave differently before and after the complexity ramp, regardless of staffing?*

### Strengths

- Captures the largest source of variance: complex_pct roughly doubles.
- Fewer degrees of freedom; more data per regime; avoids overfitting to small differences.
- Operationally, if the complexity increase is permanent (e.g., due to a product change or coding change), then the pre/post boundary is the natural planning horizon.

### Weaknesses

- **Buries the outage-recovery dynamics.** Apr 16 (129 completions) and Apr 15 (58 completions) are averaged together with ordinary days, masking the transient backlog swing.
- **Buries the staffing intervention effect.** By pooling Apr 20–28 with Apr 29–May 3, the beneficial effect of adding 1 FTE is averaged away. A manager looking only at A2 would see   0.85 and might conclude the system is adequately resourced, when in fact it was badly under-resourced for 9 days before the intervention.
- **Weekend/weekday pooling within each regime** still creates Simpson's paradox, as weekend arrivals are ~40 % of weekday volume.

## Comparison

| Criterion | Primary (4-regime) | Alternative (2-regime) |
|---|---|---|
| Outage handling | Explicit sub-regime | Buried in A1 |
| Staffing intervention visibility | Explicit R3 vs. R4 | Masked in A2 |
| Statistical power per regime | Lower (fewer days) | Higher |
| Actionability | Higher — regime-specific staffing recs | Lower — may miss intervention effect |
| Risk of overfitting | Higher — 4 regimes for 28 days | Lower |

I use the 4-regime primary segmentation in subsequent sections because the operational question (did the staffing intervention work?) requires separating R3 from R4.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## Hourly arrival vectors: qualitative comparison

| Day | Peak hour | Peak arrivals | Peak as % of daily | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr 7 | 08–09 | 20 | 20/101 = 20 % | Smooth bell, slight afternoon tail |
| Apr 15 | 08–09 | 22 | 22/101 = 22 % | Similar to Apr 7 (outage starts ~11:00) |
| Apr 21 | 08–09 | 34 | 34/115 = 30 % | Extreme spike; also 24 in 16–17 |
| Apr 25 | 08–09 | 7 | 7/51 = 14 % | Flat weekend pattern |

Apr 21 is strikingly bursty: 34 arrivals in one hour is 3–4× the median hourly rate for a weekday. The 16:00–17:00 secondary spike of 24 suggests either a second arrival stream or batched routing. This cannot be inferred from daily data.

## Index of dispersion (Fano factor) on daily arrivals

The Fano factor is $F = \text{Var}(X) / \text{E}(X)$, where $X$ is daily arrivals.

- **Weekday arrivals** (R1+R3+R4, 20 days): mean  108, var  84, F  0.78  slightly under-dispersed (close to Poisson).
- **All days pooled** (28 days): mean  88, var  860, F  9.8  massive over-dispersion caused by mixing weekday and weekend streams.

This is a textbook Simpson's-paradox-like overdispersion: the pooled Fano factor is 12× the within-regime factor.

## Coefficient of variation of daily completions

- R1 weekdays: CV  0.04 (very stable).
- R3 weekdays: CV  0.05 (stable but with a downward trend as backlog grows).
- R2 days: CV  0.38 (highly variable due to outage/recovery).
- Pooled: CV  0.33 (driven by R2 and weekend mixing).

## Serial dependence in completions

Using the daily completion sequence, a lag-1 autocorrelation across all 28 days shows moderate positive dependence (~0.3–0.4), reflecting the carry-over backlog effect: a low-completion day (like Apr 15) is followed by a high-completion day (Apr 16). This is *not* a property of the service process but of the backlog buffer.

Within R1 (stable), lag-1 autocorrelation is near zero, consistent with a stationary system.

## Change-point evidence

A simple heuristic: compute the rolling 5-day mean of complex_pct. The first time this mean exceeds 35 % is around Apr 19–20, consistent with the case-mix boundary near Apr 18–20.

For staff_fte, the change point is discrete at Apr 29 (weekday staff jumps from 6.0 to 7.0).

## Limitation: four representative days cannot establish a stochastic model

Four hourly vectors give us:

- One "normal" weekday (Apr 7)
- One weekday with an outage (Apr 15)
- One "bursty" weekday after mix change (Apr 21)
- One weekend day (Apr 25)

We lack:
- A normal R3 weekday (without the Apr 21 anomaly)
- An R4 weekday hourly profile
- Any understanding of within-day serial dependence beyond one snapshot per hour
- The ability to separate arrival burstiness from routing batch effects

A full stochastic arrival model would require at least hourly (or sub-hourly) data across all regimes, ideally with per-case timestamps.

# 6. Little's Law applicability matrix

Little's Law states $L = \lambda \cdot W$, where $L$ is mean WIP, $\lambda$ is long-run average throughput, and $W$ is mean flow time. It requires stationarity, no buildup of infinite backlog, and consistency between the measurement boundaries of $L$, $\lambda$, and $W$.

| Window / Regime | Boundary stability | Throughput proxy | WIP comparability | Carry-over risk | Stationarity | Implied W (if defensible) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 weekdays (Apr 6–14) | Stable arrivals  completions | arrivals  100/day | L  20 | Low: arrivals  completions | High | $W = L/\lambda = 20/100 = 0.20$ days = **1.4 hours** (flow time, not service time) | **Applicable** (note: this is system time including queue wait) |
| R1 weekends (Apr 6–14) | Stable | arrivals  40/day | L  13 | Low | Moderate (only 2 days) | $W = 13/40 = 0.325$ days = **2.3 hours** | **Approximate only** (2 data points) |
| R2 (Apr 15–17) | **Unstable** — backlog forming and clearing | completions = 58, 129, 112 (non-stationary) | L = 47, 52, 35 | **Very high** | **Not stationary** | — | **Not defensible** |
| R3 weekdays (Apr 20–28) | Backlog growing (arrivals > completions) | arrivals  114, completions  100 | L  46 and rising | **High** — arrivals consistently exceed completions | **Not stationary** (WIP trending up) | $W = 46/100 = 0.46$ days = **3.3 hours** (biased low because L is a snapshot average, not steady-state) | **Approximate only** — the rising WIP means $L/\lambda$ underestimates true average flow time |
| R4 weekdays (Apr 29 – May 3) | Backlog clearing (completions > arrivals) | completions  122, arrivals  116 | L  36 and falling | Moderate — backlog still present but shrinking | Approximately stationary over 3 days | $W = 36/122 = 0.30$ days = **2.1 hours** | **Approximate only** — short window, still some backlog drain |
| Pooled (all 28 days) | **Non-stationary** on every axis | Mixed | Mixed | High | **Not stationary** | — | **Not defensible** |

### Key notes on the applicable case (R1 weekdays)

The implied flow time of 1.4 hours includes queue waiting. The median service time of 25 minutes means that the median *queue wait* is approximately 1.4 h  0.4 h  **1.0 hour**. This is plausible for a system at  = 0.58, though the distribution of waits will be right-skewed. The R1 weekend implied flow time (2.3 hours) is *longer* despite lower traffic because capacity is proportionally even lower (2.5 FTE vs. 6.0), giving   0.56 as well but with fewer parallel servers and potentially longer overnight waits for weekend-only arrivals.

### Why not use completions as throughput in R3/R4?

In R3, completions < arrivals because backlog is growing. The arrival rate is the correct "input" rate for Little's Law in steady state, but the system is not in steady state — work is accumulating. In R4, completions > arrivals because backlog is draining. Neither arrival nor completion rate represents the long-run steady-state throughput for Little's Law. A proper estimate would require tracking individual case flow times, which the aggregates do not provide.

# 7. Operational implications

## Staffing recommendations

| Regime | Recommended weekday FTE | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| R1 (complex ~20 %) | 6.0 (unchanged) |   0.58; comfortable margin |
| R3 (complex ~44 %) |  7.5 |   0.93 at 6.0 FTE  queueing delays exploding;  = 0.75 at 7.0 FTE still tight; 7.5 gives   0.78 using p90-driven estimate |
| R4 (complex ~44 %, 7.0 FTE) | 7.0–7.5 |   0.76; acceptable if case mix stabilizes; monitor for re-accumulation |

The R3 period (Apr 20–28) represents a system operating near saturation for 9 days. The daily completion shortfall of ~14/day accumulated to roughly 100–125 undischarged cases by Apr 28. This backlog explains why R4 completions exceed arrivals — the system is draining.

## Peak smoothing

Apr 21 shows 34 arrivals (30 % of daily total) in a single hour. If service times in the complex regime average ~35 minutes, this peak creates a transient queue of up to 34 cases that takes several hours to drain. **Policy:** Consider a scheduled "warm-up" period (07:00–08:00) where the team processes overnight backlog before the 08:00 peak, or stagger case assignment to smooth the 08–09 burst.

## Backlog recovery

After the Apr 15 outage, the system completed 129 cases on Apr 16 vs. 112 arrivals, clearing ~17 of the backlog. At that rate, the ~54-case outage backlog would take ~3 days to fully clear. **However**, Apr 16 also saw elevated service times (p90 = 72 min), suggesting that staff were working overtime or handling cases less efficiently under pressure.

After the R3 buildup, R4 cleared ~6 cases/day above arrivals. At that rate, ~120 accumulated cases would take ~20 working days to clear — nearly a month. **Recommendation:** If backlog is confirmed, consider temporary overtime, case triage (prioritize simpler cases for faster throughput), or deferring non-urgent complex cases.

## Data-collection recommendations

1. **Log individual case timestamps** (arrival, service start, service end, departure) to enable proper flow-time measurement and PM4Py process analysis.
2. **Record opening and closing backlog** daily, including overnight arrivals.
3. **Log intervention start times** precisely (the Apr 29 staffing change is undated within that day).
4. **Collect sub-hourly arrival data** routinely, not just for selected "representative" days.
5. **Distinguish "complex" at intake vs. complexity discovered during service**, to separate demand-mix from routing effects.

## One policy that looks good pooled but risky segmented

**"Average staffing of 6.5 FTE is sufficient."** Pooled over 28 days, the mean offered load is manageable and average WIP stays below 30. Under the segmented view, 6.5 FTE would have been *catastrophically inadequate* during R3 (  0.88, queue growing by ~10–15 cases/day). A policy based on pooled averages would have left the system in a backlog crisis for weeks.
