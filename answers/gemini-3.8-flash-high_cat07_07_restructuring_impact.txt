# 1. Mechanism map

The proposed organizational restructuring shifts the operating model from geographically distributed, cross-functional teams with embedded technical specialists to a functional shared-services model with centralized queues. In process mining and organizational theory, structural shifts directly reshape the event log topology, handover networks, waiting time distributions, and resource interaction graphs.

Below is the causal mechanism map connecting management interventions to their underlying operational dynamics and observable process-mining manifestations.

```
[Restructuring Actions]
  │
  ├── 1. Pool N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech into Central Technical Shared Service (TSS)
  ├── 2. Centralize Specialist Review, Legal, Risk, and Difficult Quality Check into Shared Queues
  ├── 3. Enforce Rigid Routing Rules (All High-Complexity -> TSS; Legal/Risk/Security -> Specialist Queue)
  ├── 4. Consolidate Supervisors (Remove 1 Local Supervisor; Create Shared Approval Queue)
  └── 5. Retain Local Intake, Simple Eligibility, and Customer Contact
        │
        ▼
[Operational Mechanisms & Behavioral Responses]
  │
  ├── A. Handover Formalization & Disconnection: Informal "corridor" consultations become logged queue handovers.
  ├── B. Queue Saturation & Kingman's Amplification: Targeting +15% utilization pushes specialists (especially Legal/Risk) to >90-100%, causing non-linear waiting time explosions.
  ├── C. Context Stripping & Rework Feedback Loops: Remote specialists lack local customer/regional nuance, inducing ticket ping-ponging and rework.
  ├── D. Defensive Routing & Caseworker Deskilling: Local caseworkers offload complexity to avoid blame, reducing learning and capability.
  ├── E. Asymmetric Regional Rebalancing: East gains capacity relief; North and South lose dedicated specialist responsiveness.
  └── F. Diffusion of Responsibility: Shared queues encourage cherry-picking (FIFO violations) and dilute end-to-end case ownership.
        │
        ▼
[Observable Process-Mining Footprint (Event Log & PM4Py)]
  │
  ├── Handover-of-Work Network: Centralized hub-and-spoke topology; extreme betweenness centrality at TSS.
  ├── Performance DFG: Arc durations between caseworker and specialist expand; long waiting times.
  ├── Variant & Entropy Metrics: Variant explosion due to multi-hop ping-pong paths (caseworker ↔ TSS ↔ Legal).
  ├── Case Service vs. Waiting Time: Ratio of service time to cycle time drops; queue dwell time dominates.
  ├── Social Network Role Discovery: Erosion of distinct regional role clusters into diffuse execution pools.
  └── Rework & Defect Metrics: Surging `rework_flag` at Quality Check; elevated `reassignment_count`.
```

### Detailed Causal Pathways

#### 1. Nominal Handover Reduction vs. Actual Handover Inflation
*   **Management Premise:** Centralizing specialist advice and approvals will standardize decisions, eliminate redundant local specialist loops, and reduce cross-team handovers (projected from 6 to 3 per 100 cases).
*   **Process Reality:** In the baseline model, embedded specialists provide early, informal triage (pre-handover advice) that resolves ambiguity before formal routing. Centralization converts tacit, unlogged micro-interactions into explicit, asynchronous handovers (`local caseworker → Technical Shared Service` projected to increase from 36 to 41 per 100 cases). Furthermore, because advice is returned asynchronously through a general queue, if the advice is incomplete or misinterprets regional context, it triggers ping-pong handovers (`TSS → caseworker → TSS`), expanding the variant space and inflating the total logged handover count per case.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** Handover-of-work network density; edge frequencies between caseworkers and TSS; variant length distribution.

#### 2. Central Queue Formation and Kingman’s Queueing Dynamics
*   **Mechanism:** According to Kingman’s approximation for queueing delays ($W_q \approx \left(\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\right) \left(\frac{c_a^2 + c_s^2}{2}\right) \tau$), waiting time increases non-linearly as resource utilization ($\rho$) approaches 1.0, scaled by the variability of arrivals ($c_a$) and service durations ($c_s$). Management explicitly targets a 15% increase in specialist utilization.
*   **Vulnerability:** Baseline specialist utilization is already high: E_Tech is at 82%, Legal at 93%, and Risk at 91%. Merging technical specialists, routing all high-complexity cases centrally, and funneling difficult Quality Checks into the same queue dramatically increases both arrival volume and arrival variability ($c_a^2$). For Legal and Risk, increasing referrals from 9 to 12 per 100 cases mathematically exceeds 100% capacity, guaranteeing unbounded queue growth.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` tracking queue dwell time preceding `Technical Assessment` and `Specialist Review`; ratio of waiting time to total cycle time.

#### 3. Specialist Utilization vs. Flow Efficiency Paradox
*   **Mechanism:** Management conflates resource utilization with process efficiency. In knowledge work, maximizing specialist utilization creates resource choke-points. When specialists operate at >85–90% utilization, they have zero buffer capacity to absorb natural workload surges or complex case investigations. Flow efficiency (the percentage of cycle time during which active work is being performed) plummets, even while resource utilization metrics look superficially favorable to management.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** Resource activity profiles; waiting-to-service time ratio; performance DFG arc weights.

#### 4. Context Loss, Defect Propagation, and Rework Loops
*   **Mechanism:** Local caseworkers possess tacit knowledge regarding regional customer conventions, documentation idiosyncrasies, and regional regulatory enforcement (captured by `local_expertise_flag`). When technical assessments and quality checks are severed from local teams, specialists evaluate cases purely on generic documentation. Misunderstandings lead to erroneous advice, customer dissatisfaction, and late-stage rejections at `Quality Check`, which subsequently route back to intake or caseworker assessment.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity`; transition frequency from `Quality Check` and `Specialist Review` back to `Local Customer Contact` or `Local Technical Assessment`.

#### 5. Local Expertise Loss and Defensive Routing (Deskilling)
*   **Mechanism:** Under the embedded model, caseworkers sit beside N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech, observing complex assessments and gradually internalizing technical skills. Under the proposed model, routing rule 4a dictates that *all* high-complexity cases go directly to TSS. Caseworkers are stripped of complex case exposure. Over time, caseworkers lose the confidence and capability to evaluate borderline cases, triggering "defensive routing"—caseworkers label moderate cases as high-complexity or required-specialty simply to transfer liability, further congesting the central queues.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** Proportion of cases routed to TSS where `case_complexity` is moderate/low; correlation over time between caseworker tenure and specialist referral rates.

#### 6. Role Clarity, Accountability, and Cherry-Picking
*   **Mechanism:** Removing one local supervisor and introducing a shared approval queue for medium/high-value cases decouples approval from operational team ownership. In a shared queue without dedicated case ownership, two behavioral pathologies emerge:
    1.  *Cherry-picking:* Approvers pull simple, clear-cut cases to maximize throughput counts, leaving ambiguous or complex cases languishing in the queue (FIFO violations).
    2.  *Diffusion of responsibility:* Approvers do not know the caseworker or customer personally; they either rubber-stamp without rigorous scrutiny (increasing downstream defects) or reject defensively with queries (adding handover loops).
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`; variance of dwell time in `shared approval queue` stratified by `case_complexity`; `reassignment_count`.

#### 7. Resilience to Absence: Systemic vs. Local Fragility
*   **Mechanism:** The baseline model exhibited localized vulnerability: Team East had a single point of failure (E_Tech at 82% utilization), making it fragile during staff absences. Central pooling eliminates single-resource failure by distributing load across three specialists. However, it replaces localized resilience with systemic fragility: any disruption in the shared queue (e.g., an IT outage, a surge in high-complexity cases, or absence in Legal/Risk) cascades across *all three regions simultaneously*, halting work across North, South, and East.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** Cross-case queue correlation; network betweenness centrality Gini coefficient; temporal variance of cycle times during peak weeks.

#### 8. Inter-Team and Customer-Segment Fairness Disparities
*   **Mechanism:** Restructuring has asymmetric impacts across teams and customer segments:
    *   *Team East* (historically underserved, 8.6d cycle time, 82% E_Tech util, 9.8% SLA miss) stands to gain capacity from North and South specialists, driving down East cycle times.
    *   *Team North* and *Team South* (historically high-performing, 6.8d and 7.4d cycle times, CSAT 4.3 and 4.1) suffer capacity dilution and longer queues, subsidizing East's operational deficit.
    *   *Customer Segments:* Customers requiring regional, linguistic, or specialized handling (`customer_segment`) suffer from standardized, context-blind shared services, facing higher rework and dissatisfaction.
*   **Process-Mining Metric:** Segment-stratified performance DFG; cycle time distributions and SLA miss rates grouped by `team` and `customer_segment`.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

### Scenario A: Optimistic Scenario ("Frictionless Standardization & Load Balancing")
*   **Underlying Conditions:** Caseworker case documentation is clear and thorough; technical specialists communicate effectively via queue notes; TSS successfully balances workloads, relieving East without overwhelming North and South; Legal/Risk triage rules prevent frivolous referrals.
*   **Handover Pattern:** Handover network transitions smoothly into a balanced hub-and-spoke model. Unnecessary local loops decrease. `local caseworker → TSS` stabilizes at ~38 per 100 cases. Ping-pong transitions (`TSS → caseworker → TSS`) remain below 4%. Cross-team emergency transfers collapse from 6% to 2%.
*   **Queue and Waiting-Time Pattern:** Median queue wait for TSS settles at 3.5h (lower than management's conservative 4.0h). Shared approval queue wait averages 2.5h. Pooling cushions Team East's historical queue spikes. Total waiting time as a fraction of cycle time remains stable at ~35%.
*   **Cycle-Time Distribution:**
    *   *Team East:* Median cycle time drops from 8.6d to 7.2d; 90th percentile improves from 22.8d to 17.5d.
    *   *Team North & South:* Median cycle times rise marginally (+0.3d) to 7.1d and 7.7d, but 90th percentile stabilizes at 16.0d and 17.8d.
    *   *Overall:* Mean cycle time decreases by ~0.4 days across the enterprise; tail variance narrows.
*   **Rework and Quality Pattern:** Enterprise rework rate decreases slightly from 9.2% to 8.5%. Centralized QC catches standardized defects earlier. First-time-right approvals in shared queues exceed 92%.
*   **Role Clarity and Accountability:** Shared queues operate under strict FIFO protocols. Role discovery shows distinct, well-defined clusters: Local Intake/Casework, Technical Shared Service, and Executive Approval. Caseworkers maintain primary ownership and track queue progress actively.
*   **Fairness Implications:** Disparities across North, South, and East shrink significantly. Team East customer satisfaction rises from 3.8 to 4.1. Regional customer segments experience minimal friction due to high-quality case summaries.
*   **Early Warning Signs:** Queue dwell time at TSS remains flat during weeks 1–3; handover ping-pong rates do not exceed baseline; E_Tech utilization drops to ~74% while N_Tech/S_Tech settle at ~75%.

---

### Scenario B: Central Scenario ("Bimodal Bifurcation & Operational Friction" — Most Likely)
*   **Underlying Conditions:** Workload pooling provides genuine relief to Team East, but local context loss, coordination friction, and queue congestion emerge in North and South. Legal/Risk shared queues experience persistent strain.
*   **Handover Pattern:** Handovers to TSS hit 42 per 100 cases due to strict compliance routing. Return loops (`TSS → caseworker`) frequently require clarifying questions, resulting in a 12% ping-pong rate. Handover network exhibits high centralization around TSS and Legal/Risk hubs.
*   **Queue and Waiting-Time Pattern:** Median wait at TSS rises to 5.2h (exceeding management's 4.0h estimate). Shared approval queue wait increases to 4.1h due to supervisor consolidation. For Legal/Risk, median wait escalates to 14.5h (baseline 9.6h). Total waiting time expands to 48% of total case duration.
*   **Cycle-Time Distribution:** A pronounced *bimodal distribution* emerges:
    *   *Low/Medium-Complexity Cases:* Median cycle time improves by 0.5d due to fast local processing and automated intake.
    *   *High-Complexity Cases:* Cycle time degrades significantly. 90th percentile across the enterprise climbs from 18.5d to 24.2d.
    *   *Regional Split:* East median improves to 7.8d (P90: 20.5d); North median degrades to 7.6d (P90: 21.0d); South median degrades to 8.2d (P90: 22.5d). Overall average cycle time appears flat, masking severe tail delays.
*   **Rework and Quality Pattern:** Overall rework rises to 11.2%. Rework is heavily concentrated in high-complexity cases (surging to 18%) and regional accommodation segments. Quality Check failure rates rise because shared specialists miss nuances previously caught locally.
*   **Role Clarity and Accountability:** Moderate role ambiguity. Caseworkers express frustration over lack of control once cases enter TSS. Shared approval queue begins exhibiting cherry-picking: easy approvals are cleared in <1 hour, while complex files wait >24 hours.
*   **Fairness Implications:** East benefits noticeably at the expense of North and South. Customers requiring language or regional accommodations suffer a 25% increase in cycle time and higher rejection/rework rates.
*   **Early Warning Signs:** In weeks 2–4, P90 waiting time at TSS diverges sharply from median wait; Legal/Risk queue depth accumulates monotonically; caseworker reassignment counts rise by >15%.

---

### Scenario C: Adverse Scenario ("Central Bottleneck Choke & Context Collapse" — Failure Mode)
*   **Underlying Conditions:** Technical specialists are overwhelmed by absorbing Legal/Risk triage and difficult Quality Checks; Legal/Risk capacity saturates; caseworkers engage in defensive routing; absence of informal advice causes defect rates to surge.
*   **Handover Pattern:** Handover network displays severe congestion and fragmentation. `caseworker → TSS` surges to 50+ per 100 cases as caseworkers offload all borderline tasks. Ping-pong loops proliferate (`caseworker ↔ TSS ↔ Legal ↔ Supervisor`), averaging 2.4 handovers per complex case. Reassignment count triples.
*   **Queue and Waiting-Time Pattern:** TSS queue enters saturation ($\rho > 0.95$). Median wait at TSS explodes to 12.0h; 90th percentile wait reaches 38h. Legal/Risk queue collapses with median waits exceeding 35h. Shared approval queue becomes a massive bottleneck (median wait 8.5h) due to supervisor burnout. Waiting time accounts for >65% of case cycle time.
*   **Cycle-Time Distribution:** Catastrophic tail degradation across all teams:
    *   *Enterprise Median:* Rises to 9.8d (baseline ~7.4d).
    *   *Enterprise 90th Percentile:* Explodes to 34.5d (North: 31d, South: 33d, East: 38d).
    *   *SLA Miss Rate:* Enterprise-wide SLA failures jump from ~7.5% to 22.0%.
*   **Rework and Quality Pattern:** Rework rate climbs to 16.8%. Because specialists are rushed, advice quality declines, causing downstream failures at implementation and customer dispute stages. Defect escape rate to end customers increases by 40%.
*   **Role Clarity and Accountability:** Severe role confusion and blame-shifting. Caseworkers disown case outcomes ("it was stuck in shared services"). Shared supervisors engage in widespread cherry-picking and cursory rubber-stamping. Staff morale collapses; specialist turnover accelerates.
*   **Fairness Implications:** Massive inequity. High-complexity and regional-accommodation cases are effectively stranded in queues. Regional customers abandon the process or escalate complaints. East’s initial gains are wiped out by enterprise-wide systemic failure.
*   **Early Warning Signs:** Within days 5–10, daily arrivals at TSS consistently exceed daily completions; backlog in shared approval grows week-over-week; frontline caseworkers report zero informal consultations; unassigned tickets in shared queues age beyond 48 hours.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

Management evaluations of organizational redesign frequently commit the **Flaw of Averages** (and succumb to Simpson’s Paradox). An executive dashboard displaying a stable or marginally improved average cycle time (e.g., dropping from 7.4 to 7.1 days) can conceal critical systemic failures. At least seven distinct operational risks remain invisible in aggregated average cycle times:

### 1. Tail Latency and Bimodal Distribution Masking
Average cycle time is heavily weighted by the high volume of simple, standard cases (Intake $\to$ Eligibility $\to$ Contact $\to$ Close). If the 70% simple cases speed up by a modest 0.5 days due to automated intake improvements, the enterprise average drops by 0.35 days. Simultaneously, the 30% medium- and high-complexity cases requiring TSS, Legal, and Approval may see their cycle times blow out from 16 days to 32 days. The net mathematical average appears completely healthy or even slightly improved, while 30% of the customer base—typically the highest-value, highest-risk accounts—experiences devastating delays. Process mining must inspect variance, interquartile ranges, and the 90th/99th percentiles.

### 2. High-Complexity Case Stagnation and Cherry-Picking (Starvation)
When individual work lists are replaced by a shared pool, workers naturally optimize their personal productivity metrics by selecting faster, well-documented, lower-friction cases. High-complexity files involving ambiguous technical specifications or multi-party dependencies remain untouched at the bottom of the queue. Because completed simple cases continually register low cycle times, the running average remains suppressed. The true backlog of stalled complex cases only registers in cycle time metrics when they are *finally closed* weeks or months later, producing a sudden, catastrophic lag spike long after the damage is done.

### 3. Local Knowledge Loss and Tacit Context Blindness
Local teams rely on tacit conventions—such as regional building codes, municipal permitting idiosyncrasies, or established customer communication patterns. In the baseline log, this is reflected in `local_expertise_flag == True` keeping rework at 8.2% in North. When assessments are centralized, cases are evaluated under rigid, decontextualized rules. The resulting defects do not manifest as immediate delays in the technical assessment step; rather, they manifest weeks later as rejected implementations, customer appeals, or regulatory penalties. The initial activity duration appears short, hiding the fact that the work was defective.

### 4. Cross-Team Inequity and the "East Subsidy" Distortion
In aggregate, overall enterprise cycle time may improve purely because Team East's historical bottleneck (E_Tech at 82% utilization, cycle time 8.6d, SLA miss 9.8%) is alleviated by absorbing capacity from N_Tech and S_Tech. However, this gain comes at the direct expense of North and South, whose performance degrades. If East improves by 2.0 days while North and South each degrade by 0.5 days, the weighted average for the company improves. Yet North and South clients experience degraded service, internal employee satisfaction drops in the highest-performing units, and established regional client relationships deteriorate.

### 5. Deskilling, Frontline Atrophy, and the Training Void
Under the embedded model, junior caseworkers learn technical nuance through daily, informal interaction with embedded specialists (osmosis learning). Under the shared-service model, all technical ambiguity is packaged into a ticket and routed away. In the short term, this appears highly efficient on paper: caseworkers spend less time wrestling with technical details. Over a 6- to 18-month horizon, however, frontline staff lose technical competency. They become unable to resolve minor customer queries, driving up referral volumes to TSS and causing irreversible long-term operational dependence. This capability drain is entirely invisible in immediate cycle time data.

### 6. Diffusion of Accountability and Orphaned Cases
In the current structure, the local supervisor (e.g., N_Supervisor) oversees the entire case lifecycle for their team and intervenes when cases stall. In a shared-queue model, case transitions are fragmented across anonymous pools. When a case sits idle, no single individual feels personal accountability for its delivery. Average cycle time reflects the cases that move; it is completely blind to cases in an "orphaned" state (waiting in limbo for missing information without active ownership). Only tracking waiting time distributions, idle state frequencies, and `reassignment_count` exposes this breakdown.

### 7. Exception Handling Deadlocks and Serial Queueing
Certain cases require concurrent inputs (e.g., both Technical Shared Service and Legal/Risk review). In an embedded environment, the caseworker can convene a 10-minute ad hoc huddle with N_Tech and part-time Legal to align on an approach. In a shared-service architecture, multi-specialty reviews are serialized: caseworker routes to TSS $\to$ waits $\to$ TSS completes $\to$ caseworker routes to Legal $\to$ waits $\to$ Legal raises a question conflicting with TSS advice $\to$ re-routed to TSS. This serial handover loop creates exponential tail delays and deadlock loops that do not show up in the average cycle time until the entire process breaks down.

# 5. Pilot design

To mitigate the catastrophic failure modes outlined in the adverse scenario while testing the legitimate load-balancing benefits for Team East, management must execute a controlled, phased pilot rather than a big-bang restructuring.

```
PILOT TIMELINE & GO-NO-GO GATES
├─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────┤
  Baseline Capture (Pre)    Active Pilot Window       Post-Pilot Evaluation
  Weeks -8 to 0             Weeks 1 to 10             Weeks 11 to 12
  • Establish counter-      • South + East Pooled     • Full PM4Py DFG Audit
    factual benchmarks      • North = Pure Control    • Tail & Fairness Analysis
  • Trace informal loops    • Monitor Weekly Gates    • Decision: Scale or Revert
```

### 1. Pilot Scope and Architecture
*   **Intervention Group:** Form a partial shared service pooling **Team South** and **Team East** (S_Tech and E_Tech combined into a 2-person pilot shared service; S_Supervisor and E_Supervisor sharing approval for South/East). This directly tests whether pooling can relieve East’s overburdened specialist without disrupting the entire enterprise.
*   **Comparison / Control Group:** **Team North** remains completely untouched as an embedded control unit (N_Tech and N_Supervisor remain embedded locally with Team North caseworkers). North serves as the counterfactual baseline to isolate external market or seasonal demand shocks.
*   **Inflow Control:** High-complexity cases and cases with `required_specialty` from South and East will be routed through the pilot shared queues, while North maintains its local routing.

### 2. Pre- and Post-Observation Windows
*   **Pre-Pilot Baseline Window:** 8 weeks of retrospective and prospective logging under identical tracking attributes.
*   **Active Pilot Window:** 10 weeks of operational runtime. (Weeks 1–2 are treated as a "burn-in" phase to account for learning curves and system configuration; formal evaluation is measured across Weeks 3–10).
*   **Post-Pilot Freeze & Analysis Window:** 2 weeks to allow all active cases in the pipeline to reach completion (`Close`), preventing censored-data bias in tail cycle time measurements.

### 3. Multi-Dimensional Stratification
Every performance metric must be evaluated across four cross-cutting dimensions:
1.  **By Team / Location:** North (Control) vs. South (Pilot Pooled) vs. East (Pilot Pooled).
2.  **By Case Complexity:** Low vs. Medium vs. High. (Explicitly checking if High-Complexity cases stall).
3.  **By Required Specialty:** None vs. Technical vs. Legal/Risk/Security.
4.  **By Customer Segment:** Standard vs. Regional/Linguistic Accommodation vs. Enterprise Accounts.

### 4. Guardrail Metrics (Hard Stop-Loss Triggers)
If any of the following guardrail thresholds are breached during the active pilot (evaluated weekly), the pilot protocol mandates an immediate operational review or structural rollback:

*   **Guardrail 1 (Queue Waiting Time):** Median waiting time in the pilot Technical Shared Service exceeds **5.0 hours**, or P90 waiting time exceeds **12.0 hours** for two consecutive weeks.
*   **Guardrail 2 (Tail Cycle Time Degradation):** 90th percentile cycle time in Team South degrades by more than **15%** compared to its baseline (i.e., exceeding **20.9 days**).
*   **Guardrail 3 (Rework Surge):** Case rework rate in either South or East exceeds **13.0%** (baseline: 9.0% and 11.5%).
*   **Guardrail 4 (SLA Breach Rate):** Enterprise or regional SLA miss rate in the pilot group exceeds **11.0%**.
*   **Guardrail 5 (Ping-Pong Frequency):** More than **12%** of cases routed to the shared service experience $\ge 2$ handovers between the caseworker and the specialist.

### 5. Staff Feedback and Behavioral Instrumentation
Quantitative log mining must be augmented with weekly qualitative and behavioral pulses:
*   **Weekly 3-Minute Pulse Survey:** Administered to caseworkers and specialists assessing: (a) perceived cognitive load, (b) clarity of case ownership (1–5 scale), and (c) confidence in technical advice received.
*   **Informal Consultation Tracking:** Caseworkers log instances where they needed informal advice but were forced to create a formal ticket (or conversely, where they reached out via chat/phone outside the ticket system).
*   **Bi-Weekly Focus Groups:** Structured 45-minute debriefs with South/East caseworkers and specialists to capture emerging coordination frictions before they crystallize into log defects.

### 6. Rollback and Adjustment Triggers
*   **Adjustment Trigger (Mild Strain):** If queue wait times at TSS rise but rework and SLA remain healthy, adjust routing rules: permit simple/medium technical reviews to be handled locally by designated senior caseworkers, reserving TSS strictly for complex files.
*   **Selective Rollback (Approval Bottleneck):** If the shared approval queue generates cherry-picking and delays exceeding 4.0h wait, immediately dissolve the shared approval pool and return supervisory approval to local team leads while keeping the technical specialist pool intact.
*   **Full Rollback Trigger (Systemic Failure):** If Guardrails 2, 3, or 4 are violated across Weeks 4–6, the pilot is immediately halted. S_Tech and E_Tech return to their embedded team reporting lines, and the shared queues are flushed.

# 6. Recommendation

### Final Recommendation: **PILOT FIRST WITH A MODIFIED HYBRID MODEL**

Management’s proposed full-scale restructuring should **NOT** proceed in its current all-or-nothing form. A full rollout carries an unacceptable risk of queue saturation, tail cycle time explosion, deskilling, and loss of local client context. Conversely, outright rejection is also untenable: Team East is demonstrably suffering under the status quo (E_Tech at 82% utilization, 11.5% rework, 9.8% SLA misses), and scarce Legal/Risk specialists are already at 91–93% capacity.

Management must execute a **Phased Pilot of a Modified Hybrid Architecture**, structured as follows:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│                                                                         │
```

### Essential Structural Modifications to Management’s Proposal

1.  **Adopt a "Primary-Secondary" Virtual Pooling Model (Not Complete Physical Detachment):**
    Rather than physically or administratively severing N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech from their teams, maintain their primary assignment to their regional units (handling ~70% of their local demand to preserve local context and informal coaching). Establish a *virtual shared overflow pool* where idle capacity automatically pulls backlog cases from overloaded regions (specifically relieving Team East).
2.  **Retain Local Supervisors; Reject the Consolidated Approval Queue:**
    Do not remove a supervisor or establish an anonymous shared approval queue. The nominal savings of one supervisory head are drastically outweighed by the costs of cherry-picking, lack of case ownership, and coordination delays. Local supervisors must retain end-to-end accountability for case cycle time and quality within their teams.
3.  **Mandate Legal/Risk Capacity Injection Prior to Routing Changes:**
    Management’s plan to increase Legal/Risk referrals from 9 to 12 per 100 cases while utilization is already at 91–93% represents a fundamental mathematical violation of queueing capacity. Management must hire or cross-train additional legal/risk capacity *before* broadening routing rules; otherwise, the entire workflow will choke at the specialist review stage.
4.  **Implement Tiered Triage to Preserve Frontline Learning:**
    Introduce a formal 15-minute synchronous "triage window" where caseworkers consult with specialists before a case is officially transferred. This preserves knowledge transfer, prevents unnecessary formal handovers, and curbs defensive routing.

### Critical Executive Guidance: What Management Must NOT Infer from Early Averages

Management must be explicitly cautioned against reading early dashboard summaries through rose-colored glasses. In the first 30 to 60 days following any structural change:

1.  **Do NOT interpret stable or decreasing average cycle times as proof of success.**
    As established, simple cases process quickly and dominate the arithmetic mean. If tail latency is stretching, the average will conceal the disaster until weeks later when stalled complex cases finally complete. Insist on reviewing the **90th percentile, 95th percentile, and interquartile range**.
2.  **Do NOT equate 15% higher specialist utilization with increased organizational productivity.**
    Specialist utilization is a local resource metric; flow efficiency and throughput are system-level outcome metrics. Pushing knowledge specialists from 70% to 85%+ utilization invariably clogs process pipelines and increases waiting times. Idle capacity in specialists is not waste; it is the necessary economic buffer that preserves organizational agility and responsiveness.
3.  **Do NOT assume a decrease in recorded cross-team reassignments implies seamless teamwork.**
    Fewer recorded transfers can simply indicate that caseworkers have given up seeking help from overloaded central queues and are either holding onto cases indefinitely or guessing, driving up downstream defect rates.
4.  **Beware the "Honeymoon Flush" Phenomenon.**
    In the first 2–4 weeks of restructuring, staff often clear existing backlogs of simple work while delaying entry of complex new cases into unfamiliar queues. Early metrics frequently show an artificial spike in completions followed by a severe slump once complex work clogs the shared service.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The following complete, modular Python script demonstrates how to evaluate the before-and-after restructuring impact using PM4Py and pandas. It analyzes organizational social networks, performance DFGs, waiting times, role discoveries, rework loops, and multidimensional tail performance.

```python
"""
Process Mining Evaluation Script: Organizational Restructuring Impact Analysis
Author: Process Intelligence & Mining Architecture
Framework: PM4Py (Process Mining for Python) & Pandas
"""

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from pm4py.algo.discovery.dfg import algorithm as dfg_discovery
from pm4py.algo.discovery.sna import algorithm as sna_discovery
from pm4py.algo.organizational_mining.roles import algorithm as roles_discovery
from pm4py.algo.organizational_mining.resource_similarity import algorithm as res_sim_discovery


def load_and_preprocess_event_log(csv_filepath: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Loads, cleans, formats, and enriches event log with pre/post restructuring flags.
    """
    # 1. Load data
    df = pd.read_csv(csv_filepath)

    # 2. Format dataframe to PM4Py standards
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
        timest_format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"
    )

    # Ensure temporal ordering
    df = df.sort_values(by=["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).reset_index(drop=True)

    # Convert timestamps to datetime if not already
    df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"])

    return df


def split_pre_post_logs(df: pd.DataFrame, cutoff_timestamp: str):
    """
    Splits the log into baseline (pre-restructuring) and pilot/post-restructuring logs
    based on the case start timestamp.
    """
    case_start_times = df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].min()
    pre_cases = case_start_times[case_start_times < pd.to_datetime(cutoff_timestamp)].index
    post_cases = case_start_times[case_start_times >= pd.to_datetime(cutoff_timestamp)].index

    df_pre = df[df["case:concept:name"].isin(pre_cases)].copy()
    df_post = df[df["case:concept:name"].isin(post_cases)].copy()

    return df_pre, df_post


def analyze_organizational_networks(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame):
    """
    Computes Handover of Work, Working Together, and Subcontracting Networks
    to evaluate structural disconnection, hub formation, and communication density.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("ORGANIZATIONAL SOCIAL NETWORK ANALYSIS (SNA)")
    print("="*50)

    for label, log_df in [("PRE-RESTRUCTURING", df_pre), ("POST-RESTRUCTURING", df_post)]:
        print(f"\n--- {label} NETWORKS ---")

        # 1. Handover of Work Network
        hw_values = sna_discovery.apply(
            log_df,
            variant=sna_discovery.Variants.HANDOVER_LOG,
            parameters={
                sna_discovery.Parameters.RESOURCE_KEY: "org:resource",
                sna_discovery.Parameters.TIMESTAMP_KEY: "time:timestamp"
            }
        )
        print(f"[{label}] Handover Network Matrix Discovered across {len(hw_values.nodes)} resources.")

        # 2. Working Together Network
        wt_values = sna_discovery.apply(
            log_df,
            variant=sna_discovery.Variants.WORKING_TOGETHER_LOG,
            parameters={sna_discovery.Parameters.RESOURCE_KEY: "org:resource"}
        )
        print(f"[{label}] Working Together Network Matrix Discovered.")

        # 3. Subcontracting Network
        sub_values = sna_discovery.apply(
            log_df,
            variant=sna_discovery.Variants.SUBCONTRACTING_LOG,
            parameters={sna_discovery.Parameters.RESOURCE_KEY: "org:resource"}
        )
        print(f"[{label}] Subcontracting Network Matrix Discovered.")

        # Extract top handover edges
        matrix = hw_values.matrix
        nodes = hw_values.nodes
        top_handovers = []
        for i in range(len(nodes)):
            for j in range(len(nodes)):
                if matrix[i][j] > 0:
                    top_handovers.append((nodes[i], nodes[j], matrix[i][j]))
        top_handovers.sort(key=lambda x: x[2], reverse=True)

        print(f"Top 5 Handover Transitions ({label}):")
        for src, dst, weight in top_handovers[:5]:
            print(f"  {src} -> {dst}: {weight:.1f}")


def analyze_performance_and_dfg(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame):
    """
    Discovers directly-follows graphs (frequency & performance) to quantify
    bottlenecks, transit latency, and variant expansion.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("PERFORMANCE DFG & VARIANT ANALYSIS")
    print("="*50)

    for label, log_df in [("PRE-RESTRUCTURING", df_pre), ("POST-RESTRUCTURING", df_post)]:
        # Frequency and Performance DFG
        dfg_freq = dfg_discovery.apply(log_df, variant=dfg_discovery.Variants.FREQUENCY)
        dfg_perf = dfg_discovery.apply(log_df, variant=dfg_discovery.Variants.PERFORMANCE)

        # Variants Discovery
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(log_df)
        print(f"\n[{label}] Total Unique Process Variants: {len(variants)}")
        top_variants = sorted(variants.items(), key=lambda x: len(x[1]), reverse=True)[:3]
        for idx, (var, cases) in enumerate(top_variants):
            print(f"  Variant {idx+1} ({len(cases)} cases): {var}")

        # Measure specific transition delay: e.g., Caseworker -> Technical / Specialist Assessment
        print(f"Key Arc Durations ({label} Performance DFG):")
        for (src, dst), duration in dfg_perf.items():
            if any(k in dst for k in ["Technical", "Specialist", "Approval"]):
                hours = duration / 3600.0
                freq = dfg_freq.get((src, dst), 0)
                print(f"  Arc [{src} -> {dst}] | Freq: {freq} | Mean Duration: {hours:.2f} hours")


def analyze_waiting_and_service_times(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame):
    """
    Enriches event logs with waiting and service times to expose queue saturation.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("SERVICE VS. WAITING TIME DISSECTION")
    print("="*50)

    for label, log_df in [("PRE-RESTRUCTURING", df_pre), ("POST-RESTRUCTURING", df_post)]:
        # Insert case service and waiting times using PM4Py temporal feature enrichment
        enriched_df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
            log_df,
            resource_key="org:resource",
            timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
            auto_aggregate=False
        )

        # In enriched_df, service_time and waiting_time are calculated per event
        if "service_time" in enriched_df.columns and "waiting_time" in enriched_df.columns:
            mean_service = enriched_df["service_time"].mean() / 3600.0
            mean_waiting = enriched_df["waiting_time"].mean() / 3600.0
            p90_waiting = enriched_df["waiting_time"].quantile(0.90) / 3600.0
            ratio = (mean_waiting / (mean_service + 1e-6))
            print(f"\n[{label}] Temporal Breakdown (Hours):")
            print(f"  Mean Service Time : {mean_service:.2f} h")
            print(f"  Mean Waiting Time : {mean_waiting:.2f} h")
            print(f"  P90 Waiting Time  : {p90_waiting:.2f} h")
            print(f"  Waiting/Service Ratio: {ratio:.2f}x (Higher indicates queue choking)")


def analyze_roles_and_similarity(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame):
    """
    Identifies organizational role clustering and resource behavioral similarity.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("ORGANIZATIONAL ROLES & RESOURCE SIMILARITY")
    print("="*50)

    for label, log_df in [("PRE-RESTRUCTURING", df_pre), ("POST-RESTRUCTURING", df_post)]:
        # 1. Organizational Roles
        roles = roles_discovery.apply(log_df)
        print(f"\n[{label}] Discovered Organizational Roles ({len(roles)} distinct roles):")
        for idx, role in enumerate(roles):
            role_resources = role.resources
            role_activities = role.activities
            print(f"  Role {idx+1}: Activities: {list(role_activities)} | Resources: {list(role_resources)}")

        # 2. Activity-based resource similarity
        sim_matrix = res_sim_discovery.apply(log_df)
        print(f"[{label}] Resource Similarity Matrix computed.")


def analyze_rework_and_defect_metrics(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame):
    """
    Quantifies rework loops, activity repetitions, and SLA misses.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("REWORK, DEFECT PROPAGATION & SLA ANALYSIS")
    print("="*50)

    for label, log_df in [("PRE-RESTRUCTURING", df_pre), ("POST-RESTRUCTURING", df_post)]:
        # 1. Rework cases per activity
        rework_dict = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log_df)
        print(f"\n[{label}] Rework Occurrences by Activity:")
        for act, count in sorted(rework_dict.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:5]:
            print(f"  Activity [{act}]: {count} rework cases")

        # 2. Case-level rework flag aggregation
        case_rework = log_df.groupby("case:concept:name")["rework_flag"].any()
        rework_pct = (case_rework.sum() / len(case_rework)) * 100.0

        # 3. Case Reassignment counts
        reassign_mean = log_df.groupby("case:concept:name")["reassignment_count"].max().mean()

        print(f"[{label}] Overall Case Rework Rate: {rework_pct:.2f}%")
        print(f"[{label}] Mean Max Reassignments per Case: {reassign_mean:.2f}")


def multidimensional_tail_evaluation(df_pre: pd.DataFrame, df_post: pd.DataFrame, sla_days: float = 20.0):
    """
    Performs stratified slice analysis across Team, Complexity, Specialty, and Customer Segment.
    Computes Median, 90th percentile, 99th percentile cycle time, and SLA breaches.
    """
    print("\n" + "="*50)
    print("MULTIDIMENSIONAL STRATIFICATION & TAIL LATENCY EVALUATION")
    print("="*50)

    def compute_case_metrics(log_df: pd.DataFrame, period_label: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
        # Calculate case-level metrics
        grouped = log_df.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
            start_time=("time:timestamp", "min"),
            end_time=("time:timestamp", "max"),
            team=("team", "first"),
            complexity=("case_complexity", "first"),
            specialty=("required_specialty", "first"),
            segment=("customer_segment", "first"),
            rework=("rework_flag", "any")
        ).reset_index()

        grouped["cycle_time_days"] = (grouped["end_time"] - grouped["start_time"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400.0
        grouped["sla_breached"] = grouped["cycle_time_days"] > sla_days
        grouped["period"] = period_label
        return grouped

    cases_pre = compute_case_metrics(df_pre, "Pre")
    cases_post = compute_case_metrics(df_post, "Post")
    all_cases = pd.concat([cases_pre, cases_post], ignore_index=True)

    # Stratified Evaluation Function
    def summarize_slices(df_slice: pd.DataFrame, slice_col: str):
        print(f"\n--- Stratification by {slice_col.upper()} ---")
        summary = df_slice.groupby([slice_col, "period"]).agg(
            case_count=("case:concept:name", "count"),
            median_cycle_time=("cycle_time_days", "median"),
            p90_cycle_time=("cycle_time_days", lambda x: np.percentile(x, 90)),
            p99_cycle_time=("cycle_time_days", lambda x: np.percentile(x, 99)),
            rework_rate=("rework", lambda x: (x.sum() / len(x)) * 100.0),
            sla_miss_rate=("sla_breached", lambda x: (x.sum() / len(x)) * 100.0)
        ).round(2)
        print(summary.to_string())

    for dimension in ["team", "complexity", "specialty", "segment"]:
        summarize_slices(all_cases, dimension)


def run_full_process_mining_audit(csv_path: str, restructuring_cutoff_date: str):
    """
    Master execution pipeline.
    """
    # 1. Ingestion & Preprocessing
    df = load_and_preprocess_event_log(csv_path)

    # 2. Partition Pre vs. Post
    df_pre, df_post = split_pre_post_logs(df, cutoff_timestamp=restructuring_cutoff_date)

    print(f"Log Loaded Successfully. Pre-restructuring cases: {df_pre['case:concept:name'].nunique()} | Post cases: {df_post['case:concept:name'].nunique()}")

    # 3. Execute Deep Analytical Modules
    analyze_organizational_networks(df_pre, df_post)
    analyze_performance_and_dfg(df_pre, df_post)
    analyze_waiting_and_service_times(df_pre, df_post)
    analyze_roles_and_similarity(df_pre, df_post)
    analyze_rework_and_defect_metrics(df_pre, df_post)
    multidimensional_tail_evaluation(df_pre, df_post, sla_days=20.0)
```
