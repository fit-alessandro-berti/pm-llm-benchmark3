# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic

- Handover pattern: Explicit cross-team cover drops, local technical loops are replaced by cleaner shared-service loops, and unnecessary referrals fall because routing rules are accurate. Total specialist-related handovers stay roughly flat rather than truly falling.
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: Shared-tech and approval waits rise slightly versus current local waits, but stay within management estimates. Queue growth remains bounded because pooled specialists absorb East demand better than the old structure.
- Cycle-time distribution: Median cycle time improves modestly, mainly from East and from fewer absence-related interruptions. `p90` is flat or slightly better. High-complexity cases do not materially worsen.
- Rework and quality pattern: Rework is flat or slightly down because specialist decisions become more consistent. Quality check rework does not rise despite the central queue.
- Role clarity and accountability: After an initial transition dip, case ownership remains with the local caseworker and approval ownership is explicit in the queue. Reassignment stays low.
- Fairness implications: East benefits most. North and South lose some local convenience but not enough to create major disparity. No customer segment shows a materially worse tail after controlling for complexity and priority.
- Early warning signs: Rising shared-service wait beyond estimate, a jump in second referrals on the same case, or worsening `p90` for regional-accommodation segments would indicate the optimistic path is failing.

## Central

- Handover pattern: Ad hoc cross-team cover falls, but queue-mediated specialist and approval handovers increase. The visible network looks cleaner while the effective dependency on the center becomes stronger.
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: The Technical Shared Service and shared approval queue become persistent waiting points. Legal/Risk and difficult quality work interfere with technical throughput.
- Cycle-time distribution: Overall median is flat or slightly better because simple cases still move quickly, but `p90/p99` worsen. East improves, while North/South high-complexity cases slow down. The aggregate can look acceptable because simple cases dominate counts.
- Rework and quality pattern: Rework rises in complex, regional, or specialty-sensitive cases because local teams lose early informal advice and documentation is not yet consistent enough for queue-based work.
- Role clarity and accountability: Local caseworkers increasingly act as coordinators rather than end-to-end owners. More cases show multiple reassignments or return-to-sender loops from approval or specialist queues.
- Fairness implications: Team-level averages may converge because East improves, but fairness across customer segments worsens. Customers needing local convention knowledge, language accommodation, or exception handling wait longer and are reworked more often.
- Early warning signs: Sustained specialist utilization above the mid-80% range, increasing `reassignment_count`, higher return-loop variants, and widening `p90` gaps between segments are consistent with this scenario.

## Adverse

- Handover pattern: The restructuring reduces some visible local loops but creates more formal referrals, more queue returns, and more internal specialist bouncing. The network becomes highly centralized around a few shared resources.
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: Shared-tech, legal/risk, and approval queues become bottlenecks. High-complexity and specialty cases stack behind simpler work unless routing and prioritization are very disciplined.
- Cycle-time distribution: Mean or median may still look stable because easy cases finish quickly, but `p90/p99` deteriorate sharply. High-complexity, high-priority, and accommodation-sensitive cases absorb most of the pain. SLA misses rise first in those segments.
- Rework and quality pattern: Rework rises because local teams escalate more cases, receive less contextualized advice, and have to implement decisions they do not fully understand. Exception handling quality worsens before average throughput visibly collapses.
- Role clarity and accountability: Ownership becomes ambiguous between local caseworker, shared specialist, and shared approver. Cases with many touches, repeat approvals, or unclear `handover_reason` become more common.
- Fairness implications: East may gain somewhat, but North/South complex cases and vulnerable customer segments can become materially worse. Averages hide that the burden is unevenly distributed.
- Early warning signs: Queue waits exceed estimates for multiple weeks, high-complexity variants multiply, specialist utilization stays near saturation, rework after specialist review rises, and fairness gaps widen after controlling for case mix.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. Tail latency can worsen while the mean stays stable. Simple local cases may still finish quickly, offsetting severe delays in shared queues. The right question is not "did average cycle time improve?" but "did `p90/p99` waiting after specialist and approval handovers worsen?"

2. High-complexity cases can absorb most of the downside. The proposed rules send all hard cases to the center, so the average can improve even while the very cases that most need expertise wait longer and miss SLAs more often.

3. Local knowledge loss can hide behind short-term throughput. North and South currently benefit from embedded specialists and regional conventions. If informal advice disappears, borderline cases that used to be solved locally may become formal referrals or later rework, which may not show up immediately in average duration.

4. Fairness problems can be invisible in aggregate performance. A single standardized queue can be "fair" in order but unfair in effect if customers needing language, regional accommodation, or unusual documentation are routed more often, wait longer, or are reworked more. Aggregate cycle time will not reveal that without segmented analysis.

5. Learning erosion is slow and therefore easy to miss. If local staff stop handling difficult cases, short-term averages may improve because experts do more of the hard work centrally. Months later, local teams may become less capable, create weaker case notes, and become more dependent on the queue, reducing both quality and resilience.

6. Accountability blur does not have to slow every case to be harmful. Shared approval can standardize decisions but weaken ownership. A case can still close on time while having too many reassigned owners, unclear approval rationale, or avoidable back-and-forth that damages auditability and staff confidence.

7. Exception handling can degrade while standard flow improves. A centralized model often performs best on repeatable work and worst on ambiguous cases. Average cycle time can therefore look better even as difficult exceptions, quality-check failures, or special-accommodation cases become more brittle.

8. Hidden bottlenecks can be masked by lower visible cross-team transfers. A drop in ad hoc cross-team handovers does not mean the system is simpler if those transfers are replaced by larger queues, more waiting, and more internal specialist bouncing.

# 5. Pilot design

- Pilot scope: Do not pilot the full restructuring all at once. Start with a phased pilot of the Technical Shared Service for East high-complexity technical cases and difficult quality checks, because East has the clearest overload signal and the highest plausible upside. Keep North and South embedded technical support unchanged in phase 1. Run shared approval in shadow mode first rather than live authority transfer.
- Comparison group: Use North and South as contemporaneous controls for comparable case types. Within East, keep low-complexity cases on the current model to create an internal comparator. If phase 1 succeeds, add a limited North or South cohort in phase 2 before any broad rollout.
- Pre/post observation window: Use at least `8-12 weeks` pre-pilot and `8-12 weeks` post-pilot, with the first `2 weeks` after cutover treated as stabilization and analyzed separately rather than blended into the post period.
- Segmentation: Compare outcomes by `team`, `location`, `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, `customer_segment`, `priority`, and `local_expertise_flag`. Fairness checks should control for case mix instead of comparing raw averages only.
- Guardrail metrics: Track `p90/p99` wait after specialist and approval handovers, `p90` cycle time for high-complexity cases, SLA miss rate, rework after specialist review and quality check, `reassignment_count`, network centralization, specialist utilization, and fairness gaps by team and customer segment.
- Staff feedback collection: Require weekly pulse checks from local caseworkers, specialists, and approvers. Add structured `handover_reason` categories for "missing local context," "unclear ownership," and "insufficient notes" so qualitative complaints become measurable.
- Rollback or adjustment triggers: Pause or adjust if shared-service `p90` wait rises materially above baseline for two consecutive weeks, if high-complexity SLA misses worsen meaningfully, if rework after specialist review rises, if fairness gaps widen for accommodation-sensitive segments, if utilization remains near saturation, or if ownership confusion is repeatedly reported.
- Adjustment options before broader rollout: Keep local technical champions in North/South, split legal/risk from technical queues, reserve priority capacity for high-complexity work, or retain local approval for specific exception classes even if standard approvals are centralized.

# 6. Recommendation

Pilot first, with modifications.

The prompt does not support a full-rollout assumption that handovers will naturally fall. In the listed routing estimates, explicit specialist and approval handovers actually increase, while only ad hoc cross-team cover falls. East is the strongest candidate to benefit because its embedded specialist is already overloaded, but North and South are the strongest candidates to lose value from reduced local expertise. Legal/Risk are already near capacity, so combining more work into a central queue without proof is the highest structural risk.

The best path is a phased pilot that separates the main mechanisms instead of changing everything at once. Test shared technical work first where East has the clearest need, keep live approval local until shadow results show that role clarity and fairness are preserved, and do not treat a short-term improvement in average cycle time as evidence of success. Management should not infer from early averages that the redesign is working if `p90/p99` delays, high-complexity outcomes, fairness gaps, rework, or local learning proxies are moving the wrong way. Proceed beyond pilot only if those guardrails remain stable or improve, not merely because the mean looks better.

import pandas as pd

    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"

CUTOVER = pd.Timestamp("2026-01-01")  # replace with actual pilot or rollout date
before = df[df["time:timestamp"] < CUTOVER].copy()
after = df[df["time:timestamp"] >= CUTOVER].copy()

def slice_log(log, team=None, complexity=None, specialty=None, segment=None):
    out = log
    if team is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(out, "team", [team], level="event", retain=True)
    if complexity is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(out, "case_complexity", [complexity], level="event", retain=True)
    if specialty is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(out, "required_specialty", [specialty], level="event", retain=True)
    if segment is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(out, "customer_segment", [segment], level="event", retain=True)
    return out

def add_time_features(log):
    timed = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
    service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(timed)
    temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(timed)
    return timed, service_time, temporal

def discover_org(log):
    hov = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log, resource_key="org:resource")
    wt = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log, resource_key="org:resource")
    sub = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log, resource_key="org:resource")
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log, resource_key="org:resource")
    similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log, resource_key="org:resource")
    return {
        "handover": hov,
        "working_together": wt,
        "subcontracting": sub,
        "roles": roles,
        "resource_similarity": similarity,
    }

def discover_flow(log):
    dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    perf_dfg, perf_starts, perf_ends = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    return {
        "dfg": dfg,
        "perf_dfg": perf_dfg,
        "variants": variants,
        "starts": starts,
        "ends": ends,
    }

def case_view(log):
    ordered = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).copy()
    case_tbl = ordered.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        start_ts=("time:timestamp", "min"),
        end_ts=("time:timestamp", "max"),
        team=("team", "last"),
        location=("location", "last"),
        required_specialty=("required_specialty", "last"),
        priority=("priority", "last"),
        case_complexity=("case_complexity", "last"),
        customer_segment=("customer_segment", "last"),
        outcome=("outcome", "last"),
        rework_flag=("rework_flag", "max"),
        reassignment_count=("reassignment_count", "max"),
        local_expertise_flag=("local_expertise_flag", "max"),
    )
    case_tbl["cycle_hours"] = (
        (case_tbl["end_ts"] - case_tbl["start_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    )
    return case_tbl.reset_index()

def wait_after_handover(log):
    x = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).copy()
    x["next_activity"] = x.groupby("case:concept:name")["concept:name"].shift(-1)
    x["next_resource"] = x.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(-1)
    x["next_team"] = x.groupby("case:concept:name")["team"].shift(-1)
    x["next_ts"] = x.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].shift(-1)
    x["post_handover_wait_h"] = (
        (x["next_ts"] - x["time:timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    )
    x["handover_edge"] = x["org:resource"].astype(str) + " -> " + x["next_resource"].astype(str)
    return x.dropna(subset=["next_ts"])

def summarize_case_metrics(case_tbl, label):
    grp = case_tbl.groupby(["team", "case_complexity", "customer_segment"]).agg(
        cases=("case:concept:name", "count"),
        median_cycle_h=("cycle_hours", "median"),
        p90_cycle_h=("cycle_hours", lambda s: s.quantile(0.90)),
        p99_cycle_h=("cycle_hours", lambda s: s.quantile(0.99)),
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean"),
        median_reassignments=("reassignment_count", "median"),
    ).reset_index()
    grp["period"] = label
    return grp

        v_before = pm4py.get_variants(subset_before)
