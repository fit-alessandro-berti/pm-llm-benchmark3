Three temporal observations drive the rest of the answer:

1. **Recovery is staggered.** Hospital medians recovered by Day 7; pharmacy and bulk tails had not recovered by Day 21; compliance evidence was still open after that. A single "recovered" date does not exist.
2. **Adaptations decay.** The snapshot (D) was sound on Day 0 afternoon and wrong by Day 2. The cost shows up later as 740 pick lists that never reached packing in T2, 1,400 reconciliations in T4, and 300 T4 cancellations for stock that was promised but not there.
3. **Risk moves downstream in time.** Every cancellation edge visible in the T4 DFG (450 + 300 = 750) comes from an incident-legacy activity, and the audit work appears *after* Invoice close, where cycle-time metrics no longer see it.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**The distinction.** Both look identical in a conformance check against the normal model: both are deviations. The difference is whether the *control objective* survived.

- **Resilience** changes the *means* and keeps the *end*. The control is performed another way, at the time, by a qualified person, with evidence that can be attributed and dated, within limits that someone with authority approved, and its cost is visible.
- **Concealed noncompliance** keeps the *output* and drops the *end*. The order ships, the control did not happen, and the record is either absent or rebuilt later so that the case looks complete.

The conformance findings show this split directly. Move-on-log deviations (Skip system authorization check, Dispatch without scan, Phone POD, Spreadsheet invoice hold) are things people did instead. Move-on-model deviations (missing Quality/release check, scan, POD, temperature record) are things that did not happen. A substitute activity with evidence, such as B, is a replaced control. A missing activity with nothing in its place, such as the 1,800 packed orders with no release step, is a control that disappeared.

Five tests applied to each pattern:

1. Is the control objective still met in real time?
2. Is the relaxation proportionate to product and customer risk?
3. Is the evidence contemporaneous, attributable and original?
4. Was it authorized and time-limited, with an expiry condition?
5. Is the resulting workload and residual risk visible in the metrics?

**True resilience**

- **B** passes all five tests except capacity.
- **A, as a prioritization rule.** The 91% hospital fill rate and 10h median are the mission outcome.
- **F, for controlled substances.** 100% pharmacist checking of the highest-risk class under pressure, and a 12% catch rate shows it worked.
- **I and J, as capacity-protection logic,** provided the rules are explicit.
- **K,** as honest repair of the stock record.

**Risky shortcuts and concealed noncompliance**

- **G.** Chain of custody broken on 100% of T2 dispatches with no recorded substitute.
- **Manual pack -> Dispatch without scan (1,800 orders).** Two controls missing in the same case: no release and no handoff evidence.
- **C, as executed.** An assumption recorded as if it were a decision; 31% of triaged orders.
- **The authorization-free part of A.** 920 orders with no authorization event.
- **F's sampling applied to cold-chain and recall-sensitive product.**
- **H for controlled and cold-chain product.**
- **D after roughly the first shift.**
- **L, when treated as equivalent to compliance.** The sequence Invoice close -> Audit remediation is the concealment mechanism: it lets cycle time and closure counts look recovered while the compliance debt remains.

Throughput in T2 is therefore not a resilience result on its own. Of the 3,250 orders that shipped, 55% (1,800) had no release control and none had a handoff scan.

**Ambiguous, depending on risk appetite**

- **C for genuinely ambient product to long-standing customers.** Acceptable to some organisations if backed by a dated whitelist and limited to a short window; not acceptable as open-ended "customer history".
- **E.** Acceptable if lot data was written at pick time; a liability if it was reconstructed from memory.
- **H for ambient product.**
- **Sample-based F for ambient product.**
- **D in the first hours.**
- **I's impact on pharmacies.** Deferring pharmacy orders protected hospitals but a 61% fill rate may have moved patient risk into the community rather than removed it.

# 4. Recovery process model

**Design principle.** Define an approved *degraded-mode reference model* now, so that in the next incident deviations can be checked against it: sanctioned substitution conforms, anything else is a real deviation. Every event carries the operating mode, the capture mode (system or manual), the time it happened and the time it was recorded.

## 4.1 Modes and explicit trigger conditions

| Transition | Trigger | Who declares |
|---|---|---|
| Normal -> **Degraded manual** | WMS unavailable or scan failure above an agreed threshold (for example 5% for 30 minutes), or a security containment order. | Incident lead with the quality-responsible person and operations; time of declaration logged. |
| Degraded manual -> **Hybrid** | Order intake restored, but cycle-count mismatch above tolerance or scan success below target. Manual evidence rules stay in force for every step that is not reliably system-captured. | Same group, on IT confirmation. |
| Hybrid -> **Stabilization** | Scan success sustained (for example 99.5% for 24h), controlled-substance stock fully counted and reconciled, count accuracy on cold-chain and recall-sensitive locations within tolerance. | Quality sign-off required. |
| Stabilization -> **Normal (incident closed)** | Backlog age back within baseline p90 per segment, remediation queue empty or formally dispositioned, control-completeness rates at baseline for five business days, fitness against the normative model back near 0.96. | Quality and management sign-off. |

Expiry triggers inside a mode:

- Inventory snapshot older than one shift, or tally variance above tolerance: recount before further picking from that zone.
- Authorization whitelist older than an agreed age (for example 72h): ambient orders go back to manual checks.
- Hospital-critical orders waiting more than 2h for authorization: add trained deputies; skipping is not an option.
- Pharmacist release queue above capacity: reduce ambient sampling first, never the 100% classes.

## 4.2 Triage rules

Triage uses two dimensions, customer criticality and product risk, plus a clinical-urgency flag that any customer can raise.

| Priority | Who / what | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Hospital-critical orders; any order flagged life-critical. | Fast lane, first in every queue. Authorization still required (whitelist or manual). |
| P2 | Pharmacy-standard orders for essential or time-sensitive medicines; cold-chain with stability limits. | Protected capacity share (for example at least 25% of pick capacity) so pharmacy fill does not fall below an agreed floor (for example 80%). |
| P3 | Pharmacy-standard routine ambient. | Served after P1/P2; may be deferred with notice. |
| P4 | Wholesale-bulk non-urgent. | Deferred by default with notice and promised date. |

Deferral rules:

- No deferral before triage (the 500 direct deferrals in T2 would not be allowed).
- Reason code, customer notification and promised date for every deferral.
- Maximum deferral age per class.
- No cancellation without customer contact.

## 4.3 Minimum controls by product and customer risk

| Control point | Ambient | Recall-sensitive | Cold-chain | Controlled-substance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Authorization | Dated, signed offline whitelist; manual check if not listed. | Manual check, or whitelist plus check against the current recall/quarantine list. | Manual check. | Manual check by compliance staff, 100%, including licence validity. No fast-lane exception. |
| Stock confirmation | Tally-adjusted snapshot. | Physical lot check. | Physical check with storage temperature status. | Physical count from secure store, two persons. |
| Pick and pack | Lot and expiry written on numbered form. | Lot/batch captured on every line; mandatory. | Pack-out time and temperature logger ID recorded. | Two-person pick and pack, both signatures, sealed container with seal number. |
| Release | Risk-based sample with defined rate. | 100% lot verification. | 100% pharmacist check including temperature record. | 100% pharmacist check plus second signature. |
| Dispatch | Signed manifest. | Signed manifest with lots. | Signed manifest with handoff time and logger ID. | Signed manifest with courier identity and seal number. |
| Proof of delivery | Phone or photo with named recipient and time; written confirmation within 48h. | Same as ambient plus lot confirmation. | Signed POD with receipt time and temperature. | Signed POD by an authorized recipient; phone-only not accepted. |
| If the minimum cannot be met | Ship only with supervisor approval, logged. | Hold. | Hold. Exception only for a life-critical hospital need, with documented quality approval before dispatch. | Hold. No exception without quality approval. |

Customer type changes *priority and queue position*. Product type sets the *control floor*. A hospital order for a controlled substance is first in the queue and still gets every control.

## 4.4 Manual evidence capture

- **Pre-positioned contingency kit:** sequentially numbered order travellers, pick sheets, release forms, temperature forms, dispatch manifests and POD forms.
- **Each form records** order ID, incident ID, performer, time, lot/expiry/quantity, checker, logger ID, seal number, courier and recipient name and signature.
- **Capture at the moment of the act,** on paper or an offline-capable device with photos.
- **Daily collection, scanning and indexing** of forms in a controlled repository.
- **Transcription into the system as manual-capture events** with both occurrence time and recording time and a link to the source document. Reconstructed data is labelled as reconstructed and never stored as a scan.
- **Inventory:** scheduled snapshot exports in normal operation (including lot, expiry and quarantine status), then a running decrement tally and shift counts during the outage.

## 4.5 Process model

Notation: `->(a, b)` is sequence, `X(a, b)` is exclusive choice, `AND(a, b)` is parallel, `LOOP(a, b)` means do `a`, then optionally `b` and repeat `a`. Text in square brackets is a guard.

```
Site_level = AND(
  LOOP(Monitor triggers and expiry conditions, Change mode),
  LOOP(Maintain inventory tally and shift counts, Recount zone),
  LOOP(Publish capacity, backlog age and control-completeness dashboard, Rebalance staff),
  Order_level (for every order)
)

Order_level = ->(
  Receive order [mode tag],
  Triage [P1..P4, product risk],
  X(
    ->( Defer with customer notice [P3/P4 only],
        LOOP(Backlog queue, Re-prioritise by published score),
        X( Re-enter at Authorize, Cancel with customer confirmation ) ),
    ->( Authorize = X( Whitelist check [ambient, whitelist valid],
                       Manual authorization check [all other cases] ),
        Confirm stock = X( Tally-adjusted snapshot [ambient, snapshot valid],
                           Physical check [all other cases] ),
        Manual pick with lot capture [two-person if controlled],
        Manual pack [AND temperature pack-out record if cold-chain],
        Release = X( Sampled release [ambient],
                     Full pharmacist release [recall-sensitive, cold-chain, controlled] ),
        Dispatch with signed manifest,
        Proof of delivery = X( Phone/photo plus written confirmation [ambient, recall-sensitive],
                               Signed POD [cold-chain, controlled] ),
        Evidence completeness gate,
        X( Invoice close [evidence complete],
           ->( Remediation,
               X( Invoice close [evidence recovered, labelled reconstructed],
                  ->( Deviation record and risk assessment, Provisional close ) ) ) ) )
  )
)
```

The main structural changes against what happened:

- No path from triage to stock confirmation without an authorization step.
- No path from pack to dispatch without a release step.
- No dispatch without handoff evidence.
- The evidence gate sits *before* Invoice close instead of audit remediation after it.

## 4.6 Backlog prioritization

- **Score** = customer criticality + product risk (stability, controlled status) + age relative to segment SLA, with an ageing escalator so that no order waits indefinitely.
- **Rules published** to staff and customers; manual overrides logged with approver and reason.
- **Backlog orders re-enter at authorization and stock confirmation** (as the T4 DFG already shows for 1,050 orders); nothing is released on the basis of a pre-incident reservation.
- **Customer confirmation before cancel,** and before shipping an order older than an agreed age, to avoid shipping duplicates of re-orders.

## 4.7 Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

1. **Inventory first, location-driven.** Count controlled substances 100%, then cold-chain, then recall-sensitive lots, then cycle counts by movement class. Post manual transactions in time order from tally sheets. Investigate variances above tolerance before the system reservation is trusted.
2. **Case-level evidence check** for every order handled in degraded or hybrid mode, with three outcomes:
   - *Complete contemporaneous manual evidence:* transcribe and close.
   - *Recoverable from independent sources* (courier records, logger downloads, written customer confirmation): remediate and label as reconstructed.
   - *Unrecoverable* (for example a temperature never recorded, an unsigned controlled-substance handoff): formal deviation, product-risk assessment, decision by the quality-responsible person, customer notification and regulatory reporting where required, possible product retrieval, corrective action.
3. **Remediation queue as a managed process:** named owner, SLA by risk (for example controlled 48h, cold-chain 72h, others 10 business days), daily burn-down by age and class, reported next to cycle time.
4. **Incident closure** only when the queue is empty or dispositioned, followed by a process-mining review of the incident log against the degraded-mode model.

The single most informative number is the **gap between operational recovery and compliance recovery**. In this case it was at least two weeks and still open.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (now) | **Retrospective risk review of T2/T3 shipments:** the 1,600 skipped authorizations, the roughly 920 fast-lane orders without authorization evidence, the 1,800 dispatches with no release control, the 650 dispatches without POD, all cold-chain orders lacking temperature records and all controlled orders lacking dual control. Run a mock recall on lots shipped in T2. | Low to medium; a few weeks of quality and analyst time. | Quantifies real exposure, separates recoverable from unrecoverable gaps, supports honest regulatory dialogue. | Some gaps cannot be closed; possible notifications or product retrieval. |
| 2 | **Approve a degraded-mode procedure and control matrix** with hard stops for controlled, cold-chain and recall-sensitive product, mode triggers and expiry rules. | Medium; cross-functional, requires quality approval and training. | Turns improvisation into sanctioned substitution; removes patterns C and G as local choices. | Staff under pressure may still deviate; hard stops may delay some urgent orders. |
| 3 | **Contingency evidence kit and manual capture standard** (numbered forms, signed manifests, offline photo capture, capture-mode flag in the log). | Low. | Closes the largest traceability gap at the lowest cost; makes later reconciliation transcription rather than reconstruction. | Paper errors and legibility; transcription workload. |
| 4 | **Offline reference data:** daily signed authorization whitelist and scheduled inventory snapshots with lot, expiry and quarantine status, stored outside the WMS; manual tally procedure. | Low to medium. | Replaces "assumed standing authorization"; keeps stock information usable beyond the first shift. | Data up to a day old at the start; tally discipline degrades over long outages. |
| 5 | **Evidence gate before invoice close and a visible remediation queue** with owner, SLA and burn-down; incident closure criteria that include compliance. | Medium; workflow and reporting change. | Ends the hidden compliance tail; management sees the true recovery date. | Slower billing and reported cycle times during incidents; pressure to relax the gate. |
| 6 | **Explicit triage and backlog rules with fairness safeguards:** product-criticality dimension, protected pharmacy capacity, deferral notices, ageing escalator, logged overrides. | Low. | Keeps hospital protection while limiting the pharmacy fill-rate collapse and deferral-driven cancellations. | Some hospital capacity given up; rules cannot cover every clinical situation. |
| 7 | **Surge capacity for control roles:** trained deputies for manual authorization and release, with a call-out roster. | Medium; recurring training cost. | Removes the bottleneck that made skipping attractive; allows 100% release for high-risk classes. | Deputies are less experienced; availability out of hours. |
| 8 | **Technical resilience:** offline-capable scanning with local buffering, an isolated read-only stock and authorization replica, tested restore sequence that brings inventory integrity back before order intake. | High; capital and IT project. | Shrinks the manual phase and the hybrid phase where records diverge. | A severe attack may still disable replicas; new systems add their own failure modes. |
| 9 | **Drills and monitoring:** tabletop and live exercises twice a year; mode-tagged event logging; conformance checking against the degraded-mode model; collect a T3-type DFG next time. | Medium. | Tests the procedure before it is needed; gives real-time separation of sanctioned and unsanctioned deviations. | Drill conditions understate real stress. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept that during a system outage the business will run slower, that non-urgent bulk and routine pharmacy orders will be deferred and some will be cancelled, that hospitals go first, and that paper, phone and sampling will replace system steps for low-risk ambient product. These are deliberate, visible trade-offs, and the hospital results in this incident (10h median, 91% fill) show they work. Management should not accept shipments of controlled, cold-chain or recall-sensitive product without real-time authorization, release, temperature and custody evidence; "assumed" authorizations; handoffs with no signed record; or the practice of closing and invoicing cases first and repairing evidence afterwards while reporting the operation as recovered. Half of the packed orders in the manual phase left without a release check and 8.3% of closed invoices later needed audit remediation, so the headline cycle times overstate how well the organisation coped. The recommendation would be relaxed if case-level review shows that skipped authorizations were confined to ambient product and all customers were in fact authorized, that manual pick records contain lot data written at the time, and that a mock recall on T2 lots succeeds within the normal time limit. It would be tightened, including stopping ambient shortcuts and holding more orders, if the review finds controlled or cold-chain product in the skipped or unscanned flows, evidence that cannot be recovered, controlled-substance stock variances, or signs that the 61% pharmacy fill rate caused patient harm.
