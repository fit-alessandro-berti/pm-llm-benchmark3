def aalen_johansen_cif_at(prep, cause, horizon):
    # returns CIF for cause at horizon using all terminal events as competing/censoring as encoded
    times = prep["time"].to_numpy()
    etypes = prep["event_type"].to_numpy()
    # event times for any non-censored event <= horizon
    event_times = sorted(set(times[(etypes != "censored") & (times <= horizon)]))
    S = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = np.sum(times >= t)
        if n == 0:
            continue
        d_all = np.sum((times == t) & (etypes != "censored"))
        d_cause = np.sum((times == t) & (etypes == cause))
        cif += S * d_cause / n
        S *= (1 - d_all / n)
    return cif, S

    # event = any terminal (non-censored)

        d = np.sum((times == t) & (etypes != "censored"))

# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let \(t\) be elapsed hours since `Submit`, so the time origin is the `Submit` event for every case. The primary SLA horizon is \(t=72\). The observation cutoff is heterogeneous in calendar time but common in the extract; for open cases, `duration_or_age_h` is age at cutoff, and `last_event_gap_h` is the time since the last recorded event to cutoff. For terminal cases, `duration_or_age_h` is time from `Submit` to the observed terminal event, and `last_event_gap_h` should be 0 for the terminal event.

Define terminal statuses observed internally as `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`. `Transfer` exits the internal system; eventual external resolution is unobserved. `open` means no terminal disposition observed by cutoff. A case can breach the 72-hour SLA either by still being unresolved at 72 hours, by already having a non-`Resolve` terminal disposition before 72 hours, or by resolving only after 72 hours.

Useful estimands:

1. **E1: Probability of not being internally resolved by 72 hours / Resolve-breach risk.**  
   Let \(F_R(t)=P(\text{first terminal event is Resolve by }t)\) under an Aalen-Johansen competing-risk interpretation, with `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` as competing terminal outcomes under the primary transfer assumption. Then  
   \[
   \text{breach}_{72} = 1-F_R(72).
   \]
   This counts as breach anyone not observed to reach `Resolve` by 72 hours, including withdrawals, rejections, transfers, and still-open cases. It is closest to the statement “case is operationally successful when it reaches `Resolve`.”

2. **E2: Probability of no terminal disposition by 72 hours / active backlog risk.**  
   Let \(S_{\text{term}}(t)=P(T_{\text{any terminal}}>t)\), where any of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer` is a terminal event and `open` is right-censored at cutoff. This measures the probability that a case is still active/unfinished at 72 hours, regardless of whether eventual disposition would be success or failure. It is the best match for live workload, queue capacity, and active SLA backlog.

3. **E3: Late-resolution risk after the SLA.**  
   For \(u>72\), define the cumulative incidence of `Resolve` in \((72,u]\) among cases still at risk at 72, preferably using a landmark at 72 or a multi-state model. This measures tail recovery: among cases not yet terminated at 72, how many later resolve? It matches decisions about backlog clearance and whether a breached case is likely to recover.

4. **E4: Cause-specific cumulative incidence by disposition.**  
   \(F_k(t)\) for \(k\in\{\text{Resolve},\text{Withdraw},\text{Reject},\text{Transfer}\}\). This is best for understanding diversion, cancellation, and transfer behavior, and avoids treating competing outcomes as ordinary censoring.

Case-specific contribution under the primary competing-risk assumptions:

| Case | E1: no `Resolve` by 72 | E2: no terminal disposition by 72 | E3: late `Resolve` after 72 | E4: cause-specific contribution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76h | At 72h unresolved, so contributes to breach at 72; resolves in the tail after 72. | No terminal disposition by 72; terminal `Resolve` occurs at 76. | Enters the 72h landmark risk set and contributes to late `Resolve` if \(u\ge76\). | `Resolve` incidence at 76; no competing terminal event before it. |
| S09, open at 92h, gap 31h | Known not resolved through 92h, so definitely contributes to “no `Resolve` by 72”; censored after 72. | Known no terminal disposition through 92h, so contributes to active/backlog at 72; censored at 92. | At risk at the 72h landmark; censored at 92 without observed late `Resolve`. | No disposition event observed; contributes to censoring/risk sets. |
| S06, withdrawn at 27h | Not resolved by 72; withdrawal is a competing terminal event at 27. | Terminal disposition occurred at 27, so not active/open at 72. | Not at risk at the 72h landmark because already terminal. | Contributes to `Withdraw` cumulative incidence at 27; not to `Resolve`. |
| S08, transferred at 68h | Primary: not resolved internally by 72; transfer is a competing terminal event at 68. | Terminal internal disposition occurred at 68, so not active at 72 under primary transfer-is-terminal encoding. | Not at risk at 72 under primary transfer-is-terminal encoding; under censor-at-transfer it would be censored before 72. | Contributes to `Transfer` incidence at 68; under best-case transfer assumption could be treated as `Resolve` at transfer. |

Right censoring, competing events, and already-observed breaches must be kept distinct. Open cases are right-censored at cutoff unless they are already known to have had no terminal event by 72. `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are competing terminal events for E1 and E2; they are not ordinary non-informative censoring unless explicitly chosen as a sensitivity assumption. A resolved case at 76 hours is an observed late resolution, not a censored case, and it is a known 72-hour breach under E1. An open case at 92 hours is also a known 72-hour breach under E1 even though its ultimate outcome is unknown.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A completed-resolved-case analysis would keep only cases whose observed status is `resolved`: S01, S02, S03, S04, S07, S13, S16, S17, S18, S19, S20, S27, and S28. It would exclude open, withdrawn, rejected, and transferred cases. That conditions on eventual success and, for late cases, on surviving long enough to be resolved and observed.

Concrete biases:

- **Survivor bias:** S03 resolves at 76 hours and S13 at 110 hours. These appear in a resolved-only analysis and may look like “eventually resolved” successes, but both breached the 72-hour SLA. Meanwhile S09 is open at 92 hours, S11 open at 81 hours, S25 open at 79 hours, and S26 open at 104 hours. These are old, unresolved cases that a resolved-only analysis silently drops. The remaining resolved cases are not representative of the full risk set.

- **Informative censoring and long gaps:** Open cases are not all equally “active.” S11 has age 81 and last-event gap 55 hours; S26 has age 104 and gap 72 hours; S25 has age 79 and gap 48 hours. These look like long-inactive or stalled cases, not random administrative censoring. Treating them as exchangeable with S15 (age 35, gap 2) or S31 (age 23, gap 3) assumes the future resolution hazard is the same after long inactivity, which is doubtful. Ignoring this can bias both `Resolve` and backlog estimates.

- **Competing risks:** Early withdrawals and rejections such as S05 at 9 hours, S12 at 15 hours, and S23 at 11 hours are terminal non-resolutions. If Kaplan-Meier for `Resolve` censors them, the resulting curve estimates a hypothetical resolution probability in a world where withdrawal/rejection could not occur. That is not the operational probability that a case reaches `Resolve` by 72 hours.

- **Transfer censoring:** S08, S21, and S32 are transferred after escalation and are all P1 in the supplied data. If transfers are simply censored, the analysis assumes their unobserved outcomes are independent of transfer. Because transfer is an escalated, priority-selected event, that assumption is questionable. Treating transfer as non-informative can understate or overstate tail risk depending on whether transferred cases would have resolved externally.

- **Cohort/policy imbalance:** Cohort B began after an automated-screening and escalation-policy change, but rollout was incomplete and non-randomized. B contains several `Submit>AutoCheck>...` prefixes, e.g., S16, S18, S21, S24, S25, S27, S28, S29, S31, S32, whereas cohort A mostly starts `Submit>Screen>...` except for S01, S15, and possibly early routing differences. A crude cohort comparison mixes policy, selection, case mix, priority, and rollout compliance. It cannot be interpreted as a causal policy effect.

- **Open below versus above 72:** S15 (open at 35h, gap 2), S24 (open at 67h, gap 4), S29 (open at 51h, gap 6), and S31 (open at 23h, gap 3) are censored before or near 72. Their 72-hour breach status is unknown and must be estimated. In contrast, S09 (92h), S11 (81h), S25 (79h), and S26 (104h) are open beyond 72, so they are already known to have had no `Resolve` by 72 and no terminal disposition by 72. Treating all open cases as the same censored category confuses observed breaches with unknown future status.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Primary analysis: stratified Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence for E1, with supporting Kaplan-Meier/Aalen-Johansen summaries for E2.**  
Use `Resolve` as the event of interest and `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` as competing events under the primary transfer assumption. Report \(F_R(72)\) and breach risk \(1-F_R(72)\), overall and stratified by priority and cohort. For E2, use the same terminal-event encoding but treat any terminal disposition as the event; report \(S_{\text{term}}(72)\). This directly estimates absolute risks under competing events, does not require a proportional-hazards assumption, and is appropriate for a small dataset. Variance estimation can use the Aalen-Johansen delta-method variance or bootstrap over cases; because of small strata, confidence intervals will be wide and should be shown.

Secondary methods:

- **Cause-specific hazards Cox models:** Useful for exploratory covariate analysis of the transition intensity to `Resolve` and to competing events, with open cases right-censored and transfers handled by scenario. Because `Escalate` and rework are post-`Submit`, they must enter as time-varying covariates or landmark predictors, not baseline covariates. With 32 cases, only one or two covariates at a time, penalization/Firth correction, or Bayesian shrinkage is defensible.

- **Landmark analysis at 72 hours:** For E3, restrict to cases still at risk and not terminally dispositioned at 72, then model later `Resolve` or terminal outcomes using covariates known by 72. This avoids immortal-time bias from classifying “ever escalated by end of follow-up” or “final rework count” at baseline. The 72-hour landmark set here is small and should be descriptive/exploratory.

- **Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards:** Useful as a secondary direct model of the cumulative incidence of `Resolve` for prognostic risk stratification. It is less mechanistically interpretable than cause-specific hazards and does not remove confounding. With this sample size, avoid overfitting and present only if calibrated and stable under bootstrap.

- **Restricted mean time (RMT):** Report restricted mean time to terminal disposition, or restricted mean time free of `Resolve`/breach, up to \(\tau=72\) or \(\tau=120\). RMT is interpretable for operational burden, but the horizon and event definition must be stated. For competing risks, use cause-specific restricted mean time or restricted mean time lost, not a naive mean among resolved cases.

- **Multi-state model:** A process-aware multi-state model can represent `Submit`, early screening/auto-check, review, escalation, rework, terminal outcomes, and censoring. It is conceptually attractive for process mining, but with 32 cases it should be exploratory or hierarchical/regularized. It is not the primary estimator for precise 72-hour absolute risk.

The primary recommendation is Aalen-Johansen because the key decision is “what fraction reaches `Resolve` by 72, or remains as active/competing non-resolution?” and the data are too small for a stable multivariable survival model. Cause-specific hazards, landmarking, and multi-state modeling are secondary for covariate/process insight.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Process-aware features should be tied to the prediction time. A feature is not safe merely because it appears in the prefix; using the full final prefix or final `rework_count` at `Submit` leaks future events.

| Feature | Example from data | Timing/type | Safe use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Priority | P1, P2, P3 | Baseline | Allowed as baseline, but monitor by priority; P1 is highest. |
| Cohort A/B | Policy era proxy | Baseline but non-randomized | Use for stratification/adjustment; not a causal policy variable by itself. |
| Initial routing pattern | First activity after `Submit`, e.g., `Screen` vs `AutoCheck` | Baseline/early policy feature if assigned at submission; otherwise early time-varying | Can be a baseline process-policy feature if known at time 0. Interpret descriptively because it is tied to cohort rollout. |
| Current stage | Last observed state, e.g., `Review`, `Escalate`, `AutoCheck` | Time-varying | Use in landmark/dynamic prediction; allows hazard to depend on current stage. |
| Repeated review | Count of `Review` up to time \(t\) | Time-varying | Safe if counted only through the prediction time. Final repeated-review count is leakage. |
| Rework | Indicator/count of `Rework` up to \(t\) | Time-varying, often post-treatment | Use only when known at prediction time; may be on causal pathway and confounded by indication. |
| Escalation status | `escalated=yes` in supplied row | Occurs after start; post-treatment/time-varying | Never use final escalation status as baseline. Use “escalated by landmark \(t\)” only in a landmark model, and interpret as prognostic, not causal. |
| Escalation timing | Time from `Submit` to first `Escalate` | Time-varying/post-treatment | Enter as time-varying covariate or landmark feature; check immortal-time bias. |
| Full path variant | `Submit>Screen>Review>Rework>Review>Resolve` | End-of-follow-up process summary | Descriptive/control-flow feature only. Using the full final variant at baseline is leakage. Use prefix up to \(t\) for dynamic models. |
| Inactivity gap | `last_event_gap_h`, or gap at landmark calculated from age and last-event age | Time-varying/censoring-related | Essential for informative-censoring sensitivity; for prediction at time \(t\), compute time since last event as of \(t\), not the final cutoff gap. |
| Open/censored age | Age at cutoff | Follow-up/censoring feature | Use as censor time, not as a baseline outcome predictor unless a landmark is used. |

A proposed process-level state-transition representation is:

`Submit` -> `Screen` or `AutoCheck` -> `Review` -> optional repeated `Review`/`Rework` cycles -> optional `Escalate` -> `Review` -> terminal state.

Terminal states: `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`, and right-censoring/open at cutoff. In a multi-state model, transitions might be: early routing (`Screen` vs `AutoCheck`), entry to `Review`, `Rework` back to `Review`, `Escalate` from `Review`, post-escalation `Review`, and terminal transitions from the current state. Each transition hazard can depend on priority, cohort, current stage, cumulative review count, prior escalation, and time since last event. Open cases remain in their current nonterminal state until censored. This representation naturally distinguishes a case that is stalled in `Review` from one that has been escalated and is awaiting outcome.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least four sensitivity dimensions should be reported:

1. **Transfer treatment.** Compare T1 transfer-as-competing, T2 censor-at-transfer for latent `Resolve`, and T3 best-case transfer-resolves-at-transfer. T4 worst-case unresolved is also useful. If transfers S08, S21, and S32 are high-risk escalated P1 cases, treating them as non-informative censoring may understate internal tail risk; if they are transferred to effective external care, the competing-event analysis may overstate failure. Conclusions about the P1 tail and about cohort differences could reverse.

2. **Withdrawal/rejection handling.** Primary: `Withdraw` and `Reject` are competing terminal non-resolutions. Sensitivity: censor them for a hypothetical “time to `Resolve` if withdrawal/rejection did not occur” estimand. Because early withdrawals S12 at 15h and S06 at 27h remove cases from the risk set, censoring them can materially change the apparent `Resolve` cumulative incidence. If one priority/cohort has more early withdrawals, its ranking on “success by 72” can reverse.

3. **Informative censoring and inactivity gaps.** Primary: open cases are independently right-censored at cutoff conditional on covariates. Sensitivity: analyze long-gap open cases as likely inactive/stalled, use IPCW with covariates such as priority, cohort, current stage, and last-event gap, or apply delta-adjustment assumptions such as “long-inactive open cases have no future `Resolve`.” S26 (gap 72), S11 (gap 55), and S25 (gap 48) are particularly influential. If long-inactive open cases are truly low-hazard, ordinary censoring can understate backlog and breach risk. A conclusion that cohort B has better tail behavior could reverse if B’s long open cases are treated as stalled failures.

4. **Cohort-policy effects.** Compare unadjusted cohort A/B with priority-stratified and process-adjusted comparisons. Because cohort B was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, a cohort effect is a mixture of policy, referral selection, case mix, priority mix, and process changes such as `AutoCheck` placement. Without dates and compliance data, no causal policy effect should be claimed. Sensitivity should vary adjustment for priority and early routing; conclusions about “the new policy reduced tail risk” could reverse under adjustment or under different transfer/censoring assumptions.

Other useful sensitivities are the SLA horizon (72h only versus late window to 120h), landmark choice (e.g., 24h, 48h, 72h), and tie handling at exactly 72h. These should be prespecified or clearly labeled exploratory because the dataset is small.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival outputs should be translated into prognostic risk, not causal effects. A 72-hour breach probability is “risk of not reaching `Resolve` by 72 under the stated outcome/censoring assumptions,” not “the effect of escalation” or “the effect of cohort B.”

Operational uses:

- **Dynamic alerts:** At `Submit` or shortly after initial routing, estimate baseline 72-hour breach risk using priority, cohort/policy era, and early routing pattern. At 24h/48h/72h landmarks, update risk using current stage, number of reviews, rework so far, escalation by that landmark, and inactivity gap. Do not use final path variants or final rework counts before they occur.

- **Workload actions:** Use E2 \(S_{\text{term}}(72)\) to forecast active backlog at SLA and E1 breach risk to identify cases likely to miss the resolution target. Cases with rising risk and long inactivity gaps, e.g., open beyond 72 with gap >48h, should trigger a review or welfare/status check, because missing events may indicate either low activity or informative censoring.

- **SLA governance:** Report both resolution cumulative incidence and competing disposition incidence. A dashboard should show: `Resolve` by 72; non-resolution by withdrawal/rejection/transfer by 72; still active at 72; late `Resolve` after 72; and counts censored before 72. This prevents “resolved count” from hiding transfers and withdrawals.

- **Calibration and fairness:** For any risk score, check calibration by priority and cohort using reliability curves or observed-to-expected breach ratios, plus Brier score/AUC only as secondary. Because priority and cohort contain small subgroups, show bootstrap confidence intervals and avoid overinterpreting small differences. Check alert rates and calibration separately for P1/P2/P3 and A/B. Cohort must not be used as a performance target unless policy rollout is properly measured; it is a proxy for non-random process change and possibly inequitable access. Also monitor whether long-gap open cases or transferred cases are systematically under-alerted.

    times = prep["time"].to_numpy()
    etypes = prep["event_type"].to_numpy()
    event_times = sorted(set(times[(etypes != "censored") & (times <= horizon)]))
    S = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = np.sum(times >= t)
        if n == 0:

        d_cause = np.sum((times == t) & (etypes == cause))
        cif += S * d_cause / n
        S *= (1 - d_all / n)
    return cif, S

    times = prep["time"].to_numpy()
    etypes = prep["event_type"].to_numpy()

    S = 1.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = np.sum(times >= t)
        d = np.sum((times == t) & (etypes != "censored"))
        if n > 0:
            S *= (1 - d / n)
    return S

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The largest limitation is that exact event timestamps are not supplied; only total duration/age and the last-event gap are available. This prevents proper process-mining conformance checking, exact stage-entry times, calendar-time adjustment, and precise time-varying covariate construction. Additional data needed in priority order:

1. **Exact event timestamps** for every activity, including `Submit`, `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`, terminal events, and the observation cutoff. These would allow time-varying covariates, proper multi-state modeling, tie handling, and validation of derived gaps.

2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`**, including whether and when the external organization resolves, rejects, or withdraws the case. Without this, transfer must remain a sensitivity-dependent censoring/competing outcome.

3. **Start dates, cutoff date, and policy rollout dates/compliance.** Cohort B is a non-randomized policy-era proxy. To estimate a policy effect, we need actual exposure to automated screening/escalation, local rollout timing, compliance, and case arrival dates for interrupted time-series or difference-in-differences designs.

4. **Reasons and mechanisms for open/censored cases.** Is an open case awaiting review, stalled, lost, or already externally transferred but not recorded? Long gaps such as S26’s 72-hour gap are ambiguous without event/activity data.

5. **Time-varying workload and capacity**, staffing, queue length, and priority changes. These are important confounders for escalation, rework, and resolution time, and they affect whether censoring is informative.

6. **Assignment mechanism for escalation and priority.** `Escalate` and `escalated=yes` are post-`Submit` and likely confounded by indication. A causal effect requires a time-aware design, ideally randomization or an instrument/encouragement, not a baseline “ever escalated” indicator.

7. **More cases and repeated cohorts.** With 32 cases, stratified estimates by priority and cohort are very imprecise. More cases would support regularized cause-specific or multi-state models and meaningful calibration/fairness assessment.

8. **Measurement details for `Withdraw` and `Reject`.** Are these subject decisions, administrative closures, or eligibility failures? The competing-risk interpretation depends on what these dispositions mean operationally.
