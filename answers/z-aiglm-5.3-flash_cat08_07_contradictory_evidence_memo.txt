## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures how much observed behavior the model can replay; precision penalizes behavior the model allows that was never observed. The pilot generated far more behavioral variety — skipped surveys, repeated installs, manual activations, billing before activation — so a model that fits it well must license many path combinations, crushing precision (0.790.51) and generalization (0.700.56). This is a signal of reduced process control and drift, not a contradiction: "the model fits" and "the process is disciplined" are different claims. The team's reading (many combinations of skips, manual activations, and billing starts) is the most plausible one, though rare-combination artifacts can contribute.

**Improved median but worse tail.** The pilot produced a bimodal process. The simple-house path (merged validate/schedule, fewer handovers) pulls the median down; the exception loops — install repeated without survey  failed visit  second visit; missing permits on old buildings; billing disputes — stretch p90/p95. Queueing mechanics amplify this: at 93% coordinator utilization there is no slack, so small variability becomes long waits, which is exactly where the temporal profile shows failure (failedsecond-visit delay worst in high-utilization regions). A median is an average experience; the tail is the queue experience. Both improved and worsened simultaneously because different segments ride different paths.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handover count is a flow-efficiency metric; resilience is a redundancy property. Sole ownership centralized context in one person — visible in the social network analysis (simpler handover network, but working-together network concentrated on coordinators and activation specialists). When that person is absent, context is not shared and cases stall; the implicit cross-checks that multiple handovers provided also disappeared, consistent with the FTR drop. Fewer handovers was the goal; the side effect was removal of the system's shock absorbers.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost.** The cost accounting boundary moved with the process. R removed visible upstream effort (survey 78%52% utilization, fewer handovers) and generated downstream effort (manual activation, second visits, complaints) — and complaint handling after week 4 is excluded entirely. So EUR 161 vs 184 flatters R: cost was not eliminated, it was relocated across the measurement boundary and deferred into the tail. The incomplete full-cost estimate already shows R worse (198207) before all downstream cost is even counted.

**Simple houses improve; apartments/old buildings deteriorate.** R is not one treatment but four, with opposite signs by segment. The skip rule misclassifies medium-risk apartments; permit checks are genuinely needed for old buildings; business customers hit billing disputes from post-billing activation. Averaging across segments produces a Simpson's-paradox-like aggregation where the portfolio metric depends on mix. Compounding this, the pilot's case mix was skewed toward the hard segments, so portfolio deterioration overstates the like-for-like effect — while the segment table, not the portfolio table, is the trustworthy basis for decisions.

Scale R nationally** | Captures median (3.4d), handover (22pp), and visible-cost (EUR 23) gains quickly; operationally simple | Nationalizes the tail (p95 +17d), rework (FTR 6pp, second visits +7pp), and compliance-like exposure (billing-before-activation, missing permits); coordinator bottleneck at 93% already; NPS erosion; locks in before attribution | Essentially only after segment gating, capacity, and conformance fixes — i.e., not today in its piloted form |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Stops harm to apartments/old buildings/business immediately; returns to a known, better-disciplined process (precision 0.79) | Discards proven simple-house value (6.0d median, +3pp FTR, 1pp complaints) and the handover win; baseline itself is mediocre (13% SLA breach, 45d p95); destroys learning and morale | If M4/M7 show severe regulatory or financial exposure, or harms are proven causal and unfixable within the design |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skip rule and post-billing activation** | Keeps the most robust gain (accountability, 22pp handovers) while removing the two clearly harmful components; restores activation/permit discipline | Coordinator bottleneck likely worsens at national scale without a staffing plan; resilience flaw unaddressed; tail from permits/second visits may persist; still little measurement to verify | If a capacity plan plus backup coverage exists and leadership accepts slower evidence accumulation |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Strongest causal evidence; components tested separately; avoids nationalizing mistakes | Delays simple-house benefits (34% of volume) — real opportunity cost; control regions keep an inferior baseline; design requires M1–M3 instrumentation first; risk of analysis paralysis | If the time horizon tolerates ~8–12 weeks and uncertainty is the binding constraint on the decision |
| **E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale** | Captures proven segment value now; caps harm via controls; preserves option value; matches the segment evidence; builds the rollout gate from real data | Operational complexity of running two variants; gating logic must be reliable (depends on M6); monitoring overhead; risk of segment misclassification at intake | If gating is implementable quickly and M7 guardrails are live — which is achievable now, which is why it is recommended |

**Synthesis.** No alternative dominates all metrics, and the evidence does not support a binary call. E, with C's suspensions embedded, is the only option that realizes the proven gains, caps the proven harms, and buys the information that resolves the scale question. A and B are premature; D alone forgoes realized value, though D's machinery (M1–M3) is embedded inside E.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Adopt a modified Option E — segment-gated hybrid deployment — with Option C's suspensions embedded and Option D's measurement machinery running underneath. Do not scale nationally in the piloted form; do not roll back.**

- **Immediate action (this week):** Freeze the survey-skip rule for medium-risk apartments; suspend post-billing activation outside simple houses; reinstate permit checks as a hard conformance gate with automated flags for old buildings; stand up M7 dashboards; launch M2 and M3 instrumentation; begin M6 offline validation of the risk rule; start the M4 60-day full-cost tracker; impose coordinator capacity guardrails (utilization cap 85%, named backups, shared case notes).

- **What to pause:** Survey skipping everywhere except where M6/M1 subsequently validate it; post-billing activation everywhere except simple houses pending dispute evidence; any further geographic or segment expansion of R.

- **What to scale:** Coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule — but **only for simple low-risk houses**, nationally, with the resilience safeguards above. Keep field-survey capacity warm (52% utilization); it is needed for M1 and for complex segments.

- **What to measure next, in sequence:** M7, M3, M2, M6 immediately; M1 (stratified randomized skip trial, two regions) once M6 indicates the rule is salvageable; M4 running continuously; M8 for the customer trade-off; M5 before extending sole coordinator ownership to complex segments.

- **Thresholds justifying broader rollout (proposed targets, for management ratification):** apartment/old-building p95 back to 45 days or within 10% of baseline; portfolio FTR 80% and complaints 7%; full cost (M4)  EUR 198; billing-before-activation deviations near zero or explicitly customer-consented; permit conformance  baseline; coordinator utilization sustained 85% with a passed absence stress test. Skip-rule re-enablement is decided stratum-by-stratum on M1/M6 evidence, not on portfolio averages.

- **Contingency:** Roll back (B) only if M4 shows full cost is worse even for simple houses, or if permit/activation exposure proves regulatorily severe.

**Bottom line.** R is a bundle whose components succeed and fail on different segments. The defensible move is to unbundle it: keep the proven core (ownership, merged scheduling) where it works, suspend the failing components where they fail, and spend a few weeks of cheap instrumentation to convert the current conflicting signals into a confident scale/no-scale decision per segment.
