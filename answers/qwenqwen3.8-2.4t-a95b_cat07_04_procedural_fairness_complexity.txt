# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness cannot be judged from raw approval rates alone. The relevant question is whether applicants in similar legitimate circumstances receive comparable treatment, comparable access to rights-preserving support, comparable procedural burden, and comparable timeliness, without being disadvantaged by biased measurements or under-resourced support routes.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A fair assessment should distinguish four concepts:

1. **Disparity**: an observed difference between groups, raw or adjusted.
2. **Legitimate routing**: a difference explained by valid policy-relevant needs, such as risk, accommodation, or language preference.
3. **Discrimination or unfair differential treatment**: a difference that remains after comparing applicants with similar legitimate needs and that cannot be explained by valid complexity or resource constraints.
4. **Uncertainty**: residual ambiguity due to biased covariates, unobserved case characteristics, or limited sample size.

## Matching or stratification variables

The analysis should compare applicants who are similar on legitimate case-complexity dimensions, while being careful not to treat rights-preserving support needs as illegitimate “costs” to be controlled away.

Recommended stratification or matching variables:

| Variable | Why include it | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| `risk_score_band` | Policy requires higher scrutiny for higher risk. | Must not be treated as unquestionably neutral. It may encode historical bias or unequal prior contact with the system. |
| `documentation_completeness` | Incomplete documentation legitimately increases requests and review. | May itself be affected by language barriers, accessibility, digital access, or prior case history. |
| `channel` | In-person and online cases may have different processing constraints. | Channel may reflect access barriers rather than free applicant choice. |
| `language_preference` | Minority-language support is a legitimate need. | Should be treated as a protected access need, not as a defect. |
| `disability_accommodation_requested` | Accommodation review is a legitimate rights-preserving step. | Should not be used to justify slower service without assessing whether the support step is adequately resourced. |
| `household_size_band` | Larger households may increase complexity. | Should be checked for interaction with income, region, and documentation burden. |
| `prior_case_count` | Prior cases may indicate complexity or system familiarity. | May also reflect prior adverse treatment or surveillance. |
| `region` | Regional capacity and backlog differ. | Region may be a source of unequal resource availability. |
| `month` | Seasonality and policy changes may affect processing. | Needed to avoid confounding time trends with group differences. |

Recommended modeling approach:

- Use **coarsened exact matching**, propensity-score weighting, or stratified regression to compare similar cases.
- Estimate two main adjusted comparisons:
  1. **Total adjusted disparity**: control for baseline case mix but do not control away support-step usage. This captures the full burden associated with being in a group.
  2. **Within-support-need disparity**: compare applicants with the same language preference, accommodation request, risk band, documentation status, channel, region, and month. This tests whether support steps themselves create unequal treatment.
- Avoid adjusting for post-routing variables such as `Request Missing Documents`, `Supervisor Review`, `Fraud Review`, or `Appeal Intake` when estimating the total procedural burden, unless the goal is mediation analysis.

## Path/variant comparison

Variant analysis should answer:

- Are groups equally likely to receive the fast, standard path when their case characteristics are similar?
- Are Group B and Group C overrepresented in slower support-related variants even after controlling for language and accommodation needs?
- Is Group D overrepresented in fraud and appeal variants beyond what risk_score_band predicts?
- Within the same variant, do cycle times and waiting times still differ by group?

Key comparisons:

- Compare Group A and Group B cases with the same documentation completeness, channel, language preference, and risk band.
- Compare Group C and non-Group C cases with the same accommodation request status.
- Compare Group D and non-Group D cases within the same risk_score_band.
- Compare approval, appeal, and rework rates within each variant, not only across variants.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Resource analysis should test whether groups receive comparable access to decision-making capacity.

Recommended metrics:

- Number of distinct caseworkers per case by group.
- Number of handovers per case by group.
- Share of cases handled by specialized bottleneck resources.
- Caseload per resource by group.
- Resource similarity: whether Group B and Group C are handled by a narrow set of resources while Group A is spread broadly.
- Organizational roles: whether bilingual caseworkers or accommodation specialists are also required for supervisor review, creating queue conflicts.
- Working-together network: whether certain groups are passed through longer chains of resources.

Fairness concern arises if Group B or Group C cases are formally entitled to support but are assigned to under-capacity specialists, causing avoidable delay.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Cycle time should be decomposed into:

- Service time: active processing time.
- Waiting time: queue time between activities.
- Transition-specific waiting time: for example, Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment.
- Case-level waiting time inserted using PM4Py’s service/waiting-time functions.

Recommended decomposition:

| Transition | Fairness question |
|---|---|
| Submit  Identity Verification | Are in-person or non-online applicants waiting longer for basic intake? |
| Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents | Are missing-document requests delayed by lack of bilingual staff? |
| Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment | Is language support creating a queue penalty? |
| Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment | Is accommodation support creating a queue penalty? |
| Risk Screening  Fraud Review | Is fraud review delay proportionate and consistent across groups? |
| Supervisor Review  Decision Draft | Are supervisor backlogs concentrated in regions or groups? |
| Decision Draft  Applicant Notification | Are final notifications delayed for some groups? |

The key fairness test is whether waiting-time differences remain after comparing cases with the same legitimate routing needs.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

Because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may not be neutral, the analysis should include sensitivity checks:

1. **Exclude risk_score_band** from the adjustment set and compare results.
2. **Include risk_score_band** but test whether its predictive validity differs by group.
3. **Audit risk_score_band calibration**:
   - Compare false-positive rates for fraud review by group.
   - Compare appeal success rates among cases routed to fraud review by group.
   - Check whether high-risk classification leads to similar outcomes for similar applicants.
4. **Recode documentation_completeness**:
   - Distinguish initial incompleteness from repeated requests.
   - Check whether incomplete documentation is associated with language preference, channel, or accommodation request.
5. **Alternative fairness specifications**:
   - Treat language support and accommodation review as protected access needs rather than complexity penalties.
   - Estimate whether applicants using these rights experience worse timeliness than similar applicants not using them.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

Subgroup analysis should identify whether disparities are system-wide or localized.

Recommended checks:

- By region:
  - Are the two regions with supervisor backlogs responsible for most Group B and Group C delays?
  - Are handover loops concentrated in specific regions?
- By channel:
  - Do in-person Group B cases experience longer waits than online Group B cases?
  - Are online applicants with language-support needs still delayed?
- By language preference:
  - Among minority-language applicants, do Group B applicants still experience longer waits than other minority-language applicants?
- By accommodation request:
  - Among applicants requesting accommodation, does Group C still experience longer waits?
- By risk band:
  - Within the same risk band, does Group D still have higher appeal or supervisor review rates?

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least four mechanisms could generate the observed patterns. Some are legitimate, while others raise fairness concerns.

## 1. Legitimate case-complexity routing

Higher measured risk, incomplete documentation, accommodation requests, and language-support needs may legitimately trigger additional steps.

Evidence:

- Group D has higher high-risk share and is more often routed through Fraud Review.
- Group C has high accommodation-request share and is more often routed through Accommodation Review.
- Group B has high language-support need and incomplete documentation.

Fairness implication:

- This mechanism can explain part of the raw disparity.
- It does not justify excessive delay or poor-quality support.

## 2. Resource scarcity and queue design

Rights-preserving support steps may be delayed because too few staff are available.

Evidence:

- Only 7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B language-support cases.
- Only 5 accommodation specialists for Group C cases.
- Two accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review.
- Language Support  Eligibility and Accommodation Review  Eligibility have long waits.

Fairness implication:

- This is a strong procedural fairness concern.
- Applicants may be formally entitled to support but practically disadvantaged by insufficient capacity.

## 3. Measurement bias in risk and documentation indicators

`risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may reflect historical disparities rather than neutral case complexity.

Evidence:

- Group D has high prior-case count and high measured risk.
- Group B has high incomplete documentation and high language-support need.
- Documentation completeness may be affected by language barriers, inaccessible forms, or lack of translated guidance.

Fairness implication:

- If risk scores or documentation measures are biased, routing may appear legitimate while reproducing disadvantage.
- Auditing these variables is necessary before treating adjusted comparisons as definitive.

## 4. Communication burden and unclear decision-making

Applicants may receive requests, decisions, or explanations that are not accessible, leading to missing documents, appeals, and rework.

Evidence:

- Group B has higher adjusted missing-document probability.
- Group B and Group D have higher adjusted appeal probability.
- Group B and Group C have higher raw reopen/rework rates.

Fairness implication:

- If forms, notices, or explanations are not linguistically or accessibly designed, the process imposes avoidable burden.
- Appeals may reflect not only applicant disagreement but also poor explanation or perceived unfairness.

## 5. Regional capacity and handover friction

Localized backlogs and handover chains may produce group-specific delays.

Evidence:

- Supervisor review backlog in two regions.
- Group B has more general  bilingual  general handovers in two regions.
- Group C has more general  accommodation specialist  supervisor handovers.

Fairness implication:

- Disparities may be partly regional rather than system-wide.
- Targeted process redesign could reduce delays without changing substantive eligibility rules.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event-log attributes support useful process-mining analysis, but they are not sufficient to make a definitive discrimination finding.

## Measurement bias in risk_score_band

`risk_score_band` may be correlated with legitimate risk, but it may also encode historical disparities. Possible problems include:

- Prior cases may reflect prior surveillance rather than actual wrongdoing.
- Risk models may be trained on outcomes that are themselves biased.
- Risk thresholds may be calibrated differently across regions or applicant populations.
- High-risk classification may trigger more scrutiny, which can produce more findings and appear self-justifying.

Therefore, risk_score_band should be audited, not treated as a neutral control variable.

## Documentation completeness may be endogenous

`documentation_completeness` is important, but it may not be purely applicant-caused. It may be affected by:

- Language barriers.
- Lack of translated forms.
- Digital access problems.
- Disability-related access needs.
- Unclear requests from caseworkers.
- Repeated requests caused by internal handovers.

If documentation incompleteness is partly produced by the process, controlling for it may hide unfairness.

## Missing applicant needs and circumstances

The log does not appear to capture:

- Literacy level.
- Digital access.
- Health condition severity.
- Caregiving responsibilities.
- Housing instability.
- Evidence availability.
- Whether the applicant received assistance.
- Whether communication was understandable to the applicant.
- Whether accommodations were actually effective.

These unobserved factors may explain part of the remaining disparity.

## Outcome quality is not captured

`case_outcome` does not show:

- Whether the decision was correct.
- Whether the explanation was adequate.
- Whether the applicant was treated with dignity.
- Whether partial benefits were offered.
- Whether the benefit amount was appropriate.
- Whether the decision was timely relative to applicant need.
- Whether the applicant understood the reason for denial.

Procedural fairness includes explanation quality and respectful treatment, not only approval/denial.

## Appeal quality is not captured

The log has `appeal_flag`, but not:

- Whether appeals were successful.
- Why appeals were filed.
- Whether appeals revealed process errors.
- Whether appellants had representation.
- How long appeals took.
- Whether appeal outcomes differ by group.

A high appeal rate could indicate applicant burden, poor communication, legitimate contestation, or actual errors.

## Resource workload is only observed at daily granularity

The resource data do not show:

- Concurrent case load.
- Time spent per case.
- Interruptions.
- Staff seniority.
- Training quality.
- Regional staffing levels.
- Whether bilingual staff are also handling non-language cases.
- Whether accommodation specialists are overloaded because of dual roles.

This limits the ability to determine whether delays are caused by unavoidable workload or poor allocation.

## Observational inference limits

Process mining can identify patterns, but it cannot by itself prove discriminatory intent or causation. The evidence can show:

- Unequal routing.
- Unequal waiting.
- Unequal handovers.
- Residual adjusted disparities.

It cannot fully distinguish:

- Legitimate unobserved complexity.
- Caseworker discretion.
- Applicant behavior.
- Historical bias.
- Regional capacity shocks.
- Measurement error.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigation should preserve rights-preserving support while reducing avoidable burden. Support steps should not be removed merely to improve cycle time.

| Mitigation option | How it helps | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add capacity for language support** | Reduces the 3.8-day wait after Language Support Contact and reduces dependence on 7 bilingual caseworkers. | Requires hiring, training, or contracting bilingual staff. May increase cost. If new staff are less experienced, quality may initially suffer. May require redistribution of cases from other teams. |
| **Add capacity for accommodation review** | Reduces the 4.7-day wait after Accommodation Review and reduces bottleneck among 5 specialists. | Cost and training burden. Accommodation expertise is specialized; general staff may not handle complex accommodation decisions well. Need to preserve quality and legal compliance. |
| **Create separate service-level targets for support steps** | Makes language-support and accommodation-review timeliness visible and accountable. | Risk of metric gaming, such as closing support contacts prematurely or rushing complex accommodation assessments. Should be paired with quality and applicant-experience metrics. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** | Reduces general  bilingual  general and general  accommodation specialist  supervisor loops. Could reduce repeated explanations and lost context. | May require cross-training general caseworkers. Could reduce specialization if done poorly. Must ensure that language and accommodation needs are still identified and handled correctly. |
| **Improve decision explanations** | May reduce appeals and rework by making decisions clearer, especially for Group B and Group D. | Takes more time initially. Requires translation, plain language, and accessibility review. May expose decision inconsistencies and increase short-term legal scrutiny. |
| **Monitor adjusted disparities continuously** | Detects whether changes reduce residual differences in approval, cycle time, supervisor review, missing documents, and appeals. | Requires data governance, statistical expertise, and privacy protection. Risk of over-reliance on simplistic metrics. Monitoring must lead to action, not just reporting. |
| **Audit risk_score_band** | Tests whether risk classification is accurate, calibrated, and applied consistently across groups. | May reveal model problems requiring retraining or policy changes. Could reduce short-term automation efficiency. May create operational uncertainty while the model is revised. |
| **Audit documentation_completeness** | Determines whether incompleteness is applicant-caused, process-caused, or communication-caused. | May require redesigning forms, notices, and document-request workflows. Could increase upfront staff time but reduce repeated requests later. |
| **Provide proactive applicant support** | Helps applicants submit complete documents, understand requests, and access language or accommodation support earlier. | Requires outreach resources. May be difficult for hard-to-reach applicants. Could increase application volume or staff workload initially. |
| **Regional backlog relief for supervisor review** | Reduces supervisor delay in regions where Group B and Group C waits are high. | Temporary staffing shifts may affect other regions or case types. Need to avoid lowering review quality. |

A key design principle is that language support, accommodation review, and risk-based review should be timely and well-resourced. The fairness problem is not the existence of these steps, but the burden and delay associated with them.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does not appear fully procedurally fair, but the evidence does not support a definitive conclusion of intentional discrimination. Raw outcome differences are substantially associated with case mix, legitimate routing, and rights-preserving support needs. However, after controlling for documented complexity, meaningful residual disparities remain, especially for Group B and Group C.

The strongest fairness concerns are procedural rather than purely outcome-based:

- Group B experiences higher adjusted missing-document requests, higher supervisor review probability, longer cycle time, and long waits tied to language support.
- Group C experiences longer adjusted cycle time, higher supervisor review probability, and long waits in accommodation review.
- Group D experiences higher adjusted appeal probability and heavier fraud-review routing, raising concerns about the validity and calibration of `risk_score_band`.
- Rights-preserving support steps appear under-resourced, creating queue penalties for applicants using language support or disability accommodation.
- Resource concentration and handover loops for Group B and Group C suggest unequal operational access to decision-making capacity.

Some routing is likely legitimate: high measured risk can justify fraud review, accommodation requests require accommodation review, and minority-language needs require language support. But legitimacy of routing does not justify avoidable delay, repeated handovers, or insufficient capacity for support steps.

The most defensible conclusion is that the process shows **significant procedural fairness concerns**, particularly in timeliness, procedural burden, resource allocation, and appeal burden, while remaining **indeterminate on whether residual outcome disparities reflect unlawful or intentional discrimination** without further audit of risk scores, documentation measures, decision quality, appeal outcomes, and unobserved case complexity.
