# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

Management often relies on average cycle time to declare success. The following risks may persist or worsen even if the average improves.

1.  **Tail Latency:** The average may drop while the 90th/95th percentile cycle time increases significantly. High-priority or complex cases get stuck in the central queue, causing critical SLA breaches that are masked by simple cases.
2.  **High-Complexity Case Degradation:** Local experts currently handle complexity informally. Centralization formalizes this but adds queue time. The average may look stable, but the "hard" cases take 2x longer, damaging reputation with key customers.
3.  **Local Knowledge Loss:** As `local_expertise_flag` cases are routed away, local staff lose the ability to handle exceptions. This risk is invisible in cycle time until a crisis occurs where no one knows the regional convention.
4.  **Fairness Across Segments:** `customer_segment` data may reveal that high-value clients suffer longer waits due to stricter routing rules in the shared queue, while low-value clients move faster. Average time hides this stratification.
5.  **Learning and Skill Atrophy:** Junior staff no longer see complex cases (`pm4py.get_variants` shows reduced variety for local resources). This creates a future capability gap not visible in current performance metrics.
6.  **Accountability Vacuum:** In a shared approval queue, `outcome` attribution becomes diffuse. If a case fails, it is unclear if the fault lies with the local intake or the shared approver, leading to "pass the buck" behavior that increases rework over time.

# 5. Pilot design

A full rollout is too risky. A controlled pilot allows for measurement of the mechanisms identified above.

*   **Pilot Scope:** Centralize **Technical Specialists only** for **Team East** (highest utilization, most at risk of burnout). Keep North and South as control groups with embedded specialists.
*   **Comparison Group:** Team North and Team South (Current State).
*   **Pre/Post Observation Window:** 3 months pre-change, 3 months post-change.
*   **Segmentation:**
    *   **Team:** East (Pilot) vs. North/South (Control).
    *   **Complexity:** Low vs. High (`case_complexity` attribute).
    *   **Specialty:** Tech vs. Legal/Risk (Legal/Risk remain local/shared as per current).
    *   **Customer Segment:** Standard vs. Premium.
*   **Guardrail Metrics:**
    *   Max wait time for High Priority cases < 48h.
    *   Rework rate increase < 5%.
    *   Specialist utilization < 90% (to avoid bottleneck).
*   **Staff Feedback Collection:** Weekly surveys on "Role Clarity" and "Ability to get informal advice."
*   **Rollback or Adjustment Triggers:**
    *   If `rework_flag` increases >10% in East vs Control.
    *   If `SLA miss rate` for High Priority cases increases >2%.
    *   If `org:resource` similarity drops significantly (indicating role confusion).

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: Pilot First (Conditional Proceed)**

Do not proceed with a full organizational rollout. The current data indicates that while East is overloaded, North and South have stable, high-performing local loops. Centralizing across all teams risks destroying the resilience and local context that currently prevents high rework rates.

**Conditions for Proceeding:**
1.  **Execute the Pilot:** Run the East-only pilot described in Section 5.
2.  **Monitor Tail Metrics:** Do not approve full rollout based on average cycle time. Approval requires evidence that the 90th percentile wait time does not degrade for high-complexity cases.
3.  **Validate Fairness:** Ensure `customer_segment` satisfaction does not diverge negatively between East and North/South.
4.  **Check Learning:** Verify that local staff in the pilot are not losing `local_expertise_flag` relevance (i.e., they still handle appropriate complexity).

**What Management Should Not Infer:**
Management should not infer that "higher utilization equals efficiency." If utilization rises to 95%+, the queue becomes unstable (Little's Law), and small disruptions cause massive delays. High utilization without slack reduces resilience to absence or spikes in volume. Early averages often hide the initial "learning curve" dip in quality that will manifest later as rework.
