## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Fiber installation leadership
**Subject:** Split Redesign R — keep the simple-house path, stop survey-skipping and billing-before-activation
**Date:** 22 September 2026

**Recommendation:** Choose alternative E, tightened. Pause survey-skipping and post-billing activation in every segment. Keep coordinator ownership and merged validation-and-scheduling for simple, low-risk houses, with a named deputy. Hold apartments, old buildings, business, and rural cases on a controlled path. Measure the separated components before any wider rollout. No part of R improves every metric that matters, so the bundle should not travel as one decision.

The center of the distribution improved in a harder housing mix. Across 9,200 cases in six weeks, median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%, and cost before complaint handling fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161. The four pilot regions contain more apartments and older buildings than the 24,000-case baseline. Simple, low-risk houses, 34% of pilot volume, improved on the median (−6 days), the 90th percentile (−8 days), first-time-right (+3 points), and complaints (−1 point). Those comparisons come from a bundled, non-randomized six-week pilot against a ten-week baseline, and segment attributes are missing before week 3.

The tail, rework, and conformance picture deteriorated. The 90th percentile rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and the 95th from 45.0 to 62.0. First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, second visits rose from 11% to 18%, manual activation from 7% to 15%, 30-day complaints from 6.1% to 9.8%, SLA breach from 13% to 16%, and NPS from +21 to +17. Apartments (29%) and permit-sensitive old buildings (18%) show the large deteriorations: 90th percentile +12 and +25 days, first-time-right −8 and −11 points. Business and rural cases also lost first-time-right. Fitness rose from 0.87 to 0.94 while precision fell from 0.79 to 0.51 and generalization from 0.70 to 0.56. The pilot log is easier to replay and harder to bound: skipped survey, a repeated installation, manual activation, and billing before a successful activation test combine too freely. Alignments flag those deviations, including permit checks missing on older apartments. On the directly-follows graph, installation→activation fell from 78% to 60%, installation→manual activation rose from 8% to 19%, failed visit and second visit both rose, and billing→complaint rose from 3% to 8%. Failed-visit to second-visit delay grew where coordinator utilization reached 93%. Survey utilization fell from 78% to 52%; second-visit utilization rose from 66% to 92%. The handover network became simpler; the working-together network tightened around coordinators, and cases stalled when a coordinator was absent.

EUR 161 is the visible installation ledger. The partial estimate that includes complaint and revisit handling is EUR 207 against a baseline of EUR 198, and it still omits complaint cost after week 4. Fewer handovers increased single-point accountability and reduced shared context when that person was absent or fully loaded.

**Trade-off.** Scaling the whole bundle would keep the median gain and spread the longer tail, early billing, and a low-precision process. A full rollback would restore permit and activation control and would also surrender a gain already visible on about a third of volume. Splitting R keeps the supported gain, stops the two changes tied to the worst alignments, and spends one more cycle on identification. Two paths add operating complexity. One untested national model puts the cost on apartment and old-building customers.

**Next step.** In the pilot regions, suspend the predictive survey skip and require a successful activation test before billing start. Restore the permit check for old and apartment buildings. Run coordinator ownership plus merged scheduling only for simple, low-risk houses, with a deputy owner. Add no regions this cycle. Reopen scope only when, for every in-scope segment, the 90th percentile and first-time-right are at least at baseline, billing-before-activation and missing permit checks are closed, and 60-day fully loaded cost is at or below baseline.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

The conflicts are expected once a bundled redesign meets a mixed housing stock, a truncated cost boundary, and a more permissive process. They are not a reason to discard the mining results.

**High fitness with low precision.** Fitness asks whether the model can replay what happened. Pilot fitness of 0.94 means almost all observed pilot cases fit, including the new coordinator path and the merged validate/schedule task (38% of pilot flow). Precision asks whether the model also allows behavior that did not happen. Precision of 0.51 means the discovered model is permissive: skipped survey, repeated installation, manual activation, and billing start can combine in many ways the log did not fully exhibit. That is why fitness can rise while the model becomes a worse description of allowed behavior. Generalization fell as well, from 0.70 to 0.56. A cleaner, simpler process would ordinarily be more precise. The joint drop means discovery opened many exception branches to cover this sample, and those branches are a weak guide to the next case. The alignments make the extra behavior concrete. Moves on the log show billing before a successful activation test, a repeat installation with no field survey, and complaint escalation after billing. The move on the model shows a permit check that the model expects and the log omits for older apartments. The directly-follows frequencies move with those alignments: the standard installation→activation edge loses share, and the manual, failure, second-visit, and billing→complaint edges gain share.

**Better median, worse tail.** The median is the middle case. Simple houses are 34% of the pilot, 6 days faster at the median, and 8 days faster at their own 90th percentile, which is enough to pull the overall median from 14.2 to 10.8 days. The 90th and 95th percentiles are the slow minority. Apartments, old buildings, business, and rural cases all have a higher segment p90 (+12, +25, +5, and +9 days). Overall p95 moving from 45 to 62 days is consistent with permit delay, a late second visit, complaint-after-billing, and queues behind coordinators at 93% utilization. The temporal profile matches this split: address validation→appointment improved for simple houses, while installation→activation worsened for apartments and billing→complaint appeared as a new long-tail relation. Mix cuts both ways. The pilot’s heavier apartment and old-building share would, by itself, make the aggregate tail look worse and the aggregate median harder to improve. The median improvement is therefore the stronger aggregate signal. The within-segment p90 split is the stronger tail signal, with the limit that risk attributes are missing before week 3.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** Handover count measures transfers between people. Resilience measures whether the case continues when one person is absent or one queue is full. Coordinator ownership cut cases with three or more handovers from 34% to 12% and simplified the handover-of-work network. The same design centralized the working-together network on coordinators and activation specialists. Context that used to sit with several teams sat with one role. At 93% utilization in two regions there is little slack, and reported absence stalled cases because that context was not shared. The longer failed-visit→second-visit delay in high-utilization regions is the queueing trace of that concentration. Fewer handovers can reduce waiting on the standard path and, at the same time, remove the backup that absorbed shocks.

**Lower visible cost, higher rework and downstream cost.** The two cost figures draw the boundary in different places. EUR 184→161 stops before complaint handling and records the activities R removed: fewer handovers, and field survey down from a 31% to an 18% edge, with survey-team utilization 78%→52%. Rework sits on the other side of that boundary. Second visits 11%→18%, manual activation 7%→15%, and second-visit utilization 66%→92% are extra work, and 30-day complaints nearly rose by half (6.1%→9.8%). Adding the incomplete complaint and revisit estimate already flips the result, EUR 198→207, and that estimate drops complaint cost after week 4. A lower visible unit cost is compatible with a higher fully loaded cost when the saved activity causes a later, excluded activity. The complaint-theme shift, from appointment waiting to a non-working install and billing before activation, points at that later activity.

**Simple houses improve while apartments and old buildings deteriorate.** The helpful mechanisms and the harmful ones hit different buildings. A single coordinator and a merged schedule shorten a path that often needs neither a survey nor a permit. The predictive skip was applied to some medium-risk addresses, and apartments in that band show worse first-time-right and a longer tail. Older apartments show a permit check absent where the model expects one, and their p90 is the worst movement in the table (+25 days). Post-billing activation and manual activation line up with business billing disputes and with the new billing→complaint relation. Simple houses are the only reported segment that improved on median, p90, first-time-right, and complaints together. The other four segments improved modestly, or not at all, on the median and deteriorated on the tail and on first-time-right. An average of those segments will show a better median and a worse tail at the same time. Because the four changes were launched together, that pattern is strong guidance and incomplete proof of which single change produced which cell.

## 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
Advantages: takes the median reduction, the handover reduction, and the visible-cost reduction into the whole base in one operating model; simple houses are a large class and their gains are broad.
Risks: also scales the p90/p95 deterioration, the loss of first-time-right, the rise in second visits and manual activation, billing before a successful activation test, missing permit checks, complaint growth, the NPS dip, and coordinator load at 93%. The pilot model’s precision of 0.51 would become the national control problem. Regions in the pilot were already richer in the harmed building types, the design was not randomized, and full cost is incomplete.
Reasonable only after the gates in section 6 are met for every segment included in the national scope, with early billing and missing permits closed. On the evidence now, A is not a defensible next step.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process.**
Advantages: restores survey, permit, and activation-before-billing behavior that the alignments show is breaking; likely to ease second-visit and manual-activation load; avoids institutionalizing a single-owner design that stalls on absence.
Risks: gives up the simple-house gains on speed, tail, first-time-right, and complaints, and restores handover delay that previously showed up as waiting for an appointment. The worsened aggregate metrics are partly the harmed segments, so a total rollback treats a localized failure as a total failure.
Reasonable if fully loaded 60-day cost and complaints on simple houses are worse than baseline once early billing is isolated, if two operating paths cannot be run safely, or if permit and billing-sequence exposure is unacceptable for even one more cycle. A rollback should leave an explicit route back to coordinator ownership for simple houses, because that is the strongest positive pattern in the data.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
Advantages: keeps the accountability change most consistent with fewer handovers, and removes the two changes most consistent with the bad alignments and the rework edges. One ownership model is easier to manage than a segment split.
Risks: ownership at 93% utilization, with stalls on absence, is not yet shown to be safe nationally. Merged scheduling, which C as stated does not scale, is part of the simple-house timing result and would be left behind. Hard segments can remain slow if the permit check is not explicitly restored. National ownership before an absence test repeats the resilience failure at larger volume.
Reasonable if deputies are funded so absence no longer stalls the case, survey policy and activation-before-billing return for every segment, permit checks are mandatory on permit-sensitive stock, and merged scheduling for simple houses is included with ownership. C is a credible later national target once hard segments have recovered. It is early as this cycle’s decision.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment and risk measurement.**
Advantages: the identification gaps are real — bundled changes, non-random regions, a harder housing mix, attributes missing before week 3, cost cut off after week 4, and sparse start times. A stratified experiment is the honest way to attribute effects. It also matches a low-precision model: more measurement, tighter controls.
Risks: if the full current bundle keeps running, customers continue to receive billing before activation and buildings continue to skip an expected permit check, in order to study deviations that are already frequent. Delay has a cost for simple houses whose gains are already visible.
Reasonable as the measurement system attached to a controlled operating decision. Weak as a standalone choice that leaves survey skipping and post-billing activation in place.

**E. Hybrid: the beneficial path only for simple low-risk houses, controls for apartments and old buildings, measurement before wider scale.**
Advantages: matches the only segment that improved on median, own p90, first-time-right, and complaints. Stops the flows the alignments and the directly-follows graph flag. Leaves business and rural cases off the loosened path. Preserves the option to widen, narrow, or restore components after M1–M6. Accepts that the current evidence cannot crown a national winner.
Risks: two paths add classification error, especially while the risk rule is unvalidated and attributes were incomplete early in the pilot. A mislabeled apartment on the simple path recreates the harm. Coordinators can still overload on simple-house volume. Boundary cases are easy to drift into the loose path.
Reasonable now, with the tightening that post-billing activation stays off even for simple houses until it is tested on its own. E is the alternative that uses the segment split without pretending the bundle has been identified.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by ability to change the choice among A–E, not by ease.

**1. M1 — Randomized survey skip by building-risk stratum.**
Resolves the causal effect of the skip, separated from ownership, the task merge, regional mix, and the billing change. A clear loss in medium-risk apartments, and a neutral result on low-risk houses, locks the suspension inside E. A loss in every stratum removes skip from any future design. A neutral or better result on first-time-right and p90 inside a pre-registered band reopens a narrow skip and moves the later decision toward a modified C or a wider E. Run it only where M6 shows the rule is actually in doubt, and keep permit-sensitive buildings out of the skip arm.

**2. M6 — Validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes.**
Resolves whether “medium risk” distinguishes addresses that truly need a survey. Poor agreement with survey outcomes makes any skip policy indefensible and can shrink or cancel M1 in the bad strata. Strong agreement focuses M1 on the borderline band and could later add volume back to a controlled skip. It should start immediately, in parallel with M1’s design. It cannot replace M1: surveyed cases are a selected set, so agreement on historical surveys is not the effect of skipping.

**3. M4 — Fully loaded cost for 60 days after billing start, by segment.**
Resolves the sign of the economic result that EUR 161 and EUR 207 currently contradict, including the complaint cost missing after week 4. If simple houses are cheaper than their baseline on a 60-day basis and hard segments are more expensive, E’s financial split is confirmed. If simple houses are more expensive once revisits and complaints are counted, the case for keeping even that path fails and B, or a much smaller C, becomes reasonable. If every segment is cheaper after the controls, widening later becomes easier to justify. This is the measurement most likely to reverse the “keep simple houses” leg.

**4. M3 — Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint escalation, and second visit.**
Resolves which mechanism dominates the rework. Codes concentrated on “survey skipped” or “risk class wrong” confirm the skip suspension. Codes concentrated on permit delay confirm the permit gate for old buildings. Codes concentrated on “signal looked acceptable, service did not work” confirm the billing pause. Codes concentrated on customer access or coordinator queue point at scheduling and capacity, which would make a pure skip suspension incomplete and would raise the priority of M5. Reason codes are what stop the organization from pausing the wrong lever inside the bundle.

**5. M5 — Coordinator ownership versus shared-team ownership when the coordinator is absent.**
Resolves whether the handover reduction caused the resilience loss. A large stall on absence means E’s simple-house path needs a staffed deputy before any added region, and extending ownership to rural or business cases is unattractive. A small stall means ownership can broaden once quality gates are met, which is the condition under which C becomes the national target. M5 does not, by itself, justify restoring survey skip or early billing.

**6. M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing before a successful activation test, and for permit-check absence.**
Resolves whether those two deviations stay frequent after the pause, and whether they still precede the complaint tail. The deviations are already common, so M7 is less likely than M1, M4, or M3 to change today’s choice among A–E. It is the gate on any later widening: residual early billing or missing permits blocks rollout. Instrument it now; it is a control as well as a measurement, and it directly watches the alignment findings.

**7. M8 — Customer experience split into speed to appointment versus working service after installation.**
Resolves which experience dimension produced the NPS move from +21 to +17 and the theme shift. If speed rose and working service fell, median cycle time is the wrong success metric for a scale decision and the complaint increase is the binding customer signal. That result would rarely change E’s operating split. It would change the threshold management uses, and it would block a later argument for A based on speed alone.

**8. M2 — Start timestamps for installation, activation test, and manual activation.**
Resolves how much of cycle time is queue versus work, which completion-only timestamps cannot separate. A large coordinator queue would push hiring and the deputy design; a long manual-activation duration would push the activation fix. Useful for the capacity plan, and necessary before fine-grained temporal claims are trusted. Least likely of the eight to choose among A–E, because the segment pattern and the conformance deviations are already strong enough to pause skip and early billing.

Commission M6, M3, M4, M7, and M8 immediately. They do not require exposing more customers to a skipped survey. Use M6 to draw the strata for M1. Run M5 before any geographic expansion of ownership. Schedule M2 as infrastructure for the next model, not as a reason to wait on the pauses above.

## 6. Final recommendation

Adopt tightened alternative E. The pilot earned a limited keep, not a national model and not a full reversal.

**Immediate action.** In the current pilot regions, operate two paths from the next scheduling cycle. Simple, low-risk houses: one coordinator owns the case after address validation, with a named deputy, and validation and scheduling stay merged. Every other segment: controlled path, with field survey where the pre-pilot policy required it, a permit check on old and apartment buildings, and a successful activation test before billing start. Publish the segment rule in writing so boundary cases have an owner.

**Pause.** Pause the predictive survey-skip rule for all risk bands. Pause any activation check that is allowed to happen after billing start, including on simple houses. Pause geographic expansion. Pause any reading of EUR 161 as the economic result.

**Scale.** Scale, inside the pilot regions only, coordinator ownership and merged validate-and-schedule for simple, low-risk houses, and only with deputy coverage. Do not scale survey skipping, post-billing activation, or a single national variant of R. Leave business, rural, apartment, and old-building cases on the controlled path.

**Measure next, in this order.** Start M7 conformance monitoring, M6 rule validation, M3 reason codes, M4 60-day fully loaded cost by segment, and M8 split experience measures now. Design M1 as a randomized skip test only in strata where M6 is inconclusive, excluding permit-sensitive stock. Run M5 before adding regions or extending ownership. Add M2 start timestamps so the next cycle time is interpretable.

**Evidence that would justify broader rollout.** All of the following, for each segment proposed for the wider design:

- Segment p90 cycle time at or below that segment’s baseline, and segment median no worse than baseline.
- First-time-right and 30-day complaints at least at that segment’s baseline.
- Billing start before a successful activation test no longer a routine path, at or below the baseline rate of that deviation and aimed at zero.
- Permit-check completion on permit-sensitive stock at the baseline or the required rate.
- Sixty-day fully loaded cost, from M4, at or below the comparable baseline for that segment, with complaint cost included past week 4.
- An absence test, from M5, in which a coordinator’s absence does not recreate the failed-visit→second-visit delay seen at 93% utilization.
- Survey skipping restored only in strata where M1 shows first-time-right and p90 no worse than the surveyed control.

Until those gates are met, the decision stays a contained simple-house path plus controls, not alternative A and not alternative B.
