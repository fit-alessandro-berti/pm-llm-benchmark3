# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Time origin is the `Submit` event, taken as \(t = 0\) hours for every case. The basic time scale is elapsed hours since `Submit`, and the analysis horizon for SLA purposes is \(t^* = 72\) hours. For each case, `duration_or_age_h` is interpreted as the observed follow-up time: for terminal statuses (`resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, `transferred`) it is the elapsed time from `Submit` to that terminal outcome; for `open` cases it is the age of the case at the common observation cutoff. `last_event_gap_h` is the time between the last recorded internal event in the prefix and the terminal outcome or observation cutoff.

The main internal success event is `Resolve`. Other internal terminal outcomes (`Withdraw`, `Reject`) and hand-off to an external organization (`Transfer`) are treated as distinct terminal states. Open cases at cutoff are not terminal: they are right-censored for any further internal or external outcomes, but for some estimands they already represent observed SLA breaches (for example, an `open` case at 92 hours is known to have missed the 72‑hour SLA).

I define three concrete “probability of breaching the SLA” estimands:

1. **Estimand E1: probability that a case is *not resolved* by 72 hours**  
   \(E1 = P(T_{\text{resolve}} > 72 \text{ or no resolution before 72})\).  
   Operationally this is the probability that the internal case has *not* reached `Resolve` by 72 hours, regardless of whether it is later resolved, withdrawn, rejected, transferred, or still open. Statistically, a convenient way to estimate \(1-E1\) is as the **cumulative incidence of internal resolution by 72 hours** in a competing-risks framework where `resolved` is the event of interest and `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and `transferred` are competing events, with `open` cases right-censored at their observed ages. Then \(E1 = 1 - \text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72^-)\).

2. **Estimand E2: tail risk among cases that do resolve – probability of *late* resolution**  
   \(E2 = P(T_{\text{resolve}} > 72 \mid \text{eventually resolved})\).  
   This is the conditional probability that, among cases that achieve `Resolve` at some point, their resolution occurs after 72 hours. It is directly relevant for assessing whether resolution times among successful cases are acceptable, but it ignores cases that never resolve or exit via competing events.

3. **Estimand E3: probability that the case is *still open* at 72 hours**  
   \(E3 = P(\text{no terminal disposition by } 72\text{h})\).  
   Here the event of interest is being in the `open` state at the 72‑hour horizon, meaning no internal resolution, withdrawal, rejection, or transfer has occurred yet. This is essentially the point prevalence of active workload beyond the SLA, which drives queue length and staffing.

For all three estimands, `open` cases and transfers must be treated carefully as potential right-censoring or competing events, not simply dropped. The contributions of the four illustrative cases are:

- **Case resolved at 76 hours (e.g. S03/S13 pattern)**  
  - Under **E1** (not resolved by 72h): this case has an internal `Resolve` event at \(T_{\text{resolve}} = 76\). In the CIF for resolution, it contributes an event at 76 hours. When we evaluate the cumulative incidence at 72 hours, this case is *still unresolved* at that horizon, so it contributes to the estimated **probability of breaching the 72‑hour SLA** (it is counted in \(E1\)).  
  - Under **E2** (late resolution among resolved): this case is included in the denominator (it resolves) and in the numerator (resolution time \(> 72\)). It is an observed late-resolution breach.  
  - Under **E3** (open at 72h): until 76 hours the case remains internally open. At 72 hours it is still active and unresolved, so it contributes to the event “open at 72h”. In a survival formulation where the event is “no terminal disposition yet at 72h”, this case has an event at time 72.

- **Case open at 92 hours (e.g. S09)**  
  - Under **E1**: the case has not resolved and has no internal terminal disposition by 92 hours, so it is *known* to have been unresolved at 72 hours. For the CIF of resolution, it is a right-censored observation at 92 hours with no resolution event; such a case contributes to \(E1\) as an observed SLA breach (since it survives without resolution beyond 72h).  
  - Under **E2**: the case never resolves within the observed window, so it is excluded from the conditional risk set of “cases that eventually resolve” or treated as right-censored in time-to-resolution models used to estimate \(E2\). It provides information that some cases never resolve, but not a realized resolution time.  
  - Under **E3**: because it is still open at 92 hours, it is definitely open at 72 hours, so it contributes an `open-at-72h` event at time 72. After 72 hours it remains in the open state but is administratively censored at 92 hours.

- **Case withdrawn at 27 hours (e.g. S06/S12/S22/S30)**  
  - Under **E1**: the case never reaches internal resolution and exits via `Withdraw` at time 27. From the standpoint of “not resolved by 72h”, this is an *SLA breach* with a known time at which success became impossible. In a competing-risks setup for internal resolution, it is a competing event at 27 hours; when we transform the CIF for resolution into \(E1\), this case contributes to the breach probability by removing probability mass that could otherwise resolve by 72h.  
  - Under **E2**: since the case never resolves, it is not in the target population of E2 and is handled as a competing event or excluded from the conditional analysis.  
  - Under **E3**: the case is open from 0 to 27 hours and closed thereafter. It is *not* open at 72 hours, so it contributes a non-event for E3 and leaves the risk set at time 27 in the “time to any terminal outcome” analysis.

- **Case transferred at 68 hours (e.g. S08)**  
  This is the most ambiguous: after transfer the internal system stops observing the case, and we do not know whether the external organization resolves it before or after 72 hours. We therefore consider it under three transfer-treatment scenarios:
  1. **Transfers as competing terminal outcome (baseline)**: treat `Transfer` as a competing risk representing “handed off / unresolved internally”.  
     - Under **E1**, this case is *very likely* an SLA breach for internal resolution, because it has not resolved internally by 68 hours and is no longer under internal control. We count it as contributing to \(E1\) (via the complement of CIF for internal resolution).  
     - Under **E2**, it never resolves internally, so it is excluded or treated as a competing event; its eventual external resolution time is unknown.  
     - Under **E3**, the case is open from 0 to 68 hours and not open at 72 hours (because it left the system before then), so it is a non-event for “open at 72h”.
  2. **Transfers as independent administrative censoring**: assume transfer simply truncates internal observation but does not change the underlying resolution process.  
     - Under **E1**, the case is censored at 68 hours with unknown breach status; it no longer contributes beyond 68h. This generally *reduces* the estimated breach probability compared with treating transfers as failures.  
     - Under **E2**, it is right-censored at 68 hours among the “time to resolution” data.  
     - Under **E3**, because the case is known not to be open at 72 hours (it left at 68), it is a non-event.
  3. **Best- and worst-case external-resolution bounds**: assume all pre-72h transfers resolve externally before 72h (best case) or that all transfers ultimately breach the SLA (worst case).  
     - Under **E1**, S08-type cases are counted as non-breaches in the best case and as certain breaches (event at 68h) in the worst case; this yields an interval for \(E1\).  
     - Under **E2**, we may either exclude them (best case) or include them as “late external resolutions” with unknown times (worst case), acknowledging that either extreme may distort E2.  
     - Under **E3**, they remain non-events because they have left the open internal workload by 72h, but the number of cases contributing to the open-at-72h risk set is unchanged.

In summary, **E1** focuses on failure to achieve `Resolve` by 72 hours, aggregating across competing dispositions; **E2** conditions on eventual resolution and quantifies how often that happens late; **E3** measures the burden of cases still open at the SLA horizon. All three depend on careful handling of open cases, competing events, and transfers.

The key distinctions are:

- **Right censoring**: e.g. `open` cases below 72 hours, or transfers when treated as censoring. These cases are still at risk, and their future is unknown.
- **Competing events**: `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and `transferred` (under the competing-risk assumption) are genuine alternative outcomes that preclude internal resolution.
- **Already-observed breaches**: cases that are open beyond 72 hours or that have a terminal disposition before 72 hours that is not `Resolve` (withdrawn/rejected; and transfers under some assumptions) are already known to have breached the resolution SLA at or before 72 hours, even though they may be right-censored for later outcomes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive “completed-case” analysis that keeps only cases with observed `Resolve` (S01–S04, S07, S13, S16–S20, S27, S28) and treats their `duration_or_age_h` as time to resolution will be severely biased.

- **Survivor bias and informative censoring from open cases**  
  Consider S09 (cohort A, P3), which is open at 92 hours, and S26 (cohort B, P3), which is open at 104 hours with a `last_event_gap_h` of 72 hours. These cases have extremely long ages and long periods of inactivity, indicating that hard or neglected cases tend to remain open and unobserved at the cutoff. If we drop S09 and S26, we remove precisely the worst performers, making average resolution times appear shorter and the tail of the distribution appear lighter than it truly is. Similarly, S11 (open at 81h), S25 (P1, open at 79h), and S24 (open at 67h) are high‑risk tails that vanish under a completed-case approach.

- **Competing risks misclassified as censoring or ignored**  
  Cases such as S06 (withdrawn at 27h), S12 (withdrawn at 15h), S22 (withdrawn at 44h), S30 (withdrawn at 70h), S05 (rejected at 9h), S14 (rejected at 63h), and S23 (rejected at 11h) are genuine outcomes where the SLA for resolution is effectively failed (no internal resolution is ever reached). Treating them as if they were independent right-censoring at those times—common in naive KM analyses that only focus on “time to resolution” and censor everything else—implicitly assumes that if they had not been withdrawn or rejected, their resolution times would have been similar to the resolved cases. That is unlikely: for example, S30 and S14 are fairly long-running before being closed negatively, so they probably represent difficult or problematic cases.

- **Informative and policy-driven transfers**  
  Transfers such as S08 (P1, 68h), S21 (P1, 26h), and S32 (P1, 96h) are particularly suspect as independent censoring. P1 cases are likely high-risk or complex; escalation and transfer may be triggered *because* the internal team anticipates SLA breach or difficulty, not randomly. Dropping transferred cases or censoring them as if independent will make internal resolution appear faster and less risky than it is, especially if cohort B uses transfers differently than cohort A.

- **Cohort imbalance and confounding**  
  Cohort B began after an automated-screening and escalation-policy change. In the data, cohort B has more prefixes including `AutoCheck`, and several P1 cases (S21, S25, S29, S32) show early escalation and transfer or long open tails. If we analyze only resolved cases and pool cohorts, we might conclude that cohort B has similar or better resolution times (e.g. S16, S18, S19, S27 are relatively fast). However, this ignores that cohort B may also be routing the hardest P1 cases to transfer (S21, S32) or leaving them open longer (S25). The non-random policy change and incomplete rollout mean the two cohorts have different case mixes and decision rules; naive pooling or naive cohort comparisons will mix policy effects with selection effects.

- **Difference between open cases below vs above 72 hours**  
  Open cases like S10 (47h), S15 (35h), S24 (67h), S29 (51h), and S31 (23h) are still within the SLA window; they are at risk but not yet breaching. Open cases like S09 (92h), S11 (81h), S25 (79h), and S26 (104h) have *already* breached the SLA. Treating all open cases simply as right-censored at their ages, without distinguishing whether they are below or above 72 hours, conflates these very different situations and can understate how many cases have already violated the SLA at the cutoff.

Overall, restricting attention to resolved cases and treating everything else as uninformative censoring systematically understates tail risk and mischaracterizes both process performance and cohort effects.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

We compare standard survival and competing-risk tools and select approaches that fit this process and its data structure.

- **Kaplan–Meier (KM)**  
  KM is appropriate when modeling time to a single failure type with non-informative censoring. A naive KM on “time to resolution” with withdrawals, rejections, transfers, and open cases censored would be badly biased here because those alternatives are informative competing outcomes. However, KM *is* reasonable for modeling **time to any internal terminal outcome** (first of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`) when we wish to estimate the probability that a case is still internally open at time \(t\). In that setup, open cases at cutoff are right-censored, and terminal events are all treated as failures of the “still open” state. KM could support estimand E3 by providing \(S_{\text{open}}(72) = P(\text{still open at }72\text{h})\), recognizing that censoring may still be informative due to transfers.

- **Aalen–Johansen and cumulative-incidence functions (CIFs)**  
  For estimand E1 (probability of not being resolved by 72h) and for cause-specific SLA questions, **cumulative-incidence estimation in a competing-risks framework is primary**. We treat `resolved` as the event of interest and `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and (under baseline) `transferred` as competing events; `open` cases are right-censored. The Aalen–Johansen estimator yields \(\text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(t)\) and CIFs for other terminal outcomes, giving process-aware probabilities such as “probability of being resolved by 72h” and “probability of being withdrawn by 72h”. From these, we derive \(E1 = 1 - \text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72^-)\). This approach correctly accounts for the fact that withdrawal and transfer are not simple censoring but alternate states.

- **Cause-specific hazards (e.g. Cox models)**  
  To understand how covariates such as cohort, priority, current stage, escalation, rework count, and inactivity gaps influence the *instantaneous* risk of different outcomes, we use **cause-specific hazard models**. For example, a Cox model for the hazard of internal resolution treats withdrawal/rejection/transfer as censoring for that model, while a separate model for the hazard of withdrawal treats resolution and transfer as censoring. With time-varying covariates derived from prefixes (e.g. “currently escalated”, “number of reviews so far”) we can estimate how being escalated or undergoing rework changes the hazard of timely resolution versus negative outcomes, without directly conflating these with baseline risk.

- **Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray)**  
  Fine–Gray models the subdistribution hazard associated with a specific CIF, directly modeling how covariates shift cumulative incidence of resolution (or of a negative outcome) over time. For estimand E1, a Fine–Gray model for `resolved` could estimate how cohort B vs A, and priority tiers, change the probability of resolution by 72h in the presence of competing withdrawals, rejections, and transfers. However, subdistribution hazards are harder to interpret operationally than cause-specific hazards, and in small samples they can be unstable. For these data, Fine–Gray could be a **secondary modeling choice** for quantifying covariate effects on CIFs after exploratory Aalen–Johansen estimation.

- **Restricted mean time and restricted mean time lost (RMST/RMTL)**  
  RMST up to 72 hours provides an interpretable summary: expected time still unresolved (or expected time still open) within the SLA window. Comparing RMST between cohorts A and B or between priority groups can highlight whether policy changes actually reduce expected unresolved time, even if medians are similar. RMTL (restricted mean time lost to SLA breach) could express, for example, how many hours beyond 72 a typical breached case remains unresolved, again conditionally on not exiting via competing fates.

- **Landmarking**  
  Many predictors (e.g. escalation, rework, inactivity gaps) occur after case start and must not be used as baseline covariates. Landmarking addresses this: we choose one or more landmark times (e.g. 24h, 48h) and construct prediction datasets containing only cases that are still under internal management at the landmark with covariates reflecting their history up to that time (number of reviews, whether escalated yet, current stage, last-event gap). For each landmark, we estimate the **conditional probability of SLA breach after the landmark** (e.g. not resolved by 72h given survival to 24h) using KM, CIF, or Cox/Fine–Gray models. This yields dynamic, time-updated risk predictions aligned with operational decision points.

- **Multi-state model**  
  A multi-state model explicitly represents the process path: states such as `Submitted`, `Screened`, `UnderReview`, `Escalated`, `Rework`, `Resolved`, `Withdrawn`, `Rejected`, `Transferred`, and an absorbing `Censored` state at cutoff. Transitions between states are modeled over continuous time, potentially with covariates, yielding transition probabilities like “probability of reaching `Resolve` before `Withdraw` given current state and covariates”. This is highly compatible with the prefixes and is the natural framework to integrate survival analysis with process-mining insights.

Given the richness of competing outcomes and time-varying process features, my recommendations are:

- **Primary analysis**:  
  - Use an **Aalen–Johansen multi-state / competing-risks framework** to estimate CIFs for `resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and `transferred`, stratified by cohort and priority.  
  - From these CIFs, derive \(E1\) and cause-specific SLA metrics (e.g. probability of externalization via transfer by 72h).  
  - Use KM or Aalen–Johansen on the “open vs any terminal” multi-state view to estimate \(E3 = P(\text{open at }72\text{h})\).

- **Secondary analyses**:  
  - Fit **cause-specific Cox models** with process-aware, time-varying covariates to explain differences in hazards between cohorts and priorities.  
  - Optionally use **Fine–Gray** models for CIFs of resolution and of negative outcomes (withdraw/reject/transfer) as a complementary view, and compute **RMST** up to 72h for policy comparison.  
  - Apply **landmarking** at, e.g., 24 and 48 hours to provide actionable predictions that can drive escalations and workload balancing.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The `prefix` field encodes the activity sequence for each case. From this we can derive several types of features:

- **Baseline features (safe as time-zero covariates)**  
  - `cohort` (A/B): defined at or before submission, representing pre‑ vs post‑policy era.  
  - `priority` (P1–P3): defined at intake; higher P1 priority indicates urgency.  
  - Any static case attributes not shown here (e.g. product, channel, or customer segment) would also belong here.

- **Time-varying process features (must be indexed by elapsed time)**  
  Derived from prefixes and `last_event_gap_h`, these should be updated over time, for example at each event or at fixed landmark times:  
  - **Current stage**: the last activity in the prefix (e.g. `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`). At 30 hours, S03 might still be at `Review` before its escalation.  
  - **Escalation status and timing**: indicator of having passed through `Escalate` at or before time \(t\), time since first escalation, and whether escalation currently holds (in the last observed activity). For instance, S04 (`Submit>Screen>Escalate>Resolve`) has an early escalation followed quickly by resolution, whereas S03 escalates only after an initial review.  
  - **Rework count so far**: cumulative number of `Rework` cycles at time \(t\) (we have `rework_count` at cutoff, but in a full event log this would be time-varying). S07 and S13, S27 have `Rework>Review` loops indicating complexity.  
  - **Number of reviews to date**: count of `Review` steps, capturing indecision or complexity (e.g. S03 and S13 have multiple `Review` steps).  
  - **Inactivity duration**: rolling version of `last_event_gap_h`, i.e., time since last event at each time point. Large gaps (e.g. S26 with gap 72h) suggest neglect or waiting on external input and likely predict SLA breach.  
  - **Path pattern indicators**: whether the path has gone through `AutoCheck` before manual `Screen`/`Review`, whether escalation occurs before or after first review, whether the path contains loops (`Review>Rework>Review`). These can be encoded as dummy variables or as variant IDs.

- **Post-treatment or leakage-prone features (should not be used as baseline)**  
  Some features can only be known by looking at the *entire* prefix up to resolution or cutoff; using them as baseline covariates would cause immortal-time bias or label leakage:  
  - “Ever escalated” as a binary variable ignoring the time of escalation: this can only be known after follow-up and would give escalated cases an artificial survival advantage because they must survive long enough to be escalated.  
  - “Total number of reviews” or “ever reworked” without distinguishing when they occur.  
  - “Longest inactivity gap” computed over the full case.  
  These should instead be modeled as time-varying summaries (e.g. number of reviews so far at time \(t\)) or used only in retrospective descriptive analysis, not in predictive models.

A **multi-state representation** consistent with the prefixes could define the following states:

- S0: Submitted  
- S1: AutoChecked  
- S2: Screened (manual)  
- S3: Under Review  
- S4: In Rework Loop  
- S5: Escalated  
- S6: Resolved (absorbing)  
- S7: Withdrawn (absorbing)  
- S8: Rejected (absorbing)  
- S9: Transferred (absorbing for internal process)  
- S10: Right-censored at cutoff (absorbing administrative state)

Typical transitions include:

- S0 → S1 (Submit→AutoCheck) or S0 → S2 (Submit→Screen)  
- S1 → S2 (AutoCheck→Screen)  
- S2 → S3 (Screen→Review) or S2 → S7/S8 (Withdraw/Reject)  
- S3 ↔ S4 (Review↔Rework loops)  
- S3 → S5 (Review→Escalate)  
- S5 → S3 (Escalate→Review) or S5 → S6/S9 (Escalate→Resolve/Transfer)  
- S3 or S5 → S6/S7/S8/S9 (terminal resolution/withdraw/reject/transfer)  
- Any non-absorbing state → S10 at the observation cutoff (right censoring)

In Mermaid syntax (described in text), the core structure is: stateDiagram-v2 with [*] → Submit; Submit → AutoCheck or Screen; AutoCheck → Screen; Screen → Review; Review → Rework → Review (loop); Review → Escalate; Escalate → Review or Resolve or Transfer; from Review you can also go to Resolve, Withdraw, or Reject. This multi-state model allows estimation of stage-specific hazards (e.g. hazard of escalation from Review, hazard of resolution from Escalate) and transition probabilities that are directly linked to process redesign options.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

We propose at least three families of sensitivity analyses focused on transfers, competing outcomes, informative censoring, and cohort effects.

1. **Transfer treatment scenarios (three-way sensitivity)**  
   Using the estimand E1 (“not resolved by 72h”):  
   - **Scenario T1 – transfers as competing risks / internal breach**: treat S08, S21, S32 as competing events that represent failure of internal resolution. Under this scenario, cohort B may look worse for P1 cases, because S21 and S32 (cohort B, P1) are transferred rather than resolved, while cohort A’s P1 cases include one transfer (S08) and one escalation-resolve path (S04).  
   - **Scenario T2 – transfers as independent censoring**: censor at transfer time and assume that transfer does not depend on unobserved resolution potential. This will reduce the estimated internal breach probability, particularly for high-priority cases, and may make cohort B appear better if it relies more heavily on transfer.  
   - **Scenario T3 – best/worst-case bounds**: treat all pre-72h transfers as successes in the best case (assume external partners always resolve quickly) and as certain breaches in the worst case. This produces an interval for E1. If the conclusion “cohort B improves tail risk for P2 cases” holds under both extremes, it is robust to transfer assumptions; if the ordering of cohorts reverses between best and worst cases, transfer handling is pivotal.

2. **Handling withdrawal and rejection (competing risks vs censoring)**  
   - **Baseline**: treat `Withdraw` and `Reject` as competing risks; they preclude internal resolution and count as SLA breaches for E1.  
   - **Alternative W1 – censoring early withdrawals/rejections**: treat very early withdrawals/rejections (e.g. S05 at 9h, S23 at 11h, S12 at 15h) as administrative censoring when they likely reflect invalid or duplicated submissions rather than genuine process failures. This will reduce the apparent breach rate for lower priorities (P3) and potentially improve cohort B if it rejects quickly via automated screening (S23).  
   - **Alternative W2 – combined “negative outcome” end-point**: treat `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` as a single negative outcome, and compute separate CIFs for “favorable resolution” vs “unfavorable closure or transfer”. Comparing cohorts on the ratio of favorable to unfavorable CIFs by 72h may yield different policy impressions than looking only at time to resolve.  
   Some qualitative conclusions—such as “cohort B has fewer negative outcomes among P2 cases by 72h”—could reverse if, for example, cohort B uses automated rejection more aggressively and we decide to treat some of those rejections as censoring rather than failures.

3. **Informative censoring and inactivity gaps**  
   Open cases with long `last_event_gap_h` (e.g. S11 with gap 55h, S25 with 48h, S26 with 72h) suggest non-random loss of follow-up: idle cases may be more likely to be late or abandoned. To examine sensitivity to informative censoring:  
   - **Gap-weighted IPCW**: construct inverse-probability-of-censoring weights where the probability of remaining uncensored decreases with inactivity gap length or with process stage. This upweights high-risk open cases when estimating CIFs and E1.  
   - **Pattern-mixture grouping**: stratify analyses by gap patterns (e.g. last_event_gap_h ≤ 10h vs >10h) and compare SLA breach estimates; large differences imply strong informative censoring.  
   - **Worst-case imputation for long-gap opens**: assume that open cases with long gaps and age >72h (e.g. S09, S11, S25, S26) will *never* resolve, and treat them as permanent breaches; compare with treating them as right-censored at age. If overall conclusions about cohort B vs A or P1 vs P3 tail risk change dramatically, we must report that SLA estimates are highly sensitive to censoring assumptions.

4. **Cohort-policy effects and selection bias**  
   Because cohort B is post-policy and not randomized, its case mix and routing decisions (e.g. more automated rejection as in S23, more transfer for P1 as in S21/S32) may differ systematically from cohort A. Sensitivity steps include:  
   - **Stratified analyses by priority and cohort**: compute E1–E3 and CIFs separately for each (cohort, priority) cell to avoid conflating P1-heavy mixes with cohort effects.  
   - **Propensity-style standardization (conceptual)**: if more covariates were available, estimate the probability of being in cohort B given baseline characteristics and reweight to a common target population; check whether tail-risk differences persist after standardization.  
   Qualitative conclusions like “cohort B reduces SLA breach risk for P2 cases” could attenuate or reverse if cohort B handled more complex P2 cases or if its apparent improvement is driven mainly by transfers being treated as censoring.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival and competing-risk outputs must be translated into operational actions without overclaiming causality:

- **Real-time alerts and workload actions**  
  - Use dynamic predictions from landmarked, stage-aware models (e.g. probability of breaching E1 given history up to 24 or 48 hours) to trigger alerts: when an individual case’s predicted breach probability exceeds a threshold, create work items for escalation, reassignment, or additional review.  
  - From E3 estimates, obtain predicted counts of cases that will still be open at 72h by cohort and priority; use these to size staffing, plan overtime, and manage backlog. For example, if P1 cases in cohort B have high E3 while P2/P3 do not, resources can be reallocated towards high-priority escalated cases.

- **SLA governance and monitoring**  
  - Report **CIFs by outcome type**: e.g. probability of on-time resolution, late resolution, withdrawal, rejection, and transfer by 72h. Governance committees can decide whether, for example, increased transfers in cohort B are an acceptable trade-off for fewer very late internal resolutions.  
  - Track **restricted mean unresolved time** up to 72h per cohort and priority as a summary metric, complementing simple breach rates.

- **Calibration and fairness checks**  
  - For any risk scores derived from survival models, examine calibration: compare predicted vs observed breach frequencies across bins of predicted risk, separately for each priority and cohort. Poor calibration in cohort B or P1 may indicate model misspecification or policy drift.  
  - Check for **unfair performance differences**: for example, if P3 cases in cohort B have a much higher probability of rejection or transfer than similar P3 cases in cohort A at the same predicted risk level, this may reflect unwanted policy bias. Survival models should be used descriptively and for prioritization, not to justify unequal treatment without careful fairness review.

- **Non-causal interpretation**  
  Even if escalation or auto-check features are predictive of reduced tail risk, we must avoid interpreting their coefficients as causal effects. Escalation may be triggered precisely for high-risk cases, and cohort B may have been introduced in response to prior SLA problems. Any policy recommendations (e.g. “expand auto-check to all P2 cases”) should be tested prospectively or in controlled experiments; survival models provide *risk stratification* and *diagnostic insight*, not causal guarantees.

def encode_estimand1(row, transfer_scenario):
    """
    Estimand E1: 'guaranteed breach of internal resolution SLA by 72h'.
    Event = 1 when we know the case cannot be resolved internally by 72h.
    """
    t = float(row["duration_or_age_h"])
    status = row["observed_status"]
    time = min(t, HORIZON)
    event = 0  # default: no guaranteed breach yet

    if status == "resolved":
        if t > HORIZON:
            # Resolved after 72h -> breach; treat breach time as 72h
            time = HORIZON
            event = 1
        else:
            # Resolved before or at 72h -> no breach; censor at resolution
            time = t
            event = 0
    elif status in ("withdrawn", "rejected"):
        # These internal outcomes preclude internal resolution.
        time = min(t, HORIZON)
        event = 1
    elif status == "transferred":
        if transfer_scenario == "transfer_as_censoring":
            # Assume transfer simply censors the case.
            time = min(t, HORIZON)
            event = 0
        elif transfer_scenario == "transfer_as_breach":
            # Assume all transfers are SLA breaches for internal resolution.
            time = min(t, HORIZON)
            event = 1
        elif transfer_scenario == "transfer_as_external_resolution":
            # Assume transfers before 72h resolve successfully elsewhere; transfers after 72h are late.
            if t < HORIZON:
                time = t
                event = 0
            else:
                time = HORIZON
                event = 1
    elif status == "open":

            # Still open beyond 72h -> observed breach
            time = HORIZON
            event = 1
        else:
            # Open but still within the horizon -> right-censored at age
            time = t
            event = 0

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The current extract is highly simplified and omits many details needed for robust inference:

- **Exact event timestamps and inter-event times**: we only observe total case duration and a single `last_event_gap_h`. Full event timestamps are needed to model time-varying covariates accurately, estimate stage-specific hazards, and handle calendar-time effects such as working hours and weekends.
- **Outcomes after transfer**: we do not know whether externally handled cases are resolved quickly, slowly, or ever. Without external follow-up, we must rely on strong assumptions or wide sensitivity bounds for transfers, and cannot assess whether transfer policies genuinely improve end-to-end SLA performance.
- **Cutoff and start dates, and left-truncation**: the observation cutoff time is not linked to calendar dates, so we cannot assess how long cases have been eligible for follow-up or whether some cohorts are more left-truncated than others. This matters for interpreting censoring and for aligning workload with policy rollout.
- **Time-varying workload and resource levels**: queue lengths, staffing levels, and other contemporaneous pressures likely affect both processing times and decisions like escalation or withdrawal. Without such data, estimated hazards may mix process effects with congestion effects.
- **Assignment mechanisms and policy compliance**: case allocation rules, escalation triggers, and adherence to the new automated-screening policy in cohort B are not observed. Without them, comparisons between cohorts A and B are vulnerable to confounding and selection bias.
- **Richer case attributes and external context**: additional baseline features (e.g. product line, customer type, initial channel), external outcomes (e.g. customer satisfaction, downstream resolution), and reasons for withdrawal or rejection would allow more nuanced multi-state models and fairness analysis.

Collecting these data would enable a more reliable assessment of SLA tail risk, better adjustment for confounding, and stronger guidance for policy changes.
