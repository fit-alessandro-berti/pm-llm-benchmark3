## 1. What is observed

Automation availability began for Alpha and Beta on 2026-04-01 and for Gamma on 2026-07-01; Delta and Epsilon remained without availability through August. Availability is not equivalent to use: `automated_fast_path_pct` was approximately 4–6% before adoption, rose to 25–27% in each adopter’s first month, and subsequently reached 32% in Gamma and 48–50% in Alpha and Beta by August. This pattern is consistent with gradual uptake, learning, eligibility limits, or changing routing into the automated path.

Adoption was targeted rather than random. Alpha and Beta were selected because of high volumes and historically poor cycle times, and they had worse baseline cycle times, rework, or complexity than Gamma and Delta. Gamma adopted after a readiness pilot. Consequently, adopters and non-adopters differed in levels and possibly in subsequent trajectories before treatment began.

Several contemporaneous changes overlapped adoption: a centralized fraud desk began in March; routing increased arrivals to Alpha and Beta from April and to Gamma after July; Alpha lost two FTEs in May, while Beta, Gamma, and Delta experienced other staffing changes; and high-complexity shares rose in May, especially in Alpha. Adoption periods therefore also contain demand, capacity, queueing, routing, and case-mix changes.

Outcomes are branch-month aggregates. Medians, p90 values, rework, and escalation summarize different parts of case distributions, while CSAT lacks response counts and may represent different respondent mixtures across months. Event-level sequences, waiting times, timestamps, fraud-desk queues, and case-level eligibility are unavailable.

After the table, the primary group-time/event-study framing is preferable because it respects the actual design: two adoption cohorts, a later adopter that can serve as not-yet-treated for part of the early period, never-treated branches, and effects that plausibly evolve as `automated_fast_path_pct` rises from the mid-20s to about 50%. It forces cohort- and event-time-specific contrasts instead of one pooled “automation effect,” making the implementation dip around May–June and the July–August improvement visible rather than averaged away. It also aligns estimands with policy: leadership first needs the ITT effect of offering the system, then separate descriptive intensity analyses. However, with five branches, three pre-months for early adopters, targeted selection, and branch-specific shocks, this design should be used as a structured descriptive-causal framework with wide uncertainty—not as proof. Synthetic control is useful as a branch-specific visual check but has too few donors; interrupted time series alone extrapolates from an extremely short baseline. The primary strategy is therefore least misleading, provided results are reported as fragile and sensitivity-driven.

For Alpha/Beta, April-only DiD is mixed: medians barely move, rework falls relative to flat controls, escalation rises similarly to the March fraud-desk shift, and CSAT is flat.

**3) Treatment-intensity analysis**

Binary adoption hides dose. Baseline automated-fast-path percentages are nonzero (4–6%), then rise: Alpha 2548%, Beta 2750%, Gamma 2532%. Within adopters, higher intensity coincides with recovery from May–June: Alpha intensity 35.5% in May–Jun 47% in Jul–Aug while median falls 5.9h; Beta 37.549% while median falls 2.3h; Gamma 2532% from July to August while median falls 2.4h. A naive within-adopter slope is roughly **-0.2 to -0.5 median hours per +1 percentage-point** automated fast path, but this is almost entirely time-trend confounded: intensity mechanically rises with calendar time/learning while the May shock recedes and August arrivals fall. Rework does not fall with intensity in Alpha/Beta after April; escalation is flat after the initial jump. Intensity therefore supports a substitution/learning narrative for median time, but does not identify a dose causal effect.

**4) Pre-trends and regression to the mean**

Pre-trends are short and noisy. JanMar median changes: Alpha -1.0h with a February dip, Beta +1.0h, Gamma +3.2h driven by March, Delta +1.8h, Epsilon -1.2h. Escalation jumps +2 points in March in every branch, coincident with the fraud desk—so March is not a clean pre-period for escalation. Only three pre-months cannot establish parallel trends. Alpha/Beta were selected for poor historical performance, creating regression-to-the-mean risk if selection followed a transient bad spell. However, their Jan–Mar values are stably worse than Delta/Gamma rather than a single spike, so pure RTM is less obvious for levels; the larger risk is selection on branch type (volume, complexity, capacity) with different responses to the May shock and routing campaign.

**5) Sensitivity / bounded conclusions**

*Median cycle:* Gamma-window DiD  -4h; early-adopter mature DiD vs Jan–Mar  -3 to -7h; using May–Jun as baseline shrinks Alpha/Beta contrasts toward -2 to -6h before control adjustment and about -1 to -2h after. A defensible scenario interval for mature availability effect on median is approximately **-7 to 0h**, with a favorable Gamma-like case near -9% and a null case plausible if controls’ recovery is understated. A single % claim is not stable across windows.

*p90 cycle:* Within adopters vs Jan–Mar, p90 rises +7 to +10h; vs May–Jun peak it falls -4 to -16h; DiD sign flips depending on whether controls’ May–Aug tail deterioration is treated as counterfactual. Scenario interval: **-9 to +10h**, i.e., not identified in sign robustly. Reporting median improvement as tail improvement would be unjustified.

*Rework/escalation sensitivity:* Rework ranges from April dip (-2 points) to mature Alpha +1.3 points vs baseline; Gamma DiD  -2 points. Escalation consistently rises +1 to +3 points after March/adoption, but fraud-desk timing prevents attribution.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

`expand with an embedded evaluation`

Expand next to Delta first, and delay Epsilon as the contemporaneous comparison for one additional cycle, unless operational need forces both. Delta is operationally closer to Gamma in baseline cycle time and lower complexity than Epsilon, while Epsilon’s high and rising complexity (38%) makes it a poor first test and a valuable control. Rather than manager-chosen go-live, use a randomized or lottery-based phase-in within Delta if feasible: randomize eligible case streams, teams, shifts, or weeks to automated-triage availability, stratified by complexity and arrival channel. If case-level randomization is impossible, randomize the start week across Delta sub-units and keep Epsilon untreated for at least three months, with a pre-registered crossover date.

Collect at least six months of retrospective event-log pre-data before Delta go-live—not merely branch-month aggregates—and a minimum of six post months to separate implementation, learning, and mature effects. Required process-mining data: case ID, registration risk/complexity, eligibility, assigned versus actual path, activity timestamps, queue entry/exit for manual triage, specialist review, rework loops, and the centralized fraud desk, FTE by queue, arrivals/routing source, automation version, overrides, and survey response linkage with denominator counts. Monitor spillovers weekly: fraud-desk arrivals, waits, and p90 by referring branch, including effects on Epsilon and existing adopters.

Guardrails: pause or roll back if, for two consecutive weeks, escalated-case p90 or fraud-desk wait rises >10% versus matched control, rework rises >2 points, CSAT among escalated cases falls >0.2, or high-complexity median worsens. Decision rule for scale-up to Epsilon: pre-specify success as mature-month median improvement at least 5% with no p90 deterioration >3%, no rework increase >1 point, and no fraud-desk spillover breach, estimated with heterogeneity-robust staggered/event-time methods and branch-level sensitivity. If median improves only while tail/rework worsen, redesign triage thresholds before scaling.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- `Observed:` Automation availability was followed by rising automated-fast-path use to 32–50%, an immediate escalation increase, an April rework dip, May–June deterioration across adopters and controls, and July–August median recovery while p90 remained above Jan–Mar baselines in adopters.
- `Suggestive:` In the cleanest Gamma contrast, mature adoption months show about a 4-hour larger median reduction and about a 2-point larger rework reduction than Delta/Epsilon after June, consistent with a possible modest median benefit once use reached roughly 25–32%.
- `Conditionally causal:` Only if conditional parallel trends, no anticipation, no differential May/routing/staffing shocks, and negligible fraud-desk spillovers held could one interpret the mature availability effect as roughly 0 to -7 median hours, with tail effects still ranging from harmful to beneficial.
- `Not supported:` The statement “automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a single organization-wide causal claim—especially one implying median, p90, rework, and CSAT all improve—is not supported by these data and should not be used for immediate unmonitored expansion.
