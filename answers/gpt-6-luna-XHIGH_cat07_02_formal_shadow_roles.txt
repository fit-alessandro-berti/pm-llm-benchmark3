# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

- **Automated intake and entitlement precheck (Nora):** Registration and precheck are core by frequency. The low case-complexity distribution and sequence position mark this as an automation boundary. Treat its “Validate” events as prechecks unless the process definition confirms the bot makes the same entitlement decision as an agent.
- **Front-office service resolution (Amira and Ben):** Validation, standard responses and closure are core. Their occasional technical, commercial and reopen events are peripheral. Amira’s higher medium/high share suggests a more experienced or trusted assignment pattern, but the log alone cannot distinguish that from queue allocation.
- **Seasonal capacity (Kai and Lina):** Standard responses and simple closures are core. Their validation counts are substantial but should be treated as bounded support, not proof of permanent-agent competence. The incomplete notes and subsequent 17 reopens make entitlement documentation a training and control concern. Temporary substitution into simple queues does not establish readiness for complex work.
- **Technical analysts (Chen and Dina):** Diagnosis, configuration and implementation are core. Reopens, security-adjacent activities, negotiation, and KB updates are peripheral. Their shared activity pattern supports a common functional role, while the complexity split argues for different assignment thresholds or a senior tier for Chen rather than assuming interchangeability.
- **Security specialist (Erik):** Security review is the defining core activity even though it is less frequent than routine service or quality events. Occasional technical work does not make those his core role. Critical-case exposure, not volume, should drive coverage and escalation design.
- **Commercial specialist (Fatima):** Pricing review and customer negotiation are core; occasional approvals or other activities are peripheral. The substitution episode shows that someone can temporarily perform the work while turnaround worsens. This is evidence of coverage value and a possible capacity or knowledge gap, not by itself proof that Giorgio has the same depth or authority.
- **Giorgio’s shadow role:** Front-office work is core by routine volume; A7/A8 work is a consequential secondary function. His cross-functional work is useful resilience, but its scope, escalation limits and access to commercial guidance should be made explicit. Track quality and duration separately for shadow assignments.
- **Supervisor (Hana):** Final A9 decision authority is core. Escalation participation, reopens and quality checks are peripheral. Her role is distinguished by decision rights, not simply the number of approval events.
- **Escalation broker (Mateo):** Routing, coordination and reopen handling are core. Preliminary approvals and touches on security, technical and commercial work are peripheral. The 13 rechecks of his 21 preliminary approvals during Hana’s absence show why activity overlap must not be read as delegated final authority.
- **Quality and knowledge (Ivan and Julia):** Quality checks and KB updates are core. Technical/security support and rework are peripheral. Their strong pair similarity supports shared role coverage, while their shifts and different complexity profiles merit examination before moving work between them.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

1. **R07_Giorgio — commercial shadow and front-office bridge.** Giorgio combines substantial routine front-office work with meaningful A7/A8 work. During Fatima’s six-day absence he performed 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations, sustaining coverage. The increase in median pricing duration from 38 to 74 minutes is a sign that this coverage was less efficient or involved harder cases; it does not establish poor quality or improper role boundaries. Make the shadow scope explicit, provide decision support, and monitor outcomes and turnaround. His activity similarity with both Fatima and Amira is evidence of bridging, not evidence that he holds either role’s full competence or authority.

2. **R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker.** Mateo appears throughout intake, specialist referral, coordination, approval-related work and reopens. Filtering low-risk security referrals reduced false escalation complaints, which is a useful triage benefit. Two critical cases waited more than two days, however, so referral precision must be balanced against critical-case latency. His preliminary approvals during Hana’s absence were rechecked, indicating a boundary between coordination and final decision rights. Preserve that distinction in access, workflow labels and performance measures.

3. **R05_Erik — rare but critical specialist.** Erik’s event frequency is modest compared with routine roles, but his cases are unusually complex and critical. His low similarity with Mateo (0.39) supports a distinct expertise role; Mateo routes work while Erik performs specialist review. Staffing should protect timely access to Erik or qualified backup, especially during surges, and monitor waiting time for critical cases. Rarity is not a reason to merge the function into technical analysis.

4. **R14_Nora — automation boundary role.** Nora performs much of intake and prechecking at high volume but handles only low-complexity work. Keep automated completion and human accountability distinguishable in event data and service measures. Exceptions should have a clear human owner.

5. **R11_Kai and R12_Lina — temporary capacity bridge.** Their near-identical profiles efficiently absorb seasonal simple work. Their use is beneficial when tasks are bounded and supervision is available. Reopened cases tied to incomplete validation notes show a risk at the handoff from temporary throughput to durable resolution; targeted coaching and note-quality controls can address it.

6. **R09_Ivan and R10_Julia — quality gatekeepers.** Their checks and knowledge updates provide a control layer across technical and other resolution work. They can bridge technical work and closure, but the event sequence should be checked to ensure the quality gate remains independent where required. Their different shifts also make handover design relevant.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The code below assumes an event-level pandas dataframe named `events` with the stated PM4Py columns and case-level attributes repeated on events or available to join by case. The benchmark provides summaries rather than raw events, so the results cannot be computed from the tables alone. Treat mined roles as candidate behavioral groups; reconcile them with formal titles, certifications, decision rights and substitution records.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

# events: one row per event. Convert timestamps before formatting.
events = events.copy()
events[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(events[TIMESTAMP], utc=True)
formatted = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
)
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(formatted)

# Candidate role groupings and activity-based resource similarity.
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log, activity_key=ACTIVITY, resource_key=RESOURCE
)
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    log, activity_key=ACTIVITY, resource_key=RESOURCE
)
print("Discovered roles:", roles)
print("Similarity result type/value:", type(similarity), similarity)

# Inspect collaboration and transfer patterns separately from activity similarity.
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    log, activity_key=ACTIVITY, resource_key=RESOURCE
)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log, resource_key=RESOURCE
)
print("Handover network:", handover)
print("Working-together network:", working_together)

# PM4Py versions commonly represent each role as (activities, resources).
# Inspect the returned structure above and adjust this small adapter if a
# version uses a different representation. Keep multiple memberships if any.
role_rows = []
for role_id, item in enumerate(roles):
    activities, resources = item[0], item[1]
    for resource in resources:
        role_rows.append({
            "role_id": role_id,
            RESOURCE: resource,
            "role_activities": tuple(activities),
        })
role_membership = pd.DataFrame(role_rows)

# Join each event to all candidate roles containing its resource. Preserve
# event-level complexity and substitution evidence rather than averaging it away.
keep = [CASE, ACTIVITY, RESOURCE]
for col in ("case_complexity", "temporary_substitution"):
    if col in formatted.columns:
        keep.append(col)
event_roles = formatted[keep].merge(role_membership, on=RESOURCE, how="left")

# If substitution is encoded as text, normalize it for counts. If the column
# is absent, the code reports zero; absence is not evidence of no substitutions.
if "temporary_substitution" in event_roles.columns:
    event_roles["is_substitution"] = (
        event_roles["temporary_substitution"].astype(str).str.lower()
        .isin({"true", "1", "yes", "y"})
    )
else:
    event_roles["is_substitution"] = False

# Numeric complexity scores can be summarized; categorical labels should be
# inspected with a cross-tab instead of being forced into an arbitrary scale.
if "case_complexity" in event_roles.columns:
    event_roles["complexity_numeric"] = pd.to_numeric(
        event_roles["case_complexity"], errors="coerce"
    )
    role_summary = event_roles.groupby(
        ["role_id", RESOURCE], dropna=False
    ).agg(
        event_count=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
        mean_numeric_complexity=("complexity_numeric", "mean"),
        substitution_events=("is_substitution", "sum"),
    ).reset_index()
    print(role_summary)
    print(pd.crosstab(
        [event_roles["role_id"], event_roles[RESOURCE]],
        event_roles["case_complexity"],
        dropna=False,
    ))
else:
    print(event_roles.groupby(["role_id", RESOURCE]).agg(
        event_count=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
        substitution_events=("is_substitution", "sum"),
    ))
```

For a production analysis, add a separate resource reference table containing formal title, home team, contract, certification and known authority, then compare those fields with role membership. Check handovers and working-together edges at case and complexity level, and inspect resource/activity counts conditioned on complexity and substitution. A similarity score or discovered cluster describes observed behavior; it does not establish competence, permanent assignment, or approval authority.

# 6. Operational interpretation

Use the structure as a staffing and control map. Maintain dependable coverage for security review and supervisor approval, and give Mateo a documented critical-case escalation path with time limits so that referral filtering does not leave urgent cases waiting. Train Giorgio for the commercial tasks he is expected to shadow, define when Fatima must decide or review, and track shadow-case outcomes and duration. Keep seasonal assignments bounded until entitlement-note quality is demonstrated. Use Chen’s and Dina’s different case mixes to plan mentoring and risk-appropriate routing. Preserve quality coverage across shifts and examine whether handoffs delay checks or KB updates. Treat Nora’s precheck as a distinct process step with a visible route to an accountable human for exceptions.

Mined roles can guide targeted cross-training, backup planning and workload review. Management should also use case complexity, criticality, customer/product mix, rework, service times and decision rights to set staffing and controls. Two cautions are especially important:

- **Do not reassign work solely because two resources have similar activity profiles.** Similarity does not establish equal certification, experience, authority or exposure to high-risk cases; the Chen/Dina and Fatima/Giorgio comparisons illustrate this.
- **Do not size a critical capability by event volume alone.** Security work is rare but consequential, and low counts can coexist with high risk and long waits. Monitor critical-case coverage and delay, not only throughput.
- **Do not treat temporary coverage as evidence of permanent competence.** The commercial substitution episode shows that work can be absorbed with a turnaround cost; the temporary agents’ reopen pattern shows a quality risk at broader validation work.
- **Do not use a cluster as an organization chart.** Automation, triage, shadow coverage and final approval can produce overlapping traces while carrying different accountability. Confirm formal decision rights and process ownership before changing access, staffing or sign-off rules.
