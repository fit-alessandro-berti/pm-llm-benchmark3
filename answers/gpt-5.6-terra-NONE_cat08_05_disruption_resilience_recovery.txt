# 1. Disruption timeline

| Period | Operational state | Dominant process variants | Performance and risk posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T0: Day -30 to Day -1** | Normal, system-supported operations | Standard path: receive  authorization  reservation  pick  pack  quality/release  dispatch  proof of delivery (POD)  invoice close. Limited, controlled exceptions for compliance holds, backorders, and repack/recheck. | Strong conformance: fitness **0.96**, 91% fitting traces. Median cycle times were 8h for hospital-critical, 23h for pharmacy-standard, and 36h for wholesale-bulk. Exception rates were low: traceability 0.8%, missing temperature documentation 0.4%, missing dual-control 0.2%. |
| **T1: Day 0 morning** | Abrupt system outage following cyber incident | Warehouse-management and workflow controls unavailable; normal authorization, reservation, scan, and traceability sequence could not be reliably executed. | This is the transition from controlled digital execution to contingency mode. The central decision was not whether to deviate, but whether deviations retained equivalent controls and evidence. |
| **T2: Day 0 afternoon to Day 2** | Manual workaround / emergency continuity mode | Emergency triage; hospital-critical fast lane; manual authorization and inventory checks; paper/spreadsheet picking; temporary release controls; dispatch without scan; phone POD; spreadsheet invoice holds; deferral of noncritical orders. | Service continuity was selectively preserved: hospital-critical fill rate remained **91%**, but pharmacy-standard fill fell to **61%**. Cycle time deteriorated sharply, especially for noncritical segments: hospital median 10h versus 8h baseline; pharmacy 54h versus 23h; bulk 96h versus 36h. Conformance fell materially: fitness **0.71**, only **44%** fitting traces. Traceability exceptions rose to **9.5%**, missing cold-chain documentation to **4.8%**, and missing controlled-substance dual-control to **2.9%**. This period contains both legitimate resilience and concealed noncompliance. |
| **T3: Day 3 to Day 6** | Partial recovery with unreliable synchronization and scanning | Order intake returned, but hybrid process variants remained: partial digital intake combined with manual inventory, scan reconstruction, workaround evidence, and continued backlog management. | Operational performance improved but remained materially degraded: hospital median 9h, pharmacy 38h, bulk 84h. Traceability exceptions declined to **5.4%**, but were still far above baseline. Fitness recovered to **0.82**, with 59% fitting traces. The principal risk was false confidence: systems were partially available, while inventory synchronization and scanning were unreliable. |
| **T4: Day 7 to Day 21** | Stabilization and reconciliation | Return to standard authorization, reservation, pick/pack/release, dispatch, and POD; added backlog prioritization, reconciliation of manual reservations, POD remediation, and audit remediation after invoice close. | Core operations mostly recovered: hospital median returned to **8h**, pharmacy to 28h, bulk to 58h. However, tail performance and control quality remained weak: p90 pharmacy was 74h and p90 bulk 120h; traceability exceptions remained **2.2%**; rework was **5.8%** versus 3.5% baseline. Fitness rose to **0.90**, but only 76% of traces fitted. The DFG shows a persistent hidden workload: **2,300** invoice-close-to-audit-remediation transitions and **1,200** dispatch-to-POD-remediation transitions. Operational recovery therefore preceded compliance recovery. |

The temporal evidence supports a differentiated interpretation:

- The hospital-critical fast lane and capacity protection were resilient because they protected a mission-critical outcome.
- The movement from digital controls to manual controls was not inherently noncompliant; manual controls can be valid if they are risk-based, documented, independently checked, and reconciled promptly.
- The move-on-log deviations in T2—especially **skip system authorization check**, **dispatch without scan**, **phone proof of delivery**, and **spreadsheet invoice hold**—show that some continuity measures were not equivalent substitutes for the normative controls.
- The move-on-model deviations—missing quality/release, scan, POD, and temperature records—are more serious because they represent absent required control events, not merely a different execution route.
- T4’s audit-remediation tail demonstrates that apparent recovery in cycle time was partly achieved by transferring work and risk into a later compliance queue.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this case, **resilience** means maintaining essential pharmaceutical supply while preserving risk-appropriate controls, evidence, accountability, and a credible route back to normal operation. It does not mean restoring volume at any cost.

A deviation from the baseline process can be resilient when it substitutes an equivalent or proportionate control. For example:

- **Manual authorization check (B)** is resilient if trained staff use current offline eligibility data, document their decision, and escalate uncertain cases.
- **Manual pick lists (E)** are resilient if the paper process captures order, lot, quantity, expiry, picker, verifier, and custody information sufficiently to reconstruct the transaction.
- **Temporary release control (F)** is resilient for high-risk products when it provides documented pharmacist review rather than merely reducing the number of controls.
- **Deferral of noncritical demand (I)** and **backlog prioritization (J)** are resilient if they are transparent, risk-based, and bounded by fairness and maximum-delay rules.
- **Reconcile manual reservation (K)** is essential resilience recovery work because it restores the integrity of inventory records after emergency operations.

By contrast, **concealed noncompliance** occurs when throughput is preserved by removing or deferring a required control without an equivalent real-time substitute. The process may look successful operationally, but it creates unmeasured regulatory, patient-safety, inventory, or legal exposure.

The clearest examples are:

- **Skip system authorization check (C):** customer history and standing assumptions do not prove current eligibility or product authorization.
- **Dispatch without scan (G):** a shipment can physically leave the warehouse, but without a contemporaneous manual custody record it lacks reliable traceability.
- **Phone proof of delivery (H):** it may establish that someone says delivery occurred, but it is weaker than authenticated POD and inadequate as the sole evidence for many controlled or high-risk shipments.
- **Spreadsheet invoice hold:** this is useful as an accounting containment measure, but it does not replace missing authorization, release, dispatch, temperature, or POD controls.
- Missing quality/release, scan, POD, and temperature-control records are especially material because they are **move-on-model** deviations: required activities are absent, not merely rerouted.

Some adaptations are inherently **ambiguous and risk-appetite dependent**:

- **Hospital-critical fast lane (A)** is appropriate for continuity of care, but only if it does not turn priority into authorization bypass. Management may accept a short delay in electronic processing, but should not accept shipment of a high-risk product without a documented emergency authorization decision.
- **Manual inventory snapshot check (D)** is reasonable immediately after failure, but becomes progressively unsafe as the inventory state changes. Its acceptability should expire after a defined period or volume threshold.
- **Temporary release control (F)** is acceptable if it is more stringent for higher-risk products; sampling may be acceptable for defined low-risk ambient orders, but not for controlled substances or cold-chain items.
- **Deferral of noncritical orders (I)** is valid during a capacity crisis, but broad or prolonged deferral can be unfair, can harm pharmacy continuity, and can conceal a failure to manage capacity transparently.

The metrics reinforce this distinction. Hospital-critical performance was protected in T2, which is a meaningful resilience outcome. However, traceability exceptions rose from 0.8% to 9.5%, missing temperature documentation from 0.4% to 4.8%, and missing dual-control from 0.2% to 2.9%. Therefore, the organization achieved **partial service resilience**, but not full control resilience. The audit-remediation queue in T4 further shows that some of the apparent operational recovery was financed by deferred compliance work.

# 4. Recovery process model

The future recovery process should be a pre-approved, risk-tiered contingency model rather than an ad hoc collection of workarounds.

## A. Activation triggers and governance

**Trigger incident-continuity mode when any of the following occurs:**

1. Warehouse management system unavailable for more than 30 minutes during operating hours.
2. Inventory reservation or scan reliability falls below a defined threshold, for example:
   - more than 2% failed reservation confirmations in an hour, or
   - more than 1% unmatched scan events for two consecutive hours.
3. Cybersecurity incident creates uncertainty about data integrity, not merely system availability.
4. Cold-chain, controlled-substance, recall, or authorization data cannot be reliably retrieved.
5. Backlog age or hospital-critical service level breaches defined thresholds.

**Governance actions on activation:**

- Appoint an incident commander, warehouse lead, compliance/pharmacy lead, IT recovery lead, transport lead, and customer-service lead.
- Declare one of three modes:
  - **Mode 1: Degraded digital operation** — systems available but unreliable.
  - **Mode 2: Manual continuity operation** — core warehouse systems unavailable.
  - **Mode 3: Controlled stop for high-risk flows** — data integrity or product-control risk cannot be managed safely.
- Freeze nonessential configuration changes and preserve system logs, printed snapshots, manual forms, and communications for forensic and compliance purposes.
- Establish a fixed incident cadence: for example, hourly operational huddles in the first day and twice-daily risk reviews thereafter.

## B. Risk-tiered triage rules

### Priority tiers

| Priority | Customer/product combination | Default response |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Hospital-critical orders; emergency replenishment; life-supporting or shortage products | Process first, subject to minimum mandatory controls. Escalate unresolved authorization, inventory, temperature, or custody uncertainty immediately. |
| **P2** | Controlled-substance, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, or otherwise restricted products for any customer | Prioritize based on clinical need and product risk; do not release without enhanced manual controls. |
| **P3** | Pharmacy-standard ambient orders with pre-approved, current authorization profile | Process through controlled manual route when capacity permits. |
| **P4** | Wholesale-bulk and nonurgent replenishment orders | Defer where necessary, but apply published maximum wait times, customer communication, and age-based escalation. |

### Triage decision logic

1. **Is the order P1 or P2?**
   - If yes, obtain manual authorization, verified inventory/lot status, and enhanced release/custody controls.
   - If any critical control cannot be completed, escalate to incident commander and compliance lead; do not silently bypass.
2. **Is the order P3?**
   - Permit controlled manual handling only if the customer/product combination is on a current pre-approved offline authorization list and all required evidence can be captured.
3. **Is the order P4?**
   - Place in a visible backlog queue with reason code, promised reassessment time, and customer communication.
4. **Does the order involve a recall, expiry concern, controlled substance, cold chain, or restricted customer status?**
   - Route to enhanced control regardless of customer priority.

## C. Minimum controls by product and customer risk

| Risk category | Authorization | Inventory / lot control | Release control | Dispatch / custody | POD / closure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ambient, pre-approved pharmacy or hospital order** | Offline authorization list or documented phone verification | Time-stamped manual reservation ledger; pick confirmation | Second-person check for defined exceptions; pharmacist escalation when uncertain | Pre-numbered manual dispatch manifest signed by warehouse and courier | Named receiver, date/time, and later digital entry |
| **Hospital-critical ambient** | Manual authorization or emergency authorization approval | Manual reservation ledger plus priority allocation check | Mandatory documented release check | Manual manifest and courier handoff signature | Authenticated POD as soon as practicable; escalation if absent |
| **Cold-chain** | Manual authorization required | Lot, expiry, and temperature-device association required | 100% pharmacist/quality release; verified temperature evidence before release | Manual custody record plus temperature logger or validated temperature indicator | Receiver identity, delivery time, temperature evidence, and exception escalation |
| **Controlled-substance** | Manual authorization with current controlled-product eligibility verification | Controlled-stock ledger, lot/serial where applicable, quantity reconciliation | 100% dual control and pharmacist release; no sampling | Two-person custody handoff or equivalent documented secure transfer | Strong POD: named authorized recipient, signature/authenticated evidence, and immediate discrepancy escalation |
| **Recall-sensitive product** | Manual authorization and recall-status check from validated offline source | Lot-level verification mandatory | 100% release check | Lot-linked dispatch record | Lot-linked POD and immediate reconciliation |
| **Authorization, inventory, temperature, or custody cannot be verified** | Do not use assumed authorization | Do not allocate from uncertain stock | Hold pending escalation | No dispatch except explicitly authorized emergency exception with senior clinical/compliance approval | Exception remains open; cannot be treated as routine completed order |

## D. Manual evidence capture

Every manual-continuity case should receive a **unique contingency case ID** linked to the original order ID where available.

A controlled manual form or offline application should capture:

- order ID and contingency case ID;
- customer, site, contact, and priority tier;
- product, quantity, lot/batch, expiry, and serial data where applicable;
- authorization source, list version, checker identity, time, and phone confirmation reference if used;
- inventory snapshot version and timestamp;
- manual reservation identifier and remaining balance;
- picker, checker, pharmacist/quality releaser, and time stamps;
- cold-chain temperature evidence and device/log reference;
- controlled-substance dual-control signatures;
- courier identity, vehicle or manifest ID, handoff time, and custody signatures;
- POD evidence, receiver identity, time, location, and method;
- exception reason code and escalation decision.

The paper or offline record should be sequentially numbered, access-controlled, and scanned or uploaded as soon as systems are restored. A blank spreadsheet with free-text notes is not an adequate primary control record.

## E. Recovery flow

A BPMN-style representation:

```text
START
  -> Detect disruption / data-integrity concern
  -> Activate continuity mode and incident governance
  -> Receive order
  -> Classify customer criticality + product risk + order age
  -> [P1/P2?]
       Yes -> Manual authorization verification
              -> Verify inventory, lot, expiry, and relevant temperature/security status
              -> [Control evidence complete?]
                   No -> Compliance/incident escalation
                         -> [Senior emergency approval justified and documented?]
                              Yes -> Controlled emergency release with mandatory post-event review
                              No -> Hold / source alternative / communicate customer
                   Yes -> Manual reservation
                         -> Pick with documented chain of custody
                         -> Risk-tiered quality/release control
                         -> Manual dispatch manifest and courier handoff
                         -> Risk-tiered POD
                         -> Provisional close: "awaiting reconciliation"
       No -> [P3 eligible for controlled manual route?]
                   Yes -> Manual authorization / reservation / pick / release / dispatch / POD
                   No -> Place in backlog queue with reason and review SLA
  -> Reconcile each manual record against restored systems
  -> [Mismatch or missing evidence?]
       Yes -> Risk-based remediation queue
              -> [High-risk unresolved?]
                   Yes -> Compliance investigation, customer action, stock/custody review, possible recall or notification
                   No -> Correct record and close under remediation SLA
       No -> Final invoice close and incident record closure
END
```

## F. Backlog prioritization

Backlog must not be a single first-in-first-out queue. It should be ranked using a documented score:

1. Patient/clinical criticality.
2. Product risk: controlled-substance, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, shortage status.
3. Customer service commitment and downstream patient impact.
4. Order age.
5. Product expiry and temperature exposure.
6. Ability to execute safely with available controls.
7. Fairness constraint: prevent indefinite or repeated deprioritization of pharmacy-standard customers.

Recommended rules:

- P1 hospital-critical orders: immediate handling, with escalation if controls cannot be met.
- P2 high-risk products: process as soon as appropriate enhanced controls are available.
- Pharmacy-standard orders: guaranteed review interval and age-based promotion.
- Wholesale-bulk: defer when necessary, but communicate status and periodically reassess.
- No order should remain deferred beyond its maximum acceptable age without an explicit documented decision.

## G. Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

Reconciliation begins during the incident, not after it.

1. **Near-real-time reconciliation**
   - Enter manual reservations and dispatches into an offline controlled ledger.
   - Perform frequent inventory balance checks for scarce, cold-chain, controlled, and recall-sensitive stock.
   - Reconcile controlled-substance and cold-chain transactions at least each shift.

2. **System restoration reconciliation**
   - Match every contingency case ID to an electronic order.
   - Reconstruct missing scan, pick, release, dispatch, POD, lot, temperature, and authorization events.
   - Identify duplicate reservations, stock discrepancies, unmatched courier handoffs, and invoices lacking supporting evidence.

3. **Risk-based remediation queue**
   - **Critical:** controlled-substance custody gap, temperature evidence missing, recall-sensitive lot uncertainty, absent authorization evidence, or unverified delivery. Escalate immediately; do not treat as ordinary audit cleanup.
   - **High:** missing dispatch/POD evidence for hospital or pharmacy supply, manual inventory mismatch, late authorization evidence.
   - **Standard:** administrative scan reconstruction or invoice documentation defect where core safety/custody evidence is intact.

4. **Closure discipline**
   - High-risk unresolved cases must remain operationally and compliance-visible, even if invoicing is provisionally completed.
   - Audit remediation must have owner, due date, root-cause category, corrective action, and evidence of closure.
   - Report both “physical delivery complete” and “compliance evidence complete”; do not conflate them.

# 5. Resilience indicators

## Leading indicators

| Indicator | Definition and purpose |
|---|---|
| **Continuity-mode activation time** | Time from outage detection to formal declaration of degraded/manual mode, assignment of roles, and use of approved forms. Measures readiness. |
| **Hospital-critical protected-service rate** | Percentage of hospital-critical orders delivered within contingency target, with all mandatory controls complete. This is stronger than fill rate alone. |
| **Risk-tiered control-completeness rate at release** | Percentage of orders with required authorization, reservation, release, temperature/security, dispatch, and custody evidence completed before dispatch. Report separately by product risk. |
| **Manual authorization availability and accuracy** | Percentage of manual authorization decisions supported by a current offline source, documented confirmation, and later successful validation. |
| **Manual inventory freshness** | Age of inventory snapshot or last verified balance when an order is allocated. Escalate when freshness exceeds defined limits. |
| **Manual reservation conflict rate** | Percentage of manual reservations later found to conflict with actual inventory or other reservations. |
| **Dispatch evidence captured at handoff** | Percentage of manual dispatches with contemporaneous courier identity, manifest ID, timestamp, and custody signature. |
| **Cold-chain evidence completeness before release** | Percentage of cold-chain orders with required temperature evidence at release and dispatch, not only after delivery. |
| **Controlled-substance dual-control completion** | Percentage with documented dual control and secure handoff before dispatch. Target should be effectively 100%. |
| **Backlog age distribution** | Median, p90, and maximum age by customer group and product risk. This identifies hidden deterioration earlier than aggregate backlog volume. |
| **Fairness / allocation parity** | Comparative deferment, cancellation, and delay rates across hospital, pharmacy, and wholesale customers after adjusting for priority and product risk. |
| **Compliance capacity utilization** | Workload and queue age for pharmacists/compliance staff performing manual checks. Prevents manual controls from becoming a new bottleneck or being bypassed. |

## Lagging indicators

| Indicator | Definition and purpose |
|---|---|
| **Throughput recovery** | Cases completed per day versus baseline, segmented by customer group and product risk. Avoid interpreting aggregate volume without control metrics. |
| **Cycle time recovery** | Median and p90 cycle time by hospital-critical, pharmacy-standard, and wholesale-bulk. The p90 is important because T2 and T4 showed long tails. |
| **Hospital-critical fill rate** | Measures protected clinical service, but must be paired with control completeness and adverse-event measures. |
| **Traceability exception rate** | Percentage of orders with missing or inconsistent lot, scan, dispatch, custody, or POD evidence. Compare to the 0.8% baseline. |
| **Cold-chain temperature-documentation missing rate** | Track separately from general traceability. T2’s 4.8% rate shows that generic controls did not adequately protect temperature evidence. |
| **Controlled-substance dual-control missing rate** | Track as a near-zero-tolerance metric. The rise from 0.2% baseline to 2.9% in T2 is a material control failure. |
| **Authorization exception rate** | Orders shipped without contemporaneous validated authorization, plus later reversals or failed retrospective validation. |
| **Rework/correction rate** | Percentage requiring correction after release, dispatch, invoice, or reconciliation. T2’s 12.5% and T4’s 5.8% show deferred operational debt. |
| **Cancellation rate by segment** | Identify whether recovery protects one group by transferring service loss to another. Pharmacy-standard cancellations and fill performance need separate attention. |
| **Reconciliation mismatch rate** | Manual reservations, picks, dispatches, and invoices that do not match restored system records. |
| **Audit-remediation volume and aging** | Number of open remediation cases, severity mix, median/p90 age, and percentage past SLA. This makes hidden post-recovery workload visible. |
| **POD remediation rate** | Percentage of dispatches requiring later POD reconstruction; track delay from handoff to valid POD. |
| **Adverse quality/compliance outcomes** | Mis-shipments, temperature excursions, controlled-substance discrepancies, recalls affected by poor traceability, regulatory notifications, and customer complaints. |
| **Customer fairness outcome** | Comparative delivery delay, cancellation, and backlog age by customer group, adjusted for approved triage priority. |

Test hospital-critical, cold-chain, controlled-substance, and recall scenarios. | Medium | Validates that the contingency process works under pressure and identifies capacity constraints before an incident. | Simulations cannot fully replicate duration and scale of a real cyber event. |

| **12** | Establish incident dashboards combining service, control, reconciliation, and fairness metrics. Require management review of both real-time and deferred-work indicators. | Medium | Prevents a narrow focus on throughput and hospital fill rate; supports timely escalation. | Metrics can be gamed unless definitions and data quality are governed. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept controlled, documented deviations from the normal digital workflow when they protect hospital-critical supply and retain risk-appropriate authorization, release, temperature, custody, and evidence controls. It should not accept assumed authorization, unrecorded dispatch, weak POD for controlled or cold-chain products, or the treatment of later audit remediation as equivalent to real-time compliance. The recommendation would become more permissive only if evidence showed that offline authorization data were current, manual chain-of-custody and temperature controls were complete, reconciliation mismatches were low, and pharmacy customers were not being disproportionately or indefinitely harmed; it would become more restrictive if controlled-substance discrepancies, temperature evidence gaps, unresolved delivery records, or aged remediation queues increased.
