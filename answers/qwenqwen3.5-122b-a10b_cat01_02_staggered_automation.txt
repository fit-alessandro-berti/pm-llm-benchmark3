## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Primary: Staggered DID with Controls | Event-time dummies (t=-3 to t=+4) | Delta, Epsilon, Gamma (pre-Jul) | `high_complexity_pct`, `fte`, `arrivals` | E1, E4, E5 | Parallel trends conditional on covariates; no spillovers | Placebo tests on pre-trend; sensitivity to covariates | Small N (5 branches); unobserved time-varying confounders |
| Alt 1: Synthetic Control Method (SCM) | Binary step function at adoption | Weighted combination of untreated branches | None (weights derived from pre-period fit) | E1 (Alpha/Beta specific) | Convex hull of donors contains counterfactual | Pre-period RMSPE; donor pool stability | Overfitting due to few donors (only 2 never-treated) |
| Alt 2: Interrupted Time Series (ITS) | Slope change at adoption month | Internal branch history (own pre-trend) | `month` linear trend | E2, E4 | Stable underlying trend interrupted only by intervention | Visual inspection of breaks; autocorrelation check | Cannot distinguish intervention from coincident shocks (e.g., May product change) |

**Primary Strategy Preference**
The Staggered Difference-in-Differences (DID) approach is preferred because it leverages the variation in adoption timing across Alpha/Beta (April) and Gamma (July) while utilizing Delta and Epsilon as contemporaneous controls. Unlike simple ITS, this method accounts for common temporal shocks affecting all branches, such as the May product change that increased complexity globally. By adjusting for `high_complexity_pct` and `fte`, we attempt to isolate the automation signal from staffing and case-mix noise. However, with only five branches, statistical power is low, and standard errors may be underestimated. Consequently, inference should rely heavily on qualitative pattern matching alongside coefficient estimates. The inclusion of event-time dummies allows us to test for pre-trends and dynamic effects, which is critical given the gradual ramp-up of `automated_fast_path_pct`. While SCM is attractive for small samples, the lack of sufficient donor units (only two never-treated) risks poor counterfactual construction, making the DID with explicit covariate adjustment more transparent for policy auditing.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**expand with an embedded evaluation**

Leadership should proceed with expansion to Delta and Epsilon but strictly condition it on a rigorous evaluation framework. Immediate full-scale expansion is premature given the ambiguity around P90 performance and the confounding effects of recent operational changes. Instead, implement a phased rollout: equip Delta in Month 1 and Epsilon in Month 3, maintaining a holdout period for each to establish baselines.

This approach maximizes operational benefit by allowing the organization to capture efficiency gains while minimizing risk through continuous monitoring. To ensure causal learning, assign half of Delta's incoming cases (randomly routed) to manual triage for the first three months post-deployment, creating an internal control group. Collect granular queue-state data (time in specific nodes like `Automated Triage` vs `Manual Triage`) rather than relying solely on branch-month aggregates. Monitor the fraud desk waiting times specifically, as the centralized service acts as a potential spillover point.

Establish guardrails: if `p90_cycle_h` exceeds 110 hours or `csat_1to5` drops below 3.7 in any treated branch, trigger a pause for root-cause analysis. Decision rules for scale-up should depend on a composite score: if the treatment effect on median cycle time is >5% reduction AND P90 does not exceed control trends by >10%, proceed to full rollout. This balanced strategy acknowledges the current data limitations while leveraging the remaining untreated branches to generate higher-quality evidence before committing fully.

## 10. Final claim ladder

*   `Observed:` Adoption of automation coincided with stabilization of median cycle times in Alpha and Beta, whereas untreated branches Delta and Epsilon experienced significant degradation during the same period.
*   `Suggestive:` Higher utilization of the automated fast path (`automated_fast_path_pct`) is associated with lower median cycle times within treated branches, though this correlation may reflect learning effects rather than pure technology efficacy.
*   `Conditionally causal:` Automation likely mitigated the negative impact of the May product complexity change on cycle time, assuming the routing and staffing shocks were balanced across groups.
*   `Not supported:` A definitive statement that "automation reduces cycle time by X%" cannot be made due to confounding from staffing reductions, routing increases, and the inability to isolate the specific contribution of the software from concurrent operational changes.
