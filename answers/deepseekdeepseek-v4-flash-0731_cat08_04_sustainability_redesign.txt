## 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Environmental scope included in this analysis:**

- **Order fulfillment process** from `Receive order` to `Close`, including availability checks, stock picking, planned or emergency production, batch waiting, CNC and heat-treatment processing, inspection, packaging, shipment, delivery confirmation, and exception paths such as partial shipment, rework, and cancellation due to delay.
- **Direct operational emissions and material flows** supported by the event log:
  - `energy_kwh` for production, picking, inspection, and rework-related process steps.
  - `transport_kgco2e` for shipment by `parcel_ground`, `parcel_air`, `dedicated_van`, and `consolidated_truck`.
  - `material_scrap_kg` from machining and production rework.
  - `package_material_kg` from standard and reinforced packaging, including packaging rework.
  - Obsolescence waste from overproduction and batching: 6% of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong.

**Outside of this boundary:**

- Upstream embodied emissions of purchased spare parts and raw materials.
- Customer use-phase impacts.
- End-of-life disposal or recycling of sold products.
- Emissions from buildings, IT infrastructure, employee commuting, or vehicle manufacturing.
- Full lifecycle impacts of energy generation mix, although `energy_kwh` is treated as a proxy for production-related climate impact.

**Key assumptions and uncertainties:**

- The log is flattened by order, not by line item. Multi-line orders may hide differences in packing weight, transport route, or production batch allocation.
- `transport_kgco2e` is available per order but not per ton-kilometre, so empty return trips and partial truck loads are not directly visible.
- The electricity mix behind `energy_kwh` is unknown, so the same kWh may imply different lifecycle emissions over time or region.
- Return/damage events are not fully separated from rework events in the provided summary. We therefore use rework loops and packaging rework as proxies for quality risk.
- Resilience risk is assessed qualitatively from policy findings and route alternatives, not from a formal supply-chain risk model.

## 3. Intervention options

### S1. Green shipping recommender using promised lead time, route reliability, and customer segment

- **Target path or segment:** All shipment decisions, especially Standard-stock Service-contract orders currently violating SP1 and MTO paths using `parcel_air`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** At the shipment step, compare promised lead time against real route reliability. Recommend `parcel_ground` or `consolidated_truck` when the order can still be delivered with high probability. This reduces `transport_kgco2e` without broadly banning air.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Service is protected by the 85% reliability threshold. Air and dedicated van remain available as fallback routes, preserving resilience.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Add a `route_recommendation` decision event and compare it with actual `route_mode`. Monitor SP1 violation rate, late-delivery risk, and `transport_kgco2e` by segment.

### S2. Dynamic batching for CNC and heat treatment with maximum waiting-time caps

- **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order paths through `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production` and `Heat treatment`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Larger batches reduce `energy_kwh` per item, especially on CNC_A and heat treatment, but only if the batch wait does not destroy due-date slack.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Batch size becomes conditional on customer type and remaining slack. Service-contract and critical orders can be pulled forward to smaller batches; Regular MTO orders can wait longer. This reduces late delivery risk caused by batch waits.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Track `batch_size`, `batch_id`, batch-wait time, `energy_kwh` per item, late-delivery rate, and obsolescence of batch-produced items.

### S3. Shift Made-to-order production from CNC_B to CNC_A when queue and due-date slack allow it

- **Target path or segment:** MTO paths using `CNC_B`, especially the high-rework path `MTO -> batch wait -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework -> ground`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** CNC_B is faster but has an 18% rework rate versus 9% for CNC_A. Shifting to CNC_A when slack permits reduces rework loops, `material_scrap_kg`, and downstream packaging/transport waste.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** CNC_A may be slower, so the shift must be limited to orders with enough due-date slack. Otherwise, queue pressure increases.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Compare rework rate by `machine_group`, CNC_A/CNC_B utilization, due-date slack, cycle time, and scrap.

### S4. Replace air for Standard-stock Service-contract orders with priority pick + ground, unless slack is insufficient

- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Service-contract orders currently using `parcel_air`; the conformance finding shows SP1 is violated in 22% of these cases.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Switching from air to ground reduces transport emissions substantially, especially because air emits approximately 4–6x more per order than ground.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Priority picking can compensate for the slower ground route. Air is retained as fallback when promised lead time cannot be met with 85% probability.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Track `route_mode`, promised lead time, actual cycle time, SP1 conformance, and late-delivery rate within Standard-stock Service-contract.

### S5. Consolidate truck departures twice daily for non-urgent stock orders

- **Target path or segment:** Stock paths using `Package standard -> Ship ground`, especially orders with enough slack for `consolidated_truck`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Consolidated trucks reduce `transport_kgco2e` per order by roughly 35–55% versus individual ground parcels.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Adds 12–24 hours of consolidation wait. This is acceptable only for non-urgent orders and must be cut off by promised lead time.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Monitor consolidation wait, share of `consolidated_truck`, late-delivery rate, and load factor if available.

### S6. Improve packaging-route compatibility checks before shipment

- **Target path or segment:** `Package standard -> Rework packaging`, `Package reinforced -> Rework packaging`, and Q1-related damage/return loops.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Selecting the correct packaging first time reduces packaging rework, duplicate packaging material, extra handling, and returns.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Reduces delays caused by packaging rework and lowers damage-related returns. Reinforced packaging remains available for MTO parts over 8 kg under Q1.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Track packaging rework rate, `package_material_kg`, `rework_flag`, and return/damage proxy.

### S7. Add inspection quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations

- **Target path or segment:** `Inspection -> Rework production` loops, especially after `CNC_B` and after rushed inspections.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Catching defects before packaging avoids rework production, extra machining energy, material scrap, and repeat transport.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** An added inspection step can increase queue time. Capacity must be planned so the inspection gate does not become a new bottleneck.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Monitor `Inspection` queue time, rework rate, `material_scrap_kg`, and late-delivery risk.

### S8. Replace some dedicated vans with shared emergency courier lanes and split Critical-downtime into true downtime and precautionary urgent categories

- **Target path or segment:** `Critical -> emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van`, which is 8% of orders and has very high transport emissions.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Shared emergency courier lanes reduce empty return trips and improve vehicle utilization. The split between true downtime and precautionary urgent prevents dedicated vans from being used as a default.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** True downtime still receives dedicated or emergency service. Precautionary urgent cases can use shared lanes or ground if promise allows.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** SP2 justification events, share of `dedicated_van`, empty-return proxy, true-downtime classification, and service outcomes by criticality subsegment.

### S9. Introduce carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular made-to-order customers

- **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order Regular customers, where median cycle time is 94h and p90 is 180h.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Offering a longer lower-carbon delivery option allows MTO production to be batched more efficiently and avoids expedited air or immediate production.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Service is protected by making the trade-off transparent. Customers who need speed can still choose it, but the default can shift toward lower impact.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Track accepted promised lead times, actual route mode, `energy_kwh`, `transport_kgco2e`, and late-delivery rate.

### S10. Reduce partial shipments by changing availability-check rules

- **Target path or segment:** Stock paths with `Partial shipment` and `Backorder wait`, especially `Stock -> partial shipment -> ground + backorder`, which has 24% late delivery and high customer contact.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Fewer partial shipments reduce duplicate packaging, second deliveries, extra transport, and backorder handling.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Waiting for full availability can increase cancellation risk if not handled with good promise dates. The rule change should reserve stock at order receipt.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Partial-shipment rate, cancellation due to delay, cycle time, transport per order, and package material per order.

### S11. Create a sustainability exception log for SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides

- **Target path or segment:** All high-impact overrides, including air shipments, dedicated vans, and Q1 packaging violations.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Direct effect is small, but the exception log prevents silent policy erosion and reveals recurring patterns where environmental rules conflict with service or quality.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Adds a small administrative step. Resilience improves because overrides become auditable rather than hidden.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Exception event completeness, override reason, approval timestamp, and associated order attributes.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

### 1. Batching rebound and obsolescence

Larger CNC_A and heat-treatment batches reduce `energy_kwh` per item, but batch waiting increases cycle time. If due-date slack is overestimated, the resulting late orders may switch to emergency production or air transport. The extra transport and expediting emissions can offset the energy gain. In addition, large batches can increase obsolescence: 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong.

### 2. Transport modal-shift rebound

Replacing air with ground or individual ground with consolidated truck reduces `transport_kgco2e` per shipment but adds transit or consolidation time. If route reliability is lower than expected, missed promises may require second shipments, backorders, or emergency couriers. The final carbon impact is negative only if the shift does not trigger additional high-emission rescue shipments.

### 3. Packaging material versus damage-return trade-off

Reinforced packaging reduces damage-related returns by 40% for MTO heavy parts, but it doubles `package_material_kg`. If reinforced packaging is applied too broadly, packaging waste and shipping weight increase without a corresponding return reduction. Conversely, choosing lighter packaging to save material can increase damage and rework loops.

### 4. Quality-gate and inspection bottleneck rebound

Adding an inspection gate for high-rework machine/part combinations can reduce rework and scrap. However, if inspection capacity is not expanded, the queue grows. To keep cycle times acceptable, operators may rush inspections and miss defects, recreating the same rework and return loops further downstream.

### 5. Machine substitution displacement

Shifting work from CNC_B to CNC_A lowers the rework rate but may increase cycle time and machine utilization. If the shift is made for orders without true due-date slack, CNC_A becomes a bottleneck. The resulting waiting time can force expedited production on CNC_B or air transport, offsetting the quality and scrap gains.

### 6. Customer behavior and promise-date rebound

Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation may lead some MTO Regular customers to accept longer lead times, enabling more efficient batching. But other customers may react by placing earlier or duplicate orders to hedge the longer promise date, increasing the total number of orders, cancellations, and returns. If customers still choose expedited options, the intervention may create only a displacement of emissions rather than an absolute reduction.

### 7. Partial-shipment reduction rebound

Changing availability-check rules to reduce partial shipments can lower duplicate packaging and transport. But if customers are forced to wait for full availability, more orders may end in `Cancel due to delay`. Those cancelled orders may later be rebooked as emergency or Critical-downtime orders, with higher transport and production emissions.

### 8. Resilience and exception-creep risk

Creating an exception log for SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides can become a mechanism that normalizes high-emission decisions. If the exception criteria are too broad, the share of justified air shipments and dedicated vans grows over time. Also, if consolidation and shared-lane initiatives reduce the number of available routes, Service-contract customers may have fewer fallback options during carrier disruptions.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

The recommendation is a coherent redesign package, not a set of isolated green options.

### Layer 1: Demand and promise management

- Implement **S10** for Made-to-order Regular customers so that delivery dates are negotiated with carbon consequences in mind.
- Implement **S11** carefully, using availability-check rules that reserve stock at order receipt and reduce partial shipments without forcing full-order waits that create cancellations.
- Use **S1** as the ordering principle: choose lower-carbon transport only when the promised lead time can still be met with 85% probability.

### Layer 2: Production and quality

- Implement **S2** dynamic batching with explicit waiting-time caps by customer type and due-date slack.
- Use **S3** to shift from CNC_B to CNC_A only when queue and slack permit.
- Add **S7** as a quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations, with inspection capacity planned so that it does not create a new bottleneck.

### Layer 3: Fulfillment and transport

- Implement **S4** for Standard-stock Service-contract orders that currently use air even though ground still meets the promise.
- Use **S5** consolidation for non-urgent stock orders, with twice-daily cutoffs and no deviation from the promised lead time.
- Add **S6** packaging-route compatibility checks before shipment.
- Split **Critical-downtime** orders into true downtime and precautionary urgent, and use **S8** shared emergency courier lanes for the precautionary urgent group. True downtime retains dedicated van capability.

### Layer 4: Governance

- Implement **S12** sustainability exception log as an integrated part of order fulfilment, not as a separate reporting afterthought.
- Monitor the SP1/SP2/Q1 policies continuously and review exception patterns weekly.

This package balances environmental impact with service, quality, and resilience because every environmental lever is paired with a guardrail:

- Batching is allowed, but capped by due-date slack to prevent late-delivery and obsolescence.
- Ground and consolidated trucks are preferred, but only when promise reliability is high enough.
- Dedicated vans are not banned; they are reserved for genuine downtime situations.
- Reinforced packaging is not applied everywhere; it is targeted by Q1 and damage-risk evidence.
- Rework is attacked at the source through machine routing and inspection gates, rather than relying only on downstream correction.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

### Baseline and shadow mode

Before rollout, freeze a baseline from the historical 12,000 orders. Add decision attributes to the event log:

- `route_recommendation` and `route_reason`
- `batch_wait_cap` and `due_date_slack`
- `criticality_subtype`: `true_downtime` or `precautionary_urgent`
- `package_compatibility_check_result`
- `exception_reason` for SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides

Run the new recommender, batching logic, and packaging check in shadow mode first, comparing their recommendations to actual decisions without changing live operations.

### Conformance monitoring

Monitor the three key policies continuously:

- **SP1:** For every order with high-emission transport, compute whether ground or consolidated truck could have met the promised lead time with 85% probability. Track the violation rate by segment. If violations remain high, the slack estimate is wrong or the recommender is being ignored.
- **SP2:** Every `dedicated_van` order must have an explicit `true_downtime` justification event. Monitor the percentage of dedicated vans with no justification. Reject or log exceptions when missing.
- **Q1:** For MTO parts over 8 kg, verify that reinforced packaging or an inspection-based low-damage-risk certification is present. Monitor the relationship between Q1 violations and return/rework loops.

### Segment-level service monitoring

Track service performance by `case_type` and `customer_type`, especially:

- Median and p90 cycle time
- Late-delivery rate
- Partial-shipment rate
- Cancellation due to delay
- Backorder wait time

Define acceptable deterioration thresholds before implementation. For example, p90 cycle time may increase for Regular MTO customers if the promise date is explicitly negotiated, but should not increase for Service-contract or true-downtime segments.

### Sustainability and rebound monitoring

Use the indicators defined in Section 2 to monitor:

- `energy_kwh` per order and per item by path
- `transport_kgco2e` per order by route mode
- `material_scrap_kg` per order
- `package_material_kg` per order
- Rework rate by machine group
- Obsolescence of batch-produced items

Also watch rebound indicators: expedite rate, emergency production rate, partial-shipment rate, rework rate, cancellation rate, and exception volume. If energy savings coincide with increased air transport or rework, the design is not balanced and needs adjustment.

### Resilience monitoring

Validate that Service-contract customers always have at least one alternative route during carrier disruption. Use the event log to identify whether fallback routes are actually available and actionable. For dedicated vans, track whether the true-downtime classification is stable and whether shared lanes can absorb precautionary urgent demand during peak periods.

### Governance review

Review the sustainability exception log weekly. If the same override reason appears frequently, revise the policy rather than accepting the exception. The goal is not to punish overrides, but to understand where environmental rules conflict with service, quality, or resilience constraints and to resolve those conflicts through better process design.
