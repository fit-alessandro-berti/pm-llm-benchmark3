# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this benefits process cannot be assessed only by comparing raw approval rates. The evidence must be read across multiple dimensions, especially because the process contains legitimate, rights-preserving steps that may still be implemented in ways that impose unequal burden.

- **Outcome parity**  
  Raw approval rates differ substantially: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, Group D 58%. These differences are important as a starting point, but they do not by themselves establish discrimination. Groups differ in risk, documentation completeness, channel, prior-case history, and support needs. Equal approval rates are not required by fairness; unequal treatment must be explained by legitimate case factors, and the process itself must remain accessible, consistent, and respectful.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity**  
  After controlling for observable case-mix factors, Groups B, C, and D still have lower adjusted approval probabilities than Group A: Group B 5.5 percentage points, Group C 2.2, Group D 3.1. This suggests that observable complexity does not fully explain outcome differences. However, the adjusted differences are not proof of discrimination because some controls may themselves be biased or may absorb legitimate effects of language and accommodation needs.

- **Procedural burden**  
  Procedural burden includes extra steps, repeated document requests, supervisor review, fraud review, handovers, appeals, and rework. Group B has adjusted increases in supervisor review (+8.0 percentage points) and requests for missing documents (+9.5 percentage points). Group C has adjusted increases in supervisor review (+5.5) and cycle time (+4.1 days). Group D has adjusted increases in appeal probability (+5.7 percentage points). These patterns matter even if approval rates are similar, because they affect applicant effort, dignity, and access to benefits.

- **Timeliness**

Waiting-time data show that rights-preserving support steps are major contributors: language support cases wait a median 3.8 days before eligibility assessment, and accommodation review cases wait a median 4.7 days before eligibility assessment.

- **Consistency of routing**

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate four things that are often conflated:

1. **Disparity**: an observed difference between groups.  
2. **Legitimate routing**: differences required by policy, risk, language, or accommodation needs.  
3. **Discrimination or unfair differential treatment**: differences that remain after legitimate factors and that reflect bias, inadequate resourcing, or inaccessible procedure.  
4. **Uncertainty**: statistical noise, model misspecification, measurement error, and unobserved confounding.

## Matching and stratification variables

Use a layered comparison rather than a single adjusted model.

- **Core observable complexity controls**  
  Risk score band, documentation completeness, channel, household size band, prior-case count, region, and month. These help distinguish simple cases from complex cases.

- **Rights-preserving need variables**  
  Language preference, language support need, and disability accommodation request. These should be used carefully. They are not merely confounders; they are legitimate needs. Controlling for them can answer: “Among applicants with the same support need, does one group receive worse process?” Not controlling for them can answer: “Is the support step itself creating unequal burden?”

- **Process-complexity controls**  
  Variant ID, number of activities, number of handovers, supervisor review, fraud review, request missing documents, and rework. These should not be used to “adjust away” unfair delays caused by under-resourced support steps. Instead, they should be analyzed as outcomes or mediators.

- **Suggested nested models**  
  1. Raw group differences.  
  2. Adjusted for risk, documentation, channel, household size, prior cases, region, and month.  
  3. Additionally adjusted for language/accommodation needs to test whether support needs explain disparities.  
  4. Excluding or instrumenting risk score and documentation completeness to test whether those controls mask unfairness.  
  5. Interaction models by region and channel to identify local capacity effects.

## Path and variant comparison

Use variants to compare procedural burden conditional on case characteristics.

- Compute variant frequencies overall and within matched strata.
- Compare the share of low-burden V1 versus high-burden V2, V3, V4, V5, and V6.
- For each variant, compare median and 90th percentile cycle time by group.
- Test whether two cases with the same risk band, documentation completeness, channel, region, and support need follow different paths.
- Use performance DFGs to compare transition-level waiting times, especially around Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, Fraud Review, Supervisor Review, and Request Missing Documents.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Resource assignment should be analyzed as a fairness dimension, not only as an operational metric.

- Compare handover networks by group: general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker for Group B, and general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor for Group C.
- Compare working-together networks to identify whether support staff are isolated or integrated.
- Compare activity-based resource similarity to see whether similar activities are performed by similar staff across groups.
- Compute resource concentration, such as the share of cases handled by the top 10% of caseworkers for each group.
- Compare workload per resource, including queue length, rework, supervisor review, and appeals, not only daily activity counts.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Total cycle time should be decomposed into:

- **Service time**: active work by staff.
- **Internal queue time**: waiting for a resource, bilingual staff, accommodation specialist, fraud reviewer, or supervisor.
- **Applicant response time**: waiting for documents or applicant action.
- **Handover time**: waiting between organizational units.
- **Rework time**: additional cycles caused by errors, incomplete information, or appeal.

PM4Py-style waiting-time insertion can help separate case service waiting from total duration. The key fairness question is whether delays are caused by legitimate complexity or by capacity constraints in rights-preserving steps.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

Some controls may be contaminated by the very disparities being studied.

- **Risk score sensitivity**  
  If risk_score_band encodes historical over-surveillance or prior contact, controlling for it may understate unfairness. Test results with and without risk score, and with alternative measures such as confirmed fraud rate, manual override rate, or appeal success rate.

- **Documentation completeness sensitivity**  
  Documentation completeness may be affected by language barriers, digital access, in-person intake quality, or unclear requirements. Test whether adjusted disparities change when documentation completeness is excluded, measured as a latent variable, or audited using rework and appeal outcomes.

- **Model uncertainty**  
  Use bootstrap confidence intervals, quantile regression for cycle time, survival analysis for time-to-decision, and multiple imputation for missing values. Report practical significance, not only statistical significance.

- **E-value or sensitivity threshold**  
  Estimate how strong an unobserved confounder would need to be to explain the adjusted approval gap for Group B, the cycle-time gap for Group C, or the appeal gap for Group D.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

The summary notes backlog in two regions and longer waits for in-person applications. Therefore, compare:

- Group by region.
- Group by channel.
- Region × channel.
- Region × support need.
- Region × risk band.
- Region × supervisor review.
- Region × language/accommodation queue.

This can reveal whether disparities are national process effects or local capacity effects.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available attributes are useful but insufficient for a definitive fairness conclusion.

- **Risk score may encode historical bias**  
  risk_score_band cannot be treated as unquestionably neutral. It may reflect prior contact, past enforcement patterns, geographic surveillance, or proxies for protected characteristics. If the score is biased, then controlling for it may hide unfair routing rather than remove it.

- **Documentation completeness may be affected by language and access**  
  documentation_completeness may measure not only applicant behavior but also intake quality, clarity of requirements, digital access, in-person assistance, and interpreter availability. Group B’s higher request-documents rate could reflect genuine missing information, language barriers, or inconsistent completeness standards.

- **Language and accommodation attributes capture requests, not needs or quality**  
  language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested show whether support was requested, but not the severity of need, the quality of support, whether the accommodation was effective, or whether the applicant understood the process.

- **Outcome data do not capture dignity, explanation, or decision quality**  
  case_outcome records approval or denial, but not whether the decision was well explained, whether the applicant could understand the notice, whether the process was respectful, or whether the decision was substantively correct.

- **Appeal flag does not capture appeal merit or success**  
  appeal_flag shows whether an appeal occurred, not whether the appeal was granted, whether the original decision was wrong, or whether the appeal process was accessible. High appeal rates may signal bad decisions, but they may also signal more active advocacy or easier appeal access.

- **Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity**  
  Daily resource counts do not reveal queue length, overtime, case difficulty, staff turnover, interpreter quality, specialist availability, or whether a caseworker is overloaded. This makes it difficult to separate legitimate workload effects from unfair resource allocation.

- **Timestamps may mix internal and applicant waiting**  
  The log may not distinguish waiting for bilingual staff from waiting for the applicant to respond to a document request. Without decomposition, it is hard to assign responsibility for delays.

- **Adjusted models rely on causal assumptions**  
  The adjusted differences are not automatically causal estimates. They assume the controls are not themselves biased, not mediators of unfairness, and not colliders. Because some controls may be contaminated by language barriers or historical risk scoring, the adjusted results require sensitivity analysis.

- **Small subgroups create uncertainty**  
  Group C has 820 cases and Group D has 980 cases. Subgroup estimates, especially by region and channel, may be noisy. Confidence intervals and practical significance are needed.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigations should preserve rights-preserving steps. The goal is not to remove language support or accommodation review to improve cycle time, but to make those steps timely, accessible, and well resourced.

| Mitigation option | How it addresses fairness | Possible side effects or trade-offs |

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence suggests that the process is **not clearly procedurally fair after controlling for observable case complexity**, but the evidence is **not sufficient to conclude intentional discrimination or a single causal mechanism**.

What can be inferred is that Groups B, C, and D experience different procedural burdens. Group B has the largest adjusted approval gap and strong evidence of language-support-related delay, handovers, and additional document requests. Group C has the largest adjusted cycle-time gap, with accommodation-review queues and supervisor handovers creating burden. Group D has a smaller cycle-time gap but a large adjusted appeal gap and heavy fraud-review routing, which may be legitimate if risk scores are valid but is concerning if risk scores encode historical disparities.

What cannot be inferred is whether these adjusted differences are caused by bias, by unmeasured applicant needs, by inadequate support capacity, by measurement error in risk and documentation variables, or by legitimate policy routing. The event log also cannot show whether language support and accommodation review were delivered with adequate quality, dignity, or accessibility.

A calibrated conclusion is: **the process shows concerning procedural disparities, especially in timeliness, handover burden, and resource allocation for Groups B and C, and in appeal and risk-routing burden for Group D. These disparities are not explained solely by case mix. However, the evidence is indeterminate regarding discrimination and requires targeted audits of risk scores, documentation completeness, support-step quality, regional capacity, and decision explanations before stronger causal claims can be made.**
