When utilization approaches capacity, ordinary variation in arrivals, case effort, and staff availability can increase waiting sharply. # 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Use originating team and characteristics recorded at intake for comparisons. Report volumes, denominators, and a consistent follow-up window. Show subgroup results alongside the aggregate, including intersections such as East high-complexity cases and cases with a scarce specialty. Use the process event log for activity, resource, path, and elapsed-gap measures. Add queue lifecycle events for direct wait measurement, staffing data for utilization, and outcome and survey data where the log has no corresponding fields.

| Leading · daily or weekly | Queue and pickup delays | Measure median, p90, and p95 from queue entry to work start and from advice ready to local acknowledgment. With completion timestamps alone, report sequential completion gaps and label them as elapsed gaps. |

| Leading · weekly | Ownership and exception handling | Measure ownerless hours, overdue acceptances or escalations, wrong-queue returns, and reassignment reasons. Check whether approvals and returns identify a named accountable person. |

| Leading · weekly | Access and fairness | Compare routing rates among eligible cases, relative queue age and pickup delay, and accommodation-related transfers by team, priority, complexity, specialty, and segment. |

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency.** A fast path for simple cases can lower the mean while p90, p95, and overdue open work rise. Closing easy cases first also makes a dashboard of completed cases look better while the oldest cases remain open.
2. **High-complexity cases.** Mandated routing can add a technical review, a specialist review, and a shared approval to the same case. Examine queue time and repeat loops for high-complexity, multi-review variants alongside their SLA outcomes.
3. **Local knowledge loss.** A central reviewer can complete a task quickly while missing a regional convention, prompting avoidable contact, disputes, or rework later. Review case notes and check quality after implementation.
4. **Fairness.** East can gain while North or South loses service quality. A team-level gain can also conceal worse access for a customer segment, including people needing language or regional accommodation. Report actual outcomes and case-mix-adjusted comparisons, and inspect queue priority and transfer reasons.
5. **Learning.** Fewer difficult technical cases handled locally can erode judgment and future backup capacity. Monitor local practice, paired reviews, and assessed capability as well as current output.
6. **Accountability.** Advice or approval may be recorded as complete while nobody accepts the next action. Track named ownership, acknowledgment delay, and escalation responsibility through implementation.
7. **Exception handling.** Standard paths can accelerate while unusual cases cycle through reassignment or disappear into informal workarounds. Inspect rare variants, return reasons, and sampled staff accounts.
8. **Resilience.** A pooled queue can handle routine absences better while making every team vulnerable to the same outage or scarce-skill absence. Measure service during disruptions and the time required to clear accumulated work.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Scope and sequencing.** Pilot a staffed Technical Shared Service for a limited share of eligible cases from East, North, and South, with a named local owner throughout each case. Preserve staffed local capacity for controls and fallback. Add triage, difficult quality checks, specialty reviews, and shared approvals in measured stages so their incremental workload is visible. Confirm authorized backup and capacity for Legal/Risk/Security before adding volume. Pilot approvals with local supervisor cover still available. Test the proposed staffing level under controlled load before removing capacity.
- **Comparison.** Randomly assign eligible incoming cases within team and routing strata to pilot or existing service where both paths are operationally feasible. Use protected staffing blocks and track staffing, spillovers, and exceptions in both arms. If individual assignment is impractical, stagger comparable routing cohorts and compare changes with contemporaneous cohorts still using the existing model. Check pre-pilot trends and record other changes, including automation and staffing. Analyze cases by assigned path for the primary comparison and record actual paths to explain deviations.
- **Window.** Use about 8–12 weeks of baseline intake cohorts, followed by a short instrumentation check and 6–8 weeks of pilot intake. Follow both arms until the relevant SLA deadlines and rework observation windows mature. Set sample requirements for meaningful differences in advance and extend observation for sparse specialty or segment groups. Report open work by age throughout.
- **Segmentation.** Stratify by originating team, intake complexity, required specialty, and customer segment. Also report priority and approval eligibility, accounting for case value if it governs routing. Review high-risk intersections and preserve confidentiality for small groups. Where feasible, record accommodation needs explicitly. Review both the effect of who gets routed and the experience after routing.
- **Guardrails and triggers.** Set tolerances before launch for p90 queue and cycle times, aged backlog, SLA misses, rework and audit defects, unowned cases, and subgroup gaps. Illustrative triggers are p90 queue wait more than 20% above the comparable control for two sufficiently populated weekly cohorts, or an SLA miss rate more than 2 percentage points above control in a mature cohort. Apply guardrails to East, North, South, and priority subgroups as well as the whole pilot. Investigate immediately if inflow persistently exceeds completions and backlog ages, or if a critical case loses ownership or breaches a mandatory deadline. Pause new pilot routing for the affected stage or group, reinstate local coverage or rebalance capacity, reconcile open cases under named owners, and resume after backlog and service recover.
- **Staff feedback.** Collect brief weekly input from local caseworkers, specialists, supervisors, and implementation staff. Review a sample of cases with missing context, workarounds, escalations, or accommodation needs with the people who handled them. Ask about informal advice, decision ownership, workload, and learning opportunities. Compare feedback with log observations and use periodic case reviews to assess local capability.
- **Scale test.** Increase demand in steps only while capacity and guardrails hold. Test realistic coincident demand and a specialist absence with backup in place. A lightly loaded pilot provides limited evidence about waiting under full demand.

# 6. Recommendation

Pilot a modified shared-service model first. Pool technical capacity with a named local owner, referral notes that capture regional and customer needs, a clear acknowledgment step, and designated backup by skill. Preserve local consultation and paired reviews. Gate additional specialist work and supervisor capacity changes on demonstrated capacity and clear responsibility for approval and implementation.

Expand if East shows sustained relief under representative demand and the pilot meets predefined limits on tail delays, SLA misses, rework, and service for North, South, and affected customer segments. Require evidence that backlog recovers from disruptions, advice reaches the owner promptly, and local staff retain opportunities to develop technical capability. Adjust scope, routing, or staffing where these conditions fail.

An early improvement in the average can reflect faster easy cases, a light initial load, or open complex cases that have yet to enter the completed-case statistic. A rise in utilization measures occupancy, and a drop in ad hoc transfers measures only one type of handover. A rollout decision needs mature cohort outcomes, queues tested at representative loads, and subgroup results alongside those early measures.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

Python-style pseudocode. The PM4Py calls illustrate the analysis, and the named helpers describe the surrounding preparation and calculations. Use a stable case ID, unchanged resource IDs, a consistent mapping of activities, and a known within-case sequence for tied timestamps. If tie order is unknown, check how excluding ambiguous transitions changes the results.

~~~python
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
END = "time:timestamp"

# Build one record per intake case with assigned arm, intake time, and
# baseline case attributes. Keep the origin team even after transfer.
cases = build_intake_cohorts(events, pilot_assignment)
events = attach_case_fields_to_events(events, cases, case_id=CASE)

def prepare(frame):
    frame = order_by_case_timestamp_and_source_sequence(frame)
    return pm4py.format_dataframe(
        frame, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACTIVITY, timestamp_key=END
    )

def segment(log, **case_fields):
    # These fields were frozen at intake and copied onto every event.
    for field, value in case_fields.items():
        log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            log, attribute_key=field, values=[value], level="case"
        )
    return log

def maps(log):
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    performance_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    return {
        "handover": pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log),
        "working_together": pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log),
        "subcontracting": pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log),
        "variants": variants,
        "dfg": (dfg, starts, ends),
        "performance_dfg": performance_dfg,
        "resource_similarity": pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log),
        "roles": pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log),
    }

def measures(log, cohort_cases, as_of):
    result = {
        "transfers": count_consecutive_transfers_and_returns(
            log, case_denominator=len(cohort_cases)
        ),
        "elapsed_gaps": consecutive_completion_gap_quantiles(log),
        "cycle": cycle_time_and_open_case_age(log, cohort_cases, as_of),
        "repeat_activity": pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log) if len(log) else {},
        "flagged_rework": share_of_mature_cases_with_rework(log, cohort_cases),
    }

    if validated_activity_intervals_available(log):
        result["activity_service"] = pm4py.get_service_time(
            log, start_timestamp_key="work_started_at", timestamp_key=END
        )
        result["case_timing"] = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
            log, start_timestamp_key="work_started_at", timestamp_key=END
        )

    if validated_queue_lifecycle_available(log):
        result["queue_timing"] = queue_waits_and_open_ages_by_stage(log, as_of)

    if official_sla_clocks_available(cohort_cases):
        result["sla"] = sla_rate_for_cases_due_by(cohort_cases, as_of)

    return result

for arm in ("before", "pilot", "control"):
    cohort_cases, as_of = select_input_cohort(cases, arm)
    # Apply comparable follow-up. Keep observed prefixes of open cases.
    log = prepare(events_for_cases_through(events, cohort_cases, as_of))
    save_overall(arm, maps(log), measures(log, cohort_cases, as_of))

    for group in reporting_strata:
        # For example, origin_team, intake_complexity, intake_specialty,
        # and intake_customer_segment, singly or in combination.
        group_cases = select_case_rows(cohort_cases, **group)
        if len(group_cases) > 0:
            part = segment(log, **group)
            save_group(arm, group, maps(part) if len(part) else {},
                       measures(part, group_cases, as_of))

    if target_variants_present(log):
        exception_traces = pm4py.filter_variants(log, target_variants(log))
        inspect_exception_traces(exception_traces)

compare_pilot_vs_control_with_baseline(saved_results)
~~~

Use the handover network to locate transfers between people, working-together ties to show participation in the same cases, and subcontracting patterns to locate work that returns to an earlier resource. Verify from traces whether a return was a planned consultation or avoidable reassignment. Compare network changes with independently counted edges per 100 intake cases, stratified by routing eligibility where relevant. Use both stable person-level IDs and a documented roll-up to organizational units. Report automated and human transitions separately without deleting events and creating new adjacencies. Use resource similarity and discovered roles to compare observed activity profiles, then validate decision authority and cross-cover skills with assignment records and staff.

The supplied timestamp supports elapsed gaps and observed case spans under a known event convention. Performance DFGs show where those gaps sit. Compare gap quantiles and distinguish open prefixes from completed variants. True service and waiting times require validated activity starts and ends or queue events. Record referral, queue entry, acceptance, work start, completion, and pickup, with a work-item ID for repeated referrals. Check how the timing calculation handles parallel work. For elapsed case service, count overlapping intervals once, and keep queue-specific waits separate. Use temporal features or arrival and completion counts by shift to diagnose peaks. Estimate utilization with workload and staffing records.

Count flagged rework once per case over a common follow-up window and use repeated-activity counts to investigate specific steps. Derive SLA misses from the official eligibility, start, deadline, pause, and stop rules, joining the needed policy or case data. An eligible case open past its applicable deadline counts as a miss where the SLA rules specify one. The supplied fields alone cannot establish that deadline. Keep cases still under observation in the backlog and cycle-time analysis, and compare the pilot and control using equivalent follow-up and case-mix reporting.
