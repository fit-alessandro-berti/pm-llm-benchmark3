# 1. Fairness dimensions

Evaluating procedural fairness in public-benefits case management requires moving beyond raw approval rates to examine how applicants experience the process, how administrative burdens are distributed, and whether institutional resources are allocated equitably across legitimate needs. Eight interdependent dimensions are central here:

- **Outcome parity (unadjusted):** Compares raw approval rates across groups (Group A: 78%, Group C: 69%, Group B: 63%, Group D: 58%). While large raw gaps (up to 20 percentage points) signal a need for investigation, they cannot be equated with discrimination because statutory eligibility, documentation status, and risk flags differ substantially across groups.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** Evaluates whether approval gaps persist among observably similar cases. Controlling for risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior cases, region, and month narrows the gaps substantially, but meaningful residual disparities remain—most notably for Group B (-5.5 percentage points), followed by Group D (-3.1 pp) and Group C (-2.2 pp).
- **Procedural burden:** Measures the administrative friction imposed on applicants (extra steps, document requests, handovers, and secondary reviews). Even after controlling for recorded case characteristics, Group B faces substantially higher rates of `Request Missing Documents` (+9.5 pp) and `Supervisor Review` (+8.0 pp), and Group C also experiences elevated `Supervisor Review` (+5.5 pp) and `Request Missing Documents` (+4.2 pp).
- **Timeliness:** Captures both central tendency and tail delays. Raw median cycle times are 50%–96% longer for Groups B–D than for Group A, and 90th-percentile delays reach 34.2 days (Group B) and 38.8 days (Group C) versus 21.4 days (Group A). Adjusted median delays remain substantial for Group C (+4.1 days) and Group B (+2.6 days), indicating that waiting-time penalties are not explained solely by case mix.
- **Consistency of routing:** Assesses whether cases with identical policy triggers follow the same decision paths. Legitimate policy rules explain higher `Fraud Review` routing for Group D (V3: 28%), `Language Support Contact` for Group B (V4: 24%), and `Accommodation Review` for Group C (V5: 31%). However, consistency is compromised if rights-preserving steps systematically trigger extra downstream scrutiny (e.g., V5 routing accommodation cases through `Supervisor Review`) or extra handovers only in certain regions.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** Examines whether rights-preserving steps (`Language Support Contact`, `Accommodation Review`, in-person intake) function effectively without penalizing applicants who invoke them. Currently, invoking language or accommodation support incurs multi-day specialist queues without dedicated service-level protections, turning statutory rights into a procedural bottleneck.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Reflects the frequency with which applicants must contest decisions or endure repeated processing. Rework/reopen rates are roughly double for Groups B–D (20%–24% vs. 11% for Group A). Adjusted appeal probabilities remain significantly elevated for Group D (+5.7 pp) and Group B (+4.4 pp), suggesting heightened dispute rates, error correction burdens, or poor decision communication.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Assesses whether staffing capacity and organizational design match demand across applicant populations. # 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To separate **observed disparity**, **legitimate policy-driven routing**, **structural/procedural unfairness**, **potential discriminatory treatment**, and **statistical/measurement uncertainty**, the evaluation should use a multi-stage analytical strategy rather than relying on a single regression.

### Matching and stratification variables
- **Core legitimate routing strata:** Stratify first on variables that directly trigger statutory or policy workflows: `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `disability_accommodation_requested`, and `language_preference` (or documented language-support need).
- **Operational and context controls:** Match or weight (e.g., coarsened exact matching or inverse probability weighting with overlap checks) on `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and calendar `month` (to absorb seasonal backlogs).
- **Support-step separation:** Compare outcomes and cycle times *within* comparable support strata (e.g., Group B vs. Group A among applicants who do *not* require language support; Group C vs. Group A among non-accommodation cases) to isolate disparities unrelated to specialist queues from disparities created by the support queues themselves.

### Path and variant comparison
- Compare **conditional variant distributions** within identical strata (e.g., same `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`) to test whether certain groups are routed more frequently into `Request Missing Documents`, `Supervisor Review`, or `Fraud Review` than policy rules warrant.
- Separate **policy-mandated branching** (e.g., `Accommodation Review` when requested) from **discretionary or redundant loops** (e.g., repeated `Request Missing Documents`, avoidable `Supervisor Review`, or `Appeal Intake` leading to `Reopen`).
- Use process conformance/declarative checks to test whether procedural rules (e.g., mandatory sequence constraints, exclusion rules, or maximum rework loops) are violated at different rates across groups.

### Resource-assignment comparison
- Evaluate whether group membership predicts assignment to specific worker pools, high-backlog workers, or multi-hop handover chains *conditional on case requirements*.
- Measure **handover intensity** (number of distinct `org:resource` transitions per case), **ping-pong handovers** (e.g., General  Bilingual  General), and **role overlap** (cases where an accommodation specialist also acts as supervisor reviewer).
- Estimate caseworker fixed-effect or mixed-effect models to separate **within-caseworker disparities** (same worker treating groups differently) from **between-caseworker / between-queue sorting** (groups routed to under-resourced queues).

### Waiting-time decomposition
- Decompose total case duration into:
  1. **Active service time** (processing time within activities, where start/complete timestamps exist),
  2. **Legitimate intake/external waiting time** (e.g., applicant time to return missing documents),
  3. **Support-queue waiting time** (delays entering `Language Support Contact` or `Accommodation Review`), and
  4. **General/downstream administrative queuing** (e.g., `Submit  Identity Verification`, `Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents`, `Supervisor Review  Decision Draft`).
- Compute counterfactual cycle times (e.g., Kitagawa–Oaxaca–Blinder or step-level substitution) to quantify how much of the Group B and C delay comes from specialist queues versus general administrative queues versus extra steps.

### Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates
Because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may themselves encode structural disparities, controlling for them naively risks **over-control (mediator) bias**, while omitting them risks **omitted-variable bias**.
- **Nested model bounding:** Estimate disparities under three specifications: (1) demographic + region/month/household size only; (2) adding `channel` and `prior_case_count`; (3) adding `documentation_completeness` and `risk_score_band`. Comparing how group coefficients shift reveals how much disparity is mediated through potentially biased risk/completeness scores.
- **Measurement-error and bias simulation:** Conduct quantitative bias analysis (e.g., simulating differential misclassification where 10%–25% of Group B's "incomplete" flags reflect language friction rather than missing eligibility facts, or where Group D's risk scores are systematically shifted upward by one band) to bound true adjusted disparities.
- **Unobserved confounding sensitivity:** Report E-values or Rosenbaum bounds for residual adjusted gaps (such as Group B's -5.5 pp approval gap and Group D's +5.7 pp appeal gap) to quantify how strong an unobserved eligibility factor would need to be to explain them away.

### Subgroup checks by region and channel
- **Region × Group stratification:** Test whether disparities concentrate in the two backlogged regions with ping-pong handovers and overloaded regional officers, or occur system-wide. Regional concentration points toward local capacity/management failures rather than uniform policy bias.
- **Channel × Group stratification:** Compare online vs. in-person applicants within each group to distinguish an **in-person channel penalty** (which disproportionately affects Group B at 38% and Group C at 24%) from language- or disability-specific treatment.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Multiple mechanisms—some legitimate, others structurally or procedurally problematic—could jointly generate these patterns:

1. **Legitimate statutory complexity and multi-step verification (Legitimate mechanism):**
   Cases involving higher household complexity, genuine documentation gaps, prior benefit histories, disability accommodations, or verified risk indicators legitimately require additional administrative steps (`Fraud Review`, `Accommodation Review`, `Language Support Contact`, `Request Missing Documents`). Even in an ideally resourced system, additional mandatory steps add modest service time and some variance in approval rates if true underlying eligibility differs across groups.
2. **Under-resourced specialist queues and missing SLA governance (Concerning structural mechanism):**
   Allocating 52 caseworkers to majority-language general processing but only 7 bilingual workers and 5 accommodation specialists—combined with omitting support steps from SLA monitoring—creates chronic queuing bottlenecks. Rights-preserving steps inadvertently become an "access penalty," lengthening cycle times, increasing applicant drop-off during document requests, and lowering effective approval rates for Groups B and C.
3. **Fragmented routing and ping-pong handovers (Concerning organizational mechanism):**
   In the two backlogged regions, splitting ownership between general caseworkers, bilingual workers, accommodation specialists, and supervisors creates repeated queue re-entry (General  Specialist  General/Supervisor). Every handover adds waiting time, context loss, and risk of inconsistent document requests—explaining why Group B has +9.5 pp adjusted `Request Missing Documents` and why rework/reopen rates double (22%–24% vs. 11%).
4. **Endogenous measurement and communication barriers (Concerning measurement/accessibility mechanism):**
   Online forms and missing-document notices designed for majority-language, non-disabled applicants can make eligible Group B and C applicants appear "incomplete" at intake (`documentation_completeness`). Once flagged, language barriers delay clarification (`Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents` takes 1.6 days for Group B), causing missed deadlines or repeated document requests that depress adjusted approval rates (-5.5 pp for Group B) and drive appeals (+4.4 pp).
5. **Risk-averse escalation and conflation of accommodation/language needs with case risk (Concerning discretionary mechanism):**
   Caseworkers unfamiliar with minority-language documentation or accommodation protocols may defensively escalate cases to `Supervisor Review` (+8.0 pp for Group B, +5.5 pp for Group C), or routing rules (as suggested by V5) may automatically couple `Accommodation Review` with `Supervisor Review`. Where accommodation specialists double as supervisors, this adds delay without necessarily adding independent quality assurance.
6. **Historical feedback loops and opaque denials in risk-based routing (Concerning algorithmic/procedural mechanism):**
   Group D's high `risk_score_band` (51%) and `prior_case_count` (49%) may reflect historical scrutiny or socioeconomic instability rather than actual fraud propensity. Heavy routing into `Fraud Review` (28% in V3), if paired with adversarial scrutiny or poorly explained denials, could trigger false-positive denials and push Group D applicants into `Appeal Intake` (+5.7 pp adjusted) to vindicate valid claims.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event log and tabular indicators cannot establish definitive proof of discriminatory intent or fully validate procedural fairness due to several critical data limitations:

- **Measurement bias in `documentation_completeness`:** This attribute is recorded *within* the process, not objectively observed prior to agency interaction. Poorly translated instructions, inaccessible web portals, or rushed in-person intake can cause eligible Group B and C applicants to be coded as "incomplete." Controlling for it may therefore mask accessibility failures rather than adjust for exogenous case complexity.
- **Historical and structural bias in `risk_score_band`:** The risk score cannot be treated as a neutral ground-truth measure of fraud or ineligibility. If the scoring model uses `prior_case_count`, regional proxies, or historical enforcement outcomes (where certain groups were scrutinized more heavily in the past), it embeds feedback loops. Adjusting for `risk_score_band` may conceal unfairness affecting Group D.
- **Missing applicant needs and unrecorded eligibility facts:** The log does not observe true statutory eligibility, literacy/digital literacy, severity or type of disability, exact language dialect (which may not match the 7 bilingual workers' languages), or unrequested needs (applicants who needed language or accommodation support but did not know how to request it or gave up). It also lacks timestamps separating *agency waiting time* from *applicant response time* during `Request Missing Documents`.
- **Coarse resource workload data:** Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. Without hourly/queue-depth logs, task-complexity weights, caseworker tenure/training, or active start/complete lifecycle timestamps (`lifecycle:transition`), it is impossible to distinguish whether a delay stems from instantaneous queue congestion, slow active processing, multitasking across roles (e.g., specialists doing supervisor reviews), or deliberate deprioritization.
- **Missing appeal outcomes, quality, and dignity metrics:** `appeal_flag` and `case_outcome` do not tell us *who wins on appeal* (overturn rate), why appeals were filed, or how many wrongfully denied applicants *failed to appeal* due to exhaustion, language barriers, or lack of clear denial explanations. Crucial procedural fairness elements—explanation clarity, respectful treatment, consistency of legal reasoning, and perceived legitimacy—are entirely unobserved.

# Compare group shares in slow_doc_req_cases vs. overall log

    log, min_performance=30 * 86400, max_performance=365 * 86400

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Waiting Time, Service Time, and Case Duration Analysis
# ---------------------------------------------------------
# Overall case durations (seconds -> days) per group
duration_stats = {}
for g in groups:
    g_log = filter_by_case_attrs(log, {"applicant_group": [g]})
    durations_days = np.array(pm4py.get_all_case_durations(g_log)) / 86400.0
    duration_stats[g] = {
        "median_d": round(float(np.median(durations_days)), 2),
        "p90_d": round(float(np.percentile(durations_days, 90)), 2),

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

Based on the available evidence, the process exhibits **clear evidence of structural procedural unfairness (inequitable administrative burden and timeliness)**—particularly for **Group B** and **Group C**—while the question of **discriminatory decision-making or unlawful bias in final eligibility outcomes remains indeterminate**.

Specifically:
- **Where procedural unfairness is strongly supported:** Even after controlling for recorded complexity, region, channel, and risk band, Group B and Group C experience materially slower processing (+2.6 and +4.1 adjusted median days; 34–39 days at the 90th percentile), higher adjusted rates of `Supervisor Review` (+8.0 pp and +5.5 pp) and `Request Missing Documents` (+9.5 pp and +4.2 pp), ping-pong handovers, and severe specialist bottlenecks (3.8–4.7 day waits) without SLA protections. In effect, applicants who exercise statutory rights to language support or disability accommodations pay a substantial procedural penalty driven by under-resourced staffing and fragmented routing.
- **Where major concerns require urgent audit (currently indeterminate as to root cause):** Group B's residual **-5.5 pp** adjusted approval gap, Group D's **+5.7 pp** adjusted appeal gap (and -3.1 pp adjusted approval gap), and Group D's heavy fraud-review routing (28% in V3) cannot be dismissed as benign case-mix differences, nor can they be conclusively labeled intentional discrimination. Because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may themselves embed historical or accessibility bias—and because appeal overturn rates, true eligibility, and active vs. waiting timestamps are unobserved—determining whether these outcome and appeal gaps stem from biased measurement, poor communication, unequal scrutiny, or unobserved eligibility differences requires targeted case audits and the stratified PM4Py diagnostics outlined above.

In short, the agency does not need to wait for proof of discriminatory intent to act: the resource, handover, and waiting-time disparities in rights-preserving workflows are already well-documented procedural deficiencies that warrant immediate capacity, routing, and SLA reforms alongside a formal audit of risk scoring, documentation coding, and appeal outcomes.
