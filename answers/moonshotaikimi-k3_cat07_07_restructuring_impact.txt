With flat capacity, pooled utilization lands near 84–85% — matching management's "+15% utilization" target. In queueing terms, mean waiting scales roughly with /(1): moving  from 0.74 to 0.85 approximately **doubles** the congestion factor. A median wait of 3.2h becoming 5–7h (not the assumed 4.0h) is the arithmetic center of gravity.

**Legal/Risk is the sharpest red flag.** These specialists run at 91–93% utilization and the plan raises demand from 9 to 12 per 100 cases (+33%) with no stated capacity increase. That implies  > 1 — an unstable, growing queue. The 10.5h estimate is not credible without added capacity or much shorter service times.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Indicative likelihoods (judgmental): optimistic ~20%, central ~55%, adverse ~25%.

### Optimistic scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Hub-and-spoke stabilizes. TSS edges at ~41/36 per 100 cases as planned; cross-team genuinely falls to 3/100 with standardized case notes; no hidden workarounds appear in the working-together network.
- **Queue/waiting:** TSS median wait holds at ~4h; pooling absorbs absence peaks; Legal/Risk demand growth is offset by better triage cutting service time ~25%, keeping waits near 10–11h.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East median improves 8.6d  ~7.3d; North/South roughly flat (±0.2d); overall p90 improves slightly, driven by East's tail.
- **Rework/quality:** Standardized QC and better routing cut rework ~1–1.5pp overall; complex-case rework stable in North/South because informal advice is partially preserved via a liaison arrangement.
- **Role clarity/accountability:** Approval queue decisions carry named-decision-maker attribution; ping-pong variants <2% of cases.
- **Fairness:** Inter-team gap narrows (East converges up); no segment degraded.
- **Early warning signs even here:** Legal/Risk queue depth; whether North/South referral rates start creeping after month 3.

### Central scenario (most likely)

- **Handover pattern:** Total handovers up ~5%; specialist edges at 41–45/100 (referral creep begins); cross-team nominally 3/100 but subcontracting network shows informal cross-team consultations re-emerging unlogged.
- **Queue/waiting:** TSS median wait 5–7h (utilization ~85%, congestion factor ~2× baseline); Legal/Risk waits 14–20h as  approaches 1; approval queue ~3–4h.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** **Median roughly flat overall (0.2 to +0.3d) — and misleading.** East median improves 0.8–1.2d; North/South medians worsen 0.4–0.8d; high-complexity p90 worsens +2–4d; simple cases get slightly faster and dominate the average.
- **Rework/quality:** Overall rework flat; complex-case rework in North/South +2–3pp (lost informal advice); East rework 1–2pp.
- **Role clarity/accountability:** Approval ping-pong variants 3–6% of cases; `reassignment_count` up; `handover_reason` = "unclear ownership" appears; follow-up after pulled advice occasionally orphaned (Local Customer Contact loops).
- **Fairness:** Team gap narrows (good), but customer-segment gap widens: regional/language-accommodation cases accumulate +1–2 handovers and longer tails.
- **Early warning signs:** TSS queue depth trending up week-over-week; specialist off-hours activity; referral rate +10–15% by month 4–6; North/South complex-case rework; rising share of "queue" resources in approval events.

### Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Legal/Risk queue unstable (>1): waits 2–5 days, SLA misses concentrate in legal/risk/security cases. TSS saturates during absence/peak windows — the single point of failure materializes. Nominal cross-team handovers stay at 3/100 while hidden cross-team work explodes in the working-together/subcontracting networks.
- **Queue/waiting:** TSS p90 wait >24h; queue abandonment behaviors (caseworkers chasing, re-assigning) visible as `reassignment_count` spikes.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Overall median +0.5–1.0d; North/South p90 +4–6d; high-complexity tail +5d or more; East's early gains reverse because it is most dependent on the shared queue.
- **Rework/quality:** Rework +3–5pp overall; misclassified-complexity gaming appears (cases downgraded to dodge the queue — visible as a shift in the `case_complexity` distribution); quality escapes rise as difficult QC queues.
- **Role clarity/accountability:** Accountability vacuum: contested approvals, re-approvals logged as new work rather than rework; ownership disputes between caseworkers and queue; supervisor removal leaves no escalation path.
- **Fairness:** North/South degrade toward or below East's old baseline; vulnerable segments hit hardest; perceived unfairness drives attrition risk among local senior staff.
- **Early warning signs:** Shared-queue WIP growing for 2+ consecutive weeks; specialist overtime/attrition signals; variant entropy rising; `local_expertise_flag` collapse; referral creep >25%; after-hours event share climbing.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency.** Queues produce heavy right tails. Median and mean can hold while p90/p95 degrade sharply, because the congestion factor /(1) hits the tail first and hardest. Only percentile-stratified performance DFGs and cycle-time distributions reveal this.
2. **High-complexity cases.** They are a minority of volume, so the average is dominated by simple cases that may genuinely speed up. Mandatory TSS routing adds queue time precisely to the cases that can least absorb it. An "improved average" can coexist with every high-complexity case getting slower.
3. **Local knowledge loss.** The informal advice channel (caseworker walks to N_Tech's desk) is *not in the event log today* — its disappearance is invisible directly. It surfaces months later as referral creep and complex-case rework in North/South, long after an "average cycle time improved" verdict has been declared.
4. **Fairness redistribution.** East's plausible gain (1d median) can arithmetically cancel North/South's plausible loss (+0.5d each). A flat enterprise average would then describe a process that got better for one team and worse for two — and worse for customer segments needing regional/language accommodation. Averages conceal *who pays*.
5. **Learning and capability erosion.** Flow metrics measure today's cases, not tomorrow's capacity. If local staff stop handling hard cases, referral rates rise over 6–18 months, increasing load on the shared service — a slow feedback loop that no point-in-time average captures. The organization can look healthier while becoming more fragile.
6. **Accountability and decision quality.** Shared approval queues anonymize decisions. Contested or reversed approvals may be logged as *new* activities rather than rework, inflating activity counts without flagging quality problems. Average cycle time hides decision latency, reversal rates, and the escalation vacuum left by the removed supervisor.
7. **Exception handling and hidden work.** Nominal cross-team handovers can fall to 3/100 while actual cross-team *work* rises through unlogged chats, calls, and pull-queue browsing. Absence cover may look solved until the first specialist vacation or sick leave hits a queue running at 85–93% utilization. The working-together and subcontracting networks, plus off-hours temporal features, are the only log-based windows into this.
8. **Gaming and measurement drift.** If routing rules are strict, staff may reclassify complexity or specialty to avoid queues. The average then "improves" partly because the case-mix labels changed, not because the process did.

# 5. Pilot design

**Pilot scope.** Pilot the **Technical Shared Service only**, starting with **East** (highest expected benefit: E_Tech at 82%, slowest cycles). Explicitly *exclude* from the pilot: (a) folding Legal/Risk into the shared queue — the >1 arithmetic makes this the riskiest element and it needs a capacity plan first; (b) the shared approval queue — pilot it separately later, and only for high-value cases, keeping medium-value approvals local.

**Comparison group.** South (and North) continue with embedded specialists. Use difference-in-differences: change-in-East vs. change-in-South over the same calendar window controls for seasonality and demand shocks.

**Observation window.** 8–12 weeks pre-change baseline; 12–16 weeks post-change; weekly cadence for leading indicators, monthly for lagging. Pre-register the analysis plan and thresholds before go-live.

**Segmentation.** All metrics cut by `team` × `case_complexity` × `required_specialty` × `customer_segment` × `priority`. Minimum cell reporting: median, p90, SLA miss, rework, handovers per case, referral rate.

**Guardrail metrics (pilot continues only if all hold):**
- TSS median wait 5h, p90 12h
- East median cycle time improves *without* North/South (non-pilot) degradation >0.5d from routing spillovers
- Complex-case rework in pilot  +1pp vs. baseline
- SLA miss rate  +1pp in any segment
- Referral rate 45/100 sustained
- Specialist utilization 85%; off-hours share  baseline +5pp
- Approval ping-pong <3% (when approval pilot starts)

**Staff feedback collection.** Weekly pulse survey (pilot and comparison teams); structured biweekly interviews with caseworkers specifically about lost informal advice; a lightweight **log of informal consultations** (make the invisible channel visible); a named channel for queue-routing disputes; specialist workload check-ins.

**Rollback / adjustment triggers.**
- Legal/Risk implied  >0.95 or waits >2× baseline for 2 weeks  halt expansion, add capacity or retriage *before* any Legal/Risk centralization.
- TSS p90 wait >12h for 2 weeks  add specialist capacity or raise the complexity threshold for mandatory routing.
- North/South complex-case rework +3pp (post-expansion)  reinstate a named local first-touch tech liaison.
- Ping-pong approvals >5%  restore named approver accountability.
- Evidence of complexity-gaming  audit routing labels before trusting any further metrics.
- Pre-defined decision gates at weeks 6 and 12: **expand / adjust / rollback**, with criteria fixed in advance.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with modified scope.** Do not approve the full restructuring as proposed.

Rationale:
1. **The plan's own numbers predict more handovers and more waiting** (343359 per 100 cases; ~+1.3h wait per case). The "fewer handovers, faster process" narrative is not supported even by management's estimates — the honest value proposition is standardization, resilience of cover, and East's relief, which should be tested on their merits.
2. **The Legal/Risk element is arithmetically unsound** as stated (demand +33% onto 91–93% utilization). Either add capacity, reduce their service scope, or defer this element. This is a modify, not a reject: pooling technical review has genuine queueing and cover benefits, especially for East.
3. **Preserve a local knowledge channel deliberately**: a named, rotating local "tech liaison" with time budgeted for informal advice, and log those consultations. This directly targets the biggest slow-burn risk (deskilling and referral creep) at low cost.
4. **Sequence approvals last.** Standardized approval has plausible benefits but the highest role-clarity risk; pilot it only after TSS stabilizes, with named decision-maker attribution inside the queue.

**Conditions for expansion:** all guardrails in Section 5 hold for 8 consecutive weeks, segmented (not just aggregate) results show no degraded team/segment/complexity class, and leading indicators (queue depth, referral rate, off-hours work) are flat or improving.

**What management must not infer from early averages:**
- Flat or improved average cycle time  success — check p90, complex cases, and per-team/per-segment cuts first.
- Early pilot numbers are contaminated: warm-up effects, attention effects, cases started pre-change still in flight, and possible case-mix shifts. Wait for steady-state windows.
- Higher specialist utilization  efficiency — beyond ~85% it is a *cost* appearing as queue time borne by customers and caseworkers.
- Fewer logged cross-team handovers  less cross-team work — verify against working-together/subcontracting networks before claiming it.
- East improving while North/South degrade is a *redistribution decision*, not a Pareto improvement — it should be made explicitly, not discovered in a dashboard.
