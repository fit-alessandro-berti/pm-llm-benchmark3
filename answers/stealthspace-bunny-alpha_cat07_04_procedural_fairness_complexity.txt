# 1. Fairness dimensions

The assessment should distinguish **case complexity** from **complexity imposed by the organization’s procedures**. Adjusting for documented case mix is useful, but it does not justify additional delays, handovers, or documentation demands created by weak language access, scarce specialist capacity, or inconsistent routing.

- **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates differ substantially: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, and Group D 58%. These differences warrant investigation, but they are not evidence of discrimination by themselves. Groups B–D have greater documented complexity, and approval is only one aspect of procedural fairness. Equivalent approval rates would not establish fair procedures if, for example, one group faced substantially more burdensome documentation requests or appeals.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After controlling for the listed covariates, approval differences remain: 5.5 percentage points for Group B, 2.2 for Group C, and 3.1 for Group D relative to Group A. These are more concerning than raw differences alone, but they remain conditional associations, not causal estimates of discrimination. The preliminary summary provides no confidence intervals, model diagnostics, or information about unobserved confounding. Outcome quality, explanation quality, and dignity are also not captured by the approval indicator.

- **Procedural burden:** Comparable cases should not face unnecessary extra steps, repeated submissions, travel, or handovers merely because they use language or accommodation support. The adjusted probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents are higher for Group B by 8.0 and 9.5 percentage points, respectively. Additional review can be a safeguard, and requesting genuinely missing documents can be legitimate, but their necessity, consistency, and accessibility need testing.

- **Timeliness:** Median cycle-time differences remain after adjustment: +2.6 days for Group B, +4.1 for Group C, and +0.9 for Group D. The 90th-percentile gaps are also substantial in the raw data: Group B is 12.8 days above Group A, Group C 17.4 days above, and Group D 8.1 days above. Language-support and accommodation queues have median waits of 3.8 and 4.7 days, respectively, without separate timeliness thresholds. This is evidence of unequal timing burden, although it does not establish that every additional day was avoidable.

- **Consistency of routing:** Some differences align with policy: higher-risk cases require Fraud Review, accommodation requests require Accommodation Review, and language-support requests require Language Support Contact. However, actual routing consistency cannot be verified without knowing which cases were required to take each step. For example, Group D accounts for 28% of the risk-screening/fraud/supervisor path versus 9% of Group A, but only the variant distribution—not complete routing eligibility—is supplied.

- **Quality and accessibility of support:** The presence of Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review is evidence that a support activity occurred, not that support was timely, effective, accessible, or respectful. The absence of separate service-level targets for these rights-preserving steps is a policy concern. It should not be resolved by removing the steps or discouraging applicants from requesting them.

- **Appeal and rework burden:** Raw appeal rates are higher in Groups B, C, and D, and adjusted differences remain: +4.4 percentage points for Group B, +1.1 for Group C, and +5.7 for Group D. Reopen/rework rates are also substantially higher, especially for Groups B and C. Appeals can be a legitimate remedy rather than an undesirable applicant behavior. High appeal or rework rates may indicate problems in initial decision quality, communication, or accessibility.

- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Assignment is concentrated: Group B is disproportionately assigned to seven bilingual caseworkers and three regional officers, while Group C is disproportionately assigned to five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. Group D’s greater fraud-queue assignment is consistent with higher measured risk, but measured risk is not unquestionably neutral. Unequal workload, limited escalation capacity, and repeated handovers can themselves create procedural unfairness even when final decisions are correct.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The strategy should distinguish four concepts:

- **Disparity:** a measurable difference between groups.
- **Discrimination:** unjustified differential treatment or an unjust institutional process, which need not require proof of discriminatory intent.
- **Legitimate routing:** additional review or support demonstrably required by case needs or predeclared policy, implemented consistently and proportionately.
- **Uncertainty:** imprecision, limited common support, measurement error, and residual confounding that prevent a firm conclusion.

A defensible strategy would include the following steps.

1. **Define total and stage-specific estimands.**

   Measure approval, notification, activation, appeal, rework, duration, active work, waiting, and handovers separately. For example:

   - From application receipt to Identity Verification.
   - From Completeness Check to a complete, accurate application.
   - From a support request to meaningful resolution of that request.
   - From Decision Draft to notification.
   - From notification to the end of any appeal.
   - Full lifecycle duration, analyzed separately for closed and still-open cases.

   Report both **total disparities**, which include burdens generated during the process, and **conditional disparities among comparable cases**. The latter must not be used to normalize away institution-created delays.

2. **Use matching or stratification on legitimate, preferably pre-routing complexity.**

   At minimum, stratify or match on:

   - `risk_score_band`;
   - initial documentation completeness;
   - channel;
   - household-size band;
   - prior-case count;
   - region and calendar month;
   - case type and substantive eligibility criteria, if available;
   - language-support need;
   - accommodation request and, where appropriate, accommodation severity;
   - application assistance, digital access, and case-manager assignment, if collected.

   Use coarse exact strata or propensity-score methods with overlap checks. Group A may be the reference for interpretability, but an analysis confined to Group A–B pairs can discard useful overlap for Groups C and D. Positivity and standardized mean differences should be reported. Sparse strata should be identified rather than silently extrapolated.

   Importantly, documentation completeness measured *after* language barriers or procedural requests may be a mediator, not an intrinsic applicant characteristic. Likewise, pathway, resource assignment, and handoffs may be mechanisms through which disparity arises. Separate models should distinguish adjustment for legitimate need from adjustment for the process generated by earlier barriers.

3. **Compare paths and variants, not just their prevalence.**

   For each applicant group, calculate case-level variant shares and within-variant approval, duration, rework, and appeal outcomes. Then compare:

   - comparable cases receiving the same route;
   - applicants with the same support need who do and do not receive timely support;
   - low-complexity cases that receive standard processing versus additional review;
   - regions and channels within the same variant.

   Test whether required routing occurs consistently and whether any additional route is necessary. A longer route is not automatically less fair, but its incremental burden should be proportionate to the risk or support requirement.

   Also estimate the **support-route effect**: among applicants with comparable language or accommodation needs, how much additional time and handoff burden is associated with the mandated support process, holding case complexity constant. Variant membership should not simply be controlled away, because it may be the mechanism of the disparity.

4. **Compare resource assignment and capacity.**

   Construct daily resource measures for each worker, team, queue, region, and skill:

   - open caseload and arrivals;
   - available staff hours and leave/overtime;
   - active processing time;
   - case-mix complexity;
   - bilingual or accommodation-specialist demand;
   - queue age and escalation requirements;
   - cases waiting per available specialist.

   Model assignment to bilingual staff, accommodation specialists, regional queues, and supervisor review conditional on need, region, channel, and month. Use a multilevel or fixed-effects model where staffing levels are stable. Normalize handoff counts by eligible cases or transitions: a group with more cases will naturally generate more raw handoffs.

   A mediation or counterfactual decomposition can then estimate how much of the adjusted disparity is associated with support routing, regional queues, and resource assignment, and how much remains unexplained. This is associational unless stronger causal assumptions are established.

5. **Decompose waiting and cycle time.**

   For each stage, distinguish:

   - active caseworker time;
   - queue waiting before work starts;
   - time between activities;
   - handoff-related delay;
   - applicant response or document-return time;
   - calendar time versus business time.

   Compare stages within matched cases and within common paths. The supplied step medians are inter-event intervals and should not automatically be interpreted as pure queue time because they may include processing of the preceding event.

   Test whether longer overall duration is explained by one or two identifiable bottlenecks. For example, the 3.8-day language-support wait cannot explain the entire adjusted Group B duration difference, but it may be a substantial component for affected cases.

6. **Run sensitivity analyses for potentially biased covariates.**

   Repeat the analysis:

   - including and excluding `risk_score_band`;
   - using initial versus final documentation completeness;
   - coding missing documentation or risk values explicitly;
   - adjusting for measured workload versus omitting it;
   - allowing nonlinear effects and group-by-region/channel interactions;
   - bounding results under plausible unobserved confounding;
   - using both case-level matching and hierarchical models.

   A useful distinction is between an audit finding that the score is inaccurate for some applicants and a policy decision to stop using a risk measure that serves a legitimate protective function. The audit should assess calibration, error rates, historical bias, and the consequences of routing—not assume that risk-based review is illegitimate.

7. **Check region, channel, and intersecting needs.**

   Report results by region and channel, and by region × channel, language need, accommodation need, risk band, and documentation status. Broad applicant categories conceal important intersections, such as minority-language applicants also requesting accommodations.

   Use case-level or applicant-level uncertainty estimates, with clustering for repeated applicants where identifiers permit. Report confidence or credible intervals, practical effect sizes, and sensitivity to specification. Statistical nonsignificance is not proof of fairness, and a significant difference is not automatically proof of discrimination.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least seven mechanisms could generate the observed patterns:

1. **Legitimate needs-based routing.** Higher measured risk in Group D can require Fraud Review; incomplete documentation can require document requests; accommodation and language needs can require specialized review. This likely explains part of the variant differences, but not necessarily the size of the delays.

2. **Legitimate safeguards with cumulative burden.** Supervisor Review, Fraud Review, Language Support Contact, and Accommodation Review may improve accuracy or protect rights. Requiring them serially, however, can create additional waiting and handoffs. The safeguard is legitimate; avoidable implementation burden still requires justification.

3. **Scarce specialist capacity and regional queues.** Seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists may be insufficient for the relevant demand. Regional backlogs compound the delay. This mechanism could explain why Group C has the largest adjusted cycle-time difference even though its approval difference is smaller.

4. **Language and documentation barriers.** Group B’s higher incomplete-documentation share may reflect unequal access to clear instructions, translated materials, or assistance rather than greater intrinsic case complexity. Adjusting for final documentation completeness may therefore absorb part of an access barrier into the “need” adjustment.

5. **Historical or institutionally shaped risk measurement.** `risk_score_band` may encode prior screening, reporting, or enforcement patterns. It may also measure some genuine current risk. If so, Group D can be both legitimately routed and exposed to feedback effects in which scrutiny, rejection, appeals, and subsequent risk labels reinforce one another.

6. **Handoff-related errors and rework.** Repeated transfers can lose context, produce inconsistent document requests, or delay corrections. Higher rework in Groups B and C is consistent with this mechanism, although the log summary does not establish the error mechanism directly.

7. **Uneven access and assignment by place or channel.** Online access, application assistance, regional staffing, and case-manager assignment may affect both case mix and process quality. Region, month, and broad channel controls help, but do not eliminate differences in digital access, travel burden, local language capacity, or caseworker practices.

These mechanisms can coexist. For example, accommodation review can be substantively appropriate while its specialist queue imposes an avoidable procedural burden.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Historical bias in risk scores.** `risk_score_band` lacks provenance, score components, decision thresholds, calibration history, and validation results. A high-risk classification may be relevant to current risk, but it may also reproduce historical disparities. Controlling for it can embed that bias in the reference model.

- **Measurement bias in documentation completeness.** Completeness may depend on language access, document availability, disability-related barriers, or the clarity of instructions. It may also be recorded differently across regions or caseworkers. “Missing” does not necessarily mean the applicant could reasonably have supplied the document.

- **Incomplete needs and complexity measures.** The attributes do not adequately capture language proficiency, interpretation needs, disability type and severity, accommodation urgency, substantive case type, application assistance, digital literacy, travel distance, repeat-visit costs, or the actual difficulty of eligibility questions. Group labels are composite and overlapping, limiting causal interpretation.

- **Unobserved resource workload.** Daily resource granularity cannot reveal intraday queue formation, hours actually available, leave, overtime, task switching, or aging caseloads. A headcount of seven or five does not establish equivalent productive capacity. Resource-level fairness cannot be assessed reliably from assignment counts alone.

- **Weak outcome measurement.** `case_outcome` omits explanation quality, accuracy, timeliness, dignity, accessibility, and whether the applicant understood the decision or appeal rights. An approval and an informed, respectful rejection can be procedurally fair; an accurate outcome reached through a burdensome or opaque process may not be.

- **Insufficient appeal and rework measurement.** `appeal_flag` does not show filing reasons, representation, language used, evidence supplied, acceptance or reversal, processing time, or notification quality. Rework must also be distinguished from legitimate follow-up rather than treating every repeated activity as an error.

- **Analytical limitations.** No confidence intervals, model diagnostics, missing-data rates, sensitivity analyses, or complete route histories are supplied. The variant table contains only top paths, and its medians may reflect different lifecycle endpoints. The observed fields therefore support a fairness investigation, not a definitive causal finding.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Language support and accommodation review should be preserved. The objective is to make them timely, accessible, and minimally burdensome—not to discourage or remove them.

| Mitigation | Fairness benefit | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Expand language-support capacity** | Add bilingual caseworkers, contract interpreters, or a pooled remote-interpretation service; provide protected capacity for urgent cases. | Higher staffing and technology costs; training time; unequal availability across shifts and regions; confidentiality risks with remote tools. Capacity must be monitored for actual service quality, not just interpreter contacts. |
| **Expand accommodation-review capacity** | Increase dedicated specialist capacity, reduce queue age, and separate accommodation review from supervisor review where feasible. | Specialist recruitment may be difficult; separating roles can create a new bottleneck. Independence may improve, but cross-training alone may not provide enough review capacity. |
| **Set service-level targets for support steps** | Establish separate targets for language and accommodation review, with clock start, meaningful completion, regional demand adjustments, and escalation when targets are missed. | Targets can encourage premature closure, reclassification of cases, or avoidance of formal support requests. Targets must measure successful resolution, not merely a contact or ticket being opened. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** | Use a single accountable case owner, remote specialist consultation, structured warm handoffs, shared checklists, and electronic document exchange. | Fewer handoffs can remove useful checks or specialist input. Redesign should distinguish necessary consultation from repeated transfer without removing required review. |
| **Improve decision explanations** | Provide clear reasons, the evidence still needed, accessible language/format, document checklist, appeal rights, and contact routes. Offer explanations that enable correction without requiring repeated submission. | Explanations add staff time and require translation quality assurance. Applicants may misunderstand technical language or over-rely on explanations, so communication quality should be checked. |
| **Monitor adjusted and total disparities** | Publish a regular dashboard covering stage waits, support completion, handovers, approval, appeal, and rework, stratified by need, region, and channel. Use thresholds to trigger case review and resource investigation. | Metrics can become punitive quotas or encourage gaming. Small-cell suppression may protect privacy but obscure disparities; model revisions should be documented. |
| **Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`** | Examine score provenance, historical calibration, group-specific error rates, threshold consistency, document-request accuracy, and barriers to obtaining documents. Use independent review and applicant input. | Removing a useful risk tool could weaken legitimate safeguards. Recalibration may temporarily increase referrals or disparities. Documentation reforms may initially raise recorded incompleteness while improving accuracy. |
| **Review appeal and rework root causes** | Examine incorrect document requests, inconsistent decision rationales, accessibility failures, and repeated corrections. Track appeal filing, acceptance, completion, and time to resolution. | More extensive review consumes staff capacity and may initially increase queues. A low appeal rate should not be treated as success if legitimate challenges are suppressed. |

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**Overall assessment: indeterminate, with credible and material procedural-equity concerns.**

The evidence does not demonstrate that the process is uniformly procedurally fair. Some raw disparities are explained by documented complexity and legitimate routing, but adjusted differences in approval and cycle time, persistent appeal and rework burdens, concentrated specialist capacity, and unequal support-step waits remain. The strongest concerns concern **timeliness, procedural burden, and resource capacity**, rather than proof of intentional discrimination.

At the same time, the evidence is insufficient to conclude systematic discrimination or that every additional review, handoff, or document request was unjustified. The appropriate next step is a matched, route-level, workload-aware audit that preserves language and accommodation rights, tests whether additional burdens are necessary and proportionate, and audits the risk and documentation measures on which routing depends.
