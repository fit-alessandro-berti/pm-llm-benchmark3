# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness concerns whether people can access a consistent, timely, understandable and appropriately individualized process, including the support needed to participate. Different paths are not automatically unfair: equal treatment can require different services. Conversely, a formally required step can be administered unfairly if it is systematically harder to access or slower for those who need it.

- **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates range from 78% for A to 58% for D. These are important descriptive disparities, but they do not establish discrimination or show whether similarly situated applicants receive similar decisions.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** The preliminary estimates put approval 5.5 percentage points lower for B, 2.2 for C and 3.1 for D relative to A after the listed controls. These are conditional associations, subject to model specification, overlap, uncertainty and the fairness of the controls. They do not prove discriminatory decisions.
- **Procedural burden:** Document requests, repeated contacts, handovers and reviews can impose time, travel, communication and evidence-gathering costs. Extra burden should be assessed against the purpose and necessity of each step, not just counted.
- **Timeliness:** Both overall time and time spent waiting at specific steps matter. Adjusted cycle time is 2.6 days higher for B and 4.1 days higher for C. Long support queues may deny timely access to rights even when the support itself is appropriate.
- **Consistency of routing:** Comparable cases should receive comparable checks under the same rules, with justified exceptions recorded. Required fraud, language and accommodation routes should be checked for both inappropriate omissions and unnecessary or inconsistent referrals.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** A recorded contact or review does not establish that support was timely, effective, in the preferred language, accessible or respectful. Equal counts of support events would not establish equal access.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Raw appeal and rework rates are higher for B, C and D than A. Appeals may reflect incorrect decisions, poor explanations, higher willingness or ability to appeal, or different eligibility; success, quality and burden matter as well as frequency. The preliminary adjusted appeal differences are +4.4 points for B and +5.7 for D.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Concentrating people who need support in small or overloaded teams can systematically restrict timely access. Compare capacity, workload and handovers against needs, including whether regional resources are adequate.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

First define the population, observation window, outcomes and time origin. Check one group and baseline covariate value per case, timestamps, duplicate events, missingness, group overlap, cases still open, and whether the same follow-up period is available for decisions, appeals and rework. Report counts, denominators and uncertainty intervals alongside absolute differences, including tails and not just means or medians. Compare like stages: application-to-initial-decision separately from notification-to-appeal resolution and final closure, so an appeal does not mechanically inflate the initial processing measure. Handle open cases and unequal follow-up with appropriate censoring or fixed-horizon measures.

Stratify or match on pre-application or intake characteristics relevant to the comparison: region, intake month, channel, household size, prior-case count, risk band and initial documentation completeness where measured before routing. Assess overlap and balance; restrict comparisons to common support and report excluded cases and any loss of representativeness. Compare within relevant need strata using language preference and accommodation request, and distinguish applicants who need or request support from those who do not. These variables may differ substantially by group, so some cross-group comparisons will have little overlap. When direct matches are sparse, use carefully specified multivariable or hierarchical models and report the uncertainty and dependence on assumptions.

Use more than one estimand. For an overall disparity in the experience of applying, do not automatically adjust away support use, document requests, actual route, assignment or delays that may be consequences of group-related barriers. For a conditional comparison of implementation, compare cases with similar documented needs and complexity, then compare within a policy-required route or at a common decision point. Within-route estimates describe differences among selected cases, not necessarily a causal effect of group membership. Language preference and accommodation need may themselves be connected to group identity and access barriers; state the interpretation of controlling for them. Neither matching nor a regression can by itself distinguish discrimination from unmeasured need, unequal access or administrative practice.

Compare variants and transition frequencies within these strata, including repeat requests, reviews, appeals and returns, and examine both prevalence and time within each route. The displayed variants are summaries and are not necessarily complete case histories; verify their exact event sequences and outcome and follow-up definitions in the log. Avoid comparing only identical complete variants: a variant may already encode the burden or decision being studied, and conditioning on it can hide disparities or create selection bias. Check the mandated routes and documented exceptions at the individual case level.

At common routing points, compare assignment to workers, specialist teams and regions; handover counts and sequences; queue size and daily workload where available; and subsequent waits. Adjust for legitimate specialization, case mix and calendar time, but examine whether capacity follows demand. Worker-level comparisons need enough cases, uncertainty intervals and care with clustering and uneven case assignment; worker identity alone does not explain performance.

Decompose elapsed time into intervals between recorded events, repeated loops, and, where lifecycle timestamps support it, active service and queue waiting. Compare the same transition and need stratum, then estimate how much of the overall gap is associated with route mix versus within-route timing, using a clearly specified standardization and reference population. Event-to-event elapsed time is not necessarily pure waiting: it may include processing, applicant response, nonbusiness time or unlogged work. Do not add medians of individual intervals to reconstruct a median total.

For sensitivity, repeat analyses with and without risk band and documentation completeness; compare intake-only measures with later values, alternative specifications, missing-data assumptions and plausible unmeasured confounding. Audit how risk and completeness are created and whether their meaning and predictive validity differ by group. Conditioning on a biased score or a barrier-affected document measure can mask inequity, while excluding genuine need or eligibility information can confound comparisons. These alternatives show dependence on assumptions, not a definitive causal decomposition. Examine region-by-group and channel-by-group results, including the two identified backlog regions and in-person cases; report small-cell uncertainty, multiple-comparison concerns and any suppressed cells. Investigate persistent differences with policy review, case-file sampling and applicant and staff feedback before attributing them to discrimination.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

B uses the summarized language-support path V4 in 24% of cases versus 1% for A; its median is 19.2 days across the cases in that variant. B's wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment is 3.8 days, and its median Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents interval is 1.6 days versus 0.4 for A. B also has an adjusted 9.5-point higher probability of a document request. Language support is required for relevant requests, but seven bilingual workers, three overloaded regional officers and extra handovers in two regions suggest a capacity and coordination concern. The summaries do not prove that a particular wait causes the adjusted difference or that every applicant needing support received it. The stated 57% needing language support is not directly comparable with the 24% in one summarized variant.

C uses V5, which includes Accommodation Review and Supervisor Review, in 31% of cases versus 2% for A; its overall variant median is 21.6 days. C's median Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment interval is 4.7 days, and five specialists serve this work, with two also performing supervisor review. Accommodation review is required for requests and can legitimately add steps; a specialist bottleneck or excessive handovers remain concerns. The observed adjusted cycle-time difference of +4.1 days warrants investigation, but is not an estimate of the effect of accommodation review.

D has V3, which includes Fraud Review, in 28% of cases versus 9% for A, alongside a higher measured high-risk share, 51% versus 18%. Risk-based routing is required by policy and plausibly explains some difference. It does not establish that the risk measure is unbiased, that referrals follow policy consistently, or that fraud review is proportionate. D's adjusted appeal probability is 5.7 points higher and merits examination of notification, reasons and appeal results.

Across groups, V1's share is 42% for A but 18–22% for B, C and D; V2, involving a document request, is 24% for A and 26–34% for the others. These route differences may reflect genuine needs, incomplete applications, eligibility and process barriers. Variant medians are pooled over cases in each variant, not group-specific effects, and the listed paths need not exhaust the log. B and C also have longer median waits from Supervisor Review to Decision Draft, 1.9 and 1.6 days versus 1.0 for A, with a backlog in two regions. Region-specific, comparable-case analyses are needed to distinguish local capacity from group-specific treatment. In-person applications wait longer at identity verification, which may contribute to B's experience but is not established as its cause.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate specialization:** Requested language support and accommodation require appropriate expertise and coordination; high measured risk triggers policy-mandated fraud review. These steps can add time while protecting rights or managing risk.
2. **Insufficient specialist capacity:** Demand for a small bilingual or accommodation workforce, and shared supervisor duties, may create queues that fall disproportionately on applicants who need those services.
3. **Handover and regional congestion:** Transfers between generalists, specialists and supervisors can create waiting, repeated explanations and lost context, especially in the two backlogged regions.
4. **Language or accessibility barriers in documentation:** Unclear forms or requests can produce incomplete submissions, repeated requests and delays. Controlling for completeness may then obscure part of the process problem.
5. **Biased or miscalibrated risk assessment:** Historical data, prior enforcement patterns or proxy variables may produce higher scores for some applicants even at similar underlying risk, affecting referrals and decisions.
6. **Decision communication and review quality:** Explanations that are difficult to understand or inaccessible may increase appeals or rework; alternatively, appeals may reflect substantive errors or differences in access to appeal. The current data cannot distinguish these explanations.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Risk bands and prior-case counts may reflect historical surveillance, prior access or administrative decisions as well as current risk. Documentation completeness may be measured inconsistently and may result from inaccessible forms, translation difficulties or earlier staff actions. Channel and region may proxy unequal service access, and the timing of covariate measurement determines whether an adjustment removes part of the disparity being studied. Group labels and event attributes may be missing, incorrect, overlapping or too coarse to represent intersecting experiences.

Language preference and an accommodation-request flag do not establish actual needs, whether applicants knew how to request help, the type or effectiveness of support, or the unmet needs of people who did not request it. Household and risk bands omit many relevant circumstances, eligibility details and case complexity. Case outcome records neither the quality of reasons nor dignity, accessibility, accuracy or consistency of treatment.

Daily resource workload does not reveal intraday queue state, staff availability, case difficulty, off-log work, leave or competing duties. A timestamped event may not mark start and completion of work; applicant response time and agency delay may be mixed. Missing, delayed or differently recorded events can distort variants, handovers and intervals. The log also does not by itself show appeal grounds, access, representation, quality, success or whether a changed decision corrected an error. Unequal follow-up and open cases can distort approval, cycle time, appeal and rework comparisons. These limitations leave room for both legitimate explanations and inequitable treatment; neither can be established solely from the displayed summaries.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Add bilingual and accommodation capacity**, cross-train staff and allocate capacity according to demand and regional backlog. This can shorten queues while preserving support. Hiring and training cost money and take time; cross-training must not dilute specialist expertise or take capacity from other necessary work.
- **Set and monitor service-level targets for support steps**, including time to first contact, completion and escalation for overdue cases. Define start and stop points, distinguish agency waits from applicant response, and monitor quality and accessibility as well as speed. Targets can encourage rushed reviews, superficial contacts or gaming unless audited and resourced.
- **Redesign routing to reduce unnecessary handovers**, for example through coordinated case ownership, shared information and parallel work where policy permits. Preserve required specialist input and applicant consent and accessibility. Fewer handovers may concentrate workload, reduce independent review or require careful privacy controls.
- **Improve decision explanations and document requests** with plain language, translations, accessible formats, clear evidence requirements and appeal instructions; test them with applicants. Translation and individualization require resources, and simplified text can omit necessary nuance if not checked.
- **Monitor adjusted disparities and process measures** by group, region, channel and relevant need, alongside raw outcomes, uncertainty, missingness, queues, repeat requests, appeals and support quality. Use common definitions and investigate changes. Small samples and multiple comparisons can produce unstable alerts; an exclusive focus on a dashboard can also obscure individual harms or incentivize gaming.
- **Audit risk scores and completeness measures:** examine data provenance, historical bias, group-specific validity, overrides, thresholds and accessibility of evidence requests; review samples with policy and affected-community input. Revise problematic rules with appropriate validation and oversight. Changing scores or documentation rules can alter fraud detection, eligibility accuracy, workload and comparability over time; assess these effects before and after implementation.
- **Review appeals and rework**, including reasons, accessibility, outcomes and repeat corrections, and provide practical assistance to appeal. More accessible appeals may initially increase appeal counts and workload, so a rising rate alone should not be treated as deterioration.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The available evidence makes procedural fairness **indeterminate, with substantial reasons for concern**. Required support and risk-based routes may explain some differences, but B and C face notable support-related queues and concentrated specialist resources, and adjusted differences in time, document requests, review and appeals persist. The preliminary model and summarized logs do not establish discrimination, the causal contribution of any queue, or whether support and decisions were delivered well. A stratified, time-resolved review of comparable cases, risk and documentation measures, capacity, accessibility and appeal outcomes is needed, alongside action to address the evident support-capacity risks.
