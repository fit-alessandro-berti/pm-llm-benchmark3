## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation
The raw-to-mapped fitness gap (0.550.90 in March, persisting thereafter) indicates that the majority of conformance loss after migration is label- and duplicate-induced rather than genuine ordering violations. Mapping recovers fitness because it restores the normative “Validate Eligibility” node and removes the technical Open Case artifact, yet this recovery does not prove the business process remained unchanged; the mapped log still shows elevated rework rates and altered DFG densities after May that survive the mapping.

Duplicated Open Case events inflate activity counts and distort resource attribution without altering control-flow semantics, whereas missing resource identifiers primarily degrade organizational mining and queue reconstruction. Post-May DFG shifts (AssessRequestInfo rising from 12 to 29 per 100 cases) and the accompanying cycle-time deterioration cannot be dismissed as logging drift alone because the same patterns appear in the already-mapped normalized rates, are concentrated in Supplier Z cases, and coincide with documented supplier and staffing events rather than further schema changes.

## 9. Provisional attribution
Strongest conclusions concern the March logging drift: the abrupt raw-fitness collapse, appearance of “Eligibility Screen”, and duplicated Open Case events align exactly with the documented migration date and are largely reversed by the supplied mapping. Supplier-driven behavioral drift after 5 May is also well supported; the doubling of rework arcs and cycle-time increase occur only after the supplier disruption, are concentrated in Supplier Z cases, and persist after mapping. Staffing effects on queueing are plausible from June onward but are partly confounded by the simultaneous priority rule and residual supplier backlog.

Seasonal population drift is clearly visible in the October high-complexity surge and Senior Assessment increase, yet the priority rule mediates part of the observed redistribution. Interaction between supplier disruption and staffing cuts prevents clean separation of their contributions to the June–August performance peak. The December improvement most likely reflects both reduced seasonal load and the overtime campaign, but the two cannot be disentangled without case-level timestamps.

The account would be revised if supplier-stratified logs showed no rework difference, if priority-rule start date proved earlier than stated, or if a formal change-point algorithm on daily data placed any discontinuity outside the documented event windows.
