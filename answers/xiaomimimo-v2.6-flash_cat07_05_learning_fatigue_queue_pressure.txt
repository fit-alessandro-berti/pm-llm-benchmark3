# 2. Integrated explanation

## 2.1 What the marginal table actually contains

The "by sequence position" table is a mixture distribution. Each row simultaneously averages over four resources tiers, four shifts, nine activity families, four complexity levels, and a WIP level that systematically rises with position. Interpreting its columns as causal effects of "time on task" is a specification error. The correct reading is: *the composition of work and the conditions under which it is performed change predictably over a shift, and performance changes with both.*

The observed pattern has three regimes, each driven by a different dominant mechanism.

**Regime 1 — Positions 1–15 (the opening): slow and error-prone for compositional and inherited reasons.**
Service time is highest (18.8 min) and errors are elevated (3.4%, rework 6.1%). Complexity is at its maximum (0.72) and high-priority share at its maximum (41%), because the incoming queue is *inherited*: the Day shift starts with mean queue 38 but the Early shift starts at 61 and the Late shift at 70, and these carryovers are disproportionately complex (48–56% high-complexity in the first hour). Senior resources deliberately take complex cases early. So the opening is a **selection + backlog-inheritance** effect first, a warm-up effect second, and possibly a circadian effect third (Early shift starts at 06:00, Late at 14:00). It is *not* primarily fatigue — no one is fatigued twenty minutes into a shift.

**Regime 2 — Positions 16–50 (the middle): the "learning" plateau that is largely case-mix.**
Service time falls to 13.6  12.9 min and errors bottom out at 2.5–2.7%. This is where naive analysis declares "learning". Part of it is genuine warm-up: workers settle into activity routines, handoffs stabilize, juniors in particular gain. But complexity has fallen from 0.72 to 0.50 and high-priority share from 41% to 25% over exactly this range, and queue length has dropped from 46 to 31. Three of the four things that make work hard have improved *at the same time*. A realistic decomposition would attribute a substantial share of the mid-shift improvement to **case-mix and queue drainage**, with a smaller genuine conditional improvement from learning. The evidence that learning is real but *not* the whole story: seniors, who have the least left to learn, still show 15.9  11.8 min.

**Regime 3 — Positions 51–76+ (the close): fast, and increasingly wrong.**
Service time continues to fall (11.4  10.8 min) while error rate more than doubles (4.6%  7.9%) and rework more than doubles (8.2%  13.7%). This is the crucial observation, and it is where single-mechanism stories break down:

- *Complexity keeps falling* (0.50  0.43) and high-priority share keeps falling (25%  19%). **Case-mix predicts improving quality here, not degrading quality.** Selection bias therefore cannot be the cause of the late error rise; if anything it works in the opposite direction.
- *Queue length more than doubles* (31  62  88) across exactly these bins. The stratified table confirms what the marginal table cannot: holding complexity band fixed, error rises 2.1%  3.0%  5.8% (low complexity) and 4.2%  5.6%  10.9% (high complexity) as WIP rises, while service time *falls* in the same direction. That speed-up-with-quality-loss combination is the signature of **rushing under congestion**, not of learning and not of fatigue (fatigue should slow people down).
- The resource-experience table is the strongest discriminator. Seniors' error rate later in shift is 2.2% under low WIP but 4.9% under high WIP; juniors' is 4.4% under low WIP but 12.4% under high WIP. **The same workers, at the same elapsed shift time, do not degrade when the queue is manageable.** This is decisive against a pure fatigue or pure circadian explanation of the late-shift error rise: the degradation is conditional on congestion and moderated by experience. Seniors hold a buffer; juniors do not.
- Two artefacts amplify the apparent late degradation. First, **batching**: Junior_H and Junior_I produce 9–18 consecutive very short events late in the Day shift, which mechanically lowers the late mean service time and, if those batched shortcuts skip checks, raises late errors. Second, **detection-time displacement**: Quality Check is deferred to end of shift, so corrections "bursts" in the last 45 minutes. Errors *committed* mid-shift are *detected* and *logged* at the end of the shift, and "rework within 7 days" inherits the same lag. Part of the late error/rework spike is therefore a **measurement artefact of when defects are found**, not when they are made.

## 2.2 Why service time falls while errors rise — only in some conditions

Service time and error rate are not driven by the same variable, and the late-shift divergence occurs because the drivers **decouple**:

1. **Service time is mostly a function of task difficulty and urgency.** Difficulty falls monotonically over the shift (complexity 0.72  0.43), so service time falls monotonically — *even before* any learning or rushing is added. The remaining decline comes from a mix of genuine learning (early bins) and pressure-induced shortening (late bins), plus batching artefacts.

2. **Error rate is mostly a function of the margin between task difficulty and the capacity available to meet it.** In the middle of the shift both sides improve: tasks get easier *and* workers have warmed up *and* the queue has drained — so errors fall. In the last third, tasks keep getting easier, but **capacity per task collapses**: WIP doubles, each case receives less attention per unit of difficulty, and quality checks are compressed into the final 45 minutes. When demand growth outruns the improvement in task difficulty, the margin goes negative and errors rise.

3. **The divergence only appears when a threshold is crossed.** Below roughly WIP 70, the system absorbs pressure: seniors' errors stay at 2.2%, juniors at 4.4%. Above it, errors jump disproportionately (4.9% and 12.4% respectively). This threshold behaviour also appears independently in the bot-correction rate past `queue_length_at_start > 70`. So there is a **capacity knee**: up to the knee, congestion buys speed at little quality cost; past the knee, it buys speed at accelerating quality cost.

4. **Experience moderates the knee rather than removing it.** Seniors have a larger buffer (their low-WIP and early error rates are nearly identical), so they degrade later and less. Juniors have little buffer, so they are hit twice — by complex early cases *and* by late congestion — which is why their early error rate (5.1%) and high-WIP late error rate (12.4%) are both poor. Night staff sit between, with an additional circadian penalty (14.9 min and 8.2% late under high WIP) and a case-mix penalty (56% high-complexity first hour, urgent exceptions).

5. **Circadian effects are real but secondary and overlapping.** Day shift's 7.5% error in the last two hours (15:00–17:00) sits in a window where queue has risen to 75 *and* the post-lunch/evening circadian dip applies; Night's 8.2% sits in the 02:00–05:00 trough with queue at 55. The two mechanisms are confounded by design because shift boundaries and clock time are correlated. The honest statement is: **circadian effects likely add a modest, clock-time-locked increment on top of a queue-pressure effect that is larger and better evidenced**, and both are further distorted by batching and by deferred quality-check detection.

## 2.3 The synthesis

A defensible causal picture is:

> Over a shift, work becomes *objectively easier* (complexity and priority mix fall) and workers become *marginally better* (warm-up/learning), so service time falls for at least three compounding reasons. Error rate initially falls because the improvement in task difficulty and the drained queue dominate. In the final third, arrivals outpace throughput, WIP doubles past a capacity knee, quality checks are compressed into a burst at shift end, and workers — especially juniors and night staff — shorten per-case effort to keep up. Errors and rework then rise *despite* the cases being easier, because attention per case has fallen faster than difficulty has. Circadian timing adds a clock-locked increment; batching and deferred detection inflate both the apparent late speed and the apparent late error count; and automation (Bot_Risk) shifts residual correction work onto exactly the moments of highest congestion.

No single line of this story survives alone: fatigue alone cannot explain falling service time; learning alone cannot explain rising errors on easier cases; case-mix alone cannot explain the WIP gradient within complexity bands; queue pressure alone cannot explain the early-shift elevation (which is inherited backlog and selection); and circadian effects alone cannot explain why the same worker is fine late in shift when WIP is low.

## 3.0 Data preparation (PM4Py)

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# --- 1. Standardise the event log -------------------------------------------
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

# Keep start_timestamp explicit so service time = end - start (not inter-arrival)
assert "start_timestamp" in df.columns

# --- 2. Derived event-level fields ------------------------------------------
# Shift hour index and clock time (separates circadian from shift position)
df["clock_hour"]      = df["time:timestamp"].dt.hour + df["time:timestamp"].dt.minute / 60
df["hour_of_day"]     = df["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
df["day_of_week"]     = df["time:timestamp"].dt.dayofweek
df["is_weekend"]      = df["day_of_week"].isin([5, 6])

# Experience tier (for interactions)
df["exp_tier"] = pd.cut(df["resource_experience_months"],
                        bins=[-1, 12, 48, 1e9],
                        labels=["junior", "mid", "senior"])

# Position bins used in the report
df["pos_bin"] = pd.cut(
    df["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, 10**9],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
)

# Complexity and WIP strata
df["complexity_band"] = np.where(df["case_complexity_score"] >= 0.6, "high", "low")
df["wip_band"] = pd.cut(df["wip_at_start"], bins=[-1, 30, 70, 10**9],
                        labels=["low", "medium", "high"])

# Service time at EVENT level (this is the dependent variable of interest)
df["service_min"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"])
).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Flag bot-derived work so human-only analysis is clean
df["is_bot"] = df["org:resource"].eq("Bot_Risk") | df["concept:name"].str.contains("Bot", na=False)
df["bot_origin_correction"] = df["concept:name"].str.contains("Rework|Correction", na=False) & \
                              df["activity_family"].str.contains("Risk", na=False)
```

If `np.log1p(wip_at_start)` is negative for `service_min` and positive for `error_flag`, queue pressure with a speed–quality tradeoff is confirmed. If `hour_of_day` remains significant after both, circadian effects are present.

## 3.3 WIP / queue stratification and threshold testing

```python

knee = smf.ols(
    "error_flag ~ np.log1p(wip_at_start) + I(np.maximum(wip_at_start - 70, 0)) "
                 "+ case_complexity_score + C(activity_family)",
    data=humans,
).fit()
# A significant positive hinge coefficient => evidence for a threshold near WIP 70.

# Break reverse causality: use INHERITED backlog (queue at shift start) as the instrument
iv = smf.2sls(
    "error_flag ~ np.log1p(wip_at_start) + case_complexity_score "
                 "+ np.log1p(minutes_since_shift_start) + C(hour_of_day)",
    instruments=["queue_length_at_start"],   # determined by previous shift, not by this worker
    data=humans,
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": humans["org:resource"]})
```

Also run a **placebo test**: WIP of the *next* event should not predict the *current* event's error. If it does, the relationship is largely simultaneity.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**5.1 Worker-level conclusions from event logs are systematically unfair.**
The log records *outcomes the worker was exposed to*, not *actions the worker chose*. A junior on the Night shift inherits a queue that is 56% high-complexity and staffed thin; their 12.4% error rate under high WIP is a property of the assignment and the staffing model as much as of the person. Comparing raw worker error rates compares **exposure profiles**, not competence. Any worker-level ranking must be residualised on complexity, WIP, activity family, shift, and hour-of-day — and reported with confidence intervals, because many workers will have too few events for a stable estimate.

**5.2 Selection bias runs in both directions and is not "the workers' fault".**
Seniors taking complex cases early is a *rational, value-adding* adaptation; juniors batching late is partly an adaptation to congestion. Both create the appearance of skill differences that are actually **assignment-policy differences**. Conversely, if high performers are assigned harder cases (load-dependent selection), raw error rates *understate* their true performance. Either way, the naive comparison is biased in an unknown direction. Randomisation (#4) fixes measurement, but as permanent policy it can degrade service by breaking expert–case matching.

**5.3 Measurement error is not random.**
Three specific artefacts distort worker comparisons: (a) **detection timing** — deferred Quality Checks attribute defects to whoever is present at shift end, so the last-two-hours error rate partly measures *when checks happen*, not *when errors happen*; (b) **batching** — 9–18 consecutive sub-minute events by specific juniors mechanically lowers their mean service time and may concentrate shortcuts; (c) **case-level vs. event-level denominators** — error flags attached to events but "rework within 7 days" attached to cases produce inconsistent rates for the same worker. Until defect origin is traced back through rework paths, late-shift error rates should be labelled *detection* rates.

**5.4 Management, not workers, creates queue pressure — and then measures workers under it.**
The single strongest driver of late-shift degradation is WIP, and WIP is a *system* variable determined by arrival rates, staffing, shift design, and handover practice. The Day shift ends at queue 75 because demand peaks late and coverage does not. Penalising a worker for error under WIP 88 is penalising them for a staffing decision. WIP also has **simultaneity**: fast workers drain queues, so "good" workers appear to work under low WIP, producing a virtuous-looking correlation that flatters exactly the people already favoured by assignment. Instrumental-variable or shift-start-backlog approaches are needed before attributing causation.

**5.5 Automation quality is a system responsibility, and its errors land on humans.**
Bot_Risk's instant classifications are excluded from human service-time averages (correctly), but humans *correct* bot output far more often when `queue_length_at_start > 70`. The bot therefore does not remove risk; it **concentrates residual, harder-to-verify work onto the most congested moments**, and the human who catches it absorbs the error in their own error rate. Automation quality must be audited separately (bot-origin correction rate stratified by WIP) and must never be charged to the correcting worker. Likewise, "instant" bot events must be excluded from any service-time mean, or the mean will understate human workload.

**5.6 Punitive use of error rates damages the data it depends on.**
If workers are judged on raw error rates, the predictable responses are: under-reporting errors and rework; avoiding complex and high-priority cases (worsening selection bias and leaving the hardest work for whoever cannot refuse); slowing down defensively under pressure (raising queue pressure for everyone, potentially pushing WIP further above 70); refusing to correct bot output; and hiding batches. Every one of these degrades both service quality *and* analytical validity. Improvement cultures require that error reporting is treated as a safety signal, and any drop in reported errors that is not matched by a drop in downstream rework or post-close rework should be treated as a warning sign.

**5.7 Specific cautions on individual interventions.**
- Deferring Quality Checks (#6) to improve throughput reduces *measured* errors while increasing *actual* defect latency — this is metric gaming and can cause customer-impacting escapes.
- Removing or weakening quality checks of any kind to raise throughput is not acceptable: the checks are the detection mechanism for the very late-shift degradation being analysed.
- Blanket shift caps (#1) can transfer the problem to the next shift's first hour, which is already the most complex window.
- Complex-case deferral (#2) must never delay high-priority cases; waiting-time metrics must be watched alongside error metrics, because error rate alone can always be improved by making people slower or by refusing work.

**5.8 Fair comparison requirements (if any worker-level feedback is given).**
Report only: complexity-standardised *and* WIP-standardised error rates; residuals from a model with resource, activity, shift and hour-of-day controls; medians with bootstrap intervals and minimum sample thresholds; paired within-worker comparisons over time (is *this* worker improving?) rather than cross-worker league tables; and always alongside workload indicators (WIP exposure, complex-case share, batch exposure, bot-correction share) so that a reviewer can see the conditions under which the number was produced.

**Dashboard rules:**
1. Every indicator appears in **four cuts** — raw, complexity-standardised, WIP-stratified, and by shift/hour — because the analysis in §1–§3 shows each is necessary.
2. **Service time and error rate are always shown on the same panel with WIP**, since the central finding is that falling service time is ambiguous (learning vs. pressure) until WIP is visible.
3. **No worker-level leaderboard view.** Worker indicators are exposure and residual views with confidence intervals and minimum sample thresholds (§5.8).
4. Indicator 5 (rework/defect latency) is a **validity check on indicator 4** and must never be omitted when error rate is reported.
5. All thresholds (e.g., WIP 70) are shown as *estimated* knees with confidence bands, not as fixed truths, and are re-estimated monthly from segmented regressions.
