**Interference, exposure-induced confounding, treatment-confounder feedback:**  
Interference arises because flagged cases share manual-review and escalation queues with all cases; one flagged case’s mandatory manual review occupies capacity and increases wait for later-arriving cases (flagged or unflagged), violating the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA). Exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding is present because `U_underlying_risk` and `complexity` affect both the flag (exposure) and the mediators `manual_review`, `escalation`, and their waiting times, creating a need for more than standard covariate adjustment. Treatment-confounder feedback is less severe here because risk is not updated mid-process, but queue length can feed back into triage prioritization decisions if resources become overloaded, creating dynamic confounding.

**Recommended pilot: P2 – Keep priority triage, remove automatic mandatory manual review.**  

*Eligibility:* All cases that would normally receive a flag (risk_score  threshold, e.g., 0.6) during the pilot period.  
*Assignment rule:* Randomized at case level: 50% receive the current P0 policy (flag + triage priority + automatic manual review); 50% receive the P2 policy (flag + triage priority, but manual review is left to triage nurse’s judgment without automatic routing). The flag is still displayed to indicate elevated risk.  
*Queue-capacity guardrail:* Monitor manual-review queue length daily. If the average wait for manually reviewed cases exceeds 8hours for two consecutive days, pause the P2 arm and revert to automatic review for flagged cases until queue clears; capacity is not increased during the pilot.  
*Primary outcome:* Total cycle time (arrival to close).  
*Secondary outcomes:* Rejection rate, rate of subsequent adverse events (if tracked), manual-review rate in the P2 arm, escalation rate, and triage-to-manual-start wait for flagged cases.  
*Minimum process data needed:* For each case, risk_score, flag status, timestamps for all stages, final outcome, and the reviewer’s decision to route to manual review (P2 arm).

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk dominates:** If a perfect measure of underlying risk (e.g., a later audit) shows that flagged cases have much higher intrinsic processing time even without the flag, and that the association between flag and cycle time vanishes after adjusting for this measure, then the flag itself is not the primary cause of extended cycle time.  
2. **Queue interference is negligible:** If queue lengths at manual review and escalation are independent of the volume of flagged cases (e.g., capacity is perfectly elastic), then the spillover mechanism is weak and the delay is purely per-case compositional, reversing the recommendation to add protected capacity (P3).  
3. **Decision quality degrades sharply without mandatory review:** Should a pilot of P2 show that rejection (or an independent error metric) increases substantially for high-risk unflagged cases when manual review is discretionary, the quality risk would outweigh cycle-time gains, favoring retention of automatic review.  
4. **Timestamp semantics are misleading:** If “triage_start” for priority-lane cases is recorded when the nurse picks up the file but not when the patient actually arrives, the apparent triage acceleration may be an artifact. An audit showing systematic measurement bias would nullify the triage speed advantage.  
5. **Positivity/overlap failure:** If the risk-score threshold separating flag from no-flag is so sharp that no unflagged cases have comparable complexity and risk, all regression or matching estimates become extrapolations; the inability to credibly adjust for confounding would undermine the entire mediation analysis.  
6. **Behavioral adaptation by reviewers:** If, after removing automatic mandatory review, reviewers learn to manually route virtually all the same cases to review anyway (relying on the risk score), P2 would devolve into P0, and cycle time would not improve. This would reverse the conclusion that P2 meaningfully reduces load.

## 9. Final synthesis

**What is directly observed:** The data show that flagged cases receive triage 10–12 times faster than unflagged cases (median wait 0.2h vs 2.5h) but subsequently spend 4–9 extra hours waiting for manual review and another several hours in escalation, resulting in a total cycle time roughly three times longer (16.5h vs 5.8h). These raw contrasts are unmistakable.

**Plausible mechanism:** The flag bundles a priority triage lane (direct acceleration) with a mandatory manual review routing rule (indirect delay). Because flagged cases carry higher risk and complexity, they also naturally require more service and are more likely to be escalated. In addition, the sudden injection of mandatory reviews into a shared queue likely causes congestion that spills over to unflagged cases, making all manual-review waits longer. The net outcome is a classic “fast triage, slow downstream” pattern driven by a mix of per-case treatment and system-level interference.

**What remains unidentified:** We cannot separate the causal effect of the flag from that of the underlying risk without strong assumptions. The magnitude of interference is unknown, as is the decision-quality benefit of mandatory review. The absence of a credible comparison group with high risk but no flag prevents clean estimation of the flag’s direct contribution to downstream waiting versus compositional delay. Additionally, the small sample size and lack of pre-flag data limit power.

**Most useful next evidence:** A randomized encouragement pilot that suppresses the flag display while leaving the risk score visible (P2/P1 hybrid) would create a contrast between the full policy bundle (flag + automatic review) and a world where only the risk information drives downstream decisions. This design directly identifies the total causal effect of the flagging policy without requiring cross-world assumptions, provides a clean test of whether the flag’s automatic routing is necessary for safety, and generates credible evidence on interference by observing queue dynamics during control and treatment periods.
