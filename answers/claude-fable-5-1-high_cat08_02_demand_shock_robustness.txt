# Robust redesign of the diagnostic-lab sample process

## Preliminary: what the two logs actually say (evidence base used below)

Before defining scenarios, a few derived numbers that drive the whole analysis. Weekly figures are normal (8 weeks, 18,400 cases = 2,300/week) versus peak (3 weeks, 9,900 cases = 3,300/week).

| Quantity | Normal /week | Peak /week | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| All cases | 2,300 | 3,300 | +43% |
| Routine cases | ~1,403 | ~1,386 | **-1% (flat)** |
| Urgent cases | ~414 | ~957 | **+131%** |
| Complex-panel cases | ~299 | ~660 | **+121%** |
| Repeat-after-failure cases | ~184 | ~297 | +61% |
| Assay runs (incl. batch and repeat re-entries) | 2,283 | 3,417 | +50% |
| Repeat assays triggered | 143 | 420 | **+194%** (6.2% -> 12.3% of runs) |
| Entries into Medical validation | 530 | 1,367 | **+158%** |
| Entries into Hold for specialist review | 36 | 203 | **+460%** |
| Recollection requests (share of received samples) | 4.6% | 9.1% | doubled; 35% -> 42% of them end in cancellation |
| Manual accession corrections | 63 | 140 | +124%, on a pool of 2 people |
| Test preparation -> Batch wait share | 4.4% | 13.3% | tripled; Urgent bypass fell 74% -> 58% |

Three structural conclusions follow:

1. **The peak is not a volume problem; it is a mix problem.** Routine volume is essentially unchanged. Everything that grows is the Urgent/Complex/Repeat work that needs machines twice, medical validators, and specialists. Interventions that target Routine efficiency (I2, I9) can only help by freeing shared capacity; they do nothing about the demand that actually grew.
2. **Downstream escalation is amplified beyond the mix shift.** The Urgent+Complex share rose 1.6x (31% -> 49%), but Technical validation -> Medical validation rose from 24.5% to 47% of TV output (1.9x), and Hold for specialist review from 1.8% to 8.4% of TV output (4.7x). Part of this is genuinely sicker winter samples; part is almost certainly behavioural: technical validators running at 101% utilisation push borderline decisions upward, and "Hold" becomes a parking lot when Medical validation is congested (22h median queue). The peak log also shows 840 more exits from Technical validation than entries (8,080 vs 7,240), which is consistent with hidden send-back loops (Medical validation -> Technical validation) that the DFG does not display. This should be verified in the raw event log before any validator-capacity decision.
3. **The Urgent SLA is already fragile in the normal period.** Urgent p90 is 19h against an 18h penalty threshold in the normal period; at peak the median is 17h and the p90 is 33h. At peak an Urgent order that is caught in a batch loses roughly 36h (Test preparation -> Batch wait 14h + Batch wait -> Assay run 22h), and one that needs Medical validation waits 24h on the TV -> MV edge alone. Either event alone is a contractual breach. Urgent performance at peak is therefore decided by two binary events (was it batched? did it need MV?) rather than by average speed.

Priority is a scarce resource: at most one case class can be first in line at each station. The redesign below is explicit about who yields to whom in each scenario.

## 1. Stress scenarios

The scenarios differ in which case type grows and which resource is removed, not just in total volume. Each is a plausible winter reality; S5 is their realistic combination.

**S1 - Winter wave mix shift (demand-type shock).** Volume +40-50% with Urgent share ~30% and Complex-panel share ~20%, as in the peak log; Routine flat. Duration 3-5 weeks. Additional feature: more abnormal results, so the TV -> MV share rises above 45%. Stressed resources: Assay machines, Technical validators, Medical validators, Specialist review, Accession correction.

**S2 - Assay-machine degradation or outage (equipment shock).** Two sub-cases: (S2a) M2 develops an elevated failure rate (repeat share from 6% to 15%+ on M2 runs, clustered on night shifts) while nominally available; (S2b) M1 is down for 3-5 days for a service call, leaving only the faster-but-flakier M2. Assume normal or mildly elevated demand. Stressed resources: Assay capacity, Repeat loop, Preparation benches (prepared samples waiting past stability), Technical validators (more repeat interpretation).

**S3 - Medical validator / specialist absence (validation shock).** The every-second-Friday specialist absence coincides with one medical validator on sick leave for 1-2 weeks, during a Complex-heavy mix (Complex 18-20%). Stressed resources: Medical validation, Hold for specialist review, and the Complex-panel class specifically; Urgent cases needing MV are collateral damage.

**S4 - Courier arrival compression or delay (intake shock).** Weather or driver shortage collapses routes so that 60-70% of the day's samples land in the 17:00 wave, several hours late, with degraded sample quality (temperature, haemolysis, clotting) from the extended transport. Stressed resources: Courier intake, Registration, Pre-analytical check, Accession correction; then a synchronous wave hits Preparation and Assay late in the evening when staffing is thinnest, feeding the night-shift repeat cluster.

**S5 - Compound scenario (the realistic bad week).** S1 mix shift plus one snow day (S4) plus the second-Friday specialist absence (S3) in the same week, and M2 running with its normal higher failure rate on the night shift after the snow-day wave. This is the scenario the redesign must degrade gracefully under, not survive perfectly.

**S6 - Recollection storm (quality shock at the source).** New collection sites or seasonal phlebotomy staff push the pre-analytical rejection rate from 4.6% to 10-12%. Volume unchanged. Stressed resources: Pre-analytical check, Accession correction, Registration clerks (physician calls), and the Receive sample step (recollected samples re-enter intake 42-60h later, in the middle of the next day's peaks).

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The general mechanism in this process: the stations form a chain in which utilisation at peak is 85-101% at seven consecutive stations. Relieving any one station releases a wave into the next, and since queue time grows non-linearly above ~85% utilisation, a station at 93% (Medical validation) or 98% (Assay) turns a modest extra inflow into a very large extra queue. The migration paths below use the DFG and edge timings.

### S1 - Winter mix shift

1. **First bottleneck: Assay machines (98%).** Runs per week rise 50% against a 43% case increase because repeats double and batch re-entries triple. Test preparation -> Assay run and Batch wait -> Assay run edges stretch (Batch wait -> Assay run 9h -> 22h). Urgent bypass of batching falls from 74% to 58%: the benches, under pressure to keep setup efficiency, put more Urgent samples into batches. That is the single largest driver of Urgent breaches.
2. **Immediately behind: Technical validators (101%).** Anything that unblocks machines (I3, repeat reduction, I8) increases the flow into a station already over capacity. Technical validators respond by escalating (TV -> MV share 47%, Hold share 8.4%), which is bottleneck migration by behaviour rather than by volume.
3. **Then Medical validation (93%, 22h queue) and Specialist review (87%, 36h queue).** MV inflow is +158%/week. A Complex case that enters Hold spends 12h + 48h on the two Hold edges at peak. Once MV is congested, Hold is used as a waiting room, which shows up as the 5.6x increase in Hold entries. Adding MV capacity (I5) without changing the escalation rules would pull work out of Hold faster but leave the escalation rate untouched; the queue then reforms at MV as soon as the evening shift ends.
4. **Sideways: Accession correction (91%, 14h queue, two people).** Correction volume doubles. Its 16h Manual correction -> Test preparation edge at peak means corrected samples arrive at benches a shift later, in unpredictable lumps that break batch planning. Corrections unblock many cases, so this small pool has a leverage effect on WIP.
5. **If Urgent-first (I1) is applied strictly**: Routine and Complex-panel accumulate in front of preparation and assay; WIP rises; Routine samples wait past their stability windows and generate repeats; Complex panels miss the specialist's available days and land on the Friday without the specialist. The bottleneck moves from "Urgent lateness" to "Complex p90 explodes and repeat share rises", both of which consume machine time again.

### S2 - Machine degradation / outage

1. **S2a (M2 flaky).** The first symptom is not in the assay queue but in the repeat loop: Assay -> Repeat -> Assay costs 8h + 26h at peak. Repeat share doubling costs ~10% of machine capacity, which moves the machines from 89-90% effective to 98%. If I3 is active (Routine on M2), the Routine class carries the repeat burden and Repeat-after-failure p90 (already 240h) worsens; since repeats also traverse Technical validation, TV inflow rises. The 80/120 Repeat assay -> Medical validation edges (repeats that skip technical validation entirely) also grow: this is a quality-control shortcut under pressure, and it will become more common when the repeat queue is long.
2. **S2b (M1 down).** All Urgent and Complex work lands on M2, whose higher failure rate now hits time-critical classes. An Urgent repeat loop (34h at peak) is an automatic breach. Preparation benches become a de facto buffer: prepared samples age in front of the one machine, which raises pre-analytical failure at the assay stage, which raises repeats: a positive feedback loop. Any dedication rule keyed to a specific machine (I3) simply ceases to exist.
3. **Migration on recovery.** When M1 returns, the accumulated backlog is run through in 1-2 days and lands on Technical validation and Medical validation as a single wave. The validation queue after the outage is usually worse than the machine queue during it.

### S3 - Medical validator / specialist absence

1. **First: Specialist review.** With one specialist away, capacity halves on a station already at 87% at peak; the queue on Hold -> Medical validation (48h at peak) becomes multi-day. Complex-panel p90 (210h) is dominated by this edge.
2. **Second: Medical validation.** MV needs specialist input for held cases; held cases stay open on the MV worklist, cluttering it and slowing the Urgent cases that need MV only for a quick sign-off. Urgent-with-MV p90 breaches even though Urgent volume did not change. This is the clearest case of one class (Complex) starving another (Urgent) at a shared station.
3. **Third: Release and physician contact.** Held Complex results generate physician enquiries, which land on the Registration clerks (the same phenomenon the variant notes describe for recollection loops). Intake capacity is quietly eroded.
4. **If I10 (partner lab) is switched on for Complex overflow**: transport adds 12h, traceability work lands on Accession correction (two people), and the partner's own capacity is unknown. If switched on late (after the queue has formed), the cases sent away were already 48h old and arrive back after the specialist has returned.

### S4 - Courier compression / delay

1. **First: Courier intake and Registration (85-88%).** A 17:00 wave carrying most of the day's samples hits registration at the end of the shift; overtime past 9h raises registration error rates, which feeds Accession correction the next morning.
2. **Second: Pre-analytical check (96%).** Receive sample -> Pre-analytical rises from 2h to 9h. Late, warm samples fail more often, so recollection (9.1%) and correction (4.3%) rise together. Recollected samples re-enter intake 42-60h later, i.e. inside the next day's 10:00 or 17:00 spike, making the following days' peaks worse: intake shocks are self-propagating over a 2-3 day horizon.
3. **Third: night-shift assay.** The compressed wave reaches preparation late in the evening, and the night shift runs it on M2 (fast), which is exactly the condition under which repeats cluster. Urgent cases from the 17:00 wave have their 18h clock running through the night with the least staffed validation coverage; they hit Medical validation at 08:00 together with everything else.
4. **If I7 (slot smoothing) succeeds**: intake is relieved but Pre-analytical and Assay see a continuous stream rather than lumps. This is good for those stations but it removes the "quiet hours" that the two accession specialists and the benches used to catch up on corrections and calibrations. Steady flow with no slack hours is fragile against any single failure.
5. **If I8 (registration automation) is deployed**: registration is no longer the constraint; the exposed bottleneck is Pre-analytical at 96%, exactly as the conflict hint says. Automation also removes the human check that today catches some metadata errors, so accession-correction demand may rise even as registration demand falls.

### S5 - Compound week

The order of failure is: intake wave (Tue snow) -> pre-analytical and correction backlog (Tue-Wed) -> night-shift repeat cluster (Tue night) -> assay and TV saturation (Wed-Thu) -> escalation to MV and Hold (Thu) -> specialist absent Friday -> Complex cases that entered Hold on Thursday are released the following Tuesday, and the Urgent cases that shared the MV worklist with them breach on Friday. The mechanism that links the intake shock to the validation shock is escalation behaviour under load, so a redesign that only adds capacity at the ends will not break the chain.

### S6 - Recollection storm

Pre-analytical is the first bottleneck, but its rejects create work at three other places: Registration (physician calls), Receive sample (re-entry 42-60h later), and Cancel (42% of recollections at peak are lost orders, which is a clinical and revenue problem, not a flow problem). Fixing pre-analytical throughput (I6) without fixing the source (collection-site feedback) raises the rejection rate further, because a faster, stricter triage rejects more. The bottleneck then migrates outside the lab, to the collection sites and the couriers.

## 3. Robust redesign package

These interventions are chosen because they improve behaviour in at least three of the six scenarios and do not require guessing the mix. Each states the assumptions it depends on and where it fails.

### R1. Replace fixed priority with slack-based dispatch plus a capped Urgent reservation (replaces I1)

At preparation, assay and both validation worklists, sort by *remaining slack to due time* (Urgent 18h, Routine 72h, Complex-panel and Repeat 72h or the contractual target), not by class. Add a hard rule: an Urgent order never enters "Batch wait"; it goes into the next express run, and express runs start at least every 2h during the day. Reserve a capped share of assay slots and one MV sign-off slot per hour for Urgent; Urgent volume above the cap queues by slack like everyone else.

- *Why robust*: in the normal mix it behaves like FIFO with Urgent bypass; in S1 it protects Urgent automatically as the Urgent share grows while preventing Routine starvation, because Routine slack shrinks as it ages and it eventually out-ranks a fresh Urgent order that still has 16h. It works whichever machine is running (S2) and does not depend on who is present (S3). It directly addresses the peak observation that 42% of Urgent orders were batched.
- *Assumptions*: due times are known at registration (case type is recorded correctly); the LIS can sort worklists by a computed field; the cap for Urgent slots is set from the observed Urgent arrival rate (~140/day at peak), not from the normal 60/day.
- *Where it fails*: slack-based sorting produces synchronised deadlines: many Routine cases with similar registration times reach zero slack together and demand machines at the same moment. If the cap is set too low in S1, Urgent breaches; too high, Routine WIP grows. It cannot conjure capacity: in S5 something will breach, and this policy makes the choice explicit (Routine target slips to ~96h) rather than accidental.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: Technical validation receives Urgent work in a steadier stream, which is manageable, but MV receives Urgent sign-offs that jump the Complex queue; Complex-panel waiting at MV grows unless R4 is in place.

### R2. Front-end defect prevention: early triage (I6) + digital registration (I8) + arrival smoothing (I7), deployed together with a source-feedback loop

Introduce a 2h pre-analytical defect triage at receipt (I6), automate registration only for orders with complete digital metadata (I8), and negotiate courier slots so that no single arrival wave exceeds ~35% of daily volume (I7). Add weekly rejection feedback to each collection site.

- *Why robust*: each recollection removes 42-60h from one case, consumes intake and pre-analytical capacity twice, and generates physician calls that erode registration. Halving the peak recollection rate (9.1% -> ~5%) frees roughly 4% of intake capacity and reduces re-entries into the intake spikes, which helps S1, S4, S5 and S6. Early triage also shortens the recollection loop itself (the physician is called on day 0 instead of day 1), improving the recollection -> cancellation ratio (42% at peak). I8 frees clerk time for the calls that remain and for the 17:00 wave.
- *Assumptions*: digital metadata is available for a meaningful share of orders (if under 40%, I8 is not worth the exposure); couriers have slot flexibility (S4 weather scenarios remove it); the triage step is staffed at 10:00 and 17:00, otherwise it is a new queue.
- *Where it fails*: as the conflict hint says, I8 exposes Pre-analytical (96%) and Assay (98%). Faster registration into a saturated pre-analytical station is zero-sum unless the triage step (I6) is the same people doing a shorter check. Stricter triage raises the *rejection* rate before it lowers it; the first two weeks will look worse. Automated registration removes a human check, so some errors now surface at Accession correction (two people, 91%) instead of at Registration (68-85%): the redesign moves a cheap correction to an expensive one unless the automation includes validation rules for the fields that cause corrections today.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: Pre-analytical check, Accession correction, and then Assay.

### R3. Flexible validator/specialist pool with guard rails (modifies I4)

Cross-train two technical validators for accession correction (I4), but also cross-train two registration/pre-analytical staff for accession correction, and formalise that Medical validators can perform Technical validation for their own Urgent/Complex cases. Floating rules: a technical validator may float to correction only when the TV queue is under 4h; a registration cross-trainee floats to correction whenever the correction queue exceeds 6h. Training takes 4 weeks, so it must start in autumn.

- *Why robust*: Accession correction is the highest-leverage small pool (two people, corrections unblock many cases, 14h peak queue). A four-person flexible pool removes the single-pool fragility in S1, S4, S6 and during S3 (when validators are stuck waiting for the specialist, they can absorb corrections). It also lets validators cover the 17:00 wave in S4.
- *Assumptions*: cross-trained staff maintain competence (minimum weekly hours in the second role); regulatory sign-off allows the second role; the TV queue threshold is enforced by a dispatcher, not by goodwill.
- *Where it fails*: at peak, Technical validation is at 101% and the conflict hint is correct: without the 4h guard rail, I4 converts a 14h correction queue into a longer TV queue and more escalations to MV. In S5 the guard rail is never satisfied and the correction relief must come from the registration-side trainees only.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: Technical validation (if guard rail ignored); Preparation benches (corrected samples arrive in lumps).

### R4. Escalation hygiene and consolidated Medical validation (demand shaping at MV/specialist, no new headcount)

Define explicit escalation criteria for TV -> MV and TV -> Hold, with auto-release rules for Routine results within reference ranges and delta-check limits (already 74% of TV output releases directly in the normal period; the target is to keep that share above 65% at peak rather than falling to 56%). Complex-panel orders receive one consolidated MV review per order, not one per assay, and Hold is only permitted with a named specialist and an expected review date; anything else stays on the MV worklist. Specialist review is scheduled: Complex panels are run Monday-Wednesday so that Hold cases reach the specialist before the second-Friday absence; a remote review slot on the absent Friday is pre-agreed.

- *Why robust*: the peak log shows escalation growing faster than mix (TV -> MV 1.9x, Hold 4.7x against mix 1.6x). Removing the behavioural component returns MV to ~75-80% at peak mix and shrinks Hold back toward its normal 2% share. This helps S1, S3 and S5 and costs nothing in S2 and S4.
- *Assumptions*: the escalation increase is partly behavioural (must be verified: compare abnormal-flag rates on escalated vs released results); medical governance accepts the auto-release rules; specialists accept scheduled rather than ad hoc review.
- *Where it fails*: if the winter results are genuinely more abnormal, escalation rates will stay high and MV capacity (I5) is unavoidable; the rule set must then not be tightened further, because that trades flow for clinical risk. Consolidated Complex review holds the whole order for its slowest assay, which lengthens Complex median slightly in normal periods.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: Release result (more direct releases; low risk), and physician enquiries to registration if auto-released borderline results generate questions.

### R5. Dynamic, time-boxed batching and WIP caps (replaces I2 and I9)

Batches close on whichever comes first: size (12 default; up to 24 for Routine only when no Routine case in the batch has less than 30h slack) or a time limit (90 minutes during the day). A WIP cap sits between preparation and assay: when the assay queue exceeds 6h of work, benches stop preparing Routine samples and instead prepare only cases with under 24h slack, because prepared samples waiting more than a few hours raise the repeat rate.

- *Why robust*: it captures the setup efficiency of larger batches when the lab is quiet (normal period, Routine-heavy) and switches itself off when slack disappears (S1, S2). The WIP cap breaks the "prepared samples age -> repeats -> more machine load" loop that appears in S2 and S4.
- *Assumptions*: bench staff can see slack and assay queue on their worklist; the stability window per assay is documented so the cap is set from evidence.
- *Where it fails*: in S2b (single machine), the cap causes benches to idle while a queue exists at the machine, which looks wasteful and will be overridden by managers unless the rationale is understood. Large batches during quiet nights land on Technical validation at 07:00 as a lump; the batch policy should therefore prohibit 24-batches on the night shift.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: Technical validation (morning lump); Repeat loop if the cap is ignored.

### R6. Repeat-reduction programme aimed at the M2/night-shift cluster

Investigate the variant evidence (repeats clustered after night shifts and on M2): shift-start QC on M2, a per-assay list of M2-fragile assays that run on M1 by default, calibration schedule aligned to night staffing, and a rule that a repeat must pass Technical validation (closing the Repeat assay -> Medical validation shortcut, 80 normal / 120 peak cases).

- *Why robust*: repeats are the only demand the lab creates for itself. At peak they consume ~10.5% of assay runs and 34h of elapsed time per affected case; halving them takes the machines from 98% to roughly 93% effective utilisation, which in queueing terms is the difference between an 18h and a ~6h median queue. This helps every scenario that touches the machines (S1, S2, S4, S5) and reduces TV load.
- *Assumptions*: the cluster has an assignable cause (QC, temperature, operator experience at night) rather than being a property of the sample population; M1 has spare night capacity for the fragile assays.
- *Where it fails*: if the cause is winter sample quality, the programme yields little and the lab must fall back on capacity policies; routing fragile assays to M1 increases M1 load and lengthens Urgent waits when M1 is the reserved machine.
- *Downstream station it may overload*: M1 (fragile-assay routing); Technical validation (repeats that previously skipped it).

### Explicit priority order under stress (not everyone can be first)

- Station-level default: slack-based.
- When slack ties or the cap binds: Urgent (penalty) > Complex-panel (specialist calendar dependency) > Repeat-after-failure (already old; keep moving) > Routine (widest tolerance).
- Routine floor: no Routine case waits behind a fresh Urgent case once its age exceeds 60h; Routine target is formally relaxed to 96h during a declared peak and communicated to requesters, so the trade-off is decided rather than suffered.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Each policy has an on-trigger, an off-trigger and a named downstream risk. Policies are declared by a daily 08:30 flow meeting using the dashboard in section 7; nothing switches on by inertia.

### P1. Peak mode (S1, S5): evening medical-validation shift (I5) and Urgent cap raised

- *Actions*: evening MV shift 16:00-22:00; Urgent reservation raised from ~25% to ~40% of assay slots; Routine target relaxed to 96h with requester notification; 24-batch permission withdrawn for daytime.
- *On*: any two of: Urgent+Complex share of daily intake above 40% for 3 consecutive days; MV median queue above 6h for 2 days; oldest Urgent case on the MV worklist with under 4h slack at 15:00.
- *Off*: MV queue under 3h and Urgent+Complex share under 35% for 5 consecutive days.
- *Downstream risk*: the evening shift releases a wave of Complex results that generate physician calls the next morning on registration; the evening shift may also take TVs' escalations as a free service and mask R4. Watch TV -> MV share; if it rises during P1, R4 is being bypassed.

### P2. Degraded-machine mode (S2a, S2b): time-critical reservation on the healthy machine; Routine overflow externally

- *Actions*: healthy machine reserved for cases with under 24h slack; remaining slots batched (24) for Routine with the WIP cap active; M2-fragile assays suspended on M2 (S2a) or run at reduced batch size with duplicate QC (S2b); Routine (not Complex) overflow to the partner lab, because 72h Routine tolerates 12h transport while 18h Urgent does not; Repeat-after-failure cases are given TV pre-review of the failure cause before re-running to avoid a second failure.
- *On*: machine availability under 80% of planned hours, or M2 repeat share above 10% for two consecutive shifts.
- *Off*: 48h after both machines are back and the assay queue is under 4h.
- *Downstream risk*: recovery wave onto Technical and Medical validation when the machine returns; the healthy machine's own failure risk rises when run continuously. Plan the recovery day with R3 floaters on TV, not on corrections.

### P3. Validation-absence mode (S3): scheduled specialist cover and Complex overflow (I10)

- *Actions*: the second-Friday absence is pre-planned every cycle: Complex panels registered Wednesday afternoon onward are flagged, and those likely to need Hold (based on panel type and the 2.4x variant statistic) are either run Thursday morning or sent to the partner lab at registration, not after 48h in Hold; remote specialist review slot booked; Hold is closed to new entries without a named reviewer; Urgent MV slots protected first.
- *On*: forecast specialist capacity under 1 FTE for any day in the next 72h, or Hold -> MV median above 24h, or MV headcount down by one for more than 2 days.
- *Off*: full complement returned and Hold queue under 12h.
- *Downstream risk*: Accession correction absorbs partner-lab traceability (two people); partner turnaround is not under the lab's control; Complex cases sent out cannot be recalled if the specialist returns early. Cap partner volume at what correction can trace (suggest 20 orders/day) and never send Urgent.

### P4. Intake-surge mode (S4): pre-registration, staffed triage windows, night-shift protection

- *Actions*: I8 pre-registers digital orders before samples arrive so the 17:00 wave is receipt-only; two R3 cross-trainees join pre-analytical triage 16:30-19:30; the night shift is told which Urgent cases are in the wave and runs them on M1 first; Routine from the wave is held (WIP cap) rather than prepared at 22:00; the following morning's 10:00 wave is pre-warned to collection sites so that recollections from the wave are not sent into it.
- *On*: any single arrival window carrying more than 40% of expected daily samples, or courier intake queue above 3h, or a weather warning for the route area.
- *Off*: two consecutive days with intake queue under 1.5h.
- *Downstream risk*: pre-analytical at 96% and Assay at 98% receive the wave faster (the I8 conflict); Registration overtime past 9h raises errors, so overtime is capped and pre-registration used instead; Repeat-after-failure grows if the night shift ignores the M1-first instruction.

### P5. Recollection-storm mode (S6)

- *Actions*: daily site-level rejection report; a phlebotomist sent to any site above 12% rejection; recollected samples given a fixed 14:00 courier slot so they avoid the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes; physician communication templated and moved off the registration clerks to a nurse liaison.
- *On*: pre-analytical rejection above 7% of received samples for 3 days, or any site above 15%.
- *Off*: rejection under 5% for 5 days.
- *Downstream risk*: if the triage becomes stricter as a reaction, cancellations (42% of recollections at peak) rise; monitor recollection -> cancel ratio, not just rejection rate.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Compound weeks still breach.** Under S5 the lab is short of machine hours and validator hours at the same time. The redesign makes the breach fall on Routine (96h) by design, but if the Urgent share exceeds ~35% of intake, the Urgent cap saturates and Urgent breaches return. No dispatch rule fixes a true capacity shortfall.

2. **Caused by the redesign: slack synchronisation.** Slack-based dispatch (R1) makes all Routine cases registered in the same morning reach low slack together, producing a deterministic demand pulse at Assay and TV roughly 48-60h later. In a 3-week peak these pulses recur every day at the same hour. Mitigation: randomise or stagger Routine due times within the 72h window; monitor the hourly variance of assay queue length.

3. **Caused by the redesign: Technical validation as the sink.** R3 (floating validators), R4 (repeats must pass TV) and R6 (repeats validated) all add work to the station that is already at 101% at peak. If the escalation-hygiene rules (R4) do not reduce TV effort per case, TV becomes the new dominant bottleneck and the behavioural escalation resumes. Cross-training must also run in the other direction (MV staff able to do TV for their own cases), and the floater guard rail must be enforced.

4. **Caused by the redesign: automation moves errors downstream.** I8 removes the clerk's implicit check; metadata errors surface at Accession correction (two people plus trainees) or, worse, at Medical validation as wrong-patient/wrong-panel risk. Validation rules in the automated path must be built from the current correction categories before go-live.

5. **Caused by the redesign: stricter triage raises cancellations.** I6 will reject more samples before it rejects fewer. With 42% of recollections at peak already ending in cancellation, an aggressive triage converts a delayed result into no result. The recollection -> cancel ratio, not the rejection rate, is the safety metric.

6. **Partner-lab dependency (P2, P3).** The partner's capacity is presumably also under winter pressure; 12h transport is a median, not a guarantee; traceability lands on the smallest pool in the lab. Outsourcing is a scenario-specific release valve, not a plan.

7. **Specialist single point of failure remains.** Scheduling around the second Friday helps; an unplanned two-week specialist absence in a Complex-heavy wave has no in-house answer. Hold entries would have to be capped and requesters informed.

8. **Data quality of the process-mining evidence itself.** 10.7% of normal-period and 13.3% of peak-period cases have no END in the log, TV outflow exceeds inflow by 840 at peak, and 40-50 cases vanish between Batch wait/Hold and their successors. Some of this is snapshot truncation (peak WIP), some may be hidden loops. The escalation-hygiene programme (R4) depends on the TV -> MV numbers being real; verify in the raw event log before setting targets.

9. **Human override of WIP caps and float rules.** Idle benches in front of a queue (S2b) and validators refusing to float are predictable. Rules that only work with discipline fail in exactly the weeks they are needed unless the worklist enforces them.

### Lagging outcome metrics (weekly, to confirm rather than steer)

- Urgent p90 cycle time and % released after 18h (contractual exposure).
- Routine p90 and % over 72h (or 96h when peak mode declared).
- Complex-panel p90 and median Hold -> MV time.
- Repeat-after-failure median and p90.
- Overall rework % and recollection %.
- % cases with at least one queue over 12h (target: back toward the 22% normal level during peaks, not the 54% observed).

- Policy log: which scenario modes were on which days, and whether the trigger and release thresholds were respected, so that next winter's thresholds are tuned from evidence rather than from the same historical averages this redesign is built to avoid.
