Normalization is applied **before** four-eyes checks, because an un-normalized log makes a self-approval look like two distinct people (this is exactly what happens in CHG-508).

Service-account attribution rule: `svc_qms` is *never* a human key by default. It is materialized as a distinct pseudo-human `SVC:svc_qms` with an attached `attributed_human` field and an `attribution_strength` in {NONE, WEAK, STRONG}. Only STRONG attribution (individual re-authentication or a possession/biometric factor tied to one person at the event time) is allowed to substitute the human key in SoD checks; WEAK attribution (room badge + shared terminal, no re-auth) is recorded as a *hypothesis* and the event is simultaneously flagged as an RR5 evidentiary defect regardless of which human is hypothesized. This means a WEAK-attributed event can be an RR5 nonconformance even if the underlying human would have been perfectly authorized.

## 2. Rule Interpretation

Formalization. Let `H(e)` be the normalized human key of event `e`, `case(e)` its case, `t(e)` its timestamp, and `role(h, t)` the role held by `h` at time `t` (evaluated from the *role interval table*, never from current master data).

- **RR1** — for every case `c`: ¬∃ `d ∈ Draft_Change_Request(c)`, `a ∈ Approve_Change(c)` with `H(d) = H(a)` and `H(·)` human. Violated ⇒ four-eyes failure on authorization.
- **RR2** — for every case `c`: ¬∃ `i ∈ Implement_Change(c)`, `v ∈ Verify_Implementation(c)` with `H(i) = H(v)`. If `H(v)` is a service account with WEAK attribution to `H(i)`, the rule is *not satisfied* — it is unproven, which is itself a finding.
- **RR3** — for `risk(c) = HIGH` and `a ∈ Approve_Change(c)`: `role(H(a), t(a)) = QM` **or** ∃ delegation `D` with `principal(D) = QM`, `delegate(D) = H(a)`, `t(a) ∈ interval(D)`, `scope(c) ⊆ scope(D)`, `activity(a) ⊆ permitted_acts(D)`, `absent(principal, t(a))` if `D` requires it, and `chain(D)` contains no prohibited subdelegation. All five conjuncts must hold; failing any one voids the substitution.
- **RR4** — for `risk(c) = HIGH ∧ emergency(c) = true`, approval by IC or a valid IC delegate is admissible **provided** ∃ `r ∈ Retrospective_QM_Review(c)` with `H(r)` QM or valid QM delegate *for review of that scope*, and `t(r) ≤ deadline(c)` where `deadline` is two business days after the triggering event. RR4 is a *conditional* exception: if the retrospective leg fails, the original approval reverts to an RR3 violation rather than remaining a justified exception.
- **RR5** — a service-account event carries conformance weight only under STRONG attribution; aliases are collapsed to human keys before RR1/RR2 are evaluated.
- **RR6** — pattern indicators (repeated pairs, reciprocity, shared workstations, handover concentration) raise investigation priority and may justify controls, but may never by themselves upgrade a classification to UNAUTHORIZED or to "collusion".

Business-day counting for RR4. Business days = Monday–Friday excluding site holidays; the site calendar shows no holiday in this window, and 2026-08-15/16 are the weekend. The clock starts at the **end of the emergency action being reviewed** — I take the `Implement_Change` timestamp, because that is when the unreviewed change reaches the plant. "Within two business days" means *by end of the second business day after the trigger day*. Trigger 2026-08-11 (Tuesday) ⇒ business day 1 = Wed 08-12, business day 2 = Thu 08-13 ⇒ deadline 2026-08-13 23:59 UTC. (A more lenient reading — 48 business hours from the trigger instant — gives 2026-08-13 13:10; both readings put the same cases on the same side of the line, so the ambiguity does not change any classification here.)

Action by a principal while on leave. Leave is an *availability* fact, not a *role* fact. Asha remains QM throughout; an approval by `u_as` on 08-13 is therefore role-conformant on its face. Two consequences follow. First, a leave record cannot by itself make her act unauthorized — it makes the act's *authenticity* the open question, and the correct treatment is an authenticity caveat, not a nonconformance. Second, the same act partially undermines the *absence precondition* of D-QM-01: if Asha was demonstrably working, delegate approvals made while she was reachable are weaker. I read the precondition narrowly and temporally — Asha was available only during the observed 09:15–09:40 window on 08-13, so delegate acts outside that window remain covered, but the precondition should be treated as "presumed satisfied, contradicted at the margin" rather than as firmly established.

Can a later database entry prove an earlier delegation? No — not on its own. A record created on 08-12 09:15 cannot, by itself, establish authority that was exercised on 08-11 09:00; that is retrospective self-authorization, and in a GxP context it would fail an ALCOA+ contemporaneity test. What rescues D-QM-01 here is the *independent, earlier* artifact: a signed PDF timestamped 08-09 18:00, before any use, with the migration providing a plausible innocent account of why the QMS row is late. So the correct posture is: the late DB row is corroboration of an already-evidenced delegation, not the delegation's proof. Evidence grade "moderate–good", and it would become "strong" only if the PDF's signature and timestamp are cryptographically verified and the migration batch is shown to have carried other records with the same lag.

## 4. Collusion and Alternative-Explanation Analysis

Repeated Clara→Ben pair (18 of 23 HIGH-risk production approvals in 60 days; Asha only 3). Benign: Clara and Ben are assigned to the same production cell, so co-occurrence is the expected consequence of work allocation, and with no pair-rotation policy nobody was asked to spread the work; the QM's low share is explained by leave and by delegation being the designed mechanism. Adverse: a fixed drafter/approver dyad erodes the independence four-eyes is meant to buy, and would be the natural substrate for a quid-pro-quo arrangement. Discriminating evidence: compare the pair's approval latency, rejection rate, and comment volume against site baselines — a near-zero rejection rate and sub-five-minute approvals on complex changes indicate rubber-stamping; also test whether the concentration exceeds what the cell-assignment roster mechanically predicts (if Ben is the only qualified approver in that cell, 18/23 is arithmetic, not affinity).

Reciprocal behavior (Ben drafted four LOW-risk training changes Clara approved). Benign: reciprocity is the ordinary shape of a two-person team covering each other's paperwork on low-risk items, and no financial benefit is known. Adverse: mutual approval capability establishes exactly the trust channel that later gets used on material changes; it also means each holds leverage over the other's record. Discriminating evidence: whether the reciprocal approvals cluster in time with the HIGH-risk approvals, whether any of them waived a QA step, and whether other pairs at the site show similar reciprocity (if reciprocity is site-wide, it is culture, not conspiracy). RR6 governs: none of this is proof.

Shared workstations (CHG-503 on WS-DM-04). Benign: shared terminals with long-lived service sessions are a common and tolerated legacy pattern on a plant floor, and Diego's badge entry proves only presence in a shared room. Adverse: an implementer using a service account to self-verify his own change is precisely the RR2 bypass the control exists to prevent. Discriminating evidence: interactive logon records and session-start times for WS-DM-04, whether the service session predated Diego's entry, application-level audit of which UI performed the verification, and the badge list of everyone else in the room at 12:30.

Absence conflicts. Two distinct patterns. (a) Asha acting on leave (CHG-504): benign — a conscientious QM handling one urgent case over VPN, which she has admitted; adverse — a shared credential used by someone who needed a QM stamp. Discriminator: device attestation and MFA logs for that VPN session, plus whether the session's activity profile matches Asha's normal behavior. (b) Ben's account acting during certified sick leave (CHG-506 approval, CHG-505 retrospective review): benign — Ben worked briefly while unwell, or a colleague used an already-open session administratively; adverse — someone appropriated a delegated approver's credential precisely because the delegation window was open. The office-IP detail is the sharpest fact here: it is *inconsistent* with the innocent "worked from home while sick" story and *consistent* with another person at the site. Discriminator: physical badge records for that office at 13:00 on 08-13 and 16:00 on 08-14, cross-referenced against everyone who could plausibly want those two specific actions completed.

Credential compromise as a unifying hypothesis. Three of the anomalies (CHG-504, CHG-506, CHG-505's review) share a shape: a privileged account acting when its owner is documented as unavailable. Benign reading: this is what happens in an organization where availability records are aspirational and people work through leave. Adverse reading: one or more accounts with QM-equivalent authority are being operated by someone else, and the delegation windows are being exploited as cover. Discriminating evidence: an authentication-forensics sweep — source IPs, device fingerprints, MFA method, and session-overlap analysis across all of `u_as`, `u_bk`, and `svc_qms` for 08-09 → 08-16. Overlapping concurrent sessions from different devices would be close to dispositive; a clean single-device pattern would substantially exonerate.

Handover concentration. The log shows a near-invariant Clara→(Ben|Asha)→Diego→Eva chain. Benign: a small site with one maintenance lead and one verifier has no alternative routing. Adverse: a single chain means a single point of collusion and no natural cross-check. Discriminator: the site's qualified-personnel matrix — concentration is only an indicator where alternatives existed and were not used.

The code decides mechanically: alias collapse, four-eyes outcomes under both the conservative and the counterfactual attribution, role membership at each event timestamp, each delegation conjunct (interval, scope, permitted activity, chain validity, absence precondition and its contradiction), RR4 business-day deadlines, and the RR5 service-account inventory. It deliberately does **not** decide: whether the 08-09 signed PDF is authentic; whether CHG-505's cooling change really falls under incident CI-44; who physically operated `u_bk` during Ben's sick leave or `u_as` during Asha's; whether a weekend counts as "formally unavailable" for D-QM-02; and whether any repeated pair reflects collusion. Those are evidentiary and interpretive judgments — the code surfaces the inputs and leaves the verdict to the investigator, exactly as RR6 requires.

## 6. Control Improvements

Delegation creation. Require delegations to be created *in the QMS* before first use, with a system-enforced rule that authority cannot be exercised before the record exists; retire signed-PDF-plus-later-entry as an acceptable pattern. Make scope structured rather than prose — an explicit list of (change category, risk class, permitted activities) — so that "QM approval for production equipment" cannot be stretched to cover a cooling-system retrospective review, as happened on CHG-505 and CHG-510. Encode `no_subdelegation` as a machine-checked attribute that makes any child delegation impossible to create, rather than a sentence a supervisor can override by email.

Effective-time recording. Store delegation validity as an explicit UTC interval with timezone and second-level precision, keep an immutable creation timestamp separate from the effective interval, and forbid retroactive effective dates entirely. During data migrations, preserve original creation timestamps and mark migrated rows so a late row is never mistaken for back-dating — the D-QM-01 ambiguity is purely a migration-hygiene artifact.

Principal availability. Define "absent" and "formally unavailable" in the SOP with objective tests (rostered off, out-of-office set, escalation call unanswered within N minutes), and require the system to record *which* test was satisfied at the moment a delegate acts. Integrate the leave/roster system with the QMS so an absence precondition is evaluated automatically. Add a hard interlock: if the principal's account is active in the same window, delegate approvals prompt for justification — that single control would have surfaced both the CHG-504 and CHG-509 questions at the time.

Alias management. Reconcile all accounts to a single person identifier in the IAM system, and key every SoD and four-eyes check on the person ID, never the login. Run a periodic duplicate-account detection sweep, deprovision legacy aliases such as `b.koch`, and retroactively re-run four-eyes analysis over the last 12 months on person-normalized data — CHG-508 shows the automated check is currently blind to this.

Individual authentication. Eliminate shared service accounts from any GxP-signed step; where automation genuinely must act, use a named non-repudiable system identity whose actions are labeled as automated and can never satisfy a human-verification requirement. Require step-up re-authentication (password + possession factor) at every approval and verification e-signature, and bind the signature to a device attestation. The CHG-509 pattern (badge + FIDO) is the right evidentiary standard; CHG-503 shows what happens without it.

Emergency review. Auto-create the Retrospective_QM_Review task the moment an emergency approval is recorded, with a system-computed business-day deadline, escalation to the Site Director at 75% of the window, and a hard block on closing the change while the review is open or was performed by someone outside QM scope. Require the emergency flag to carry a structured safety rationale and an incident reference, so a claim like CHG-505's link to CI-44 is verifiable rather than assumed.

Pair rotation. Introduce a formal rotation or concentration limit for drafter/approver pairs on HIGH-risk changes (for example, no pair to exceed a defined share per quarter), with a documented exception path when the qualified pool genuinely does not permit rotation. Qualify at least one additional approver per production cell so rotation is actually feasible — the 18-of-23 concentration is currently as much a resourcing constraint as a behavioral choice.

Network monitoring. Run the handover and working-together analyses on a schedule, tracking pair concentration, reciprocity, approval latency and rejection rates against site baselines, and route outliers to a human reviewer as *investigation triggers only*. Publish explicitly that these metrics never constitute findings on their own, so RR6 is embedded in the tooling rather than left to individual judgment.

Evidential retention. Retain authentication logs, VPN/session records, workstation interactive-logon history, badge events and CCTV for a period at least as long as the change-control record itself, and make them queryable jointly by case ID and timestamp. The two hardest cases here — CHG-503 and CHG-506 — are unresolvable mainly because the corroborating evidence either does not exist (no CCTV) or has not been joined to the process record.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

Of the ten cases, three are clean or defensible (CHG-501 a valid substitution, CHG-504 role-authorized with an authenticity caveat, CHG-505's approval leg a valid IC substitution). Four are genuine authority or four-eyes failures: CHG-507 (approval by someone who has never held QM authority), CHG-510 (self-approval plus a void subdelegation on an emergency change), CHG-505's retrospective leg (out of scope and late), and the two self-approvals CHG-502 and CHG-508. Two turn primarily on identity rather than authority (CHG-503, CHG-506), and one is genuinely indeterminate on a single unverified precondition (CHG-509).

Weighting by risk rather than count: roughly half of the material nonconformance is **genuine authority failure** — people acting outside any valid delegation, or delegations stretched beyond their written scope. This is not a paperwork problem; CHG-507 and CHG-510 would fail a regulatory inspection on their face. Perhaps a third is **identity and data quality** — the alias split that defeats the four-eyes check, service accounts performing GxP verifications, and privileged credentials active while their owners were recorded as unavailable. The remainder is **organizational-pattern risk**: an extreme drafter/approver concentration with no rotation policy, reciprocal low-risk approvals, and a single handover chain with no natural cross-check. On the evidence presented, none of the pattern indicators supports a collusion conclusion, and I do not draw one.

My principal uncertainty is attribution. For CHG-503, CHG-506 and the CHG-505 retrospective review, I cannot say who acted, and the classification would shift materially — in CHG-506's case from a data problem to a serious unauthorized approval, possibly a credential compromise — depending on what the authentication forensics show. A secondary uncertainty is definitional: whether "absent" in D-QM-02 encompasses a weekend determines whether CHG-509 is conformant or a fourth authority failure.

Three highest-priority investigations:

1. **Authentication forensics on `u_bk` for 2026-08-13 13:00 and 2026-08-14 16:00.** A privileged delegated approver's credential was used twice during certified sick leave, from an office IP inconsistent with the innocent explanation, and the owner denies one of the acts. Pull workstation logon records, badge data for that office, MFA method and device fingerprints, and interview everyone present. This is the single fact pattern most likely to indicate real compromise.
2. **Authority remediation for CHG-507 and CHG-510.** Both are unambiguous: an approval by a non-QM with no delegation, and a self-approval resting on an expressly prohibited subdelegation. Determine whether the affected product or equipment state requires impact assessment and possible retrospective quality review, establish whether the QMS performed any authority check at all (if not, that is a systemic validation defect), and address Farid's creation of D-IC-04 as a control breach in its own right.
3. **Service-account and alias eradication sweep.** Determine who verified CHG-503 — including whether the shared session predated Diego's entry — and in parallel re-run person-normalized four-eyes analysis across the past 12 months to find every self-approval currently hidden by duplicate accounts. CHG-508 proves the detective control is blind; the question is how much it has already missed.
