# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee
**From:** Process Excellence / Process Mining
**Subject:** Next step for Redesign R (fiber installation)
**Decision requested:** Approve Alternative E — segment-gated deployment with hard controls and a controlled experiment. Do not scale nationally. Do not roll back.

**Bottom line.** Redesign R is not one intervention; it is four bundled changes: (1) single coordinator ownership, (2) merged validate/schedule for simple cases, (3) predictive survey skipping, (4) activation checks moved after billing start. The evidence says (1) and (2) work, and (3) and (4) cause most of the damage, concentrated in apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings and business customers. Scale what works where it works, stop what fails where it fails, and buy the information to decide the rest within one quarter.

**What we know (facts).** Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. But p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0; first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%; second visits rose from 11% to 18%; manual activation from 7% to 15%; 30-day complaints from 6.1% to 9.8%; SLA breaches from 13% to 16%. Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume) improved on every metric. Apartments, old buildings and business customers worsened on tail, first-time-right and complaints. Alignments show billing before a successful activation test, repeated installation visits without a survey, and missing permit checks in old buildings. Coordinator utilization reached 93% in two regions; second-visit team utilization rose from 66% to 92%.

**How we read it (interpretation).** The survey-skip rule misclassifies medium-risk apartments and the merged path drops the permit check for old buildings; the failed first visit then feeds an overloaded second-visit queue, and manual activation after billing start turns technical failures into billing complaints. Fewer handovers sped up simple cases but removed shared context, so cases stall when a coordinator is absent. The visible cost gain (EUR 184 to 161) is largely a scope artifact: it excludes complaint handling and stops after week 4. The best available all-in estimate (EUR 198 to 207) says R is currently cost-negative.

**What we do not know (uncertain).** Pilot regions had more apartments and old buildings than baseline, so headline comparisons are confounded. Segment attributes are missing before pilot week 3. Without start timestamps we cannot separate waiting from working time. Six weeks is too short for 60-day complaint and billing-dispute effects to surface.

**Recommendation (Alternative E, borrowing C's controls).**
1. Scale now, for simple low-risk houses only: merged validate/schedule plus coordinator ownership. Roughly one third of volume; -6 days median, -8 days p90, +3pp first-time-right, -1pp complaints.
2. Suspend immediately, everywhere: (a) survey skipping for apartments and old buildings — reinstate the survey and a mandatory permit check; (b) billing start before a passed activation test. Item (b) is a revenue-assurance and customer-fairness exposure, not a tuning parameter.
3. Keep coordinator ownership in pilot regions, but cap utilization near 80% and require a named backup with shared case notes so absence does not stall cases.
4. Launch a stratified, randomized experiment on survey skipping (M1, preceded by a retrospective validation of the rule, M6), and start measuring 60-day downstream cost (M4), structured reason codes (M3) and conformance monitoring on billing-before-activation and permit absence (M7).
5. Formal decision gate in 12 weeks.

**Trade-off.** We forgo, for one quarter, roughly two thirds of the potential median-time and handover gains and keep survey costs for apartments. In exchange we stop the growth in rework, complaints and billing disputes, and obtain evidence that can legitimately extend R to apartments if the rule can be fixed. Rolling back would discard a proven benefit for a third of customers; scaling nationally would export a known failure mode to segments that make up about two thirds of the pilot mix.

**Rollout threshold.** Extend R to a further segment only when, in a controlled comparison for that segment: first-time-right is within 1pp of baseline, p90 is no worse than baseline, 30-day complaints are within 0.5pp of baseline, full 60-day cost is at or below baseline, permit-check conformance is at least 98% for old buildings, billing never precedes a passed activation test, and coordinator utilization stays at or below 80%.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures whether the model can replay observed behavior; precision measures whether the model forbids behavior that was not observed. The pilot log contains many more distinct variants: survey skipped or not, manual vs. automatic activation, second visits, billing before or after activation, complaint escalation after billing. A discovery algorithm that must replay all of these ends up with a permissive, near "flower" structure in the middle of the process. Fitness rises to 0.94 precisely because the model allows almost anything; precision falls to 0.51 for the same reason. Two things are happening at once: a modeling effect (short window, mixed regions, high variant count relative to 9,200 cases) and a substantive effect — Redesign R removed the control points (survey gate, activation-before-billing) that made the baseline process constrained, so the real process genuinely allows more combinations. The precision drop should therefore be read as partly a measurement artifact and partly a warning that process discipline was lost.

**Improved median but worse tail.** The median is robust to what happens in the worst 20-30% of cases; p90 and p95 are determined entirely by them. Simple houses (34% of pilot) gained six days each and pull the median down. Apartments and old buildings (47% of pilot) that go through failed visit -> second visit -> manual activation -> complaint accumulate delays, and the second-visit team at 92% utilization and coordinators at 93% add nonlinear queueing delay (the temporal profile shows Failed visit -> Second visit stretching exactly in high-utilization regions). The fraction of cases on the failure path (roughly 18-25%) coincides with the p80+ region of the distribution, so p90 and p95 worsen while the median improves. Both metrics are correct; they describe different customers.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handovers are costly, but each one transfers context to another person and creates redundancy. Single coordinator ownership eliminated handovers and simplified the handover-of-work network, but the working-together network became centralized around coordinators and activation specialists: knowledge about each case now lives in one head. At 93% utilization there is no slack to absorb absence or surges, so when a coordinator is out, cases stall because nobody else knows the state. Accountability improved; redundancy and load-balancing capacity fell. The two are not contradictory — they are the two sides of concentration.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.** The visible cost metric is defined "before complaint handling," and downstream cost data stop after pilot week 4. Survey skipping removes a cost that is incurred early and inside the installation budget; the costs it creates (second visits, manual activations, complaint handling, billing disputes, credits) are incurred later, land in other cost centers, and are partly outside the observation window (right-censoring). The result is a genuine EUR 23 reduction on visible cost and an estimated EUR 9 increase all-in. The gap is the cost shifted in time and across departments plus the part not yet observed. The all-in number is itself incomplete, so the true delta is more likely worse than EUR +9, not better.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings.** The redesign's value depends on whether a field survey and permit check carry information. For a simple low-risk house they rarely do, so skipping the survey and merging tasks is pure savings: -6 days median, better FTR, fewer complaints. For apartments, in-building wiring, access and shared infrastructure are exactly what a survey reveals; for old buildings, the permit check is a hard prerequisite that the merged path dropped. The predictive rule was calibrated (implicitly) on the simple-house pattern and is "often wrong" for medium risk. This is a heterogeneous treatment effect: the same intervention has opposite signs in different strata, and because pilot regions over-represent the harmed strata, the aggregate looks worse than a national mix might — while still being genuinely harmful for those strata.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
- Advantages: captures the full median-time and handover gains immediately; simplest change-management story; survey team capacity freed nationwide.
- Risks: exports a known failure mode (wrong skip rule, missing permit checks, billing before activation) to all apartments and old buildings; complaint rate already +3.7pp and rising; billing-before-activation is a compliance-like exposure that scales with volume; coordinator utilization would likely exceed 93% nationally; all-in cost is currently negative.
- Reasonable only if: the pilot's harms were shown to be an artifact of regional mix and the national mix were overwhelmingly simple houses; neither is supported by the evidence. Not recommended.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process.**
- Advantages: restores FTR, permit checks, activation-before-billing and the survey gate immediately; stops complaint growth; low risk.
- Risks: discards a proven improvement for the 34% simple-house segment (-6 days, +3pp FTR, fewer complaints); loses learning and staff buy-in for coordinator ownership; returns 34% of cases to 3+ handovers.
- Reasonable only if: the organization cannot operate two process variants, or a regulator requires immediate cessation of billing-before-activation with no partial fix possible. Otherwise it over-reacts to aggregate metrics that hide segment benefits.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
- Advantages: removes the two components most clearly tied to rework and complaints; keeps the accountability gain; simple rule set.
- Risks: coordinator ownership alone is not proven to be safe — 93% utilization, stalling under absence, and rising second-visit delay are attached to it; scaling it nationally before fixing capacity and backup may reproduce the tail problem; forgoes the merged validate/schedule gain for simple houses unless bundled in.
- Reasonable if: management wants a single national process now and accepts a slower path to survey-skipping benefits; should still be paired with a utilization cap and backup rule.

**D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
- Advantages: directly addresses the confounding (regional mix, missing attributes, incomplete cost); produces causal evidence on the skip rule; lowest risk of a wrong national decision.
- Risks: delays benefits for simple houses that are already well-evidenced; leaves pilot regions running harmful components during the experiment unless controls are added; organizational fatigue with "another pilot."
- Reasonable if: leadership regards the simple-house evidence as insufficient, or if operational constraints prevent segment-differentiated routing. Better used as a component than as the whole answer.

**E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measurement before wider scale.**
- Advantages: locks in the well-evidenced benefit for a third of volume; stops the harmful components where they harm; keeps the coordinator model alive under safer conditions; embeds the experiment (D) and the controls (C).
- Risks: two process variants raise operational complexity and require reliable up-front segment classification — if a building is misclassified as "simple," the failure mode returns; coordinator capacity still needs management; delays apartment-segment benefits by a quarter.
- Reasonable if: address/building-risk classification can be made at order intake with acceptable accuracy (this itself is something M6 will show), and management accepts a staged rollout. Recommended.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by how likely the result is to change the decision, weighted by cost and speed.

1. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** Resolves the central uncertainty: how often the rule wrongly says "no survey needed," by stratum. Can be done retrospectively on baseline data (all medium-risk cases were surveyed) within days at near-zero cost. If the rule's false-negative rate is low for a definable sub-stratum, survey skipping can be extended there; if it is high everywhere except simple houses, skipping is confined permanently. Also tests whether up-front segment classification is reliable enough for Alternative E to work.
2. **M1 — Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.** The only design that removes the regional-mix confound and gives a causal estimate of skip-rule effect on FTR, tail and complaints per stratum. Directly determines whether apartments ever move from "controls" to "R." Higher cost and 8-12 weeks, so it follows M6 and uses M6 to define strata.
3. **M4 — Full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start.** Resolves the sign of the cost effect (EUR 198 vs. 207 is an incomplete estimate). If all-in cost turns out lower than baseline even in apartments, the case for tolerating some rework strengthens; if it is materially higher, the controls become permanent. Essential to the rollout threshold.
4. **M7 — Conformance monitoring for Billing start before Activation test and Permit check absence.** Less about learning than about control: it confirms the suspension is actually followed and quantifies residual exposure. Cheap, immediate, and a precondition for any further scaling. Could change the decision if violations persist despite suspension (indicating a system or incentive problem rather than a design choice).
5. **M3 — Structured reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, Second visit.** Separates competing explanations: skipped survey vs. coordinator overload vs. installer skill vs. post-billing activation. Without it, M1 results are harder to interpret and remedies are guesswork. Moderate cost, needed before the 12-week gate.
6. **M5 — Coordinator ownership vs. shared-team ownership under absence.** Resolves whether the resilience problem requires abandoning single ownership or only adding backup and capacity. Determines the form in which coordinator ownership scales (solo vs. pod). Important but secondary, since the utilization cap and backup rule can be applied now and observed.
7. **M2 — Start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, Manual activation.** Separates waiting time from service time, sharpens cost and capacity estimates, and improves model precision. Valuable infrastructure but unlikely to flip the scale/hold decision by itself.
8. **M8 — Separate customer experience for speed-to-appointment vs. working-service.** Confirms what complaint topics already suggest. Useful for communicating the trade-off and setting targets; lowest decision leverage because the direction is already clear.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E, implemented with the controls of Alternative C and the experiment of Alternative D.**

**Immediate action (this week).**
- Reinstate field survey for all medium-risk apartments and all old permit-sensitive buildings; make the permit check a mandatory, logged step for old buildings.
- Stop starting billing before a passed activation test in every region, for every segment. Add a conformance monitor (M7) that flags any violation daily.
- Cap coordinator utilization at approximately 80%; assign a named backup per coordinator with shared case notes so absence does not stall cases.
- Run the retrospective validation of the predictive rule (M6) on baseline survey data.

**What to pause.**
- Survey skipping for every segment except simple low-risk houses.
- Post-billing activation, everywhere.
- Any geographic expansion of R beyond the four pilot regions until the 12-week gate.

**What to scale.**
- For simple low-risk houses, in all regions where building-risk classification is available at intake: merged address validation/appointment scheduling and coordinator ownership. This segment shows -6 days median, -8 days p90, +3pp first-time-right and -1pp complaints and represents roughly one third of volume.
- Coordinator ownership in the pilot regions for other segments, but only with the utilization cap and backup rule; monitor Failed visit -> Second visit delay as the early-warning signal.

**What to measure next (in order).**
- M6 now; M7 now; M4 and M3 instrumented within four weeks; M1 launched once M6 defines the strata; M5 run inside the pilot regions; M2 and M8 as supporting instrumentation.
- Re-discover the process model at the gate and require precision to recover toward the baseline level (at least 0.70) as evidence that control points are back in force.

**Threshold that would justify broader rollout (evaluated per segment, in a controlled comparison, at the 12-week gate).**
- First-time-right within 1pp of baseline for that segment.
- p90 cycle time no worse than baseline for that segment; p95 not more than 10% above baseline.
- Complaint within 30 days within 0.5pp of baseline.
- Full 60-day cost including complaint and revisit handling at or below baseline.
- Permit-check conformance of at least 98% for old buildings; zero billing starts before a passed activation test.
- Coordinator utilization at or below 80% with no stalled-case incidents attributable to absence.
- Predictive rule false-negative rate below 5% for any stratum where skipping is proposed.

If apartments meet these thresholds under a corrected rule, extend R to them. If they do not, the permanent design is R for simple houses and the baseline sequence with coordinator ownership for everything else — which is still a material improvement over both the baseline and the current pilot.
