The strongest evidence for this structure is that the activity matrix, complexity table, pairwise similarity caveats, substitution episodes, and sequence fragments all point to **role overlays**: routine execution, formal authority, complexity exposure, and temporary delegation do not align perfectly.  

# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

**Automated intake and entitlement precheck — R14_Nora.** Core work is limited to A1 and A2. Nora should not be interpreted as a normal front-office resource even though the activity counts are very high. Its “role” is a boundary role: it creates or pre-validates cases and then hands them to humans. The low median complexity and absence of downstream activities suggest no approval, negotiation, quality, or exception authority.

**Certified routine front office — R01_Amira and R02_Ben.** Their core is A1/A2/A4/A12: intake, entitlement validation, standard response, and closure. Peripheral work includes limited diagnosis, negotiation, and pricing touches. Amira’s higher complexity profile suggests she is not merely another Ben-like high-volume agent; she may absorb harder front-office cases. Still, neither Amira nor Ben should be inferred to have supervisor or specialist authority from occasional peripheral events.

**Temporary front-office surge capacity — R11_Kai and R12_Lina.** Their core activities resemble routine front office, but their authority and risk profile are narrower. They handle low-complexity registration, validation, standard response, and closure. The reopen evidence matters: 17 reopened cases were traced to incomplete Validate Entitlement notes after temp closures. That does not mean temps are bad performers; it means their role should be interpreted as constrained surge capacity requiring validation guardrails.

**Technical diagnosis/configuration/implementation — R03_Chen and R04_Dina.** The core is A3/A5/A10. A6 and A14 are peripheral but meaningful: security-adjacent work by Chen, and KB updates by both, suggest technical expertise spills into adjacent control and learning activities. Chen and Dina are activity-similar, but not complexity-equivalent. Chen’s higher median complexity and critical load imply a senior or specialist layer inside the technical role.

**Security/risk specialist — R05_Erik.** Erik’s core is A6 Security Review. His low total activity count is misleading because his median complexity is the highest. Peripheral technical and quality activities likely arise when security review requires diagnosis, implementation constraints, or documentation. Mateo’s filtering of low-risk security referrals reduced false escalation complaints but also contributed to two critical cases waiting more than two days, so Erik’s role should be capacity-protected rather than optimized away.

**Commercial pricing and negotiation — R06_Fatima.** Fatima’s core is A7/A8. Some A9 activity appears, but the evidence does not justify treating her as a supervisor; it is safer to interpret those approvals as commercial signoff or participation in approval flows unless the underlying log proves final authority. Her temporary absence is informative: Giorgio could substitute, but pricing duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes, so substitution demonstrates coverage, not equal permanent capacity.

**Hybrid senior front-office/commercial shadow — R07_Giorgio.** Giorgio’s core is mixed. He is a senior front-office resource with meaningful commercial work, not a clean member of either cluster. His A7/A8 counts and pricing certification support a shadow commercial role, while his A1/A2/A4/A12 counts keep him anchored in front office. The key distinction is authority: he may be an approved backup for some pricing exceptions, but the data alone does not prove he has the same decision rights as Fatima.

**Supervisor/final approval — R08_Hana.** Hana’s core is A9. Peripheral negotiation, reopen, and quality work fit an escalation manager who resolves difficult cases around the approval point. The week-5 substitution episode is decisive: Mateo performed 21 preliminary approvals, but 13 were later rechecked by Hana. That suggests Hana owns formal authority, while Mateo can prepare, coordinate, or temporarily cover only under review.

**Quality gatekeeping and knowledge management — R09_Ivan and R10_Julia.** Their core is A11/A14. Peripheral A10/A12/A3/A5 events are compatible with quality follow-up, not necessarily technical ownership. Their similarity is high and their complexity profiles are close, so this is one of the cleaner roles. Still, quality authority should be treated separately from technical implementation authority.

**Cross-functional escalation broker — R13_Mateo.** Mateo’s core role is not a single activity but a network position. He touches reopen, approval, negotiation, validation, diagnosis, security, implementation, close, and KB activities. His high median complexity and large critical-case count make him a high-risk coordinator, not a general-purpose backup. His peripheral-looking activity spread is exactly what makes the role central: he brokers handovers between technical, commercial, security, quality, and management. 

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**R07_Giorgio as commercial shadow role.** Giorgio is the clearest shadow role. He has front-office volume, pricing certification, and substantial A7/A8 work. During Fatima’s absence he performed 31 Pricing Exception Review events and 18 Customer Negotiation events. The benefit is resilience: the commercial process does not stop when Fatima is absent. The risk is slower or less consistent exception handling, as median pricing exception duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes during that episode. The operational answer is not to forbid the shadow role, but to formalize its limits: for example, define which pricing exceptions Giorgio may handle independently, which require Fatima/Hana review, and which require post-hoc audit.

**R13_Mateo as cross-functional escalation broker.** Mateo bridges front office, technical, security, commercial, quality, and management. He is involved in reopened cases, preliminary approvals, security filtering, and cross-functional routing. This is valuable because many escalations are not “owned” by one specialist; they need coordination. The risk is authority ambiguity. If Mateo filters security referrals, he can reduce false escalation complaints, but the evidence also shows two critical cases waited more than two days during high Enterprise Security load. If he performs preliminary approvals, many may still need Hana’s recheck. Mateo’s role should therefore be designed as broker/triage authority, not silently upgraded into final approval or risk authority.

**R05_Erik as rare but critical specialist.** Erik is a bridging role in a different sense: he bridges normal service resolution with security risk control. His A6 count is not huge compared with routine front-office work, but his case complexity is the highest. This role is easy to under-resource if management optimizes by event volume. The benefit of concentrating A6 with Erik is risk expertise and consistency. The risk is bottleneck formation, especially when Mateo’s triage reduces routine referrals but critical work still queues.

**Automation boundary role — R14_Nora.** Nora bridges system execution and human service work. The bot handles high-volume registration and validation but does not close, negotiate, configure, approve, or review quality. The benefit is throughput and standardization. The risk is that downstream humans may trust the automated entitlement precheck too much, or that automated validation hides case-mix shifts until reopen/rework appears.

**Temporary workforce bridge — R11_Kai and R12_Lina.** The temps bridge seasonal demand and routine closure. Their role is legitimate, but it should be bounded. The reopen evidence suggests their work needs stronger note-quality checks or narrower eligibility rules for closure.

**Quality gatekeeping bridge — R09_Ivan and R10_Julia.** Ivan and Julia bridge implementation and organizational learning. They do not merely inspect; they also update the knowledge base. This can turn rework and quality failures into process improvement, provided their findings are fed back to front office, temps, and technical staff.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

CASE_ID = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1) Load and format event log
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# The code assumes the full event log, not the summarized table.
# Required columns:
# case:concept:name, concept:name, time:timestamp, org:resource,
# case_complexity, customer_segment, product_line,
# temporary_substitution, escalation_level

raw = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution_event_log.csv")
raw[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(raw[TIMESTAMP], errors="coerce", utc=True)

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw,
    case_id=CASE_ID,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP
)

# Optional resource metadata from the resource table in the benchmark.
resource_master = pd.DataFrame(
    [
        ("R01_Amira", "Service Agent", "Front Office", "permanent", "entitlement"),
        ("R02_Ben", "Service Agent", "Front Office", "permanent", "entitlement"),
        ("R03_Chen", "Technical Analyst", "Technical", "permanent", "configuration"),
        ("R04_Dina", "Technical Analyst", "Technical", "permanent", "configuration"),
        ("R05_Erik", "Security Specialist", "Risk", "permanent", "security"),
        ("R06_Fatima", "Commercial Specialist", "Commercial", "permanent", "pricing"),
        ("R07_Giorgio", "Senior Service Agent", "Front Office", "permanent", "entitlement,pricing"),
        ("R08_Hana", "Supervisor", "Management", "permanent", "approval"),
        ("R09_Ivan", "Quality Analyst", "Quality", "permanent", "quality"),
        ("R10_Julia", "Quality Analyst", "Quality", "permanent", "quality"),
        ("R11_Kai", "Temp Agent", "Front Office", "temporary", "none"),
        ("R12_Lina", "Temp Agent", "Front Office", "temporary", "none"),
        ("R13_Mateo", "Incident Coordinator", "Coordination", "permanent", "escalation"),
        ("R14_Nora", "Automation Bot", "Automation", "system", "n/a"),
    ],
    columns=[RESOURCE, "formal_title", "home_team", "contract", "certifications"]
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2) Discover organizational roles and activity-based similarity
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
roles_raw = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    df,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP
)

similarity_raw = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    df,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP
)

# PM4Py versions may return role structures differently.
# Normalize conservatively so that the mined output can be joined to resource stats.
def normalize_roles(roles):
    rows = []
    for i, role in enumerate(roles):
        if isinstance(role, dict):
            resources = role.get("resources", role.get("resource", []))
            activities = role.get("activities", role.get("activity", []))
        elif isinstance(role, (list, tuple)) and len(role) >= 2:
            # Common pattern: role = (activities, resources) or similar.
            activities, resources = role[0], role[1]
        else:
            resources = getattr(role, "resources", [])
            activities = getattr(role, "activities", [])

        if isinstance(resources, str):
            resources = [resources]
        if isinstance(activities, str):
            activities = [activities]

        for r in resources:
            rows.append({
                "mined_role_id": i,
                RESOURCE: r,
                "mined_role_activities": tuple(sorted(set(activities)))
            })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

role_map = normalize_roles(roles_raw)

# Activity count profile: useful, but not sufficient for authority.
activity_counts = (
    pd.crosstab(df[RESOURCE], df[ACTIVITY])
    .reset_index()
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3) Complexity and substitution-aware resource profile
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Avoid counting the same case-resource pair many times just because a resource
# touched the case repeatedly.
case_resource = (
    df[[CASE_ID, RESOURCE, "case_complexity", "temporary_substitution", "escalation_level"]]
    .drop_duplicates([CASE_ID, RESOURCE])
    .copy()
)

# If complexity is categorical, map it to an ordered score for aggregation.
if case_resource["case_complexity"].dtype == "object":
    complexity_order = {
        "low": 0.25,
        "medium": 0.50,
        "high": 0.75,
        "critical": 1.00
    }
    case_resource["case_complexity_score"] = (
        case_resource["case_complexity"]
        .str.lower()
        .map(complexity_order)
    )
else:
    case_resource["case_complexity_score"] = pd.to_numeric(
        case_resource["case_complexity"],
        errors="coerce"
    )

complexity_stats = (
    case_resource
    .groupby(RESOURCE)
    .agg(
        cases_handled=(CASE_ID, "nunique"),
        median_complexity=("case_complexity_score", "median"),
        p90_complexity=("case_complexity_score", lambda s: s.quantile(0.90)),
        critical_or_high_share=(
            "case_complexity_score",
            lambda s: np.mean(s >= 0.75)
        ),
        temporary_case_share=(
            "temporary_substitution",
            lambda s: np.mean(s.astype(bool))
        )
    )
    .reset_index()
)

event_substitution_stats = (
    df.groupby([RESOURCE, "temporary_substitution", ACTIVITY])
    .size()
    .rename("events")
    .reset_index()
    .sort_values([RESOURCE, "temporary_substitution", "events"], ascending=[True, True, False])
)

resource_profile = (
    resource_master
    .merge(activity_counts, on=RESOURCE, how="left")
    .merge(complexity_stats, on=RESOURCE, how="left")
    .merge(role_map, on=RESOURCE, how="left")
)

# This table is where the analyst should compare:
# - mined_role_id and mined_role_activities
# - formal_title/home_team/certifications
# - median and tail complexity
# - temporary substitution share
# - activity counts
#
# A high activity similarity should not automatically become a formal role label.
print(resource_profile.sort_values(["home_team", "median_complexity"]))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4) Handover-of-work and working-together networks
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    df,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE_ID
)

working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    df,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    case_id_key=CASE_ID
)

subcontracting = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    df,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE_ID
)

def network_to_edges(network_obj, source_col="source", target_col="target"):
    """
    Many PM4Py network outputs are dictionaries keyed by (source, target).
    Adjust this adapter if your PM4Py version returns a DataFrame or NetworkX object.
    """
    if isinstance(network_obj, pd.DataFrame):
        return network_obj.copy()

    rows = []
    if isinstance(network_obj, dict):
        for key, value in network_obj.items():
            if isinstance(key, tuple) and len(key) == 2:
                src, tgt = key
                rows.append({source_col: src, target_col: tgt, "weight": value})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

handover_edges = network_to_edges(handover, "from_resource", "to_resource")
working_edges = network_to_edges(working_together, "resource_1", "resource_2")
subcontracting_edges = network_to_edges(subcontracting, "from_resource", "to_resource")

# Examples of inspections that matter for this benchmark:
critical_resources = [
    "R05_Erik", "R06_Fatima", "R07_Giorgio",
    "R08_Hana", "R13_Mateo", "R14_Nora"
]

print(
    handover_edges[
        handover_edges["from_resource"].isin(critical_resources)
        | handover_edges["to_resource"].isin(critical_resources)
    ].sort_values("weight", ascending=False)
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5) Detect shadow/substitution patterns without treating them as formal roles
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Example: Giorgio pricing work during temporary substitution.
giorgio_pricing = df[
    (df[RESOURCE] == "R07_Giorgio")
    & (df[ACTIVITY].isin(["Pricing Exception Review", "Customer Negotiation"]))
]

giorgio_pricing_by_substitution = (
    giorgio_pricing
    .groupby(["temporary_substitution", ACTIVITY])
    .size()
    .rename("events")
    .reset_index()
)

# Example: Mateo preliminary approvals followed by Hana approval in the same case.
approval_events = (
    df[df[ACTIVITY] == "Supervisor Approval"]
    .sort_values([CASE_ID, TIMESTAMP])
    [[CASE_ID, TIMESTAMP, RESOURCE, "temporary_substitution", "escalation_level"]]
)

approval_paths = (
    approval_events
    .groupby(CASE_ID)[RESOURCE]
    .agg(lambda x: tuple(x))
    .reset_index(name="approval_resource_sequence")
)

mateo_then_hana_cases = approval_paths[
    approval_paths["approval_resource_sequence"].apply(
        lambda seq: "R13_Mateo" in seq and "R08_Hana" in seq
    )
]

# Example: temp closures followed by reopen.
ordered = df.sort_values([CASE_ID, TIMESTAMP]).copy()
ordered["next_activity"] = ordered.groupby(CASE_ID)[ACTIVITY].shift(-1)
ordered["next_resource"] = ordered.groupby(CASE_ID)[RESOURCE].shift(-1)

temp_close_then_reopen = ordered[
    (ordered[RESOURCE].isin(["R11_Kai", "R12_Lina"]))
    & (ordered[ACTIVITY] == "Close Request")
    & (ordered["next_activity"] == "Reopen Request")
]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6) Analyst decision rule
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Use mined roles as candidates, then annotate each candidate:
# - Is it formal authority or merely performed work?
# - Is it permanent or temporary substitution?
# - Is it high-volume routine or low-volume critical control?
# - Does similarity remain after stratifying by complexity/product_line/escalation_level?
# - Do handovers show brokerage that activity counts hide?
#
# This prevents conclusions like:
# "Giorgio = Fatima because both do pricing"
# "Mateo = Hana because both touch approvals"
# "Temps = front office because they close simple cases"
# "Erik is low importance because A6 has fewer events than A2/A4"
```

# 6. Operational interpretation

Management should use the discovered structure as a **staffing and control map**, not as a mechanical clustering output.

For **staffing**, the routine front-office role and the temporary surge role should be planned separately. R11/R12 can absorb simple cases, but their closure authority should be bounded by case complexity and entitlement-note quality. R01/R02 should remain available for medium-complexity validation, rework, and cases that automation or temps cannot safely finish.

For **technical capacity**, Chen and Dina should not be treated as fully interchangeable despite high activity similarity. Dina can cover normal diagnosis/configuration/implementation, while Chen appears more exposed to high/critical and security-adjacent cases. A resilience plan should include training Dina toward higher-complexity work, but not by abruptly shifting critical cases without review.

For **commercial resilience**, Fatima is the formal pricing specialist and Giorgio is a valuable shadow/backup. The data supports formalizing Giorgio’s backup role rather than pretending it does not exist. However, the increase in pricing duration during substitution means management should define thresholds: small exceptions can be delegated, larger or unusual exceptions should be reviewed by Fatima or Hana.

For **risk control**, Erik’s role must be protected even if his event count is small. Security review is low-volume but high-consequence. Mateo’s security filtering can improve flow by reducing false escalations, but it needs service-level rules for critical cases so that triage does not accidentally delay the cases that most need Erik.

For **approval governance**, Hana should remain the final approval authority unless the organization explicitly delegates authority to Mateo. Mateo’s preliminary approvals are useful for continuity, but the recheck evidence suggests they are not equivalent to Hana’s formal approval.

For **quality improvement**, Ivan and Julia should be used as feedback channels. Reopens after temp-handled validations should feed into knowledge-base updates, temp training, and validation checklists. Quality should not be treated only as an end-of-process inspection; it is also a learning mechanism.

Two cautions are especially important. First, **activity similarity is not authority similarity**: Giorgio/Fatima, Hana/Mateo, and Chen/Dina all show why similar or overlapping activity profiles can hide different certification, complexity, and decision rights. Second, **temporary substitution is not proof of permanent competence**: Giorgio and Mateo can cover gaps, but the duration increase and rechecks show that substitution has cost and risk. A third caution follows from Erik and Nora: **event volume is not importance**. High-volume automation and low-volume security review both matter, but for completely different operational reasons.
