# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring is not one change but five coupled changes: (a) pooling of technical capacity, (b) scope expansion of that pool (legal/risk triage plus difficult Quality Check), (c) a new routing rule that mechanically increases referral volume, (d) removal of one supervisor combined with a new shared approval queue, and (e) a utilization target of +15%. Most of the forecasting difficulty comes from the fact that (a) is beneficial in isolation while (b), (c), (d) and (e) all consume exactly the slack that makes (a) work.

Before the mechanism-by-mechanism map, two arithmetic checks on management's own tables, because they constrain every scenario below.

**Check 1 — the proposal increases nominal handovers, it does not reduce them.** Summing management's own expected frequencies per 100 cases: current = 92+88+36+31+42+39+9+6 = **343**; post = 92+83+41+36+49+43+12+3 = **359**. That is **+16 handovers per 100 cases (+4.7%)**. The specialist loop grows from 67 to 77 per 100 and the approval loop from 81 to 92 per 100. The only genuine reduction is cross-team handover (6 → 3) and intake-to-caseworker (88 → 83), and those savings are smaller than the additions. So the stated benefit "fewer local-specialist handover loops" is contradicted by the estimate table: the loops are not removed, they are *relocated* from inside a team to across an organizational boundary, and slightly multiplied. What falls is the *number of distinct handover types that stay inside one team*, which is a different thing and is not the thing that drives cycle time.

**Check 2 — weighted handover wait rises even on management's own optimistic per-edge estimates.** Multiplying frequency by median wait and summing: current = 588 hours per 100 cases (≈5.9 h/case); post = 722 hours per 100 cases (≈7.2 h/case). That is **+23%** in handover-induced wait, produced by management's *favourable* assumptions. Note also that ~6 h/case of handover wait cannot explain median cycle times of 6.8–8.6 days, which means the great majority of elapsed time lives in queueing, batching and calendar effects that management's table does not model at all. The table is therefore not a forecast of cycle time; it is a forecast of the small, best-understood part of cycle time, and it points the wrong way.

With that established:

**Nominal handover reduction → handover network topology, not handover count.** What actually changes is the *shape* of the handover-of-work network. Today it is modular: three dense local clusters (intake → caseworker → tech → same caseworker → supervisor) with thin, poor-quality bridges between them (cross-team, 6 per 100, 18% rework). After pooling it becomes hub-and-spoke: a small number of shared-service resources with very high in-degree and out-degree. Observable measures: **edge count falls but degree centralization rises**; network modularity (community structure aligned to `team`) falls; **betweenness centrality concentrates** on shared-service resources; the number of *distinct* resource pairs shrinks while the flow per pair multiplies. This matters because a hub-and-spoke handover network has a single point of congestion and a single point of failure, whereas a modular one degrades locally. In PM4Py terms: compare `discover_handover_of_work_network` edge sets pre/post and track the Gini or Freeman centralization of node throughput, not just the number of edges.

**Central queue formation → waiting time separates from service time.** Pooling converts three short local queues into one longer central queue. An illustrative Erlang-C calculation makes the sensitivity concrete. Current tech load: 0.71+0.68+0.82 = **2.21 server-equivalents on 3 servers**. As three independent M/M/1 silos, demand-weighted mean queue wait ≈ **3.1 service times** (North 2.45, South 2.13, East 4.56). Pooled as M/M/3 at the same 73.7% utilization, mean wait ≈ **0.7 service times** — a genuine, large pooling gain, and the strongest argument for the proposal. But the routing rule takes referrals from 36 to 41 per 100 (+14%), giving load 2.52 (84% utilization) and wait ≈ **1.5 service times**; at 90% utilization wait ≈ **2.7**; at 95% ≈ **6.0**. The crossover where pooling stops beating the status quo *on average* is around **90% utilization**, and where it stops beating the status quo *for North and South* is around **87–88%**. Management's explicit target of +15% utilization lands at ~85%. The entire benefit of the restructuring therefore sits inside a 2–5 percentage-point band of headroom, before any of the scope additions in item 2 of the proposal are counted. Each additional 0.1 server-equivalents of absorbed work (legal/risk triage, difficult Quality Check) moves utilization by ~3.3 points. This is the central mechanism: **pooling buys a queueing gain, and the utilization target spends it.**

**Legal/Risk queue → likely unstable, not merely slower.** Legal and Risk are already at 93% and 91%, i.e. ~1.84 server-equivalents on 2 servers, and both are described as part-time. The routing rule raises detection from 9 to 12 referrals per 100 cases (+33%), requiring ~2.45 server-equivalents. That is **ρ > 1**: not a longer wait but an unbounded backlog. Management's estimate of a 9.6 h → 10.5 h median wait presumes a new steady state that cannot exist without added capacity, which the proposal does not mention. The falsifiable prediction: legal/risk waiting time will show a **positive time trend by arrival cohort (a ramp), not a level shift**, and work-in-progress in that queue will grow roughly linearly. This is the single most testable claim in the whole forecast and it should be checked within two to three weeks.

**Shared approval queue → second likely bottleneck.** Approvals go from 42 to 49 per 100 (+17%) while supervisor count goes from 3 to 2 (−33%). Per-supervisor approval load rises by 1.167 × 1.5 = **+75%**. The change is only feasible if approval work currently consumes less than ~57% of a supervisor's capacity; supervisors also do coaching, exception handling and escalation, which the proposal does not reassign. Management's 2.4 h → 3.0 h estimate is optimistic under any plausible baseline. Note the compounding effect: 49 per 100 cases pass through this queue, versus 41 through the technical queue, so the approval queue touches *more* of the case population than the technical one and its congestion is broadcast to every case, including simple ones that currently never wait.

**Specialist utilization → an ambiguous measure that can rise for bad reasons.** Utilization is busy-time ÷ available-time. It rises when throughput rises (good) and equally when service time inflates (bad). Removing local specialists removes the informal, undocumented advice that currently prevents unnecessary referrals and, crucially, that supplies *case context*. A central specialist working a case they have no local knowledge of must re-acquire context from notes that the log already tells us are "inconsistent across locations." Expect **service time per specialist activity to rise for cases where `local_expertise_flag = 0`**. If it rises by 15%, the pooled load goes to ~2.9 server-equivalents (~97% utilization) and waits explode. So the correct diagnostic is not utilization but **utilization decomposed into throughput and mean service time, stratified by `local_expertise_flag` and `case_complexity`**. A +15% utilization figure accompanied by flat throughput is a failure dressed as a success.

**Rework → two opposing forces with different time constants.** Standardization should reduce rework from inconsistent decisions, and specialists working higher volumes of similar work should get better at them; this force acts slowly and shows up over months. Working against it: local teams currently "know regional customer conventions; this reduces rework in complex cases," and cross-team work already shows 18% rework versus 6–9% for local work. Central specialists are, structurally, cross-team workers. The pessimistic reading is that the 18% figure is the natural rework rate of context-poor work and that pooling converts a large share of the 36-per-100 local specialist loop (9% rework) into context-poor work. This force acts immediately. So expect **rework to worsen first and improve later, if it improves**. Any evaluation window shorter than one learning cycle will see only the bad half; any window that stops at the first improvement will miss the possibility that it never fully recovers. Stratify `rework_flag` by `local_expertise_flag`, `case_complexity` and `location` — the aggregate rate will be a blend of a falling and a rising component and will look deceptively stable.

**Local expertise loss → a slow, self-reinforcing feedback loop.** Routing all high-complexity cases away from local staff means local caseworkers stop seeing hard cases, which reduces their ability to handle hard cases, which increases the referral rate, which loads the central queue further, which increases pressure to route more away. This is a positive feedback loop with a delay of quarters, and it is invisible in any short pilot. Observable proxies available from the log: the **activity repertoire per local resource** (count and entropy of distinct `concept:name` values performed) should shrink; the share of high-complexity cases with any local technical activity should fall; the fraction of cases with `local_expertise_flag = 1` should fall; and in `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity`, local caseworkers should become *more similar to each other* (de-differentiation) while the local–central similarity gap widens into a bimodal distribution. In `discover_organizational_roles`, expect a role cluster to **disappear** — the embedded-technical role — and the remaining local role to narrow.

**Role clarity → measurable as ownership dispersion, not opinion.** Today a case has an identifiable owner: a local caseworker who hands out and takes back work (the "local loop" edge, 31 per 100). Shared approval for medium/high-value cases plus a pull-based specialist queue removes the named decision-maker. Log-observable consequences: **distinct resources per case rises**; **`reassignment_count` rises** (this column exists precisely to measure this); the concentration of a case's activities on its most active resource (a Herfindahl index over resources within a case) falls; and `handover_reason` should show a growing share of routing/queue reasons versus specialty reasons. Ambiguous ownership also produces a characteristic pathology: cases that sit in no queue at all because everyone believes someone else has them, which appears as a **long right tail with no corresponding resource activity** — measurable as idle gaps not attributable to any working resource.

**Resilience to absence → improves in the common case, worsens in the tail.** Pooling genuinely fixes today's worst failure mode: cross-team cover with 8.9 h wait and 18% rework, which fails because notes are inconsistent. Any of several pooled specialists can pick up work, so single absences no longer stall a team. But two things get worse. First, at 85–90% utilization a queue has no slack to absorb an absence, so the *magnitude* of the disruption per absence grows non-linearly. Second, failures become **correlated instead of localized**: a central outage or absence now delays North, South and East simultaneously, whereas today an E_Tech absence affects only East. The right measure is not "does the work get done" but **blast radius**: the fraction of in-flight cases whose waiting time exceeds baseline p90 on days with a known central absence, compared with local-absence days in the baseline. Note the important corollary: the root cause of poor cross-team cover is *inconsistent case notes*, and standardizing notes would deliver much of the resilience benefit without any structural change. That is a cheaper intervention and also a precondition for pooling to work at all.

**Fairness → at least four distinct dimensions, moving in different directions.** (i) *Across teams*: pooling transfers capacity from the slack teams to the loaded one. East (E_Tech at 82%, median 8.6 d, p90 22.8 d, rework 11.5%, satisfaction 3.8) should improve materially; North and South (71%/68%, 6.8/7.4 d) should get somewhat worse. This is a deliberate redistribution and should be argued as an equity choice, not smuggled in as an efficiency claim — the total may improve while two of three teams and roughly 75% of case volume experience degradation. (ii) *Across complexity*: routing rules send high-complexity cases into the most congested queue while simple cases stay local and get faster. Expect **divergence**: the simple-case distribution improves, the complex-case distribution worsens, and the average can improve while the complex stratum is materially harmed. (iii) *Across customer segments*: the scenario explicitly flags customers "needing language or regional accommodation." Those needs are held as local knowledge, so those segments face more handovers, more context loss and more rework — a fairness effect that correlates with exactly the groups least able to advocate for themselves. Measure waiting time, SLA miss and rework by `customer_segment` crossed with `location`. (iv) *Queue discipline*: a shared queue with priority ordering starves low-`priority` work in a way three local queues did not. Watch the p90/p99 of low-priority waiting time and the count of aged work-in-progress, not the mean.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Assumptions common to all three: the change is implemented as written unless stated; volumes stay near 2,100 cases/month; no capacity is added to Legal/Risk; the observation horizon is 6–12 months. Because p90 cycle time is 16–23 days, any statement about outcomes requires at least 8–12 weeks of completed cases per arm.

 ## Optimistic scenario

This requires conditions that the proposal as written does not guarantee: Legal/Risk capacity added before detection is broadened, the supervisor position not removed until approval-queue headroom is demonstrated, note standardization completed first, and a reserved local-advice channel retained.

- **Handover pattern.** Total handovers stay near 343–359 per 100 cases, but cross-team handover collapses from 6 to 2–3 and its rework rate falls from 18% toward 9% because standardized notes make context transferable. The handover network becomes hub-and-spoke with high but *stable* central throughput; the local loop (specialist → same caseworker) survives at reduced volume rather than disappearing, so ownership is preserved.
- **Queue/waiting pattern.** Technical shared service settles at 78–84% utilization. Median wait to the pool rises to 3.5–4.5 h (close to management's 4.0 h) but the *variance* of wait falls sharply because pooling smooths per-team demand spikes. East's specialist wait falls from ~3.2 h and its tail improves most. Waiting time as a share of total cycle time stays flat or falls.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** Median improves modestly, 7.4 d → 6.6–7.0 d organization-wide, driven mostly by East (8.6 d → 7.2–7.6 d). p90 improves for East (22.8 d → ~18 d) and is flat to slightly worse for North/South (16.4 d → 16–17 d). Critically, **the high-complexity stratum does not get worse**: its p90 is flat or improves, which is the signature that distinguishes this scenario from the central one.
- **Rework and quality.** A short transition bump (2–4 weeks, +1 to +2 points) then decline: organization-wide rework 9.0% → 7.5–8.0% within two quarters, with East improving most (11.5% → ~9%). Rework on cases with `local_expertise_flag = 0` converges toward the rate for flagged cases as notes improve. `get_rework_cases_per_activity` shows falling rework specifically at Specialist Review and Quality Check, i.e. standardization working where it was supposed to.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** `reassignment_count` stays flat because a named local case owner is retained through the shared queues. Distinct resources per case rises by less than one. Shared approval reduces decision variance (measurable as reduced variance in approval-to-implementation paths across `location`) without dispersing ownership.
- **Fairness.** East gains substantially; North/South are roughly neutral; the gap in median cycle time and satisfaction between the best and worst team narrows from 1.8 d to under 1.0 d. Segment-level disparity (ratio of p90 wait between best- and worst-served `customer_segment`) does not widen. Language/regional-accommodation segments show no increase in handovers because local intake retains those touchpoints.
- **Early warning signs that this scenario is holding.** Central-queue work-in-progress is stationary (no positive slope over 4 consecutive weeks); specialist service time for `local_expertise_flag = 0` cases is within 5% of flagged cases; waiting share of cycle time flat; p90 for high complexity flat; low-priority p90 wait flat.

 ## Central scenario

The most likely outcome if the proposal is implemented as written.

- **Handover pattern.** Handovers rise as management's own table implies (~359 per 100), with the specialist and approval loops both growing. Cross-team handover falls to 3 but its rework rate stays near 15–18% because notes were never standardized; the improvement is in volume, not quality. The handover network centralizes strongly: a handful of shared-service resources account for a large share of all edge weight, and `discover_subcontracting_network` shows subcontracting concentrated on two or three nodes rather than distributed across nine local pairs.
- **Queue/waiting pattern.** Technical shared service runs at 86–92% once legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Check are absorbed. Median wait to the pool 5–7 h, materially worse than the 4.0 h estimate, with p90 wait 2–3 days. **Legal/Risk becomes the binding constraint**: with demand up 33% against 91–93% utilization the queue does not reach a steady state — waiting time grows through the observation window, from ~9.6 h to 20–40 h and still rising, with visible backlog accumulation. The shared approval queue runs hot from per-supervisor load up 75%: median approval wait 4–6 h rather than 3.0 h, and because 49 of 100 cases pass through it, this delay is broadcast to nearly half of all work including simple cases that previously never waited.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** This is the scenario that produces the deceptive headline. Median cycle time is **roughly flat to slightly improved** (7.4 d → 7.0–7.5 d) because East improves and because a larger share of simple cases now completes entirely locally with less specialist involvement — a mix effect, not a process improvement. Meanwhile p90 worsens organization-wide (18.2 d → 20–24 d) and the high-complexity stratum degrades clearly: its median +15–30% and its p90 +30–60%. Within-stratum, **every complexity band may be worse while the overall average improves** — a textbook Simpson's-paradox trap, and precisely the trap the scenario warns about.
- **Rework and quality.** Organization-wide rework roughly flat, 9.0% → 8.5–10%, hiding divergence: rework falls where standardization bites (routine Specialist Review) and rises where context is lost (complex cases, non-standard customer segments, cases with `local_expertise_flag = 0`, which trend toward the cross-team rate of 14–18%). The loss of informal local advice raises the *unnecessary* referral rate, so part of the 36 → 41 increase is not "more cases correctly routed" but defensive referral by staff who no longer have someone to ask. Measurable as a rising share of specialist referrals that produce no change in case handling.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** `reassignment_count` rises 20–50%. Distinct resources per case rises by 1–2. Ownership concentration falls. A new failure mode appears: cases idle in ambiguity between the shared queues, visible as multi-day gaps with no responsible resource. Rework attribution becomes contested, which slows the very learning loop that was supposed to reduce rework.
- **Fairness.** East improves (median 8.6 d → 7.5–8.0 d, satisfaction up), North/South degrade (median +0.3–0.8 d, p90 +2–4 d, satisfaction down 0.1–0.3), so the *dispersion* across teams narrows while the majority of volume is worse off. Complex, low-priority, and accommodation-needing cases carry the cost: their p90 waits grow fastest. The disparity ratio between best- and worst-served `customer_segment` widens.
- **Early warning signs.** Legal/Risk work-in-progress rising for 3+ consecutive weeks; specialist service time on unflagged-expertise cases 10%+ above flagged; waiting share of cycle time rising from ~30% toward 45–55% while total is flat; `reassignment_count` p90 rising; referral rate rising faster than complexity mix justifies; local resources' activity repertoire shrinking; growing count of cases aged beyond baseline p90 with no recent event.

 ## Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern.** Handovers rise above 380 per 100 as defensive referrals, re-queueing and clarification loops multiply. New pathological variants appear: caseworker → technical queue → back with insufficient advice → technical queue again; caseworker → approval queue → returned for information → approval queue again. `get_variants` shows **variant proliferation** — the long tail of one-off variants grows, which is a direct signature of process breakdown, and the top-5 variant coverage falls sharply.
- **Queue/waiting pattern.** Technical shared service above 95%; queues in all three shared services grow monotonically. Legal/Risk wait reaches days. Approval queue becomes a universal tollbooth. Waiting time dominates: waiting share of cycle time rises past 60%.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** Median degrades to 8.5–10 d, p90 to 28–35 d, and a genuinely new failure population appears — cases exceeding 45–60 days that had no analogue in the baseline. SLA miss rate roughly doubles (7.2% → 13–16%). East's early gain reverses once the pool saturates.
- **Rework and quality.** Rework rises to 12–15% organization-wide, converging on the cross-team benchmark because nearly all specialist work is now context-poor. Quality Check failures rise on complex cases. A secondary effect: under queue pressure, staff work around the routing rules (closing cases without needed specialist input, downgrading recorded complexity to avoid the queue), which shows in the log as falling recorded high-complexity share alongside rising downstream rework — i.e. the metrics improve because the data degrades.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** Ownership effectively absent for shared-queue cases. Reassignment counts rise sharply. Complaints and escalations route to whoever is reachable rather than whoever is responsible. Staff satisfaction and retention fall, which removes capacity, which worsens the queues — a second reinforcing loop.
- **Fairness.** Everyone worse, complex and accommodation-needing cases much worse. Local satisfaction falls across all teams (4.3/4.1/3.8 → below 3.8). East's structural disadvantage is replaced by a structural disadvantage shared by all, which is a worse outcome than the status quo even on a strict equity view.
- **Early warning signs.** Any queue's work-in-progress growing with a stable positive slope; the appearance of case-level idle gaps exceeding several days; variant count growing while top-variant coverage falls; recorded complexity mix shifting without a plausible demand explanation; rising rate of cases closed without the specialist step that routing rules require.

Reading a lagging indicator early produces survivorship bias, because only fast cases have finished.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

The average is a poor instrument here for three structural reasons before any specific risk: it is a mean over a heavily right-skewed distribution (p90/p50 ratios of 2.4–2.7), it is a mean over a *changing mix* of cases, and computed early it is a mean over *completed* cases only, which excludes precisely the cases the restructuring hurts.

**1. Tail latency.** With p90 at 16–23 days versus medians of 7–9, the tail is where the harm and the cost live. Pooling compresses the middle of the distribution (variability smoothing) while a high-utilization single queue fattens the tail — queue-wait tails grow far faster than means as utilization rises. A flat or improved mean is fully compatible with p90 up 30% and p99 up 60%, plus an entirely new population of 45–60 day cases with no baseline analogue. Those cases drive SLA penalties, escalations, complaints and rework. **Fix:** report p50, p90, p99 and the count of cases beyond a fixed absolute threshold, by arrival cohort.

**2. High-complexity cases and the mix-shift / Simpson's-paradox trap.** The routing rules send high-complexity cases into the most congested queue and keep simple cases local, where they get faster. If simple cases speed up and grow as a share of completions, the overall average improves **while every complexity stratum individually worsens**. This is not a hypothetical: it is the arithmetically expected consequence of the design. It is compounded by censoring — complex cases take longer, so in the first weeks post-change the completed-case pool is disproportionately simple, biasing the average downward by an amount that has nothing to do with performance. **Fix:** stratify by `case_complexity` and never compare periods on completed cases without matching arrival cohorts; use survival analysis for in-flight work.

**3. Local knowledge loss.** "Local teams know regional customer conventions; this reduces rework in complex cases." That knowledge is tacit, undocumented, and not represented in cycle time at all. Its loss shows up first as longer *service* times (context re-acquisition) which pooling gains can mask, then as higher rework on complex and non-standard cases, and only much later as degraded outcomes. Meanwhile, the loss of informal local advice removes a filter: referrals that a nearby specialist would have resolved in a two-minute conversation become formal queue entries. That both raises demand on the constrained pool and adds a full handover of latency, and it can happen while the average looks fine because the affected cases are a minority. **Fix:** track `local_expertise_flag` rate, service time split by that flag, referral rate within complexity stratum, and rework by `location` × `customer_segment`.

**4. Fairness across teams and customer segments.** An average is a volume-weighted number and is silent on distribution. Three separate fairness failures can hide inside a flat average: East improves while North and South (about 75% of volume) degrade; complex and low-`priority` cases are starved by shared-queue prioritization while high-priority simple cases sail through; and customers needing language or regional accommodation face more handovers and more context loss, so harm concentrates on the segment with the least voice. The redistribution from North/South to East may be a legitimate equity choice, but it must be *chosen* explicitly, with a stated tolerance for how much North/South may degrade, rather than discovered later inside an aggregate. **Fix:** disparity ratios and stratum-level SLA miss rates as first-class guardrails, plus pre-agreed tolerances.

**5. Learning and capability erosion.** This is the risk with the longest time constant and the weakest signal in cycle time — plausibly *zero* signal in the first year, then a structural shift. If local staff never see hard cases, they stop being able to handle them, so the referral rate rises, so the central queue loads further, so more is routed away: a reinforcing loop with a delay of quarters. The organization also loses its pipeline of future specialists, since specialists have historically grown out of local technical work. By the time this appears in cycle time it is expensive to reverse, because reversing it requires rebuilding capability, not re-drawing an org chart. **Fix:** treat activity repertoire per local resource, entropy of resource activity profiles, resource-similarity drift and role-cluster structure as capability instruments; set a minimum share of complex cases that must retain local involvement.

**6. Accountability and ownership.** Shared approval plus a pull-based specialist queue removes the named owner. Cycle time cannot see this, but it produces: cases idling between queues because everyone assumes someone else has them; rising `reassignment_count`; contested rework attribution that disables the learning loop standardization was meant to enable; and customers with no one to call. Some of these *shorten* recorded activity times (work is touched briefly by many people) while lengthening true elapsed time in ways that only show up as idle gaps. **Fix:** distinct resources per case, ownership concentration index, `reassignment_count` p90, and explicit detection of idle gaps not attributable to any working resource.

**7. Exception handling and resilience.** Standardized shared queues are optimized for the modal case, and exceptions — absence cover, urgent escalations, unusual specialty combinations, accommodation requests — are exactly what standardization handles worst. Today the failure is localized and visible (cross-team cover: 8.9 h, 18% rework). After pooling, failures become correlated: one central absence or outage delays all three teams at once, and at 85–90% utilization there is no slack to absorb it. Exceptions are a small share of volume, so they are invisible in the average while accounting for a large share of the tail, the complaints and the cost. **Fix:** blast-radius measurement, variant-level analysis of exception paths, cross-team handover rework tracking, and explicit exception-route capacity rather than a shared queue for everything.

**8. Measurement corruption and gaming.** Under queue pressure, the cheapest way to improve a metric is to change the data: record complexity lower to avoid the routing rule, close cases without required specialist input, split a case into two shorter ones, or stop the clock at a shared-queue boundary. Each of these *improves* average cycle time while worsening real performance, and each makes every other metric unreliable. **Fix:** audit the stability of `case_complexity` and `required_specialty` mix, watch for cases closed without required steps, monitor variant proliferation, and reconcile case counts against demand.

# 5. Pilot design

The design principle: the proposal bundles a capacity-*pooling* change (which queueing theory says should help) with capacity-*reducing* and demand-*increasing* changes (supervisor removal, broadened legal/risk detection, +15% utilization target). Bundling them makes the result uninterpretable and, on the arithmetic above, probably makes it fail. **Unbundle and sequence.**

 ## Phase 0 — Prerequisites and instrumentation (weeks −12 to 0)

Establish baseline and remove the known confounder. Specifically:
- Standardize case notes and handover documentation across all locations *before* any pooling. This is the documented root cause of 18% cross-team rework and 8.9 h cross-team wait; it is cheap, it delivers resilience benefits on its own, and pooling cannot succeed without it. Measure the effect independently — if cross-team rework falls toward 9%, part of the restructuring's case is already satisfied without restructuring.
- Instrument the log so queue entry/exit are distinct events (otherwise waiting time inside a shared queue is unobservable), and ensure `handover_reason`, `reassignment_count`, `local_expertise_flag` and `case_complexity` are recorded consistently and at a defined point in the case (complexity assessed *before* routing, so it cannot be gamed retrospectively).
- Collect 8–12 weeks of clean baseline: full stratified distributions (p50/p90/p99) by team × complexity × specialty × segment × priority, all seven handover edges with frequency and wait, rework by activity, SLA miss by stratum, and per-resource activity repertoires. Pre-register the analysis plan, guardrail thresholds and decision rules **before** seeing post-change data.

 ## Phase 1 — Pilot scope

Pool the three technical specialists into a shared service **with capacity, scope and headcount held constant**, and apply the high-complexity routing rule. Explicitly *out* of scope for Phase 1:
- Do **not** remove the supervisor position, and do **not** create the shared approval queue. Per-supervisor load would rise 75% with no evidence of headroom; this is the change most likely to produce broad harm and it touches 49% of cases.
- Do **not** broaden legal/risk detection from 9 to 12 per 100. Legal/Risk is at 91–93% utilization; +33% demand is arithmetically infeasible. Broaden detection only after capacity is added, and then measure whether the added detection finds real risk (i.e. whether newly detected cases actually change outcomes) rather than assuming it does.
- Do **not** set a utilization target. A +15% utilization goal is a goal to consume the pooling benefit. Let utilization land where demand puts it and measure the resulting wait.
- Reserve a protected **local advice channel**: roughly 15–20% of each pooled specialist's time held for same-day informal consultation with their originating team, not queue work. This preserves the referral filter and the learning path, and its removal is a testable variable in a later phase.
- Retain a **named case owner** (the local caseworker) for every case regardless of which shared queue touches it, so Phase 1 tests pooling without also testing accountability dispersion.

Difficult Quality Check centralization: hold for Phase 2, since adding it in Phase 1 both confounds the pooling test and consumes the headroom the test depends on.

 ## Comparison group

A stepped-wedge design across the three teams, which handles secular time trends, gives every team eventual access, and is more defensible than a simple pilot-vs-control:
- **Weeks 1–10: East only** in the pooled model; North and South continue as-is (controls). East is the right starting point — E_Tech at 82% utilization, worst cycle time (8.6 d median, 22.8 d p90), worst rework (11.5%), worst satisfaction (3.8/5) — so it has the most to gain and pooling theory predicts the largest benefit there. Starting with East also tests the most favourable case: if pooling does not help East, it will not help anyone.
- **Weeks 11–20: East + South.** South added; North remains control.
- **Weeks 21–30: all three,** if guardrails hold.

Where volume allows, layer **case-level randomization** within the pooled teams for high-complexity cases (pooled routing vs retained local handling) during weeks 1–10. This is the cleanest possible estimate of the effect on the group at highest risk, and it removes team-level confounding. East produces ~520 cases/month, of which roughly 20–30% are high-complexity, giving ~110–160 per month and ~300–450 over ten weeks — adequate for medians and for detecting large p90 shifts, underpowered for p99. Be explicit about that limit rather than over-claiming precision on the tail.

Important caveat to state up front: pooling one team's specialist is not really pooling — the theoretical benefit comes from multi-server pooling. So East-only measures the *routing rule plus loss of embedded co-location* without the pooling benefit, which is a conservative (pessimistic) test. Read Phase 1 as a floor and Phase 2 (East + South, two pooled servers) as the first real test of the pooling mechanism. Alternatively, pool all three specialists from week 1 but pilot the *routing rules* on East only — this gets the pooling benefit into the test earlier at the cost of contaminating North/South as controls. Choose one and pre-register it; do not decide after seeing results.

 ## Pre/post observation window

- Baseline: 8–12 weeks (≥3× p90 cycle time of 22.8 d).
- Transition/washout: 4 weeks, **excluded from all comparisons.** Learning curves, tooling changes and queue transients dominate here; including it manufactures both a false negative early and a false positive later.
- Post-measurement: 12–16 weeks per step, minimum 8. Rationale: p90 cycle time is 16–23 days, so a 12-week window gives roughly 4–5 turns of the tail and enough completed complex cases to estimate stratified p90 with usable confidence.
- **Analyze by arrival cohort, not completion date.** Completion-date analysis in a transition period is survivorship-biased toward fast cases. Report, for each weekly arrival cohort, the fraction still open — if that fraction is rising, no comparison of completed-case averages is valid, full stop.
- Total realistic timeline to a full-rollout decision: 9–12 months. If management cannot accept that horizon, the honest conclusion is that the decision is being made on faith, not evidence, and the guardrails become the only protection.

 ## Segmentation

Every primary metric reported across:
- **Team / location**: North, South, East separately, never pooled into an organization-wide average as the headline. East is expected to move opposite to North/South; averaging them destroys the finding.
- **Complexity**: low / medium / high, with high-complexity as a mandatory named reporting line, since the routing rules target it.
- **Specialty**: none / technical / legal / risk / security, since the constrained queues differ sharply in headroom and legal/risk is the predicted failure point.
- **Customer segment**: all segments, with explicit identification of language- and regional-accommodation segments, since they depend most on local knowledge.
- **Priority**: to detect starvation of low-priority work in a shared queue.
- **`local_expertise_flag`**: the single most direct measure of the mechanism under test.
- Key interactions, minimally team × complexity and segment × complexity. Report cell counts alongside estimates so thin cells are not over-interpreted.

 ## Guardrail metrics

Each with a pre-agreed threshold, an owner, and a weekly review. Guardrails are protections against harm, not success criteria; a pilot can clear all guardrails and still not justify rollout.

| Guardrail | Threshold | Review cadence |
|---|---|---|
| Legal/Risk queue WIP slope | No positive slope sustained 3 consecutive weeks | Weekly |
| Any shared-queue WIP slope | No positive slope sustained 4 consecutive weeks | Weekly |
| High-complexity p90 cycle time (pilot team) | Not worse than baseline +15% | Bi-weekly, by arrival cohort |
| Overall p90 cycle time (pilot team) | Not worse than baseline +10% | Bi-weekly |
| Control-team p90 (North/South) | Not worse than baseline +5% (spillover check) | Bi-weekly |
| SLA miss rate, worst-served segment | Not worse than baseline +2 points | Bi-weekly |
| Rework rate, `local_expertise_flag = 0` cases | Not above 12% (midpoint between local 9% and cross-team 18%) | Bi-weekly |
| Specialist service time, unflagged vs flagged | Gap not above 10% | Weekly |
| `reassignment_count` p90 | Not above baseline +1 | Weekly |
| Referral rate within high-complexity stratum | Not above baseline +20% | Weekly |
| Aged WIP (open beyond baseline p90 with no event in 5 days) | Not above baseline count | Weekly |
| Recorded complexity/specialty mix | Within ±3 points of baseline distribution | Weekly (data-integrity check) |
| Variant count / top-5 coverage | Top-5 coverage not falling more than 5 points | Bi-weekly |
| Customer satisfaction, pilot team | Not below baseline −0.2 | Monthly |
| Specialist and local-caseworker attrition or stated intent to leave | No increase | Monthly |

 ## Staff feedback collection

Staff raised the countervailing risks and are the best-instrumented sensor for the ones the log cannot see. Collect systematically, not anecdotally:
- Weekly 15-minute structured check-in with pooled specialists and pilot-team caseworkers: three fixed questions (what did you need and not get, what took longer than it should, what did you work around) plus one open.
- Monthly short survey with tracked quantitative items: role clarity ("I know who owns this case"), perceived support ("I can get technical advice when I need it"), skill development ("I am learning"), and perceived fairness ("cases are handled consistently regardless of team or customer"). Track the *same* items pre and post so movement is interpretable.
- A no-blame workaround log. Workarounds are the leading edge of both process failure and data corruption; if staff cannot report them safely you will find them later as unexplained metric improvements.
- Cross-check qualitative claims against the log: if staff say informal advice has disappeared, look for the referral-rate rise within complexity stratum; if they say ownership is unclear, look at `reassignment_count` and ownership concentration. Agreement between the two sources is the strongest evidence available.
- Include the control teams in the survey, so pilot-team changes can be separated from organization-wide mood and from Hawthorne effects.

 ## Rollback and adjustment triggers

Distinguish three responses, with pre-committed rules so the decision is not made under pressure:

**Adjust (continue with modification)** — triggered by a single guardrail breach with an identifiable cause:
- Technical queue utilization above 88%, or its wait above 6 h → add capacity, or raise the complexity threshold for mandatory routing so fewer cases enter the queue.
- Referral rate rising within complexity stratum → increase the protected local-advice allocation.
- Unflagged-expertise service time gap above 10% → improve note standards; consider assigning specialists a "home team" affinity so context is retained (partial pooling, which recovers most of the queueing benefit at much lower context cost).
- `reassignment_count` rising → strengthen explicit case ownership and handoff protocol.

**Pause (freeze the wedge, do not advance to the next team)** — triggered by two or more simultaneous guardrail breaches, by any control-team degradation attributable to the pilot, or by data-integrity failure (complexity mix shift, cases closed without required steps). Do not advance to the next step until the cause is understood; advancing a wedge over an unexplained breach converts a contained problem into an organization-wide one.

**Roll back** — triggered by any of:
- Any shared queue with a sustained positive WIP slope over 4+ weeks and no feasible capacity fix (i.e. genuine ρ ≥ 1).
- High-complexity p90 cycle time worse than baseline +25%.
- Rework on unflagged-expertise cases reaching the cross-team benchmark (≥16%).
- SLA miss rate in any segment worse than baseline +4 points.
- Appearance of a new failure population (cases beyond ~2× baseline p90) that did not exist in baseline.
- Specialist attrition, which mechanically removes pooled capacity and is the trigger for the adverse reinforcing loop.

Preconditions for rollback to be a real option, which must be arranged *before* Phase 1: do not delete the supervisor position, do not disband local team structures, do not remove local tooling or access, and keep specialists' local reporting lines nominally intact for the pilot duration. An unbundled, reversible pilot is only reversible if you preserve the ability to reverse it — and the proposal as written (remove a position, merge the specialists) destroys that ability in its first two steps.

# 6. Recommendation

**Modify and pilot. Do not proceed with the proposal as written, and do not reject the underlying idea.**

The core mechanism is sound and the evidence for it is in management's favour: three specialist queues at 71%, 68% and 82% utilization are demonstrably inefficient, and pooling them should cut mean specialist waiting time substantially — on an illustrative M/M/c basis, from roughly 3.1 to 0.7 service times at constant load. East, with E_Tech at 82%, the slowest cycle time (8.6 d median, 22.8 d p90), the highest rework (11.5%) and the lowest satisfaction (3.8/5), has a real and specific problem that pooling addresses directly. Rejecting the proposal outright would leave East's structural disadvantage in place and would forgo a genuine efficiency gain. Anyone arguing against pooling has to explain what else fixes East.

But the proposal as written should not proceed, for four reasons that are arithmetic rather than judgemental:

1. **Its own numbers contradict its own premise.** Handovers rise from 343 to 359 per 100 cases and weighted handover wait rises 23%, on management's favourable estimates. The stated benefit "fewer handovers" is not what the estimate table describes.
2. **The Legal/Risk queue is infeasible as specified.** Specialists at 91–93% utilization cannot absorb +33% demand; that is ρ > 1, an unbounded backlog, not a 9.6 h → 10.5 h shift. This is not a risk to monitor; it is a capacity error to fix before implementation.
3. **The approval change is a capacity cut disguised as standardization.** Demand +17% against headcount −33% is per-supervisor load +75%, affecting 49 of every 100 cases. It is only feasible if approvals currently occupy under ~57% of supervisor capacity, which nobody has demonstrated.
4. **The utilization target spends the benefit.** The pooling gain exists only while the shared service stays below roughly 88–90% utilization; below ~87% for North and South to be no worse off. The +15% target lands at ~85%, leaving 2–5 points of headroom to absorb the legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Check that the proposal also loads onto the same pool. Targeting high utilization on a shared queue is not efficiency; it is the deliberate removal of the slack that makes queues tolerable.

So: implement the part that works, with the slack intact, one team at a time, reversibly. Concretely — standardize case notes first and measure it separately, since it addresses the documented root cause of poor cross-team cover (18% rework) and may deliver much of the resilience benefit with no restructuring at all; pool technical capacity with headcount and scope held constant; protect 15–20% of specialist time for local advice, which preserves both the referral filter and the learning path; keep a named case owner through every shared queue; add Legal/Risk capacity *before* broadening detection; defer the supervisor removal and shared approval queue entirely until approval-queue headroom is measured; drop the utilization target and treat utilization as an outcome to observe rather than a goal to hit; start with East via a stepped wedge.

**Conditionality.** Continued rollout should be contingent, at each wedge step, on: no shared queue showing a sustained positive WIP slope; high-complexity p90 cycle time no worse than baseline +15%; rework on `local_expertise_flag = 0` cases below 12%; no segment's SLA miss rate worse than baseline +2 points; `reassignment_count` p90 no worse than baseline +1; the specialist service-time gap between unflagged and flagged cases under 10%; stable recorded complexity mix; and no increase in specialist attrition. Two simultaneous breaches pause the wedge; the rollback triggers in section 5 end it. Rollout is authorized one step at a time, never as a single decision, and the authority to advance should require the stratified numbers on the table, not a summary.

**What management should explicitly not infer from early averages.** This deserves to be stated as a decision rule, because the most likely outcome (the central scenario) is one where the headline number looks *good* while the process degrades:

- **An improved average cycle time in the first 4–6 weeks is not evidence of success.** It is the expected artifact of survivorship: complex cases have not finished yet, so the completed-case pool is unrepresentatively simple. Before reading any average, check the fraction of each arrival cohort still open; if it is rising, the average is uninterpretable.
- **A flat or improved average is compatible with every stratum getting worse.** Routing simple cases locally (faster) and complex cases centrally (slower) shifts the completion mix. Insist on the stratified table; a request for "just the headline number" should be declined here, and the reason explained.
- **Higher specialist utilization is not evidence of higher productivity.** It rises equally when throughput improves and when service time inflates from lost context. Require utilization to be reported decomposed into throughput per specialist-hour and mean service time, split by `local_expertise_flag`.
- **Fewer handovers is not less waiting.** The proposal's own table has fewer handover *types* inside teams and more total handovers and more total wait. Count wait, not edges.
- **Early rework improvement is not proof, and early rework worsening is not disproof.** Standardization improves rework slowly; context loss worsens it immediately. A 6-week reading catches only one of the two forces. Judge rework at 12+ weeks, stratified.
- **Learning and capability effects will show nothing in the pilot window.** A clean 12-week result is *not* evidence that deskilling will not occur; the loop that erodes local capability and raises referral demand operates over quarters. Capability instruments (activity repertoire, resource similarity, role structure) must continue past the pilot into ongoing monitoring, and the absence of a signal at 12 weeks should be reported as "not yet observable," not as "not a problem."
- **An improved average with an unchanged or better fairness picture is the only combination that justifies rollout.** An improved average with widening dispersion means the gain was taken from someone — most likely complex cases, low-priority cases, accommodation-needing customers, or North and South. That is a redistribution to be decided deliberately, with the losers named, not a result to be reported as an efficiency win.
- **Finally, if the metrics improve while the recorded complexity mix shifts, treat the data as compromised, not the process as improved.** Under queue pressure the cheapest way to make a number better is to change how work is classified.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

```python
"""
Before/after comparison of a shared-service restructuring.

Design notes baked into the code:
  * Periods are split on CASE ARRIVAL time, not completion time. Comparing
    completed cases by completion date during a transition is survivorship-
    biased toward fast (simple) cases.
  * A washout window is excluded from both arms.
  * Every headline metric is reported stratified. The unstratified average is
    computed only to be shown next to the strata it hides.
  * Open-case censoring is reported explicitly; if it differs between arms,
    completed-case comparisons are flagged as unreliable.
"""

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE, ACT, TS, RES = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "org:resource"
STRATA = ["team", "location", "case_complexity", "required_specialty",
          "customer_segment", "priority", "local_expertise_flag"]

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 0. Load and normalize
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def prepare(path):
    df = pd.read_csv(path)
    df[TS] = pd.to_datetime(df[TS], utc=True)
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS)
    df = df.sort_values([CASE, TS], kind="mergesort")

    g = df.groupby(CASE)[TS]
    case_meta = pd.DataFrame({"arrival": g.min(), "last_event": g.max()})

    # A case is "complete" if it reached a terminal activity, NOT if it is old.
    terminal = df[df[ACT].isin(["Close"])].groupby(CASE)[TS].max()
    case_meta["completed_at"] = terminal
    case_meta["is_complete"] = case_meta["completed_at"].notna()
    case_meta["cycle_days"] = (
        (case_meta["completed_at"] - case_meta["arrival"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    )
    # Case-level attributes: take the FIRST recorded value so that later
    # re-classification (a known gaming route) cannot rewrite history.
    first = df.groupby(CASE).first()
    for col in STRATA + ["outcome", "rework_flag", "reassignment_count"]:
        if col in df.columns:
            case_meta[col] = first[col] if col in first else np.nan
    case_meta["rework_any"] = df.groupby(CASE)["rework_flag"].max()
    case_meta["n_resources"] = df.groupby(CASE)[RES].nunique()
    case_meta["n_events"] = df.groupby(CASE).size()
    # Ownership concentration: Herfindahl over a case's resource activity shares.
    case_meta["ownership_hhi"] = (
        df.groupby([CASE, RES]).size()
          .groupby(level=0)
          .apply(lambda s: ((s / s.sum()) ** 2).sum())
    )
    return df, case_meta


def split_periods(df, case_meta, pre, washout, post):
    """pre/washout/post are (start, end) timestamp pairs. Split on ARRIVAL."""
    def window(lo, hi):
        ids = case_meta.index[(case_meta["arrival"] >= lo) & (case_meta["arrival"] < hi)]
        return df[df[CASE].isin(ids)].copy(), case_meta.loc[ids].copy()
    pre_df,  pre_meta  = window(*pre)
    post_df, post_meta = window(*post)
    # washout deliberately discarded
    return (pre_df, pre_meta), (post_df, post_meta)


def censoring_report(pre_meta, post_meta, horizon_days=90):
    """If open-case fractions differ, completed-case comparisons are invalid."""
    rows = []
    for name, m in [("pre", pre_meta), ("post", post_meta)]:
        rows.append({
            "period": name,
            "cases": len(m),
            "open_frac": float((~m["is_complete"]).mean()),
            "open_frac_high_cx": float(
                (~m.loc[m["case_complexity"] == "high", "is_complete"]).mean()),
        })
    rep = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    gap = abs(rep.loc[0, "open_frac"] - rep.loc[1, "open_frac"])
    rep.attrs["reliable"] = gap < 0.03
    if not rep.attrs["reliable"]:
        print(f"WARNING: open-case fraction differs by {gap:.1%}. "
              "Completed-case cycle-time comparison is survivorship-biased. "
              "Use arrival-cohort survival curves instead.")
    return rep

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 1. Handover-of-work / working-together / subcontracting networks
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def edges(sna):
    """pm4py SNA results expose a dict of (r1, r2) -> weight."""
    conns = getattr(sna, "connections", sna)
    return pd.Series(conns, dtype=float).rename_axis(["source", "target"]).rename("weight")


def networks(log, resource_key=RES):
    return {
        # beta=0: unweighted by handover distance; also run beta=1 to weight
        # direct handovers more heavily than distant ones.
        "handover":         edges(pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
                                log, beta=0, resource_key=resource_key)),
        "handover_beta1":   edges(pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
                                log, beta=1, resource_key=resource_key)),
        "working_together": edges(pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
                                log, resource_key=resource_key)),
        # n=2 -> A hands to B who hands back to A (the "local loop" pattern).
        "subcontracting":   edges(pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
                                log, n=2, resource_key=resource_key)),
    }


def network_shape(e):
    """Centralization measures. The interesting change is topology, not size."""
    if e.empty:
        return {}
    out = e.groupby(level="source").sum()
    inn = e.groupby(level="target").sum()
    thr = out.add(inn, fill_value=0.0).sort_values(ascending=False)
    p = (thr / thr.sum()).values
    n = len(thr)
    srt = np.sort(p)
    gini = (2 * np.arange(1, n + 1) - n - 1).dot(srt) / n if n > 1 else 0.0
    return {
        "n_nodes": n,
        "n_edges": int(len(e)),
        "total_weight": float(e.sum()),
        "top1_share": float(p[0]),
        "top3_share": float(p[:3].sum()),
        "throughput_gini": float(gini),          # rises as hub-and-spoke forms
        "freeman_centralization": float((p.max() - p).sum() / (n - 2)) if n > 2 else np.nan,
        "hhi": float((p ** 2).sum()),
    }


def compare_networks(pre_log, post_log, top=25):
    pre_n, post_n = networks(pre_log), networks(post_log)
    shape = pd.DataFrame({
        k: {"pre": network_shape(pre_n[k]), "post": network_shape(post_n[k])}
        for k in pre_n
    }).T
    diffs = {}
    for k in pre_n:
        a = pre_n[k].rename("pre").to_frame()
        b = post_n[k].rename("post").to_frame()
        j = a.join(b, how="outer").fillna(0.0)
        j["delta"] = j["post"] - j["pre"]
        j["status"] = np.select(
            [j["pre"] == 0, j["post"] == 0], ["new", "disappeared"], "changed")
        diffs[k] = j.reindex(j["delta"].abs().sort_values(ascending=False).index).head(top)
    return shape, diffs


def cross_boundary_share(df, boundary="team"):
    """Fraction of handovers that cross a team boundary, and their rework rate."""
    d = df.sort_values([CASE, TS])
    nxt = d.groupby(CASE)[[RES, boundary]].shift(-1)
    pair = d.assign(next_res=nxt[RES], next_grp=nxt[boundary]).dropna(subset=["next_res"])
    pair = pair[pair[RES] != pair["next_res"]]
    crossing = pair[boundary] != pair["next_grp"]
    rework_on_crossing = (
        df[df[CASE].isin(pair.loc[crossing, CASE])]
          .groupby(CASE)["rework_flag"].max().mean()
    )
    return {
        "handovers_per_100_cases": 100 * len(pair) / d[CASE].nunique(),
        "cross_boundary_share": float(crossing.mean()),
        "cross_boundary_per_100_cases": 100 * int(crossing.sum()) / d[CASE].nunique(),
        "rework_rate_on_cross_boundary_cases": float(rework_on_crossing),
    }

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 2. Per-edge frequency and waiting time  (rebuilds the scenario's tables)
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def handover_edge_stats(df, group_by=RES, min_freq=5):
    """Frequency per 100 cases + wait quantiles per resource pair.

    This is the table management supplied. Recomputing it from the log is how
    you test the estimate: watch for waits that RAMP across cohorts rather
    than shifting to a new level -- a ramp means rho >= 1.
    """
    d = df.sort_values([CASE, TS])
    s = d.groupby(CASE)
    nxt = pd.DataFrame({
        "next_res": s[group_by].shift(-1),
        "next_act": s[ACT].shift(-1),
        "next_ts":  s[TS].shift(-1),
    })
    p = pd.concat([d[[CASE, ACT, TS, group_by, "rework_flag", "handover_reason"]], nxt], axis=1)
    p = p.dropna(subset=["next_res"])
    p = p[p[group_by] != p["next_res"]]
    p["wait_h"] = (p["next_ts"] - p[TS]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    n_cases = d[CASE].nunique()

    agg = p.groupby([group_by, "next_res"]).agg(
        freq=("wait_h", "size"),
        wait_p50=("wait_h", "median"),
        wait_p90=("wait_h", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)),
        wait_p99=("wait_h", lambda x: x.quantile(0.99)),
        wait_mean=("wait_h", "mean"),
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean"),
    ).reset_index()
    agg["freq_per_100"] = 100 * agg["freq"] / n_cases
    agg["wait_hours_per_100_cases"] = agg["freq_per_100"] * agg["wait_p50"]
    agg = agg[agg["freq"] >= min_freq].sort_values("wait_hours_per_100_cases",
                                                   ascending=False)
    total = {
        "handovers_per_100": agg["freq_per_100"].sum(),
        "weighted_wait_h_per_100_cases": agg["wait_hours_per_100_cases"].sum(),
    }
    return agg, total

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 3. Service vs waiting time; the waiting-share indicator
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def timing(df, log):
    enr = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
        df,
        service_time_column="@@service_time",
        sojourn_time_column="@@sojourn_time",
        waiting_time_column="@@waiting_time",
    )
    per_case = enr.groupby(CASE).agg(
        service_s=("@@service_time", "first"),
        waiting_s=("@@waiting_time", "first"),
        sojourn_s=("@@sojourn_time", "first"),
    )
    per_case["waiting_share"] = (
        per_case["waiting_s"] / per_case["sojourn_s"].replace(0, np.nan))

    svc_mean = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="mean")
    svc_med  = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")
    svc = pd.DataFrame({"service_mean_s": svc_mean, "service_median_s": svc_med})
    return per_case, svc


def service_time_by_expertise(df, activities=("Specialist Review", "Local Technical Assessment")):
    """THE key diagnostic: is the utilization gain throughput, or context re-acquisition?

    If service time for local_expertise_flag == 0 exceeds the flagged case
    by >10%, the specialists are busier without being more productive.
    """
    out = []
    for act in activities:
        for flag in df["local_expertise_flag"].dropna().unique():
            sub = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
                df, "local_expertise_flag", [flag], level="event", retain=True)
            log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(sub)
            svc = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")
            if act in svc:
                out.append({"activity": act, "local_expertise_flag": flag,
                            "service_median_s": svc[act]})
    t = pd.DataFrame(out).pivot(index="activity", columns="local_expertise_flag",
                                values="service_median_s")
    if {0, 1}.issubset(t.columns):
        t["gap_pct"] = 100 * (t[0] / t[1] - 1)
    return t

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 4. Queue backlog trend -- distinguishes a LEVEL SHIFT from a RAMP (rho >= 1)
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

SHARED_QUEUES = {
    "technical_shared": ["Local Technical Assessment", "Technical Shared Service"],
    "specialist":       ["Specialist Review"],
    "approval":         ["Supervisor Approval", "Shared Approval"],
    "quality":          ["Quality Check"],
}

def queue_backlog(df, queue_activities, freq="W"):
    """WIP in a queue by week: entries minus exits, cumulated.

    A positive, sustained slope is the falsifiable signature of an unstable
    queue. For Legal/Risk (91-93% utilization pre-change, +33% demand) this
    is the single most important chart in the whole analysis.
    """
    q = df[df[ACT].isin(queue_activities)]
    entries = q.groupby(CASE)[TS].min().dt.to_period(freq).value_counts().sort_index()
    exits   = q.groupby(CASE)[TS].max().dt.to_period(freq).value_counts().sort_index()
    idx = entries.index.union(exits.index)
    wip = (entries.reindex(idx, fill_value=0) - exits.reindex(idx, fill_value=0)).cumsum()
    x = np.arange(len(wip))
    slope = np.polyfit(x, wip.values, 1)[0] if len(wip) > 2 else np.nan
    return wip, {"wip_slope_per_period": float(slope),
                 "unstable": bool(slope > 0 and len(wip) > 3)}


def wait_by_arrival_cohort(df, case_meta, queue_activities, freq="W"):
    """Wait to enter a queue, grouped by case ARRIVAL week. Ramp vs level shift."""
    q = df[df[ACT].isin(queue_activities)].groupby(CASE)[TS].min().rename("q_entry")
    j = case_meta.join(q, how="inner")
    j["wait_h"] = (j["q_entry"] - j["arrival"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    j["cohort"] = j["arrival"].dt.to_period(freq)
    return j.groupby("cohort")["wait_h"].agg(
        n="size", p50="median", p90=lambda x: x.quantile(0.90))

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 5. Variants and performance DFG
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def variant_profile(log, top=15):
    v = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    counts = pd.Series({k: (len(x) if hasattr(x, "__len__") else x)
                        for k, x in v.items()}).sort_values(ascending=False)
    total = counts.sum()
    return counts.head(top) / total, {
        "n_variants": int(len(counts)),
        "top5_coverage": float(counts.head(5).sum() / total),
        "singleton_variants": int((counts == 1).sum()),
        # Variant proliferation + falling top-5 coverage = workaround behaviour.
        "variant_entropy": float(-(counts / total * np.log(counts / total)).sum()),
    }


def dfg_compare(pre_log, post_log):
    f_pre,  s_pre,  e_pre  = pm4py.discover_dfg(pre_log)
    f_post, s_post, e_post = pm4py.discover_dfg(post_log)
    p_pre,  _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(pre_log)
    p_post, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(post_log)

    def frame(freq, perf, label):
        rows = [{"edge": k, f"freq_{label}": v,
                 f"mean_s_{label}": (perf.get(k, {}) or {}).get("mean", np.nan)}
                for k, v in freq.items()]
        return pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("edge")

    j = frame(f_pre, p_pre, "pre").join(frame(f_post, p_post, "post"), how="outer")
    j["freq_delta"] = j["freq_post"].fillna(0) - j["freq_pre"].fillna(0)
    j["mean_delta_h"] = (j["mean_s_post"] - j["mean_s_pre"]) / 3600
    return j.sort_values("mean_delta_h", ascending=False)

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 6. Resource similarity and role discovery -- the capability instruments
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def similarity_matrix(log):
    sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log, resource_key=RES)
    s = pd.Series(getattr(sim, "connections", sim), dtype=float)
    return s.rename_axis(["r1", "r2"]).rename("similarity")


def similarity_summary(df, log):
    s = similarity_matrix(log).reset_index()
    team_of = df.groupby(RES)["team"].agg(lambda x: x.mode().iat[0]).to_dict()
    s["t1"], s["t2"] = s["r1"].map(team_of), s["r2"].map(team_of)
    s["same_team"] = s["t1"] == s["t2"]
    return {
        "mean_within_team": float(s.loc[s["same_team"], "similarity"].mean()),
        "mean_across_team": float(s.loc[~s["same_team"], "similarity"].mean()),
        # Rising across-team similarity among caseworkers = de-differentiation,
        # i.e. local staff converging on a narrower, identical activity set.
        "similarity_bimodality": float(s["similarity"].std()),
    }


def repertoire(df):
    """Activity repertoire per resource. Shrinking repertoire = deskilling."""
    r = df.groupby(RES).agg(
        n_activities=(ACT, "nunique"),
        n_events=(ACT, "size"),
        teams_served=("team", "nunique"),
    )
    ent = (df.groupby([RES, ACT]).size().groupby(level=0)
             .apply(lambda s: -((s / s.sum()) * np.log(s / s.sum())).sum()))
    r["activity_entropy"] = ent
    # Share of HIGH-complexity work each resource touches: the learning channel.
    hi = df[df["case_complexity"] == "high"].groupby(RES).size()
    r["high_cx_events"] = hi.reindex(r.index, fill_value=0)
    r["high_cx_share"] = r["high_cx_events"] / r["n_events"]
    return r


def roles(log):
    out = []
    for i, role in enumerate(pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)):
        acts = role[0] if isinstance(role, (list, tuple)) else role.activities
        imp  = role[1] if isinstance(role, (list, tuple)) else role.originator_importance
        out.append({"role_id": i,
                    "activities": sorted(acts),
                    "resources": sorted(imp.keys()),
                    "n_resources": len(imp)})
    return pd.DataFrame(out)


def role_diff(pre_log, post_log):
    """Watch for the DISAPPEARANCE of the embedded-technical role cluster."""
    a, b = roles(pre_log), roles(post_log)
    key = lambda t: set(map(lambda r: tuple(r), t["activities"]))
    return {"pre_n_roles": len(a), "post_n_roles": len(b),
            "pre_roles": a, "post_roles": b,
            "vanished_activity_sets": key(a) - key(b),
            "new_activity_sets": key(b) - key(a)}

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 7. Stratified outcomes: cycle time, rework, SLA, fairness
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

SLA_DAYS = {"low": 20.0, "medium": 14.0, "high": 10.0}   # illustrative

def stratified_outcomes(case_meta, by):
    """The table that must replace any single headline average."""
    m = case_meta.copy()
    m["sla_days"] = m["priority"].map(SLA_DAYS).fillna(14.0)
    done = m[m["is_complete"]]
    g = done.groupby(by, dropna=False)
    t = g.agg(
        n=("cycle_days", "size"),
        p50=("cycle_days", "median"),
        p90=("cycle_days", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)),
        p99=("cycle_days", lambda x: x.quantile(0.99)),
        mean=("cycle_days", "mean"),
        rework_rate=("rework_any", "mean"),
        mean_reassign=("reassignment_count", "mean"),
        p90_reassign=("reassignment_count", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)),
        mean_resources=("n_resources", "mean"),
        mean_ownership_hhi=("ownership_hhi", "mean"),
    )
    t["sla_miss_rate"] = g.apply(
        lambda d: float((d["cycle_days"] > d["sla_days"]).mean()))
    t["open_frac"] = m.groupby(by, dropna=False)["is_complete"].apply(
        lambda s: float((~s).mean()))
    return t.reset_index()


def all_strata(case_meta):
    """Every dimension the constraints require, plus the key interactions."""
    views = {}
    for dim in ["team", "location", "case_complexity", "required_specialty",
                "customer_segment", "priority", "local_expertise_flag"]:
        views[dim] = stratified_outcomes(case_meta, [dim])
    views["team_x_complexity"]    = stratified_outcomes(
        case_meta, ["team", "case_complexity"])
    views["segment_x_complexity"] = stratified_outcomes(
        case_meta, ["customer_segment", "case_complexity"])
    views["overall"] = stratified_outcomes(case_meta.assign(_all="all"), ["_all"])
    return views


def simpson_check(pre_meta, post_meta, dim="case_complexity", metric="p50"):
    """Flag the trap: overall metric improves while every stratum worsens."""
    a = stratified_outcomes(pre_meta.assign(_all="all"), ["_all"])[metric].iat[0]
    b = stratified_outcomes(post_meta.assign(_all="all"), ["_all"])[metric].iat[0]
    sa = stratified_outcomes(pre_meta, [dim]).set_index(dim)[metric]
    sb = stratified_outcomes(post_meta, [dim]).set_index(dim)[metric]
    strat = pd.DataFrame({"pre": sa, "post": sb}).dropna()
    strat["worse"] = strat["post"] > strat["pre"]
    paradox = (b < a) and bool(strat["worse"].all())
    if paradox:
        print(f"SIMPSON WARNING: overall {metric} improved "
              f"({a:.2f} -> {b:.2f}) while EVERY {dim} stratum worsened. "
              "Do not report the overall number without this table.")
    return {"overall_pre": a, "overall_post": b, "strata": strat, "paradox": paradox}


def fairness(case_meta, dim="customer_segment", min_n=30):
    """Dispersion, not central tendency. Also compare North/South vs East."""
    t = stratified_outcomes(case_meta, [dim])
    t = t[t["n"] >= min_n]
    return t, {
        "p90_disparity_ratio": float(t["p90"].max() / t["p90"].min()),
        "sla_miss_spread_pts": float(100 * (t["sla_miss_rate"].max()
                                            - t["sla_miss_rate"].min())),
        "worst_served": t.loc[t["p90"].idxmax(), dim],
        "best_served":  t.loc[t["p90"].idxmin(), dim],
    }


def rework_detail(log, df, case_meta):
    per_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
    rw = pd.Series(per_activity, dtype=float).rename("cases_with_rework")
    rw = (rw / df[CASE].nunique()).rename("share_of_cases").to_frame()
    # Cross-check against the flag, stratified by the expertise mechanism.
    by_flag = case_meta.groupby(["local_expertise_flag", "case_complexity"],
                               dropna=False)["rework_any"].agg(["size", "mean"])
    return rw.sort_values("share_of_cases", ascending=False), by_flag


def defensive_referral_check(df, case_meta):
    """Referral rate WITHIN complexity stratum. A rise here is not 'better
    routing' -- it is staff referring because local advice vanished."""
    ref_acts = ["Specialist Review", "Local Technical Assessment"]
    referred = df[df[ACT].isin(ref_acts)][CASE].unique()
    m = case_meta.assign(referred=case_meta.index.isin(referred))
    return m.groupby(["case_complexity", "team"], dropna=False)["referred"].agg(
        ["size", "mean"]).rename(columns={"mean": "referral_rate"})


def aged_wip(df, case_meta, baseline_p90_days, stale_days=5):
    """Cases lost between queues: open past baseline p90, no recent event."""
    now = df[TS].max()
    open_cases = case_meta[~case_meta["is_complete"]].copy()
    open_cases["age_days"] = (now - open_cases["arrival"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    open_cases["idle_days"] = (now - open_cases["last_event"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    lost = open_cases[(open_cases["age_days"] > baseline_p90_days)
                      & (open_cases["idle_days"] > stale_days)]
    return lost, {"n_aged_stale": len(lost),
                  "share_of_open": float(len(lost) / max(len(open_cases), 1))}


def data_integrity(pre_meta, post_meta):
    """Classification gaming makes every other metric untrustworthy."""
    checks = {}
    for dim in ["case_complexity", "required_specialty", "priority"]:
        a = pre_meta[dim].value_counts(normalize=True)
        b = post_meta[dim].value_counts(normalize=True)
        j = pd.DataFrame({"pre": a, "post": b}).fillna(0.0)
        j["delta_pts"] = 100 * (j["post"] - j["pre"])
        checks[dim] = j
        if j["delta_pts"].abs().max() > 3:
            print(f"INTEGRITY WARNING: '{dim}' mix moved "
                  f"{j['delta_pts'].abs().max():.1f} pts. Possible "
                  "reclassification to avoid queues; treat metrics as suspect.")
    return checks

 # 8. Filtering helpers and difference-in-differences
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def slice_log(df, **filters):
    """e.g. slice_log(df, team=['East'], case_complexity=['high'],
                      required_specialty=['legal','risk'],
                      customer_segment=['sme'])"""
    out = df
    for attr, values in filters.items():
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, attr, list(values), level="case", retain=True)
    return out


def focus_variants(df, keep_top=5):
    log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)
    v = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    counts = pd.Series({k: (len(x) if hasattr(x, "__len__") else x) for k, x in v.items()})
    top = list(counts.sort_values(ascending=False).head(keep_top).index)
    return pm4py.filter_variants(log, top, retain=True), \
           pm4py.filter_variants(log, top, retain=False)   # the long tail


def temporal_features(df):
    """Day-of-week / hour effects, so calendar artifacts are not read as
    process change (much of the multi-day cycle time is calendar time)."""
    return pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
        df, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS, case_id_key=CASE)


def did(pre_meta, post_meta, treated_teams, control_teams,
        metric="cycle_days", q=0.90):
    """Difference-in-differences on a QUANTILE, with a bootstrap CI.
    Averages are the wrong estimand here; the tail is where the risk lives."""
    def stat(m, teams):
        s = m.loc[m["is_complete"] & m["team"].isin(teams), metric].dropna()
        return s.quantile(q), s.values

    t0, t0v = stat(pre_meta,  treated_teams)
    t1, t1v = stat(post_meta, treated_teams)
    c0, c0v = stat(pre_meta,  control_teams)
    c1, c1v = stat(post_meta, control_teams)
    point = (t1 - t0) - (c1 - c0)

    rng, boots = np.random.default_rng(7), []
    for _ in range(2000):
        r = lambda v: rng.choice(v, size=len(v), replace=True)
        boots.append(((np.quantile(r(t1v), q) - np.quantile(r(t0v), q))
                      - (np.quantile(r(c1v), q) - np.quantile(r(c0v), q))))
    lo, hi = np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5])
    return {"quantile": q, "did_days": float(point),
            "ci95": (float(lo), float(hi)),
            "treated_pre": float(t0), "treated_post": float(t1),
            "control_pre": float(c0), "control_post": float(c1),
            # Parallel-trends assumption is untestable with two periods;
            # verify on pre-period sub-windows before trusting this.
            "note": "check pre-trends on split baseline before interpreting"}

 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------
 # 9. Orchestration
 # --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def run(pre_path, post_path, pre_win, wash_win, post_win,
        treated=("East",), control=("North", "South")):
    df_all, meta_all = prepare(pre_path)          # or one combined log
    (pre_df, pre_meta), (post_df, post_meta) = split_periods(
        df_all, meta_all, pre_win, wash_win, post_win)

    pre_log  = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(pre_df)
    post_log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(post_df)

    report = {}

    # Gate 0: is the comparison even legitimate?
    report["censoring"]  = censoring_report(pre_meta, post_meta)
    report["integrity"]  = data_integrity(pre_meta, post_meta)

    # Structure
    report["net_shape"], report["net_edge_diff"] = compare_networks(pre_log, post_log)
    report["boundary_pre"]  = cross_boundary_share(pre_df)
    report["boundary_post"] = cross_boundary_share(post_df)
    report["edges_pre"],  report["edge_total_pre"]  = handover_edge_stats(pre_df)
    report["edges_post"], report["edge_total_post"] = handover_edge_stats(post_df)

    # Queueing -- level shift or ramp?
    report["queues"] = {}
    for name, acts in SHARED_QUEUES.items():
        wip, diag = queue_backlog(post_df, acts)
        report["queues"][name] = {
            "wip": wip, **diag,
            "wait_by_cohort": wait_by_arrival_cohort(post_df, post_meta, acts),
        }
        if diag["unstable"]:
            print(f"ROLLBACK CANDIDATE: '{name}' queue WIP has a positive slope "
                  f"({diag['wip_slope_per_period']:.1f}/week) -- rho may exceed 1.")

    # Time decomposition
    report["timing_pre"]  = timing(pre_df,  pre_log)
    report["timing_post"] = timing(post_df, post_log)
    report["svc_by_expertise_pre"]  = service_time_by_expertise(pre_df)
    report["svc_by_expertise_post"] = service_time_by_expertise(post_df)

    # Behaviour
    report["variants_pre"]  = variant_profile(pre_log)
    report["variants_post"] = variant_profile(post_log)
    report["dfg_diff"]      = dfg_compare(pre_log, post_log)

    # Capability
    report["similarity_pre"]  = similarity_summary(pre_df,  pre_log)
    report["similarity_post"] = similarity_summary(post_df, post_log)
    report["repertoire_pre"]  = repertoire(pre_df)
    report["repertoire_post"] = repertoire(post_df)
    report["roles"]           = role_diff(pre_log, post_log)

    # Outcomes, stratified -- never a single headline number
    report["strata_pre"]  = all_strata(pre_meta)
    report["strata_post"] = all_strata(post_meta)
    report["simpson_complexity"] = simpson_check(pre_meta, post_meta, "case_complexity")
    report["simpson_team"]       = simpson_check(pre_meta, post_meta, "team")
    report["fairness_segment"]   = fairness(post_meta, "customer_segment")
    report["fairness_team"]      = fairness(post_meta, "team")
    report["rework"]             = rework_detail(post_log, post_df, post_meta)
    report["defensive_referral"] = defensive_referral_check(post_df, post_meta)

    base_p90 = pre_meta.loc[pre_meta["is_complete"], "cycle_days"].quantile(0.90)
    report["aged_wip"] = aged_wip(post_df, post_meta, base_p90)

    # Causal-ish estimates on the tail, for the highest-risk stratum
    report["did_p90_all"] = did(pre_meta, post_meta, treated, control, q=0.90)
    hi_pre  = pre_meta[pre_meta["case_complexity"] == "high"]
    hi_post = post_meta[post_meta["case_complexity"] == "high"]
    report["did_p90_high_complexity"] = did(hi_pre, hi_post, treated, control, q=0.90)
    report["did_p50_high_complexity"] = did(hi_pre, hi_post, treated, control, q=0.50)

    # Deep dive on the predicted binding constraint
    lr = slice_log(post_df, required_specialty=["legal", "risk", "security"])
    report["legal_risk_edges"], report["legal_risk_total"] = handover_edge_stats(lr)

    return report
```

**How to read the output, in order.** First check `censoring` and `integrity`; if open-case fractions differ between arms or the recorded complexity mix has moved, stop — nothing downstream is interpretable. Second, check `queues`: a positive WIP slope, especially on Legal/Risk, is the decisive finding and does not require waiting for outcomes. Third, check `svc_by_expertise_post["gap_pct"]`: if unflagged-expertise cases take more than 10% longer, the utilization gain is context re-acquisition and the pooling arithmetic no longer holds. Fourth, read `strata_post` and both `simpson_*` checks before any aggregate; the expected central-scenario pattern is an aggregate median that is flat-to-better with p90 worse and the high-complexity stratum clearly worse. Fifth, read `did_p90_high_complexity` — that is the effect on the group the routing rules actually target. Only then look at the overall average, and look at it as the thing to be explained by the strata rather than as the result.
