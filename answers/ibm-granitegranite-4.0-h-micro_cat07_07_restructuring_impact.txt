# 1. Mechanism map

The proposed restructuring introduces several structural changes that will affect various aspects of the process. These changes are mapped to expected process-mining outcomes:

- **Nominal handover reduction**: By centralizing specialists and shared services, the frequency of handovers between local teams and specialists should decrease, as cases will flow more internally through shared queues.
  
- **Central queue formation**: The proposed restructuring introduces centralized queues for technical specialists, legal/risk reviews, and approvals. This will create performance metrics for these new queues, such as average wait times and queue lengths.

- **Specialist utilization**: Centralizing specialist reviews and legal/risk assessments into a single shared service is expected to increase utilization, as specialists will handle a higher volume of cases.

- **Rework**: The restructuring aims to reduce rework by standardizing handover processes and improving the clarity of roles. However, centralization may also increase the complexity of cases routed to specialists, potentially raising rework rates.

- **Local expertise loss**: With the shift to shared services, local expertise may diminish as embedded specialists are absorbed into centralized queues. This could impact the ability to provide nuanced, region-specific advice.

- **Role clarity**: The restructuring may reduce role clarity as roles are integrated into broader, shared responsibilities. This could lead to ambiguity in accountability and decision-making.

- **Resilience to absence**: The centralized model may improve resilience to staff absence by distributing workload more evenly across specialists in the shared service. However, it could also create dependency bottlenecks if one specialist becomes a critical resource.

- **Fairness across teams/customer segments**: The new structure aims to reduce fairness issues by standardizing processes and ensuring equitable access to specialist expertise. However, it may also introduce new fairness concerns if certain teams or customer segments face longer wait times in shared queues.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic scenario

- **Handover pattern**: Significant reduction in handovers, particularly between local teams and specialists. 
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**: Queues in the shared service may experience moderate increases in wait times initially but should stabilize as processes become more efficient.
- **Cycle-time distribution**: Overall cycle times may decrease due to more efficient routing and utilization of specialists.
- **Rework and quality pattern**: Rework rates should decrease as standardized processes reduce errors and improve clarity.
- **Role clarity and accountability**: Role clarity may improve as roles become more defined within shared services, though some ambiguity may remain.
- **Fairness implications**: Fairness should improve as all teams have access to shared specialist resources, though initial imbalances may occur.
- **Early warning signs**: Early signs of success include reduced handovers, improved SLA adherence, and higher specialist utilization rates.

## Central scenario

- **Handover pattern**: Handovers remain high, with potential bottlenecks in the centralized queues.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**: Queues may become congested, leading to increased waiting times, especially for high-complexity cases.
- **Cycle-time distribution**: Cycle times may not improve significantly, or could even increase due to inefficient queue management.
- **Rework and quality pattern**: Rework rates may not improve or could rise if the centralized model struggles to handle complex cases efficiently.
- **Role clarity and accountability**: Role clarity may remain unclear, as the centralized model may not fully replace local expertise.
- **Fairness implications**: Fairness may be compromised if certain teams or customer segments experience longer wait times in shared queues.
- **Early warning signs**: Indicators of potential issues include high queue wait times, low specialist utilization, and increased rework rates.

## Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern**: Increased handovers, particularly as local teams struggle with the transition to centralized services.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern**: Queues may experience prolonged delays, leading to increased wait times across the organization.
- **Cycle-time distribution**: Cycle times may worsen due to the inefficient distribution of cases and increased bottlenecks.
- **Rework and quality pattern**: Rework rates may increase due to misrouting or inadequate specialist coverage.
- **Role clarity and accountability**: Role clarity may decrease further as the transition creates confusion and ambiguity.
- **Fairness implications**: Fairness issues may intensify, particularly for local teams that lose direct access to specialists.
- **Early warning signs**: Early warning signs include high rework rates, increased SLA miss rates, and negative feedback from staff regarding workflow disruptions.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency**: Centralized processes may exacerbate delays for cases requiring specialized attention, leading to longer tail latencies that are not captured by average cycle time alone.

2. **High-complexity cases**: Centralization might concentrate high-complexity cases in a few queues, leading to bottlenecks and prolonged delays that could impact SLA compliance.

3. **Local knowledge loss**: The loss of localized expertise could result in poor decision-making for region-specific cases, leading to increased rework and customer dissatisfaction.

4. **Fairness**: While the restructuring aims to reduce fairness issues, localized knowledge and team-specific strengths could be lost, leading to perceived unfairness among teams.

5. **Learning**: The shift to centralized processes may hinder knowledge transfer and learning opportunities for staff, as informal advice and informal learning become less prevalent.

6. **Accountability**: With roles becoming more centralized, accountability for individual case outcomes may become less clear, potentially leading to issues with ownership and responsibility.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope
- **Teams**: Select North and East teams as pilot teams, with East benefiting from existing high utilization of specialists.
- **Cases**: Focus on medium-complexity cases to balance risk and learning opportunities.
- **Timeframe**: Conduct the pilot over a 3-month period, with pre-pilot and post-pilot observations.

## Comparison group
- **Control group**: South team, which will remain on the current structure for comparison.
- **Alternative group**: A third team (e.g., West) could be used to test different routing rules or additional pilot scenarios.

## Pre/post observation window
- **Pre-pilot**: Observe baseline metrics for 2 weeks to establish a reference point.
- **Post-pilot**: Observe metrics for 2 weeks after the restructuring to assess the impact.

## Segmentation
- **By team**: Compare metrics for North, East, and South.
- **By complexity**: Segment cases by complexity levels (simple, medium, high).
- **By specialty**: Analyze outcomes for specialist utilization across different specialties.
- **By customer segment**: Evaluate impacts on different customer segments, particularly high-value and regional customers.

## Guardrail metrics
- **Maximum queue wait time**: Should remain within acceptable limits (e.g., < 4 hours).
- **Rework rate**: Should not exceed the current average rework rate by more than 10%.
- **Specialist utilization**: Should increase by at least 15%.
- **SLA adherence**: Should improve by at least 5%.
- **Customer satisfaction**: Should remain stable or improve.

## Staff feedback collection
- Conduct weekly surveys to gather feedback from staff on workflow changes, role clarity, and perceived fairness.
- Hold focus group sessions to discuss any emerging issues or suggestions for improvement.

## Rollback or adjustment triggers
- If handover times increase by more than 20% or SLA compliance drops below 90%, initiate a review and consider adjustments.
- If specialist utilization does not increase by the target rate after 2 weeks, adjust routing rules or provide additional training.
- If role clarity metrics indicate significant confusion, schedule additional training or clarification sessions.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation**: Proceed with a phased pilot of the restructuring, with close monitoring of the defined indicators. Do not roll out the restructuring organization-wide without first validating the pilot outcomes.

**Conditions**:
- **Monitor leading indicators**: Ensure that initial reductions in handovers and increases in specialist utilization are maintained.
- **Evaluate queue performance**: Ensure that shared service queues do not become bottlenecks; if they do, adjust routing rules or increase capacity.
- **Assess role clarity**: Implement measures to maintain or improve role clarity, such as clear documentation and regular communication.
- **Address fairness concerns**: Establish mechanisms to monitor and mitigate any perceived inequities in access to specialist resources.
- **Continuous feedback loop**: Maintain a robust feedback mechanism with staff to address any operational issues promptly.

**What not to infer from early averages**:
- Do not assume that improved average cycle times will translate into sustained efficiency without addressing underlying issues like bottleneck formation or reduced local expertise.
- Do not assume fairness improvements will persist without ongoing monitoring and adjustment.
- Do not rely solely on centralized metrics; continue to track localized performance metrics to ensure no loss of specialized knowledge or local advantages.
