# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let time (t=0) be the recorded `Submit` event, and let (H=72) elapsed hours be the SLA horizon.

For each case, define:

* (T): time from `Submit` to the first internally observed terminal disposition.
* (J\in{R,W,K,X}): terminal cause, where (R=) `Resolve`, (W=) `Withdraw`, (K=) `Reject`, and (X=) `Transfer`.
* (C): age at the observation cutoff when no terminal disposition has been observed.
* (Y=\min(T,C)): the observed duration or age.
* Open cases have (Y=C) and are right-censored for their eventual terminal time.

**Assumption 1 — horizon convention.** A case resolved at exactly 72 hours is timely; a case is an SLA breach when it has not reached `Resolve` by the end of hour 72.

**Assumption 2 — internal terminality.** `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` end internal observation. For the primary internal-system estimand, a withdrawal, rejection, or transfer prevents a later internal `Resolve`.

**Assumption 3 — one first terminal outcome.** Each terminal row records the first terminal disposition, with no reopening represented in the supplied extract.

## Estimand A: probability of no internal resolution by 72 hours

Define the resolution cumulative incidence

[
F_R(H)=P(T\le H,\ J=R).
]

Then the primary SLA tail-risk estimand is

[
\theta_{\text{no-resolve}}(H)
=1-F_R(H).
]

Under the internal absorbing-state definition,

[
1-F_R(H)
=S(H)+F_W(H)+F_K(H)+F_X(H),
]

where (S(H)=P(T>H)) is the probability that the case is still internally active at 72 hours.

This is the most appropriate estimand for **SLA governance based specifically on successful resolution**. A withdrawn or rejected case has a terminal disposition but has not achieved the stated operational success. A transferred case has not achieved an internally recorded resolution.

## Estimand B: probability of still having no terminal disposition at 72 hours

Define

[
\theta_{\text{active}}(H)=S(H)=P(T>H).
]

This measures the probability that the case remains in the internal workload with no `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer` by 72 hours.

This estimand is most useful for **backlog, staffing, queue management, and workload forecasting**. It is not equivalent to SLA success. For example, a case withdrawn at 27 hours is no longer active at 72 hours but still failed to reach `Resolve`.

The distinction is material in these data. Under the primary Aalen–Johansen encoding described below, the estimates at 72 hours are approximately:

* Resolution cumulative incidence: 30.4%.
* Withdrawal cumulative incidence: 14.0%.
* Rejection cumulative incidence: 10.1%.
* Transfer cumulative incidence: 7.5%.
* Still active: 38.0%.

Thus estimated non-resolution by 72 hours is approximately (1-0.304=69.6%), while estimated still-active backlog is approximately 38.0%. The roughly 31.6 percentage-point difference consists of non-resolution terminal dispositions before the horizon.

These are estimates under a censoring assumption, not directly observed proportions.

## Estimand C: late-resolution or breach-recovery incidence

For (t>72), an unconditional late-resolution estimand is

[
F_R(t)-F_R(72)
=P(72<T\le t,\ J=R).
]

For operational remediation among cases already active at 72 hours, a conditional landmark estimand is more directly useful:

[
G_R(t\mid72)
=P(72<T\le t,\ J=R\mid T>72).
]

This answers: among cases known to have breached while still active, what proportion resolve by a later time before withdrawal, rejection, or transfer?

It should not include cases that withdrew, were rejected, or transferred before 72 hours because those cases were no longer internally active at the landmark.

## Directly observed bounds at 72 hours

Under the internal definition:

* Nine cases have an observed `Resolve` at or before 72 hours.
* Eighteen cases are known not to have resolved by 72 hours.
* Five open cases were observed for fewer than 72 hours and have unknown 72-hour outcomes: S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31.

Without making a censoring-model assumption, the empirical proportion not internally resolved by 72 hours is therefore bounded by

[
18/32=56.25%
\quad\text{and}\quad
23/32=71.875%.
]

Similarly:

* Nine cases are known to have remained active past 72 hours: S03, S09, S11, S13, S20, S25, S26, S28, and S32.
* The same five open-before-72 cases have unknown active status at 72 hours.

The empirical active-at-72 proportion is bounded by

[
9/32=28.125%
\quad\text{and}\quad
14/32=43.75%.
]

These are finite-extract bounds, not population confidence intervals.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use the following primary competing-risk codes:

* 0: right-censored/open.
* 1: resolved.
* 2: withdrawn.
* 3: rejected.
* 4: transferred.

Let (\delta_R) denote the resolution-event indicator and (\delta_{\text{any}}) the indicator for any internal terminal disposition.

| Observed status | (\delta_R) | Primary competing-risk code | (\delta_{\text{any}}) for active-backlog KM | Primary censoring rule                             | Sensitivity alternatives                                                                                                                  |
| --------------- | ---------: | --------------------------: | ------------------------------------------: | -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `resolved`      |          1 |                           1 |                                           1 | Event at `duration_or_age_h`; no censoring         | A resolution after 72 remains a resolution event but is an observed SLA breach                                                            |
| `withdrawn`     |          0 |                           2 |                                           1 | Competing event at withdrawal time                 | Combine with rejection as an adverse terminal cause; or censor only for a hypothetical net-resolution hazard                              |
| `rejected`      |          0 |                           3 |                                           1 | Competing event at rejection time                  | Combine with withdrawal; or censor only for a hypothetical world in which rejection is eliminated                                         |
| `transferred`   |          0 |                           4 |    1 under the internal-workload definition | Primary: competing terminal event at transfer time | Treat as SLA failure; treat as informative loss to external follow-up; assume external success by a specified time; use best/worst bounds |
| `open`          |          0 |                           0 |                                           0 | Right-censored at age at cutoff                    | IPCW, pattern-mixture, dependent-censoring bounds, or joint longitudinal/event modeling when more data are available                      |

For a cause-specific resolution hazard model, withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are conventionally assigned a resolution-event indicator of zero and removed from the resolution risk set at their event times. This mathematical treatment should not be confused with assuming that those events are ordinary non-informative censoring. They are observed competing events.

## Plausible treatments of `Transfer`

At least four interpretations are defensible, depending on the business boundary.

**1. Internal-system competing event — primary.**
`Transfer` is an absorbing terminal cause. It contributes to (F_X(t)), counts as no internal resolution, and removes the case from internal workload. This best matches the stated fact that the internal system stops observing the case.

**2. Transfer as an explicit SLA-failure event.**
Collapse transfer into a composite adverse terminal cause. For (P(\text{not resolved by 72})), this produces the same aggregate result as keeping transfer as a separate competing cause, but it loses information needed to distinguish handoffs from withdrawals or rejections.

**3. External-resolution loss to follow-up.**
If the estimand is eventual resolution by either organization, censor the internal record at transfer. This requires a strong assumption that external loss to follow-up is independent of resolution time after conditioning on observed history. That assumption is doubtful here because all three transferred cases—S08, S21, and S32—are P1 and escalated.

**4. External-outcome sensitivity or bounds.**
At one extreme, assume a transferred case resolves immediately at handoff; at the other, assume it remains unresolved through the horizon or indefinitely. Immediate resolution is best interpreted as an optimistic bound, not as an observed fact.

For the 72-hour customer-wide outcome, S08 at 68 hours and S21 at 26 hours have unknown external outcomes by 72. S32 transfers at 96 hours and therefore was already a breach at 72 regardless of its eventual external outcome.

## Open-case censoring

Open cases must remain in the analysis:

* S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open beyond 72 and therefore are known breaches and known active cases at the horizon.
* S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are censored before 72 and have unidentified horizon outcomes.

A common observation cutoff produces administrative censoring in a data-collection sense, but it is not automatically statistically non-informative. Cohort B began later, start dates are unavailable, and the open cases have heterogeneous stages and inactivity gaps. Conditional independence may require adjustment for calendar entry, cohort, priority, workload, stage, and time since the last event.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Analyzing only the 13 resolved cases would classify:

* Nine as timely.
* Four as late: S03, S13, S20, and S28.

The resulting resolved-case breach proportion is (4/13=30.8%). That quantity is

[
P(T_R>72\mid\text{observed resolved by the cutoff}),
]

not (P(\text{not resolved by 72})).

It omits the nine open cases and ten observed non-resolution terminal outcomes. It therefore conditions on both the eventual outcome type and being observed sufficiently long to reach that outcome.

## Selection and survivor bias

The cases most relevant to the right tail are selectively absent from a resolved-only analysis:

* S09 is still open at 92 hours.
* S11 is still open at 81 hours.
* S25 is still open at 79 hours.
* S26 is still open at 104 hours.

All four are already-observed breaches. Excluding them necessarily makes the tail appear shorter.

The five younger open cases also cannot simply be discarded:

* S24 is open at 67 hours, only five hours from the SLA boundary.
* S10 is open at 47 hours.
* S29 is open at 51 hours.
* S15 is open at 35 hours.
* S31 is open at 23 hours.

Their eventual 72-hour statuses are unknown. Treating them as compliant because they are currently below 72 would be wrong; treating them as breaches would also be an assumption.

## Competing risks are outcomes, not missing data

S06 withdrew at 27 hours and S05 was rejected at 9 hours. Neither is merely an incomplete observation of a future internal resolution. Both reached observed absorbing outcomes that preclude internal resolution.

Ordinary Kaplan–Meier estimation of resolution that censors such cases would redistribute their probability mass over cases still at risk. It would estimate a hypothetical “net” resolution distribution in a world where competing events disappear, generally overstating the actual cumulative incidence of resolution.

Transfer is even more consequential. S08, S21, and S32 are all P1 and escalated. Treating them as independent censoring would selectively remove difficult or urgent cases and could make the remaining resolution experience look artificially favorable.

## Open censoring may be informative

The long inactivity gaps among open breaches are operationally suggestive:

* S09: age 92, gap 31.
* S11: age 81, gap 55.
* S25: age 79, gap 48.
* S26: age 104, gap 72.

These cases may have lower subsequent resolution hazards than recently active cases. A standard independent-censoring estimator would implicitly let later-observed cases represent them after censoring.

The gap variable cannot be naively used across all rows, however. Every terminal row has gap zero because its terminal event is the last event. Comparing zero-gap terminal rows with positive-gap open rows would directly leak outcome status.

## Cohort imbalance and incomplete rollout

The cohorts are not randomized:

* Cohort A contains 3 P1, 7 P2, and 5 P3 cases.
* Cohort B contains 5 P1, 7 P2, and 5 P3 cases.
* `AutoCheck` appears in 2 of 15 Cohort A prefixes and 13 of 17 Cohort B prefixes.

Thus cohort, calendar period, priority mix, and actual automation exposure are intertwined. The overlap—some A cases use `AutoCheck`, while some B cases do not—also shows that cohort is not identical to treatment received.

Among resolved cases alone, the late-resolution proportions are:

* Cohort A: 2 of 6, or 33.3%.
* Cohort B: 2 of 7, or 28.6%.

That suggests a small B advantage. Under the primary competing-risk estimator, however, estimated non-resolution by 72 is approximately:

* A: 70.8%.
* B: 68.4%.

The difference is only about 2.4 percentage points and is highly uncertain. Moreover, estimated active backlog is approximately:

* A: 33.9%.
* B: 41.6%.

Therefore B can look slightly better for timely resolution while looking worse for active workload.

Priority-specific point estimates are also heterogeneous:

* P1: A 66.7% versus B 80.0% non-resolution.
* P2: A 61.9% versus B 51.4%.
* P3: both 80.0%.

These cells contain only 3–7 cases each and should not be treated as stable effects, but they demonstrate why an unadjusted cohort comparison is not causal.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method                                | Appropriate role in this dataset                                                                                                       | Main limitation here                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Kaplan–Meier                          | Estimate time to **any internal terminal disposition**, with open cases censored; its survivor curve is the active-backlog probability | Not appropriate for actual resolution probability if withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are simply censored                         |
| Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence | Primary method for resolution, withdrawal, rejection, transfer, and active-state decomposition                                         | Depends on independent right censoring; estimates become unstable when few cases remain                                              |
| Cause-specific hazards                | Study instantaneous transition mechanisms among cases still active; potentially useful for stage hazards                               | Hazard coefficients do not directly equal cumulative SLA risk; only 13 resolutions and very few competing events                     |
| Subdistribution hazards               | Model covariate associations with the resolution CIF directly                                                                          | Standard Fine–Gray models are difficult to interpret operationally, fragile at (n=32), and awkward with time-varying process history |
| Restricted mean time                  | Quantify mean hours spent internally active through 72 hours                                                                           | Must define the state being integrated; “time without resolution” is not the same as active workload after a terminal failure        |
| Landmarking                           | Dynamic prediction at 24, 48, 60, or 72 hours using history observed by the landmark                                                   | Exact activity timestamps and historical snapshots are missing                                                                       |
| Multi-state model                     | Estimate stage-to-stage and stage-to-terminal hazards and state occupancy                                                              | Prefix order is available, but transition times and dwell times are not                                                              |

## Primary analysis

Use a nonparametric Aalen–Johansen estimator from `Submit`, with:

* Cause 1: `Resolve`.
* Cause 2: `Withdraw`.
* Cause 3: `Reject`.
* Cause 4: `Transfer`.
* Open rows right-censored at their observed ages.

Report at minimum:

[
\widehat F_R(72),\quad
\widehat F_W(72),\quad
\widehat F_K(72),\quad
\widehat F_X(72),\quad
\widehat S(72).
]

Then report the principal SLA measure as

[
1-\widehat F_R(72).
]

For these data, the approximate primary decomposition is:

| Quantity           | Estimate |
| ------------------ | -------: |
| Resolution by 72   |    30.4% |
| Withdrawal by 72   |    14.0% |
| Rejection by 72    |    10.1% |
| Transfer by 72     |     7.5% |
| Still active at 72 |    38.0% |
| Not resolved by 72 |    69.6% |

Every curve should be accompanied by numbers at risk. Only nine cases remain in the observed risk set immediately before 72 hours, so precision is limited.

Confidence intervals could be obtained with Aalen–Johansen variance estimates or case-level bootstrap resampling, but with 32 cases, emphasis should remain on effect sizes, bounds, and sensitivity rather than significance tests.

## Secondary analysis 1: active-backlog KM and restricted mean active time

Treat any internal terminal disposition as the event and open cases as censored. Kaplan–Meier then estimates (S_{\text{active}}(t)).

The resulting estimate at 72 hours is approximately 38.0%, matching the active-state component of the Aalen–Johansen decomposition.

The restricted mean active time through 72 hours is

[
\operatorname{RMST}*{\text{active}}(72)
=\int_0^{72} S*{\text{active}}(t),dt,
]

estimated here at approximately 50.4 active case-hours per submitted case through the SLA horizon.

Unadjusted cohort estimates are approximately:

* A: 49.6 active case-hours.
* B: 51.1 active case-hours.

That measure is directly relevant to staffing and queue occupancy and reinforces that a cohort can have a slightly higher resolution incidence while also carrying more active work.

## Secondary analysis 2: 72-hour breach-recovery landmark

The known active set at 72 contains nine cases:

* Four subsequent resolutions: S20 at 74, S03 at 76, S28 at 88, and S13 at 110.
* One competing transfer: S32 at 96.
* Four open censors: S25 at 79, S11 at 81, S09 at 92, and S26 at 104.

The crude late-resolution fraction is (4/9=44.4%). A landmark Aalen–Johansen estimate evaluated at the final observed time reaches approximately 79.3%, because censored cases are assumed to have the same subsequent transition distribution as cases remaining under observation. At 110 hours, only S13 remains in the risk set and resolves.

This divergence is a warning, not evidence that 79.3% is a reliable recovery rate. Tail curves should be truncated or visually de-emphasized when the risk set becomes very small. In this dataset, the conditional late-resolution CIF is approximately 37.8% at 88 hours, where the risk set just before the event is five.

## Regression models

A cause-specific Cox model could eventually model:

* Resolution hazard.
* Adverse terminal-disposition hazard.
* Transfer hazard.

With only 13 resolutions, 4 withdrawals, 3 rejections, and 3 transfers, separate multivariable models are not credible. At most, baseline priority and cohort could be explored descriptively or through a strongly regularized or hierarchical model.

A Fine–Gray model might be considered when the direct goal is standardized prediction of (F_R(72)), but subdistribution hazard ratios should not be interpreted as effects on processing speed. For these data, nonparametric stratification and direct standardized risks are clearer.

## Multi-state extension

Once exact event timestamps are available, a semi-Markov multi-state model should be the principal process-aware secondary model. Transition hazards can depend on:

* Total elapsed time since `Submit`.
* Time spent in the current stage.
* Priority.
* Cohort or policy eligibility.
* Previous review and rework counts.
* Whether automation or escalation has occurred.

State-occupancy probabilities at 72 hours can then be decomposed into review backlog, escalated backlog, rework backlog, and terminal outcomes.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The frozen prefixes support control-flow features, but most are not baseline variables.

| Feature                        | Construction from supplied data                                       | Timing classification              | Valid use and risk                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Priority                       | P1/P2/P3                                                              | Baseline                           | Safe baseline stratifier, subject to how priority itself was assigned                     |
| Cohort                         | A/B                                                                   | Baseline calendar-policy proxy     | Safe as a recorded baseline attribute but not a randomized treatment                      |
| `AutoCheck` executed           | Presence in prefix                                                    | Time-varying, post-policy          | Use only after it has occurred; actual execution is not equivalent to assignment          |
| AutoCheck order                | `AutoCheck` before `Screen`, after `Screen`, absent, or screen bypass | Time-varying path feature          | Useful for process monitoring; potentially a policy mediator                              |
| Current stage                  | Last nonterminal activity observed so far                             | Time-varying                       | Safe for a real-time open-case model; terminal suffixes must be excluded                  |
| Review count                   | Number of `Review` events observed so far                             | Time-varying                       | Repeated review may represent cycling or additional scrutiny                              |
| Rework count                   | Number of observed `Rework` events                                    | Time-varying and post-treatment    | Potential mediator of policy and complexity; future rework is leakage                     |
| Escalated by time (t)          | Indicator switching from 0 to 1 at `Escalate`                         | Time-varying and post-treatment    | Must not be coded from time zero as “ever escalated”                                      |
| Post-escalation review         | A `Review` observed after `Escalate`                                  | Time-varying path/backflow feature | Indicates return from escalation to review, but only after the transition                 |
| Inactivity gap                 | Current time minus last event time                                    | Time-varying                       | Useful among currently open cases; the supplied endpoint value leaks terminal/open status |
| Elapsed age and time remaining | Current time since `Submit`; (72-t)                                   | Time-varying clock                 | Essential for dynamic risk; not a baseline case attribute                                 |
| Complete frozen prefix         | Entire sequence through terminal event or cutoff                      | Future-containing summary          | Invalid as a baseline predictor and directly outcome-leaking for terminal rows            |

## Concrete control-flow observations

`AutoCheck` appears in 2 of 15 Cohort A cases and 13 of 17 Cohort B cases. Ten B cases show `AutoCheck` before `Screen`; other cases use different orders or omit it. This supports an order-sensitive feature rather than a simple cohort indicator.

Repeated `Review` appears in S03, S07, and S13, all in Cohort A. That is an observation about this small extract, not evidence that Cohort B eliminates review cycling.

For currently open cases, stage and inactivity can distinguish operational situations:

* S09 and S26 are in `Review` with gaps of 31 and 72 hours.
* S11 and S25 are in `Escalate` with gaps of 55 and 48 hours.
* S24 is in `Review` at age 67 with a gap of only 4 hours.
* S29 is back in `Review` after escalation, with a 6-hour gap.
* S31 is at `Screen` at age 23 with a 3-hour gap.

S24 is close to the SLA despite recent activity; S11 and S25 have already breached and appear inactive. A dynamic model should represent both age and stage dwell rather than treating all open cases alike.

## Immortal-time bias from `Escalate`

Coding the frozen `escalated=yes` field as if known at `Submit` is invalid. A case classified as “ever escalated” must remain active until the escalation event occurs. The pre-escalation period is then incorrectly assigned to the escalated group.

It also uses future information for predictions made before escalation. The correct alternatives are:

* A counting-process covariate that switches from 0 to 1 at the exact escalation time.
* A landmark design using “escalated by landmark (L)” among cases still active at (L).
* A multi-state transition into an `Escalated` state.

The same issue applies to actual `AutoCheck`, rework, repeated review, and full path variants.

For causal policy analysis, escalation and rework may be mediators or colliders affected by case severity and the new policy. Adjusting for them can block part of a policy effect or introduce selection bias. They are more naturally used for prediction or mechanism analysis than as ordinary confounders.

## Proposed state-transition representation

> `Submit` → `{Screen, AutoCheck}` in either observed order; preprocessing → `Review`, `Escalate`, or a terminal state; `Review` → `Rework`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, or `Reject`; `Rework` → `Review` or `Resolve`; `Escalate` → `Review`, `Resolve`, or `Transfer`; `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are absorbing.

For a Markov or semi-Markov implementation, “latest activity” alone may be insufficient. The state should include flags such as `screen_completed`, `autocheck_completed`, escalation history, and review count. Otherwise two cases both currently in `Review` but with different prior routes are incorrectly treated as identical.

Potential transition hazards include:

[
\lambda_{\text{Review}\rightarrow\text{Resolve}}
(t,\text{review dwell},\text{priority},\text{history}),
]

[
\lambda_{\text{Review}\rightarrow\text{Rework}},
\quad
\lambda_{\text{Review}\rightarrow\text{Escalate}},
\quad
\lambda_{\text{Escalate}\rightarrow\text{Transfer}}.
]

The supplied prefixes identify the transition topology but not the transition times. Therefore they support variant and path-feature analysis, not valid elapsed-time stage-hazard estimation.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

## Sensitivity 1: transfer treatment

| Transfer treatment                                                         | Estimated resolution CIF at 72 | Estimated no-resolution probability | Interpretation                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -----------------------------: | ----------------------------------: | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Separate internal competing event                                          |                          30.4% |                               69.6% | Primary internal SLA estimand                                                  |
| Informative loss to external follow-up, provisionally treated as censoring |                          31.3% |                               68.7% | Requires an implausibly strong independent-censoring assumption unless modeled |
| Transfer collapsed into an SLA-failure cause                               |                          30.4% |                               69.6% | Same aggregate tail risk as primary, less cause-specific detail                |
| Optimistic resolution at transfer time                                     |                          37.9% |                               62.1% | Best-case sensitivity, not an observed fact                                    |

The relatively modest change from 69.6% to 68.7% when transfers are censored should not be read as evidence that transfer handling is unimportant. All transferred cases are P1 and escalated, so censoring them may selectively remove high-risk cases. The estimate is model-dependent.

For a customer-wide outcome, the external statuses of S08 and S21 by 72 are unknown. Allowing both pre-72 transfers and all five open-before-72 cases to vary between timely resolution and no timely resolution gives a broad empirical bound of approximately 50.0% to 71.9% for customer-wide non-resolution.

## Sensitivity 2: withdrawal and rejection

The primary analysis treats withdrawal and rejection as competing outcomes. If they are censored along with transfers, a Kaplan–Meier-style net-resolution analysis gives an estimated no-resolution probability of approximately 63.9%.

That is not an estimate of actual operational failure. It describes a hypothetical world in which withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are removed without otherwise changing resolution hazards.

A useful reporting strategy is therefore to show both:

* Resolution SLA risk: (1-F_R(72)).
* Timely-disposition backlog: (S(72)).

This prevents a high rejection or withdrawal rate from making the process look efficient simply because cases leave the queue.

P3 illustrates the issue. Its estimated non-resolution probability is approximately 80%, but its active probability is only about 30%, largely because more cases terminate through rejection or withdrawal. Whether that is “good” depends on whether the decision concerns resolution success or queue clearance.

## Sensitivity 3: informative open-case censoring

For the internal 72-hour endpoint, the direct observed-data bound is 56.25%–71.875%. The primary Aalen–Johansen estimate of 69.6% lies close to the pessimistic end because of when censoring occurs relative to observed events.

Recommended informative-censoring analyses are:

* Inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting using baseline priority, cohort, exact submission date, workload, and process history available before censoring.
* Pattern-mixture or tipping-point analysis assigning different timely-resolution probabilities to the five open-before-72 cases.
* For late recovery, sensitivity parameters that reduce the post-censor resolution hazard for cases with long inactivity gaps.
* A joint model of stage activity and terminal outcome when repeated event-history snapshots become available.

The late-recovery estimate is especially sensitive. Its final-time Aalen–Johansen value of approximately 79.3% contrasts with the crude 44.4% because four open cases are censored and the final remaining case resolves. If long-gap open cases are less likely to recover than observed cases, the true recovery incidence could be much lower.

## Sensitivity 4: cohort-policy interpretation

The crude primary estimates are:

* A: 70.8% not resolved; 33.9% active at 72.
* B: 68.4% not resolved; 41.6% active at 72.

Direct standardization to the overall priority distribution gives approximately:

* A: 68.8% not resolved.
* B: 67.5% not resolved.

The estimated resolution advantage of B therefore shrinks from about 2.4 to about 1.3 percentage points after this simple adjustment. Standardized active probabilities are approximately:

* A: 35.4%.
* B: 42.5%.

Several qualitative conclusions can reverse:

* B looks slightly better for timely resolution but worse for active workload.
* Within P1, B looks worse; within P2, B looks better; P3 is tied.
* Different outcomes for only one or two censored or transferred cases can reverse the small aggregate cohort difference.
* An “ever escalated” baseline analysis may make escalation appear harmful or protective depending on immortal-time and severity selection.
* An as-treated comparison based on actual `AutoCheck` can disagree with a cohort comparison because actual automation is post-start and rollout compliance is incomplete.

No cohort contrast should be described as a causal policy effect without the assignment rule, eligibility, rollout timing, compliance, and sufficient baseline confounders.

# 7. Operational interpretation

## Dynamic alerts

For an open case at time (t<72), the relevant prediction is

[
P(T_R>72\mid T>t,\ \mathcal H(t)),
]

where (\mathcal H(t)) contains only process information observed by time (t).

The alert should also decompose risk into:

* Remaining active past 72.
* Withdrawal.
* Rejection.
* Transfer.
* Timely resolution.

Different risks require different interventions. A high active-backlog probability may justify staffing or queue reassignment; a high transfer probability may require handoff coordination; a high rejection probability may require evidence completion or customer communication.

For a case already open beyond 72 hours, the probability of breach is 100%. S09, S11, S25, and S26 should not receive a probabilistic “breach warning.” They need a recovery-oriented alert based on the probability of resolution within an additional operational window.

The alert policy should combine predicted risk with:

* Time remaining.
* Priority.
* Current stage.
* Inactivity gap.
* Expected benefit and capacity of the intervention.

For example, S24 is at 67 hours and therefore urgent despite a recent event four hours ago. S31 at 23 hours has a similar recent-event pattern but substantially more time remaining. S11 and S25 are already-breached P1 cases with long gaps and are natural remediation candidates.

## Workload actions

Use predicted state occupancy rather than only a binary SLA score:

[
E[\text{active cases at future time }u]
=\sum_i P_i(T>u\mid\mathcal H_i).
]

Stage-specific occupancy can determine whether additional capacity is needed in review, escalation, or rework.

Restricted mean active time provides expected active case-hours per submission. Multiplying it by submission volume gives a workload quantity, subject to stability of the arrival and processing environment.

## SLA governance

A governance dashboard should report together:

* Timely resolution CIF.
* Still-active probability.
* Withdrawal CIF.
* Rejection CIF.
* Transfer CIF.
* Numbers at risk.
* Raw identified bounds.
* Transfer assumption.
* Cohort and priority-standardized results.

Reporting only “closed by 72 hours” would classify withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as successful queue clearance even though the scenario defines operational success as `Resolve`.

## Calibration

A deployed dynamic model should be evaluated at fixed landmarks using predictions created before outcomes occur. Checks should include:

* Calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope.
* Calibration curves for resolution and each competing cause.
* Censoring-adjusted Brier scores at relevant horizons.
* Temporal validation using later submission periods.
* Separate validation before and after policy changes.
* No sharing of later snapshots of the same case across training and validation.

Because cohort B follows a policy change, a random row split could hide calendar drift and rollout effects.

## Fairness and service consistency

At minimum, calibration and alert behavior should be compared by priority and cohort:

* Predicted versus observed cumulative incidence.
* False-negative alert rates among known breaches.
* Alert burden per submitted and per active case.
* Transfer, escalation, and intervention rates.
* Resolution and active-backlog outcomes after comparable elapsed time.

P1 is operationally prioritized, so a single common threshold may not be appropriate. A lower action threshold for P1 may be justified, but the model should still be calibrated within P1.

No demographic or protected-characteristic fields are supplied, so broader fairness assessment is not possible from this extract. Additional attributes should be collected only with a legitimate governance purpose and appropriate privacy controls.

Risk scores must not be presented as causal effects. An association between escalation and late resolution may reflect case difficulty and the fact that escalation occurs after start. It does not establish that escalation caused the delay or that removing escalation would improve the outcome.

def encode_transfer_scenario(frame, scenario):
    """Return a cause label; None denotes right censoring."""

    def cause(status):

def aalen_johansen(frame, horizon, scenario="internal_competing"):
    """
    Nonparametric Aalen-Johansen estimator.

    Events are processed before censors when an event and censor share a time.
    lifelines.AalenJohansenFitter or scikit-survival may be used in production;
    this function is a self-contained fallback.
    """
    x = encode_transfer_scenario(frame, scenario)
    causes = sorted(x["cause"].dropna().unique())

    survival = 1.0
    cif = {cause: 0.0 for cause in causes}
    event_rows = []

    for t in sorted(x.loc[x["time"] <= horizon, "time"].unique()):
        at_risk = int((x["time"] >= t).sum())
        at_t = x[x["time"] == t]

        d_by_cause = (
            at_t.loc[at_t["event"] == 1]
            .groupby("cause")
            .size()
            .to_dict()
        )
        d_all = int(sum(d_by_cause.values()))
        censored = int((at_t["event"] == 0).sum())
        survival_before = survival

        for cause, d in d_by_cause.items():
            cif[cause] += survival_before * d / at_risk

        if d_all:
            survival *= 1.0 - d_all / at_risk

        event_rows.append(
            {
                "time": t,
                "at_risk": at_risk,
                "events": d_all,
                "censored": censored,
                "survival": survival,
            }
        )

    return survival, cif, pd.DataFrame(event_rows)


def km_active_and_rmst(frame, horizon):
    """
    KM for time to any internal terminal disposition and restricted mean
    active time through the horizon.
    """
    x = frame[["time", "observed_status"]].copy()
    x["event"] = (x["observed_status"] != "open").astype(int)

    survival = 1.0
    area = 0.0
    previous = 0.0

    for t in sorted(x.loc[x["time"] <= horizon, "time"].unique()):
        area += survival * (t - previous)

        at_risk = int((x["time"] >= t).sum())
        d = int(
            ((x["time"] == t) & (x["event"] == 1)).sum()
        )

        if d:
            survival *= 1.0 - d / at_risk

        previous = t

    area += survival * (horizon - previous)
    return survival, area


def identified_bounds(series):
    known_failures = int((series == 1).sum())
    unknown = int(series.isna().sum())
    n = len(series)

    lower = known_failures / n
    upper = (known_failures + unknown) / n
    return lower, upper, unknown

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The following additions are ordered by their importance to valid survival and process analysis.

1. **Exact timestamps for every activity.**
   Terminal duration plus activity order is insufficient for escalation timing, stage dwell, rework-cycle duration, dynamic landmarking, or time-varying covariates. Each event needs a timestamp tied to the same clock used by the elapsed-time SLA.

2. **External outcomes after transfer.**
   Record whether and when the external organization resolves, rejects, withdraws, retransfers, or loses the case. Also record whether the SLA remains owned by the internal organization after transfer.

3. **Exact submission dates and the exact observation-cutoff timestamp.**
   These are needed to identify calendar-entry effects, common administrative censoring, weekday or holiday effects, and whether Cohort B systematically has shorter potential follow-up.

4. **Time-varying workload and capacity.**
   Queue length, reviewer caseload, staffing levels, shift schedules, escalation-team capacity, and system outages are likely determinants of both stage duration and censoring patterns.

5. **Policy assignment and rollout compliance.**
   Required fields include automation eligibility, intended treatment, assignment rule, actual activation time, policy version, exception reason, manual override, and compliance. Cohort alone is an inadequate treatment indicator.

6. **Baseline case complexity and assignment mechanism.**
   Case type, evidence completeness, customer or request category, complexity indicators, source channel, reviewer assignment, and the rule generating P1/P2/P3 are needed to reduce confounding.

7. **Reasons for withdrawal, rejection, escalation, rework, and transfer.**
   These outcomes may represent distinct mechanisms. Pooling them can conceal whether delays arise from case quality, capacity, policy, or external dependencies.

8. **Repeated snapshots or complete event histories for open cases.**
   A single endpoint inactivity gap cannot show whether a case was continuously stalled, repeatedly reactivated, or recently entered its current state. Historical snapshots also enable honest landmark training sets.

9. **More cases and longer external follow-up.**
   With 32 cases, cause-specific regression, cohort-by-priority interactions, calibration, and fairness estimates are unstable. More observations are especially necessary for transfer, rejection, and withdrawal, which have only three, three, and four events respectively.

10. **Outcome-definition governance.**
    The organization must explicitly decide whether SLA reporting concerns internal resolution, customer-wide resolution, timely terminal disposition, or internal active backlog. These are distinct estimands and can lead to different—and even opposite—policy conclusions.
