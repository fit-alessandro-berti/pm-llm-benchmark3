## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is not “same as baseline.” In this case, resilience means preserving the intent of a control — authorization, custody, temperature assurance, release, proof of delivery — through a visible manual alternative. A resilient workaround may move the process off the system, but it still creates evidence at the moment the action happens.

Concealed noncompliance happens when throughput is preserved by dropping a control and then reconstructing the record after the fact. The output can look compliant, but the control did not occur in real time.

Examples from the data:

- **True resilience:** Manual authorization check by trained staff is resilient because it replaces the system authorization with an offline equivalent. Temporary release control for controlled substances is resilient because it adds a manual control rather than removing one. Hospital-critical fast lane is resilient if it is limited to pre-validated customers and product-risk checks are retained.

- **Risky shortcuts:** Skip system authorization check as implemented is a risky shortcut because it replaces verification with “assumed standing authorization” and leaves no evidence. Dispatch without scan is a risky shortcut because it removes the custody handoff event. Audit remediation is risky if presented as normal compliance, because it does not recreate the moment of release.

- **Ambiguous adaptations:** Manual inventory snapshot is resilient early in T2 but becomes risky once stale. Manual pick lists are acceptable for ambient products if lot/expiry fields are captured, but they are not acceptable for cold-chain or controlled substances without additional manual checks. Phone POD is acceptable for standard ambient deliveries with caller verification, but not for controlled substances. Backlog prioritization is fair only if rules are explicit and monitored.

The distinction is visible in the DFG and conformance findings. Baseline fitness was 0.96 with 91% fitting traces. Manual workaround fitness fell to 0.71 with 44% fitting traces. Some of that loss reflects necessary triage and manual control. But the move-on-log deviations — Skip system authorization check, Dispatch without scan, Phone proof of delivery, Spreadsheet invoice hold — are not just process deviations; they are points where compliance evidence is not being created in real time.

There is also hidden workload after operational recovery. T4 looks stabilized: cycle times are closer to baseline and DFG fitness is 0.90. But the DFG still shows 1,200 proof-of-delivery remediation cases and 2,300 invoice-close-to-audit-remediation cases. If management only sees operational cycle time, it misses this compliance tail. That hidden workload is not resilience; it is deferred risk.

## 4. Recovery process model

For future disruptions, use a three-mode model:

- **Green mode:** normal WMS, scanning, and control workflow.
- **Yellow mode:** system degraded but partially available; use manual evidence for scanning, POD, and unreliable inventory sync.
- **Red mode:** order intake or WMS unavailable; use the full manual recovery process below.

### Trigger conditions

Enter degraded mode when any of these occurs:

- WMS unavailable for more than 15 minutes.
- Scanning unavailable for more than 30 minutes.
- Inventory synchronization lag exceeds 2 hours.
- Traceability exception rate exceeds 2% for two consecutive hours.
- Manual workaround has been active for more than 4 hours without a defined control card.

Exit only when:

- Systems are restored, reconciliation of manual records is complete, and the audit queue is under control.
- Exiting because “the system looks normal” without reconciliation repeats the T4 hidden-workload problem.

### Triage rules

Classify every order into a customer segment and product-risk class.

- Customer segments: Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, Wholesale-bulk.
- Product risk classes: Controlled-substance, Cold-chain, Recall-sensitive, Ambient.

Triage policy:

- Hospital-critical and high-risk product orders are always top priority.
- Controlled-substance, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive orders must complete manual authorization and manual release checks before dispatch.
- Pharmacy-standard ambient orders can proceed if they are on a pre-approved offline authorization list; otherwise they require manual authorization.
- Wholesale-bulk ambient orders may be deferred, but only with customer notification and a maximum deferral age.

| Risk class | Authorization | Pick / pack / release | Dispatch / POD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ambient hospital-critical | Offline authorization list; manual authorization if unknown | Paper pick ticket; manual release sign-off | Printed courier manifest; phone/photo POD with caller verification |
| Ambient standard / wholesale | Pre-approved offline list; manual authorization for new customers | Paper pick ticket; manual release sign-off | Printed courier manifest; phone/photo POD with caller verification |
| Cold-chain / recall-sensitive | Manual authorization plus recall eligibility check | Record lot/expiry/temperature logger ID; release checklist with temperature review | Signed custody manifest; temperature download; signed POD |
| Controlled-substance | Manual authorization and authorized recipient check | Dual-control signatures; custody count; release checklist | Signed custody manifest; signed POD with recipient identity; no phone-only POD |

### Manual evidence capture

Use one structured form per order, not a batch.

The form must include:

- Order ID.
- Event type.
- Timestamp.
- Actor name.
- Customer and delivery address.
- Product, lot, and expiry.
- Cold-chain temperature log ID if applicable.
- Controlled-substance custody signatures if applicable.
- Courier name and handoff time.
- Recipient and POD verification.

If barcodes can be printed before the outage, print them. If not, use a defined order-ID range issued by the incident coordinator.

All manual forms should be scanned or entered into a “degraded-mode evidence” queue within 24 hours. If evidence is still missing after 7 days, escalate it.

### Backlog prioritization

Use a simple score:

- Customer criticality weight: 40%.
- Product risk weight: 30%.
- Order age weight: 20%.
- Promised SLA weight: 10%.

Process high scores first, but monitor fairness. If pharmacy-standard fill rate falls below a pre-agreed target, add capacity or stop deferring that segment. Do not let deferrals silently become cancellations.

### Reconciliation and audit remediation

When systems return:

1. Freeze SKUs where manual counts and WMS counts differ beyond a threshold.
2. Reconcile manual reservation logs against WMS before processing new orders.
3. Classify each case into one of three categories:
   - **Complete:** all controls and evidence were performed in real time.
   - **Reconstructed:** evidence was recovered from paper, calls, or logs, but the control was not in the system.
   - **Residual risk:** critical evidence is missing and cannot be recreated.

Reconstructed and residual-risk cases must remain separate from normal closed orders. Audit remediation can close a file administratively, but it should not be counted as real-time compliance.

## 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Build a pre-approved offline authorization matrix for known customers and products, with an expiry date and quarterly refresh. | Medium | Eliminates the need to “skip” authorization during an outage; makes manual authorization faster and evidence-based. | List can become stale if not maintained. |
| P1 | Institute a snapshot/reservation protocol: use a read-only inventory snapshot with an as-of time, refresh every 2–4 hours, and use a shared reservation sheet. | Medium | Reduces overselling and the 1,400-case reconciliation tail seen in T4. | Snapshot still lags reality; manual reservation discipline required. |
| P1 | Require 100% manual release checklists for controlled-substance, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive orders before dispatch. | Low | Protects the highest-risk products; controlled-substance dual-control gap should stay near baseline instead of rising to 2.9%. | Emergency override usage could erode the rule unless overrides are tracked and reviewed. |
| P2 | Implement a scanless-dispatch protocol: printed manifest, custody signature, and scan within one hour after departure. For controlled/cold-chain, no dispatch without signed handoff. | Low | Rebuilds chain of custody; directly addresses the largest T2 traceability gap. | Paper manifests can be lost; timely scanning discipline is needed. |
| P2 | Formalize backlog prioritization with a published score and automatic customer notification. Cap deferral duration and auto-escalate aging orders. | Medium | Prevents pharmacy fill from falling to 61%; reduces cancellations and unfairness. | Scoring rules can be gamed or mis-specified; monitor fairness spread. |

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept temporary degradation in cycle time and some deferral of pharmacy-standard or wholesale-bulk orders to protect hospital-critical supply and high-risk product integrity, but it should not accept controls disappearing silently. The T2 data show that hospital fill can be held at 91% while traceability exceptions rise to 9.5% and rework to 12.5%; a moderate increase in those metrics is an acceptable incident cost if it is visible and bounded. What is not acceptable is letting an audit-remediation queue grow after operations look recovered, or treating reconstructed records as if they were real-time controls. The decisive evidence is whether every controlled-substance, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, and hospital-critical order has a manual control artifact at the moment of dispatch. If future simulations show that the degraded-mode process maintains high-risk control completeness and keeps the audit queue small and aging quickly, a narrowly scoped fast lane can be retained. If not, management should choose conservative recovery at the cost of throughput.
