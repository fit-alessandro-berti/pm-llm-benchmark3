# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness should not be reduced to equal approval rates. In this process, fairness should be assessed across several complementary dimensions.

## 1.1 Outcome parity

Raw approval rates differ substantially:

- Group A: 78%
- Group B: 63%
- Group C: 69%
- Group D: 58%

However, raw outcome parity is not sufficient evidence of unfairness because the groups differ in legitimate case-mix characteristics: risk band, documentation completeness, channel, language needs, accommodation needs, prior case count, and likely routing requirements.

Raw outcome differences therefore establish only that there is a disparity requiring investigation, not that there is discrimination or procedural unfairness.

## 1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity

The preliminary adjusted comparison reduces, but does not eliminate, disparities:

- Group B: approval probability 5.5 percentage points, cycle time +2.6 days.
- Group C: approval probability 2.2 percentage points, cycle time +4.1 days.
- Group D: approval probability 3.1 percentage points, cycle time +0.9 days.

These adjusted gaps are smaller than the raw gaps, suggesting that part of the raw disparity is associated with documented case complexity. But residual adjusted differences still matter, especially where they coincide with additional procedural burden, longer waits, or higher appeal rates.

At the same time, the adjusted model should not be treated as definitive. Some covariates, especially `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`, may themselves reflect historical disadvantage, language barriers, disability access issues, or prior unequal treatment.

## 1.3 Procedural burden

Procedural burden concerns whether some applicants are required to go through more steps, more repeated requests, more reviews, or more handovers than similarly situated applicants.

Relevant signals include:

- Group B has an adjusted +9.5 percentage-point higher probability of `Request Missing Documents`.
- Group B has an adjusted +8.0 percentage-point higher probability of `Supervisor Review`.
- Group C has an adjusted +5.5 percentage-point higher probability of `Supervisor Review`.
- Group B and Group C have higher raw reopen/rework rates: 22% and 24%, compared with 11% for Group A.
- In some regions, Group B and Group C cases experience more handovers between generalists, bilingual staff, accommodation specialists, and supervisors.

Some additional burden may be legitimate, for example where documents are genuinely missing or where accommodation review is legally required. But if the burden arises from inaccessible forms, poor translation, insufficient bilingual staffing, or unnecessary handovers, it becomes a procedural fairness problem.

## 1.4 Timeliness

Timeliness is a core procedural fairness value. Even if the final decision is correct, excessive delay can undermine access to benefits.

The raw cycle-time differences are large:

- Group A median: 8.1 days.
- Group B median: 13.7 days.
- Group C median: 15.9 days.
- Group D median: 12.1 days.

The adjusted differences remain meaningful:

- Group B: +2.6 days.
- Group C: +4.1 days.
- Group D: +0.9 days.

The waiting-time table shows that the largest group-specific waits are concentrated in rights-preserving support steps:

- `Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment`: 3.8 days for Group B.
- `Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment`: 4.7 days for Group C.

These delays are especially concerning because the steps exist to preserve access, not to penalize applicants.

## 1.5 Consistency of routing

Routing should be consistent with policy and with observable case needs.

Legitimate routing appears to include:

- Higher `risk_score_band` leading to `Fraud Review`.
- Disability accommodation requests leading to `Accommodation Review`.
- Minority-language preference leading to `Language Support Contact`.

Potential fairness concerns arise if:

- Applicants with similar documented needs are routed differently by group.
- Group B or Group C cases receive extra supervisor review beyond what risk, documentation, and case complexity explain.
- Group D’s higher fraud routing is driven partly by biased risk scoring rather than actual risk.
- Handovers occur because of resource shortages rather than applicant need.

The adjusted higher probability of supervisor review for Groups B and C deserves scrutiny because it may reflect extra scrutiny not fully explained by documented complexity.

## 1.6 Quality and accessibility of support

Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving steps. They should improve access, not create hidden penalties.

The evidence suggests that formal access exists, but practical accessibility is weaker:

- Only 7 bilingual caseworkers handle a disproportionate share of Group B cases.
- Only 5 accommodation specialists handle a disproportionate share of Group C cases.
- Two accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review, increasing bottleneck risk.
- Group B cases wait 3.8 days after language support before eligibility assessment.
- Group C cases wait 4.7 days after accommodation review before eligibility assessment.
- Service-level targets do not include separate timeliness thresholds for these support steps.

This creates a pattern where support is nominally available but operationally slow.

## 1.7 Appeal and rework burden

Appeals and rework can indicate unclear decisions, poor communication, mistrust, or errors.

Relevant evidence:

- Group B raw appeal rate: 13%, adjusted +4.4 percentage points.
- Group D raw appeal rate: 16%, adjusted +5.7 percentage points.
- Group C raw appeal rate: 10%, adjusted +1.1 percentage points.
- Group B and Group C have high raw reopen/rework rates.
- Variant V6, the appeal path, is most common for Group D at 13%, compared with 4% for Group A.

Appeals are not automatically evidence of unfairness; they may reflect applicant engagement or legitimate disagreement. But higher adjusted appeal rates, especially for Group D, raise questions about decision quality, explanation quality, trust, or the perceived legitimacy of adverse decisions.

## 1.8 Resource-allocation fairness

Fairness also depends on whether applicants have access to adequately resourced decision-making capacity.

The current resource structure appears uneven:

- Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers.
- Group B cases are concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers.
- Group C cases are concentrated on 5 accommodation specialists.
- Group D cases are concentrated in fraud-review queues.
- Some regions have repeated handovers for Group B and Group C cases.

This suggests that part of the disparity may be structural rather than individual bias: the process assigns scarce specialized resources to legally required support functions but does not provide enough capacity to prevent delays.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to distinguish:

1. **Disparity**: a measurable difference between groups.
2. **Legitimate routing**: differences explained by lawful, policy-based case needs.
3. **Discrimination or unfair treatment**: differences that remain after accounting for legitimate needs and that cannot be explained by neutral operational factors.
4. **Uncertainty**: differences that cannot be interpreted confidently because of measurement bias, omitted variables, or endogenous covariates.

## 2.1 Matching or stratification variables

A fair adjusted comparison should use a layered modeling strategy rather than one single regression.

### Baseline pre-routing case characteristics

Use these variables for matching or stratification:

- `risk_score_band`, with sensitivity analysis because it may be biased.
- `documentation_completeness`, with sensitivity analysis because it may reflect language or accessibility barriers.
- `channel`, because online and in-person cases may differ operationally.
- `language_preference`, because language support needs are legitimate case characteristics.
- `disability_accommodation_requested`, because accommodation needs are legitimate case characteristics.
- `household_size_band`.
- `prior_case_count`.
- `region`.
- `time:timestamp` month or week, to control for backlog and seasonality.

### Important modeling distinction

There are two different estimands:

1. **Total group disparity**: compare applicants with similar pre-application characteristics, without over-controlling for downstream consequences of group membership, such as documentation problems caused by inaccessible communication.
2. **Operational disparity after legitimate routing**: compare applicants with similar documented needs, including language support and accommodation needs, to see whether the process treats them similarly once those needs are known.

Both should be reported. Conditioning only on post-routing or post-contact variables can hide unfairness if those variables are themselves affected by language access, disability access, or historical disadvantage.

### Suggested matching approach

- Use coarsened exact matching or propensity-score matching within `region` and month.
- Match on `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `language_preference`, and `disability_accommodation_requested`.
- Then report results with and without `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` to test sensitivity.
- Where sample size permits, use exact strata for high-need combinations, for example: minority language + incomplete documentation + in-person channel.

## 2.2 Path and variant comparison

Variant analysis is essential because the same final outcome can be reached through very different procedural experiences.

The analysis should compare:

1. **Variant distribution by group**  
   Determine whether Groups B, C, and D are concentrated in slower or more burdensome variants.

2. **Within-variant outcomes**  
   Compare approval rates, cycle times, appeals, and rework within the same variant. If Group B applicants take longer than Group A applicants even within the same variant, that is stronger evidence of procedural disparity.

3. **Within-variant waiting times**  
   Compare step-level waiting times within variants. This identifies whether the same path is slower for some groups.

4. **Variant legitimacy**  
   Check whether variant assignment matches policy rules. For example:
   - Minority-language cases should include `Language Support Contact`.
   - Accommodation cases should include `Accommodation Review`.
   - High-risk cases should include `Risk Screening` and, where required, `Fraud Review`.

Important variant questions:

- Why is Group A much more likely to use fast Variant V1?
- Why is Group B much more likely to use Variant V4 with language support and document requests?
- Why is Group C much more likely to use Variant V5 with accommodation review and supervisor review?
- Why is Group D much more likely to use Variant V3 with fraud review and Variant V6 with appeal?
- Within V2, V4, and V5, are cycle times and rework rates similar across groups?

## 2.3 Resource-assignment comparison

Resource analysis should determine whether disparities are caused by applicant-level treatment or by structural resource allocation.

Compare:

- Number of distinct resources per case.
- Number of handovers per case.
- Share of cases handled by bilingual caseworkers.
- Share of cases handled by accommodation specialists.
- Share of cases handled by fraud-review resources.
- Caseload per resource by applicant group.
- Resource experience and role combinations.
- Whether the same resource handles multiple support roles, creating bottlenecks.

Useful comparisons:

- Group B cases assigned to bilingual caseworkers versus general caseworkers.
- Group C cases assigned to accommodation specialists versus generalists.
- Group D cases assigned to fraud-review queues versus standard queues.
- Regions with high handover chains versus regions with single case ownership.

If group disparities disappear after controlling for resource and queue, the problem may be resource allocation. If disparities persist within the same resource, queue, and variant, the concern about differential treatment increases.

## 2.4 Waiting-time decomposition

Cycle time should be decomposed into:

1. **Service time**: active processing time.
2. **Waiting time**: idle time between steps.
3. **Handover waiting time**: delay caused by transfer between resources.
4. **Support-step waiting time**: delay associated with language support or accommodation review.
5. **Review waiting time**: delay associated with supervisor review or fraud review.
6. **External waiting time**: delay caused by applicant document submission or third-party responses, if observable.

Key transitions to compare by group:

- `Submit Application  Identity Verification`
- `Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents`
- `Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment`
- `Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment`
- `Risk Screening  Fraud Review`
- `Supervisor Review  Decision Draft`
- `Decision Draft  Applicant Notification`

The data already suggest that the largest fairness concerns are not evenly distributed across the process. They are concentrated in:

- Language-support waiting for Group B.
- Accommodation-review waiting for Group C.
- Supervisor-review waiting for Group B in some regions.
- Fraud-review and appeal burden for Group D.

## 2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

`risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` should not be treated as unquestionably neutral.

### Risk score sensitivity

Run analyses under multiple assumptions:

- Treat `risk_score_band` as legitimate and include it.
- Exclude `risk_score_band` to see whether group disparities increase.
- Stratify by risk band and compare routing within the same band.
- Test whether the same risk band predicts fraud, adverse outcome, or appeal equally across groups.
- If possible, validate the risk score against objective outcomes unrelated to prior contact history.
- Check whether Group D’s higher fraud routing remains justified after adjusting for alternative risk indicators.

### Documentation completeness sensitivity

Documentation completeness may be affected by:

- Language barriers.
- Disability access barriers.
- Digital access barriers.
- In-person versus online submission.
- Prior negative experiences with public benefits.

Sensitivity checks:

- Exclude `documentation_completeness` and compare results.
- Stratify by complete versus incomplete documentation.
- Among complete-documentation cases, test whether Group B still receives more document requests.
- Among incomplete-documentation cases, test whether Group B receives more repeated requests or longer waits.
- Examine whether document requests are triggered by the same document types across groups.

If Group B still has higher `Request Missing Documents` rates after controlling for documented completeness, that may indicate measurement error, communication problems, or differential strictness.

## 2.6 Subgroup checks by region and channel

Subgroup analysis is necessary because aggregate group differences may hide local problems.

Recommended subgroup checks:

- By `region`.
- By `channel`: online versus in-person.
- By `language_preference`.
- By `disability_accommodation_requested`.
- By `risk_score_band`.
- By `documentation_completeness`.
- By month or quarter.
- By intersectional groups, for example minority-language applicants who also request accommodation.

Special attention should be given to the two regions where Group B applicants experience more handovers between general caseworker, bilingual caseworker, and general caseworker, and where Group C applicants experience more handovers between general caseworker, accommodation specialist, and supervisor.

The key question is whether the same case characteristics produce different paths, waits, and outcomes in different regions. If so, the process may be inconsistently administered.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least four mechanisms could generate the observed patterns. Some are legitimate; others are concerning.

## 4.1 Legitimate case-mix and policy-based routing

Groups B, C, and D have higher shares of documented complexity:

- Group B has higher incomplete documentation, language-support need, and in-person channel use.
- Group C has high accommodation requests.
- Group D has high risk scores and high prior-case counts.

These characteristics legitimately trigger additional steps:

- `Request Missing Documents`.
- `Language Support Contact`.
- `Accommodation Review`.
- `Risk Screening`.
- `Fraud Review`.
- `Supervisor Review`.

This mechanism explains part of the raw disparity. However, it does not fully explain residual adjusted differences, especially the support-step waiting times.

## 4.2 Capacity constraints in language support and accommodation review

The process appears to recognize language and accommodation rights but underinvests in the resources needed to deliver them promptly.

Evidence:

- Only 7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B language-support cases.
- Only 5 accommodation specialists for Group C accommodation cases.
- Long waits after support steps: 3.8 days for language support and 4.7 days for accommodation review.
- No separate timeliness thresholds for support steps.
- Accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review, increasing bottleneck risk.

This is a structural fairness problem. The support steps are rights-preserving, but their implementation imposes delay.

## 4.3 Measurement bias or historical bias in risk and documentation variables

`risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may not be neutral.

Possible bias mechanisms:

- Risk scores may be trained on historical cases where disadvantaged groups were more likely to be investigated, appealed, or reopened.
- Prior case count may reflect prior access problems rather than fraud risk.
- Documentation completeness may be lower because forms are not available in appropriate languages, formats, or accessibility modes.
- Caseworkers may interpret ambiguous documentation more strictly for minority-language applicants.
- Applicants with prior negative experiences may submit less complete initial applications.

If these mechanisms operate, controlling for `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may understate unfairness.

## 4.4 Communication and explanation failures

Higher appeal and rework rates may reflect poor communication rather than applicant error.

Possible communication issues:

- Decision notices may not be sufficiently translated.
- Document requests may be unclear.
- Accommodation-related decisions may be poorly explained.
- Applicants may not understand why additional review is required.
- Applicants may appeal because they do not understand the basis for adverse decisions.
- Repeated document requests may signal that the agency is not helping applicants correct deficiencies efficiently.

This mechanism is concerning because it affects procedural dignity, transparency, and access to effective remedy.

## 4.5 Regional and organizational inconsistency

The two regions with more handovers for Group B and Group C may have local workflow problems:

- Insufficient bilingual staff.
- Poor handoff protocols.
- Supervisor backlogs.
- Unclear ownership of cases requiring both general and specialized review.
- Inconsistent interpretation of documentation requirements.

If regional practices differ, the process may be procedurally inconsistent, which is itself a fairness issue.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event-log attributes are useful but insufficient for a definitive fairness conclusion.

## 5.1 Measurement bias in risk score

`risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities. It may reflect:

- Prior investigations.
- Prior appeals.
- Prior rework.
- Regional enforcement practices.
- Correlates of poverty, language, disability, or housing instability.
- Feedback loops where previously scrutinized groups are more likely to be scrutinized again.

If the risk score is biased, Group D’s higher fraud-review rate may look legitimate in the data while still being unfair.

## 5.2 Documentation completeness may be endogenous

`documentation_completeness` may not be a neutral measure of applicant preparedness. It may be affected by:

- Language barriers.
- Disability access needs.
- Digital access.
- Quality of intake assistance.
- Clarity of forms.
- Prior experience with public benefits.
- Caseworker interpretation of what counts as complete.

Therefore, higher document requests for Group B may reflect inaccessible process design rather than applicant deficiency.

## 5.3 Missing applicant needs and preferences

The log may not capture:

- Actual language proficiency.
- Preferred communication format.
- Disability type and required accommodation format.
- Digital literacy.
- Caregiver or representative involvement.
- Applicant questions or complaints.
- Whether the applicant understood the decision.
- Whether interpretation was actually provided.
- Whether accommodations were effective.

Without these, the analysis cannot fully assess accessibility or dignity of treatment.

## 5.4 Unobserved resource workload

Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. This limits inference because:

- Caseworkers may have different task complexity.
- Some resources may handle many short tasks and others few complex tasks.
- Supervisor review may be delayed by meetings, interruptions, or competing duties.
- Bilingual staff may spend time on interpretation not captured as case work.
- Accommodation specialists may perform unpaid coordination work.
- Regional backlogs may not be visible from event timestamps alone.

Thus, resource disparities may be underestimated.

## 5.5 Appeal quality and appeal success are missing

`appeal_flag` shows that an appeal occurred, but not:

- Why the applicant appealed.
- Whether the appeal succeeded.
- Whether the original decision was changed.
- Whether the applicant understood the appeal process.
- Whether the appeal was caused by poor explanation.
- Whether appeals are concentrated in particular regions or resources.

A higher appeal rate could indicate unfairness, but it could also indicate applicant empowerment. The meaning is ambiguous without appeal outcomes.

## 5.6 Outcome quality is missing

`case_outcome` does not capture:

- Benefit amount.
- Duration of benefit.
- Conditions attached.
- Correctness of decision.
- Quality of explanation.
- Dignity of treatment.
- Whether the applicant received timely interim support.
- Whether the decision was later corrected.

Equal approval rates would therefore not guarantee equal procedural treatment.

## 5.7 Group definitions may overlap or be too coarse

The four groups may not be mutually exclusive. An applicant could be minority-language, request accommodation, and have high prior-case count. Aggregate group comparisons can hide intersectional disadvantage.

For example, a Group B applicant who also requests accommodation may face compounded delays that are not visible in separate Group B and Group C summaries.

## 5.8 Causal ambiguity

The event log shows association, not definitive causation. Differences may be caused by:

- Legitimate case complexity.
- Resource shortages.
- Regional practices.
- Applicant behavior.
- Measurement bias.
- Discrimination.
- Some combination of these.

Therefore, conclusions must be calibrated.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigation should preserve language support and accommodation review while reducing the burden those steps impose.

| Mitigation option | Fairness benefit | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add capacity for language support | Reduces Group B waiting time after `Language Support Contact`; improves access to eligibility assessment | Hiring and training costs; time lag before capacity is effective; possible reduced capacity elsewhere; need to maintain quality of interpretation |
| Add capacity for accommodation review | Reduces Group C waiting time after `Accommodation Review`; prevents accommodation from becoming a delay penalty | Cost; need for qualified specialists; risk of diluting expertise if staff are reassigned too quickly; possible backlog in other specialized queues |
| Service-level targets for support steps | Makes support-step timeliness visible; prevents rights-preserving steps from being ignored by general cycle-time targets | Risk of gaming; pressure to rush support; possible triage toward easier cases; targets must be adjusted for genuine complexity |
| Routing redesign to reduce handovers | Reduces coordination burden for Group B and Group C; can improve ownership and accountability | May reduce flexibility; could create workload imbalance; may require role redesign; single case owner may not always have specialist knowledge |
| Parallel processing of support and eligibility | Allows language support or accommodation review to occur alongside eligibility work where feasible | May create rework if support changes eligibility information; requires better coordination and IT support; may increase complexity for caseworkers |
| Decision-explanation improvements | May reduce appeals and rework; improves transparency and dignity | Additional upfront time and translation cost; clearer explanations may initially increase appeals if applicants become more aware of rights |
| Translated and accessible document requests | May reduce repeated document requests for Group B and Group C | Translation and accessibility costs; need to maintain legal accuracy; may require ongoing updates |
| Monitoring adjusted disparities | Detects persistent disparities after case-mix adjustment; supports accountability | Risk of metric fixation; small-sample privacy issues; potential gaming if incentives are poorly designed |
| Auditing `risk_score_band` | Tests whether risk routing is fair and valid; identifies historical bias | Audit may be operationally disruptive; changing risk thresholds may affect fraud detection; requires governance and oversight |
| Auditing `documentation_completeness` | Determines whether completeness measures reflect applicant need or process accessibility | May reveal need for major form redesign; could require changes to intake procedures; may increase short-term processing time |
| Regional workload equalization | Reduces region-specific supervisor and handover delays | Requires cross-region coordination; may face staffing constraints; may reduce local autonomy |
| Appeal feedback loop | Uses appeal reasons to improve decisions and explanations | Requires appeal-outcome data; may increase workload; may reveal systemic errors requiring remediation |

Important: support steps should not be removed merely to improve cycle time. The fairness goal is to make support timely and effective, not to eliminate rights-preserving functions.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does not appear fully procedurally fair, but the evidence does not support a definitive conclusion of intentional discrimination.

Raw approval-rate gaps are large, but they are partly explained by documented case mix. The adjusted outcome gaps are smaller, which suggests that legitimate complexity matters. However, meaningful adjusted disparities remain, especially for Group B and Group C in cycle time, document requests, supervisor review, and support-step waiting times.

The clearest procedural fairness concerns are not primarily outcome differences. They are:

- Group B’s higher adjusted probability of missing-document requests.
- Group B’s longer waits associated with language support.
- Group C’s longer waits associated with accommodation review.
- The concentration of Group B and Group C cases on scarce specialized resources.
- Regional handover patterns that add burden.
- Higher adjusted appeal probabilities for Groups B and D.
- The absence of separate timeliness thresholds for rights-preserving support steps.

For Group D, heavier fraud review and risk-based routing are more plausibly legitimate because of higher measured risk and prior-case count. But this legitimacy depends on the validity and fairness of `risk_score_band`, which cannot be assumed. Group D’s higher adjusted appeal rate also requires investigation.

The fairest conclusion is therefore calibrated:

- The process shows **evidence of procedural unfairness in timeliness, burden, and accessibility**, especially for Group B and Group C.
- The process shows **some legitimate routing**, especially for accommodation, language support, and risk-based review.
- The process shows **important uncertainty** because risk scores, documentation completeness, appeal quality, and resource workload may be biased or incompletely measured.
- The evidence is **not sufficient to conclude that raw approval differences alone are discriminatory**, but it is sufficient to require remediation, monitoring, and audit.
