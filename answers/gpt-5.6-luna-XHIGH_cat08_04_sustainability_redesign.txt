# 1. System boundary and assumptions

The primary boundary is order fulfilment from `Receive order` through `Close` (or cancellation), including availability decisions, stock picking, production and batching, heat treatment, inspection, rework, packaging and packaging rework, route selection, shipment, delivery confirmation, and any second shipment caused by a partial shipment or backorder. The unit of analysis is an order, with line-item and part-weight effects retained where available; the flattened order log must not be treated as proof that every line item followed the same path.

Inside the operational footprint are machine and heat-treatment energy (`energy_kwh`), material scrap (`material_scrap_kg`), packaging material (`package_material_kg`), transport emissions (`transport_kgco2e`), the emissions and materials associated with rework and returns, and the consequences of batching, waiting, cancellation, obsolescence, and repeat shipments. Empty dedicated-van returns should be counted in transport allocation where possible. Service quality is part of the decision boundary: cycle time, promised-lead-time adherence, cancellation, damage/return, and downtime exposure are constraints, not externalities.

I would report upstream and downstream effects separately where they are not observed: manufacture and disposal of packaging, production and disposal of raw material, electricity-generation factors, carrier fuel and vehicle manufacture, and customer-side downtime or avoided production loss. They should be included in a fuller lifecycle assessment if credible factors and quantities become available, but should not be guessed from the current log. Thus the immediate decision metric is an operational cradle-to-gate proxy plus delivery consequences, not a claim of complete product carbon footprint.

The main uncertainties are carbon factors behind the energy and transport fields, allocation of shared truck or van emissions, whether scrap is recycled or disposed of, incomplete return/damage capture, forecast accuracy, carrier reliability by lane, and the fact that emergency and partial-shipment combinations make the discovered model imprecise. Baselines should therefore be stratified by case type, customer type, machine, route, weight/geometry, due-date slack, and disruption state rather than compared only at portfolio average.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

The indicators should be calculated per order and, for production orders, per shipped good item or kilogram where possible. Each should be split by the segments and paths in the prompt and shown as a distribution (median, p90, and tail), not only as an average.

| Dimension | Indicator derived from the log | Important slices or interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Total `energy_kwh/order`, `energy_kwh/good item`, and machine/heat-treatment energy; energy from rework counted separately | CNC_A versus CNC_B, batch size, batch wait, MTO type, and emergency production |
| Transport | `transport_kgco2e/order`, per shipped item, and per delivered kilogram; number of shipments per order | Ground, air, consolidated truck, dedicated van; include repeat shipments and allocated empty van returns |
| Materials | `material_scrap_kg` per order and per good item; scrap rate by machine and route | CNC_B/rework loops, emergency work, part family, and whether scrap is recovered |
| Packaging | `package_material_kg/order`, packaging rework rate, and damage/return rate | Standard versus reinforced packaging, part weight/geometry, and route compatibility |
| Rework and returns | `rework_flag`, number of rework-production and rework-packaging loops, return/damage rate, and emissions/materials attributable to the loop | Inspection cell, machine group, packaging choice, and defect reason |
| Batching | Batch size, batch-wait hours, share meeting each wait cap, energy per item by batch band, and unused/obsolete batch quantity | CNC_A and heat treatment separately; demand forecast error and not-shipped-within-14-days rate |
| Service | Median and p90 cycle time, promised-lead-time adherence, late-delivery rate, cancellation-after-delay rate, and number of customer contacts/shipments | All six case/customer segments; especially partial shipment and air/ground substitutions |
| Quality | First-pass yield, inspection escape/return rate, packaging damage rate, Q1 conformance, and rework-free delivery | CNC_B versus CNC_A, inspection queue time, part weight, and reinforced-pack eligibility |
| Resilience | Share of service-contract orders with a qualified alternative route/carrier, recovery time during disruption, emergency-production and dedicated-van usage, and service performance under disruption | SP2 justifications, true versus precautionary critical orders, and carrier/lane concentration |
| Policy and trade-off | SP1 eligible-ground decisions and violations, SP2 justification completeness, Q1 violations, and carbon per on-time good delivery | Report both compliance and override reason; do not reward emissions reduction that increases cancellations or returns |

Useful composite views are kgCO2e per on-time, damage-free delivered order; energy plus scrap per good MTO part; and a Pareto frontier of emissions against late delivery, cancellation, and return risk. These are guardrail views, not a reason to hide a bad tail behind a favorable mean.

# 3. Intervention options

**S1/S4: Green shipping decision with slack and reliability gates.** Target Standard-stock Service-contract orders and the stock paths `standard pack -> air` and `standard pack -> ground`, while retaining express treatment for genuinely tight or unreliable lanes. At availability check or package time, calculate due-date slack from `promised_lead_time_hours`, route transit distribution, current queue, and carrier reliability. If ground or consolidated truck can meet the promise with probability above 85%, use priority picking plus ground or the next suitable consolidated departure; otherwise air remains an explicit service-protecting exception. This directly addresses the 22% SP1 violation rate and the observation that many service-contract stock orders use air despite enough slack. It should reduce transport emissions materially on substituted air orders, with a possible 24-hour median transit increase, but should not materially worsen late delivery if the reliability gate is calibrated. Monitor route choice, slack at decision time, predicted versus actual transit, late deliveries, cancellations, repeat shipments, and overrides by segment.

**S5: Twice-daily consolidated departures for eligible stock.** Target non-urgent stock orders, especially those currently using individual ground parcels. Consolidation can reduce transport emissions by the observed 35-55% relative to individual ground, but only if the 12-24-hour wait is inside the promise and the truck is sufficiently utilized. Use two fixed departures, a cutoff, and an automatic fallback to ground for orders approaching their service guardrail. This improves transport efficiency and may slightly increase cycle time; it creates a late-risk tail if cutoffs or volume forecasts are wrong. Monitor consolidation wait, fill rate, route emissions allocation, p90 cycle time, late/cancellation rates, and carrier disruption performance.

**S2: Dynamic batching with segment-specific maximum waits.** Target `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production` and heat-treatment waits. Batch CNC_A and heat treatment while there is enough demand to obtain their lower energy per item, but impose hard wait caps based on case type, customer type, due-date slack, and queue conditions. The observed CNC_A change from 2.8 to 1.3 kWh/item at batch sizes 12-20 is a reason to seek batching, not to wait 38 hours unconditionally. Critical-downtime and low-slack MTO orders should bypass or use a small batch; flexible Regular MTO orders can wait longer, subject to the 14-day obsolescence guardrail. Monitor batch size, wait, energy/item, lateness, forecast error, unused items, and emergency bypasses. Heat-treatment batches need a separate cap because heat-treatment waiting is associated with 1.7 times higher late-delivery risk.

**S3: Prefer CNC_A over CNC_B when slack permits.** Target MTO paths through `CNC_B -> inspection -> rework` and eligible immediate or batched production. Route parts to CNC_A when its queue and due-date slack allow the change, and retain CNC_B as a capacity and resilience alternative rather than banning it. CNC_A's lower rework rate (9% versus 18%) can avoid extra energy, scrap, inspection, packaging, and returns; a longer queue could otherwise increase lateness. Monitor machine assignment, queue and wait time, first-pass yield, rework loops, scrap per good part, energy per good part, and throughput during disruption. The decision should use total impact per good delivered part, not just machine energy per run.

**S7: Add an inspection quality gate for high-risk combinations.** Target CNC_B, rushed inspections, high-rework part families, and the `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production` loop. Reserve inspection capacity or apply an earlier in-process check for combinations with a high observed escape/rework rate. Do not simply make every inspection more elaborate: use risk-based sampling and a due-date-aware queue so that quality improvement does not become a new bottleneck. This should reduce the 5% observed path with 32 kWh energy and 1.8 kg scrap and the associated return/rework burden, while possibly adding a small inspection time. Monitor queue time, first-pass yield, rework cause, escaped defects, returns, scrap and energy per accepted part, and inspection bypasses.

**S6: Make packaging selection route- and geometry-aware before shipment.** Target `Package standard/reinforced -> Rework packaging` and heavy MTO parts. At packaging, validate part weight, geometry, damage risk, route handling, and intended carrier against the packaging rule. Enforce Q1 for MTO parts over 8 kg unless inspection has certified low damage risk, while preventing incompatible over-packaging when a standard pack is demonstrably safe. This avoids packaging rework and damage-related returns; reinforced packaging should remain for heavy high-risk parts because it cuts damage returns by 40%, even though it doubles package material. Monitor package material, Q1 exceptions, packaging rework, damage/return rate, route compatibility, and the total material and transport effect of package weight.

**S8/S9: Tier urgent transport and reserve resilience capacity.** Target the `Critical -> emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van` path. Split Critical-downtime orders into true active downtime and precautionary urgent orders using evidence of operational impact, required arrival time, and available stock. Keep a dedicated van for true downtime when it is needed to protect service, but use a shared emergency courier lane, compatible ground/air priority service, or a consolidated urgent movement for precautionary orders when the promise allows. Require an explicit SP2 justification and record whether the van was full, shared, or returned empty. This can reduce the path's roughly 52 kgCO2e transport burden, but an over-aggressive substitution could increase downtime. Monitor time-to-restore for true critical cases, route reliability, dedicated-van utilization and empty return, emergency production, alternative-route availability, and disruption outcomes.

**S10: Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for flexible Regular MTO.** Target Regular MTO orders with sufficient customer flexibility, not Service-contract or true critical orders. Present a lower-impact promise option that allows an efficient CNC_A/heat-treatment batch and ground or consolidated transport, alongside a faster higher-impact option with a transparent trade-off. The longer promise must be accepted before commitment and remain feasible under the wait caps. This can reduce rush production, air transport, and small-batch energy, but customer acceptance, demand shifting, and lost orders must be measured. Monitor offer/acceptance rates, promised versus actual cycle time, batch and route chosen, margin/customer impact, late delivery, cancellation, and whether demand merely moves to a later rush.

**S11: Reduce partial shipments through better availability and allocation rules.** Target `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait` and the 7% path with 79-hour median cycle time and 24% lateness. At availability check, reserve complete order kits where feasible, identify a near-term replenishment date, and ask for customer consent before splitting. For a genuine critical line, split deliberately and record why; for regular orders, wait for a complete shipment when the resulting promise remains safe. Fewer second shipments and contacts reduce transport and packaging, but holding an urgent line can worsen service. Monitor split rate, lines held versus shipped, second-shipment emissions, late/cancellation outcomes, customer choice, and stockout recovery.

**S12: Sustainability exception log and decision trace.** Target all overrides of SP1, SP2, and Q1, including air, dedicated van, bypassed batching, non-reinforced packaging, and emergency production. Record the promise, predicted probability of on-time delivery, route reliability, alternative considered, reason, approver/system rule, and realized outcome. This does not itself lower emissions, but prevents silent displacement of impact and allows the recommender and thresholds to be recalibrated. Monitor override frequency, completeness (including the missing 31% of dedicated-van justifications), outcome by reason, and repeated overrides by lane, customer, machine, or planner.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching can trade energy for lateness and obsolescence.** Larger CNC_A or heat-treatment batches lower energy per item, but 38-hour CNC_A waits, 1.7x heat-treatment late risk, and the 6% not-shipped-within-14-days rate can create cancellations, obsolete stock, or emergency replacements. A batch-size target without a wait cap and demand guardrail is therefore unsafe.

2. **Transport substitution can move emissions into service recovery.** Replacing air or vans with ground/consolidated truck can add 12-24 hours or more, cause a missed promise, and trigger a second air shipment, emergency production, cancellation, or customer downtime. Evaluate emissions per on-time delivered order, not per first leg.

3. **Consolidation can concentrate resilience risk.** A twice-daily truck is efficient only if it is reliable and has capacity. A missed departure or single carrier outage can affect many orders at once. Retain an alternative carrier/route for Service-contract customers and a controlled fallback to ground or air.

4. **Less packaging can increase total material and transport.** Standard packaging uses less package material, but an incompatible pack can cause rework, damage, returns, and another shipment. Conversely, reinforcing every part doubles packaging material unnecessarily. Apply Q1 and a geometry/route risk rule, then compare virgin material, rework, return transport, and damage together.

5. **Fast or cheap production can displace waste into rework.** Sending work to faster CNC_B or rushing inspection may reduce waiting while increasing its 18% rework rate, versus 9% on CNC_A. Rework consumes additional machine and inspection energy, 1.8 kg scrap on the observed path, packaging, transport, and potentially customer-return handling.

6. **Avoiding dedicated vans can shift risk to customer downtime.** A dedicated van's approximately 52 kgCO2e path is high, but replacing it for a true active-downtime case can create a much larger operational loss and breach the service requirement. The control is classification into true versus precautionary urgency, explicit justification, and measurement of time-to-restore—not a blanket ban.

7. **Carbon-aware promises can create forecast and demand displacement.** Customers may accept a later low-carbon MTO promise and then cancel, request an expedite, or create a later peak that requires air freight. Measure acceptance, cancellation, rush conversion, and obsolescence by promise cohort; do not assume a shifted date is a reduction.

8. **Reducing partial shipments can worsen line-level service.** Holding a ready critical line for order completeness reduces duplicate transport, but can delay a customer who could use that line immediately. Offer a deliberate split when the line is service-critical and compare the avoided second-shipment emissions with the service benefit.

9. **Priority rules can displace regular customers or create hidden inequity.** Giving Service-contract or critical orders priority may protect contractual service but increase Regular MTO waiting, batching disruption, or cancellations. Track segment-level and tail outcomes, not just aggregate emissions and on-time performance.

10. **A recommender can optimize measured CO2 while worsening unmeasured impacts.** If it sees transport but not empty van returns, return freight, packaging disposal, or supplier material impacts, it may select a superficially green route. Keep those fields in the boundary where available, maintain an uncertainty flag, and review decisions that trade a measured reduction for an unmeasured material or quality risk.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a constrained, segment-aware control loop rather than one global “greenest route” rule:

1. At availability check, classify orders by case type, customer type, due-date slack, part weight/geometry, and true operational urgency. Use S1/S4 for stock orders: priority pick plus ground or the next consolidated truck when predicted on-time probability exceeds 85%; retain air for insufficient slack or unreliable lanes, with the reason recorded. Apply S11 to avoid partial shipments unless a line-level service need justifies the split.

2. For MTO, use S2's dynamic batching and S3's CNC_A preference when slack permits. Make CNC_A and efficient heat-treatment batching the default for flexible Regular MTO, but cap waits and preserve a small-batch/immediate path for low-slack and Service-contract work. Use S10 to negotiate a later, carbon-aware promise only with Regular customers who accept it. Protect capacity for disruptions so the sustainable plan does not routinely collapse into emergency production or air freight.

3. Put S7 before final packaging for high-rework combinations and make S6 a hard route/geometry compatibility check. Enforce Q1 for parts over 8 kg unless the inspection record certifies low damage risk. This accepts the material cost of reinforced packaging where it prevents the larger combined impact of damage, rework, returns, and repeat transport.

4. For Critical-downtime Service-contract orders, use S9 to distinguish active downtime from precautionary urgency. Keep a dedicated van or emergency production for genuine downtime when needed, but route precautionary cases through S8 shared emergency lanes or a reliable lower-impact alternative. Require SP2 justification and maintain at least one viable alternative route for Service-contract customers.

5. Instrument all exceptions through S12 and optimize to a balanced scorecard: kgCO2e per on-time, damage-free delivered order; energy and scrap per good part; packaging and return burden; p90 cycle time; late delivery and cancellation; and disruption recovery. A change is accepted only if its environmental improvement is accompanied by no unacceptable deterioration in agreed service, Q1 quality, or R1 resilience guardrails. Thresholds should be set with operations and contractual owners before rollout, with Critical-downtime and Service-contract guardrails stricter than Regular MTO.

This package addresses the largest visible burdens: air and dedicated-van transport, MTO energy and batch waits, CNC_B rework, packaging rework, and partial-shipment duplication. It also preserves exceptions where they buy real service or resilience, making sustainability a controlled operating policy rather than a blanket mode or machine substitution.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

**Baseline and pilot design.** Freeze a pre-change baseline using the 12,000-order period, but reweight or stratify it by segment, route, machine, batch band, weight/geometry, slack, and disruption state. Pilot by lane, machine/part family, or fulfillment site with a contemporaneous comparison group. Compare both means and p50/p90/tail outcomes, and use order-line identifiers or shipment identifiers to connect partial shipments, returns, and rework to the originating order. Validate that the apparent change is not only a case-mix shift.

**Event instrumentation.** Add or standardize events for route recommendation and override, due-date slack, predicted on-time probability, batch join/release, inspection decision and queue, packaging compatibility check, damage/return reason, SP1/SP2/Q1 decision, dedicated-van utilization/empty return, customer acceptance of a negotiated promise, and alternative-route availability. Capture energy, scrap, package material, transport, batch, machine, rework, expedite, and promised-lead-time values consistently at the event or order-line level.

**Operational acceptance tests.** For each intervention and segment, compare transport and energy against the matched baseline, but gate deployment on service and quality: promised-lead-time adherence, p90 cycle time, late delivery, cancellation, downtime restoration, first-pass yield, packaging damage/return, rework loops, and scrap per good part. Inspect the specific paths `partial shipment -> backorder`, `batch wait -> CNC`, `inspection -> rework`, `rework packaging`, `ship air`, and `dedicated van`; aggregate averages can conceal regressions there.

**Policy conformance.** Monitor SP1 as the share of eligible orders using ground or consolidated truck when the on-time probability is above 85%, plus every override and its outcome. Monitor SP2 for 100% explicit justification on dedicated-van cases, with a separate report for true and precautionary Critical-downtime. Monitor Q1 for every MTO part over 8 kg: reinforced packaging is required unless the inspection record certifies low damage risk. Investigate the current 22% SP1 violation rate and 31% missing SP2 justification as initial control targets, and link Q1 violations to the observed return/rework loops.

**Resilience and exception review.** Run disruption scenarios and live carrier-failure reviews to verify R1: each Service-contract segment must retain at least one qualified alternative route, with measured recovery time and service performance. Review emergency production, air, dedicated-van, partial-shipment, and batching bypasses weekly at first, including the emissions and service consequences of the fallback. Use the exception log to identify systematic failures in reliability predictions, cutoffs, machine queues, forecasts, or packaging rules.

**Governance and iteration.** Assign ownership to operations for service guardrails, quality for inspection and Q1, logistics for route/carrier and SP1/SP2, production for batching and machine allocation, and sustainability for metric definitions and lifecycle-data gaps. Recalibrate thresholds monthly or after a material disruption, and audit a sample of orders end to end. Expand only when environmental improvements persist across matched segments and no guardrail breach remains unexplained; if a guardrail fails, roll back the affected rule or narrow its eligibility while retaining the traceable evidence for the next redesign.
