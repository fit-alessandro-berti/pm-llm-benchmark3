## 1. What is observed

Availability is a branch–month policy variable: Alpha and Beta switch from 0 to 1 in April, Gamma in July, while Delta and Epsilon remain 0 through August. It is not a record that a case received automation. Actual exposure is better captured by the all-case automated fast-path share. Among adopters, that share ranges from 25% to 50% after launch and generally increases over time, so availability, early use, and mature use are different treatment versions.

Adoption was selected rather than randomized. Alpha and Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma adopted after a readiness pilot. Rollout coincided with a March centralized fraud desk, April onward routing increases, branch-specific FTE changes, and a May product-driven mix shift. Learning and local work practices also evolved.

The outcomes are branch-month summaries, not case trajectories: monthly medians and p90s, case-level rework/escalation percentages, and survey means. FTE measures productive capacity, but not hours by task or skill. Case composition, queue waiting, fraud-desk congestion, and routing reasons are not recorded. CSAT response rates of roughly 20–45%, with unknown respondent counts and no nonresponse information, prevent reliable weighting. Aggregated quantiles and repeated monthly observations cannot establish within-case improvement or its cause.

## 2. Causal estimands

Here, “availability effect” means the effect of the operational policy package, including induced changes in routing, use, referrals, and learning—not the isolated algorithmic contribution. A negative cycle-time effect means an improvement.

Interference is plausible through the shared fraud desk, altered incoming volumes, work sharing, and learning. Thus E1 may legitimately include some spillovers as part of a rollout policy, but those spillovers also contaminate simple control-group interpretations. E2 additionally requires a well-defined use mechanism: routing a borderline case to automation and encouraging simple-case use are different interventions.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: cohort-specific, risk-set-matched controlled event study (“synthetic DiD”)** | Stack the April Alpha/Beta cohort and July Gamma cohort, retaining event time, cohort-specific comparisons, and separate early/later post estimates. Do not treat already-adopted branches as controls for later adopters | April risk set: Gamma, Delta, Epsilon. July risk set: Delta, Epsilon. Prefer pre-period matching or outcome-specific synthetic weights; do not force a poor match | Pre-adoption outcome levels/trends, volume, risk, and FTE; calendar fixed effects for common shocks. Retain concurrent staffing/routing in the total policy effect, with separate direct-effect sensitivities | E1 and E7 | Conditional parallel trends under matching/weights; no unmeasured rollout-specific shocks; no material unmodeled interference; consistent outcome measurement; adoption timing does not conceal an earlier intervention | Plot all branch paths; examine event-time leads, not just post coefficients; leave-one-branch-out matching; placebo timing; pre-fit and common-support checks; inspect alternative donor pools | Only three pre-adoption months, sharply timed co-interventions, and five branches. Matching cannot repair an unobserved Gamma-specific shock or shared-queue contamination |
| **Alternative 1: outcome-specific synthetic control** | Compare each adopter’s observed post-adoption path with a fixed weighted combination of untreated branches, with an intervention marker in April or July | For each treated branch, all branches not yet treated at that date; use the same donor pool for pre and post fitting | Donor weights based on pre-period outcome levels, recent trends, volume, complexity, and FTE; optional donor-specific calendar adjustments | E1 interpreted as a branch-specific policy-path deviation | Each treated branch’s counterfactual follows the weighted untreated path; donor trajectories are not materially changed by treatment | Pre-period RMSPE and path plots; leave-one-donor-out weights; in-space placebos; sensitivity of weights to including or excluding Gamma | Only two always-untreated donors. A synthetic fit can look convincing while pooling very different branches or absorbing shared shocks |
| **Alternative 2: controlled segmented interrupted time series** | Estimate adopter-specific post-level and post-slope changes, allowing different adoption dates and adding breaks for the March fraud desk and May product change | Contemporaneous untreated branches or a risk-set-weighted synthetic series | Pre-level/slope, calendar shocks, risk mix, volume, FTE; explicitly distinguish policy and conditional-capacity estimands | E7 and a controlled version of E1 | No omitted time-varying shock coincident with adoption; segmented trends correctly specified; no differential spillover | Leave-one-month-out stability; alternative break dates; residual plots; pre-trend sensitivity; common-shock exclusion checks | Three pre-period points provide little power to identify trends or distinguish an adoption break from the May product and staffing changes |
| **Alternative 3: prospective randomized encouragement with case-level process records** | Randomize immediate versus delayed access/encouragement within the next branch; measure event time and actual use separately | Randomized immediate and delayed groups within the same branch, stratified by risk, queue, arrival period, and team | Pre-specified risk and workload strata; branch/time effects only if needed; assignment is the primary treatment | E1 prospectively; E2 as an encouragement-identified complier effect if assumptions hold | Concealed assignment, limited noncompliance, no differential interference, and—using assignment as an instrument—a credible exclusion restriction and monotonicity | Assignment balance; first-stage use; contamination and override logs; subgroup overlap; randomized inference or work-cell clustering | Not available in the supplied data. Encouragement may change learning and referrals, so instrument validity cannot simply be assumed |

The primary design is preferable because adoption timing and exposure are both heterogeneous. Risk-set matching prevents Gamma’s later rollout from being treated as though it never occurred, while cohort-specific event-time estimates allow automation to improve gradually rather than assuming a single permanent April or July shock. Pooled availability and fast-path coefficients would otherwise mix learning, selection into use, maturity, and different co-interventions.

Even this design remains exploratory. March’s fraud-desk change, April routing increases, May complexity changes, and local staffing events are not randomized. The three pre-adoption observations cannot establish parallel trends convincingly, especially for Gamma, whose May–June conditions differ substantially from its July launch. Shared fraud-desk waiting could cause both adopters and controls to depart from their assumed counterfactuals. The raw median improvement in Alpha and Beta, for example, is small relative to the contemporaneous increase in untreated branches, showing why a simple pre/post comparison is inadequate.

A preferred primary analysis would estimate each outcome separately, use synthetic weights estimated from pre-period behavior, and report raw, weighted, leave-one-out, and alternative-window results. Cluster-robust or wild-bootstrap calculations would still have only five clusters; they should not be presented as well-calibrated population confidence intervals. This design can organize credible sensitivity analysis and policy learning, but it does not turn this short panel into a conclusively identified experiment.

Alpha and Beta exhibit lower medians but higher p90s as use increases. Gamma shows both median and p90 improvement over a shorter exposure gradient. The code fits descriptive intensity associations with branch and calendar effects, without treating them as causal.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** January–February paths are mostly stable, but that is too little history to establish parallel trends. The March fraud desk coincides with p90 increases in every branch. Complexity and demand then change at different times across branches. Selecting Alpha and Beta for poor historical performance also creates a regression-to-the-mean risk: some raw improvement could have occurred without automation, particularly for cycle time and possibly satisfaction.

**Window sensitivity for separate outcomes.** Extending Alpha/Beta’s March comparison through August gives median differences of 16.8 and 7.5 percentage points relative to untreated changes, and p90 differences of 12.3 and 8.4 points. For Gamma, changing the pre window from March to June changes the median contrast from 17.3 to 8.1 points and the p90 contrast from 10.7 to 6.8 points. March is closer to the adoption event but also contains the fraud-desk transition; June is less exposed to that transition but contains prior complexity and staffing shocks. Neither alternative establishes a causal estimate. The scenario ranges in Section 7 are therefore planning bounds, not confidence intervals.

## 7. Effect assessment

Confidence scores are subjective evidence-strength assessments, **not probabilities that a causal effect is true**. Scenario intervals refer to plausible short-horizon availability-policy effects relative to no rollout at the same time; negative cycle-time/rework values indicate improvement. They are not 95% confidence intervals and do not represent identified population parameters.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

`expand with an embedded evaluation`

Offer **Delta only** in the next phase. Its lower baseline complexity and smaller volume make it a less hazardous initial capacity test than Epsilon, which has higher complexity and longer cycle times. Epsilon should remain a not-yet-treated external comparison for the first evaluation and then enter its own phased introduction.

Within Delta, randomize eligible cases—or operationally necessary work cells or shifts—to immediate availability versus delayed availability after 12 weeks. Stratify assignment by registration risk, arrival period, queue, and team skill, and conceal allocation from managers selecting cases. Keep a fully usable manual fallback. Randomization at case level is preferable; if work-cell assignment is necessary, include enough cells and account for cluster dependence. Managers should not override assignment to improve the demonstration.

Collect at least **eight fresh pre-intervention weeks**, preferably twelve, and **twelve post-intervention weeks** for both groups. Continue measurement for at least eight weeks after the delayed group receives access. Recover at least six additional months of historical monthly data where possible. Publish a registered analysis plan and pre-specify the primary outcomes and harm margins.

Record case-level events and queue states: registration and risk, routing, decision timestamps, manual touches, automated recommendations, overrides, specialist entry/work/finish, information requests, rework, resolution, closure, and reopening. Collect queue arrivals, occupancy, waiting versus service time, age distribution, productive hours, skill mix, and cross-branch transfers. Measure the four path variants and the proportion of cases with loops. Analyze duration distributions directly; do not run discovery algorithms on branch-month aggregates.

Monitor Delta’s impact on the shared fraud desk, its arrival rate, waiting time, backlog, and service capacity, including effects on other branches. If Epsilon is used as a donor, measure whether Delta contaminates its comparison path.

Pre-register guardrails such as p90 no more than 10% above baseline, rework no more than 3 pp above baseline, and CSAT no more than 0.10 points below baseline. A guardrail breach triggers a hold on new assignments and investigation; serious customer harm triggers immediate operational containment. Scale further only if confidence bounds exclude unacceptable harm, specialist queues remain stable, implementation fidelity is adequate, and there is evidence of net capacity or median-time benefit. If precision is insufficient, collect more data rather than converting uncertainty into a favorable claim.

OUTCOMES = [
    "median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h", "rework_pct", "escalation_pct", "csat_1to5"
]
CYCLE = {"median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h"}
PERCENTAGES = [
    "high_complexity_pct", "automated_fast_path_pct",
    "rework_pct", "escalation_pct"
]
BRANCHES = ["Alpha", "Beta", "Gamma", "Delta", "Epsilon"]
CONTROLS = ["Delta", "Epsilon"]

# ---------- 1. Load and validate ----------
d = pd.read_csv(StringIO(CSV))
numeric = [
    "automation", "arrivals", "fte", "high_complexity_pct",
    "automated_fast_path_pct", "median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h",
    "rework_pct", "escalation_pct", "csat_1to5"
]
d[numeric] = d[numeric].apply(pd.to_numeric, errors="raise")
d["month"] = pd.to_datetime(d["month"], errors="raise")
d = d.sort_values(["branch", "month"]).reset_index(drop=True)

if d.shape[0] != 40:
    raise ValueError("Expected 40 branch-month observations.")
if set(d["branch"]) != set(BRANCHES):
    raise ValueError("Unexpected branch set.")
if d.duplicated(["branch", "month"]).any():
    raise ValueError("Duplicate branch-month observations.")
if d[numeric].isna().any().any() or d["month"].isna().any():
    raise ValueError("Missing values detected.")
if d.groupby("branch", observed=True).size().ne(8).any():
    raise ValueError("Every branch must have eight months.")
expected_months = pd.date_range("2026-01-01", periods=8, freq="MS")
if not np.array_equal(d["month"].unique(), expected_months.values):
    raise ValueError("Unexpected or incomplete calendar months.")
if not d["automation"].isin([0, 1]).all():
    raise ValueError("automation must be binary.")
if (d["arrivals"] <= 0).any() or (d["fte"] <= 0).any():
    raise ValueError("arrivals and FTE must be positive.")
if ((d[PERCENTAGES] < 0) | (d[PERCENTAGES] > 100)).any().any():
    raise ValueError("Percent measures must lie between 0 and 100.")
if not d["csat_1to5"].between(1, 5).all():
    raise ValueError("CSAT must lie between 1 and 5.")

# Validate no within-branch adoption reversal in this panel.
for branch, g in d.groupby("branch", observed=True):
    if (g["automation"].diff().dropna() < 0).any():
        raise ValueError(f"Adoption reversal detected for {branch}.")

print("Validation passed: 5 branches x 8 months = 40 observations.")
print("WARNING: only 5 clusters; cluster p-values/SEs are fragile diagnostics.")
print("WARNING: outcomes are aggregates, not case-level observations.")
print("WARNING: p90 estimation uncertainty and CSAT response-count uncertainty are unavailable.")
print("WARNING: FTE is productive capacity, not task-level labor hours or headcount.")

# This code prints ABSOLUTE changes and differences in their original units.
# It is not a parallel-trends-validated DiD estimator.
calendar_contrasts = []
controls = d.loc[d["branch"].isin(CONTROLS)]

for branch in adoption_dates.index:
    g = d.loc[d["branch"].eq(branch)]
    pre_month = g["adoption_date"].iloc[0] - pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)
    post_months = (

    )
    own_pre = g.loc[g["month"].eq(pre_month), all_measures].mean()
    own_post = g.loc[g["month"].isin(post_months), all_measures].mean()
    control_pre = controls.loc[
        controls["month"].eq(pre_month), all_measures
    ].mean()
    control_post = controls.loc[
        controls["month"].isin(post_months), all_measures
    ].mean()

    row = {"branch": branch}
    for y in all_measures:
        own_change = float(own_post[y] - own_pre[y])
        control_change = float(control_post[y] - control_pre[y])
        row[y] = (
            f"own={own_change:+.3f}; "
            f"control={control_change:+.3f}; "
            f"contrast={own_change-control_change:+.3f}"
        )
    calendar_contrasts.append(row)

print("\nCALENDAR-ALIGNED NEVER-TREATED DESCRIPTIVE CONTRASTS")
print("Original outcome units; Delta/Epsilon equally weighted within each window.")
print("These comparisons do not establish conditional parallel trends.")
print(pd.DataFrame(calendar_contrasts).to_string(index=False))

event_columns = []
for k in [-4, -3, -2, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4]:
    # Relative month -1 is the omitted event-study reference period.
    name = ("ev_m" if k < 0 else "ev_p") + str(abs(k))
    d[name] = d["rel_month"].eq(k).astype(int)
    event_columns.append(name)

d["cal_month"] = d["month"].dt.month.astype("category")
d["branch"] = d["branch"].astype("category")
d["fast_share_10pp"] = d["automated_fast_path_pct"] / 10.0

print("\nSTAGGERED-ADOPTION RELATIVE TIME")

    d[["branch", "month", "automation", "adoption_date", "rel_month"]]
    .to_string(index=False)

# ---------- 5. Descriptive panel model and treatment-intensity model ----------
def clustered_ols(formula, data, focus):
    """
    Statsmodels permits branch-clustered covariance estimates here.
    With only five clusters, these remain descriptive and potentially fragile.
    A regression coefficient does not prove any causal assumption.
    """
    result = smf.ols(formula, data=data).fit(
        cov_type="cluster",
        cov_kwds={"groups": data["branch"], "use_correction": True},
        use_t=True
    )
    print(f"\nFormula: {formula}")
    print(f"N={int(result.nobs)}; clusters=5")
    print(
        pd.DataFrame({
            "estimate": result.params.loc[focus],
            "branch_cluster_se": result.bse.loc[focus],
            "pvalue_descriptive": result.pvalues.loc[focus]
        }).round(5).to_string()
    )
    return result

event_rhs = (
    "C(branch) + C(cal_month, Treatment(reference=1)) + "
    + " + ".join(event_columns)
)

# Descriptive availability event study, not a naive binary-post regression.
# Leads help inspect pre-trends, but three pre-adoption points cannot validate
# parallel trends. Calendar FE assume common calendar effects; shared fraud-desk
# effects may be differential and remain an identification threat.
for outcome in OUTCOMES:
    clustered_ols(
        f"{outcome} ~ {event_rhs}",
        d,
        event_columns
    )

# Treatment-intensity models:
#   fast_share_10pp coefficient is per 10 percentage points of actual use.
# FE-only is intentionally transparent. The second specification shows how
# conditioning on risk and workload changes the association.
#
# Do not interpret either specification automatically as a total causal effect:
# actual use, routing, learning, staffing, and workload can be jointly caused.
# FTE/routing variables may also be downstream of availability, so conditioning
# on them can change the estimand away from a total policy effect.
for outcome in OUTCOMES:
    print(f"\n=== TREATMENT-INTENSITY ASSOCIATION: {outcome} ===")
    for label, extra in [
        ("branch_and_calendar_FE", ""),
        ("plus_risk_and_workload", "+ high_complexity_pct + cases_per_fte")
    ]:
        clustered_ols(
            f"{outcome} ~ fast_share_10pp{extra} + "
            "C(branch) + C(cal_month, Treatment(reference=1))",
            d,
            ["fast_share_10pp"]
        )

print("""
FINAL INTERPRETATION WARNINGS
1. Availability is selected, and actual fast-path use changes over time.
2. Fraud-desk interference, routing, staffing, and case mix are incompletely observed.
3. Median and p90 are analyzed separately; neither equals an average-case effect.
4. Monthly quantiles lack the case-level information needed for reliable resampling.
5. CSAT response counts and nonresponse information are unavailable.
6. Five clusters cannot support dependable asymptotic confidence intervals.
7. These regressions organize descriptive evidence; they do not prove causality.
""")

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** By August, automated fast-path completion accounted for 25–50% of adopter cases; medians were lower than in March, but p90s remained 1.4–5.4 hours higher in all three adopters.
- **Suggestive:** Alpha and Beta’s early median improvements and short-run rework reductions exceeded untreated calendar changes, but Gamma’s differing trajectory, subsequent tail deterioration, and concurrent operational changes prevent a consistent dose-response interpretation.
- **Conditionally causal:** If credible matched counterfactual trends and adequate control of shared-queue effects could be established, post-adoption increases in specialist routing may reflect automation policy effects; that attribution would still not be a pure algorithmic effect.
- **Not supported:** “Automation reduces cycle time by X%,” a guaranteed customer benefit, or a specific causal return on expanding the system to Delta and Epsilon.
