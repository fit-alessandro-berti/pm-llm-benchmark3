## 1. Current-process diagnosis

### 1.1 What the artifacts say, one at a time

**Frequency DFG (5,000 cases).**
- The fast path is the backbone of the process. 3,600 cases (72%) go to the auto screen, 2,550 go straight from the auto screen to Fast approval, and 2,790 cases in total (about 56%) pass through Fast approval. 2,300 of those go on to Dispatch replacement.
- The evidence loop is large. Request missing evidence is entered 1,430 times (650 from the auto screen and 780 from manual review), and 890 of those go back to Manual eligibility review. That is a real loop: review, request, review again.
- Reopens come mostly from the fast path. Of the 510 complaint/reopen arcs, 400 start on the fast branch: 140 from Fast approval and 260 from Dispatch replacement. Only 110 start on the repair branch: 80 from Repair quote and 30 from Repair device.
- Reopen handling partly bypasses the rules. Of the 430 complaint outflows, 120 go to the Escalation board, and from there straight to Dispatch (65), Repair (45) or Denied (10). The DFG shows no Manual eligibility review in between, which is a structural R3 exposure.
- **The log is inconsistent and must be reconciled before anything is quantified:**
  - Only 3,625 cases reach END (3,015 closed, 220 denied, 390 abandoned). About 1,375 cases (27.5%) are either still open or were cut off by edge filtering.
  - Manual eligibility review has about 2,600 arcs in (1,400 from intake, 890 from evidence, 310 from reopen) but only 1,400 arcs out. No Manual review → Fast approval/Dispatch arc is shown, yet the segment table reports a "manual path" for accessories.
  - Dispatch replacement has 2,580 arcs out but only 2,365 in.
  - The alignments report "complaint/reopen after close", but there is no Close → Complaint arc.

  So the DFG is filtered and/or contains open cases. This matters because open cases are disproportionately the slow ones (repair, evidence loop, reopens). Slow-path cycle times are therefore probably understated, and slow-path complaints are probably under-observed.

**Performance DFG.**
- The fast path's working steps take hours: Auto screen → Fast approval has a median of 0.4 h, and Fast approval → Dispatch has a median of 1.8 h.
- The costly times on the slow paths are *waiting*, not work:
  - Request missing evidence → Manual review: mean 34 h, p90 88 h.
  - Repair quote → Customer approval: mean 30 h, p90 80 h.
  - Manual review → Repair quote: mean 22 h, p90 60 h. This wait is attributed to part-price confirmation.
- Fast-path failures show up late. Dispatch → Complaint has a median of 70 h and a p90 of 180 h. Complaint → Manual review then adds a mean of 40 h, and Complaint → Escalation a mean of 54 h. A fast-path case that reopens therefore typically ends up at well over 100–200 h end-to-end. The fast path's median cycle time hides this tail.

**Conformance checking.**
- Token replay gives a fitness of 0.91, but only 68% of traces fit. Those two numbers together mean the deviations are *systematic small skips* affecting about a third of cases, not chaotic behaviour. That pattern points to routing rules, not individual misconduct.
- The alignments locate the skips precisely:
  - Fast approval happens before evidence is complete (an R2 breach).
  - High-value devices are dispatched without risk review (R1, and R2 as well).
  - Claims reopen after close.
- The discovered model has low precision: low-frequency combinations of auto approval, risk review and repair are not allowed by policy. In other words, routing is under-controlled. The system permits paths nobody designed.
- The temporal profile adds two specific findings:
  - Manual review → Repair quote is too slow for Premium customers.
  - The Dispatch → Complaint long tail is linked to *incorrect replacement part selection*. That is a defect created at the dispatch decision, not at eligibility.

**Rework statistics.**
- 26% of cases that enter the evidence loop get at least two evidence requests.
- Complaint/reopen affects 9.2% of all cases (about 460), compared with 14.1% of Fast-approval cases (about 393). So the fast path produces roughly 85% of all reopened cases, while carrying 56% of volume.
- Rework repair affects 12.5% of repaired high-value devices, against 4.0% for low-risk devices.
- The cost figures matter most here. The fast path is 31% cheaper before rework and 9% cheaper after. Roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of its apparent saving is eaten by complaints, reshipments and service credits. That 9% is an *average across segments* and *excludes* compliance exposure.

**Segment outcomes.** The contradiction resolves differently in each segment:

| Segment | Fast vs. slow evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Premium low-risk accessory (18%) | 8 h vs. 46 h. Complaints 6% vs. 3%. Service credits apply beyond 24 h. | The manual path breaches the 24 h credit threshold at its *median*. The 3-point complaint gap × EUR 95 wrong-replacement cost ≈ at most EUR 3 per case. **Fast is correct here**, and R2 explicitly allows it. |
| Standard low-risk accessory (25%) | 13 h vs. 52 h. Complaints 8% vs. 4%. Customers tolerate 72 h with visibility. | The expected quality gain from manual review (≤ ~EUR 4 per case) is likely below a manual review's labour cost. However, R2 requires evidence completeness, so the fast path **as currently run** is non-compliant here. An *automated* completeness check is needed, not manual review. |
| Premium high-value (14%) | 20 h vs. 96 h. Complaints 22% vs. 5%. R1/R2 risk is high. | The fast path is non-compliant and generates about 4× the complaints. Wrong replacement costs EUR 410. The reviewed path's 96 h conflicts with Premium expectations. **Neither current path is acceptable.** |
| Standard high-value (21%) | 28 h vs. 120 h. Complaints 18% vs. 6%. Wrong replacement cost is high. | The quality and cost case for more control is strongest here, and time sensitivity is lower than for Premium. |
| Unclear defect / missing evidence (22%) | Median 140 h. Highest abandonment and repeat requests. | The problem is *missing information*, not path choice. Neither fast nor slow review fixes it. |

The segment list does not mention mid-risk devices separately, although the context says they exist. They are probably spread across these rows or across the unclear-defect group. Any redesign must classify them explicitly, for example by the EUR 400 R1 threshold.

**Resource information.**
- Manual review capacity is 7 × 36 = 252 productive hours per week, and the 82% knee is about 207 h per week. The auto-screen rule update is expected to add 15–35% to manual-review inflow. Without extra capacity or shorter reviews, baseline utilization would have to be at most about 71% (for +15%) or about 61% (for +35%) to stay below the knee. The data does not tell us current utilization; the observed queue times (Intake → Manual review median 5 h, p90 18 h) only suggest that some queueing already exists.
- Fraud/risk has 2 specialists and already sees 980 arcs in, peaking on Mondays and after marketing campaigns. Routing all high-value claims (35% of cases) there would roughly double its inflow or more.
- Quality inspectors are underused except after repair campaigns. This is the only obvious slack in the system.
- The Escalation board is effective for customers but does not scale, and it currently bypasses R3.

### 1.2 The core contradiction, stated explicitly

The fast path looks dominant on *first-pass* speed and unit cost. That view comes from measuring the wrong endpoints:

1. **Its cost advantage mostly disappears once rework is counted** (31% → 9%). The remaining 9% is an average that mixes accessories, where the fast path is plausibly still clearly cheaper, with high-value devices, where 18–22% complaint rates and EUR 410 wrong-replacement costs make it plausibly *more* expensive than review. That last point is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion.
2. **Its speed advantage is a median effect.** Its failures arrive 70–180 h later and then re-enter the most congested resource, manual review.
3. **Its compliance cost is not priced at all.** The R1/R2 skips are concentrated exactly where compliance risk is high: high-value devices.

The evidence also does **not** support "the slow path is better":

- The repair branch has its own failure points. Of about 570 arcs into Repair quote, 80 go to complaint and 70 more cases later abandon at Customer approval. The flattering 3.3% complaint rate is measured only on cases that *completed* Quote → Approval → Repair. That is a survivor-conditioned figure.
- Reviewed/repair-path cases finish later, so part of their post-completion complaint window may not yet be in the log. With a p90 complaint lag of 180 h, the slow path's complaint rates may be under-observed.
- Routing is not random. Reviewed cases differ from auto-approved ones, so part of the quality gap may be a selection effect.
- For Premium accessories, the slow path is simply worse: it breaches the service-credit threshold.

**Design implication:** the right question is not "fast or slow". It is *where in the flow a given risk (wrong part, ineligibility, fraud, missing information) is cheapest to detect, for which segment*. Every redesign below respects a shared compliance floor:
- no Fast approval above EUR 400 without a manual or risk review (R1);
- no dispatch without confirmed evidence completeness, except for Premium low-risk accessories (R2);
- every reopen passes Manual eligibility review before close or denial (R3).

The three redesigns differ in *how* they meet that floor and which failure mechanism they attack first.

## 2. Redesign A: Risk-tiered lanes with a verified-dispatch gate ("make the fast path safe")

### Redesigned flow

1. **Structured intake.** Segment, product value band, defect-type code, serial/model number and purchase proof become mandatory fields. These feed routing rather than being free text.
2. **Segment-aware auto screen v2.** This uses the two-week rule update, rewritten as a lane classifier (segment × value band × defect type × evidence completeness × risk score) instead of a single approve/refer rule.
   - **Lane 1, Instant (Premium low-risk accessory).** Auto approval, then immediate dispatch, using the R2 exception. There is no pre-dispatch gate, because the 24 h service-credit threshold makes any 6–18 h delay costly. Instead, manual reviewers audit a 5–10% post-dispatch sample during idle time, and the audit results feed back into the rules.
   - **Lane 2, Verified-fast (Standard low-risk accessory).** An *automated* evidence-completeness check (receipt matches serial, photo present, defect code valid) runs before auto approval, which satisfies R2 without manual labour. If evidence is complete, the claim is dispatched. If not, one consolidated evidence request goes out. Proactive status notifications use the customers' 72 h tolerance, conditional on visibility.
   - **Lane 3, Reviewed-fast (all claims above EUR 400 with a clear defect code; Premium and Standard high-value, plus mid-risk above the threshold).**
     - A *time-boxed light Manual eligibility review* with a checklist satisfies R1.
     - Fraud/risk review is used only when the risk score flags the claim (claim-frequency anomalies, serial/purchase mismatch, campaign periods). This protects the 2 specialists.
     - The claim is then Fast approved and passes a **pre-dispatch part-verification gate** staffed by the underused quality inspectors. They match serial number, model and configuration to the replacement SKU and confirm evidence completeness for R2.
     - Premium claims get priority in both queues.
   - **Lane 4, Diagnose (unclear defect, failed completeness, or risk flag on accessories).** Manual review, then the evidence, risk or repair branch, as today.
3. **Reopen routing is hard-wired.** Customer complaint/reopen always goes to Manual eligibility review (R3). The Escalation board is only reachable *from* manual review, never as a bypass to dispatch, repair or denial.

### Targets

- The 2,790 Fast-approval cases, especially the high-value part of them.
- The alignment deviations "Fast approval before evidence completeness" and "Dispatch without risk review for high-value".
- The Dispatch → Complaint long tail (260 arcs) that the temporal profile links to wrong part selection.
- The 120 escalation-board bypasses of R3.

### Mechanism of improvement

- **Moves defect detection from 70–180 h after dispatch to before dispatch.** Wrong replacements cost EUR 410 on high-value devices, plus reshipment, credits and a reopen through a congested queue. Suppose, for illustration only, that half of the 12–17-point complaint excess on high-value fast paths is wrong-part errors and the gate catches half of those. The avoided cost would then be in the order of EUR 12–18 per high-value case. A 15–30-minute inspector check costs about EUR 10–19 at EUR 38 per hour, so the gate is plausibly break-even or better *on high-value devices only*. On accessories (EUR 95 error cost) the same calculation is clearly negative, which is why accessories get no gate.
- **Replaces one generic fast path with segment-conditioned controls.** Premium accessories keep their speed, where the evidence says speed is worth more than a 3-point complaint gap. High-value claims get control, where the evidence says control is worth more than about 10–20 h.
- **Uses the only slack in the system**, the inspectors, instead of adding load to fraud specialists.
- **Makes compliance structural rather than behavioural.** Lane logic and dispatch-system hard stops should raise the share of fitting traces and remove the low-precision paths.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

- **High-value speed.** The Premium high-value median rises from about 20 h to perhaps about 30–45 h (light review plus a 6–18 h gate). That is still far below the 96 h reviewed path, but it is a visible slowdown for the segment with the strictest expectations.
- **Premium accessory quality.** It accepts a complaint rate of about 6% rather than 3% for Premium accessories.
- **The evidence loop and the 22% unclear-defect segment** are left essentially as they are.
- **The slow repair path** is left as it is: 22 h to quote, 30 h to customer approval.
- **Manual-review load.** Inflow grows by the expected 15–35% and is absorbed only if baseline utilization has headroom.
- **An unproven assumption.** It bets that wrong part selection is catchable at dispatch. If errors originate upstream (wrong model data at intake) or downstream (warehouse picking), the gate adds delay without benefit.

## 3. Redesign B: Diagnose-first repair lane for high-value devices ("make the good path fast")

### Redesigned flow

For claims above EUR 400 (both high-value segments, plus mid-risk above the threshold):

1. **Intake with pre-consent.** At intake, the customer chooses a remedy preference and can pre-authorise in-warranty, zero-cost repair ("proceed without asking me; notify me"). This is consent, not coercion: consumer-protection law usually lets the customer choose between repair and replacement unless one is disproportionate.
2. **Parallel split after intake:**
   - **Technical triage.** A technician diagnoses the fault remotely or from uploaded evidence and error logs, and names the failed component *and* part number.
   - **Eligibility review in parallel.** Light manual review, with Fraud/risk review only when the claim is flagged.

   Both branches join before the Technical decision, so R1 holds without serial waiting.
3. **Technical decision using a pre-priced repair catalog** (defect type × model × standard repair price). This removes the part-price confirmation wait that is the main cause of Manual review → Repair quote delay. Quotes outside the catalog go to the existing Repair quote step.
4. **Automatic customer approval** for pre-consented, in-catalog, zero-cost repairs. This removes the Repair quote → Customer approval wait (mean 30 h, p90 80 h). Other quotes get a reminder at 24 h and a callback before abandonment.
5. **Repair device, then a defect-specific Quality check.** High-value repairs get a full test script targeted at the defect types that drive the 12.5% rework rate. Low-risk repairs are checked by sampling.
6. **Replacement becomes the exception.** It happens when the diagnosis says the device is unrepairable, when the customer chooses it, or when repair lead time would breach the SLA. The replacement SKU comes from the technician's diagnosis, not from the intake description.
7. **Premium SLA fallback.** For Premium high-value, if the repair ETA exceeds a defined threshold (for example about 48–72 h), the case switches automatically to a diagnosis-verified replacement.

Accessories keep today's fast path, with only the compliance-floor fixes: an automated completeness check for Standard accessories and hard-wired R3 routing.

### Targets

Premium and Standard high-value devices (35% of cases), and the repair branch:
- Manual review → Repair quote (mean 22 h, p90 60 h; the Premium temporal deviation);
- Repair quote → Customer approval (mean 30 h);
- Customer approval → Claim abandoned (70);
- Repair quote → Complaint (80);
- Rework repair on high-value devices (12.5%).

### Mechanism of improvement

- **Keeps diagnosis, removes waiting.** The reviewed path owes its lower complaint rate (5–6% vs. 18–22%) to diagnosis and review. Most of its extra duration is waiting for part prices and customer responses. The redesign keeps the diagnosis and review and deletes those waits: serial steps become parallel, price confirmation becomes a catalog lookup, and approval becomes pre-consent.
- **Fixes wrong-part errors at their source.** It attacks wrong part selection by having a technician who has diagnosed the device specify the part. Redesign A tries to catch the error after it is made.
- **Addresses the reviewed path's own failure points**, which are complaints and abandonment at the quote stage, instead of treating that path as already good.
- **May reduce replacement volume** for devices where repair is adequate, lowering the hardware cost of replacement units. The extent is unknown from the data.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

- **Speed for customers who value it most.** Repair is inherently slower than a correct replacement. Even with the waits removed, high-value medians would plausibly fall only to the 48–80 h range, not to the fast path's 20–28 h. Premium high-value customers who value speed may prefer Redesign A.
- **Technician capacity.** The 3 technicians become the constraint, because triage and repair load both grow.
- **Legal and operational design work.** Pre-consent and the catalog require legal review and ongoing price maintenance.
- **Accessories and the unclear-defect segment** are not the focus: 65% of cases see only compliance-floor changes.
- **Rests on an untested assumption.** It assumes the reviewed path's quality advantage is causal and not a selection effect, which the data cannot confirm.

## 4. Redesign C: Evidence-complete intake, single case ownership and closed-loop recovery ("fix the information, not the path")

### Redesigned flow

1. **Guided, defect-specific intake.**
   - A dynamic checklist per defect type (photos/video, error codes, serial capture), with automatic lookup of purchase proof and model configuration from sales and registration data where available.
   - An evidence-completeness score is computed at intake.
   - **Channels:** the evidence portal, *plus* assisted capture by phone or at a retail counter for customers with low digital literacy, so the portal is never the only route.
   - Premium low-risk accessories are exempt from the extended checklist, keeping them under the 24 h service-credit threshold.
2. **Complete at intake.** The claim moves to routing (the current auto screen with only the R1/R2 hard stops added). The completeness flag persists, and the dispatch system refuses dispatch without it (R2 enforced by the system, with the Premium-accessory exception).
3. **Incomplete at intake: single case owner.** A named manual reviewer takes ownership:
   - They issue **one consolidated evidence request** listing *all* missing items, with a deadline.
   - Automated reminders go out at about 24 h and 48 h, and the owner calls the customer before a claim is abandoned.
   - When the evidence arrives, the *same owner* completes eligibility review immediately. This removes the re-queuing in Request missing evidence → Manual review (mean 34 h, p90 88 h) and the second and third requests behind the 26% repeat rate.
4. **Recovery desk for reopens.** A rotating group of manual reviewers with delegated authority up to a value threshold handles every Customer complaint/reopen, targeting first contact within 24 h. This performs the R3 manual review and resolves most cases.
5. **Escalation board repurposed.** It handles only disputes above the threshold, and it becomes a weekly root-cause forum. Reopen reason codes (wrong part, delay, repair failure, eligibility dispute) feed back into the auto-screen rules, the intake checklist and part-selection data.

### Targets

- **The unclear-defect / missing-evidence segment** (22%, median 140 h, highest abandonment).
- **The evidence loop:** 1,430 request arcs, 26% repeats, and 320 abandonments directly after an evidence request.
- **Reopen handling:** Complaint → Manual review (mean 40 h), Complaint → Escalation (mean 54 h) and the R3 bypass via the Escalation board.
- **Wrong part selection, indirectly**, where it originates in wrong model or serial data at intake.

### Mechanism of improvement

- **Removes the information deficit.** The evidence loop exists because requests are incremental, and each response re-enters a shared queue. Asking for everything up front, with assistance, and keeping one owner turns a loop into a single pass. The portal's expected 30–45% reduction in repeat requests would take the 26% repeat rate to roughly 14–18%, *if* abandonment is controlled through the assisted channel.
- **Improves data quality.** Better intake data (verified model and configuration) improves part selection for everyone downstream, including the fast path.
- **Keeps the escalation board's satisfaction effect without its scalability limit.** The recovery desk makes R3 compliance the default route instead of a detour.
- **Closes the learning loop the current process lacks.** Complaint reasons currently fall out of the system; here they update the routing rules.

### What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved

- **Longer intake for most customers.** Intake takes minutes longer, and some simple claims will feel over-questioned.
- **Less pooling in manual review.** Case ownership makes reviewers a less pooled resource, which hurts queue performance if utilization approaches 82%, and cases stall when owners are absent.
- **High cost and slow payback.** It needs integration effort (purchase and registration lookups, the portal, the dispatch hard stop), and its benefits appear more slowly than those of Redesign A.
- **R1 handled only with a plain hard stop.** Claims above EUR 400 go to manual review, which is quicker because the evidence packet is complete. There is still no pre-dispatch part check, so the high-value fast path's *dispatch* error is addressed only to the extent it comes from intake data.
- **Repair-path waits** (price confirmation, customer approval) remain.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

### Redesign A

- **Failure mode 1: capacity knee.**
  - *What goes wrong:* the auto-screen update adds 15–35% manual-review inflow while baseline utilization is already above about 61–71%. Utilization crosses 82% and queues grow non-linearly. Lane 3 "reviewed-fast" then becomes slower than the current reviewed path, and Premium high-value customers get the worst of both worlds.
  - *Mitigation:* time-box the light review; make the post-dispatch accessory audit the first work to pause when queues grow.
- **Failure mode 2: the gate catches nothing.** The temporal profile shows only that wrong replacements are *linked* to the long tail, not where they originate. If the errors come from bad model data at intake or from warehouse picking after the gate, reopen rates barely move, and the gate adds 6–18 h plus labour for nothing.
- **Failure mode 3: misclassification.** Wrong segment or value attributes let above-EUR-400 claims fall into accessory lanes, so R1 violations continue invisibly. Agents may also learn which codes avoid the gate.
- **Failure mode 4: fraud through the Instant lane.** A lane with no evidence check invites exploitation, and campaign periods amplify it.
- **Bottleneck migration.**
  - From the reopen queue to **Manual eligibility review**, which is front-loaded instead of back-loaded.
  - To the **quality inspectors**, who are only idle "except after repair campaigns". During campaigns the gate and repair QC compete for the same 2 people.
  - Secondary: to **Fraud/risk review** on Mondays if the risk-score threshold is set too sensitively.

### Redesign B

- **Failure mode 1: repair-queue congestion.** 3 technicians now handle triage, catalog exceptions and more repairs. Shifting even a few hundred high-value cases from replacement to repair can turn the removed quote wait into an equal or longer repair queue. The end-to-end time does not improve; the wait just moves.
- **Failure mode 2: the quality advantage was selection.** Reviewed and repaired cases may have been intrinsically easier or more clearly diagnosed. Applying repair-first to all high-value claims then gives complaint rates well above the 5–6% benchmark. Rework repair, already 12.5% for high-value, may rise with volume and the loop Quality check → Rework repair → Repair device grows.
- **Failure mode 3: low uptake or backlash.** Premium customers see repair as a downgrade or too slow and exercise their legal right to replacement. The pre-consent wording triggers disputes, and the catalog goes stale, which recreates quote exceptions.
- **Bottleneck migration.**
  - From Repair quote and Customer approval to **Repair device and Quality check**. Inspectors lose the slack that Redesign A relies on, so A and B conflict on the same resource if combined carelessly.
  - To **repair logistics** (pickup and return shipping), which is not visible in the current log at all.

### Redesign C

- **Failure mode 1: digital exclusion.** The portal raises abandonment among low-digital-literacy customers faster than the assisted channel catches them.
  - *Measurement risk:* abandonment *shortens* measured cycle time and cost, so the redesign could look like a success while it is actually losing customers.
- **Failure mode 2: loss of pooling.** Case ownership removes pooling. Near the 82% knee, dedicated owners with uneven workloads, absences or holidays cause cases to stall, and the 34 h wait returns under another name.
- **Failure mode 3: intake friction.** A longer intake adds time and effort to simple claims. If the Premium-accessory exemption is misconfigured, service credits rise.
- **Failure mode 4: recovery-desk overpayment or inconsistency.** Delegated authority leads to generous or inconsistent resolutions (a cost leak). Alternatively, removing the board from routine cases loses the strong satisfaction effect the board currently produces.
- **Bottleneck migration.**
  - From the evidence loop to the **intake and assisted-capture channel** (phone agents, retail counters), which is not staffed in the current resource model.
  - To **manual reviewers**, who now act as case owners and as the recovery desk at the same time.
  - To **IT integration**, since purchase and registration lookups become a single point of failure for intake.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

### Stage 0: Measurement hygiene and compliance floor (weeks 0–3; required before any pilot)

- **Reconcile the log.** Separate the 5,000 − 3,625 ≈ 1,375 cases without END into open cases and filtering artifacts. Recover the missing arcs: manual-review outflows (about 1,200), dispatch inflows, and Close → Reopen.
- **Define case-level outcomes with a fixed follow-up window.** Count a complaint as belonging to the case if it arrives within at least the p90 complaint lag (180 h) plus a margin, for example 10–14 days after close. Treat abandonment as a *failed* outcome, not a closure. Compare cohorts only with equal follow-up, or use survival or censoring-aware estimates.
- **Add complaint reason codes**: wrong part, delay, repair failure, eligibility dispute. Also record whether a wrong part came from intake data, the selection decision or picking. This is what later tells A, B and C apart.
- **Measure weekly manual-review utilization** against the 207 h knee. This decides whether A's rule update is safe.
- **Compliance floor.** Hard-wire R3 routing, and put an R1 hard stop on auto approval above EUR 400. Only *how* to satisfy the rules is piloted (light review vs. fraud review vs. parallel review), never *whether*. If R1 enforcement pushes manual review toward the knee, add temporary capacity rather than relaxing the rule.

### Stage 1, first pilot: verified-dispatch gate on Standard high-value replacements (weeks 3–9)

- **Why first:**
  - It has the highest error cost (EUR 410).
  - This segment is less time-sensitive than Premium (28 h fast median, no stated service-credit clock).
  - It uses idle inspector capacity, so it adds no new hiring.
  - It is cheap and reversible.
  - It tests the key causal assumption behind A, and partly behind B: *are wrong-part reopens catchable before dispatch?*
- **Design.** Randomize by case ID, or alternate days. The control group runs concurrently so that Monday and marketing-campaign effects hit both arms.
- **Metrics.**
  - Reopens within the fixed window, total and wrong-part-coded.
  - Median and p90 time added by the gate.
  - Inspector utilization and queueing during repair campaigns.
  - Cost per *resolved* case, including reshipments and service credits.
  - Alignment deviation rate for R2 in the pilot cohort.
- **Go:**
  - A clear relative reduction in wrong-part reopens (roughly one-third to one-half or more);
  - gate p90 at or below about 18 h;
  - net cost per resolved case neutral or lower.
- **Stop or redesign:**
  - reopen reduction below about 15–20%;
  - gate p90 above about 24 h;
  - or inspector queues forming.

  A stop points upstream, to data quality (Redesign C), or to diagnosis-based part selection (Redesign B).
- **Extension.** Extend to Premium high-value with priority handling only if the Premium end-to-end median stays well below the 96 h reviewed path (about 40 h or less) and Premium credits and complaints do not rise.

### Stage 2: Segment-aware auto screen in capacity-guarded waves (weeks 5–12)

- **Rollout order.**
  - Wave 1: Lane 3 routing for claims above EUR 400, the compliance-critical part.
  - Wave 2: the automated completeness check for Standard accessories.
  - Wave 3: Premium Instant-lane audit sampling.
- **Guardrails.** Hold or roll back a wave if either of these holds for more than 1–2 weeks:
  - manual-review utilization stays above about 80%;
  - Intake → Manual review p90 rises above about 1.5× baseline (about 27 h or more).
- **Monitor:**
  - share of fitting traces by segment (moving up from 68%);
  - model precision;
  - Premium-accessory median staying under about 12 h, with no increase in service credits;
  - Standard-accessory completion within 72 h, with status messages delivered.

### Stage 3: Evidence-complete intake pilot on unclear-defect claims (weeks 8–20)

- **Design.** Run the pilot for selected defect types, with the portal and the assisted channel offered side by side. Randomize at the level of defect type or region, and keep the Premium-accessory exemption active.
- **Metrics:**
  - repeat evidence requests (baseline 26%; target roughly 14–18%);
  - abandonment overall and by channel and digital-literacy proxy;
  - median and p90 cycle time for unclear claims (baseline 140 h median);
  - case-owner workload variance;
  - downstream wrong-part reopens, which show whether better intake data reduces them.
- **Go:** repeat requests fall by at least about 25–30%, *and* abandonment does not rise in any subgroup.
- **Stop:**
  - abandonment rises by more than about 2–3 points in any subgroup, even if average cycle time improves;
  - or owner-level stalls exceed the pooled-queue baseline.

### Stage 4: Repair-lane components, least customer-facing first (weeks 12–26)

- **Order:**
  1. Pre-priced catalog (internal only). Measure Manual review → Repair quote time and the frequency of catalog exceptions.
  2. Parallel triage and eligibility review.
  3. Pre-consent for Standard high-value in one product family.
  4. The Premium SLA fallback, last.
- **Metrics:**
  - technician utilization;
  - repair-path median and p90 (baseline 96–120 h);
  - rework-repair rate (baseline 12.5%; stop if it trends above about 15%);
  - quote-stage complaints and abandonment (baseline 80 and 70);
  - customer uptake of repair vs. replacement.
- **Go:** repair-path p90 falls meaningfully, *without* technician queue time replacing the removed waits.

### Sequencing logic

1. **Compliance and measurement first.** The rules are not optional, and without a fixed complaint window every later comparison is biased toward the fast path.
2. **Cheapest, most reversible, highest expected value next**, with no new capacity needed: the gate on Standard high-value claims.
3. **Measure capacity before adding load.** The auto-screen change waits until Stage 0 has established manual-review headroom.
4. **Change one mechanism per segment at a time.** This keeps the results attributable; for example, the gate and the repair lane are never started on the same segment at once.
5. **Let early results decide the later investments.** If the gate works, Redesign B's case for Premium high-value weakens and B narrows to Standard high-value. If the gate fails because errors come from intake data, Redesign C moves ahead of B.

### Distinguishing real improvement from shifted problems

- **Measure end-to-end per case.** Include reopens after close, repeat reopens and abandonment as failures. Never judge a pilot on edge-level medians such as the Fast approval → Dispatch time.
- **Use concurrent controls.** Compare against randomized or matched controls (segment × defect type × week), and use difference-in-differences against segments that did not change. This separates the effect from Monday peaks, campaigns and seasonal mix.
- **Keep a bottleneck ledger.** Track queue time and utilization for *every* resource each week: manual review, fraud/risk, technicians, inspectors, the escalation board or recovery desk, and the assisted channel. An improvement that moves waiting time from one resource to another is a shifted bottleneck, not a gain.
- **Re-run conformance checking on pilot cohorts.** Track alignment cost, the share of fitting traces per segment and model precision, so that faster flows are not bought with new policy skips.
- **Use total cost per resolved case**, including service credits, reshipments, wrong-replacement write-offs and delegated-authority payouts, measured by segment. The current 9% average is exactly the kind of number that hides this.
- **Watch for gaming and mix shift.** Monitor the share of cases coded "unclear", above EUR 400, or Premium before and after each change. Cases relabelled to avoid a gate or a hard stop would create an apparent improvement while moving risk elsewhere.
- **Judge tails as well as averages.** Report p90 alongside median and mean, because the business damage in this process (service credits after 24 h for Premium, reopen lags up to 180 h, 140 h unclear-defect cases) is in the tails.
