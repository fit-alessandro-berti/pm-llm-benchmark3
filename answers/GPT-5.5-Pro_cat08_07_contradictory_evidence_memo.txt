# 1. Executive memo

**Recommendation: choose Alternative E — a segmented hybrid rollout with controls and measurement.** Do not scale Redesign R nationally as currently configured, and do not fully roll back. The pilot created real value for simple, low-risk house installations, but it also shifted risk into apartments, old buildings, manual activation, second visits, complaints, and long-tail cases. Management should scale the parts that are working for the right segment, pause the parts creating downstream failure, and run targeted experiments before broader rollout.

Redesign R achieved two important operational goals: **median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days**, and **cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%**. The segment evidence shows that simple low-risk houses benefited strongly: median duration improved by 6 days, p90 improved by 8 days, first-time-right improved by 3 percentage points, and complaints fell by 1 percentage point. That is not noise to ignore. For this segment, the merged validate/schedule activity and coordinator ownership appear directionally sound.

However, the same redesign worsened the outcomes that matter most for risk, customer trust, and avoidable cost. **p90 cycle time rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 rose from 45.0 to 62.0 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation doubled from 7% to 15%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%.** Estimated total cost including complaints and revisits is worse, even though visible pre-complaint cost improved. This means the redesign may be accelerating easy cases while converting harder cases into expensive failures.

The process-mining evidence supports that interpretation. The pilot model has higher fitness but much lower precision: many observed cases follow the simplified coordinator path, but the discovered model also permits too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, billing start, and complaints. Alignment diagnostics show recurring deviations that are operationally serious: billing before successful activation, repeated installation visits without field survey, missing permit checks for older apartments, and complaint escalation after billing start. These are not merely modeling artifacts; they map directly to worsened first-time-right, manual activation, second visits, billing disputes, and customer complaints.

The organizational evidence also argues against scaling R unchanged. Coordinator ownership reduced handovers, but coordinator utilization reached 93% in two regions. The network became simpler but more centralized, reducing resilience when coordinators were absent. Field-survey utilization fell while second-visit utilization rose to 92%, suggesting work was not eliminated so much as displaced to later, more disruptive stages.

The recommended path is therefore selective: **scale coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule only for simple low-risk houses; suspend survey skipping for medium-risk apartments and old permit-sensitive buildings; reinstate a hard control that billing start cannot occur before successful activation except under explicitly governed exceptions; and run controlled measurement before further expansion.**

The key trade-off is speed versus reliability. Redesign R improves flow for simple cases, but in riskier segments it appears to trade early speed and fewer handovers for late rework, customer dissatisfaction, and unresolved technical or permit problems. The right decision is not “scale” or “rollback”; it is **segment the operating model and make the risky shortcuts auditable before scaling them.**

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.**
The pilot model fits observed traces well because many cases follow the new simplified coordinator path. Fitness rises when the model can replay what happened. Precision falls because the same discovered model permits too many extra combinations: skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, billing start, permit absence, and complaint escalation. In business terms, the pilot created a dominant fast path but weakened guardrails around exceptions.

**Improved median but worse tail duration.**
The median improved because simple and straightforward cases move faster through merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers. The tail worsened because riskier cases encounter late failures: missed surveys, missing permit checks, failed visits, manual activations, second visits, complaints, and overloaded coordinators. The redesign compressed the center of the distribution while stretching the right tail.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.**
Coordinator ownership reduced formal handovers and simplified the handover-of-work network. But the working-together network became centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. When coordinators were highly utilized or absent, case context was not shared, so cases stalled. Fewer handovers improved accountability in normal flow but reduced redundancy in exception flow.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.**
Visible pre-complaint cost fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161, likely because fewer surveys and fewer handovers reduced early effort. But estimated full cost worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207, while complaints, manual activation, and second visits increased. This suggests cost was shifted downstream from prevention to remediation. The estimate is incomplete because complaint-handling data after week 4 is excluded.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings.**
Simple low-risk houses need less diagnostic protection, so survey skipping and merged scheduling reduce delay without much downside. Apartments and old buildings have more hidden installation, activation, access, and permit risks. For those segments, skipping surveys and missing permit checks remove controls that previously prevented second visits, activation failures, complaints, and long-tail delays.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: choose E, with C-style controls embedded.**

**Immediate action:**
Adopt a segmented rollout. Use the Redesign R fast path only for **simple low-risk houses**, where the evidence shows faster median and p90 duration, better first-time-right, and fewer complaints.

**What to pause:**
Pause **survey skipping** for apartment medium-risk, old permit-sensitive, business, and rural long-distance cases until the building-risk rule is validated. Pause or tightly restrict **post-billing activation checks**. Billing should not start before successful activation except under explicit exception approval, reason code, and monitoring.

**What to scale:**
Scale **coordinator ownership** selectively, but add resilience controls: backup coordinator coverage, shared case notes, workload caps, absence handoff rules, and monitoring where coordinator utilization approaches the 90% range. Scale the **combined validate/schedule** step for simple low-risk houses.

**What to measure next:**
Prioritize M6, M1, M7, M4, and M3: validate the risk rule, randomize survey skipping by stratum, monitor activation and permit conformance, capture 60-day downstream cost, and collect structured reason codes for failures and complaints. Add M2 and M5 to understand bottlenecks and coordinator resilience.

**Threshold for broader rollout:**
Broader rollout should require evidence that, within each major segment, R or its controlled variant does not worsen p90/p95 duration, first-time-right, complaints, second visits, manual activation, SLA breach, or full 60-day cost beyond agreed tolerances. At minimum, management should require: no systematic billing-before-activation deviations, no missing permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings, coordinator utilization below overload levels or protected by backup ownership, and demonstrated net benefit including downstream complaint and revisit cost.
