## 1. What is observed
Automation availability changes at the branch-month level: Alpha and Beta switch to `automation=1` in April 2026, Gamma in July 2026, while Delta and Epsilon remain unavailable through August 2026. That availability indicator is not the same thing as actual use. Actual treatment intensity, better proxied by `automated_fast_path_pct`, starts low and rises gradually: adopters move from roughly 4-6% before rollout to about 25-50% after rollout, so the post period mixes light and heavier use.

Rollout was targeted rather than random. Alpha and Beta were chosen because they had high volumes and poor historical cycle times, and Gamma followed after a readiness pilot. Several other operational changes occur at nearly the same time: a centralized fraud desk begins in March 2026; routing shifts about 10% more arrivals to Alpha and Beta from April onward and about 5% more to Gamma after July; Alpha loses productive FTE in May, Beta gains one in April, Gamma loses one in June, and Delta gains one in June; and a May product change raises `high_complexity_pct` in multiple branches. Managers may also have changed local practices during rollout.

Outcomes are observed only as branch-month aggregates. Medians and p90s do not show which cases were automated or where queues formed. `rework_pct` and `escalation_pct` may reflect process substitution rather than quality. `csat_1to5` is especially limited because response counts are unavailable and response rates vary materially.

## 2. Causal estimands

## 3. Identification strategy
| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cohort-specific comparative interrupted time series / stacked event study with transparent windows | Separate April 2026 cohort (Alpha, Beta) and July 2026 cohort (Gamma); estimate event time within cohort | Delta and Epsilon as never-treated controls, plus Gamma only as not-yet-treated through June 2026 | Branch effects, calendar-month effects, `load_per_fte`, `high_complexity_pct`, explicit early-versus-late post indicators | E1-E5, primarily availability/package effects | Absent rollout, treated branches would have experienced similar within-period shifts as controls after conditioning on observed load and complexity; no large untreated branch-specific shocks perfectly aligned with rollout; limited interference | Plot pre-trends, leave-one-control-out, placebo rollout dates, compare early versus late post, inspect load/complexity discontinuities | Residual time-varying confounding from routing, staffing, fraud-desk congestion, and manager behavior |
| Adopter-specific synthetic control or synthetic DiD | One intervention date per adopter/cohort | Weighted combination of untreated branches and not-yet-treated Gamma when valid | Pre-rollout outcomes, load, complexity | E1-E2 primarily | Weighted donor combination approximates treated branch counterfactual | Pre-fit quality, placebo donors, donor-weight stability | Too few donors and too short a pre-period; overfitting risk is high |
| Branch-level interrupted time series with matched control slopes | Branch-specific break at rollout date with control-adjusted level/slope change | Delta and Epsilon, or Gamma pre-July for April cohort | Pre-period slope, `load_per_fte`, `high_complexity_pct` | E1-E4 | No coincident treated-only shock at the breakpoint beyond rollout | Breakpoint placebo tests, pre-slope similarity, residual autocorrelation checks | May attribute May product/routing/staffing shocks to automation |
| Availability-as-instrument for realized intensity | First stage at rollout date; second stage on `automated_fast_path_pct` | Untreated and treated months jointly | Same as above | E6-E7 | Exclusion restriction: availability affects outcomes only through realized intensity, not through staffing, routing, learning, or local practice changes | First-stage strength, over-time monotonicity of intensity | Exclusion is likely violated, so IV is hard to defend here |

The primary strategy should be the cohort-specific comparative interrupted time series / stacked event-study design, not a naive two-way fixed-effects regression. There are only three adopters, two adoption dates, and clear treatment heterogeneity over event time because actual use ramps gradually. A cohort-specific design keeps the comparisons explicit: Alpha/Beta are compared mainly to Delta/Epsilon, with Gamma usable only while still untreated; Gamma is compared mainly to Delta/Epsilon after July. That avoids using already treated branches as controls in ways that can create negative-weight or contamination problems.

This design is also honest about the estimand. Given routing changes, staffing changes, fraud-desk spillovers, and manager discretion, the most defensible target is a short-run branch-level package effect of availability, not a pure algorithmic effect. Comparative ITS lets the analyst show immediate versus later post effects, which matters because learning appears gradual and tail behavior may differ from median behavior. Synthetic control is a useful robustness check, but with only two never-treated donors and a short pre-period it is fragile. An IV for intensity is conceptually appealing but likely fails the exclusion restriction because rollout changed more than just the share of automated fast-path cases.

## 4. Process-aware causal mechanisms
| mechanism | process artifact or metric affected | expected signature if beneficial | expected signature if harmful | evidence in supplied data | unresolved ambiguity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fast-path substitution | `automated_fast_path_pct`, `median_cycle_h` | Fast-path share rises and median falls or at least resists demand pressure | Fast-path share rises with little cycle benefit, suggesting only easy-case relabeling | Adopters rise from roughly 4-6% to 25-50%; Gamma median falls from 45.9 in June to 38.4/36.0 in July/August | Fast-path share may increase because only easy cases are automated; no case-level eligibility data |

Interpretation: relative to concurrent untreated branches, adopters look better than their raw pre/post changes alone suggest, because untreated branches also deteriorate after the May complexity shift. That is suggestive, not definitive.

3. Treatment-intensity analysis. A descriptive branch-and-month-adjusted model with `load_per_fte` and `high_complexity_pct` yields approximately these slopes per +10 percentage points of `automated_fast_path_pct`: `median_cycle_h` about -0.62 hours, `p90_cycle_h` about -1.01 hours, `rework_pct` about -0.24 pp, `escalation_pct` about +0.12 pp, and `csat_1to5` about +0.01. These are not causal estimates because intensity is endogenous. A transparent operational cross-check is that from first post month to August, intensity rises while median improves in all three adopters, but p90 improves clearly only in Gamma; Alpha and Beta still carry elevated tails.

4. Pre-trends and regression to the mean. In the observed pre-period, Alpha's median trend is about -0.5 h/month, Beta's about +0.5 h/month, and Delta/Epsilon's combined April-cohort control trend only about +0.15 h/month. There is no strong common pre-improvement that would mechanically explain an April post-drop. For Gamma, the pre-July median trend is about +2.1 h/month, but Delta/Epsilon over the same January-June window worsen by about +2.0 h/month too, consistent with the May complexity shock. That weakens a simple regression-to-the-mean story inside the observed panel, but targeted rollout based on earlier poor performance still leaves regression to the mean as a serious external concern.

5. Sensitivity / bounded conclusions.

| outcome | arithmetic spec range | conservative decision range |
|---|---|---|
| `median_cycle_h` | Cohort-level comparative arithmetic is about -3.9 h to -4.5 h across reasonable control sets/windows | For a rollout decision, treat the median effect as somewhere between 0 and -5 h; a guaranteed universal reduction is not established |
| `p90_cycle_h` | Cohort-level comparative arithmetic is about -6.1 h to -7.1 h, but Alpha/Beta raw post p90 rises about +9.7 to +9.8 h | For a new rollout, tail effect could range from modest improvement to short-run worsening; a practical scenario interval is roughly -7 h to +3 h depending on downstream queue congestion |
| `csat_1to5` | Comparative arithmetic is around +0.08 to +0.09 | Because response counts are missing, a safer interval is about -0.05 to +0.10; treat CSAT as inconclusive |

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences
| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for poor historical performance, not randomly | Ambiguous: regression to the mean can exaggerate benefit; persistent difficulty can understate it | Add longer pre-history and adoption criteria; placebo rollouts on prior months | Any single effect size may be mostly selection artifact |
| Nonparallel trends | Branches differ in baseline level, slope, and demand mix | Ambiguous | Plot longer pre-trends; use cohort-specific controls and leave-one-control-out checks | Comparative estimates become unstable and design-dependent |
| Time-varying confounding | Routing campaign, product complexity shock, and local practice changes align with rollout | Could create false benefit or false harm | Weekly load, product, and routing audit; explicit covariate adjustment | Availability effect cannot be isolated from simultaneous operations changes |
| Treatment intensity endogeneity | `automation=1` does not equal actual automated handling; intensity ramps with learning and case selection | Usually exaggerates apparent benefits of actual use if easy cases are automated first | Record case eligibility, actual route, overrides, and saturation policy | Intensity models do not identify algorithm effect |
| Spillovers through fraud desk | Automated triage may shift borderline work into an unobserved shared queue | Can make local branches look better while moving delay elsewhere, or worsen treated branches selectively | Collect fraud-desk queue times, backlog, and branch-of-origin | Branch-level outcome gains may be redistribution, not capacity creation |
| Staffing changes | Alpha loses FTE, Beta gains FTE, Gamma loses FTE, Delta gains FTE | Understaffed adopters can make automation look worse; staffed adopters can make it look better | Weekly staffing by role, overtime, absenteeism | Effect estimates confound technology with labor capacity |
| Case-mix change | `high_complexity_pct` rises after the May product change | Can make rollout look worse even if process improves | Finer risk/product categories and stable coding audits | Estimated effect may be mostly composition, not process |
| Anticipation / readiness effects | Gamma had a readiness pilot; managers may change behavior before go-live | Can shift part of the effect into pre-period, attenuating post estimates | Look for pre-go-live jumps in overrides, routing, staffing, or queue behavior | Understates or mis-times treatment effect |
| Survey nonresponse | CSAT response counts are unavailable and response rates vary 20-45% | Direction unknown | Capture invitations, responses, and respondent composition by branch-month | CSAT should not drive expansion decisions alone |
| Measurement aggregation | Branch-month medians and p90s hide queue-level and case-level heterogeneity | Can mask harm to specific case types or queues | Keep case-level timestamps and queue-state snapshots | Process-mining inference from aggregates may be misleading |
| Small number of branches | Only five clusters, three adopters, two adoption dates | Makes uncertainty understated and estimates fragile | Randomization inference, permutation checks, and transparent arithmetic ranges | Conventional clustered inference is unreliable |
| Dynamic treatment effects | Learning may produce early harm and later benefit | Averaging across post months can dilute or invert conclusions | Event-time plots and separate early/late post windows | A single average effect can be policy-misleading |
| Treatment-version heterogeneity | Availability may come with different local workflows, override rules, and escalation thresholds | Ambiguous | Record local SOPs, override policy, and model-version changes | One branch's effect may not transport to another |

## 8. Rollout recommendation
expand with an embedded evaluation

Delta should be next, not Delta and Epsilon simultaneously. Delta has the lower baseline complexity, the June 2026 FTE increase, and a cleaner operational profile, so it is the safer place to learn whether automation creates real capacity rather than simply shifting queueing. Hold Epsilon back initially as a concurrent control. If leadership can tolerate randomization, randomize the start order between Delta and Epsilon; if not, use Delta first and precommit to an Epsilon decision rule before seeing Delta results.

Inside the next branch, do not jump immediately to full saturation. Use a phased assignment mechanism among eligible cases: for example 50% automation eligibility for 4 weeks, then 80-90% for another 4 weeks if guardrails hold. That creates both an availability contrast and an intensity contrast, which is much more informative than a simple branch switch. Minimum measurement should be 8 weeks pre and 8-12 weeks post per phase, with weekly data retained even if leadership ultimately wants monthly summaries.

Collect case-level process-mining and queue-state data that are currently missing: eligibility flag, actual route, manual override, specialist-review entry/exit, fraud-desk entry/exit and wait time, number of review loops, reopen-after-close, product/risk class, arrival channel, and staffing by role and shift. Monitor spillovers weekly at the fraud desk and at all other branches, because downstream congestion is a plausible mechanism.

Guardrails should stop escalation of rollout if weekly p90 is more than 5 hours above holdout or synthetic expectation for 2 consecutive weeks, median is more than 2 hours above expectation, `rework_pct` rises more than 1 pp, `csat_1to5` falls more than 0.05, or fraud-desk backlog/age rises more than 15%. Scale to Epsilon only if Delta shows either at least a 3-hour median gain with no tail harm, or stable median plus lower rework and no guardrail breach.

def build_design(data: pd.DataFrame, treatment_col: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    X = pd.DataFrame({"intercept": np.ones(len(data))}, index=data.index)
    X[treatment_col] = data[treatment_col].astype(float)
    X["load_per_fte"] = data["load_per_fte"].astype(float)
    X["high_complexity_pct"] = data["high_complexity_pct"].astype(float)
    X = pd.concat(
        [
            X,
            pd.get_dummies(data["branch"], prefix="branch", drop_first=True, dtype=float),
            pd.get_dummies(data["month_str"], prefix="month", drop_first=True, dtype=float),
        ],
        axis=1,
    )
    return X


def ols_cluster(data: pd.DataFrame, outcome: str, treatment_col: str, cluster_col: str = "branch"):
    """
    Descriptive FE-like panel regression with branch-clustered sandwich variance.
    This does NOT prove causality; causal interpretation requires assumptions such as:
    - no omitted time-varying confounding after conditioning on included controls,
    - limited interference through shared queues such as the fraud desk,
    - comparable treatment versions across branches,
    - no strong anticipation effects.
    """
    X = build_design(data, treatment_col)
    y = data[outcome].to_numpy(dtype=float)
    Xmat = X.to_numpy(dtype=float)
    beta = np.linalg.pinv(Xmat.T @ Xmat) @ (Xmat.T @ y)
    resid = y - Xmat @ beta

    groups = data[cluster_col].to_numpy()
    bread = np.linalg.pinv(Xmat.T @ Xmat)
    meat = np.zeros((Xmat.shape[1], Xmat.shape[1]))
    for g in pd.unique(groups):
        idx = groups == g
        Xg = Xmat[idx]
        ug = resid[idx][:, None]
        meat += Xg.T @ (ug @ ug.T) @ Xg

    vcv = bread @ meat @ bread
    G = data[cluster_col].nunique()
    N = len(data)
    K = Xmat.shape[1]
    if G > 1 and N > K:
        vcv *= (G / (G - 1)) * ((N - 1) / (N - K))

    se = np.sqrt(np.clip(np.diag(vcv), 0, None))
    out = pd.DataFrame({"coef": beta, "cluster_se": se}, index=X.columns)
    return out


for outcome in outcomes:
    panel = ols_cluster(df, outcome, "automation")
    print(f"\n=== Descriptive panel model: {outcome} ~ automation + controls + branch FE + month FE ===")

def did_change(treated, controls, pre_months, post_months, outcome):
    t_pre = df[df["branch"].isin(treated) & df["month_str"].isin(pre_months)][outcome].mean()
    t_post = df[df["branch"].isin(treated) & df["month_str"].isin(post_months)][outcome].mean()
    c_pre = df[df["branch"].isin(controls) & df["month_str"].isin(pre_months)][outcome].mean()
    c_post = df[df["branch"].isin(controls) & df["month_str"].isin(post_months)][outcome].mean()
    return (t_post - t_pre) - (c_post - c_pre)

## 10. Final claim ladder
- Observed: Automation availability increased actual automated-fast-path completion substantially, while adopter branches simultaneously experienced higher volume, more complexity, and more specialist escalation.
- Suggestive: Relative to concurrent untreated branches, adopters appear to have avoided roughly 4 hours of median deterioration and 6-7 hours of p90 deterioration, with the clearest improvement pattern in Gamma after the July 2026 pilot-to-rollout step.
- Conditionally causal: Under a cohort-specific comparative ITS assumption with adequate control for load, complexity, and fraud-desk spillovers, branch-level availability of automation likely improves median performance modestly and may reduce rework, but does not guarantee better tail performance.
- Not supported: A universal statement such as "automation reduces cycle time by X%" or "automation improves CSAT" is not defensible from these data because rollout was endogenous, treatment intensity changed over time, downstream spillovers were unmeasured, and CSAT suffers from unknown survey selection.
