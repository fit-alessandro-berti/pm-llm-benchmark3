# 1. System boundary and assumptions

Use a lifecycle screening boundary from material and spare-part supply through delivery of a usable part, including subsequent damage returns, replacement shipments and inventory disposal. The functional outcome is a complete, accepted order that meets its agreed service requirement. Report environmental impact per such order, by segment, alongside absolute totals for the original order cohort. Include resources consumed by cancelled, unfinished and failed orders in those totals; otherwise, declining service could appear to improve sustainability.

Inside the boundary are production and inspection electricity, setup and relevant idle energy, material scrap, packaging and packaging rework, warehousing and obsolete stock, all outbound shipment legs, empty vehicle returns attributable to the service, customer returns and replacement production. Include upstream material and packaging impacts when credible factors become available. Track customer downtime as a separate consequence: a transport saving that prolongs a plant shutdown is not automatically a lifecycle improvement.

The supplied log supports operational energy, transport emissions and some material accounting, not a complete lifecycle carbon footprint. Electricity mix, material composition, embodied emissions, shipment weight and distance, vehicle utilization, packaging quantities by variant, returns and disposal outcomes remain uncertain. Keep kWh, kgCO2e and material kg separate until consistent conversion factors and boundaries are available. Check whether `transport_kgco2e` already includes backhauls or returns before adding them. Allocate shared batch energy and shipment emissions once across their participating items and orders.

Existing buildings, equipment manufacture and the customer's wider production system are outside the initial quantified comparison. If the redesign requires additional machines, vehicles or inventory infrastructure, assess their incremental impacts before claiming a lifecycle benefit. Do not assume an environmental credit for avoided downtime without evidence.

Important interpretation limits are:

- Orders contain multiple items, so per-order figures can conceal different quantities, weights, production routes and shipment legs. Add line-item and shipment links before implementing detailed dispatch rules.
- DFG edges count transitions, including repeats; they are not independent cases or causal effects. Terminal counts do not reconcile to all 12,000 orders, and cancellation-edge counts also differ. Investigate open cases, missing events and extraction rules before calculating intervention effects.
- Selected variants cover 89% of orders. Evaluate the remaining paths, emergency combinations and disruption periods too. Low discovered-model precision calls for checking observed traces and exception semantics, rather than forcing all cases into a rigid normal path.
- Machine and route comparisons are observational. Control for part specification, weight, destination, urgency and operating load. The 6% not shipped within 14 days is an obsolescence warning, not proof that all those items are waste.

# 3. Intervention options

**I1. Deadline-aware routing with priority picking for stock contracts — S1/S4.** Target Standard-stock Service-contract orders, especially the 22% flagged for SP1 violations. At Availability check, estimate completion under each route using remaining pick, pack and carrier times. Use priority pick plus ground when its calibrated on-time probability exceeds 85% and satisfies the applicable service requirement; retain air when slack or reliability is insufficient. This removes avoidable air emissions without making every contract order slower. Priority picking can displace Regular orders, so bound the priority workload. Monitor pick queues, decision-time slack, `route_mode`, `expedite_flag`, actual delivery and both segments' late rates. The stock air and ground variants have different observed lateness, so feasibility must be demonstrated rather than inferred from their average times.

**I2. Deadline-bounded dynamic production and heat-treatment batches — S2.** Target compatible Made-to-order parts on Batch wait → CNC_A and required heat-treatment paths. Combine confirmed demand and release a batch when its fill target, maximum waiting cap or earliest latest-start deadline is reached. Calculate latest start with downstream production, inspection, packaging and transport uncertainty included. CNC_A's observed 2.8 to 1.3 kWh per item illustrates the opportunity, but the associated median wait rises from 4 to 38 hours. Heat batches above ten items are efficient, while their waiting cases have 1.7 times the late risk. Urgent cases bypass waiting when necessary; do not produce speculative extras merely to fill a batch. Monitor batch energy, all waiting stages, expedite transitions, lateness and aging stock.

**I3. Conditional assignment from CNC_B to CNC_A — S3.** Target qualified, interchangeable Made-to-order machine/part combinations, particularly CNC_B → Inspection → Rework → ground. Prefer CNC_A only when its queue and full downstream route fit the deadline. Its observed rework rate is 9%, versus 18% on CNC_B; the rework-heavy selected path also has 32 kWh/order and 1.8 kg scrap/order. Avoiding failed production can outweigh a small processing-time saving. Preserve required heat treatment and other product specifications. Retain a qualified CNC_B fallback to avoid concentrating all capacity and resilience on CNC_A. Monitor comparable part-family first-pass yields, queue times, good-output energy, scrap, late delivery and backup readiness.

**I4. Scheduled consolidation for suitable stock orders — S5.** Target non-urgent Standard-stock orders with sufficient slack, initially Regular customers. Test twice-daily consolidated-truck departures against actual cutoff and destination schedules. Consolidation can reduce transport emissions by 35–55% relative to individual ground parcels, but the observed consolidation wait is 12–24 hours. Use it only when the departure and delivery fit the promise, with ordinary ground as a timely fallback. A truck departure with poor utilization may lose the benefit. Monitor Package standard → Consolidated truck, departure waiting, shipment utilization, delivered emissions, p90 cycle time and missed promises; retain alternative routes for any participating Service-contract orders.

**I5. Check packaging, geometry and route compatibility before packing — S6.** Target both packaging-rework branches and subsequent damage-return paths. Select a feasible route before packaging and validate part geometry, weight and packaging against that route; revalidate if the route changes. Enforce Q1 for each Made-to-order part over 8 kg: reinforced packaging unless inspection certifies low damage risk. Compatible, qualified packaging prevents discarded packs, repeated handling and replacement shipments. Correcting existing Q1 violations may increase packaging mass while reducing total lifecycle damage. Monitor packaging-loop repetitions, `package_material_kg`, route changes, inspection certificates and mature damage returns. The observed 40% reduction in damage returns supports retaining protection for heavy parts, not doubling packaging for every product.

**I6. Improve inspection capacity and targeted quality gates — S7.** Target high-rework machine/part combinations and rushed Inspection_cell paths. Correct setup or tooling causes, introduce risk-based first-piece or in-process checks, and protect enough inspection capacity to complete required checks without rushing. Detecting defects earlier prevents repeated CNC/heat treatment, packaging waste and customer replacements. Inspection itself adds energy and time; an understaffed gate could become the next bottleneck. Give urgent orders timely access without omitting required tests. Monitor queue and inspection duration, defect type, first-pass yield, Inspection → Rework production loops, downstream packaging failures and customer returns. Validate net good-output energy and scrap, not just a lower recorded rework rate.

**I7. Differentiate urgency and qualify shared emergency transport — S8/S9.** Target Critical-downtime orders, separating evidenced current downtime from precautionary urgency while respecting each customer's contractual need. Preserve rapid dedicated vans when necessary. Shared emergency lanes or feasible backhauls can reduce empty running, but qualify them against response time, delivery reliability, capacity and packaging requirements before replacing a van. The dedicated-van variant has 3% lateness versus 9% for the critical stock/ground exception: the cheaper-emission path is not an acceptable blanket replacement. Monitor decision-time urgency evidence, latest acceptable arrival, van justification, shared-route stops, empty kilometers, actual emissions and downtime. Keep a deployable alternative; missing SP2 documentation in 31% of van cases does not establish that those journeys were unnecessary.

**I8. Offer feasible lower-impact promises to Regular MTO customers — S10.** Before accepting the order, offer delivery options that fit efficient production and ground/consolidated transport, provided they meet the customer's actual need-by date. Obtain explicit agreement; retain a faster option where needed. This can reduce emergency production, rushed inspection and air shipment, but increases customer waiting or planning demands. Monitor acceptance, Receive order-to-delivery time, original and revised promises, customer need-by dates, cancellations and actual route/machine choices. A lower late rate obtained solely by extending promises is not an environmental or service improvement.

**I9. Make availability checks and partial shipments deadline-aware — S11.** Target Stock → Partial shipment → ground + backorder, with its 79-hour median, 24% lateness and 7.2 kgCO2e/order. Reserve available lines, verify the completion date for missing lines, and hold for one shipment only when complete delivery remains timely. When one essential line restores operation or a full-order hold would miss the promise, ship that line promptly and manage the backorder explicitly. This can remove second shipments and duplicated packaging without hiding unmet demand. Avoid solving every shortage through excess stock. Monitor line-level reservations, outstanding quantities, first/complete delivery, backorder age, cancellations, shipment count and any resulting inventory write-offs.

**I10. Govern sustainability and quality exceptions — S12.** Record the decision point, candidate routes, slack, predicted reliability, selected route and reason for an SP1/SP2 exception. Capture a minimal critical-dispatch justification immediately, with fuller review afterward, so paperwork does not delay an emergency. Record Q1's certified low-risk exception distinctly; an ordinary managerial override is not a substitute for certification. This improves traceability and exposes recurring avoidable expedites or incompatible packaging, but has no assured direct physical saving. Monitor justification completeness, exception frequency and outcomes by reason, plus decision-to-dispatch time. Review repeated overrides for upstream scheduling or capacity problems.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batch energy versus waiting and recovery transport.** Larger CNC or heat-treatment batches can save electricity but create late orders requiring air or vans. Include downstream queues and shipment cutoffs in release decisions; account for the resulting express emissions.
2. **Batch efficiency versus obsolescence.** Producing extra items to reach an efficient batch size moves impacts into unsold inventory. Follow the observed 6% unshipped-at-14-days cohort through later sales or disposal, including its embodied energy and materials. Avoid treating delayed recognition of waste as a saving.
3. **Transport savings versus service and other customers.** Ground or consolidation can miss critical deadlines. Priority picking for contracts can also delay Regular orders and trigger their expedites. Check both segments and customer downtime rather than just the selected green route.
4. **Shared transport versus displaced emissions.** A shared courier may add stops, miss urgent arrivals or leave another carrier with the empty return leg. A lower allocated kgCO2e/order is insufficient if whole-route fuel use or total journeys rise. Measure utilization and both directions of travel.
5. **Packaging reduction versus damage.** Lighter packaging can save material while increasing heavy-part returns, replacement production and transport. Conversely, universal reinforcement doubles packaging unnecessarily. Respect Q1 and validate protection by part/route combination over a full returns window.
6. **Inspection and rework versus new bottlenecks.** A quality gate can reduce waste while raising queues and rush transport elsewhere. A falling recorded rework rate could also mean defects escape inspection. Measure returns and good-output resource use, and provide adequate inspection capacity.
7. **Machine yield versus concentration.** Moving work to CNC_A may overload it, force emergency processing or leave the system vulnerable to its failure. Include CNC_B backup qualification and due-date feasibility in the environmental comparison.
8. **Partial-shipment reduction versus customer restoration and inventory.** Holding an entire order may withhold the one part needed to restart equipment. Raising safety stock indiscriminately may reduce backorders but increase upstream production and obsolete stock. Optimize line dependencies and selective buffers.
9. **Promise flexibility versus customer behavior.** Longer promises may cause customers to hold additional spares or buy urgent replacements elsewhere. Lower shipping costs may induce more frequent orders, while batch-friendly offers may encourage over-ordering. Track volumes, customer need-by dates, cancellations and reported substitution alongside per-order savings.
10. **Boundary and denominator displacement.** Outsourcing a difficult operation, cancelling delayed orders or postponing write-offs can reduce measured internal intensity while leaving lifecycle impact unchanged or higher. Retain the original cohort and include supplier, replacement and disposal burdens when comparing alternatives.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt one coordinated, segment-sensitive redesign rather than independent targets for bigger batches or fewer express shipments.

Start with line/shipment traceability, packaging-route checks, Q1 enforcement, targeted inspection improvement and lightweight exception capture. These address repeated production, packaging and return loops and create reliable inputs for subsequent scheduling changes. Protect inspection capacity while introducing gates.

For Standard-stock orders, combine reliable availability checks, bounded priority picking and deadline-aware ground selection. Pilot the SP1-flagged Service-contract cases first, keeping justified air and alternative routes available. Consolidate non-urgent Regular orders only when the actual departure fits the promise; avoid partial shipments only when withholding lines is safe and complete delivery remains timely.

For Made-to-order, dispatch qualified work across CNC_A and CNC_B jointly with bounded CNC/heat batching and downstream inspection capacity. Use confirmed orders, preserve all required production steps, and release urgent or low-slack work without waiting for a target batch. Offer Regular customers feasible lower-impact promises before acceptance. This focuses on a major burden: using the supplied segment averages, MTO represents approximately 87% of recorded energy and 85% of scrap despite being 34% of orders.

For Critical-downtime, establish actual urgency and the latest acceptable arrival, then preserve proven rapid service. Improve van utilization and test shared emergency lanes only where they achieve that service with an available fallback. Critical orders account for approximately 41% of reported transport emissions from 11% of orders, but this is a reason to improve delivery design, not to accept the higher lateness of the ground exception.

Select environmentally preferable options from the set that satisfies quality, contractual service and resilience constraints. SP1's greater-than-85% probability threshold is a minimum policy test, not permission to deliver only 85% of critical or contract orders on time. Where a higher service requirement prevents an otherwise SP1-eligible switch, record and review the service-based exception. Until lifecycle factors are consistent, compare energy, transport and material trade-offs explicitly rather than adding incompatible units into one score.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

**Establish a reconciled baseline.** Link order, line, part, batch, shipment and return identifiers. Add part weight/geometry, original and revised promised timestamps, customer need-by time, event start/completion times, route/carrier schedules, inspection results and low-risk certificates. Record machine recipe, setup energy allocation, shipment payload/utilization and attributable empty trips. Add inventory aging and eventual write-off/reuse events. Reconcile cancellations and unfinished traces, inspect repeated-event allocation and establish the actual scope of transport emissions. Follow each order through complete delivery and a suitable returns window.

**Test the combined design before broad rollout.** Replay or simulate queues, resource capacity, batch release, inspection and carrier cutoffs together, using observed time distributions rather than medians alone. Stress-test peak arrivals, carrier disruption, CNC_A failure and inspection overload. Then pilot by comparable operating cells, regions or time periods with contemporaneous controls; randomized or staggered rollout is preferable where practical. Match or adjust for segment, part family/weight, quantity, destination, promised slack and workload. Account for shared-batch and shared-route clustering and spillovers into other segments. A simple before/after reduction is insufficient evidence of causality.

**Set service and quality limits before the pilot.** Use the existing contractual requirements and agree any non-inferiority margins explicitly; do not invent a permissible deterioration from the log. The segment baselines provide reference points:

| Segment | Late delivery | p90 cycle time |
|---|---:|---:|
| Standard-stock Regular | 7% | 62 h |
| Standard-stock Service-contract | 5% | 41 h |
| Made-to-order Regular | 18% | 180 h |
| Made-to-order Service-contract | 13% | 156 h |
| Critical-downtime Service-contract | 4% | 30 h |

Monitor lateness and p90 separately for each segment, true downtime, partial shipments and disruption periods. Include cancellations, first essential-line delivery, complete delivery and customer downtime. Check mature return rates, heavy-part damage and first-pass yield; confirm that reduced rework has not become unrecorded defects or delayed returns. Preserve original-promise and actual-need-by comparisons for negotiated dates.

**Monitor conformance at the decision point.**

- **SP1:** Log the timestamp, available ground/consolidated alternatives, remaining work, promised deadline, predicted on-time probability and model version. Check use of an eligible lower-impact mode when probability is above 85%; audit higher-service or disruption exceptions separately. Test probability calibration against actual outcomes by segment and route.
- **SP2:** Require an explicit Critical-downtime justification event for every dedicated-van dispatch. Capture essential evidence without delaying dispatch, then review completeness and outcome. Reduce the 31% documentation gap while investigating, rather than presuming, unnecessary van use.
- **Q1:** Evaluate weight and packaging per MTO part, not total order weight. Require reinforced packaging above 8 kg unless a linked inspection certificate establishes low damage risk. Record failures as nonconformance; routine approval does not replace certification. Supply compliant protection before releasing an affected shipment.
- **R1:** Verify a qualified, capacity-available alternative for Service-contract customers during carrier disruption. Test the alternative's response time and shared infrastructure dependencies, including for shared emergency lanes.

**Assign ownership and close the feedback loop.** Operations owns dispatch and batching; quality owns packaging certification and defect controls; service management owns customer commitments; the sustainability lead reconciles environmental accounting. Give exceptions a reason, owner, timestamp, expiry and review outcome. Use a preauthorized urgent decision process so approvals do not create a new emergency queue, while keeping Q1's protection requirement intact.

Review the dashboard frequently during pilots and investigate recurring overrides, queue growth, unshipped batch output and replacement shipments. Maintain explicit rollback triggers for agreed service, quality or backup-capacity breaches. Expand only when segment-level outcomes satisfy those limits and combined environmental benefits persist after returns, obsolescence, empty running and volume changes are included. Report uncertainty, absolute impacts and normalized results together; retain effective emergency and backup capacity throughout rollout.
