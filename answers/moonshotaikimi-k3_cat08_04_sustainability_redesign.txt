## 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Boundary used:** a partial cradle-to-customer, order-to-delivery boundary beginning at **Receive order** and ending at **Close** or **Cancel due to delay**. It includes:

- Production and inspection energy recorded as `energy_kwh`
- Batch waiting and its indirect effects on service, WIP, and obsolescence
- Material losses recorded as `material_scrap_kg`
- Packaging mass recorded as `package_material_kg`
- Outbound transport emissions recorded as `transport_kgco2e`
- Additional impacts from partial shipments, production rework, packaging rework, emergency production, cancellations, and repeat shipments
- Damage-related returns where they can be linked back to the originating order

**Outside or only partially visible:**

- Embodied emissions of raw materials and purchased components
- Supplier and inbound transport
- Warehouse and building energy
- Packaging production impacts
- Product use, customer downtime consequences, and end-of-life treatment
- Water, toxicity, land use, and other non-climate impacts
- Returns that are not linked to the original case

**Assumptions and uncertainty:**

- `energy_kwh` is treated as allocated process energy; it is not converted to COe because electricity-grid factors are not provided.
- `material_scrap_kg` and `package_material_kg` are resource-use proxies, not full lifecycle impacts.
- The treatment of vehicle load factors, distance, subcontracting, and empty backhauls in `transport_kgco2e` is unknown.
- Because orders contain multiple line items but the log is flattened by order, impacts per order can be distorted by order size. Impacts should ultimately also be reported per delivered line or item.
- Shared CNC and heat-treatment batches create allocation uncertainty.
- The DFG has 12,000 starts but 11,200 closes and 530 cancellations, leaving 270 cases not fully explained in the supplied view. Emergency and partial-shipment variants also have low model precision.
- Avoided customer downtime is treated as a service and resilience requirement, not as a carbon offset against logistics emissions.

Consequently, the recommendations use directional and path-level effects rather than unsupported cradle-to-grave totals.

## 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Using the supplied segment shares and averages, the approximate mix-weighted baseline is:

- **7.4 kWh/order**
- **10.3 kgCOe/order**
- **0.35 kg scrap/order**
- **9.5% late delivery**
- **6.3% rework**

These are operational indicators, not lifecycle totals. Made-to-order orders represent 34% of volume but approximately **87% of energy** and **85% of scrap**. Critical-downtime orders represent 11% of volume but approximately **41% of transport emissions**. These are therefore different hotspots requiring different interventions.

| Transport | Total kgCOe; kgCOe per shipment, order, and delivered line; shipments per order; mode share; avoidable emissions under SP1; dedicated-van load and empty-return rate | Route, customer segment, promised slack, partial shipments, carrier disruption |
| Materials | Scrap kg per produced item and first-pass item; packaging kg per shipment; obsolete items/kg not shipped within 14 days; damage-return material losses | Product family, part weight, machine, packaging type, forecast accuracy |
| Rework | Production and packaging rework rates; loops per case; first-pass yield; incremental energy, scrap, packaging, transport, and cycle time per loop | Inspection-to-rework paths, CNC_B part families, packaging-route combinations |
| Batching | Batch size and fill; batch-wait median/p90; kWh/item; release-cap breaches; WIP age; late rate and obsolescence by batch | CNC vs heat treatment, case type, customer segment, demand uncertainty |
| Service | Median and p90 cycle time; late delivery against promise; OTIF; remaining slack at each decision; partial-shipment, backorder, cancellation, and expedite rates | Five customer/product segments and critical vs precautionary urgent orders |
| Quality | Defect rate, damage rate, customer returns, Q1 conformance, inspection escapes, reinforced-packaging compliance | Parts over 8 kg, route/package compatibility, machine/part combination |
| Resilience | Percentage of Service-contract orders with an available alternative route; disruption recovery time; expedite rate; machine concentration; SP2 exception quality | Carrier lane, machine group, critical category, shared-lane dependency |

A cross-cutting measure such as **kgCOe per on-time delivered line** is useful, but it must be reported alongside absolute emissions and service guardrails so that service reductions or volume growth cannot masquerade as sustainability improvement.

## 3. Intervention options

1. **S1 — Green shipping recommender**
   - **Target path or segment:** `Package standard/reinforced -> Ship *`, especially Standard-stock Service-contract and Made-to-order Regular orders with remaining slack. True Critical-downtime orders should not be forced onto slower modes.
   - **Mechanism:** Use remaining promised lead time, route reliability, customer segment, disruption status, and part criticality to select the lowest-impact mode predicted to meet the promise with at least the SP1 probability of 85%.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Service is preserved if the reliability model is calibrated; poorly calibrated ground recommendations could create late deliveries and later expedites. Quality is largely unaffected. Resilience is preserved by maintaining approved air and alternative ground options under R1.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Slack at the shipping decision, recommended vs selected mode, predicted and actual on-time probability, SP1 conformance, transport kgCOe, p90 cycle time, late deliveries, disruptions, and override reasons.

2. **S2 — Dynamic batching with waiting-time caps**
   - **Target path or segment:** `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production` and CNC-to-heat-treatment batching for Made-to-order cases.
   - **Mechanism:** Accumulate work until an efficient batch is reached, but release it earlier when a segment-specific waiting cap or due-date slack threshold is reached. Use different caps for CNC and heat treatment because heat-treatment waiting has a 1.7× higher late-delivery risk.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Reduces setups and energy per item while preventing unlimited waiting. Excessively large batches can still increase p90 cycle time, correlated delays, and obsolescence. Machine outages can affect a larger batch, so alternate routing and reserved capacity are needed.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** `batch_id`, `batch_size`, fill rate, batch wait, energy/item, WIP age, late rate, cancellation, expedites, unshipped items after 14 days, and machine downtime.

3. **S3 — Shift suitable Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A**
   - **Target path or segment:** The `MTO -> batch wait -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework -> ground` path, which has 32% late delivery, 32 kWh/order, and 1.8 kg scrap/order.
   - **Mechanism:** For technically comparable parts, CNC_A’s 9% rework rate versus 18% on CNC_B should reduce repeated machining, inspection, scrap, and hidden energy use.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Quality and tail-cycle time should improve, but CNC_A queuing could offset the benefit. Shift only when engineering qualification, queue length, and due-date slack allow it. Maintaining CNC_B capacity avoids a single-machine resilience risk.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Machine assignment, part-family comparability, queue time, first-pass yield, rework loops, scrap, energy, total cycle time, late delivery, utilization, and CNC_A downtime.

4. **S4 — Priority pick plus ground for Standard-stock Service-contract orders**
   - **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Service-contract cases currently violating SP1; 22% of this segment violates the policy.
   - **Mechanism:** Replace avoidable parcel air with priority picking, earlier cutoff handling, and ground transport. This attacks waiting before shipment rather than using a high-emission mode to compensate for it.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Ground adds up to the observed 24-hour transport-time difference versus air, but priority picking can offset some or all of that time. Air remains available when predicted on-time performance falls below the SP1 threshold or during disruption.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Pick-to-ship time, remaining slack, promised vs actual delivery, SP1 violations, route reliability, late penalties, transport emissions, and whether priority picking delays Regular orders.

5. **S5 — Twice-daily consolidated truck departures**
   - **Target path or segment:** Non-urgent Standard-stock orders currently sent as individual ground parcels; Service-contract orders should be included only when the consolidation wait still meets the promise.
   - **Mechanism:** Consolidation reduces transport emissions by approximately 35–55% versus individual ground parcels. Fixed twice-daily departures make the 12–24-hour consolidation window predictable.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Median time may increase, and missing a cutoff can add a day. Packaging compatibility is important to avoid damage in consolidated loads. Parcel ground must remain as a fallback to satisfy R1.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Consolidation wait, missed cutoffs, truck fill, kgCOe/order, p90 cycle time, late rate, damage, fallback use, and route availability during disruptions.

6. **S6 — Packaging-route compatibility checks**
   - **Target path or segment:** The 620 transitions into `Rework packaging`—260 from standard packaging and 360 from reinforced packaging—and Made-to-order parts covered by Q1.
   - **Mechanism:** Validate route, part geometry, weight, and packaging before the first packaging event. This avoids repacking, extra material, handling, delays, and route-incompatible shipments.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Removes a avoidable loop and should improve both service and damage performance. Reinforced packaging can double package material, but for heavy Made-to-order parts it reduces damage-related returns by 40%; it should therefore be risk-based rather than minimized indiscriminately.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Compatibility-check result, package type and mass, route changes after packing, packaging-rework loops, damage returns, Q1 conformance, and packing cycle time.

7. **S7 — Risk-based inspection quality gate**
   - **Target path or segment:** High-rework machine/part combinations, especially CNC_B Made-to-order work and the 470 `Inspection -> Rework production` transitions.
   - **Mechanism:** Detect defects before packaging or customer delivery, avoiding repeated CNC, inspection, packaging, scrap, and potential return transport.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Quality and first-pass yield should improve. However, adding inspection without capacity can lengthen the existing inspection-cell queue and encourage rushed inspections. Use risk-based inspection, added capacity, and priority rules.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Inspection queue, inspection duration, defect escapes, first-pass yield, production and packaging rework, customer returns, scrap, energy, cycle time, and late delivery.

8. **S8 — Shared emergency courier lanes**
   - **Target path or segment:** Critical orders currently using `Emergency production -> Package reinforced -> Dedicated van`, especially the 31% of dedicated-van cases missing an SP2 justification.
   - **Mechanism:** Combine compatible emergency shipments on planned emergency lanes and improve backhaul utilization instead of sending partially loaded vans that often return empty.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Shared stops can add time, so true downtime orders with insufficient slack should retain dedicated transport. Shared lanes need backup carriers and clear door-to-door service guarantees to avoid creating a new single point of failure.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Orders per lane, vehicle fill, empty kilometers, kgCOe/order, door-to-door median/p90 time, missed downtime commitments, SP2 justifications, and fallback activations.

9. **S9 — Separate true downtime from precautionary urgent demand**
   - **Target path or segment:** The 11% Critical-downtime Service-contract segment at order receipt.
   - **Mechanism:** Use confirmed equipment status, downtime consequence, inventory position, and customer confirmation to distinguish true downtime from precautionary urgency. Precautionary orders can use stock, scheduled production, ground, or consolidation when feasible.
   - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** True downtime receives faster and more reliable access to scarce emergency capacity. Misclassification is the major risk and could cause severe customer downtime; require reason codes, customer confirmation, and rapid override.
   - **Evidence to monitor:** Critical category, justification, category changes, actual downtime impact, selected route, late delivery, overrides, emissions, and recurrence of misclassification.

10. **S10 — Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation**
    - **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order Regular customers, particularly the `immediate CNC -> inspection -> reinforced pack -> air` path with 24 kWh and 23 kgCOe per order.
    - **Mechanism:** Offer customers feasible delivery-date options showing the operational and emissions consequences. Additional slack enables efficient batching, CNC_A routing, ground transport, or consolidation.
    - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** The accepted promise may be longer, but reliability and schedule flexibility can improve. The date must be voluntary; otherwise this is merely service deterioration disguised as sustainability.
    - **Evidence to monitor:** Original requested date, accepted promise, offer acceptance, mode and machine selected, batch size, energy, transport, on-time performance against both dates, cancellations, complaints, and later expedites.

11. **S11 — Reduce avoidable partial shipments**
    - **Target path or segment:** The 1,010 `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment` cases, especially the partial-plus-backorder path with 79-hour median cycle time, 24% late delivery, and 7.2 kgCOe/order.
    - **Mechanism:** Use line criticality, customer preference, expected backorder duration, and inventory allocation rules to avoid automatic splits that create a second parcel, extra packaging, additional customer contacts, and cancellation risk.
    - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Complete shipment may delay the first line, so partial shipment must remain available for downtime-critical lines. Inventory reservation can also starve other orders if not governed.
    - **Evidence to monitor:** Shipments per order, first-line delivery time, complete-order OTIF, backorder wait, cancellations, stockouts, transport and packaging totals, and effects on other customer segments.

12. **S12 — Sustainability exception log**
    - **Target path or segment:** All SP1, SP2, Q1, and R1 overrides, especially emergency and partial-shipment variants where model precision is low.
    - **Mechanism:** Record the decision, alternatives considered, predicted service and environmental effects, reason, approver, and expiry. This converts silent trade-offs into auditable decisions and identifies recurring process causes.
    - **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Direct environmental impact is neutral, but governance enables sustained reductions. Emergency approvals should be retrospective rather than blocking urgent shipments.
    - **Evidence to monitor:** Missing justifications, reason-code frequency, approval time, repeated exceptions, policy conformance, exception outcomes, and whether exceptions trigger corrective action.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching-to-expedite rebound:** Larger CNC or heat-treatment batches reduce energy per item but can create enough waiting to trigger air, dedicated vans, emergency production, or cancellation. The avoided production energy can be overwhelmed by the resulting transport and rework impacts. Waiting caps and SP1-compliant recovery rules are therefore essential.

2. **Obsolescence displacement:** Efficient batches can become waste when forecasts are wrong; 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days in those cases. Their embodied material and production energy are only partially visible in the log. Batch-size rules must consider demand uncertainty and WIP age, not only kWh/item.

3. **Transport mode-shift rebound:** Moving from air to ground or consolidated truck can increase missed promises, safety stock, partial shipments, or later emergency transport. Conversely, blanket retention of air creates avoidable emissions. Decisions must use remaining slack and calibrated route reliability rather than a blanket mode preference.

4. **Consolidation and shared-lane risk:** Consolidated trucks and shared emergency lanes improve load factors, but missed cutoffs, shared stops, or a lane failure can delay critical orders. A delayed true-downtime order may create much larger operational consequences and later require an even more disruptive recovery shipment.

5. **Packaging burden shifting:** Minimizing `package_material_kg` can increase damage, replacement production, returns, and return transport. Overusing reinforced packaging doubles packaging mass even for low-risk parts. The correct comparison is marginal packaging impact versus expected damage and replacement impact.

6. **Rework and inspection displacement:** A stronger inspection gate can move waiting upstream and create pressure to bypass or rush inspection. Shifting work from CNC_B to CNC_A can also create queues and concentration risk. Quality capacity must be added with the gate, and machine-level service must be monitored.

7. **Partial-shipment suppression:** Preventing a partial shipment may delay a line needed to restart customer equipment, leading to an express shipment or downtime. Reserving inventory to complete one order can also starve another order and create a second expedite elsewhere.

8. **Customer-behavior rebound:** Carbon-aware date options may cause customers to inflate urgency, classify precautionary demand as downtime, split orders into smaller shipments, or arrange their own freight. Longer lead times can also increase order changes and forecast error. Original requested dates and subsequent expedites must be tracked.

9. **Priority and volume rebound:** Priority picking for Service-contract orders can displace Regular orders into later, less efficient paths. More generally, lower cost or emissions per order can support higher order volume. Absolute energy, emissions, scrap, and packaging must therefore be monitored alongside intensity metrics.

10. **Boundary displacement:** A carrier may subcontract a longer ground route, a shared van may take a long detour, or packaging waste may move to a supplier. Actual distance, load factor, backhaul, and supplier data are needed to confirm that improvements are not merely outside the current measurement boundary.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a **segment-aware, guarded redesign package**, rather than selecting the lowest-emission path for every order.

| Decision point | Recommended redesign | Main guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Receive and classify | Introduce S9 true-downtime vs precautionary-urgent classification | True downtime retains emergency production and the fastest justified route |
| Promise date | Use S10 for Made-to-order Regular customers | Voluntary customer choice; track original requested date |
| Availability | Pilot S11 line-aware partial-shipment rules | Partial shipment remains available for critical lines |
| Production | Combine S2 due-date-capped batching, S3 qualified CNC_A routing, and S7 risk-based inspection | No unconstrained batching; preserve CNC_B and inspection capacity |
| Packaging | Implement S6 compatibility checks and enforce Q1 | Heavy/high-risk parts retain reinforced packaging |
| Shipping | Deploy S1, with S4 for eligible Standard-stock Service-contract orders and S5 for non-urgent stock | Apply the SP1 85% feasibility rule and retain R1 alternatives |
| Critical logistics | Pilot S8 shared emergency lanes | Dedicated vans remain available with SP2 justification |
| Governance | Introduce S12 before broad automation | Emergency overrides can be completed after shipment |

This package attacks the principal hotspots in different ways:

- **Made-to-order energy and scrap:** Dynamic batching, CNC_A routing where suitable, and stronger inspection reduce the large energy and scrap burden without relying on slower delivery alone.
- **Transport emissions:** Avoidable Standard-stock air is shifted to priority pick plus ground, while non-urgent orders use predictable consolidation. True Critical-downtime orders are not forced onto the lower-emission but riskier stock/ground exception path.
- **Hidden rework:** Packaging compatibility and inspection quality gates address waste that would otherwise appear later as scrap, returns, repeat production, or extra transport.
- **Service:** Eligibility is based on remaining slack, route reliability, customer segment, and line criticality. Faster modes remain available when the 85% feasibility condition cannot be met.
- **Quality:** Q1 remains a constraint, and reinforced packaging is retained where damage risk justifies the additional material.
- **Resilience:** Alternative ground and air routes, CNC_B capability, parcel fallback, shared-lane backup, and documented emergency overrides are retained.

The redesign should explicitly avoid four blunt rules: no blanket air ban, no elimination of all dedicated vans, no maximum-size batching without due-date caps, and no blanket prohibition on partial shipments.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

1. **Improve the event data before full rollout**
   - Add line-item identifiers, quantities, weights, original requested date, accepted promise, and links between outbound shipments, returns, and original orders.
   - Add recommendation ID, recommendation accepted/rejected, remaining slack, predicted route reliability, disruption status, selected alternative, and override reason.
   - Record vehicle fill, empty return, actual distance, shared-lane ID, packaging compatibility result, inspection defect type, critical category, and batch-release reason.
   - Clarify how batch energy and shared transport emissions are allocated to orders and lines.

2. **Create a mix-adjusted baseline**
   - Freeze a pre-implementation baseline by the five product/customer segments and by major paths.
   - Report absolute totals and impacts per delivered order and delivered line.
   - Separate mode-shift effects from changes in volume, product mix, batch size, customer promises, and carrier network conditions.
   - Do not combine kWh and kgCOe until credible electricity emission factors are approved.

3. **Pilot with controls and guardrails**
   - Start with Standard-stock Regular and low-risk Standard-stock Service-contract orders.
   - Run recommenders in shadow mode before automation.
   - Use a stepped-wedge or matched-control rollout by region, carrier, machine group, and product family.
   - Extend to Made-to-order and then to Critical-downtime only after route reliability and shared-lane performance are validated.
   - Predefine segment-specific rollback thresholds for late delivery, p90 cycle time, cancellation, damage, returns, and critical-service failures based on contractual requirements.

4. **Monitor policy conformance directly from the event log**
   - **SP1:** Flag air or dedicated transport when ground or consolidated truck was predicted to meet the promise with at least 85% probability and no valid exception exists. Compare the current 22% Standard-stock Service-contract violation rate over time.
   - **SP2:** Require a true-downtime justification for every dedicated van. Distinguish “missing justification” from a legitimate approved exception; the current 31% missing-justification rate should be addressed without banning justified vans.
   - **Q1:** Flag Made-to-order parts over 8 kg without reinforced packaging or certified low damage risk, then link violations to damage and return loops.
   - **R1:** During a disruption, verify that every affected Service-contract order has at least one feasible alternative route.

5. **Validate service and quality by segment**
   - Track median and p90 cycle time, late delivery, OTIF, first-line delivery, complete-order delivery, backorders, cancellations, and expedites separately for each segment.
   - Track first-pass yield, production and packaging rework, damage returns, inspection escapes, and Q1 violations.
   - Check that priority picking and emergency capacity for one segment do not materially degrade another segment.
   - For negotiated promises, measure performance against both the original requested date and the accepted date to prevent metric gaming.

6. **Validate environmental improvement and rebound**
   - Compare path-level energy/item, kgCOe/shipment, scrap/item, packaging/shipment, shipments/order, and obsolete inventory.
   - Attribute the incremental burden of each rework loop, second shipment, return, and cancellation to the original case.
   - Monitor absolute totals to catch volume rebound.
   - Decompose changes into mode shift, load factor, batch efficiency, rework reduction, volume, and mix effects.
   - Periodically add BOM-level material factors, supplier transport, warehouse energy, and end-of-life data to move toward a fuller lifecycle assessment.

7. **Govern exceptions and resilience**
   - Use a standard reason taxonomy: customer downtime, carrier disruption, inventory shortage, quality risk, route closure, machine outage, forecast error, and customer-selected speed.
   - Record alternatives considered, predicted service probability, estimated environmental effect, approver, and expiry.
   - Allow emergency action first with retrospective justification so governance does not delay downtime recovery.
   - Review repeated exceptions as process-design failures rather than isolated employee deviations.
   - Run carrier-disruption and machine-outage scenarios to test consolidated lanes, shared emergency lanes, CNC_A concentration, and R1 route availability.
   - Re-discover the process model after implementation and monitor its precision, especially for emergency and partial-shipment combinations, before tightening automated controls.
