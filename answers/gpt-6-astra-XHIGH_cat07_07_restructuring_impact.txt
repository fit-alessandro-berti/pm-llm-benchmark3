# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring can improve access to expertise and consistency, especially for East, but its benefits depend on capacity, routing, retained local knowledge, and explicit ownership. The most defensible forecast is conditional: pooling can reduce unequal access and absence-related transfers while concentrating work into queues that increase tail delays.

The supplied routing estimates do not establish a reduction in handovers. If the listed categories are comparable and non-overlapping, they sum to **343 handovers per 100 cases before versus 359 afterward, an increase of 4.7%**. Cross-team transfers fall from 6 to 3, but technical outward/return transfers rise from 67 to 77, and approval-related transfers rise from 81 to 92. These are transfer occurrences, not percentages of cases. The estimates also increase median waits at technical, approval, and Legal/Risk boundaries. A simpler organization chart or fewer named local loops could therefore coexist with more actual transfers and longer waits. The table may omit new triage and difficult-quality-check transfers, which must also be measured.

Pooling is limited by actual qualifications. Legal, risk, security, technical assessment, and difficult Quality Check are not interchangeable tasks merely because they share a queue. No security-qualified resource is identified in the scenario; capability and capacity must be established before routing security work there. A high-complexity legal case also needs an explicit routing rule to prevent duplicate or unnecessary sequential reviews.

The utilization target requires clarification. With equal available technical capacity, the simple technical average is about 73.7%; a 15% relative increase would make it 84.7%, while an increase of 15 percentage points would make it 88.7%. These are illustrations, not forecasts: hours, skill mix, and additional demand are unknown. Applying a 15% relative increase to Legal's 93% and Risk's 91% would exceed 100% available capacity. Legal/Risk referrals are also projected to rise from 9 to 12 per 100 cases. Without reduced effort per referral or additional qualified hours, pressure increases rather than disappears. Queueing delay rises nonlinearly as demand approaches effective capacity, particularly with variable case duration and limited interchangeability.

Single event timestamps support event ordering and inter-event elapsed times. They do **not** establish active service time, actual queue waiting, or utilization. The supplied handover-wait summaries can inform hypotheses, but their measurement definition should be checked. Do not sum edge medians to predict a case's cycle time.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency and unfinished work.** A few long-waiting cases can suffer substantially while many automated cases improve. Reporting only completed cases excludes the very backlog created by congestion. Track p90/p95, overdue open cases, oldest-item age, and censored time-to-completion estimates.

2. **High-complexity case disadvantage.** Mandatory central routing and long service durations can create a separate slow path. Priority rules may further delay low-priority complex cases. Compare distributions within initial complexity and priority, and inspect competing specialist/Quality Check workloads.

3. **Loss of local knowledge.** A faster standardized review may misunderstand regional conventions or customer circumstances. Errors may emerge only at implementation, Quality Check, reopening, or complaint. Follow downstream rework and outcomes, especially where local expertise was previously used.

4. **Fairness and compositional effects.** Improvements in North's large volume of simple cases can outweigh worse outcomes in East or a small customer segment; alternatively, East may gain while North/South deteriorate. Report raw and adjusted team/segment outcomes, absolute SLA gaps, and relevant intersections. An aggregate improvement does not show that comparable needs receive comparable service.

5. **Learning and future capacity.** Removing hard cases from local staff may improve short-term speed while reducing local judgment and promotion-ready skills. Subsequent referral demand and dependence on shared specialists can increase. Track supported participation in difficult work, independent competence, mentoring, and role profiles over months.

6. **Accountability gaps.** A specialist task can finish quickly while its advice waits for a local claimant, or an approval can lack a clearly accountable decision-maker. Track named ownership, acknowledgment delays, reversals, escalations, and responsibility reported by staff. A network edge cannot establish accountability.

7. **Exception handling and hidden work.** A standard path may speed up while unusual cases are bounced, handled through unlogged calls, informally bypassed, or closed and reopened under another case identifier. Audit exceptions, handover reasons, out-of-system work, and linked reopenings; ensure reported closure means completion of the customer need.

8. **Resilience and concentration risk.** Good performance in a fully staffed week can hide failure during specialist absence, demand spikes, or a shared-queue outage. Test loss of a technical specialist and interruption of scarce-specialty coverage, including backlog recovery time.

9. **Quality and workload displacement.** Faster front-end decisions may push unresolved issues to difficult Quality Check, customer contact, or unpaid overtime. Measure end-to-end work, labor hours, quality severity, and downstream outcomes. A short local segment duration can conceal a worse whole-case result.

Adjustment itself needs care. Show both unadjusted outcomes and adjustment for pre-routing need. Do not adjust away restructuring-induced reassignments, loss of local expertise, or increased waiting: these are potential mechanisms of harm. If central review changes how complexity is classified, retain the intake assessment for a consistent comparison and separately analyze the new classification.

# 5. Pilot design

**Scope and sequence.** Pilot first, with technical pooling as the initial change. Include bounded volumes from North, South, and East, stratified by initial complexity; include a controlled set of genuinely complex technical cases so the pilot tests the main risk. Retain a named local case owner and a designated regional technical liaison. Use a standard referral packet and an explicit owner for receiving and acting on advice. Keep the supervisor position during this phase so technical pooling can be evaluated without simultaneously reducing approval capacity.

Use one service entry point with visible, qualification-specific work queues, capacity limits, priority rules, and aging alerts. Preserve opportunities for local staff to join reviews and receive feedback. Resolve overlapping high-complexity/specialty rules explicitly. Do not expand Legal/Risk demand or route security work until qualified capacity and escalation coverage are established. Introduce shared approval, then difficult Quality Check and additional specialty routing as separately measured phases. Removing a supervisor should be a later, evidence-dependent step.

**Comparison group.** Where operationally feasible, randomly allocate eligible cases within team × complexity × specialty × priority strata to the pilot or the existing local path. Define assignment at intake and analyze by assigned arm, including crossovers and escalations. Protect and account for staff hours in both arms: a control deprived of its specialist because that person serves the pilot is not a clean counterfactual. Record interference when shared specialists or common queues serve both arms. If separation is infeasible, use a staggered rollout across comparable work groups or time blocks with concurrent controls, assess pre-trends, and acknowledge the weaker causal attribution. East alone should not be compared with North/South as if their starting conditions were equivalent.

**Observation windows.** Use approximately 8–12 weeks of baseline, 6–8 weeks of pilot intake, and at least 4–6 weeks of subsequent follow-up, extending follow-up for unresolved long cases and uncertain subgroup estimates. The current p90 reaches 22.8 days, so a one- or two-week completed-case comparison is inadequate. Label the transition/backlog migration cohort separately. Continue learning and resilience monitoring for 3–6 months and through absence or demand-stress episodes. Keep calendar conventions, seasonality, and staffing changes visible.

**Segmentation and analysis.** Report origin team, initial complexity, required specialty, customer segment, and priority, including important intersections. Also distinguish cases requiring local knowledge or accommodation where directly recorded. Use arrival cohorts with the same follow-up horizon; show completed durations alongside open-case age and survival estimates. Compare changes in each arm, not merely post-change team rankings, and check whether pre-change trends support a difference-in-differences interpretation. Report effect sizes, uncertainty, and subgroup sample counts. Reweight to a common intake case mix as a supplementary comparison while retaining raw results.

**Guardrails and adjustment triggers.** Agree thresholds before launch using actual baseline variation, demand, and service promises. The following are proposed operational starting points, not statistically derived guarantees:

| Guardrail | Proposed trigger | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Queue stability and scarce-skill capacity | Arrivals exceed completions and backlog age rises for two consecutive weekly reviews; sustained utilization exceeds the locally validated capacity buffer | Stop increasing pilot intake, rebalance work, add qualified coverage, or reduce avoidable demand; do not wait for average cycle time to worsen |
| Tail performance | Adjusted p90 exceeds the comparison expectation by more than 10% overall or 15% in a pre-specified key subgroup, supported by adequate observations or corroborating queue evidence | Diagnose the affected queue and pause or reverse that routing rule; retain cases' ownership during transfer |
| SLA and rework | A credible increase of more than 2 percentage points in SLA misses or case-level rework in a mature cohort | Review causes and quality severity; restore local support or capacity and halt the next phase |
| Fairness | A pre-specified team/segment SLA gap widens by more than 2 percentage points, or qualified-service access materially worsens after need/priority adjustment | Review allocation and accommodation support; change routing and capacity before expansion |
| Ownership and quality | Unclaimed advice or unassigned work exceeds its agreed acknowledgment limit; any serious quality failure attributable to missing ownership or qualified review | Escalate immediately, assign an accountable owner, and suspend the unsafe workflow where necessary |
| Customer experience and learning | Repeated adverse customer/staff feedback, declining complex-case local participation, or reduced competence assessment results | Restore consultation/mentoring and regional involvement; delay supervisor removal or further centralization |

Thresholds should account for uncertainty and multiple subgroup comparisons; isolated noisy rates are not sufficient evidence of a structural effect. Conversely, low sample size is not a reason to ignore a serious incident or a visibly aging queue. Specify a reversible fallback: route affected new work to the prior local path, preserve all case history and ownership, and safely complete or reassign work already in progress.

**Staff feedback.** Run short weekly surveys and structured case reviews involving local caseworkers, embedded/central specialists, supervisors, and customer-facing staff from all three teams. Ask who owns each decision, how often informal advice is lost, whether submissions contain enough local context, whether staff bypass the queue, and what they are learning. Link sampled narratives to trace patterns without turning network rankings into individual performance judgments. Collect customer feedback after specialist interaction and after closure.

**Test full-scale exposure before expanding.** A low-volume pilot can succeed with spare capacity and fail at rollout. Calibrate a skill-constrained queue simulation using measured arrival patterns, service durations, calendars, priority rules, rework, and all proposed specialist/Quality Check/approval demand. Examine planned full volumes, demand surges, absence of a technical specialist, and interruptions to Legal/Risk coverage. Use stress results to set capacity buffers and recovery plans; simulation is conditional evidence, not proof of benefit.

# 6. Recommendation

**Modify the design and pilot first.** Pooling technical capacity is worth testing because East may gain more reliable access, and shared standards may improve decisions. The proposed combination of increased specialist referrals, additional specialist/Quality Check duties, near-capacity Legal/Risk resources, and one fewer supervisor does not yet justify full rollout.

Retain local case ownership, regional consultation and mentoring; provide clear advice acknowledgment and approval responsibilities; and manage capacity separately by qualification even if the service has a common front door. Do not remove the supervisor until the shared approval phase demonstrates adequate capacity and accountability. Treat the 15% utilization increase as a capacity assumption to test, rather than a success criterion.

Expand only when mature pilot cohorts show acceptable cycle-time tails, SLA performance, rework, and fairness across all three teams and key customer groups; queue age and workload remain stable at the intended load; and staff can identify who owns each case and decision. Adjust or roll back affected routes if the guardrails are breached. Continue longer-term learning and resilience checks after any expansion.

Management should not infer success from an early fall in average cycle time, a smaller organizational network, or higher utilization. Those observations can arise from easy cases finishing first, delayed cases remaining open, relabeled handovers, overtime, or overload. Nor should a temporary migration backlog alone establish failure. The decision requires comparable cohorts, open-work visibility, downstream outcomes, and evidence that East's gains do not conceal unacceptable losses elsewhere.

def consecutive_transfers(log):

    x["next_resource"] = g[RES].shift(-1)
