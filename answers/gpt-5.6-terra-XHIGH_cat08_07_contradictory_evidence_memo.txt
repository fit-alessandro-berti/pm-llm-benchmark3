# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Fiber Operations Executive Team  
**Decision:** Adopt a controlled hybrid approach (Alternative E), not national scaling or full rollback.

**Recommendation.** Continue the redesign only for clearly eligible simple, low-risk houses, while immediately suspending two high-risk design elements across all expansion: (1) survey skipping outside a controlled experiment and (2) billing start before successful activation. Retain and test coordinator ownership, but add workload caps and shared-cover arrangements. Run targeted experiments and improve measurement before extending the redesign to apartments, old/permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, or rural installs.

**Why this is the best next step.** The pilot produced a material speed gain in the typical case: median duration fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, handovers fell sharply, and visible pre-complaint cost fell. The simple-house segment is a credible beneficiary: it improved on median and p90 duration, first-time-right performance, and complaints. Rolling that benefit back everywhere would discard value.

However, the pilot also created unacceptable warning signals for broader deployment. p90 and p95 duration worsened substantially; first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%; complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%; manual activations and second visits increased; and SLA breaches increased overall. The process log shows billing often beginning before successful activation, complaint escalation after billing, repeated installation visits without surveys, and missing permit checks for older apartment buildings. These are not merely model-quality issues; they indicate operational-control and customer-harm risks.

**Management judgment.** The evidence supports segmentation rather than a single national decision. Redesign R appears valuable for simple houses, but its survey-skip rule, post-billing activation practice, and overloaded coordinator model are not yet safe or economically validated for complex cases. The pilot-versus-baseline comparison is also not fully causal: pilot regions had more apartments and old buildings, risk attributes were incomplete early in the pilot, and downstream cost data are incomplete. Therefore, neither the median improvement nor the deterioration in tail metrics can be attributed wholly to R without further controls.

**Immediate operating policy.**
- Keep the streamlined validate/schedule path and coordinator ownership for verified simple, low-risk houses in a limited expansion.
- Require successful activation before billing start, except for a documented, approved exception workflow.
- Require survey and permit-control pathways for apartments, old/permit-sensitive buildings, and any address outside validated low-risk eligibility.
- Cap coordinator load and provide shared-team backup; 93% utilization and absence-related stalls indicate that single ownership needs resilience mechanisms.
- Treat repeated visits, manual activation, billing-before-activation, and missing permit checks as monitored control failures, not normal variants.

**Decision trade-off.** This approach sacrifices some near-term scale speed and may retain survey cost for complex cases. In return, it preserves demonstrated value for simple cases while preventing the organization from scaling long-tail delays, rework, billing disputes, and possible permit-control failures. A controlled experiment can determine whether survey skipping is genuinely safe for any risk stratum and whether coordinator ownership remains beneficial when capacity and backup are designed properly.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### High fitness but low precision

These metrics assess different properties of the discovered process model.

- **High fitness (0.94)** means the pilot model can replay most observed pilot traces. This is consistent with many cases following the simplified coordinator route.
- **Low precision (0.51)** means the model also permits many behavior combinations that are not well constrained by the observed process. In this case, it can accommodate excessive combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits, and billing start.
- Thus, high fitness does not mean the process is controlled or desirable. A flexible model can fit almost everything while providing weak guidance about the intended pathway.
- The alignment deviations reinforce this interpretation: billing before successful activation, repeat installation without prior survey, absent expected permit checks, and post-billing complaint escalation are deviations that the flexible pilot model can absorb rather than prevent.

### Improved median but worse tail duration

The redesign can accelerate common, uncomplicated cases while worsening difficult cases.

- The combined validate/schedule path and fewer handovers appear to speed simple houses, which saw both median and p90 improvement.
- Apartments, old buildings, businesses, and rural cases show the opposite pattern: some experienced modest median gains, but much worse p90 outcomes.
- Skipping a survey may remove time from the normal path but create a longer exception path when the rule is wrong: failed visit, second visit, manual activation, permit issue, or complaint.
- Coordinator congestion also plausibly amplifies tail delays. Temporal profiles show longer Failed visit  Second visit delays in high-utilization regions.
- The baseline and pilot also differ in building mix, so the aggregate median/tail contrast reflects both redesign effects and case-mix differences.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Fewer handovers can improve accountability and reduce coordination delay, but may concentrate operational knowledge.

- The redesign reduced cases with three or more handovers from 34% to 12%.
- At the same time, the working-together network became centralized around coordinators and activation specialists.
- When a coordinator was absent, cases stalled because context was insufficiently shared.
- Therefore, lower handover count is not equivalent to better resilience. A process can be simpler in normal operation but more fragile under absence, surge volume, or exception handling.

### Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

The cost measures cover different horizons and cost categories.

- Mean visible cost before complaint handling fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161, consistent with fewer handovers, fewer surveys, and faster simple-case completion.
- But manual activation, failed visits, second visits, complaints, and billing disputes shifted work downstream.
- The estimated total cost including complaint/revisit handling rose from EUR 198 to EUR 207 despite being incomplete after week 4.
- Consequently, the apparent efficiency gain may partly be cost displacement rather than true end-to-end savings.

### Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

The redesign is not equally suited to all process variants.

- Simple low-risk houses are more likely to fit the merged validation/scheduling and direct installation path; their outcomes improved across speed, quality, and complaints.
- Apartments and old buildings have more dependencies: access constraints, shared infrastructure, permit requirements, uncertain internal wiring, and higher activation complexity.
- For those segments, skipping survey or permit-control steps can create hidden work that emerges later as failed visits, manual activation, repeat visits, long-tail delay, or post-billing complaints.
- The evidence therefore supports risk-stratified process design rather than applying one “shortest path” universally.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Reasonable when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale Redesign R nationally** | Captures median-duration and visible-cost gains quickly; reduces handovers; simplifies nominal workflow. | Would scale worse p90/p95 performance, higher rework, higher complaints, billing-before-activation deviations, permit-control gaps, and coordinator overload. Case-mix confounding means national benefit is unproven. | Only after stratified evidence shows controlled tail, quality, full cost, activation, and permit outcomes, with capacity and backup controls in place. Not reasonable now. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline process** | Stops further exposure to known pilot risks; restores survey and activation controls; may reduce rework. | Discards demonstrated benefit for simple houses; reintroduces handovers and likely longer normal-path duration; treats heterogeneous segments as identical. | Reasonable if billing/activation or permit deviations cannot be controlled promptly, or if even low-risk houses fail safety and economic thresholds under controlled evaluation. |
| **C. | **D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Produces stronger causal evidence; can separate case mix, survey skipping, coordinator ownership, and activation-policy effects. | Delays some benefits; can be operationally complex; a pure “wait for data” approach leaves simple-house value unrealized. | Reasonable if eligibility data are too poor to identify low-risk cases safely or if operational controls cannot be enforced during rollout. |
| **E. Hybrid: deploy only to simple low-risk houses, add controls for complex cases, and measure before wider scale** | Preserves likely simple-case value while containing exposure; aligns process path with risk; supports targeted experiments; avoids false choice between scale and rollback. | Requires disciplined eligibility, monitoring, dual operating paths, and capacity management. # 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E — a controlled hybrid rollout with targeted experiments and measurement gates.**

### Immediate action

1. Continue limited deployment of the streamlined path for **verified simple, low-risk houses**.
2. Retain coordinator ownership only with:
   - named backup coverage,
   - shared case context,
   - escalation rules for exceptions,
   - utilization limits and active queue balancing.
3. Enforce **successful activation before billing start** unless an approved, documented exception is used.
4. Reinstate or retain mandatory survey and permit-control pathways for:
   - apartments,
   - old or permit-sensitive buildings,
   - business customers,
   - rural/complex installs,
   - any address with missing or uncertain risk classification.

### What to pause

- National rollout of Redesign R.
- Survey skipping outside a stratified controlled experiment.
- Post-billing activation as a routine operating practice.
- Any expansion into apartment, old-building, and permit-sensitive segments before the risk rule and survey-skip policy are validated.

### What to scale

Scale only the components with credible low-risk value, and only in a controlled expansion:

- combined validation/scheduling for verified simple houses;
- reduced unnecessary handovers;
- coordinator ownership with resilient shared-team backup;
- active conformance monitoring for activation, billing, surveys, and permits.

### What to measure next

Prioritize M1, M6, M4, and M7 immediately. Add M3 and M5 during the same controlled expansion. Instrument M2 where feasible and use M8 to distinguish appointment-speed satisfaction from “service working after installation” satisfaction.

### Evidence required for broader rollout

Broader rollout beyond simple low-risk houses should require a concurrent, risk-stratified evaluation showing all of the following:

1. **Tail protection:** p90 is no worse than the concurrent control by more than 2 days, and p95 does not materially worsen.
2. **Quality protection:** first-time-right is non-inferior to control, and 30-day complaint rate is no more than 1 percentage point above control.
3. **Economic viability:** measured 60-day end-to-end cost, including revisits, credits, and complaint handling, is no higher than control.
4. **Operational control:** no unapproved Billing start before successful Activation test, and no unexplained missing Permit check where one is required.
5. **Resilience:** coordinator utilization remains below a defined operating threshold, such as 85%, and absence testing shows no material case-stall increase versus shared ownership.
6. **Segment validity:** the building-risk rule is validated, and survey skipping is shown to be safe and beneficial in the specific strata proposed for expansion.

Until those conditions are met, management should treat the redesign as a segmented operating model with promising benefits—not as a nationally proven replacement for the baseline.
