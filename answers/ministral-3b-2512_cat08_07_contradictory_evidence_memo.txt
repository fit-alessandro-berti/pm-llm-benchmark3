**Subject:** Strategic Next Steps for Fiber-Installation Redesign: Balancing Speed, Resilience, and Cost
**To:** Telecom Leadership Team
**From:** [Your Name], [Your Role]

#### **Key Decision: Deploy a Segment-Specific Hybrid Rollout**
The pilot redesign (Redesign R) improved median cycle time and reduced handovers but introduced significant tail risks, rework, and operational fragility. Given incomplete data and conflicting signals, we should **not scale Redesign R nationally** or roll it back entirely. Instead, we recommend a **hybrid approach**:
- **Immediately deploy Redesign R only for simple, low-risk houses** (e.g., single-family homes with no permit-sensitive features), leveraging its proven benefits (median speed, reduced handovers, lower visible cost).
- **Pause survey skipping and post-billing activation** for now to mitigate tail risks (e.g., apartments, old buildings) until further validation.
- **Run targeted experiments (M2, M6, M7) to measure tail behavior** before expanding to other segments.
- **Measure full downstream costs (M4) and resilience under coordinator absence (M5)** to assess operational sustainability.

#### **Trade-Offs and Rationale**
The redesign’s trade-offs are structural:
- **Speed vs. Resilience**: Median improvements are real but come with worse tails (p95: +17 days), driven by skipped surveys, manual activations, and complaint escalation after billing. The process-mining model’s low precision reflects these deviations—it fits the pilot *data* but not the intended workflow.
- **Cost vs. Rework**: Visible costs dropped (EUR 184  EUR 161), but downstream costs rose (EUR 198  EUR 207), suggesting hidden rework (second visits, complaints). The coordinator ownership reduced handovers but centralized decision-making, increasing coordination bottlenecks.
- **Segment Heterogeneity**: Simple houses benefit (+6 days median), but apartments/old buildings suffer (+12–25 days). The predictive rule for survey skipping is unreliable (e.g., missed permits in 18% of old buildings).

**Why Not Scale Nationally (A)?**
- The tail risks (p95, complaints, second visits) would disproportionately harm customer experience and operational stability. The pilot’s high coordinator utilization (93%) suggests scalability issues.
- Customer NPS and complaint trends (e.g., "installed but not working") indicate poor alignment between speed and service quality.

**Why Not Roll Back (B)?**
- The baseline process has no proven resilience for high-risk cases (e.g., apartments, permit-sensitive buildings). The redesign’s handovers reduction is a net positive for accountability, and the pilot’s median improvements are real.

**Why Not Pure Experimentation (D)?**
- We lack the data to justify a full regional experiment without addressing the immediate operational risks. The segment-level effects are clear: simple houses benefit; others suffer. A hybrid approach balances speed and resilience.

#### **Next Steps**
1. **Immediate Action**:
   - Deploy Redesign R for simple houses with manual overrides for high-risk cases.
   - Pause survey skipping and post-billing activation until M2/M6 confirm tail risks.
2. **Pause**:
   - No national scaling of Redesign R until M4/M5 confirm downstream cost and resilience.
   - No full rollback; retain coordinator ownership for its handovers benefits.
3. **Measure Next**:
   - Run M2 (start timestamps) to quantify delays in high-risk segments.
   - Validate M6 (predictive rule) against field-survey outcomes.
   - Monitor M7 (conformance: billing before activation, permit checks) for compliance risks.
4. **Threshold for Broader Rollout**:
   - If M4 shows downstream costs stabilize (e.g., <5% increase) and M5 confirms resilience under coordinator absence, expand to apartments/old buildings with segment-specific safeguards (e.g., mandatory permit checks).

**Risk Mitigation**:
- Assign a cross-functional team to monitor coordinator utilization and second-visit delays.
- Collect customer feedback on "speed to appointment" vs. "working service" (M8) to align metrics with stakeholder priorities.

### **3. Explanation of Metric Conflicts**

1. **High Fitness, Low Precision**:
   The process-mining model fits the pilot data well because it was trained on simplified paths (e.g., coordinator ownership, skipped surveys). However, its low precision means it allows too many deviations (e.g., billing before activation, repeated visits without surveys). **Fitness = data fit; precision = generalizability.** The model overfits the pilot’s "simplified" cases but fails for real-world variability.

2. **Improved Median, Worse Tail**:
   Median improvements come from streamlined paths (e.g., merged validation/scheduling, reduced handovers). The tail worsens because:
   - **Skipped surveys**: High-risk cases (apartments, old buildings) lack permits or technical checks, leading to second visits and complaints.
   - **Manual activations**: Post-billing activations create billing disputes and signal quality issues.
   - **Complaint escalation**: Billing start before activation triggers "not working" complaints, which are harder to resolve.

3. **Fewer Handovers, Lower Resilience**:
   Handovers reduced because coordinators own cases, but this centralized ownership increases fragility. When coordinators are absent, cases stall due to lost context (e.g., survey notes, permit status). **Handovers  resilience**; they measure process clarity, not error recovery.

4. **Lower Visible Cost, Higher Rework**:
   Reduced handovers and time saved offset by:
   - **Second visits**: 11%  18% (costly rework).
   - **Complaints**: 6.1%  9.8% (customer churn, escalation costs).
   - **Manual activations**: 7%  15% (billing disputes, retraining costs).
   The visible cost drop is an accounting illusion; rework is a hidden cost.

5. **Improvement for Simple Houses, Deterioration for Others**:
   The predictive rule works for low-risk cases (e.g., single-family homes) but fails for medium/high-risk (e.g., apartments, old buildings). **Segment heterogeneity reveals the rule’s calibration problem.** Simple houses benefit from streamlining; others suffer from skipped steps (e.g., permits, surveys).

### **6. Final Recommendation**

#### **Immediate Action**:
- **Deploy Redesign R for simple, low-risk houses** (e.g., single-family homes with no permit-sensitive features) with manual overrides for high-risk cases. This leverages the proven benefits (median speed, reduced handovers) while isolating tail risks.

#### **Pause**:
- **Do not scale Redesign R nationally** or roll it back entirely. Avoid deploying survey skipping or post-billing activation until M2/M6 confirm their impact on tails and rework.

#### **Measure Next**:
- **Run M2 (start timestamps)** to quantify delays in high-risk segments (e.g., apartments, old buildings). Focus on:
  - Installation visit  Activation test delays.
  - Billing start  Complaint escalation timing.
- **Validate M6 (predictive rule)** against field-survey outcomes to test whether survey skipping by building-risk stratum reduces rework.
- **Monitor M7 (conformance)** for unintended workflows (e.g., billing before activation, missing permits).

#### **Threshold for Broader Rollout**:
- **Scale to apartments/old buildings** if:
  1. M2 confirms that tail risks (e.g., skipped surveys) are not systemic but segment-specific.
  2. M6 validates the predictive rule for medium-risk cases (e.g., apartments) with safeguards (e.g., mandatory permit checks).
  3. M4 shows downstream costs stabilize (e.g., <5% increase) and M5 confirms resilience under coordinator absence.
- **Alternative**: If M6 fails or M2 reveals structural tail risks, revert to baseline for high-risk cases and refine the predictive rule.

#### **Operational Safeguards**:
- Assign a dedicated team to monitor coordinator utilization and second-visit delays.
- Collect M8 (customer experience) separately for "speed to appointment" and "working service" to align metrics with stakeholder priorities.

**Justification**:
This approach balances speed (for simple houses) and resilience (for high-risk cases) while avoiding the pitfalls of national scaling (tail risks) or full rollback (lost benefits). The experiments (M2/M6/M7) provide actionable insights to justify broader deployment only when risks are mitigated.
