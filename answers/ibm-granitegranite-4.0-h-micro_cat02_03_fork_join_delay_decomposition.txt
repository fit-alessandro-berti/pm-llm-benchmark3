# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let's define the components of our delay decomposition for each case `c` and branch `i` (A, B, C):

- **upstream_elapsed**: Time from arrival to `fork_release`
- **branch_queue**: Waiting time at the branch before it starts (`fork_release` to `start` time)
- **branch_service**: Time spent actively working at the branch (`start` to `end` time)
- **branch_synchronization_wait**: Time spent waiting for other branches to complete after finishing
- **critical_branch**: Branch that determines the `join_ready` time
- **downstream_queue**: Waiting time at the final station after `final_start`
- **final_service**: Time spent at the final station (`final_start` to `final_end`)

Summing all branch synchronization waits would double count because `branch_synchronization_wait` is already included in `join_ready`. `join_ready` represents the latest completion time among all branches, meaning any additional wait after this time would be counted in downstream_queue.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

1. **Reducing A service time by 20%**:
   - Direct Benefits: A's mean service time is 60 minutes. Reducing it by 20% (12 minutes) would directly decrease the total flow time by 12 minutes in cases where A is the critical branch (3 cases).
   - Cases with Zero Benefit: B and C are not affected directly by this change.
   - Likely Secondary Effects: Reducing A's service time might reduce overall queueing at the final station if A becomes less of a bottleneck.

2. **Reducing B queue delay by 50%**:
   - Direct Benefits: B's mean queue delay is 20 minutes. Reducing it by 50% (10 minutes) would decrease the total flow time by 10 minutes in cases where B is the critical branch (3 cases).
   - Cases with Zero Benefit: A and C are not affected directly by this change.
   - Likely Secondary Effects: Reducing B's queue delay would improve overall process efficiency without creating new bottlenecks.

3. **Adding final-station capacity or reducing downstream queueing**:
   - Direct Benefits: This directly addresses the downstream queueing component, which is a significant part of the total flow time (average 45 minutes across cases). Reducing downstream queueing could lead to a substantial improvement in total flow time.
   - Cases with Zero Benefit: All cases would benefit from this improvement.
   - Likely Secondary Effects: Improving final-station capacity could lead to better resource utilization and potentially reduce waiting times for other processes.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Redesign 1: Improve Branch C's Service Time**
- Benefits: As C is the most critical branch, improving its service time would directly reduce the total flow time in all cases. It would also reduce synchronization waiting times, leading to smoother process flow.
- Risks: Improving C's service time might require additional resources or training, which could be costly. There is also a risk of over-investing if C is not the actual bottleneck in practice.

**Redesign 2: Reduce Downstream Queueing**
- Benefits: By reducing downstream queueing, we can directly decrease the total flow time, especially in cases where the final station is a bottleneck. This improvement would benefit all cases uniformly.
- Risks: Increasing final-station capacity might require significant investment. Additionally, if the bottleneck is not at the final station, this improvement might not yield the expected benefits.

# 7. Limits of inference

From the given timestamps, we cannot identify causality between activities or the capacity of individual resources. We also cannot determine if the waiting observed is due to a queue or a release policy. The analysis assumes that the timestamps accurately reflect the process flow without any hidden delays or preemptions.
