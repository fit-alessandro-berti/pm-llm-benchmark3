# 3. Supporting and contradicting cases

## QA

- **Supporting cases for near-FIFO with appointments as an overlay**
  - A02, A04, A05, A06, and A07 are processed in a way that closely follows arrival order whenever no appointment is due: A02 before A04/A05, A05 before A06/A07, and A06 before A07/A08, even when this contradicts static priority and SPT.
  - A06, despite having an appointment at 09:15, still joins the FIFO stream and is only selected at 09:20, consistent with appointments being important but not strictly enforced, and with FIFO dominating until the appointment is near.
- **Contradicting or ambiguous cases**
  - A03 is clearly favored at 08:20 over older A02 and higher-priority A04; the note "appointment arrived early" suggests an appointment-first exception rather than a simple FIFO or priority rule.
  - Calibration of A1 from 08:52–08:58 creates an unavoidable gap; if additional unlogged work occurred during that window, the effective waiting time of some jobs (e.g., A04) might be longer than we observe, potentially hiding further exceptions.
- **Interpretation**
  - Most deviations from FIFO are explainable by appointment scheduling (A03, partially A06) and the calibration window, so the remaining unexplained variance is small; the main residual doubt is whether A03 reflects a codified policy ("always serve due appointments next") or just a one-off manual override.


## QB

- **Supporting cases for FIFO with P1 preemptive overrides**
  - The first preemption of B01 at 09:16 is explicitly linked to the arrival of P1 job B02 and fits a preemptive-priority rule in which P1 jobs can interrupt ongoing work.
  - B05 (also P1) is chosen at 10:00 ahead of longer-waiting B01 and ahead of shorter B04, again consistent with P1 jobs receiving special treatment independent of FIFO or SPT.
  - Among non-P1 jobs, many choices (e.g., resuming B01 at 09:31 before B03, and running B04 before B06/B07 becomes relevant) are consistent with default FIFO.
- **Contradicting or ambiguous cases**
  - At 09:48, "dispatcher reassignment" stops B01 in favor of B03 rather than P1 B05 or shortest B04; this violates both pure priority and pure SPT and indicates a more free-form human intervention.
  - At 10:14, B01 is resumed even though B06 (P2, 20 minutes) is already waiting; this conflicts with priority-based routing and also with some fairness arguments that might favor starting the newer but higher-priority job.
  - B07’s appointment at 10:30 is not respected: it waits until after B06, even though starting B07 at or near 10:30 would have been easy; this shows that the "appointment" field is weakly or not at all operational in QB.
- **Interpretation**
  - The clean preemption episodes linked to P1 jobs support a policy of "FIFO unless P1 arrives," but the dispatcher’s discretionary stop of B01 and the neglect of B07’s appointment show that the real system is a hybrid of coded rules and manual judgment rather than a pure preemptive-priority algorithm.


## QC

- **Supporting cases for SEPT with reactive SLA handling**
  - The initial backlog C01–C04 is processed strictly in increasing est_min: 8, 14, 22, then the remaining 36-minute C01, a textbook SEPT sequence that maximally delays the long job.
  - After the long overdue C05 completes at 11:34, the dispatcher switches to very short C06 (6 minutes) before returning to C01, again consistent with SEPT once the urgent job has been handled.
- **Contradicting or ambiguous cases**
  - C05, with earliest deadline 10:50 and highest priority, is ignored until 10:54; the note "deadline already at risk when selected" implies that deadline or priority urgency is only acted on after the SLA is violated, not proactively.
  - C07 (deadline 12:00, P2, 18 minutes) is postponed until after C01 from 11:40–12:16, ensuring that C07 misses its deadline; this severely contradicts any rule that seriously considers either deadlines or priorities and is also inconsistent with SEPT, since C07 is shorter than C01.
  - If arrival scans were delayed, C05 and C07 might have been present in the queue even earlier than recorded, which would make their neglect even more extreme under any urgency-aware policy.
- **Interpretation**
  - The data fit a picture where SEPT is the main heuristic on "normal" work, but individual urgent jobs occasionally trigger ad-hoc overrides that do not follow a stable, documented deadline policy; this mixture explains both the strong SEPT signature on C01–C04/C06 and the puzzling treatment of C05 and C07.


## QD

- **Supporting cases for skill-based routing with per-resource FIFO**
  - D05 (firmware+electrical) is served only by D-F, the single resource with both skills, even though D-X could handle generic electrical work; this is direct evidence of routing based on detailed skills.
  - D01, D04, D08, and D09 (pure mechanical) all run on D-M, while D06 and D10 (pure electrical without firmware) run on D-X; cross-routing opportunities exist but are not used, pointing to a routing policy that respects preferred skill-resource mappings.
  - Within each resource, sequences like D04 before D07/D08/D09 on D-M and D07 before D10 on D-X are consistent with FIFO when no special urgency is present.
- **Contradicting or ambiguous cases**
  - D-M’s decision at 11:35 to serve D08 (P1) before older D07 (P3) indicates that priority can override FIFO after some waiting time, at least for P1 jobs.
  - D-X is idle between 11:20 and 11:30, even though electrical or mechanical+electrical jobs are waiting; this suggests either a reservation for D06’s appointment or unlogged work, neither of which is captured by a simple skill-based FIFO rule.
  - D-F remains idle from 11:36 until its 12:00–12:20 meeting rather than starting the 30-minute D05 and pausing it, indicating sensitivity to calendar constraints and preemption costs that are not included in the simple dispatching mechanisms listed.
- **Interpretation**
  - The evidence strongly supports skill-based routing as the primary structure, but resource calendars, appointment honoring, and occasional priority escalation on P1 jobs complicate the inference of any single queueing discipline for the per-resource order.


# 4. Counterfactual discrimination tests

## Episode 1 – QC at 10:10: SEPT vs. deadline-driven control

- **Observed episode**
  - At 10:10, C1 becomes free with backlog C01–C04 (and possibly C05 if its 10:11 arrival was scanned late). The dispatcher chooses C02 (8 minutes) over earlier C01 (36 minutes) and higher-priority, earlier-deadline C03 (14 minutes, deadline 12:30).
- **Competing explanations**
  - **Explanation A (SEPT):** The queue primarily follows shortest expected processing time on non-urgent work, taking C02 because it is the smallest job and viewing all deadlines as far enough away to be irrelevant.
  - **Explanation B (deadline/slack-based):** The queue uses deadline or slack, but C03 and C05 are either not yet visible (due to scan delay) or their deadlines are interpreted with a wide tolerance, so no job is considered "urgent" yet; the dispatcher then falls back to FIFO or another secondary rule that happens to pick C02.
- **Next-job prediction under each explanation if we replayed the situation**
  - Under **SEPT**, we should continue to see the sequence 8 → 14 → 22 → 36 for the initial backlog, regardless of deadlines, which is in fact what we observe for C02–C04.
  - Under **deadline/slack-based control**, once any job’s slack drops below a threshold (e.g., C05 as its 10:50 deadline approaches), that job should jump to the front, which partly happens at 10:54 but only after the deadline is already missed.
- **Additional observable to discriminate**
  - Recording **decision-time slack per job** (deadline minus [decision time + estimated remaining]) or an explicit "urgency tier" field at each dispatch decision would reveal whether the dispatcher was actively computing and reacting to slack; under SEPT we would see choices that ignore small variations in slack when jobs are similar in size, whereas under a deadline policy, jobs whose slack crosses a threshold would be systematically promoted before the smallest jobs.


## Episode 2 – QD around 11:20–11:30 on D-X: appointment reservation vs. unlogged busy time

- **Observed episode**
  - D-X is nominally available from 11:20, while several electrical-capable jobs (D03, D06, D07) are waiting. Nevertheless, D-X stays idle until 11:30 and then starts D06 exactly at its appointment time, even though D07 has been waiting longer.
- **Competing explanations**
  - **Explanation A (appointment reservation):** The dispatcher reserves D-X for D06’s 11:30 appointment to avoid preemption or lateness, willingly leaving it idle rather than starting another job that would overlap the appointment.
  - **Explanation B (hidden occupation or availability):** D-X is actually busy or unavailable (e.g., in setup, calibration, or another unlogged activity) between 11:20 and 11:30, so from the dispatcher’s perspective there is no idle capacity to start D03 or D07; D06 then happens to start when D-X truly becomes available at 11:30.
- **Next-job prediction under each explanation**
  - Under **appointment reservation**, in similar future days we would expect D-X to idle before appointment jobs whenever starting another job would risk interfering with the appointment; non-appointment jobs would only be assigned when enough time remains to finish them cleanly.
  - Under **hidden occupation**, we would instead see D-X run non-appointment jobs whenever it is actually idle, with no systematic idling before appointment times; the apparent idle gap would disappear once all activities were properly logged.
- **Additional observable to discriminate**
  - Logging a detailed **resource-state timeline** for D-X (busy, idle, setup, meeting, reserved for appointment, etc.), including a "reason for idleness or reservation," would allow us to see whether the 11:20–11:30 gap was a deliberate reservation for D06 or simply a period of unlogged work; only in the former case would we interpret this as evidence of explicit appointment scheduling discipline.


# 5. Operational exceptions and alternative explanations

1. **Arrival-scan delays and incomplete visibility (most plausible)**
   - With up to two minutes of delay between physical arrival and scan, some jobs could have been visible to dispatch slightly earlier (or, in practice, later if scanning was batched). This affects who was eligible at decision times such as QC’s 10:10 and QB’s 09:48 events.
   - If some high-priority or near-deadline jobs were not yet visible at the moment of decision, the apparent violations of priority or deadline rules would be overstated, making the true policies closer to those documented (e.g., more consistent priority use in QC and QB).

2. **Preemption and setup costs (very plausible)**
   - Interrupting a job can incur significant overhead (re-setup, re-testing), which would discourage preemption except in genuine emergencies; this helps explain why B01 is only preempted for P1 jobs and why D-F and D-M avoid starting long jobs right before meetings or appointments.
   - If such costs were explicitly modeled, QB might be better described as "priority with high preemption penalties" and QD as "skill-based scheduling with calendar-aware nonpreemptive blocks," rather than the simpler hybrids inferred here.

3. **Resource calendar constraints beyond the logged windows (plausible)**
   - We see only one calibration and one meeting; in reality, there may be additional micro-breaks, changeovers, or informal commitments that make some jobs temporarily ineligible for certain resources (e.g., D-X doing maintenance from 11:20–11:30).
   - Accounting for these would reduce the apparent discretionary element in QD and might turn some seemingly inconsistent choices (like D-X’s idleness) into straightforward applications of a skill-based, calendar-aware rule.

4. **Hidden skill nuances and operator preferences (plausible)**
   - The "skills" column is coarse; within "mechanical" or "electrical" there may be sub-specialties, training, or tooling differences that lead dispatchers to favor specific resource–job pairings even when several appear eligible on paper.
   - If we knew, for example, that D-X is less efficient on certain mechanical tasks, the adherence of D01/D04/D08/D09 to D-M would look less like a queueing choice and more like a deterministic routing constraint, simplifying QD’s diagnosis.

5. **Ad-hoc SLA overrides and undocumented urgency flags (moderately plausible)**
   - Notes such as "urgent arrival" for B01’s first preemption and "deadline already at risk" for C05 suggest that dispatchers occasionally act on information not captured in the basic priority or deadline fields (e.g., customer escalation).
   - If a hidden "escalated" flag exists, some apparent violations of FIFO or SEPT would actually be consistent with a more complex but deterministic multi-level priority scheme, particularly in QB and QC; failing to observe this flag leads us to over-interpret those episodes as noise.

Collectively, these exceptions could shift our diagnosis toward cleaner priority or deadline-based policies, but only if supported by richer data; until then, the hybrid explanations remain more defensible.


# 6. Additional event data needed

To reduce ambiguity and separate genuine policies from artifacts of data quality and hidden constraints, the following additional fields and logs would be most valuable:

1. **Core cross-queue fields (highest priority)**
   - **Queue-entry time vs. physical arrival time:** Separate timestamps for physical arrival, scan time, and moment of eligibility would clarify which jobs were actually available at each dispatch decision.
   - **Dispatch-decision time and eligibility set snapshot:** For every service start or preemption, record the exact decision timestamp and the list of jobs considered eligible (with their priorities, deadlines, estimates, and skills) to reconstruct decision sets unambiguously.
   - **Preemption reason and cost tags:** Structured reasons (e.g., P1 emergency, appointment, resource calendar, setup constraint) and an estimate of expected preemption overhead would help distinguish rule-based from ad-hoc interruptions.
   - **Dynamic estimated remaining time:** For long or segmented jobs like B01 and C01, record updates to remaining-time estimates at each pause/resume to discriminate SEPT from static-estimate heuristics.

2. **QA-specific fields**
   - **Appointment window parameters:** For each appointment, record tolerated early/late bounds and whether starting before the window is allowed; this would clarify behavior around A03 and A06.
   - **Explicit "appointment-selected" flag:** Mark when a job was chosen specifically to honor an appointment, separating such choices from ordinary FIFO or priority decisions.

3. **QB-specific fields**
   - **Emergency/override flags per job instance:** Distinguish documented P1 priority from additional ad-hoc urgency (e.g., clinical escalation) that might have driven preemptions beyond what the static priority code implies.
   - **Dispatcher action type:** Label decisions as "automatic rule," "manual override," or "reassignment due to downstream constraints" to interpret episodes like B01’s second preemption.

4. **QC-specific fields**
   - **Deadline interpretation metadata:** For each job, record whether the deadline is hard, soft, or SLA-based and what lateness tolerance exists; also capture any dynamically computed "slack" or "urgency tier."
   - **Policy version or mode at decision time:** Indicate when the system switches into an "SLA recovery" mode, which might explain the sudden service of C05 and later handling of C07.

5. **QD-specific fields**
   - **Detailed resource calendars and reservations:** Log not only meetings and long maintenance but also reserved appointment blocks (e.g., D-X held for D06), setup/changeover periods, and reasons for resource idleness.
   - **Per-resource skill preferences or penalties:** Include a matrix of effective speeds or efficiencies for each resource–skill combination, so that routing choices can be interpreted as cost-minimizing rather than arbitrary.

With these additions, it would become much easier to test precise hypotheses (e.g., pure SEPT vs. deadline/slack-based scheduling) and to separate skill-based routing from calendar or override effects.


# 7. Final synthesis

Across queues, **QD** is the most identifiable: job-to-resource assignments strongly reflect explicit skill constraints and calendars, and the remaining variation is plausibly explained by soft priorities and appointments. **QB** appears least identifiable, because its mix of FIFO behavior, P1-based preemptions, and opaque "dispatcher reassignment" episodes could arise from many different hybrids of rules and manual judgment. QC shows the clearest structural signal (a SEPT-like bias against long jobs) but also some of the sharpest contradictions to any simple policy, especially around deadlines. The most dangerous claim to infer from these observations alone would be that the system consistently enforces *formal* priority or deadline policies (particularly in QB and QC); in reality, those fields only partially explain behavior, and relying on them without richer event data could lead to badly mistaken performance and SLA analyses.
