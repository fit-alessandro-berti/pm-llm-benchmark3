# 1. Objectives and constraints

The policy should protect customers and meet deadlines while keeping work moving, developing skills, distributing load fairly, preserving useful case knowledge, and maintaining backup capacity. These aims conflict: a specialist may finish a task quickly but create a queue; a training assignment may build capacity but require supervision and take longer. The appropriate balance depends on actual task duration, availability, and customer risk, so the queue below is a conditional recommendation, not a proven optimum.

**Safety and operating rules:**

- A skill score of 0 is prohibited. Security requires level 3, or level 1–2 with explicit supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo. Final Approval requires level 2–3 for the final decision; a level-1 worker may prepare only, with a qualified final approver. High-priority work, including urgent work, requires at least level 2 unless supervised. These rules apply to the performer, regardless of ownership.
- Limit each person to four new assignments in this decision batch. Count a supervision commitment as a new assignment for this limit, as well as reserving time for it. An existing owner receiving a newly assigned task is counted too; the cap is separate from open cases.
- Do not assign new work to a high-overtime-risk resource unless the case is urgent and no safer feasible alternative exists. Quality batching is permitted only for normal/low priority with more than 24 hours remaining, and only if waiting to form the batch is safe.
- At least 20% of normal/low assignments should support the performer's documented training priority when SLA risk remains acceptable. Treat this as a conditional target: do not override skill, supervision, capacity, or deadline rules to meet it. Document any shortfall and the reason.
- Prefer continuity for high-complexity, high-handover-cost cases unless the current owner's skill is 0 or 1 and supervision is infeasible. Where the owner cannot legally perform the activity, retain them as case coordinator if useful and arrange a qualified performer; do not confuse case ownership with task performance.

Within these boundaries, SLA risk, skill fit, continuity, equitable workload, development, and resilience are scored as preferences. A level-2 competent worker is often a sensible choice even if a specialist exists. Open cases alone do not measure workload: remaining effort, complexity, availability, supervision, and overtime exposure matter too. Historical associations—1.8 times rework and 1.4 times appeals after more than two reassignments, median 2.7 hours additional waiting after a high-complexity owner change, and median 3.3 hours extra service with a weak-skilled owner—inform caution but are not causal estimates or guaranteed durations.

# 2. Assignment policy

At each decision point, refresh the queue, worker availability and skills, active commitments, supervisory capacity, and estimated remaining effort. Use activity- and complexity-specific duration and waiting-time distributions, including supervision, handover and downstream work. These estimates need calibration; if missing, flag uncertainty and ask a dispatcher to validate a reservation rather than treating hours remaining as a duration estimate.

1. **Build feasible options.** Generate performer and, where needed, named supervisor options. Enforce the rules above, availability, the four-assignment cap and non-overlapping time reservations for both people. A trainee assignment counts toward the training target only when the activity matches that performer's documented training priority and the required supervision is actually available. Record the level-2/3 final approver separately from any trainee preparer. An unowned case has no handover penalty.
2. **Rank case urgency.** Estimate slack as time to SLA minus a conservative estimate of remaining queue, service, handover, supervision and downstream time. Rank by predicted probability and consequence of breach, considering priority and uncertainty; negative or small slack and urgent priority raise urgency. Recompute after each reservation. Do not equate priority with a known breach probability.
3. **Score feasible options for each case.** Use an interpretable weighted score, with components and weights visible to workers and periodically reviewed:
   - Skill fit: favor appropriate competence and reduce expected error; allow safe, supervised learning where appropriate. Avoid giving every routine case to a specialist.
   - Continuity: reward keeping the qualified owner and subtract expected handover delay and cost, increasing the penalty for high complexity, high handover cost and repeated reassignments. Account for the separate cost of retaining a weak-skilled performer.
   - Fairness: penalize predicted work relative to available capacity, complexity-adjusted existing load, supervision commitments and overtime risk. Open-case counts are a proxy, not the sole measure.
   - Development: reward supervised work that matches a documented training priority, especially when the conditional 20% target is at risk. Subtract the supervisor's time and any expected extra service time.
   - Resilience: penalize over-concentration on scarce specialists or teams and preserve feasible backup coverage for Security and Approval, subject to urgent cases taking precedence.
   - SLA: favor options with lower predicted breach risk, including their effects on already reserved work and other cases sharing the same scarce resource.
4. Process the most at-risk cases first, tentatively reserving resources and supervisors. Check the resulting batch jointly for overlapping commitments, cascading deadline risk, training share and concentration. Reconsider earlier choices when a later case has no safe option; a constrained batch optimizer or a dispatcher can do this. Weights and thresholds are policy parameters, not established facts.
5. Break near-ties by lower predicted breach risk, then qualified ownership continuity, then lower projected load relative to capacity; use a rotating or otherwise documented fair tie-break for remaining ties. Record the chosen option, key reasons, estimated slack, training and supervision arrangements, rejected alternatives and rule exceptions.
6. If no option is safe, place the case in an explicit exception queue, alert the responsible dispatcher, protect the deadline through escalation or qualified backup, and record the reason. Never silently relax a safety rule. Workers can challenge a mismatched assignment or report unavailable capacity; a human may override preferences with a recorded reason, while mandatory constraints remain in force. Recalculate when deadlines, availability or case facts change.

# 5. Explanation to workers

Assignments take account of the deadline, the skills needed, who already knows the case, current work and available time. Some tasks must go to a qualified specialist or have a named supervisor. When there is room to learn safely, we also try to match work to your recorded development goals. Supervisors need time reserved for that work.

The recommendation is a starting point, not a judgment about your value or speed. Open-case counts do not capture all the effort involved. You can flag unavailable time, missing context, an unsuitable assignment or a training need to the dispatcher. The dispatcher can adjust the plan and will record the reason, while keeping the qualification and supervision rules. We will review workload, deadlines, handovers and development opportunities with the team and use that feedback to improve the policy.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Use a defined observation period and report counts and denominators, distributions and trends, broken down where appropriate by activity, team, priority, complexity and worker. Distinguish assignment, task execution, case ownership, supervision and completion; a handover between event performers is not automatically an ownership reassignment. Compare like case mixes, include incomplete cases in open-backlog measures, and avoid treating correlations as proof of policy effects.

| Indicator | Definition and use |
|---|---|
| Handover frequency | Performer transitions between consecutive ordered events per case or per 100 cases, including the handover network by source and destination; distinguish within-team from cross-team and exclude artificial transitions as appropriate. Separately count documented owner changes. |
| Reassignment count | Distribution and share of cases with more than two actual assignment/owner changes; reconcile `reassignment_count` snapshots with timestamped assignment history and flag resets or missing values. |
| Skill mismatch | Share of task assignments or executions below the required level, including prohibited level 0, high-priority trainee work without a valid supervisor, Security rule breaches, and Approval finalized below level 2. Join a time-valid skill matrix and supervisor record; report missing data separately. |
| Workload distribution | Per-worker new assignments, open cases, estimated and actual effort, supervision time, utilization and dispersion or percentile ratios, adjusted for available hours, complexity and part-time schedules. Track the batch cap. |
| Overtime risk exposure | Share and volume of new assignments and projected/actual work given to workers marked high overtime risk at decision time, with urgency, alternatives and documented exception reason. Risk labels are not actual overtime. |
| SLA misses | Completed cases past deadline divided by completed cases with valid deadlines, plus currently overdue open cases and time-to-deadline distribution. Stratify by priority and report censored/incomplete cases separately. |
| Rework and appeal after assignment | Share and rate of assigned cases with subsequent rework or appeal, and time to occurrence; distinguish initial work from later loops, define an observation window, and adjust comparisons for case mix and incomplete follow-up. |
| Training assignment share | Normal/low assignments matching the performer's documented priority divided by all normal/low assignments; separately show supervision, competency sign-off, acceptable SLA risk and the conditional 20% target. Report unavailable or unknown priority data. |
| Specialist bottlenecks | Security and Approval queue age, backlog, waiting/service-time distributions, utilization and share of work by specialist; compare with qualified backup capacity and absence scenarios. Network concentration alone is not a queue measure. |
| Policy deviations | Share of decisions violating a mandatory rule or differing from the recorded recommendation; distinguish authorized preference overrides, infeasibility exceptions, unknown data and actual rule violations. Audit reason, approver, timing and outcome. |

Capture assignment decision time, chosen performer, owner before and after, candidate eligibility, skill-matrix version, supervisor and reserved time, estimated effort and risk, score components, alternative/override reason, and actual start/completion. The listed log columns alone do not contain all of these, historical skill versions, available hours, supervision, actual overtime, or an explicit appeal definition. Add timestamped decision, staffing and outcome records and join them by case and activity instance.
