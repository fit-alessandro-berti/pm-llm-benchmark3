# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness concerns whether applicants with comparable needs and relevant circumstances receive justified, accessible, timely, and consistently administered procedures. Equal treatment need not mean identical paths: language support, accommodation review, and justified fraud review can require different activities. Those differences still need scrutiny when they create avoidable delay, effort, or barriers to exercising rights.

| Dimension | Assessment from the evidence |
|---|---|
| Outcome parity | Approval rates range from 58% to 78%. These are descriptive disparities, not evidence by themselves of discrimination. Eligibility, case mix, and access to the process may differ. Equalizing approval rates alone would not establish procedural fairness. |
| Adjusted outcome disparity | Relative to Group A, the preliminary model reports approval differences of −5.5 percentage points for B, −2.2 for C, and −3.1 for D. These residual associations warrant investigation; they do not identify discriminatory decisions. Model specification, omitted needs, biased covariates, and statistical uncertainty remain unresolved. |
| Procedural burden | Additional document requests, reviews, repeat contacts, and handovers can impose substantial effort. B and C have elevated adjusted document-request and supervisor-review probabilities. Necessary support should be distinguished from duplicated checks, unexplained escalation, and repeated demands for information already provided. |
| Timeliness | B and C have the largest adjusted cycle-time differences, +2.6 and +4.1 days. Their raw 90th-percentile durations are also much longer than A's. Fairness requires examining delays at particular stages, urgent cases, and access to benefits, rather than relying only on average or median completion time. |
| Consistency of routing | Higher measured risk, accommodation requests, and language-support requests justify specified routes under the stated policy. Check that comparable cases trigger the same requirements, exceptions have documented reasons, and required support is actually delivered. More supervisor review for B or C is not automatically justified by needing support. |
| Quality and accessibility of support | Recording a support activity does not establish that interpretation was accurate, an accommodation was effective, or the applicant could participate meaningfully. Support delivered late may formally satisfy a step while failing its practical purpose. |
| Appeal and rework burden | B and D have elevated adjusted appeal probabilities; B and C have high raw rework rates. These may signal unclear decisions, avoidable errors, or burdensome document requirements. Higher appeals may also reflect better awareness or access to appeal, so rates alone cannot establish poor decision quality. |
| Resource-allocation fairness | Bilingual and accommodation work is concentrated in small teams, with overloaded officers and overlapping specialist duties. Skill-based assignment is appropriate, but allocating insufficient capacity to rights-preserving services can produce structural unfairness without intentional differential treatment. |

The relevant standard is justified differentiation with effective access and reasonable timeliness. The absence of support-specific service targets leaves an important dimension of that standard unmeasured; it does not demonstrate that existing delays are acceptable.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Use several explicitly defined comparisons rather than one model that treats every recorded difference as legitimate complexity.

1. **Define the population, outcomes, and time horizon.** Use one case-level record for each application, alongside its full event history. Distinguish time to first notification, time to activation for approved cases, appeal-stage time, and time to final closure. Report activation time conditional on approval as a separate measure because it excludes denied applicants. Account for pending cases and unequal observation windows with survival methods or comparable follow-up. Use completion cohorts only as a sensitivity analysis. Establish whether the supplied cycle-time measure includes appeals and reopening. Analyze case-level document-request counts, repeated assessments, handovers, support delivery, and tail delays as well as approvals.

2. **Match or stratify on relevant information available before the routing or decision under examination.** Candidate variables are baseline risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, submission month, and channel. Also include language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested when comparing applicants with similar support needs. Prefer exact strata for essential policy triggers and region/channel, supplemented by coarser matching, weighting, or regression for other factors. Check balance, sample sizes, and common support. If comparable Group A cases needing a particular service are scarce, report that limitation rather than extrapolating from unsupported matches.

   Produce two views: an overall access comparison that retains the burden associated with needing language support or accommodation, and a comparison within similar needs to assess delivery and routing. Channel and submission documentation may themselves reflect accessibility barriers; adjusting for them answers a narrower question and should not erase those barriers from the overall assessment. The preliminary model does not explicitly control for language preference or accommodation requests, so some residual differences may reflect additional required work.

3. **Separate routing differences from differences within routes.** First compare probabilities of required support, Fraud Review, Supervisor Review, document requests, and repeated work within baseline strata. Then compare durations and burdens within exact observed variants, and within broader route families where exact sequences are too sparse. Compare equivalent stages and support episodes, including multiple requests per case. Show how much of an observed difference is associated with route composition versus delays within comparable routes, using a common reference distribution where overlap permits.

   Variants, assigned resources, extra document requests, and supervisor reviews may be mechanisms of disparity. Do not include them as routine controls in the primary overall comparison. Conditioning on them can hide unfair routing or introduce selection bias. A within-variant comparison is a useful diagnostic, not an estimate of the whole process's fairness. For example, V1 includes Benefit Activation and therefore selects a particular outcome path; it is unsuitable as a stratum for assessing approval disparity.

4. **Compare assignment and handovers.** Estimate whether cases with similar baseline complexity and support needs are assigned to different queues, regions, workers, or levels of experience. Examine assignment shares, caseload per available staff-hour, delay before a worker accepts a case, and handovers per case. Compare within the same region and calendar period, and within appropriately skilled pools. Worker or queue effects can identify where differences concentrate, but a model controlling for assignment measures disparity remaining after assignment; it cannot establish that assignment itself is fair. Assignment may respond to unrecorded complexity or congestion.

5. **Decompose elapsed time using valid timestamps.** Separate agency queueing, active processing, applicant response time, transfers, support stages, initial decision, and appeals where those states are observed. With only the supplied single event timestamp, report event-to-event elapsed time rather than calling all of it waiting. Obtain validated activity start/completion and document-request/receipt records before estimating the components separately. Analyze distributions and deadline exceedances within matched strata. Do not add the supplied stage medians to reconstruct median case duration: medians are not additive, and the stages involve different subsets of cases. Handle overlapping activities without double-counting wall-clock time.

6. **Test alternative explanations and quantify uncertainty.** Repeat adjustments with and without risk_score_band and documentation_completeness, separately and jointly; distinguish their predictive usefulness from their legitimacy as fairness controls. Where available, compare the risk band with independently audited risk indicators and documentation with an accessible, consistently applied checklist. Examine alternative specifications, interactions, missing-data assumptions, censoring, and sensitivity to unmeasured complexity. Report adjusted effect sizes, intervals, denominators, balance, and unsupported strata. Account for repeated events within cases and shared resource/queue conditions; few regional or specialist clusters will limit precision.

   Check region × group and channel × group differences, including whether the two backlogged regions account for much of B's excess burden. Compare online and in-person applicants within relevant strata, and inspect intersections such as minority-language applicants requesting accommodation. Small-cell results require cautious interpretation and privacy protection.

A **disparity** is an observed or adjusted difference. **Legitimate routing** requires an applicable rule, a relevant trigger, consistent execution, and a proportionate burden. **Discrimination** requires stronger evidence that group membership or an unjustified proxy changes treatment, such as comparable-case review or documented differential standards. **Uncertainty** includes missing comparators, measurement problems, unrecorded needs, and imprecise estimates. Statistical adjustment alone cannot resolve these distinctions.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Group B shows concentrated language-related and document-related burden.** V1 accounts for 19% of B cases versus 42% of A cases, while V2 accounts for 34% versus 24%. The language-support V4 accounts for 24% of B cases versus 1% of A cases and has a pooled median duration of 19.2 days. B also has adjusted increases of 9.5 percentage points in document requests and 8.0 points in supervisor review. Some additional document work is consistent with B's greater recorded incompleteness, but recorded incompleteness may partly reflect inaccessible forms or language barriers. The remaining adjusted differences require scrutiny for inconsistent requirements, repeated requests, and omitted complexity.

B's median Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents interval is 1.6 days versus A's 0.4 days, and its Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment interval is 3.8 days. Seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers handle a disproportionate share of these cases. General → bilingual → general handovers in two regions plausibly create queue changes and loss of continuity. Language support is justified; avoidable delay arising from how that support is staffed and integrated remains concerning. There is no comparable language-support interval reported for A, so the 3.8 days is not a measured adjusted B-versus-A support penalty.

**Group C has the strongest adjusted timing concern.** The accommodation route V5 accounts for 31% of C cases versus 2% of A cases and has a pooled median duration of 21.6 days. C's Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment interval is 4.7 days. Its adjusted cycle-time difference is +4.1 days, alongside +5.5 percentage points in supervisor review and +4.2 points in document requests. Required accommodation review explains the presence of a distinct route, but not automatically its queue duration or additional supervisor escalation. Five specialists, two also handling supervisor review, create a plausible capacity and competing-duty bottleneck. The general → specialist → supervisor chain may add necessary expertise, duplicated review, or both; records of review reasons and work performed are needed to distinguish them.

**Group D's routing has a substantial documented complexity explanation, with remaining concerns.** V3 accounts for 28% of D cases versus 9% of A cases, consistent with D's 51% high-risk share and 49% high-prior-case-count share. Risk Screening → Fraud Review takes 2.8 days for D versus 2.2 for A. Its adjusted cycle-time difference is comparatively small at +0.9 days. These patterns are compatible with required fraud review rather than arbitrary routing. However, the risk measure may embed historical inequity, and prior cases need not represent current fraud or ineligibility. The −3.1-point adjusted approval difference and +5.7-point adjusted appeal difference merit case review, including the accuracy and explanations of adverse decisions.

**Shared regional and intake conditions matter.** Submit → Identity Verification takes 1.4 days for B and 1.2 for C versus 0.8 for A; in-person intake contributes to this pattern. Supervisor Review → Decision Draft takes 1.9 days for B and 1.6 for C versus 1.0 for A, with known backlog in two regions. These intervals suggest specific sites for investigation, but regional composition and route complexity must be considered. The smaller notification-stage differences do not remove the earlier burden.

The variant medians are pooled, not group-specific adjusted durations. V4 and V5 being slower than V1 does not establish unequal treatment within an equivalent path. The displayed variants are summaries and do not exhaust each group's cases. Their shares also do not establish support coverage: 57% needing language support versus 24% taking V4, or 62% requesting accommodation versus 31% taking V5, cannot demonstrate missing support because other variants may contain those activities. Likewise, omitted activities in a summarized path cannot establish a procedural violation.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

- **Legitimate additional assessment.** Higher relevant risk can require Fraud Review; incomplete applications can require documents; language and disability needs can require specialist assistance. This can increase elapsed time even with appropriate treatment. Validate the trigger, necessity, and proportionality rather than assuming that every extra step is unfair.
- **Insufficient specialist capacity.** Small bilingual and accommodation teams can accumulate longer queues than general teams. Dual specialist/supervisor duties can intensify the problem. This would generate unequal access through allocation decisions even if individual workers apply identical standards.
- **Fragmented routing and ownership.** Each general-to-specialist transfer can require scheduling, rereading, and reassignment. Returning a case to a general queue may lose its place or prompt another check. Clear responsibility and transferable case information could reduce this burden without removing support.
- **Accessibility barriers producing apparent complexity.** Untranslated instructions, inaccessible upload systems, or unclear document requirements can increase recorded incompleteness. Repeated requests then lengthen the path. Controlling for incompleteness could conceal part of the process's contribution to the disparity.
- **Differential discretion or escalation.** Staff may escalate minority-language or accommodation cases because of uncertainty, inadequate training, inconsistent standards, or bias. The adjusted supervisor-review differences are compatible with this mechanism but do not distinguish it from omitted case complexity.
- **Historically biased risk classification.** Prior investigations, repeated contacts, and earlier unequal treatment may influence risk scores and prior-case measures. A formally consistent risk rule can reproduce unjustified historical scrutiny.
- **Regional congestion and differing appeal access.** Uneven staffing can concentrate delays where certain groups live. More appeals may follow unclear or incorrect decisions, while accessible assistance can also increase appeals by helping applicants exercise their rights. Both mechanisms fit elevated appeal rates.

These mechanisms can coexist. Link each hypothesis to observable checks: queue occupancy, assignment timing, handover histories, audit findings, support quality, escalation reasons, and appeal results.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement and timing are incomplete.** A risk band is coarse, documentation may change during the process, and channel may change after submission. Final documentation status must not be treated as baseline complexity. Timestamp meanings, concurrent activities, logging practices, and missing events can distort paths. The given schema has no activity-start timestamp or lifecycle status; it cannot identify active service and queue time separately. Case-outcome and appeal flags may be final states rather than the states known at the original decision.

**Applicant needs are only partly represented.** Language preference does not identify proficiency, interpreter availability, literacy, or the actual request for support. An accommodation-request flag does not capture the type of accommodation, urgency, whether the request was recognized, or whether the adjustment worked. Household-size bands omit relevant household and eligibility details. Digital access, transport, caregiving demands, housing instability, and the feasibility of obtaining documents are unobserved. Some needs may justify more work; others reveal barriers that the agency should address.

**Resource workload is too coarse for precise attribution.** Daily workload does not show staff-hours, leave, part-time schedules, skill mix, urgent assignments, task duration, or intra-day queue conditions. The counts of 52 general workers, seven bilingual workers, and five specialists are not comparable capacity measures without demand and available hours. Handover networks describe recorded transfers, not whether information was lost or a transfer was helpful. Shared work may indicate collaboration rather than burden.

**Appeal and rework quality are missing.** Appeal flags do not show grounds, time limits, withdrawal, representation, successful reversals, or the quality and independence of review. Assess appeal rates among applicants eligible to appeal using the initial decision and adequate follow-up; conditioning on final outcome can misclassify applicants whose appeal changed that outcome. Low appeal rates can mean satisfaction or inability to appeal. Repeated activities are not necessarily erroneous rework, and the activity list contains no explicit reopening event. Dedicated reopen records and reasons are needed to validate the summary measure.

**Historical bias is unresolved.** Risk scores may encode prior discriminatory surveillance, geographic proxies, or unequal documentation. Prior-case counts can reflect need or earlier procedural failures rather than fraud. Documentation_completeness may depend on inaccessible requirements and staff judgments. Omitting these measures can leave genuine complexity uncontrolled; including them can adjust away inequity. Both views and independent audits are necessary.

**Causal and experiential evidence is absent.** No confidence intervals, matching diagnostics, independent eligibility assessment, explanation-quality measures, or applicant experience data are provided. Unobserved complexity and selection into completed cases can affect estimates. Group definitions may bundle language, disability, channel, and risk, limiting overlap and making group effects difficult to interpret. The evidence cannot establish discriminatory intent, a legal violation, or how much of the remaining disparity is avoidable. It can identify concrete burdens and credible hypotheses for investigation.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Option | Intended benefit | Side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add bilingual and accommodation capacity, including protected specialist time and coverage | Reduce queueing and competing duties; align available skilled hours with demand and urgency. | Hiring and training take time and money. Moving existing workers may lengthen other queues; cross-training without proficiency checks can lower support quality. Monitor all queues and effective support delivery. |
| Set service-level targets for support steps | Measure time from a support request to effective assistance and subsequent assessment, with escalation for overdue cases and appropriate urgency. Include tail delays, not just medians. | Narrow targets may encourage premature completion, rushed accommodations, or prioritizing easy cases. Pair timeliness with quality measures and transparent treatment of complex cases and applicant-controlled pauses. |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers | Retain a case owner, integrate specialist consultation, transfer information once, and preserve queue priority when cases return. | Broader responsibilities can overload general workers or dilute expertise. Some independent or specialist reviews are necessary. Pilot the redesign and retain required support and separation of duties. |
| Improve decision explanations and accessible communications | Explain the evidence, missing documents, reasons for decisions, available support, and appeal rights in usable languages and formats. | Translation, accessibility testing, and individualized explanations require capacity. Templates can obscure case-specific reasons. Appeals may initially rise because access improves; assess comprehension and avoidable reversals alongside appeal volume. |
| Monitor adjusted disparities and burdens | Track route entry, repeated requests, waiting distributions, support coverage, outcomes, and appeal/rework indicators by group, region, channel, and support need. Show raw and adjusted results with uncertainty. | Weak models can hide bias or produce unstable alerts. Small cells pose privacy and precision problems, and targets can invite gaming. Keep transparent definitions, baseline covariate timing, and qualitative case review. |
| Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness | Check provenance, calibration, predictive validity, group-specific error patterns, proxy use, accessibility of requirements, and consistency of completeness judgments. Revise unjustified triggers. | Reclassification changes queue demand and may miss legitimate risk if poorly designed. Audits need sound reference outcomes and may themselves inherit biased labels. Test revisions prospectively and review downstream burden and errors. |
| Make submission and document collection more accessible | Provide usable forms, translated checklists, assisted submission, and coordinated requests for information already available. | Assistance requires staff time and secure information handling. Simplification can omit genuinely necessary evidence; accessible standards should preserve justified eligibility requirements. |

Evaluate changes with a staged rollout or another credible comparison, measuring support quality, total burden, tail delays, decision accuracy, and effects on other queues. Preserve language support and accommodation review. Success means applicants receive those rights effectively with less avoidable friction, not merely that support activities disappear from the log.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

Overall procedural fairness is **indeterminate, with credible evidence of unequal burden and serious concerns about support capacity and routing**. Case mix explains some route differences, particularly D's greater fraud review, but it does not establish that B's and C's support-related queues, handovers, and additional reviews are proportionate or avoidable only through legitimate work.

The adjusted disparities justify investigation and targeted improvements while preserving support rights. They do not establish discrimination or identify a causal group penalty. A stronger conclusion requires comparable-needs analysis, valid waiting/service measurements, audited risk and documentation measures, and evidence about decision and support quality.
