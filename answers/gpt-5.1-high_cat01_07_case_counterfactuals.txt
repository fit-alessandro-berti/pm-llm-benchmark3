```text
Conditional and declarative constraints
- Control-flow:
  - Register must be first, then Triage.
  - Identity Check and Document Check must both precede any Assessment for that cycle.
  - Originals Receipt must precede the first Document Check when originals are required.
  - High-risk or high-value cases require a Compliance Review after the latest Assessment and before Decision.
  - Decision must precede Notify.
- Rework:
  - After Request More Info_n, there must be a new Document Check_(n+1) and Assessment_(n+1) before any Decision.
  - For high-risk/high-value, Compliance Review must cover the final Assessment cycle (new review or explicit reaffirmation).
- Four-eyes and roles:
  - The resource performing the final Assessment must differ from the resource performing the final Compliance Review.
  - Preliminary compliance work by C1 or S1 does not substitute for final Compliance Review.
- Resources and queues:
  - Each named resource (T1, T2, I1, I2, D1, D2, A1, A2, C1, S1, M1) can serve at most one activity at a time.
  - Queue forecasts for each pool at snapshot times (e.g., Mon 08:00, Tue 08:00) give expected, not guaranteed, waits.
  - Service times follow the given rough ranges but may vary within those intervals.
- Timing and concurrency:
  - Identity and Document checks may overlap when no originals are required.
  - Escalation/Preliminary compliance work by S1/C1 may overlap with identity/document work but must still respect resource non-overlap.
  - Real-time timestamps must match actual start/finish, so reported concurrency must correspond to true overlap.
```

Interventions by type:
- K-104 (early escalation and provisional C1 slot) primarily alters **timing**, by starting preliminary compliance earlier and reserving capacity, while keeping the same mandatory activities and decision policy. It slightly extends the route by adding preliminary compliance work but does not remove any checks.
- K-219 (switching to D2 as soon as originals arrive) primarily alters **routing**, by changing which document-resource handles the case and the induced probability of rework; the decision policy and mandatory steps remain the same.
- K-337 (forbid second Request More Info loop) explicitly alters the **allowed decision policy**, by removing an admissible rework path after Assessment 2 and forcing a decision (approve with conditions or reject) under greater evidential uncertainty.
- K-411 (parallel identity and document checks under a case-lock workaround) primarily alters **timing and concurrency**, by creating both tasks at Triage and allowing them to run in parallel, with logging constraints to preserve an auditable route.

## 3. Counterfactual derivations

### K-104

Factual mechanism: The case is high-risk and high-value, so it ultimately requires compliance review. In the factual path, sanctions ambiguity is noticed at Triage but not escalated, so Identity and Document checks proceed normally. Assessment by A1 on Tue re-discovers the issue and only then triggers escalation to S1; compliance has to pick up the case late in its queue on Wed and read most evidence from scratch, leading to a long effective wait at the C1 bottleneck plus a relatively long review.

Minimal intervention: The minimal change is to package the sanctions concern at Triage into a preliminary escalation packet for S1 and to reserve a provisional C1 timeslot aligned with an expected post-Assessment readiness window. S1 (and possibly C1 in a preliminary capacity) can skim the case early, identify key risk dimensions, and ensure that when full documents and the final Assessment are available, compliance already has context and a reserved window.

Temporal propagation: Early escalation does not change when Identity and Document checks can start, because those are governed by the Mon 08:00–16:00 queue and service ranges for I1 and D1. The main temporal shift is that some compliance reading and reasoning happens on Mon/Tue instead of entirely on Wed afternoon, and the reserved slot reduces stochastic queueing delay at C1. When Assessment finishes on Tue evening, the case can be slotted into an earlier point in the Wed schedule (e.g., mid-morning instead of early afternoon), and the final review itself is slightly shorter because the reviewer is partially pre-familiar with the file.

Process-feasibility check: All mandatory steps remain: Identity Check and Document Check both precede Assessment; Assessment precedes Compliance; Compliance precedes Decision; and four-eyes is preserved (A1 vs C1). Preliminary escalation advice and reading do not replace the final review, they only front-load some analysis. Resource constraints are respected because S1 and C1 still execute non-overlapping intervals, and the partial-order allows preliminary work to be concurrent with identity/document tasks without violating any “start after” rule. Queue forecasts remain approximate but the reserved slot is chosen to fall after the latest plausible Assessment completion time.

Best/base/worst case: In a best case, C1 can schedule a preliminary 2–3 h skim on Mon afternoon and reserve an early Wed slot, leading to ~6–10 h faster completion with equal or better sanctions analysis quality. In a base case, preliminary work occurs later on Tue, so the final review still starts earlier than observed but by only a few hours, yielding a 3–6 h cycle-time improvement. In a worst case, the reserved slot is late on Wed due to other urgent cases, preliminary packets are incomplete, and re-reading erodes any gains, making cycle time close to or only marginally better than factual.

Evidence that would reverse the conclusion: If empirical data showed that early packets are almost always so incomplete that C1 has to re-read everything, and that reserved slots consistently displace more critical high-risk cases in the queue, the net quality and fairness impact could be negative. Likewise, if S1 and C1 capacity is already so tight on Mon/Tue that preliminary work leads to overtime or error spikes, the intervention might not be justified. Strong evidence that queue forecasts at Triage are systematically inaccurate (so reserved slots are often mistimed) would also weaken the case for this policy.

### K-219

Factual mechanism: Handwritten but standard documents are routed to D1 because D1 is considered better at difficult handwriting. At Mon 16:00, D1’s document queue is long, so even after originals arrive at 17:30 the case must wait until Tue 09:30 for a 4 h document check. That clean run (no rework) allows Assessment on Tue night and a straightforward approval on Wed morning. The main driver of delay is the D1 queue, not service time or assessment capacity.

Minimal intervention: The minimal change is to route this case to D2 as soon as originals arrive, without holding it for D1. D2 is slower on complex corporate documents but is a generalist for standard cases, so we treat this handwritten case as borderline but still eligible. All other routing (Identity to I2, Assessment to A2, Decision to M1) stays the same; no extra compliance steps are introduced.

Temporal propagation: Because D2’s queue is shorter (forecast ~5 h at Mon 16:00, versus D1’s 11 h), the case can likely start document checking on D2 during Mon evening or night rather than waiting until Tue morning. Given D2’s 4–8 h range for standard documents, this means the document result is often ready by early Tue, allowing earlier entry into the Assessment queue. If A2 can pick up the case earlier on Tue (or overnight in a shift setting), the Decision and Notify steps can move correspondingly forward. However, when handwriting is truly difficult, D2 is more likely than D1 to pass ambiguity to Assessment or to trigger Request More Info, creating a potential rework loop that adds external waiting time.

Process-feasibility check: The intervention does not change control-flow: Register and Triage remain first; Identity and Document precede Assessment; there is no Compliance Review because the case is neither high-risk nor high-value. Any Request More Info still requires a new Document Check and new Assessment before Decision, so the loop remains compliant with the rules. Resource constraints are preserved because D2, A2, and M1 do not perform overlapping tasks, and there is no four-eyes requirement beyond assessment vs compliance. The main feasibility risk is ensuring that D2 is actually competent enough with this level of handwriting to avoid silent misinterpretation.

Best/base/worst case: In the best case, D2 starts document work a few hours after Mon 17:30 and finishes by early Tue, so Assessment can occur earlier on Tue and the case might complete **10–12 h** faster with no additional rework. In a base case, D2 still gains several hours against D1’s queue, but slight clarification requests or slower reading lead to only **6–8 h** improvement in cycle time. In the worst case, D2’s difficulty with handwriting triggers a Request More Info loop with an external response of 8–36 h, making the overall cycle **longer by 4–10 h** than the factual path and possibly pushing completion into Thu.

Evidence that would reverse the conclusion: If operational data show that D2 frequently mishandles handwritten documents—either by misreading them without escalating or by almost always triggering rework loops—then routing such cases away from D1 could harm quality or even worsen average cycle time. If, conversely, D1’s queue turns out to be less volatile than forecast (e.g., many cases abandon or are re-routed) while D2’s queue grows sharply with added volume, the apparent advantage of earlier start would shrink. Finally, if handwriting quality in the targeted segment is systematically worse than assumed, the “standard but handwritten” label may hide a class that truly belongs with D1.

### K-337

Factual mechanism: This is a high-risk, high-value case with slow and inconsistent supplier responses. The first Assessment and Request More Info cycle resolves some inconsistencies, but residual discrepancies remain after Document Check 2 and Assessment 2. A second Request More Info sends the case into a weekend gap; external response returns on Mon, requiring a third document check and assessment before Compliance Review can sign off on Tue evening and Decision/Notify on Wed morning. The cumulative effect of multiple loops, external delays, and the compliance bottleneck yields a near two-week cycle.

Minimal intervention: The minimal change is to prohibit a second Request More Info loop after Assessment 2. Instead, the process must move from Assessment 2 directly to Compliance Review and Decision, either approving with protective conditions or rejecting based on the evidence available at that point. All prior control-flow, including the first loop (Request More Info 1, Document Check 2, Assessment 2), is preserved.

Temporal propagation: By forbidding Request More Info 2, the case no longer waits through the Fri–Mon external-response window, nor through Document Check 3 and Assessment 3. The earliest the case can move to Compliance Review is immediately after Assessment 2 on Fri 10:30, subject to C1’s queue. If C1 has capacity later that day, the case may be reviewed before the weekend, and Decision/Notify can follow on Mon. If C1 is already heavily loaded, the case may still wait until Mon or Tue for review, but total processing time is reduced by the service times of Document 3 and Assessment 3 and by some external delay. The key point is that we swap a long, uncertain external wait and extra internal work for additional evidential uncertainty at the time of decision.

Process-feasibility check: All mandatory structural rules remain satisfied: both Identity and Document Check 1 precede Assessment 1; each Request More Info is followed by a fresh Document Check and Assessment; and Compliance Review still occurs after the latest effective Assessment (here, Assessment 2) and before Decision. Four-eyes is maintained between A1 and C1. The intervention *does* change the allowable policy space: it forbids one class of rework path that would otherwise be compliant. Provided Compliance Review explicitly documents the unresolved discrepancy and the rationale for an “approve with conditions” or “reject” decision, the process remains defensible but with a different risk posture.

Best/base/worst case: In the best case, C1 can schedule this case on Fri afternoon, compressing the overall cycle by roughly **2–3 working days** (no weekend rework and no third loop). In a base case, C1 reviews it on Mon, still saving roughly **1–2 days** relative to the factual Tue/ Wed completion. In the worst case, C1’s queue is so long that the case is reviewed no earlier than Tue afternoon, and the main savings are the omitted Document 3 and Assessment 3 effort rather than elapsed time; quality may be lower, and expected loss or appeal risk higher, than under the factual full-evidence path.

Evidence that would reverse the conclusion: If analysis of similar historical cases shows that the third loop often uncovers materially important discrepancies that would change the decision (e.g., revealing fraud or major exposure), then suppressing that loop would be too risky. If regulators or internal audit strongly expect “all material discrepancies” to be fully reconciled for high-value cases, the compliance defensibility of early decisions would be questionable. Conversely, if data show that second loops almost never change the final decision but cost many days, this would reinforce—rather than reverse—the desirability of the intervention.

### K-411

Factual mechanism: The legacy system creates only an Identity task at Triage, and a case lock prevents the Document task from being created until Identity completes. As a result, Identity by I2 runs Tue late morning to early afternoon, after which the Document Check by D2 is created and scheduled from Tue 15:00 to 19:30. Only then does the case join the Assessment queue, which has grown by Wed morning, so Assessment runs mid-Wed, followed by Decision and Notify that afternoon. The main delays arise from serialized verification and a congested assessment queue.

Minimal intervention: The minimal change is to modify Triage so that both Identity and Document tasks are created immediately, and a controlled workaround allows I2 and D2 to work on the case concurrently despite the legacy lock. Real-time timestamps are required so that the observed concurrency in the log reflects real overlap rather than backdating or batch-entry artefacts; no changes are made to assessment or decision roles.

Temporal propagation: With both tasks created at Tue 09:35, their start times become functions of the Tue 08:00 queues: approximately 2–3 h for I2 and 4–5 h for D2. Identity can still finish around midday, but Document can now start before Identity completes, rather than waiting until 15:00. In many scenarios, both verifications finish by late Tue afternoon, so the case enters the Assessment queue earlier and can be picked up in an earlier Wed slot. The improvement is bounded by A2’s capacity; if A2 is saturated, earlier readiness mainly reduces idle waiting rather than total cycle time.

Process-feasibility check: All structural requirements are preserved: Register and Triage first; both Identity and Document completed before Assessment; Decision before Notify; no extra or missing Request More Info loops. Since the case is not high-risk/high-value, no Compliance Review is required. Resource non-overlap is preserved because I2 and D2 work independently. The workaround must maintain clear ownership and logging so that auditors can see exactly who did what and when, preventing “false concurrency” where timestamps suggest overlap but work was actually sequential.

Best/base/worst case: In the best case, Document Check starts shortly after Tue 11:00 and finishes by ~16:00, while Identity finishes around 12:30; the case thus joins the Assessment queue several hours earlier and Assessment can move to an earlier Wed morning slot, yielding a **3–6 h** cycle-time gain. In a base case, A2’s queue is such that the assessment start time advances modestly, giving **2–4 h** improvement. In the worst case, the assessment queue is already the binding bottleneck, so earlier verification only reduces waiting before the same Wed time slot and overall cycle time improves by at most **1–2 h**.

Evidence that would reverse the conclusion: If the workaround required to parallelize tasks proved operationally fragile—e.g., frequent mis-assignments, security exceptions, or inconsistent timestamps—its benefits might be outweighed by audit and error risks. If data showed that most similar cases are already constrained almost entirely by Assessment capacity, so that verification timing rarely drives completion, the value of parallelization would be smaller. Additionally, if concurrency increased error rates at I2 or D2 due to multitasking or cognitive overload, the net quality effect could be negative.

## 4. Capacity and interference analysis

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K-104: early escalation and provisional C1 slot | Adds 1–3 h of preliminary work for S1/C1 per high-risk case but reduces final compliance time by ~1–2 h; shifts some C1 effort earlier in the week. | Compliance queue for reserved cases (and possibly overall) may shrink in effective waiting time because some work is pre-done and slots are pre-allocated. | Compliance queue for non-reserved cases can grow if many slots are reserved and not fully re-optimized; S1’s advice queue may also lengthen. | Other high-risk/high-value cases that do not receive reservations, plus any urgent late-arriving compliance cases, may face longer waits or rescheduling. | Ambiguous: locally beneficial for the targeted case, but system-wide effect could be neutral or negative if C1 is already the main bottleneck. | When the number of reserved slots approaches C1’s capacity, small changes in arrivals or no-shows can cause large swings between idle time and severe queueing for non-reserved work. |

| K-411: parallel verification with accurate timestamps | Slightly increases early simultaneous load on I2 and D2 but does not change total work; advances readiness for Assessment without adding activities. | Identity and Document queues may shrink in terms of time-to-readiness for low-risk digital cases; effective waiting before Assessment shrinks for these cases. | Assessment queues (especially A2’s) may grow as more cases become ready earlier and compete for limited assessment capacity. | Other cases sharing A2 experience more competition for early time slots; some may be pushed later in the day or to the next day. | Generally positive for verification-stage flow and transparency; system-wide effect depends on whether Assessment capacity can absorb the earlier arrivals without becoming the dominant bottleneck. | When Assessment becomes the tightest bottleneck, further verification acceleration yields diminishing returns and can even worsen perceived delays as more cases pile up waiting for assessors. |

Rolling out each intervention to roughly 100 similar cases in a week amplifies these capacity effects and generates interference. For K-104, reserving C1 capacity at scale means a large fraction of the compliance schedule is pre-allocated to high-risk/high-value cases; this can smooth their flow but risks starving non-reserved urgent work and making C1 a hard bottleneck. Reservation policies are especially fragile: over-reservation yields idle C1 time when cases slip or cancel, while under-reservation means little benefit. For K-219, systematically sending handwritten but standard documents to D2 offloads D1 and can materially improve throughput for complex corporate or high-stakes files, but D2’s slower speed and higher rework probability mean more additional Document and Assessment cycles; with 100 cases, that can substantially lengthen D2 and A2 queues and propagate delays through the week.

Under K-337, the 100-case policy reduces aggregate time spent in document rework and assessor analysis, but shifts risk-management load onto C1 and M1. The weekend structure and external-response variability matter: cases that would otherwise have incurred long weekend delays benefit most, while those with quick suppliers might lose valuable clarifications. K-411’s widespread parallelization of identity and document checks pulls readiness earlier and can decongest early verification queues, but if Assessment capacity does not scale, the bottleneck clearly migrates to A1/A2. Accurate real-time timestamps become crucial in this high-throughput setting: without them, false impressions of concurrency could mask overloaded resources or obscure the true order of events, making it hard to diagnose emerging bottlenecks or quality problems. Overall, interference appears strongest around C1 and A2; policies that consume their slack nonlinearly can generate system-wide delays or quality degradation even when each local counterfactual looks beneficial.

## 6. Pilot designs

| pilot element | intervention 1 | intervention 2 |
|---|---|---|
| eligibility | Low-risk, low-value, digital or standard-document cases similar to K-411, excluding any with active investigations or special handling flags. | Low-risk, low-value cases with “standard but handwritten” documents similar to K-219, excluding extremely poor handwriting or cases flagged as complex. |
| assignment rule | Randomize eligible cases at Triage to either the new parallel-verification workflow (treatment) or the legacy sequential creation of document tasks (control). | Randomize eligible handwritten cases at Triage to D2-first routing (treatment) or status-quo D1 routing (control), stratified by day and arrival time. |
| sample/support logic | Choose a sample size that yields sufficient power to detect a 2–3 h change in median cycle time, ensuring coverage across all weekday arrival patterns and including enough cases to observe at least one queue-reset cycle. | Select a sample large enough to detect modest changes in cycle time and rework rates, while ensuring that D2’s load does not exceed safe operational limits; monitor per-resource workload to avoid overloading D2. |
| primary outcome | End-to-end cycle time from Register to Notify for eligible cases, measured in working hours and calendar time. | End-to-end cycle time from Register to Notify for eligible handwritten cases, again in both working hours and calendar days. |
| process-path outcome | Distribution of observed paths and timestamps for Identity and Document tasks (overlap, start delay after Triage, and time-to-assessment-readiness). | Distribution of document-path variants (single-pass vs rework loops), including how often cases require Request More Info and how long external responses take. |
| quality outcome | Rate of post-decision corrections, complaints, or audit findings attributable to identity or document verification errors in treatment vs control. | Misinterpretation or correction rates for handwritten fields, frequency of escalations from D2 to D1 or Assessment, and any downstream complaints about decision accuracy. |
| queue-spillover outcome | Changes in average and 90th percentile queue lengths for I2, D2, and A2, including evidence of bottleneck migration from verification to Assessment. | Changes in queue lengths and service times at D1, D2, and A2, including whether freed D1 capacity improves performance for complex or high-risk cases. |
| logging validation | Audit of a sample of treatment cases to confirm that parallel work is real (not backdated), timestamps are accurate, and ownership changes are recorded correctly. | Validation that routing rules were applied as intended, that D2 actually handled the targeted handwritten tasks, and that any handovers to D1 are fully logged. |
| stopping rule | Stop or pause the pilot if error or complaint rates in the treatment arm rise beyond a pre-specified threshold, or if Assessment queues show sustained, unacceptable congestion. | Stop or adjust if D2’s error or rework rates exceed thresholds, if D2’s queue length persistently breaches a target, or if freed D1 capacity does not materially benefit higher-priority cases. |

## 7. Final synthesis

Across the four interventions, the parallel-verification workaround in K-411 appears most robust: it leaves decision policy and mandatory checks intact, adds only modest operational complexity, and improves transparency about true concurrency. Its main risk is bottleneck migration to the Assessment queue, which is comparatively easy to monitor and mitigate through staffing or prioritization rules. The most dangerous intervention, despite its potential speed, is the K-337 policy of forbidding a second Request More Info loop for high-risk, high-value cases; it directly narrows evidential options, heightens loss and regulatory risk, and is sensitive to poorly understood external-response distributions. While K-337’s local counterfactual shows attractive time savings, this logic is least transportable to a system-wide policy, because it assumes that late supplier information is rarely decisive and that regulators will accept decisions under greater uncertainty.

K-104’s early escalation and C1 reservation is also only weakly transportable: once applied at scale, it can seriously distort compliance queues and fairness across cases, even though it looks benign for a single file. In contrast, K-219’s routing shift to D2 is more scalable because it mainly redistributes document work and frees D1 for complex tasks, provided rework and handwriting errors remain controlled. The most valuable additional data would be high-resolution event and queue logs by resource (including cancellations and no-shows), structured records of Request More Info loops and their impact on final decisions, and external-response timestamps distinguishing weekday vs weekend dynamics. These would allow better estimation of marginal information value from extra loops, resource-specific error and rework rates, and the true shape of bottlenecks under different workload regimes.

## 8. Assumption audit

1. (structural) The listed normative rules fully describe the mandatory control-flow and four-eyes requirements, and no hidden regulatory step is required for these cases.  
2. (temporal) Queue snapshots at 08:00 and 16:00 are reasonable predictors of near-term waiting times, with variability but no systematic bias that would invert waiting-order conclusions.  
3. (resource) Each resource pool operates on standard daytime shifts with limited or no weekend C1 work, so weekend intervals mainly affect external responses rather than internal service availability.  
4. (behavioral) Assessors and reviewers follow the stated policies consistently: they do not silently skip Request More Info when evidence is clearly missing, nor do they routinely overuse it for minor issues.  
5. (quality) D1 has materially lower error and rework rates than D2 on complex or difficult documents, while D2 performs adequately on standard or clearly legible handwritten cases.  
6. (interference) Changes in routing or timing for the targeted cases do not immediately trigger dynamic staffing changes (e.g., reassignment of assessors or additional compliance staff) during the analyzed week.  
7. (resource) S1 and C1 can allocate limited preliminary effort to high-risk cases (as in K-104) without breaching hard capacity constraints or violating other high-priority obligations.  
8. (interference) The mixture of case types in the wider system remains similar to that implicit in the snapshots, so adding 100 similar cases mainly scales existing queues rather than fundamentally changing arrival patterns.
