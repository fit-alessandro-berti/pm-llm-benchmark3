```text
J3 -> holds -> FIX-A [09:00, 11:40] # inferred acquire/release

J6 -> uses -> TEST-1, CAL-3 [17:25, 17:50]
L7 -> quarantine_release -> J2, J5   # inferred from shared event 10:20/13:50
```

Higher-order motifs beyond simple pairwise shared resources:

1. **Batch synchronization motif**: `J1, J2, J3  B1  OVEN-1, RACK-7`. B1 does not merely use a machine; it creates a synchronization barrier requiring three jobs and a shared rack. The timing of B1 is constrained by the last job, J3, while J1 and J2 wait.

2. **Tool-machine-job chain**: `J3  FIX-A; J4  C4, CNC-2, FIX-A`. One job’s hold on a fixture blocks another job even though the machine appears partly idle. The critical relation is not just `J4CNC-2`, but the higher-order object set `{J4, C4, CNC-2, FIX-A}`.

3. **Lot-release coupling motif**: `L7  C2, C5; C2  J2; C5  J5; L7  quarantine/release  J2, J5`. A single lot event touches multiple components and jobs, but the relationship is inconsistently attached to jobs, so traditional job flattening can miss it.

4. **Shipment consolidation motif**: `S1  J1, J4` with `J1` packed long before `J4`. S1 couples the dispatch of J1 to the completion of J4, which is not visible in machine or batch views.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

These calculations are based on supplied clock times. Each counterfactual is an assumption, not causal proof.

1. **J1 B1 synchronization-induced waiting**  
   Actual: J1 ready 08:10; B1 starts 12:05.  
   Wait = 12:05  08:10 = **3 h 55 min = 235 min**.  
   Counterfactual assumption: J1 could proceed when ready and did not need to wait for J2/J3; or B1 could start earlier. If B1 had started when J3 was ready at 11:55, J1 would have waited 3 h 45 min; the extra 10 min is unexplained start delay.

2. **J2 B1 synchronization-induced waiting**  
   Actual: J2 ready 08:25; B1 starts 12:05.  
   Wait = **3 h 40 min = 220 min**.  
   Counterfactual assumption: J2 not held for batch partners. If batch started at 11:55, wait would be 3 h 30 min, with 10 min residual.

3. **J4 delay plausibly associated with FIX-A**  
   J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50. FIX-A is held by J3 from 09:00 to 11:40. J4’s `Machine Process` starts at 11:45.  
   Wait from readiness to FIX-A release = 11:40  09:50 = **1 h 50 min = 110 min**.  
   Wait to actual start = 11:45  09:50 = **1 h 55 min = 115 min**.  
   Counterfactual assumption: if FIX-A had been available at 09:50, J4 could have started then. This is not proven because CNC-2 idled 10:05–10:47, suggesting other possible constraints or scheduling choices, and there is no explicit `Wait for Tool` event.

4. **J1 shipment consolidation delay**  
   J1 packed at 13:30. S1 dispatches at 19:25 after J4 packs at 19:10.  
   Wait = 19:25  13:30 = **5 h 55 min = 355 min**.  
   Counterfactual assumption: J1 could dispatch directly after packing. If normal load-to-dispatch median is 0.3 h = 18 min, excess waiting is about **5 h 37 min**. Not causal proof because outbound transport, documentation, or customer window may also constrain dispatch.

5. **J6 CAL-3 recalibration delay**  
   J6 ready at 16:05. J5 tests 16:10–16:35. CAL-3 recalibrates 16:35–17:20. J6 starts 17:25.  
   Earliest without recalibration after J5 finishes = 16:35.  
   Actual start  earliest no-recalibration start = 17:25  16:35 = **50 min**.  
   Composition: 45 min recalibration + 5 min post-recalibration start.  
   Counterfactual assumption: recalibration between J5 and J6 was not mandatory or could be overlapped/offline.

6. **B2 synchronization and RACK-7 interaction**  
   J4 ready 13:10; J5 ready 14:40; B2 starts 14:50.  
   J4 wait = 14:50  13:10 = **1 h 40 min = 100 min**.  
   If B2 could start when both jobs were ready at 14:40, J4 wait = 1 h 30 min. RACK-7 was quarantined until 14:35, so the rack was not binding after J5 became ready; the residual delay was 10 min.  
   Counterfactual assumption: J4 did not need to wait for J5.

7. **Component-lot L7 release interval**  
   Quarantine at 10:20; release at 13:50.  
   Interval = **3 h 30 min = 210 min**.  
   J5 is ready at 14:40, 50 min after release. Therefore the current evidence does not show that L7 release was on the final critical path for B2; it may have gated earlier stages. This is an upper-bound sensitivity calculation, not a causal delay estimate.

## 4. What flattening misses

### Job flattening

- **Visible:** job-level event sequence, activity frequencies, median elapsed times, rework loop counts.
- **Hides or duplicates:** batch synchronization waits; tool waits; lot quarantine/release; shipment partner waits; any event not directly related to the job at source.
- **Misleading conclusion:** the DFG suggests a smooth flow with no severe bottleneck. It misses that J1 and J2 waited almost four hours before batch start, or that J1 waited almost six hours packed for S1.

### Machine flattening

- **Visible:** utilization, activity sequence, longest gaps between machine events.
- **Hides or duplicates:** reasons for idle; off-machine waiting; tool holds; batch synchronization; lot release; shipment consolidation.
- **Misleading conclusion:** CNC-2 at 54% utilization looks like spare capacity, but J4 waits 110–115 min for FIX-A while CNC-2 idles.

### Batch flattening

- **Visible:** batch start/end, batch resource use, associated jobs and lots.
- **Hides or duplicates:** job readiness before batch; component-lot release that may delay readiness; shipment downstream.
- **Misleading conclusion:** B1 appears to start only 10 min after last job readiness; the long waits of J1 and J2 are invisible if readiness is not modeled as an object event.

### Tool flattening

- **Visible:** tool acquisition/release, cleaning/quarantine, calibration.
- **Hides or duplicates:** waiting jobs not explicitly linked; inferred holds can be duplicated or omitted.
- **Misleading conclusion:** high Job–Tool interaction count may suggest a tool bottleneck, but the important issue is which tool hold blocks which job; e.g., FIX-A blocks J4, CAL-3 recalibration blocks J6.

### ComponentLot flattening

- **Visible:** lot contents, quarantine/release events.
- **Hides or duplicates:** inconsistent job-lot links; one lot event affecting multiple jobs can be dropped from job view.
- **Misleading conclusion:** job flattening contains neither quarantine nor release, so J5’s gating by L7 is invisible and J2’s override looks like normal progress.

### Shipment flattening

- **Visible:** shipment creation, loading, dispatch.
- **Hides or duplicates:** packed jobs waiting for consolidation partners; shipment-level events counted without job wait.
- **Misleading conclusion:** S1 appears efficient with short LoadDispatch, but J1 waits from 13:30 to 19:25.

### Why low machine utilization can coexist with long waiting

Machine utilization measures active processing time at a machine. Waiting often occurs in states that are not assigned to a machine: waiting for batch partners, waiting for a tool, waiting for lot release, waiting for shipment partners. J4 can wait while CNC-2 is idle because the missing resource is FIX-A, not CNC-2. J1 can wait packed while no machine is involved because the missing resource is S1 consolidation. Therefore moderate machine utilization and long off-machine waiting are compatible.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

### Narrative A: J4 blocked by FIX-A while CNC-2 idles

- **Temporal precedence:** J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00; J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50; CNC-2 idles from 10:05 to 10:47; J3 releases FIX-A at 11:40; J4 starts `Machine Process` at 11:45.
- **Resource feasibility:** J4’s processing requires C4, CNC-2, and FIX-A. CNC-2 is available during part of the interval, but FIX-A is not available until 11:40.
- **Policy constraints:** no explicit preemption or priority rule is given.
- **Causal speculation:** FIX-A unavailability plausibly explains much of J4’s delay, but CNC-2’s idle period from 10:05 to 10:47 means the story is not complete; there could be additional setup or scheduling constraints.

### Narrative B: B1/B2, OVEN-1, and RACK-7 synchronization

- **Temporal precedence:** J1 ready 08:10; J2 ready 08:25; J3 ready 11:55; B1 starts 12:05; OVEN-1 idle 12:55–14:50; RACK-7 cleaning until 14:35; J4 ready 13:10; J5 ready 14:40; B2 starts 14:50.
- **Resource feasibility:** B1 requires OVEN-1 and RACK-7; B2 also requires both. After B1 completes, OVEN-1 is idle, but RACK-7 is unavailable until 14:35. B2 also cannot start before J5 is ready at 14:40.
- **Policy constraints:** batch processing requires waiting for all jobs in the batch.
- **Causal speculation:** B1’s late start forces early jobs to wait; B2’s start is dominated by J5 readiness rather than RACK availability, though the rack quarantine is a near-miss constraint. This shows that oven idle is not a sufficient bottleneck signal.

### Narrative C: L7 component-lot release couples J2 and J5

- **Temporal precedence:** L7 `Quarantine Lot` at 10:20 relates to L7, C2, C5, J2, and J5; `Release Lot` at 13:50 relates to same objects; B1 starts at 12:05; J5 ready at 14:40; B2 starts at 14:50.
- **Resource feasibility:** C2 and C5 are in L7. If lot release is required for component use, then J2 and J5 are both gated by L7. However, J2 enters B1 before release via supervisor override.
- **Policy constraints:** quarantine policy blocks use; override policy allowed J2 but not J5.
- **Causal speculation:** the lot release may have delayed J5’s upstream readiness, but because J5 becomes ready 50 min after release, the current evidence does not prove that L7 directly delayed B2’s start.

### Narrative D: CAL-3 and TEST-1 serialize J5 and J6

- **Temporal precedence:** J5 ready 16:00; J6 ready 16:05; J5 tests 16:10–16:35; CAL-3 recalibrates 16:35–17:20; J6 tests 17:25–17:50.
- **Resource feasibility:** final testing requires both TEST-1 and CAL-3. After 16:35, TEST-1 is idle, but CAL-3 is unavailable.
- **Policy constraints:** calibration policy appears to require recalibration between tests.
- **Causal speculation:** recalibration plausibly delays J6 by 50 min after J5 finishes. TEST-1 idle time from 16:35 to 17:25 is not evidence of spare testing capacity; the missing resource is CAL-3.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

### Observations that would weaken the top-ranked explanation

1. Evidence that B1’s 12:05 start was caused by OVEN-1 breakdown, maintenance, or operator unavailability rather than waiting for J3 or batch synchronization.
2. Evidence that J4’s delay was caused by missing C4 preparation, NC program unavailability, or a machine fault rather than FIX-A.
3. Evidence that S1’s 19:25 dispatch was driven by an external truck schedule or customer slot rather than consolidation with J4.
4. Evidence that J6 was not actually ready at 16:05, or that J6 needed additional preparation before testing.
5. Evidence that L7 release was not a prerequisite for J5, or that J5 could have overridden the quarantine but chose not to.
6. Evidence that RACK-7 cleaning/quarantine was non-mandatory and could have been shortened, or that B2’s start was postponed for unrelated reasons.
7. Evidence that the recorded machine utilization excludes significant setup/teardown or rework, making conventional machine capacity more important than it appears.

### Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses

1. Explicit `Wait for Tool` events with requested tool, requested time, granted time, and blocking job.
2. Job readiness or `Ready for Next Step` timestamps at each lifecycle stage.
3. Batch readiness rules: minimum/maximum batch size, maximum wait, override authorization, and planned start policies.
4. Tool policy fields: mandatory vs optional calibration, cleaning duration, and whether recalibration can be overlapped.
5. Shipment consolidation policy: maximum wait window, partner job list, freight-cost thresholds, and customer delivery requirements.
6. Component-lot disposition status and gate events linked to each job and component.
7. Machine state reason codes: `active`, `idle`, `blocked waiting tool`, `blocked waiting batch`, `cleaning`, `maintenance`.
8. Priority/preemption rules by job, lot, shipment, or customer.
