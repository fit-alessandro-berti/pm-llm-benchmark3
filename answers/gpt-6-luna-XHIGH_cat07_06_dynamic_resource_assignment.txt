# 1. Objectives and constraints

The policy should produce safe work at a predictable pace while preserving useful case knowledge, distributing new work fairly, developing skills when time and supervision permit, and avoiding reliance on a few specialists. It should optimize a balanced outcome rather than maximize assignments completed per hour alone.

**Hard safety and process constraints**

- A resource with skill 0 is ineligible for the activity. A skill score of 1 is trainee level; high-priority work requires skill 2 or explicit supervision. For Security Review, the permitted choices are a skill-3 reviewer or a skill-1/2 worker under explicit supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo. The specialist must perform or approve the security decision.
- Final Approval requires a skill-2/3 finalizer. Skill 1 may prepare material but may not finalize it. A skill-0 resource may not participate in the approval work.
- Count both a lead and a supervisee toward the limit of four new assignments per resource in this decision batch. Retaining an existing case assignment does not count as a new assignment; adding a new supervisor, trainee, or activity owner does.
- Quality Check may be batched only for normal/low priority cases with more than 24 hours to SLA. Any batch must retain a named owner and make its component cases traceable.
- Every supervised assignment must name the supervisor, the decision they review, and the point at which the supervisor must intervene. A supervisor cannot be counted as available if the projected work would take them past their staffed hours.

**Preference trade-offs**

- Prioritize the most urgent deadline and priority first, using estimated remaining service time where available. Protect some capacity for urgent exceptions.
- Prefer a capable current owner when a change would incur high handover cost, especially on a complex case. Avoid a further transfer after two or more reassignments unless the current owner is ineligible, unsafe, or cannot meet the deadline.
- Prefer lower workload and lower overtime exposure when skill and SLA coverage are adequate. Do not add discretionary work to a high-overtime-risk resource when a safe alternative exists.
- Where the SLA forecast is safe, reserve at least 20% of normal/low assignments for documented training priorities. If the current queue has no safe, available match, record the shortfall and schedule a future protected training opportunity; do not manufacture a skill match or override safety and capacity constraints.
- Use cross-team capability to reduce single-person bottlenecks, but account for coordination and handover costs. Keep the reason for every assignment understandable to the worker and auditable from the event log and roster snapshot.

# 2. Assignment policy

Use a two-stage, explainable rule: first determine which assignments are allowed, then rank the allowed choices. Urgency determines dispatch order; a resource's rank never makes an ineligible or unsafe assignment permissible.

1. **Build the decision snapshot.** For each queue item, load priority, remaining SLA time, complexity, current owner, reassignment count, handover-cost band, available hours, open cases, overtime risk, skill scores, and any documented training priority. Estimate remaining activity service time at a conservative percentile from comparable historical cases. If that estimate is unavailable, mark the SLA forecast uncertain rather than treating the deadline as safe.
2. **Filter eligibility.** Remove skill-0 resources. Apply the Security Review and Final Approval rules above. For other high-priority activities, require skill 2 or a named, qualified supervisor. A trainee can prepare but cannot make a decision reserved for a competent/specialist resource. Filter out candidates who cannot fit the work into remaining staffed capacity with a reasonable reserve, who would exceed four new assignments, or who cannot provide required supervision.
3. **Score SLA urgency.** Compute a visible 0–100 urgency score from three logged inputs: priority tier, remaining slack relative to estimated service time, and customer-wait/age. For example, use 40% priority tier, 40% deadline pressure (`estimated p90 service time / remaining SLA time`, capped at 1), and 20% time already waiting. Dispatch in descending urgency, with the smallest slack first within a tier. Recompute when service estimates, queue age, or staffing change.
4. **Rank eligible assignments.** Publish a short reason for each component. An initial, reviewable weighting is skill fit 30%, continuity and handover 25%, workload/capacity fairness 20%, overtime exposure 10%, documented development 10%, and resilience/bottleneck avoidance 5%. These are preference weights, not permissions. Skill eligibility and SLA safety are gates. A high handover band, high complexity, and an existing owner increase the continuity score; high open cases, low hours, or high overtime risk lower the workload score. A feasible documented trainee-supervisor pair increases the development score. Lower concentration of a scarce skill across teams increases the resilience score.
5. **Protect fairness and bottlenecks.** Recalculate assignment counts after every decision, including both participants in a pair. Apply a soft cap below the hard four-assignment limit when a resource has high open work or little time remaining. Keep a reserve for urgent work and avoid assigning ordinary work to high-overtime-risk resources where a qualified, safer candidate exists. Check the distribution of scarce-skill work across qualified teams, not just the current least-loaded specialist.
6. **Use continuity deliberately.** Keep the current owner by default when qualified and forecast to meet the SLA. For a high-complexity/high-handover case, retain that owner unless skill is 0/1 and feasible supervision is unavailable. When a transfer is justified by capacity or skill, name one receiving owner, carry forward prior-loop notes, and avoid another transfer before the stage is resolved.
7. **Tie-break and exceptions.** Break close ties by (a) lower projected workload per staffed hour, (b) lower overtime risk, (c) preserving the current owner, then (d) rotating opportunity among equally qualified workers. Record the runner-up and the reason it lost. If no candidate passes the gates, escalate to the duty manager for an explicit supervised pair, deadline renegotiation, or capacity action; never silently route to a skill-0 resource. If the training share is below target, record which hard constraints or forecasts prevented it and schedule a later safe opportunity.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

| Dimension | First-available routing | Specialist-only routing | Proposed dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected throughput | Often low average waiting at first, but weak matches and later rework can erase the gain. | Strong individual match, but scarce specialists can queue while other capable workers sit idle. | Likely steadier end-to-end flow: it uses competent owners, reserves scarce specialists for constrained work, and uses safe batching. A controlled handover can lower immediate throughput. |
| SLA risk | Can help short waits, but ignores skill depth, continuity, and sometimes the urgency of work already waiting. | Can protect activity quality but creates bottlenecks, notably Security and Approval. | Orders by urgency and deadline slack, includes capacity and service-time forecasts, and escalates when no safe resource is available. Forecast error remains a risk. |
| Error/rework risk | Higher risk of skill mismatch and repeat handovers. | Lower immediate skill mismatch, though delays and overloaded specialists can still cause errors. | Gates on skill and named supervision, avoids unnecessary transfers, and monitors rework/appeals. It accepts some supervised trainee work where safe. |
| Workload fairness | Lowest-open-case rule can repeatedly send work to the same nominally light resource without considering hours or case difficulty. | Concentrates work on top specialists. | Uses open cases, available hours, overtime risk, batch cap, and incoming assignment counts. It still needs worker feedback because case complexity is only a proxy for effort. |
| Skill development | Incidental; first-available ignores development plans. | Limited; work tends to stay with the most skilled person. | Reserves safe normal/low work for recorded training priorities and names the supervisor. The present queue demonstrates that the 20% target can be infeasible without a recorded and safe match. |
| Continuity of ownership | Often poor because it disregards handover cost and reassignment history. | May keep or repeatedly choose specialists without respecting case ownership. | Defaults to a capable current owner, particularly for complex/high-cost cases. Transfers are allowed when workload or skill justifies them and are made once with context. |
| Resilience to absence or bottlenecks | Can spread work but not build dependable backup capability. | Vulnerable to absence of a top specialist and specialist queues. | Shares capability across teams, tracks specialist concentration, and develops backups under supervision. The policy must still protect enough specialist time for security and approvals. |

No one policy is best in every interval. First-available may be useful during a short generic queue spike; specialist-first may be appropriate for a narrow high-risk activity. The dynamic policy is preferable for the mixed queue because it makes those exceptions visible and ties them to skill, deadline, load, continuity, and supervision evidence.

# 5. Explanation to workers

“We assign work by looking at the deadline, the skill the activity needs, the cases already on your list, your remaining scheduled hours, and whether a handover would lose important case knowledge. When a colleague is learning, we name the experienced person who will review the work and keep that review within their capacity. We try to keep a case with its current owner when that is safe, and we spread work when someone is close to capacity or a skill is becoming a bottleneck.

“For this queue, Celine keeps her knowledge of the urgent security case while Gita performs the specialist review. Amal can build diagnosis experience on C009 with Celine checking the work. We moved some low-handover work away from high-overtime-risk colleagues to people with the right skills and more available time. We did not route Security work to Eva or Jonas because their recorded Security skill is zero. If a deadline changes, a workload estimate is wrong, or a pairing is not workable, tell the duty manager; the assignment can be reviewed and the reason recorded.”

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Use a stable case identifier, event timestamp, activity, actual performer, assigned owner, named supervisor, skill-matrix version, assignment reason, priority, deadline, complexity, team, handover-cost band, reassignment count, and workload snapshot. Join events to the roster version that was valid when the assignment was made. Compare results by activity, priority, complexity, team, shift, and resource, and publish uncertainty when the sample is small.

| Indicator | Definition and audit use |
|---|---|
| Handover frequency | Count consecutive events in a case performed by different resources; report handovers per case and per 100 activities, split by activity and handover-cost band. Distinguish planned supervised pairs from owner changes. |
| Reassignment count | Distribution and tail share of `reassignment_count`; monitor cases exceeding two transfers and compare their outcomes with lower-transfer cases, controlling for priority and complexity. |
| Skill mismatch | Join actual performer and activity to the time-valid skill matrix. Report skill-0 events (target zero), below-required-level work without a permitted supervisor, approval finalizations below level 2, and security work without a level-3 performer or named R7/R8 supervisor. |
| Workload distribution | New assignments and estimated service hours per resource divided by available hours; report maximum, median, spread/Gini, open-case count, and share of assignments by team. Count pair roles separately and state whether supervision time is included. |
| Overtime-risk exposure | Share and estimated hours assigned to high/medium/low overtime-risk resources; flag high-risk assignments, their urgency/alternatives, and later actual overtime. Check whether high-risk workers receive fewer discretionary assignments. |
| SLA misses | Miss rate and minutes late by priority/activity/team, plus forecast calibration: predicted deadline risk versus actual completion. Separate delays that began before assignment from delays after assignment. |
| Rework/appeal after assignment | Rework correction and appeal within a defined follow-up window after an assignment, normalized by completed cases and stratified by skill match, handover count, complexity, and supervision. Avoid attributing all case outcomes to the last assignee. |
| Training assignment share | Normal/low assignments with a matching, recorded training priority and named supervisor divided by all normal/low assignments. Track completion, review findings, trainee hours, and SLA/rework outcomes. Report conditional or unregistered development separately; do not count it as target-compliant. |
| Specialist bottleneck | Queue length and age for specialist-only activities, utilization of skill-3 resources, fraction of work awaiting one resource/team, and concentration (for example, share handled by the top two qualified resources). Track cross-team backup coverage. |
| Batch quality and delay | For batches, verify every member met priority and >24h eligibility at batch formation; measure batch wait and per-case SLA/rework, not just batch throughput. |
| Policy deviations | Compare each assignment with eligible candidates and logged score components. Count overrides, missing reasons, unrecorded supervisors, cap breaches, high-overtime exceptions, late changes, and unavailable/missing roster data. Record who approved an override and why. |

Review these indicators with workers and supervisors on a regular cadence. A metric change should trigger case review and discussion of staffing, estimates, and policy effects; it should not be used as a standalone worker performance ranking.
