# 2. Integrated explanation

The sequence-position table is not a longitudinal experiment in which an otherwise identical worker repeatedly receives identical cases. Each bin changes along several dimensions at once: complexity, priority, queue pressure, activity mix, resource mix, and probability of being part of a batch. Position 76+ is especially selected because only high-volume workers or busy shifts reach it; it may overrepresent fast workers, simple cases, Day-shift peaks, and batched Document Review.

The early-to-middle pattern is consistent with a combination of warm-up and easier case mix. From positions 1–5 to 16–30, service time and errors both fall. That is the qualitative signature expected from warm-up, but it also coincides with complexity falling from 0.72 to 0.54, high-priority share falling from 41% to 28%, and the queue falling from 46 to 33. Senior staff also select difficult carryover work after night or weekend backlogs. Consequently, the initial 18.8-minute mean should not be interpreted as workers simply being slow at the beginning of a shift.

The mechanism changes later. Complexity and priority continue to fall, which helps explain why service time reaches 10.8 minutes. Batching and shortcuts may further reduce recorded service times. At the same time, queues rise to 62 and then 88, and errors and seven-day rework increase sharply. The WIP-stratified results provide the strongest evidence for a pressure-related speed-quality tradeoff: within both complexity bands, high WIP is associated with shorter service and substantially higher error rates. High-complexity events are particularly risky under high WIP, with a 10.9% error rate.

The resource results reinforce that interpretation. Later errors are relatively stable under low WIP, but rise under high WIP for seniors, mid-level staff, juniors, and night staff. Juniors are most vulnerable, suggesting an interaction between experience and pressure. Seniors also deteriorate under high WIP, so experience mitigates but does not eliminate the operational risk. Thus, falling service time does not necessarily mean increasing skill; in late high-WIP periods it more plausibly reflects faster handling, case selection, batching, or curtailed checking.

Fatigue remains possible, particularly as an interaction with queue pressure. A tired worker under low demand may preserve quality by working more slowly, whereas a tired worker facing a large queue may omit verification steps. The current aggregates cannot separate that from pressure because elapsed time and WIP rise together. The low-WIP stability of later errors argues against a large universal fatigue effect, but it does not rule out fatigue under demanding conditions.

Circadian effects are also not identified by the shift summaries. Day-shift endings are fast and error-prone, while Night-shift endings are slower and less error-prone than Day endings despite occurring later on the clock. These differences may reflect demand peaks, staffing, urgent exceptions, or worker selection rather than circadian biology. Clock time must be separated from time on duty using overlapping shifts or rotating workers.

Finally, some late errors may be detected late rather than created late. Quality Check bursts in the final 45 minutes can move error and correction timestamps toward shift end. Similarly, Bot_Risk can create downstream correction work that is attributed to humans. The increase in seven-day rework suggests that the late quality issue is not purely a timestamp artifact, but root-cause attribution still requires linking each correction to the originating decision.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Measurement and data validation**

1. Confirm that `sequence_position_within_shift` is calculated per **resource and shift**, not globally across all workers in a shift. A global position is not a measure of individual exposure.
2. Distinguish:
   - event active service time: `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`;
   - case service time: summed active work;
   - waiting time and total flow time.
3. Validate local time zones, daylight-saving transitions, and overnight Night shifts.
4. Exclude Bot_Risk from human service-time models and represent prior bot involvement as a separate predictor.
5. Check negative, zero, overlapping, and implausibly long durations. Very short durations should not automatically be deleted because they may identify batching.
6. Ensure complexity and queue variables were known at service start. Do not adjust with information created after the outcome.
7. For seven-day rework, exclude or censor cases without seven days of follow-up.

**Statistical design**

Use two principal outcomes:

- `log(event_service_minutes)` or a Gamma/lognormal service-time model;
- logistic or hierarchical logistic models for `error_flag`, plus a separate seven-day rework or time-to-rework model.

A conceptual specification is:

```text
outcome ~
    nonlinear(sequence_position)
  + nonlinear(minutes_since_shift_start)
  + cyclic(local_clock_time)
  + nonlinear(wip_at_start)
  + nonlinear(queue_length_at_start)
  + nonlinear(case_complexity_score)
  + priority
  + activity_family
  + shift
  + day_of_week / weekend_backlog
  + resource_experience_months
  + batch_indicator
  + prior_bot_classification
  + WIP × complexity
  + WIP × experience
  + elapsed_time × WIP
  + resource fixed effects
  + resource-by-activity effects
```

Cluster uncertainty by case and resource-shift because events from the same case and work session are not independent. With only a small number of named resources, resource fixed effects may be more transparent than relying entirely on random-effect assumptions.

Sequence and elapsed time will often be highly correlated. They should not be interpreted as independent merely because both appear in one regression. Useful comparisons include:

- same resource and elapsed time, different event counts;
- same resource and sequence position, different elapsed times;
- same clock time across overlapping shifts;
- same resource/activity/complexity under low and high WIP.

Estimate a sequence of models:

1. raw position trajectory;
2. plus resource, activity, shift, and clock controls;
3. plus complexity and priority;
4. plus WIP and queue;
5. plus batching and automation interactions.

Attenuation after each step is informative about composition, but it is not automatically causal. For example, queue pressure may mediate a time-of-shift effect, so a WIP-adjusted coefficient estimates a different quantity from the total observed trajectory.

**Specific analyses**

- **Within-resource comparisons:** Compare each worker with themselves, preferably within the same activity family. Include resource-by-activity effects because a worker may perform different work early and late.
- **Within-shift position effects:** Use both categorical bins and smooth functions of sequence and elapsed minutes. Analyze the first 20, middle, and final periods, but also avoid arbitrary cutoffs.
- **WIP/queue stratification:** Report trajectories separately for WIP `<30`, `30–70`, and `>70`, then fit continuous nonlinear effects. Check whether queue length or WIP is the more predictive operational measure.
- **Complexity adjustment:** Use the continuous score, priority, exception type, activity, and pre-service route. Examine WIP-by-complexity interactions rather than assuming additive effects.
- **Shift and time-of-day controls:** Use local clock hour, shift, day of week, and weekend/holiday indicators. Compare overlapping hours across Early, Day, and Late shifts.
- **Batching detection:** Create batch indicators, batch size, position within batch, inter-event gap, and completion-burst measures. Analyze batched and non-batched events separately.
- **Resource-experience interactions:** Test whether WIP, complexity, and elapsed-time effects differ by experience months or experience group. Avoid treating experience as randomly assigned.
- **Downstream analysis:** Link errors to later Quality Check and Rework Correction events. Analyze both immediate error flags and rework within seven days. Distinguish the resource who created the decision from the resource who detected or corrected it.

**PM4Py-oriented pseudocode**

The precise argument names and inserted column names can vary slightly by PM4Py version, so the resulting schema should be inspected.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

df = pd.read_parquet("claims_events.parquet")

# Convert to a consistent timezone, then derive local clock features.
for col in ["time:timestamp", "start_timestamp", "shift_start_time"]:
    df[col] = pd.to_datetime(df[col], utc=True)

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

df["event_service_minutes"] = (
    (df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"])
    .dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
)

# Keep human and automation analyses separate.
human = df[df["org:resource"] != "Bot_Risk"].copy()
bot = df[df["org:resource"] == "Bot_Risk"].copy()

# Recompute a worker-specific position and validate against the supplied field.
human = human.sort_values(
    ["org:resource", "shift_id", "start_timestamp", "time:timestamp"]
)
human["worker_sequence_position"] = (
    human.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id"]).cumcount() + 1
)
human["worker_elapsed_minutes"] = (
    (human["start_timestamp"] - human["shift_start_time"])
    .dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
)

human["wip_band"] = pd.cut(
    human["wip_at_start"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 30, 70, np.inf],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"],
    right=False
)
```

Add case-level service, waiting, arrival, and completion-rate features:

aug = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(human.copy())

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Assignment is endogenous.** Seniors receive complex carryover cases, night workers handle urgent exceptions, and juniors may be concentrated in batched Document Review. Raw worker comparisons therefore combine worker performance with assigned work.

- **Sequence position is also selective.** Workers with many events are more likely to appear in the 76+ bin. They may be faster, may receive simpler work, or may be using batches. Workers with low-volume complex cases may never enter that bin.

- **Error measurement is detection-conditioned.** `error_flag` reflects errors that were recorded, not all latent errors. Resources with more Quality Check exposure may appear worse because more of their errors are detected. End-of-shift Quality Check bursts can make an earlier error look like a late error.

- **Rework attribution can be misleading.** The worker performing Rework Correction is often the fixer, not the originator. Rework can also arise from new customer information rather than an avoidable prior mistake. Seven-day outcomes require equal follow-up or censoring.

- **Service timestamps may not equal active labor.** Batched starts and completions, system latency, interruptions, and delayed recording can create very short or very long durations. Timestamp anomalies should be analyzed as process signals, not automatically treated as worker behavior.

- **Queue pressure is partly management-created.** Staffing levels, scheduling, demand forecasting, assignment rules, delayed Quality Check, and automation policies determine WIP exposure. It is unfair to individualize outcomes produced by an understaffed or poorly designed system.

- **Automation quality must be included.** Bot_Risk's near-zero duration should not be interpreted as costless performance. Human correction and audit work should be attributed to the automation-supported process. Human disagreement alone is not sufficient ground truth for bot error.

- **Experience is not randomly assigned.** Juniors may receive different activities, supervision, and shifts. Higher observed error rates do not establish lower effort or negligence.

- **Small groups create privacy risks.** Night staff and named resources can be reidentified in detailed dashboards. Minimum sample sizes, aggregation, role-based access, and purpose limitations are needed.

- **Raw penalties invite gaming.** Workers may avoid complex cases, suppress errors, delay corrections, or transfer cases before Quality Check. Error data should support coaching, staffing, training, and process redesign rather than mechanical punishment.

- **Causal claims require intervention-level evidence.** Adjusted regressions reduce some confounding but cannot measure sleep, interruptions, undocumented workarounds, or latent complexity. Operational changes should be piloted with pre-specified safety and quality outcomes.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

| Indicator | Definition and display | Important cuts and safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Event service time** | Mean, median, and 90th percentile of `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`; paired chart of speed and quality rather than throughput alone. Keep Bot_Risk separate. Flag timestamp-quality problems. |

Position and elapsed-time trajectory** | Joint plots of service, error, and rework by worker-relative sequence position, elapsed duty time, and local clock hour. | Low/high WIP, complexity, activity, shift, experience, and batch status. The dashboard should support side-by-side raw and adjusted views, include confidence intervals and denominator counts, and delay seven-day rework reporting until outcomes are mature. Alerts should combine pressure and quality—for example, sustained WIP above 70 plus rising adjusted error risk—rather than rewarding throughput in isolation.
