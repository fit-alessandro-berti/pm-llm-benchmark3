# Automation Portfolio for Small-Business Loan Origination

Framing note. The event log says the end-to-end problem is *waiting and synchronization*, not *touch time*. Summed median waiting time across the path is roughly 250–300 hours per case, while summed mean service time is under 8 hours. Registration (8 min service, 1.5h wait, 4% exception) is the most frequent hand-touch, but it contributes on the order of 1% of elapsed time. The dominant contributors are: external valuation wait (58h on 2,350 cases), customer signing (44h on 7,300), entity-mismatch resolution (31h wait, 55 min service, 38% rework, on the critical path in 82% of the cases where it occurs), credit analysis queue (28h), committee packaging (26h), contract preparation (22h), normalization (20h), and the missing-document loop (8h wait but 52% repetition, 3,300 → 2,800 return arcs). The portfolio below is ranked on *elapsed-time and control leverage per unit of feasibility*, not on activity frequency.

## 2. Dependency and prerequisite map

**Upstream data-quality dependencies (the spine).**
`A1 → A2 → {A5, A7 trigger} → A6 → A8 → A9 → A10 → A11`. Every candidate to the right consumes the output quality of those to its left. Concretely:
- A2's 8% classification error propagates to A5 (wrong checklist → more missing-document loops), to A7 (collateral requirement missed for Equipment finance, where it applies in 71% of cases), and to the KYC depth chosen for High-exposure SME.
- A6 is the quality gate for A8: committee packaging explicitly "requires reconciled artifacts." Building A8 before A6 realises the stated garbage-in/garbage-out risk.
- A9 consumes structured committee-decision data; the 8.5%/14% remediation loop is evidence that this data is currently not clean enough for unattended generation.
- A11 consumes the *control state* of the whole case. It is the terminal dependent of everything.

**Legal / compliance controls.**
- *KYC clearance strictly precedes Customer signing.* This is a legal gate, currently violated in 6.5% of cases (1.1% needing remediation). Therefore A10 (signing automation) is **conditionally dependent** on a hard, system-enforced clearance check — supplied by A3/A4 status plus A12's monitoring. Automating reminders on an uncleared case would scale a legal breach.
- *Four-eyes on Credit analysis vs. Committee packaging for High-exposure SME*, violated in 5.8% of cases at month-end. A8 must carry an actor-separation check; automating assembly must not let one actor become the de facto author of both artifacts.
- *Contract must match committee decision.* A9's rule library and A12's remediation taxonomy are the same asset viewed from two ends; build the library once, in Wave 1, through A12.
- *Auditability.* Every module must persist model suggestion → human override → final decision. This is not optional decoration; it is the precondition for supervisory acceptance of A3 in particular. Automation does not transfer compliance accountability — a named human owner remains accountable for each automated decision path.

**Human escalation capacity.**
- Compliance specialists: 3 FTE, with the handover network showing concentration on two seniors — a key-person and single-point-of-failure risk. A3 raises straight-through KYC clearance and therefore *raises the exception density* of what reaches those specialists. Sequencing A4 before A3 is deliberate: build the escalation capacity (assistant + knowledge capture from the two seniors) before increasing the escalation rate.
- Credit analysts: 9 FTE, central in the working-together network. The brief states explicitly that increasing clean inflow without improving normalization overloads them. This is the reason A1 and A2 must not precede A6.
- Legal ops: 4 FTE, idle when KYC is slow and overloaded at month end. Smoothing KYC (A3/A4) *reduces* legal-ops idle time but sharpens the month-end peak unless A9 arrives to absorb it — so A9 belongs after the KYC work, not before.
- Back office: 12 FTE, below 65% utilization after 15:00. Their queue is visible but not binding; the correct response is shift-shaping and reallocating capacity to the missing-document/customer-chasing work in A5, not eliminating registration headcount first.

**External valuation dependency.**
`Collateral valuation request → Wait for external valuation → Committee packaging` (2,400 → 2,350 → 1,980) carries the largest single wait (58h) and a 19% late rate. It is outside direct control, so the levers are structural rather than automational: (a) **trigger earlier** — today the DFG shows `Credit analysis → Collateral valuation request` (2,400), i.e. valuation starts *after* analysis; classification (A2) can predict the requirement at position 3 and start the external clock in parallel with document review and credit analysis; (b) **triage** — desktop vs. full valuation by exposure and product; (c) **route** on valuer SLA performance. Note the 2,350 → 1,980 drop into packaging: roughly 370 cases leak out of that arc (re-valuation, abandonment, or re-routing) and deserve separate investigation.

**Core-banking integration constraints.**
IT can deeply integrate only **two** modules with core banking in the first six months. Deep integration is genuinely required by A9 (contract/product data), A11 (disbursement and limit setup), A1 (customer master and duplicate detection), and partly A3 (screening systems of record). It is *not* required by A5 (portal/workflow layer), A12 (case-management and audit-log layer), A7 (valuer-portal integration), or A6 (document layer with a write-back of normalized figures). This asymmetry is the reason a Wave 1 of A6 + A4 + A5 + A12 is feasible: it consumes at most one deep-integration slot (A6's write-back of normalized financials, and even that can start as a read-mostly integration), leaving a slot in reserve.

## 3. Portfolio alternatives

### Portfolio Q — Quick-win / labor-visible
**Modules: A1, A2, A5, A10.**
Optimizes: political visibility, headline touch-time savings, fastest delivery, lowest technical risk. Every module is "high" feasibility, none requires more than one deep integration, and it produces a large, easily-communicated FTE-hours number from the 11,880-case registration and 11,820-case classification steps.
Sacrifices: it does not touch a single true constraint. It accelerates intake into an unchanged KYC/analyst/valuation system, raising WIP and queue lengths downstream (A1's own risk note says so). It includes **no compliance-strengthening module**, so it violates the stated Wave-1 compliance requirement — A10 without an enforced KYC gate actively *worsens* the provisional-signing violation. It leaves the 31h mismatch wait, the 20h normalization queue, the 58h valuation wait, and every control loop untouched.

### Portfolio B — Bottleneck / synchronization (recommended)
**Modules: A6, A4, A5, A12.**
Optimizes: elapsed time at the true constraints and the protection of the two scarce pools. A6 drains the 20h normalization queue and the analyst load feeding the 28h credit-analysis queue; A4 attacks the highest per-case pathology (31h wait, 38% rework, 82% critical-path presence, plus the KYC↔mismatch cycle); A5 removes the largest rework loop at its root cause (RM instruction quality); A12 satisfies the compliance requirement and simultaneously builds the rule library that A9 will need in year two.
Sacrifices: the headline labor number is smaller and slower to appear — A4 touches only 1,750 cases, A12 saves little direct labor. Registration and classification stay manual for a year, which is politically awkward given management's initial instinct. External valuation (58h) and signing (44h) are deferred to Wave 2, so the *longest* single waits are not yet addressed. A6 carries real model risk on low-quality PDFs and needs an analyst review UI from day one.

### Portfolio C — Compliance-first
**Modules: A12, A3, A4, A9.**
Optimizes: control integrity and audit defensibility. It directly targets the 6.5% provisional signings, the 5.8% four-eyes breaches, and the 8.5%/14% contract-remediation loop, and it hardens KYC end-to-end (A3 + A4) — the strongest posture for a supervisory examination.
Sacrifices: it consumes both deep-integration slots (A3 screening systems, A9 core banking) in the first six months, leaving no capacity to react. It leaves the analyst constraint (A6) and the biggest rework loop (A5) untouched, so **cycle time may barely improve** — and A3's higher straight-through rate concentrates harder exceptions onto a 3-FTE specialist pool, where A4's relief may not be sufficient. It is also the most expensive per unit of customer-visible improvement and does nothing about the 44h signing wait or the 58h valuation wait.

### Portfolio D (variant considered, not recommended) — Synchronization-maximal
**Modules: A7, A10, A6, A12.** Attacks the two largest waits directly (58h + 44h) plus the analyst constraint, with A12 for compliance. Rejected as Wave 1 because A10 depends on a hard KYC gate that only exists once A4/A3/A12 are mature, and A7 depends on early classification (A2) to trigger valuation in parallel. It is the right *Wave 2*, and appears as such below.

## 4. Expected process-level effects

### Portfolio Q (A1, A2, A5, A10)
- **Cycle time:** small median improvement (≈2–4h from registration and classification waits, plus a meaningful cut in the 44h signing wait from e-sign adoption), but likely **worse P90**. Faster intake with unchanged KYC, normalization, credit-analysis and valuation capacity lengthens those queues.
- **Waiting time:** the 1.5h and 2.0h pre-registration/classification waits largely disappear; the 4h KYC wait, 20h normalization wait, 28h analysis wait and 58h valuation wait all grow as arrival rates into them rise.
- **Rework:** A5 genuinely cuts the `Financial document review → Request missing documents → Financial document review` loop (3,300/2,800, 52% repeat). A2 reduces the 8% misroute rate. But the mismatch cycle (1,750/1,160), the contract-remediation cycle (650/510) and the 21% signing reminder loop's underlying causes persist.
- **Handovers:** fewer back-office handovers at intake; unchanged elsewhere. The compliance handover concentration on two seniors is untouched.
- **Resource demand:** back office (already <65% utilized after 15:00) freed further — capacity released where it is not needed. **Credit analysts and compliance specialists absorb more inflow with no added capability** — the explicitly warned-against failure mode.
- **Control violations:** **likely to worsen.** Automated signing reminders on cases without enforced KYC clearance industrialise the 6.5% provisional-signing rate. Faster month-end throughput raises four-eyes breach exposure (currently 5.8%, month-end-concentrated). No compliance module — constraint violated.

### Portfolio B (A6, A4, A5, A12) — recommended
- **Cycle time:** the largest *reliable* end-to-end reduction available in year one. A6 attacks a 20h wait plus 75 min service on 5,200 cases and relieves the 28h analysis queue; A4 removes a large share of a 31h wait that sits on the critical path in 82% of its 1,750 occurrences; A5 removes an 8h wait plus a full document-review re-execution on ~2,800 loop iterations. Directionally a double-digit-percent median reduction, with the P90 improving too because the loops that generate the tail are the ones being cut.
- **Waiting time:** `Normalize financial statements` (20h) and `Credit analysis` (28h) fall as analyst work-in-process drops; `Resolve entity mismatch` (31h) falls as the 3-FTE pool is decongested and the 38% rework rate declines; `Request missing documents` (8h) falls and, more importantly, is entered far less often. `Wait for external valuation` (58h) and `Customer signing` (44h) are **unchanged** — this is the portfolio's honest gap, closed in Wave 2. Recognising the recorded-sequential-but-actually-concurrent KYC / document-review relationship matters here: measure the two branches (`Classify → KYC check` 7,250 and `Classify → Financial document review` 3,940) as parallel, or the improvement in whichever branch is not critical will be misattributed as cycle-time gain.
- **Rework:** the three real cycles in the DFG are directly targeted — document loop (A5), mismatch loop (A4), control-remediation loop (A12). Expect the `Request missing documents → Financial document review` return arc (2,800) and the `Resolve entity mismatch → KYC check` return arc (1,160) to shrink materially; the `Remediate control issue → Contract preparation` arc (510) shrinks through root-cause coding rather than automation.
- **Handovers:** fewer analyst↔analyst and analyst↔RM handovers on documents; the compliance-ops → two-senior-specialists dependency is deliberately reduced by codifying those seniors' resolution patterns into A4 — a direct mitigation of the key-person risk visible in the handover network.
- **Resource demand:** analyst effective capacity rises without headcount; compliance specialist exception time falls per case even as exception *density* stays flat (A3 is deferred, so density does not spike). Back office demand shifts from document chasing to portal-exception handling — reallocate the post-15:00 slack there. Legal ops idle time falls as upstream flow smooths, and the month-end peak becomes visible enough to justify A9 in year two.
- **Control violations:** A12 makes the 5.8% four-eyes and 6.5% provisional-signing violations measurable, alerted, and root-caused rather than merely counted; A4's cleaner KYC status reduces the ambiguity that drives provisional signing. Contract remediation (8.5%, 14% for Credit-line renewal) should fall through the causal taxonomy A12 produces, ahead of A9 automating the fix in year two.

### Portfolio C (A12, A3, A4, A9)
- **Cycle time:** modest. A3 saves part of the 4h KYC wait on 11,500 cases and A4 cuts the 31h mismatch wait; A9 cuts part of the 22h contract wait. But normalization (20h), analysis (28h), valuation (58h) and signing (44h) are untouched — well over half the elapsed path is unaddressed.
- **Waiting time:** KYC and contract waits fall; the analyst queues may *rise*, since faster KYC clearance pushes cases into `KYC check → Credit analysis` (4,200) sooner without any normalization improvement.
- **Rework:** the mismatch cycle and the contract-remediation cycle both shrink. The document loop (the largest, 3,300/2,800) is untouched — a significant omission.
- **Handovers:** compliance handovers fall in volume but the residual escalations are harder; the two-senior dependency could paradoxically intensify because A3 filters out the easy cases.
- **Resource demand:** compliance ops load falls, specialist load per case rises in difficulty; legal ops load falls at month end (A9); **credit analysts get more inflow with no relief** — the worst resource outcome of the three.
- **Control violations:** best of the three. Provisional signing should approach zero once KYC status is authoritative and A9 enforces terms-match-decision at generation; four-eyes breaches become blocked rather than detected. The cost is that both deep-integration slots are spent and customer-visible cycle time barely moves — a hard story to sustain politically for a full year.

## 5. Risks and compensating controls

| Risk | Where it bites | Compensating control |
|---|---|---|
| **Auditability gap** — model influence on a credit or KYC outcome cannot be reconstructed | A3, A4, A6, A8, A9 | Immutable per-case record of model version, input snapshot, suggestion, confidence, human override (with reason code), and final decision. Make the event log itself the audit artifact and mine it monthly. No module goes live without this. |
| **False negatives in KYC pre-classification** (severe: AML/sanctions failure) | A3 | Thresholds tuned for near-zero FN, accepting FP cost; mandatory human review of all borderline bands; periodic back-testing on known-positive samples; independent model validation and a documented named accountable owner. Defer A3 to Wave 2 so the escalation capacity exists first. |
| **False positives flooding a 3-FTE pool** | A3 → A4 | Sequence A4 before A3; monitor escalation volume per specialist weekly; pre-agree a surge protocol (cross-trained compliance ops) before A3 go-live. |
| **Silent extraction errors on low-quality PDFs** | A6 | Confidence scoring per extracted field; mandatory analyst confirmation below threshold; automatic reconciliation checks (balance-sheet identities, cross-year consistency); sample-based dual-key review of a fixed percentage of auto-accepted statements. |
| **Model drift** — portfolio mix, product mix, document formats, valuer behaviour all change | A2, A3, A6, A7 | Monthly monitoring of suggestion-acceptance rate, override rate by segment, and downstream defect rate (e.g. misroute rate, remediation rate); automatic rollback to human-default when acceptance rate drops below a pre-set band; scheduled retraining with champion/challenger evaluation. |
| **Exception handling degrades as volume shifts** — automation handles the easy 80%, leaving a harder residue for fewer, less-practised humans | All modules, acutely A3/A4 | Explicitly staff and design the exception path, not just the happy path; retain a deliberate share of manual cases to preserve skill; measure exception cycle time separately from overall cycle time; capture the two senior specialists' heuristics as documented rules (also de-risks the key-person concentration). |
| **Four-eyes erosion** — automated assembly blurs authorship | A8, A12, month-end peaks | System-enforced actor-separation check between Credit analysis and Committee packaging for High-exposure SME; block rather than warn; A12 dashboard on the 5.8% baseline with a month-end-specific alert and capacity plan. |
| **Provisional signing scaled up** | A10 | Hard system gate: signature request cannot be issued without an authoritative KYC-cleared flag. Ship the gate *before* the reminder automation, and use A12 to confirm the 6.5% rate is trending to zero as a gate criterion. |
| **Contract rule-library staleness** | A9 | Versioned rule library with a named legal owner, mandatory review cadence, and a rule-change log tied to the audit trail; test the library against the historical remediation corpus that A12 collects in Wave 1. Do not deploy A9 in year one until that corpus exists. |
| **Activation on incomplete controls** | A11 | Hard precondition checks on every control flag; refuse-and-escalate as the default; do not deploy until A12 shows a stable low violation rate. |
| **Bottleneck displacement / WIP inflation** | A1, A2 | Do not accelerate intake ahead of constraint relief; if A1 is built, cap release rate into the analyst queue (a CONWIP-style release policy) rather than pushing everything forward. |
| **Customer friction and RM resistance** | A5, A10 | Co-design checklists with RMs per product/exposure segment; make checklists adaptive rather than maximal; measure customer-response time and complaint rate as explicit guardrail metrics; A5's success case (RMs stop being blamed for loops) is the change-management argument. |
| **External partner disputes** | A7 | Triage rules agreed contractually; dispute-rate monitoring; retain full valuation as the default for High-exposure SME. |
| **Accountability displacement** — "the model decided" | All | Written allocation of decision accountability to a named human role per automated step; automation is decision-support with recorded human ownership, never a transfer of regulatory responsibility. |

## 6. Recommended phased rollout (year one)

**Wave 1 — months 0–6. Modules: A6, A4, A5, A12.** Deep core-banking integration slots used: **one** (A6 normalized-figures write-back; A4, A5 and A12 sit on the document, workflow and case-management layers). One slot is held in reserve for Wave 2.

- *Month 0–1:* baseline the process-mining measurement itself — reconstruct KYC / financial-document-review concurrency correctly, establish per-activity waiting-time and rework baselines, instrument the DFG loop arcs (3,300/2,800; 1,750/1,160; 650/510) as named KPIs. Stand up the audit-trail schema all modules will write to.
- *Month 1–3:* A5 first (fastest, lowest risk, immediately reduces dirty inflow so that A6 launches into cleaner documents) and A12 in parallel (pure observability plus workbench; no integration burden; starts accumulating the control-rule corpus for A9).
- *Month 2–5:* A6 in shadow mode — extract and normalize, but show analysts both the extraction and the source, measure field-level accuracy before allowing acceptance.
- *Month 4–6:* A4, built by codifying the two senior specialists' resolution patterns; assist-only with mandatory specialist sign-off.

*Monitoring metrics:* median and P90 end-to-end cycle time by product and applicant segment; waiting time at Normalize / Credit analysis / Resolve entity mismatch; loop-return counts on the three cycle arcs; extraction field accuracy and override rate; specialist queue length and per-specialist load concentration; four-eyes breach rate; provisional-signing rate; customer-response time and complaint rate.

*Gate criteria to exit Wave 1:* A6 field accuracy above an agreed threshold with override rate stable for two consecutive months; missing-document loop return arc down materially with no rise in customer complaints; mismatch median wait meaningfully below 31h; A12 producing a coded root-cause taxonomy covering the great majority of remediation events; **no increase** in any control-violation rate. Failing any gate, fix before adding scope — do not start Wave 2 modules on an unstable Wave 1.

**Wave 2 — months 6–12. Modules: A7, A10 (with A2 delivered as a rules-based, non-integrated recommender if capacity allows).** Second deep-integration slot goes to A10's signing/identity stack. A7 is deliberately paired with a *process redesign*: trigger `Collateral valuation request` immediately after classification rather than after `Credit analysis`, converting a 58h serial wait into a wait that runs parallel to document review and analysis. A10 goes live **only** after the KYC-clearance gate has been demonstrated hard by A12 data (provisional signing trending to zero).

*Gate criteria:* valuation wait and late rate both down; the 2,350→1,980 leakage explained; provisional-signing rate at or near zero for three consecutive months before reminder automation is switched on.

**Wave 3 — year two (planning starts month 9). Modules: A3, A8, A9, then A1 and A11.** A3 only once specialist escalation capacity is proven; A8 only once its four inputs are reliable; A9 seeded by A12's rule corpus; A1 as a cheap tactical build with a WIP-release cap; A11 last, gated on a stable low control-violation rate.

Budget note: the first-year budget covers four modules, satisfied by Wave 1. Wave 2 as described assumes year-one budget headroom from A12's low build cost or an early year-two start; if the four-module cap is strict for the whole year, Wave 1 stands alone and Wave 2 opens year two — the ordering logic is unchanged.

## 7. Final recommendation

**Recommend Portfolio B: A6 (financial-statement normalization), A4 (entity-mismatch assistant), A5 (missing-document checklist and nudging), A12 (control remediation workbench)** — with A7 and A10 sequenced immediately behind, and A1 deferred.

The reasoning is a constraint argument, not a savings argument. Touch time across the whole process is under 8 hours per case; waiting time is roughly 250–300. Any portfolio judged on minutes-saved-per-activity will optimize the 3% of the problem. Portfolio B targets, in order, the four largest *controllable* sources of elapsed time and rework: the analyst normalization/analysis queue (20h + 28h), the entity-mismatch pathology (31h wait, 38% rework, on the critical path in 82% of its occurrences), the document loop (3,300 out, 2,800 back, 52% repeat), and the control-remediation loop (650/510, 14% on Credit-line renewal). It satisfies the compliance requirement through A12 rather than by bolting on a token module, it consumes only one of two deep-integration slots, and it explicitly protects the two scarce pools — 9 analysts and 3 compliance specialists — that the resource networks identify as central and concentrated.

Portfolio C is the runner-up and would be the right answer if a supervisory examination were imminent; it is rejected here because it spends both integration slots, leaves the largest rework loop and the analyst constraint untouched, and would deliver a year with strong control metrics and almost no customer-visible improvement — which historically ends automation programmes.

**On the highest-frequency activity: A1 (auto-registration) should not be automated first.** Three grounds, all from the evidence. First, magnitude: registration contributes 8 minutes of service and 1.5h of waiting to a path measured in hundreds of hours, and 72% of intake is already digital, so the addressable share is smaller still. Second, the constraint is elsewhere and the queue is misread: back office is the *most visible* queue but drops below 65% utilization after 15:00 — it is a scheduling artefact, not a capacity constraint, and the cheaper fix is shift-shaping, not software. Third, and decisively, A1's own risk note is correct — releasing cases faster into an unchanged KYC, normalization, analysis and valuation system raises work-in-process without raising throughput, lengthening exactly the queues (28h analysis, 31h mismatch, 58h valuation) that already dominate. Automating the fastest step in front of the slowest ones converts a labor saving into a longer customer wait.

That said, A1 should not be abandoned — it is cheap, low-risk and genuinely saves back-office effort. The correct sequencing is to build it *after* the constraints are relieved, and to pair it with a release policy that caps work-in-process into the analyst queue, so the intake gain converts into throughput rather than into queue. The honest message to management is: automate the frequent step second, and the blocking steps first, because the customer experiences the process as a sequence of waits — and none of the longest ones are at the front door.
