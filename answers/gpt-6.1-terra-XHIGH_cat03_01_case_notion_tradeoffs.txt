## 1. Analytical assumptions

A **case notion** chooses an object type and makes one timestamp-ordered trace per object of that type. The baseline assigns an event to a trace only when the supplied event–object (E2O) relation directly links that event to that object. Thus e16 belongs to three order traces, two package traces, three item traces and one delivery trace. It is one original event, not nine independent executions.

I use **convergence** for several objects or their paths coming together in one shared event or object, as O2 and O3 do at P3 and V2. I use **divergence** for one object's work splitting across multiple objects or paths, as O1 does across P1 and P2. These are business relationships, not necessarily gateways, concurrency, or causal dependencies. Both can be obscured or multiplied by flattening; their ratings below describe exposure and interpretive risk, not the number of model branches.

Copied occurrences are both evidence that an event is directly relevant to several objects and duplication when counted as independent events. I distinguish original event IDs, event–object links, and distinct objects. An event frequency, an object frequency and a case-occurrence frequency have different denominators.

The primary analysis does not propagate object–object (O2O) relations. A separate sensitivity experiment may add, for example, the order associated with an item to an event directly linked to that item, recording every inferred link and its path. Such an enriched log is a different analytical dataset: static O2O relations do not establish when a relationship became valid, nor do they prove that every related event concerns every indirectly connected object. I assume the supplied timestamps order events within each trace; ties across cases, such as e29 and e30, do not imply a global execution order.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order, cross-order P3.** e13 is one Pack Package event for O2 and O3, so it appears twice in the order log. O2's Add Item→Pack Package edge and O3's Add Item→Pack Package edge represent two order participations, not two physical pack events. This is informative for per-order exposure but harmful if reported as two packing operations. P3's I21/I31 composition supplies the explanation.

2. **Order, cross-order P4.** e14 appears in both O3 and O4. O3's e13→e14 gives Pack Package→Pack Package, whereas O4 has a single pack occurrence. Treating this edge as repacking or a second attempt would be misleading: the source shows different packages P3 and P4, with P4 also containing O4's I41. The repeated activity usefully exposes multiple package work for O3 when the package IDs remain attached.

3. **Package, cross-order contents.** P3's e13→e16→e24 is one tidy package lifecycle, but P3 combines O2's I21 and O3's I31. P4's e14→e17→e27 similarly combines O3 and O4. A package-level duration is a valid elapsed time for each package, but an activity-only trace hides that delays or handling affect several orders. Inferring an order-specific service delay without order–item links is harmful.

4. **Order, consolidated delivery.** e16 and e24 are each copied into O1, O2 and O3; D2 carries P2 and P3. Its 4h55 dispatch-to-confirm interval is one delivery observation. Reporting three such occurrences as three independent deliveries inflates operational volume and gives D2 triple weight. Three affected orders is a valid, different metric. In O1, e15→e16 also produces Dispatch→Dispatch because D1 and D2 are different deliveries, not necessarily a dispatch retry.

5. **Package, consolidated delivery.** P2 and P3 both have the direct edge e16→e24, so their package log contains two Dispatch→Confirm edges generated by one D2 event pair. The two observations are valid for package exposure, but are correlated and cannot be treated as two independent delivery times. D2 has a single delivery-case edge; D1 and D3 each have one as well.

6. **Order, consolidated invoice.** e19 appears in both O2 and O3; e22 and e25 do too. An order log therefore records two Issue Invoice occurrences even though V2 is issued once, and duplicates each V2 payment event. This correctly shows both orders' involvement, but inflates counts of invoices or cash transactions. The snapshot gives no amounts or allocation, so neither duplicated record proves what either order paid.

7. **Invoice, partial payments.** V2 has e19→e22→e25, including Record Payment→Record Payment with 20h20 between the two events. The O2O qualifiers say M2 partially settles V2 and M3 completes settlement; the common activity label alone cannot distinguish instalments from corrections or retries. This edge is informative about a two-record sequence, but a claim about amounts or the share of either order paid is unsupported. V2's issue-to-final-record interval is 20h55, not the interval between payments.

8. **Payment, near-singleton lifecycle.** M2 has only e22 and M3 only e25; the same is true of M1 and M4 with their respective events. Flattening produces four singleton traces, no directly-follows edges and zero observed trace durations. These describe the limited recorded participation of payment objects, not the time to settle V2 or an instantaneous payment process. The invoice view and qualifiers reconnect the instalments.

9. **Order, interleaved threads.** In O3, e24 Confirm Delivery is followed by e25 Record Payment, then e27 Confirm Delivery. The corresponding DFG edges span a delivery and a payment thread; they do not establish that confirming D2 caused payment M3 or that M3 enabled confirmation of D3. The 17-hour e24→e25 gap is elapsed time between observed events, not measured payment-processing work. The sequence is useful chronology but a hazardous causal model.

10. **Item, missing lifecycle boundaries.** I11 begins at e05 Add Item and ends at e23 Confirm Delivery, while O1 begins at e01 and closes at e28. An item trace cannot measure O1 creation-to-close duration or prove O1 was fully delivered at e23: I12 is confirmed at e24. This truncation is useful when the intended unit is the observed item handling interval, but harmful when relabeled as end-to-end order time.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

Create the direct-E2O order log for per-order progression, activity sequences and creation-to-close elapsed time. Report copies and preserve original IDs. Create item and package logs for their respective observed fulfilment paths; the package view supports packing-to-confirmation duration. A delivery log supports one dispatch-to-confirm observation per D1–D3. An invoice log supports issue-to-recorded-payment sequences and instalment timing. The payment log is useful for counting recorded payment objects, although its singleton traces do not support lifecycle discovery. Every such metric should name its unit and population.

Use an **OC-DFG** to compare directly-follows relationships by object type without pretending that all objects share one case boundary. Inspect its event-based, unique-object and total-object edge measures separately; even object-centric counts still need interpretation. An **OTG** can summarize event-mediated transitions between object types, and an **ET–OT graph** can show which event types involve which object types. Neither should be treated as a substitute for examining the supplied qualified O2O links. An object interaction summary can flag objects jointly involved in events, such as O2/O3 at e13 and e19, but co-participation alone does not specify whether the relationship is packing, billing or a causal dependency.

For cross-order propagation questions, inspect the qualified O2O paths alongside event evidence: P3 joins O2/O3 via items, P4 joins O3/O4, and D2 joins P2/P3. The resulting overlap can connect multiple orders into a larger interaction structure, which is meaningful for shared operations but may be too coarse to be a customer case. Equivalent-execution clustering can help compare recurring object-centric structures in larger logs, but is not needed to establish the conclusions in this small snapshot; any clustering requires a specified execution boundary and review of what its equivalence preserves.

When views differ, first align the question, event IDs, unit, time window, relation rule and denominator. For example, three order occurrences, two package occurrences and one delivery occurrence for e24 are all consistent with one original confirmation involving three orders, two packages and D2. Trace each aggregate back to e24 and its direct relations, then to D2's carries links; do not average the counts together or declare one view wrong. Investigate any remaining discrepancy as a data or transformation issue.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

- **Decision criteria:** choose a lead that supports the main business outcome, has directly observed start and end markers, and includes enough relevant interactions to explain order progress. Also assess the degree of event copying and whether important many-to-many relations remain visible.
- **Strongest supporting evidence:** every O1–O4 has Create Order and Close Order events, and their direct traces include item, package, dispatch, invoice, payment and delivery events relevant to each order. O1's P1/P2 split and O3's P3/P4 participation make the order view useful for asking what each customer order experienced. Its 42 occurrences over 31 events make its cost explicit.
- **Strongest counterargument:** no single order trace faithfully represents the shared operations. e16 concerns three orders and two packages, while V2 and its instalments concern two orders. Aggregating order cases can overstate workload and produce misleading edges between interleaved threads. Delivery and invoice traces answer their own operational questions more directly.
- **Residual risk:** even with secondary item, package, delivery and invoice views, missing amounts, causal links and relationship validity times prevent allocation of V2 payments, proof of dependencies and exact reconstruction of unrecorded work. Activity-only exports may also discard the IDs needed to reconcile copies.
- **Conditions for change:** if the objective becomes transport efficiency, make Delivery primary; if cash collection, make Invoice primary. If the main question is coordinated work across orders, use an object-centric execution or interaction analysis as the primary view rather than forcing an order boundary. A richer event schema or time-qualified relationships could change which inferences are defensible, but longer traces alone would not justify the choice.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

The following uses direct relations already present in `ocel`. It keeps the separate results for comparison; it does not infer causal relationships or treat a graph as semantic validation. The named functions and signatures correspond to PM4Py 2.7.23.4; other versions may differ.

```python
import pm4py

print(ocel)

activity_counts = ocel.events[ocel.event_activity].value_counts()
object_type_counts = ocel.objects[ocel.object_type_column].value_counts()
print("Original event activities:\n", activity_counts)
print("Distinct objects by type:\n", object_type_counts)

object_types = ["Order", "Item", "Package", "Delivery", "Invoice", "Payment"]
flat_logs = {kind: pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, kind) for kind in object_types}
order_log = flat_logs["Order"]
item_log = flat_logs["Item"]
package_log = flat_logs["Package"]
delivery_log = flat_logs["Delivery"]
invoice_log = flat_logs["Invoice"]
payment_log = flat_logs["Payment"]

# DFG frequencies are flattened case occurrences, not unique original events.
dfg_results = {kind: pm4py.discover_dfg(log) for kind, log in flat_logs.items()}
order_dfg, order_starts, order_ends = dfg_results["Order"]
invoice_dfg, invoice_starts, invoice_ends = dfg_results["Invoice"]
order_skeleton = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(order_log)
invoice_skeleton = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(invoice_log)

oc_dfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
event_type_object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
object_interactions = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

print("Order DFG:", order_dfg)
print("Invoice DFG:", invoice_dfg)
print("Object interactions:\n", object_interactions)

# check edge frequencies and elapsed times against the direct relations.
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Copies and weights.** Retain an original event ID on every flattened occurrence. Recompute activity counts with one contribution per event ID and, separately, with each of its k order occurrences weighted 1/k. Thus the three e16 order occurrences sum to one event but still show three affected orders in an unweighted exposure measure. For edges, do not blindly deduplicate by activity label: preserve both event IDs and define an edge weighting rule, since the same event may have different predecessors or successors in different cases. Compare these results with the unweighted order DFG and explain the changed estimand.

2. **Direct versus propagated relations.** Build a separate enriched variant with explicit path rules and provenance, for example event→item→containing order, and compare its relation pairs and affected traces against direct E2O. Do not assume all paths are equally valid: propagating from V2 to both billed orders can obscure payment allocation, and traversing D2 to packages and items can introduce broad associations. Check newly added links individually against qualifiers and any available relationship timestamps; the supplied O2O facts alone do not establish historical validity. Keep the direct log as baseline.

3. **Change the lead.** Repeat the same clearly defined timing and frequency questions with Order, Package, Delivery and Invoice as appropriate. Compare O1's two dispatches with D1/D2, P2/P3 with D2, and O2/O3 with V2. Explain whether differences reflect unit selection, missing direct events, shared events or actual anomalies. Do not compare creation-to-close with dispatch-to-confirm as if they measured the same interval.

4. **OC-DFG counting conventions.** For each relevant typed edge compare metrics based on original events or event pairs, unique participating objects and total object occurrences as defined by the PM4Py result and version. In particular inspect Dispatch→Confirm for D2 across Order, Package and Delivery types: e16/e24 support three order participations, two package participations and one delivery participation. Verify the library's aggregation definitions before assigning those values to a particular OC-DFG measure; repeated occurrences and sets are not interchangeable.

5. **Trace and time audit.** Reconcile 31 original events with the six matrix counts, checking event IDs and qualified relations. Manually verify P1's 4h, P2's 5h50 and D3's 22h45 observed intervals, and V2's 20h55 issue-to-last-payment interval. Test whether conclusions change when an interleaved event such as e25 is viewed as part of the invoice thread rather than as a prerequisite for e27. Distinguish elapsed calendar time from processing time and treat missing events and unobserved relationship changes as limitations.
