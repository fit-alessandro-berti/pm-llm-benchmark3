# 1. Data audit and estimands

The table supports estimates of **recorded arrivals and completions per open hour**, daily active-handling quantiles, and sampled in-system occupancy. For a set of *n* days, let *H* = 12 × *n* hours, *A* be total arrivals, *D* be total recorded completions, and *G* = *A* − *D* be the recorded net balance. The observed flow rates are *A/H* and *D/H*, in cases/hour. All rate denominators include the full 07:00–19:00 opening window, including the outage unless stated otherwise. *L* is the average of the daily mean WIP values. Days have equal exposure, so this estimates open-hour mean occupancy if the snapshot schedules are comparable and adequately cover each day. WIP is defined here as cases waiting plus cases in service. Staff exposure is 12 hours times the sum of the daily FTE values.

**Service time** is active handling time per case. **Flow time** is time from queue entry to exit, including waiting and other elapsed time in the system. An open-time flow measure pauses the clock overnight. A calendar-time measure includes time spent in the system while the center is closed. The service columns are daily quantiles. Their ranges across days describe how those quantiles vary and do not identify a case-weighted mean or a regime-level service-time distribution.

- **Peak shares disagree across sources.** All four hourly arrival and completion vectors sum to their daily counts. The 08:00 and 09:00 bins imply arrival shares of 16.8%, 18.1%, 9.6%, and 11.8%, versus reported daily shares of 31%, 32%, 47%, and 22%, respectively. The gaps exceed rounding. Use the bins for timing on those dates and treat the daily peak field as a separate, provisional indicator. Its definition, bin alignment, and extraction need reconciliation.
- **The records fail a stock-flow reconciliation.** Suppose the reported counts are the only inventory movements, closing inventory carries into the next opening, and WIP covers the same cases. With nonnegative inventory at the first opening, the recorded net of +88 through April 24 implies at least 88 cases at the April 25 opening. Even if all 43 April 25 completions occurred before the first snapshot, WIP at any snapshot would be at least 45, versus the reported mean of 23. The same test gives minima of 57 versus 20 on April 26, 59 versus 22 on May 2, and 65 versus 19 on May 3. A different snapshot time or a higher initial inventory cannot reconcile those differences. Unrecorded exits or entries, off-hours movements, and differences in count or WIP scope are possible explanations. Use *G* as a recorded balance and keep actual backlogs and clearances unverified.
- **Arrival and completion cohorts differ.** Cases can carry overnight. An elevated completion count can draw from prior arrivals, and a zero daily net can coincide with old cases remaining. The service-quantile sampling cohort and treatment of unfinished cases are unspecified.
- **Occupancy snapshots miss some dynamics.** Fifteen-minute sampling can miss short spikes, and daily averaging can hide a high opening backlog, a midday queue, or a late clearance. No measured opening or closing inventory is supplied.
- **Staff availability and capacity differ.** Mean concurrent FTE obscures shift timing, skills, breaks, outages and allocations to other work. Observed completions per staff-hour depend on available cases, cohort and mix, as well as effective capacity.
- **Variant coding and staffing are plausible confounders.** The reported mix rise could reflect complexity, coding, or both. The April 29 intervention time is unknown, and its before/after comparison also changes burst profile, handling quantiles and backlog conditions.
- **The time series has calendar structure and shared state.** Consecutive days can inherit work, weekday and weekend volumes differ, and the four selected hourly profiles offer little replication. Independent-day or identically distributed interval assumptions need evidence.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

The 28-day pool contains 2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions in 336 open hours, or **7.50 arrivals/h and 7.17 completions/h**, with mean WIP 29.9 and mean nominal staffing 5.14 FTE. The gap of 0.33 cases/h conceals flows of different magnitudes and directions.

1. **Calendar composition changes apparent throughput.** Weekdays average 9.00 arrivals/h, 8.60 completions/h and WIP 34.9. Weekends average 3.74, 3.60 and 17.4, respectively. For an observed Simpson's-paradox-like reversal, compare April 6–14 with April 30–May 3, excluding the transition day. Completions rise within weekdays from **8.30 to 10.08/h** and within weekends from **3.29 to 4.08/h**, yet the unstratified rate falls from **7.19 to 7.08/h**. The weekend weight rises from 2/9 to 2/4. At the early calendar weights, the later rate is **8.75/h**. This standardizes the day mix alone, leaving other operational changes in the comparison.
2. **A near-constant completion rate can hide rising demand and heavier handling.** Before the intervention, early and high-mix weekdays both average 5.93 FTE and complete 8.30 and 8.38 cases/h respectively, or 1.40 and 1.41 per nominal staff-hour. Yet arrivals rise from 8.35 to 9.50/h, WIP from 20.3 to 44.3, and the recorded net from +4 to +94 over seven days each. The arrival-weighted reported complex share rises from 20.9% to 44.6%, versus 33.8% pooled. Daily p90 shifts from 44–50 to 78–88 minutes. If mean staff work per arrival were 40 minutes in both windows, an illustrative assumption only, demand would consume about 94% of nominal staff time early and 107% in the high-mix window. The actual mean and utilization are unknown. The mix, service and congestion changes co-occur, and no variant-specific service distribution or coding audit is supplied.
3. **A before/after average can confuse sustained capacity with short-term output.** Comparing Thursday–Friday April 23–24 with April 30–May 1 keeps day type fixed. Mean FTE rises from 5.75 to 6.75, arrivals from 9.04 to 9.54/h and completions from 8.125 to 10.08/h. Reported WIP drops from 43.5 to 34.0, and count balance moves from +22 to −13. Arrival-weighted reported mix stays near 44% in both windows, while the reported peak share and service quantiles fall. The weekend pairs likewise move from +12 to zero balance at 2.5 versus 3.0 FTE. These observations support a staffing hypothesis, with backlog, timing and changing handling conditions still in play. Similarly, pooling April 15–17 gives 8.31 completions/h, nearly the ordinary weekday rate of 8.30/h. That average conceals 4.83/h on the outage day, 10.04/h on the two recovery days, and a recorded net of +17 over the three days.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For the 12 hourly counts in each vector, the Fano factor is sample variance divided by mean, and the coefficient of variation (CV) is sample standard deviation divided by mean. CV here concerns **bin counts**, since interarrival and intercompletion times are unavailable.

| Date | 08:00–10:00 A share, bins vs daily field | Arrival Fano / CV | Completion Fano / CV | Largest hourly A and hourly net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apr 7 | 16.8% vs 31% | 3.23 / 0.62 | 1.70 / 0.45 | 20 A and +6 net at 10:00–11:00 |
| Apr 15 | 18.1% vs 32% | 4.14 / 0.69 | 2.25 / 0.68 | 22 A at 10:00–11:00, largest net +17 at 11:00–12:00 |
| Apr 21 | 9.6% vs 47% | 10.12 / 1.03 | 0.98 / 0.34 | 34 A and +21 net at 10:00–11:00 |
| Apr 25 | 11.8% vs 22% | 1.29 / 0.55 | 0.78 / 0.47 | 8 A and +2 net at 11:00–12:00 |

April 21 also has 24 arrivals against 12 completions at 13:00–14:00. Its daily average of 9.58 arrivals/h gives little indication of either surge. On April 15, zero completions in the outage bins drive part of the completion variability. The vectors record completions, so zero output does not determine how much handling continued during those hours.

The hourly Fano values describe variation over clock hours. Spread in hourly means alone can generate a Fano factor above one even with independent, time-varying Poisson arrivals. A claim of excess stochastic variation at a given intensity would require repeated comparable clock slots, adjustment for changing exposure and mean, and checks for dependence. At the daily scale the arrival Fano factor is **10.03 pooled** (CV 0.33), versus **0.66 on weekdays** and **0.73 on weekends** (CV 0.08 and 0.13). It is 0.26 and 0.29 on the early and high-mix weekday subsets, respectively. Those subsets have seven days each. Tight daily totals can coexist with strong subday bursts, and these small-sample values do not establish a count distribution.

Pearson correlations across actual lagged day pairs are 0.41 at lag 1 and 0.99 at lag 7 for arrivals, 0.30 and 0.80 for completions, and 0.61 and 0.63 for WIP. The large lag-7 values are consistent with a weekly calendar. After subtracting the observed mean for each named weekday, lag-1 correlations are about 0.80 for arrivals, 0.07 for completions and 0.76 for WIP. The residual structure can reflect the level shift, outage and recovery, and persistent operating conditions or inherited work. The recorded daily net has lag-1 correlation near zero, which does not settle backlog persistence because balances accumulate. Twenty-eight days give only four weekly cycles, and adjacent lag pairs overlap.

The clearest dated disruption is April 15. The April 20 weekday marks the start of the recorded high-mix, longer-tail and sustained positive-balance run. An exploratory screen standardizes the two reported weekday fields, complex share and peak share, and chooses the common split minimizing within-segment squared deviations with at least five weekdays on each side. April 20 reduces the squared error by about 91% versus one level. It also wins with complex share alone (96% reduction), so the split survives omitting the disputed peak field. These are in-sample fit differences. The data do not distinguish an instantaneous operational break from a coding or gradual mix transition around that weekend. April 29 is a known intervention date with uncertain timing and little follow-up. Four selected hourly days are insufficient to estimate a stochastic arrival process, a service distribution, time-varying staffing capacity or the distribution of opening backlogs. A time-varying queue with explicit availability, case classes and carried state would be a candidate model once those inputs are measured.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law is *L* = *λW*, where *L* is long-run mean occupancy, *λ* is throughput, and *W* is mean time in system for the same population on the same clock. A stable repeating calendar can support an average over complete cycles if flows and inventory reconcile. In a finite window, integrated occupancy equals the sum of case residence **within** that window. Cases already present contribute only their observed overlap, and cases still present at the end contribute time before exit. Even exactly equal arrival and completion counts can leave substantial boundary contributions to a completer-based mean. Boundary inventories and ages or case timestamps would be needed to account for them.

The two calculations below use only the 07:00–19:00 intervals and treat snapshots as an approximation to the time integral over those intervals. The resulting units are open-time minutes. Recorded completions are the primary throughput proxy conditional on completions being the relevant exits and WIP covering the same population. Arrival rates are shown as a sensitivity check where the counts are close. Agreement between the proxies checks the denominator, while the boundary-residence and measurement conditions remain unverified.

# 7. Operational implications

- **Roster by day type, skill and verified hourly demand.** A policy of removing or redeploying weekday coverage because pooled demand is only 7.50/h and recovery achieved about 10 completions/h looks attractive on those averages. High-mix weekdays before the intervention actually averaged 9.50 arrivals/h against 8.38 completions/h at nearly six FTE, with individual hour counts as high as 34. Use coverage for the relevant skills, including capacity for ongoing intake during a backlog drawdown. Track whether the extra FTE sustains output and improves wait and case age at comparable intake and mix over more matched weeks.
- **Run outage recovery against measured stock and age.** Reconcile inventory and all exit types first, then track opening and closing work, oldest case age, new intake and net clearances by class. Use a time-limited recovery roster and release it when both inventory and age return to their agreed range while current arrivals are covered. The April 16–17 completion rates are observed during recovery and should be budgeted as such.
- **Smooth peaks using audited timestamps.** Examine appointment spacing, deferrable internal releases and breaks by hour, while protecting access for urgent work. April 21 shows pressure at 10:00–11:00 and 13:00–14:00. The disagreement with the 08:00–10:00 field makes a fixed morning-only staffing response premature. Measure waiting percentiles, abandonment and any displacement of queues into later slots.
- **Instrument cases and capacity.** Record queue entry, handling start/pause/resume, final exits and their reasons, routing and transfers, variant definitions and coding versions, opening and closing inventory, effective staffing by skill and interval, and exact outage and intervention timestamps. Use unique case identifiers and define queue membership at each event. Reconcile all entries and exits to inventory before estimating flow time. With multiple comparable weeks and stable logging, compare like clock slots and case classes, use calendar-aware or blocked evaluation, and assess interventions with a comparison queue or an extended interrupted series where feasible.
