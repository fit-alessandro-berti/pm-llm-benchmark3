DRIFT AUDIT: FIELD-SERVICE BREACH-RISK MODEL, JANUARY-OCTOBER 2026

Reading conventions used throughout
- "Observed" = value present in the supplied tables or audit findings. "Inferred" = arithmetic I performed on those values. "Hypothesis" = a causal claim not yet established by the supplied evidence.
- One arithmetic device is used repeatedly, so it is stated once here. The mix-implied breach rate for a month is the variant-share-weighted average of that month's matured per-variant breach rates. Comparing it with the observed pooled breach rate separates "the pooled number moved because the case mix moved" from "the pooled number moved because outcomes conditional on a path moved" and from "the pooled number moved because the labelled subset is not representative."

Three conclusions follow immediately and they anchor the rest of the audit. First, April's label channel was healthy even though April's feature channel was not: the outcome side reconciles once you rescale for the unmapped 18%, while the prediction side is badly off (mean predicted 0.28 against an observed 0.19). Second, June's fall in the pooled breach rate is fully reproduced by the change in variant shares with per-variant rates held at their baseline values, so June requires no appeal to a changed outcome relationship. Third, October's pooled breach rate is arithmetically irreconcilable with its own variant mix and its own supplier performance; it is a property of the labelled subsample, not of the month.

## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

January to March. A stable regime and the only clean reference period. AUROC 0.81-0.82, ECE 0.022-0.025, mean predicted risk within a point of observed, 100% maturity, data-quality indicators near zero, variant shares flat at roughly 45/35/20, per-variant breach rates flat, and the parts edge drifting mildly from 28 h to 35 h. This is the baseline against which everything later should be compared, and it is also the only period whose features and labels are simultaneously trustworthy without remediation.

April. A schema and pipeline incident, not a model or process failure. Every symptom is on the input side: unknown activities at 17%, missing queue feature at 14%, unmapped variants at 18%, prefixes anchored to the wrong event or not built. Every outcome-side quantity is normal: per-variant matured breach rates within a point of the baseline band, pooled observed rate 0.19, the parts edge at 34 h in line with the March trend, the remote-fix edge unchanged. The decisive detail is the direction of the calibration error: the model over-predicted (0.28 against 0.19). A real deterioration in the world produces the opposite signature, under-prediction against a rising observed rate, which is exactly what August shows. April's metrics are therefore best read as "we scored the wrong prefixes with partly imputed features," and April must not be allowed to contribute a single gradient step or a single calibration point until it is rebuilt from raw. Treating April as drift would teach the model that a pipeline outage is a risk factor.

May. Partial and incomplete recovery, which is itself diagnostic. The 6 May mapping patch pulled unknown activities from 17% to 2% and unmapped variants from 18% to 1%, and AUROC recovered from 0.67 to 0.78. But ECE at 0.050 is still double the baseline and AUROC is still short of 0.81, and the audit tells us why: the cached "number of events before diagnosis" feature was not fully backfilled, and duplicate removal covered only the new source and only after 20 April. So May's residual gap is most plausibly leftover pipeline debt rather than a new phenomenon. This matters practically, because May sits at the boundary between "quarantine" and "training data" and the answer depends on a per-record lineage check, not on the aggregate.

June. Real process drift of the benign kind. The 1 June routing policy moved V2 from 35% to 52% and V1 from 45% to 30%. The pooled breach rate fell from 0.18 to 0.15, and the mix-implied value is 0.158, so the entire pooled movement is composition. Per-variant rates are untouched (V1 0.16, V2 0.09, V3 0.35). AUROC dips slightly to 0.79 and ECE rises to 0.060, but both are consistent with a stable scoring function evaluated on a re-weighted population: mass moved into a large, low-risk, internally homogeneous segment, which reduces the between-segment variance that AUROC feeds on, and the model's mean prediction of 0.20 now sits above a base rate of 0.15 purely because the population changed underneath a fixed function. The right response in June is mix-standardized reporting and possibly a prior/prevalence adjustment, not retraining. Anyone who retrained in June would have spent the effort chasing an intended business change.

July. The first genuine outcome-relationship movement, and it is narrow. The parts edge steps from 42 h to 65 h in the same month a supplier begins missing delivery targets, and V3's matured breach rate steps from 0.35 to 0.46 while V1 and V2 hold at 0.17 and 0.10. Pooled metrics barely notice: 0.19 observed against 0.18 predicted, AUROC 0.76. This is the important month for a monitoring system to catch, because the pooled dashboard is nearly quiet while a segment has moved by eleven points and a leading process indicator has moved by 50%. Maturity has also begun to slip (98%), and it will slip preferentially in the very segment that is deteriorating.

August and September. Three effects overlap, and unlike April they are all real. Concept drift: the parts edge reaches 130 h then 148 h, and V3's breach rate reaches 0.64 then 0.61, roughly double its baseline, for cases following the identical path. Process drift in composition: V3's share rises from 22% to 35% and then 42%, so the degraded segment simultaneously grows. Population shift: from September, Partner Z contributes 24% of cases, routes 58% of them through V3, and brings two risk-relevant attributes (rural travel distance, device age) that the model cannot see at all. The signature is now the mirror image of April's: the model under-predicts (0.19 and 0.21 against 0.28 and 0.31), ECE reaches 0.120 and 0.140, Brier reaches 0.197 and 0.214. Critically, August is the clean separator between the process/concept story and the population story, because Partner Z's share is still 0% in August while V3's share and V3's breach rate have already jumped. And because maturity is 93% and 82% with length-biased backlog, the observed pooled rates understate the damage; the mix-implied 0.313 and 0.341 are the better estimates of where the month actually landed.

October. Dominated by label maturity to the point that the predictive metrics are uninterpretable. Maturity is 55% and median label delay 52 d, up from 33 d. The observed breach rate falls to 0.16 while the variant mix is essentially September's (23/35/41) and the parts edge has worsened again to 155 h. Under September's per-variant rates that mix implies about 0.34. A decline from 0.31 to 0.16 in that context is not a recovery; it is the arithmetic consequence of a backlog that preferentially withholds labels for long paths, which are precisely the parts-dependent, Partner Z, breach-prone cases. The apparent over-prediction (0.23 against 0.16) is an artifact of the same selection, and it points in the opposite direction to the true August-September error, which is why acting on October's calibration numbers would push the model exactly the wrong way. October's AUROC of 0.64 is likewise measured on a truncated, easier cohort and is not comparable to earlier months.

Why these four episodes must not be pooled. They differ in mechanism, in direction, and in remedy. April is an input-channel outage with over-prediction and a clean label channel; the remedy is backfill and recomputation. June is an intended control-flow change with stable conditional outcomes; the remedy is mix-standardized reporting. August-September is a real, segment-localized change in the outcome relationship compounded by a growing degraded segment and a brand-new subpopulation; the remedy is features plus retraining plus possibly segment-level modelling. October is a measurement artifact in the label channel; the remedy is to stop reporting it as if it were a month. A single label such as "the model degraded from April onward" would prescribe retraining on April's corrupted features and October's censored labels, using a calibration correction fitted with the wrong sign, while leaving the one real cause, invisible supplier lead time, entirely unaddressed.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

Each test names the discriminandum, the procedure, and the outcome expected under competing hypotheses. "H" labels are local to each test.

3.1 Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting
Procedure. For each start-month cohort, plot the empirical CDF of label-arrival delay (case start to outcome timestamp) and the cumulative observed breach rate as a function of days since case start. Stratify the curves by variant and by source. Separately, freeze evaluation vintages: recompute all monthly metrics as of a common maturity horizon (for example, only cases with at least 60 days elapsed and a resolved label), and also recompute each historical month's metrics as they would have looked at the same relative maturity that October now has.
Expected under H1 (October is censoring, not improvement): October's cumulative breach curve at 30 days will resemble earlier months' 30-day curves rather than being lower, the delay CDF will show a distinct right shift concentrated in V3 and Partner Z, and re-evaluating January at 55% maturity will reproduce an artificially low breach rate and a similarly depressed AUROC. Expected under H2 (October genuinely improved): October's breach rate remains low even at matched maturity, the delay CDF shift is uniform across variants, and historical months re-truncated to 55% maturity do not show the same downward bias. Falsifier for the censoring diagnosis: if truncating January-March to October's maturity profile leaves their breach rates and AUROC unchanged, then October's numbers cannot be dismissed as an artifact and a real change must be sought.
3.2 Recomputation after activity-label normalization and duplicate cleaning
Procedure. Rebuild the April and May feature store from raw events with (a) `Diagnostic Assessment Completed` and `Initial Diagnosis Completed` mapped to a single canonical activity, (b) `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` events either resolved by source-system lookup or explicitly flagged and excluded with a count, (c) duplicate detection applied to both sources across the entire window rather than only the new source after 20 April, and (d) all cached derived features, especially "number of events before diagnosis", invalidated and recomputed rather than backfilled. Re-score the original model, unchanged, on the rebuilt features, and re-derive prediction prefixes so that each is anchored at the canonical diagnosis event.
Expected under H1 (April is pipeline failure): AUROC returns to roughly 0.80-0.82, ECE to roughly 0.02-0.03, mean predicted risk to roughly 0.19, unmapped variant share to about 0%, and the April variant distribution reverts to about 45/35/20. Expected under H2 (April contained a genuine drift on top of the outage): metrics improve but plateau materially short of baseline, and the residual gap localizes to identifiable segments or features rather than vanishing. A third, checkable possibility is that duplicates inflated the events-before-diagnosis feature and thereby the risk score; that predicts the over-prediction shrinks specifically on cases with detected duplicates. Falsifier: if recomputation does not move April's AUROC, the pipeline diagnosis is wrong and April needs to be re-opened as a real drift.
3.3 Calibration and discrimination stratified by variant, source, and month
Procedure. Report AUROC, Brier, ECE, reliability curves and observed-versus-expected ratios in cells defined by month x variant, and from September also month x source (Partner Z versus others), always on maturity-matched cohorts. Add a mix-standardized pooled metric that reweights each month's variant cells to the January-March shares, and decompose the Brier score into calibration and refinement components.
Expected under H1 (June-July is composition only): per-variant AUROC and calibration stay near baseline in every cell while the pooled AUROC falls; the mix-standardized pooled metric stays near 0.81. Expected under H2 (real degradation from June): per-variant metrics degrade too, and mix standardization does not repair the pooled number. For August-September, expected under H3 (V3-localized concept drift): V1 and V2 cells remain well calibrated while V3's observed-over-expected ratio approaches 3 (0.64 against a predicted level near 0.22); expected under H4 (global model decay): all three variants show comparable calibration drift.
3.4 Comparison of process variants and DFG / performance-DFG edges
Procedure. Discover the monthly DFG and performance DFG on normalized, deduplicated events. Track (i) the appearance or disappearance of edges, (ii) the median and 90th percentile of `Order Part -> Part Received`, `Part Received -> Dispatch Technician`, `Dispatch Technician -> Repair Completed`, and the diagnosis-to-`Remote Fix` edge, and (iii) variant shares and the rank order of variants by frequency. Compute the share of total case duration attributable to each edge.
Expected under H1 (fulfilment-side performance drift): the parts edge inflates monotonically from July while all other edges, notably the remote-fix edge at 3.8-4.6 h, stay flat, and the control-flow topology is unchanged. Expected under H2 (control-flow drift, as in June): variant shares move sharply while every edge duration stays flat. Expected under H3 (April-style pipeline breakage): new spurious edges appear involving `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` or self-loops from duplicates, unmapped variants appear, and the effects vanish under 3.2. These three signatures are mutually distinguishable, which is why this test carries a lot of the load.
3.5 Did the outcome relationship change conditional on the same path?
Procedure. Restrict to cases whose realized path is exactly V3 (and separately V1, V2) and regress the breach indicator on month with baseline features held fixed; equivalently, compare monthly V3 breach rates on maturity-matched cohorts and test the July-September rise against the January-June band. Then add a candidate mediator, the realized `Order Part -> Part Received` duration or the contemporaneous supplier lead-time index, and see whether the month effect is absorbed. Complementarily, check the label definition: recompute breach under a fixed promise-date rule to confirm the SLA target was not silently retightened, since the audit only records that no change was documented.
Expected under H1 (concept drift mediated by supplier lead time): a large, significant month effect for V3 only, which shrinks toward zero once lead time enters, and no month effect for V1 or V2. Expected under H2 (label-definition change): month effects appear across all variants simultaneously, and recomputation under the fixed promise rule removes them. Expected under H3 (pure covariate drift with no concept drift): no within-V3 month effect at all, and the pooled movement is explained entirely by shares. Falsifier for concept drift: if V3's rise disappears under maturity matching, it was censoring; if it disappears under lead-time conditioning, the correct fix is a feature, not a new label of "unexplained drift."
3.6 Population reweighting and transport-style checks for Partner Z
Procedure. Fit a source-membership propensity model on the pre-existing feature space to quantify covariate overlap between Partner Z and the historical population; report the propensity-overlap region and the effective sample size after weighting. Then reweight the non-Partner-Z September population to Partner Z's variant and feature distribution and ask whether the reweighted breach rate approaches Partner Z's 0.45. Match Partner Z and non-Partner-Z cases on variant and on maturity horizon before comparing outcomes at all. Where the proxies exist (postcode-derived distance, device age from asset records), quantify how much of the residual gap they explain.
Expected under H1 (Partner Z excess is mix and parts exposure): after variant and maturity matching the Partner Z gap collapses from 0.45-versus-0.27 to a few points, and reweighting the legacy population to Partner Z's mix reproduces most of 0.45. Expected under H2 (Partner Z carries independent, unmodelled risk): a substantial gap survives matching and reweighting, concentrated in the region of poor propensity overlap, and shrinks when travel distance and device age are added. Expected under H3 (the gap is largely a maturity artifact): the gap shrinks sharply under maturity matching alone, because Partner Z labels mature more slowly. This test must precede any statement about model fairness across partners.
3.7 Feature missingness and lineage audits
Procedure. Produce per-month, per-feature missingness and imputation-rate series; reconcile the feature store against raw events for a sampled set of cases per month; verify for each April and May record whether the mapping backfill and the derived-feature recomputation actually ran (not merely whether the patch was deployed); test for prefix-anchor errors by confirming that no feature used at prediction time is populated from an event at or after the diagnosis anchor; and quantify duplicate rates by source and date to size the 1-20 April gap.
Expected under H1 (April/May pipeline debt): missingness spikes align exactly with the 1 April and 6 May boundaries, a subset of April records shows a mapped activity label but a stale cached events-before-diagnosis value, and duplicate rates are elevated in the old source and before 20 April. Expected under H2 (a monitoring-only artifact, with features actually fine): reconciliation against raw shows agreement and the metric drop must be explained elsewhere. A leakage check is included because "prefixes built at the wrong event" can also mean post-anchor information entered the features, which would inflate rather than degrade apparent performance in any month where it happened; if April's rebuilt AUROC comes back above 0.85, suspect leakage rather than celebrate.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

Data fitness for training and calibration
- January-March: safe for both training and calibration. Fully mature, clean lineage, stable regime. This is the reference cohort.
- April: unsafe for training, unsafe for calibration, unsafe for evaluation until 3.2 and 3.7 pass. Labels are usable in isolation but the features are not, and mismatched pairs are worse than no data. If recomputation succeeds, April is promoted to safe; if it partially succeeds, keep only records with verified lineage.
- May: conditionally safe. Aggregate indicators are near-normal but not at baseline, and the cached-feature gap and the pre-20-April duplicate gap can both leak into May-adjacent derived values. Use only after per-record lineage verification; exclude cases whose features were computed from the cache.
- June: safe for training and calibration, but it defines a new operating mix. Nothing about June needs correcting in the model; what needs correcting is the reporting.
- July: safe (98% maturity), and important, because it contains the onset of the real concept drift.
- August: usable with censoring-aware handling (93% maturity, length-biased backlog). Suitable for calibration of V1 and V2 directly; for V3 use inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting or restrict to a fixed 60-day horizon.
- September: usable for diagnosis and for weighted calibration, unsafe as unweighted training data (82% maturity, and the missing labels are concentrated in V3 and Partner Z, the two segments of interest). Any unweighted use will systematically teach the model that V3 and Partner Z are safer than they are.
- October: unsafe for training, calibration and evaluation (55% maturity, informative censoring). Report it only as a maturity-adjusted estimate with explicit uncertainty, and re-open it once maturity exceeds a pre-agreed threshold (for example 90%) at a later vintage.

Stage 1, immediate containment (days 0-3, no model change)
Freeze automated retraining and freeze any auto-recalibration job. Withdraw October from all scorecards and restate August-September as maturity-matched estimates alongside the mix-implied values. Mark April as quarantined in the feature store and in any training-set selector. Publish a variant-aware risk floor for operational use: for V3 cases, override the model's output with the recent matured V3 base rate (roughly 0.6) or apply an explicit uplift, and flag the override as a temporary control with an expiry date. Add current supplier lead time to the operational dashboard so that dispatchers and promise-date setting can react without waiting for the model. Notify SLA/commercial owners that promised dates for parts-dependent cases are being set against a 155 h fulfilment reality that the promise logic was calibrated for at 30 h; that is a business-process fix with more leverage than any modelling change.
Stage 2, short-term recalibration (week 1-3, no new features)
Fit a variant-stratified calibration map (isotonic or Platt) on maturity-matched data from June through August, excluding April, excluding October, and using censoring weights for August. Expect V1 and V2 to need almost no adjustment and V3 to need a large upward shift. Do not fit a single pooled calibration map: a pooled map would smear V3's threefold under-prediction across V1 and V2 and break two well-behaved segments to partially fix a third. Recalibration is the right instrument here only for the calibration component of the failure; note explicitly that it cannot recover discrimination, because the information that separates a fast-parts case from a slow-parts case is not in the feature vector at all. Re-fit monthly while the supplier situation is unstable, and treat the calibration map's own drift as a monitored quantity.
Stage 3, feature repair (week 2-6, the highest-value work)
This stage, not retraining, addresses the root cause. Add: current and trailing supplier/part-category lead time as known at prediction time; a parts-dependency propensity or predicted-V3 indicator available at diagnosis; open-order backlog and queue depth (repaired, with the caching defect fixed); travel distance / rurality and device age, so that Partner Z's population is representable at all; and source/partner identity, used with care and audited for proxy effects. Simultaneously fix the pipeline properly: canonical activity dictionary with alerting on unmapped labels, duplicate removal applied uniformly across sources and history, derived-feature recomputation on any upstream schema change, and a hard rule that a cache is invalidated rather than backfilled. Re-run 3.2 and confirm April's metrics recover before using April data.
Stage 4, full retraining (week 4-10, after Stage 3 lands)
Retrain on a window of January-March plus rebuilt April-May plus June-August, with September included only under censoring weights and October excluded, using a fixed-vintage label snapshot so the run is reproducible. Weight recent months up to reflect the current mix, or better, train on the full window and correct prevalence explicitly rather than letting the sampling window encode the mix implicitly. Evaluate on a maturity-matched holdout with per-variant and per-source breakdowns, and require that the new model beat the old model plus the Stage 2 calibration map, not merely the uncorrected old model. Ship behind a shadow-scoring period and compare against the temporary V3 override before removing it.
Segment-specific or variant-specific modelling
Justified in a limited form. V3 is the strongest candidate: its base rate, its dynamics, its dominant driver (an exogenous lead time) and its label-maturity profile all differ from V1 and V2, and it is now 41% of volume, so a dedicated model has enough data. But the case for a separate V3 model weakens considerably if the missing lead-time feature explains the drift, because then one model with the right feature dominates two models with the wrong one; run test 3.5 first and let the mediation result decide. A pragmatic middle path is a single shared model with variant-aware calibration and variant interactions, which captures most of the benefit at a fraction of the maintenance cost. A separate Partner Z model is not justified: Partner Z has two months of partly immature data, its excess risk is substantially confounded with V3 and with maturity, and a partner-keyed model invites exactly the unexamined-disparity problem discussed in section 6. Prefer adding the features that make Partner Z's cases describable (distance, device age, rurality) over partitioning by partner. Note also that variant is only partly known at prediction time: at diagnosis the eventual path is not yet certain, so a "variant-specific model" in production means routing on a predicted or diagnosis-declared parts requirement, and that router's own error rate must be measured.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

Layered monitors, each with an owner, a cadence, and a comparison basis. The governing principle is that no monitor computed on immature or unvalidated data may trigger a model change; it may only trigger an investigation.

1. Data-quality and schema monitor (daily; upstream of everything). Unmapped/unknown activity share, missing and imputed rate per feature, duplicate-event rate by source, event-volume by source, and a schema-hash check on incoming activity vocabularies. Alert thresholds well below April's levels (for example unknown-activity share above 1%, or any previously unseen activity label at all). Blocking behaviour: while this monitor is red, all downstream predictive alerts are marked "unreliable" and no retraining or recalibration job may start.
2. Label-maturity monitor (weekly). Per start-month cohort: percent labels resolved, delay distribution and its median and 90th percentile, and maturity stratified by variant and source. Publishes, for each month, a maturity flag (mature / provisional / unusable) that every other monitor must consume. Also monitors the outcome API's own backlog depth and age as a leading indicator, so that an October-style event is detected on the pipeline side before it distorts the metrics.
3. Predictive-performance monitor (weekly, fixed-vintage). AUROC, Brier, log loss, computed only on cohorts flagged mature, and always reported both pooled and mix-standardized to a reference mix, plus per-variant and per-source. Historical values are recomputed at matched vintage so comparisons are like-for-like.
4. Calibration monitor (weekly). Reliability curves, ECE, and observed-over-expected ratio overall and per variant and per source. Separately tracks the mean-prediction-versus-base-rate gap and its sign, since the sign distinguishes an April-type input failure (over-prediction with a stable base rate) from an August-type real deterioration (under-prediction with a rising base rate) from an October-type censoring artifact (over-prediction with a falling base rate while process indicators worsen).
5. Process-variant monitor (weekly). Variant shares, new/disappeared variants, unmapped-variant share, and the share of cases whose realized path differs from the path predicted at diagnosis. Distinguishes control-flow drift (June) from performance drift.
6. Performance-DFG monitor (daily for key edges). Median and 90th percentile duration per DFG edge, with named guardrails on `Order Part -> Part Received`, `Part Received -> Dispatch Technician` and diagnosis-to-`Remote Fix`, broken out by supplier and part category. This is the earliest true signal available; it fired in July, one month before the pooled metrics moved and two months before the population shifted, and it requires no labels at all, so it is immune to maturity problems.
7. Population monitor (weekly). Source shares, per-source variant mix, PSI/KL or propensity-based overlap statistics per feature against the training reference, share of cases outside the training support, and an explicit "new source onboarded" event feed tied to commercial systems so that Partner-Z-type changes arrive as an annotation rather than a surprise.
8. Intervention and deployment monitor (event-driven). A single timeline that annotates all metric charts with model deployments, pipeline patches, threshold changes, routing-policy changes, SLA/promise-logic changes, supplier events and partner onboardings. April, June, September and October each become an annotated boundary rather than an unexplained inflection, and any metric change within a few days of an annotation is auto-labelled "candidate cause known."
9. Feedback-loop monitor. Because the prediction drives dispatch and prioritization, track whether high-risk-flagged cases receive different treatment and whether that treatment changes their outcome. Otherwise a successful intervention looks like model miscalibration, and recalibrating against it unwinds the intervention.

Alert dependency logic (so that a label-delay alarm never triggers retraining)
- Gate order: data-quality monitor, then label-maturity monitor, then everything else. Both gates are hard.
- If data quality is red, predictive/calibration alerts are suppressed and converted into a single "pipeline incident" ticket. No calibration or retraining job may run against affected partitions. This is April.
- If label maturity for the affected months is below threshold, predictive/calibration alerts are downgraded to informational and annotated "maturity-limited"; the only permitted automated action is to re-evaluate at a later vintage. This is October.
- A performance-DFG or variant-share alert alone raises a process-change ticket. It triggers mix-standardized re-reporting and a prevalence check, not retraining. This is June.
- Retraining is proposed only when: data quality is green, the implicated months are mature, per-variant calibration drift persists on maturity-matched data across at least two consecutive months, and either a within-path outcome change (test 3.5) or a support/overlap failure (test 3.6) is confirmed. This is July-August.
- Escalation to human review is mandatory whenever two or more monitors fire in the same window, because that is the overlapping-cause case where automation reliably picks the wrong single label.

```python
"""Monthly process-evidence extraction for the field-service breach model audit.

Produces auditable per-month summaries only. Deliberately fits no model and
uses no "drift detector" -- PM4Py has no such primitive, and the comparison
across months is left to the reviewer, who can see the maturity flags.
"""

import pandas as pd
import pm4py

RAW_PATH = "field_service_events.csv"

# The 1 April 2026 migration renamed the anchor activity; the pipeline that
# broke in April recognized only the left-hand side of this mapping.
ACTIVITY_NORMALIZATION = {
    "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed",
    "Initial Diagnosis Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed",
}
ANCHOR = "Initial Diagnosis Completed"
UNKNOWN = "UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY"


def load_and_normalize(path=RAW_PATH):
    df = pd.read_csv(path)
    df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["timestamp"], utc=True, errors="coerce")

    # Normalize BEFORE any feature extraction, so prefixes anchor on the same
    # semantic event on both sides of the migration.
    df["activity_raw"] = df["activity"]
    df["activity"] = df["activity_raw"].replace(ACTIVITY_NORMALIZATION)

    # Unmapped labels are quarantined explicitly, never silently dropped: the
    # per-month count is itself a headline audit number.
    quality = {
        "rows_raw": len(df),
        "rows_bad_timestamp": int(df["time:timestamp"].isna().sum()),
        "rows_unknown_activity": int((df["activity"] == UNKNOWN).sum()),
        "unmapped_labels": sorted(
            set(df.loc[df["activity"] == UNKNOWN, "activity_raw"].unique())
        ),
    }
    df = df.dropna(subset=["time:timestamp"])

    # Duplicate handling, applied uniformly to BOTH sources and to the WHOLE
    # window (the original pipeline applied it only to the new source after
    # 20 April, which is why April/May remain suspect). Strategy: exact
    # (case, activity, timestamp, source) duplicates are removed; near
    # duplicates within a small window are counted and kept, because collapsing
    # them can hide genuine rework loops.
    key = ["case_id", "activity", "time:timestamp", "source_system"]
    quality["exact_duplicates_removed"] = int(df.duplicated(subset=key).sum())
    df = df.drop_duplicates(subset=key)

    df = df.sort_values(["case_id", "time:timestamp"])
    gap = df.groupby(["case_id", "activity"])["time:timestamp"].diff()
    df["near_duplicate_flag"] = gap.notna() & (gap < pd.Timedelta("60s"))
    quality["near_duplicates_flagged"] = int(df["near_duplicate_flag"].sum())

    log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case_id",
        activity_key="activity",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    return log, quality


def month_bounds(period):
    start = period.to_timestamp(how="start").tz_localize("UTC")
    end = period.to_timestamp(how="end").tz_localize("UTC")
    return start, end


def summarize_cohort(cohort, label):
    """DFG, performance DFG and variant evidence for one monthly cohort."""
    if cohort.empty:
        return {"cohort": label, "cases": 0}

    dfg, start_acts, end_acts = pm4py.discover_dfg(cohort)
    perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(cohort)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(cohort)

    def edge_perf(a, b):
        stats = perf_dfg.get((a, b))
        if stats is None:
            return None
        # discover_performance_dfg returns aggregated stats per edge.
        return stats.get("mean") if isinstance(stats, dict) else stats

    variant_sizes = {
        v: (len(c) if hasattr(c, "__len__") else int(c)) for v, c in variants.items()
    }
    total_cases = sum(variant_sizes.values()) or 1
    top = sorted(variant_sizes.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:5]

    return {
        "cohort": label,
        "cases": total_cases,
        "events": len(cohort),
        "distinct_variants": len(variant_sizes),
        "top_variant_shares": [
            (" -> ".join(v) if isinstance(v, tuple) else v, round(n / total_cases, 4))
            for v, n in top
        ],
        "share_touching_anchor": round(
            cohort.loc[cohort["concept:name"] == ANCHOR, "case:concept:name"].nunique()
            / total_cases,
            4,
        ),
        "share_unknown_activity": round(
            (cohort["concept:name"] == UNKNOWN).mean(), 4
        ),
        # Named edges: the parts edge is the leading indicator; the remote-fix
        # edge is the control that stayed flat all year.
        "perf_order_to_part_received": edge_perf("Order Part", "Part Received"),
        "perf_diagnosis_to_remote_fix": edge_perf(ANCHOR, "Remote Fix"),
        "perf_part_received_to_dispatch": edge_perf("Part Received", "Dispatch Technician"),
        "dfg_edge_count": len(dfg),
        "start_activities": dict(start_acts),
        "end_activities": dict(end_acts),
    }


def monthly_evidence(log, quality, as_of=None):
    """Two cohort definitions, which answer different questions.

    event-calendar cohort: every event that occurred in month M, regardless of
      when its case started. Reflects what the operation and the pipeline were
      doing during M, so it is the right lens for schema incidents (April) and
      for edge-performance monitoring (the parts edge).

    case-start cohort: all events of cases that STARTED in month M, however
      long they ran. This is the prediction cohort -- it matches how the model
      is scored and how labels mature -- so it is the only correct lens for
      breach rates and label maturity, and it is systematically incomplete for
      recent months.
    """
    as_of = pd.Timestamp(as_of, tz="UTC") if as_of else log["time:timestamp"].max()

    case_start = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].transform("min")
    log = log.assign(case_start=case_start)

    rows = []
    for period in pd.period_range(
        log["time:timestamp"].min(), log["time:timestamp"].max(), freq="M"
    ):
        lo, hi = month_bounds(period)

        # Event-calendar view via PM4Py's own time filter (events mode keeps
        # only events inside the window). PM4Py expects naive "%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S".
        lo_s = lo.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S")
        hi_s = hi.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S")
        ev_cohort = pm4py.filter_time_range(log, lo_s, hi_s, mode="events")
        ev = summarize_cohort(ev_cohort, f"{period}-event-calendar")

        # Case-start view: select on the case's first timestamp, keep all events.
        cs_cohort = log[(log["case_start"] >= lo) & (log["case_start"] <= hi)]
        cs = summarize_cohort(cs_cohort, f"{period}-case-start")

        # Label maturity for the case-start cohort. Immature months must never
        # be compared with mature ones on breach rate.
        if "outcome_label" in cs_cohort.columns:
            per_case = cs_cohort.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
                label=("outcome_label", "last"),
                label_time=("label_timestamp", "last")
                if "label_timestamp" in cs_cohort.columns
                else ("time:timestamp", "max"),
                started=("case_start", "min"),
            )
            matured = per_case["label"].notna()
            cs["label_maturity"] = round(float(matured.mean()), 4)
            cs["observed_breach_rate_on_matured"] = (
                round(float((per_case.loc[matured, "label"] == 1).mean()), 4)
                if matured.any()
                else None
            )
            delay = (
                pd.to_datetime(per_case.loc[matured, "label_time"], utc=True)
                - per_case.loc[matured, "started"]
            )
            cs["median_label_delay_days"] = (
                round(float(delay.dt.total_seconds().median() / 86400), 1)
                if matured.any()
                else None
            )
            cs["metrics_usable"] = bool(cs["label_maturity"] >= 0.95)
        rows.append(ev)
        rows.append(cs)

    monthly = pd.DataFrame(rows)

    # Case-level temporal features, kept separate from the monthly rollup so
    # that lineage is inspectable rather than baked into a model input.
    temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)

    return {
        "reporting_vintage": str(as_of),
        "data_quality": quality,
        "monthly": monthly,
        "temporal_features": temporal,
    }


if __name__ == "__main__":
    log, quality = load_and_normalize()
    evidence = monthly_evidence(log, quality)
    print(evidence["data_quality"])
    print(evidence["monthly"].to_string(index=False))
```

Two notes on the code as evidence rather than as tooling. The reporting vintage is stamped on every output so that a rerun next month is comparable to this one instead of silently overwriting it, and the `metrics_usable` flag on the case-start cohort is what a downstream dashboard should gate on, so that an October-style month is structurally incapable of triggering a retraining decision.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

Failure mode 1: retraining on immature labels. Training or recalibrating on September and October as they currently stand teaches the model that parts-dependent, Partner Z, long-path cases are low risk, because those are exactly the cases whose labels have not arrived. October would contribute a 0.16 base rate against a mix-implied 0.34. The resulting model would systematically under-flag the highest-risk segment at the moment that segment matters most, and because the mistake is baked into weights rather than a calibration layer, it would persist after the backlog cleared. Mitigation: a hard maturity gate in the training-set selector; fixed-vintage label snapshots so runs are reproducible and auditable; inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting or a fixed prediction horizon for partially mature months; a released model must pass a maturity-matched holdout, and any month below the threshold is excluded by the pipeline rather than by an analyst's judgement.

Failure mode 2: calibrating around a pipeline bug. Fitting a calibration correction to April's over-prediction would install a permanent downward shift to compensate for a transient input outage. When the 6 May patch landed, that shift would turn a correctly calibrated model into a systematically under-predicting one, and the error would be invisible because the calibration layer was "validated" on April. The subtler variant is retraining on April's corrupted features, which encodes the outage's imputation artifacts as genuine signal and makes the model's behaviour depend on which upstream system a case came from. Mitigation: the data-quality gate blocks calibration and retraining on red partitions; April is quarantined until recomputation from raw restores its metrics; changes to calibration require a stated causal hypothesis that survives test 3.2; caches are invalidated on schema change rather than backfilled, and derived-feature recomputation is a required step of any mapping patch.

Failure mode 3: ignoring genuine supplier-driven concept drift by attributing everything to data quality or mix. The available narrative is seductive: April was a pipeline bug, June was an intended policy change, October is a backlog, therefore nothing is really wrong. That reading would let a doubling of V3 breach rates, in a segment that grew from 20% to 41% of volume, run unaddressed while the parts edge climbed from 42 h to 155 h. Operationally that means thousands of cases given promise dates and priorities computed against a fulfilment reality that no longer exists, and no escalation to procurement. Mitigation: label-free process monitoring on named DFG edges with its own alerting path, so a supplier problem raises an alarm without waiting for labels; per-variant metric decomposition so a segment-localized failure cannot hide inside a pooled average; a standing rule that when a segment's observed-over-expected ratio exceeds a threshold on mature data, containment (the V3 override and revised promise dates) is applied immediately even while attribution is unresolved; and escalation of the finding to the supplier and SLA owners, since the highest-leverage fix here is not in the model.

Failure mode 4: attributing Partner Z's population shift to unfair model behaviour without stratified evidence. Partner Z shows a 0.45 observed breach rate against 0.27 elsewhere, and it would be easy to conclude either that the model discriminates against Partner Z or that Partner Z performs badly. Both conclusions are currently unsupported: 58% of Partner Z's cases run through V3, whose risk roughly doubled for reasons that have nothing to do with Partner Z (V3's breach rate had already reached 0.64 in August, when Partner Z's share was zero), and Partner Z's labels mature more slowly, which inflates its observed rate relative to a faster-maturing comparison group. Acting on the raw contrast could mean penalizing a partner for a supplier's failure, or conversely launching a fairness remediation for a gap that is entirely composition and censoring. Mitigation: no source-level claim without variant-matched and maturity-matched comparison plus reweighting (test 3.6); report Partner Z metrics with confidence intervals and an explicit maturity caveat; add the features that actually describe Partner Z's cases (travel distance, device age, rurality) so that the difference becomes modelled rather than attributed; and audit any use of partner identity as a feature for proxy effects before it ships.

Failure mode 5: treating pooled AUROC as a model-quality signal under a changing mix. AUROC and the pooled base rate both respond to composition even when the scoring function and every conditional distribution are fixed. June's dip from 0.81 to 0.79 alongside unchanged per-variant outcomes is the clean example. A team that reads pooled AUROC as model health will retrain in June against a business-intended routing change, burning cycles and adding deployment risk for no gain, and will then miss July, where the pooled numbers look almost fine but a segment has moved eleven points. Mitigation: always publish mix-standardized alongside pooled metrics; make per-variant and per-source decomposition the default view rather than a drill-down; require that a retraining proposal name the segment and the mechanism, not just a pooled delta.

Failure mode 6: collapsing overlapping causes into one label and applying one remedy. April, June, August-September and October have different mechanisms, different directions of error, and mutually incompatible remedies; a single "the model has drifted, retrain on recent data" response would combine April's corrupted features, September's censored labels and October's biased subsample into one training set, apply a pooled calibration correction whose sign is set by October rather than by August, and leave the missing lead-time feature unbuilt. The model would come out worse than the one it replaced while every dashboard reported that action had been taken. Mitigation: mandatory human review whenever two or more monitors fire in the same window; the intervention/deployment timeline annotated onto every chart so each inflection is matched against a known event before a cause is assigned; a written diagnosis with named falsifiers before any model change; and the staged remedy of section 4, in which containment and feature repair precede retraining rather than following it.
