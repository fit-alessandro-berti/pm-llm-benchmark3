# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity:** Approval ranges from 78% in Group A to 58% in Group D. These raw rates describe unequal outcomes, but different risk, documentation, and channel mixes prevent a discrimination finding from them alone.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** The preliminary model estimates approval differences from Group A of −5.5 percentage points for B, −2.2 for C, and −3.1 for D. These residuals merit investigation; without uncertainty intervals, model validation, and an account of potentially biased controls, they establish neither causation nor unlawful treatment.
- **Procedural burden:** Document requests, reviews, appeals, repeated contacts, and handovers impose effort on applicants. B's adjusted probability of a missing-document request is 9.5 points higher and C's is 4.2 points higher. A required support or risk review may be justified, while avoidable repetition and transfers are not.
- **Timeliness:** B and C have adjusted cycle-time differences of +2.6 and +4.1 days. Time spent providing necessary support should be distinguished from time waiting for scarce staff. Examine the median and upper tail, including time to first decision separately from time after an appeal.
- **Consistency of routing:** Applicants with comparable needs and policy triggers should encounter comparable rules across regions, channels, and workers. The adjusted Supervisor Review differences (+8.0 points for B and +5.5 for C) call for a check against documented review criteria.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** Timely language assistance and accommodation, understandable requests and decisions, and respectful contact matter even if final approval rates match. The log records support activities, not whether assistance was effective or accessible.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Raw appeals are 13% for B and 16% for D versus 6% for A; adjusted appeal differences are +4.4 and +5.7 points. Raw rework is also higher for B, C, and D. Appeals can indicate poor initial explanations or decisions, but can also indicate better access to remedies; measure appeal reasons, reversals, and effort before interpreting their rates.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Assigning expertise by need is appropriate, but concentrating B and C cases in small, overloaded specialist pools can produce systematically worse access. Compare workload, staffing, queue times, and handovers for comparable cases, not just worker counts.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Define the unit as an application and fix a common start, first-decision, notification, activation, appeal, and closure clock. Check missing values, event order, duplicate/reopened cases, policy changes, and whether the groups overlap or encode applicant needs. Report absolute differences with confidence intervals and sample sizes, alongside distributions and upper-tail waits.

First estimate *total* group differences, then compare applicants within overlapping strata of region, application month, channel, household size, prior-case count, and pre-routing indicators of risk and documentation. Use exact or coarsened matching where practical, or weighting with overlap diagnostics; do not extrapolate to strata represented by only one group. Compare cases with the same **actual** language preference and accommodation need when assessing whether required support is delivered equally. Risk score and documentation completeness may reflect earlier disadvantage or interactions with this process, so show results both including and excluding them, and distinguish values recorded at submission from later values. Do not control for support steps, document requests, supervisor review, or worker assignment in the main total-disparity estimate: these may be mechanisms of the disparity.

Next compare variant frequencies and transition probabilities conditional on legitimate triggers, and compare waits *within* a variant or the same stage and need. Decompose the group cycle-time gap into differences in path mix, time spent at each stage, queues, handovers, and post-decision appeals. This is descriptive unless stronger assumptions justify causal attribution. Compare assignment to workers, specialist pools, and regions conditional on skills and case mix; examine caseload and queue exposure, including whether similar cases routed to different workers have different waits. Check B and C within each region and online/in-person channel, and look for concentrated problems in the two regions with extra handovers. Examine D within risk and prior-case strata, including the risk threshold around Fraud Review.

Run sensitivity analyses using alternative risk and documentation definitions, broader and narrower matching, missing-data bounds, and models with interactions for group × region/channel/support need. Audit calibration of the risk score by group and test whether documentation status is affected by language access. Report how conclusions change when these variables are treated as legitimate complexity controls versus possible products of prior or current inequity. A persistent unexplained difference is evidence of a disparity requiring investigation, not by itself proof of discriminatory intent or mechanism; disappearance after adjustment is not proof of fairness.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Group A uses the short V1 path in 42% of cases versus 19% for B, 22% for C, and 18% for D. V1's median is 5.8 days, while V2 with a document request is 14.9 days. B uses V2 more (34% versus A's 24%), consistent in part with its higher incomplete-documentation share (46% versus 21%). Yet B's adjusted excess of document requests is 9.5 points, and its Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents median wait is 1.6 days versus A's 0.4. The documentation measure and request criteria need audit; the extra queue time is not explained merely by a legitimate need to request documents.

B uses language-support V4 in 24% of cases versus A's 1%; its median duration is 19.2 days. Language assistance is required when needed. The 3.8-day median from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, seven bilingual workers, overloaded regional officers, and extra handovers in two regions suggest a capacity and coordination problem. V4's length cannot be interpreted as the causal cost of language support because its cases differ from V1 and may contain other delays.

C uses accommodation V5 in 31% of cases versus A's 2%; its median is 21.6 days. The 4.7-day median from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment, five specialists, and specialists doubling as supervisors indicate a bottleneck. Accommodation Review itself is required for requests. The additional queue and handovers are concerning, particularly with C's adjusted +4.1 days and +5.5-point Supervisor Review probability. Determine whether the supervisor step follows a consistent rule or is an avoidable duplicate.

D uses fraud/supervisor V3 in 28% of cases versus A's 9%, plausibly reflecting its 51% high-risk share versus A's 18%. Risk-based review can be legitimate if the score and thresholds are valid and applied consistently. D also uses appeal V6 in 13% versus A's 4%, with a +5.7-point adjusted appeal difference; this warrants examination of decisions and explanations even though the adjusted cycle-time difference is only +0.9 days. B's +8.0-point adjusted Supervisor Review rate and B/C's longer Supervisor Review → Decision Draft waits (1.9/1.6 versus 1.0 days for A) also warrant within-region, within-trigger checks. These path medians cannot simply be added or compared as treatment effects, and the listed variants cover only part of the log.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity:** Higher verified risk triggers Fraud Review; incomplete submissions trigger document requests; requested language or disability support triggers specialist work. These can change paths and active processing time without unfair treatment.
2. **Scarce specialist capacity:** Small bilingual and accommodation teams, plus specialists serving as supervisors, create longer queues for applicants exercising support rights.
3. **Handover friction:** Generalist → specialist → generalist/supervisor transfers can add waiting, lost context, repeated document requests, or rework, especially in the two identified regions.
4. **Uneven rule application:** Workers or regions may request documents or supervisor review more often for comparable cases, producing both burden and delayed decisions.
5. **Biased inputs or communication:** Historical bias in risk scores, language-dependent definitions of “complete,” inaccessible requests, or unclear decision letters can increase review, apparent incompleteness, appeals, and possibly adverse outcomes.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Risk bands may inherit historical enforcement or decision disparities and may be poorly calibrated across groups. Documentation completeness may be measured after a confusing or inaccessible request, making it partly an effect of the process rather than a neutral starting condition. Broad bands omit severity of need, document type, changing eligibility facts, and the content and timing of support requests. Group labels need validation and may hide intersecting needs within each group.

The log does not measure language proficiency, interpreter quality, accommodation adequacy, accessibility of communications, applicant effort and costs, explanation quality, or dignity of treatment. `appeal_flag` omits grounds, access barriers, duration, outcome, reversal, and whether an applicant was discouraged from appealing. A single event timestamp cannot reliably separate active service from queue time without start/completion or lifecycle data. Daily workload snapshots miss within-day queues, staffing absences, case difficulty assigned to each worker, and informal work. Missing, misordered, or unlogged events and variation in closure/appeal recording may distort variants and cycle times. The preliminary adjusted differences have no reported uncertainty, overlap assessment, or causal identification.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Option | Intended effect | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add bilingual and accommodation capacity, cross-train staff, and cover absences | Reduce specialist queues without withholding required support | Cost, training time, and quality risk if expertise is diluted; avoid shifting backlog to another stage. |
| Set and monitor service-level targets for support requests, contact, and completion | Make rights-preserving steps visible and timely | Speed targets may encourage superficial contact, premature completion, or gaming; pair with quality and accessibility measures. |
| Redesign routing with a named case owner, warm handovers, and shared case notes | Reduce transfers and repeated requests | More coordination work and possible specialist bottlenecks; preserve independent review where policy requires it. |
| Improve accessible, translated document requests and decision explanations; test comprehension | Reduce avoidable rework and help applicants understand decisions and appeal rights | Translation and review require resources; better appeal access may initially increase recorded appeals. |
| Monitor adjusted path, wait, outcome, appeal, and rework gaps by need, region, channel, and worker pool | Detect persistent or localized disparities and evaluate changes | Small cells create noisy estimates and privacy concerns; metrics can be gamed or overinterpreted. |
| Audit risk bands and documentation criteria for provenance, calibration, thresholds, and group-specific error | Find biased inputs and inconsistent requests | Revising scores or criteria can change fraud workload and error rates; use independent policy and accuracy review. |

Evaluate changes with before/after trends and, where feasible, staged rollout or comparable control regions. Track support quality and decision accuracy alongside speed; retain mandatory support steps.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**Procedural fairness is not established; the evidence raises specific, credible concerns, while a definitive discrimination finding is indeterminate.** Legitimate risk and support needs explain some path differences, but adjusted excess reviews and document requests, long specialist queues, and concentrated handovers suggest avoidable burdens for B and C. D's risk-driven routing may be justified, yet its adjusted appeal gap and the uncertain neutrality of risk scores require audit. The priority is to test comparable cases and policy triggers, measure support quality and queue causes, and remove avoidable delays without removing applicants' rights-preserving steps.
