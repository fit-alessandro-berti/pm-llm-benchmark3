# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness is not equivalent to equal approval rates. It asks whether cases with comparable needs receive comparable treatment, whether differences in treatment are necessary and proportionate, whether support is accessible and timely, and whether applicants can understand and challenge decisions. Equal treatment is not necessarily fair when needs differ, but a legitimate routing rule does not automatically make the resulting delays or handovers fair.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should distinguish four concepts:

- **Disparity:** a measurable difference between groups.
- **Discrimination:** an unjustified difference in treatment that cannot be explained by legitimate needs or consistent, proportionate rules.
- **Legitimate routing:** a rule that is necessary, consistently triggered by the same need, proportionate in burden, and implemented with reasonable timeliness and quality.
- **Uncertainty:** gaps that remain because of limited overlap, measurement error, sparse data, or unobserved confounding.

A stated policy is only the starting point. The actual implementation of that policy must also be audited.

## A. Specify estimands before choosing controls

At least three comparisons are useful:

1. **Observed group disparity:** raw differences, including the effects of different routes and support burdens.
2. **Need-adjusted disparity:** differences among cases comparable on objective, pre-decision complexity.
3. **Direct procedural disparity:** differences among comparable cases receiving the same legitimate route, such as cases with the same language-support need and the same risk and documentation profile.

Support receipt, Request Missing Documents, Supervisor Review, and handover count may be mediators of the group-to-outcome relationship. Including them in the primary model would estimate a narrower “direct” effect and could conceal the very procedural burden being assessed. They should be included in a separate direct-effect model and in mediation/decomposition analysis.

## B. Construct comparable cohorts

Compare each non-A group with A using:

- `risk_score_band`, subject to the audit described below;
- `documentation_completeness`, initially as recorded, with audited and alternative definitions in sensitivity analyses;
- `household_size_band`;
- `prior_case_count`, recognizing that this may partly reflect prior institutional burden;
- `channel`, analyzed both overall and within online/in-person strata;
- `region`;
- calendar month or quarter;
- language-support need;
- accommodation need and, if available, accommodation type;
- benefit type or amount, urgency, and application completeness, if they can be added.

Use exact stratification where feasible, such as risk band × documentation status × region × month, followed by matching or propensity weighting on the remaining variables. Report:

- common-support and positivity diagnostics;
- numbers matched or weighted;
- covariate balance after matching;
- results before and after trimming or weighting;
- analyses restricted to well-supported comparisons.

Do not force matches between a language-support case and a case with no language-support need. A lack of common support is itself an important finding.

The groups may also overlap—for example, a Group B applicant may request accommodation. Analyze memberships separately and examine intersections rather than assuming mutually exclusive categories.

## C. Estimate outcomes with appropriate models

Use:

- logistic models for approval, appeals, missing-document requests, and supervisor review;
- survival models or accelerated failure-time models for cycle time, retaining open cases and accounting for censoring;
- quantile regression for the 90th percentile;
- count or recurrent-event models for rework;
- multilevel models with clustering by caseworker and region where supported.

Report adjusted differences in probability and time, confidence intervals, and practical effect sizes—not only statistical significance. For approval, use marginal effects rather than relying on odds ratios alone.

## D. Compare paths and variants

For each variant:

1. Normalize its frequency by the number of cases in each group.
2. Compare cases with the same risk and documentation profile.
3. Compare groups within the same variant and need category.
4. Compare waiting and rework at each transition.
5. Test whether required routing is complete and whether exceptions are explained.
6. Use counterfactual standardization to ask: if every group experienced the same distribution of legitimate routes and within-route waiting times, what residual gap would remain?

This separates differences in route mix from differences in how routes are executed. Comparing V4 directly with V1, for example, would conflate support needs with support quality.

## E. Audit resource assignment

Measure, per 100 cases and per specialist FTE:

- caseload and skill-matched workload;
- queue length at arrival;
- median and tail waiting time;
- absence, overtime, and backlog indicators;
- handover frequency;
- whether the assigned worker has the required language or accommodation expertise;
- the number of general-to-specialist-to-general transfers;
- supervisor-review discretion by route, risk band, and region.

Resource allocation should be judged against **need**, not simply population share. A high proportion of B cases handled by bilingual workers may be appropriate; a 3.8-day support queue and repeated handovers may not be.

## F. Decompose waiting time

For each case, calculate the total elapsed time contributed by:

1. route composition, such as V2 versus V4;
2. waiting at each transition;
3. actual activity service time, where identifiable;
4. rework and appeal cycles;
5. regional and staffing queues.

Use counterfactual or Shapley-style decomposition to estimate how much of each group gap is associated with route mix versus within-route delays. Separately analyze the 90th percentile: a modest median effect can conceal severe tail delays.

The event log’s timestamp semantics must be checked. An interval between two activity events is not automatically “service time,” and it is not necessarily pure waiting unless activity start/end timestamps are available.

## G. Sensitivity analyses for potentially biased covariates

| Sensitivity | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Refit without `risk_score_band`; then refit with an independently validated score | Determine whether controlling for a historically biased score is suppressing disparity |
| Separate genuinely incomplete applications from documentation barriers caused by language or accessibility | Avoid conditioning on a barrier created by the process under review |
| Remove or separately audit `prior_case_count` | Test whether prior institutional burden is being treated as applicant complexity |
| Include language-support and accommodation receipt only in the direct-effect model | Estimate residual treatment effects without erasing the burden of rights-preserving steps |
| Analyze within channel and without channel adjustment | Separate channel access effects from channel composition |
| Leave out each region, especially the two backlogged regions | Test regional confounding |
| Exclude months affected by staffing changes or policy changes | Test time-specific confounding |
| Use alternative definitions of rework and initial versus final decision | Prevent classification choices from driving conclusions |
| Bound plausible unmeasured confounding | Assess how strong an unmeasured factor would need to be to explain a residual gap |

## H. Region, channel, and intersectional checks

Estimate group-by-region, group-by-channel, and group-by-need interactions. Within each region and channel, repeat matched comparisons. Do not rely on aggregate results that could conceal Simpson’s paradox, particularly in the two backlogged regions.

Examine at least:

- B cases with and without language-support need;
- C cases with and without accommodation requests;
- D cases at different risk bands and prior-case levels;
- online versus in-person cases;
- cases with and without missing-document requests;
- cases with and without supervisor review.

These checks should distinguish a support-related delay from a general regional backlog.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## A. Most important path differences

| Path | Observed pattern | Likely legitimate basis | Remaining fairness concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V1: straightforward route** | A 42%, B 19%, C 22%, D 18%; median cycle 5.8 days. | A has less documentation, language-support, and accommodation demand. | Non-A groups have fewer cases reaching the simplest path. It should be established whether this is entirely due to need rather than differential early exit or case capture. |
| **V2: Request Missing Documents** | A 24%, B 34%, C 29%, D 26%; median 14.9 days. | Incomplete documentation is 46% for B, 36% for C, 33% for D, versus 21% for A. | B waits 1.6 days at Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents versus 0.4 for A. If incompleteness reflects language barriers rather than applicant neglect, the burden is not legitimate applicant complexity. |
| **V3: Fraud Review and Supervisor Review** | A 9%, B 11%, C 8%, D 28%; median 18.4 days. | D has 51% high-risk cases versus 18% for A, so more fraud routing is expected. | Fraud routing remains legitimate only if the risk measure is valid and consistently applied. D’s +5.7 pp adjusted appeal difference warrants review of the quality and calibration of that routing. |
| **V4: Language Support Contact** | A 1%, B 24%, C 3%, D 2%; median 19.2 days. | B has 57% language-support need. The right-preserving step should be retained. | B’s 3.8-day post-contact wait and extra general  bilingual  general handovers are substantial. The concern is not that support exists, but that it is slow and administratively fragmented. |
| **V5: Accommodation Review** | A 2%, B 3%, C 31%, D 3%; median 21.6 days. | C has 62% accommodation requests. | C’s 4.7-day post-review wait, five-specialist concentration, and additional supervisor handovers are concerning. The preliminary model also shows +4.1 adjusted days for C. |
| **V6: Appeal route** | A 4%, B 8%, C 5%, D 13%; median 30.7 days. | More adverse or contested decisions can legitimately generate appeals. | Higher appeal and rework burdens can indicate poor initial explanations or difficult challenges. The table does not establish initial-decision accuracy or appeal quality. |

The path table contains only top paths, so it cannot establish full path diversity, transition probabilities, or support coverage. For example, the 24% B share in V4 cannot be compared directly with B’s 57% language-support need to claim that support is missing; some language-support activity may occur in paths outside the top six.

## B. Waiting-time concentration

The strongest observed delays are associated with support and review rather than final notification:

- **Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment:** 3.8 days for B.
- **Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment:** 4.7 days for C.
- **Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents:** 0.4 days for A, 1.6 for B, 0.9 for C, and 0.7 for D.
- **Supervisor Review  Decision Draft:** 1.0 day for A, 1.9 for B, 1.6 for C, and 1.1 for D, with a stated backlog in two regions.
- **Decision Draft  Applicant Notification:** only 0.5–0.8 days across groups.

The small final-notification difference suggests that notification itself is not the principal source of the disparity. The support queues and supervisor backlog deserve priority attention.

B’s higher in-person share, 38% versus 9% for A, may partly explain its longer Submit  Identity Verification interval. That does not, by itself, explain the later language-support and supervisor delays.

## C. Resource and handover concerns

The most concerning combination is:

- B cases depend on only seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers;
- C cases depend on only five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review;
- B experiences repeated general  bilingual  general transfers in two regions;
- C experiences general  accommodation specialist  supervisor transfers.

Routing by need is legitimate. The fairness issue is whether applicants receive timely, competent support without repeated transfer, redundant checks, or unnecessary escalation. The resource data do not show FTE-adjusted workload, so they cannot establish that the allocation is either adequate or deliberately unfair.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms could coexist. The observational data do not identify which ones caused the outcome gaps.

| Mechanism | Classification | How it could produce the pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Need-based case complexity** | Legitimate and supported | Higher risk drives Fraud Review for D; incomplete files drive V2 for B and C; language and accommodation needs drive V4 and V5. This explains part of the raw approval and cycle-time gaps. |
| **Channel-specific process design** | Potentially legitimate but design-dependent | In-person applicants wait longer at identity verification. B’s 38% in-person share therefore contributes to its overall delay. The concern is whether the in-person path is less efficient or more burdensome without offering compensating accessibility or accuracy benefits. |
| **Scarce specialist capacity** | Legitimate objective, potentially unfair implementation | Seven bilingual workers and five accommodation specialists create queues for B and C. Rights-preserving support consequently imposes a practical penalty through delay. Capacity investment, not removal of support, is the relevant remedy. |
| **Handover fragmentation** | Concerning | General  bilingual  general and general  accommodation  supervisor transfers can duplicate information gathering, weaken accountability, and create rework. Some transfer is necessary, but repeated returns to a general queue may be avoidable. |
| **Documentation barriers misclassified as applicant complexity** | Concerning | Language barriers, inaccessible forms, or unfamiliar requirements may produce “incomplete” files. Requesting documents then delays the case rather than resolving the access problem. |
| **Historical bias in risk scoring and discretionary review** | Concerning | If `risk_score_band` reflects prior institutional disparities, fraud routing and supervisor review can reproduce those disparities. Extra scrutiny can then increase denials, appeals, and rework. |
| **Differential escalation for uncertainty** | Concerning but unproven | The residual higher probabilities of Supervisor Review and missing-document requests could reflect unmeasured complexity, inconsistent thresholds, or unequal discretionary treatment. The log does not contain review rationales to distinguish these. |
| **Regional workload and priority rules** | Mixed | Two regional backlogs, overloaded officers, and specialist queues can create delays unrelated to applicant need. If priority rules privilege routine cases over support-dependent cases, that is a procedural-fairness concern. |
| **Appeal and rework feedback loop** | Mixed | An unclear or disputed initial decision leads to appeal, additional processing time, and reopening. Higher appeal rates may therefore be a symptom of poor earlier process quality rather than proof of improper adjudication. |

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available attributes are insufficient for a definitive fairness determination.

## A. Group definitions and missing applicant needs

- The group labels bundle language, accommodation use, prior case history, and measured risk. They are not necessarily mutually exclusive and should not be treated as direct measures of race, ethnicity, or another protected characteristic.
- There is no direct information on benefit type or amount, application complexity beyond the listed variables, urgency, severity of need, disability type, accommodation effectiveness, literacy, digital access, or quality of the applicant’s evidence.
- Language support is recorded as a need, but not whether the required language, format, or assistance was actually supplied.
- Channel may be a choice, an institutional assignment, or a consequence of prior barriers. It is not automatically a neutral applicant characteristic.

## B. Measurement bias and historical bias

- `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities, biased enforcement patterns, or unequal access to favorable case histories. Controlling for it without auditing it can adjust away the effect of earlier unfairness.
- `documentation_completeness` may reflect language barriers, inaccessible forms, different staff thresholds, or genuine applicant omissions. The log does not distinguish these causes.
- `prior_case_count` may represent repeated legitimate needs, but it can also reflect prior rework, erroneous denials, or other institutional burden.
- The preliminary adjustment has no reported confidence intervals, balance diagnostics, specification curve, or missing-data analysis. Its adequacy cannot be verified from the summary alone.

## C. Unobserved resource workload

- Workload is observed only at daily granularity. There is no case-level worker time, FTE adjustment, queue length at arrival, absence, overtime, skill proficiency, or intraday workload.
- Seven bilingual caseworkers may be appropriately matched to 57% language-support need, or severely undercapacity; the present data cannot distinguish these possibilities.
- The log cannot show whether a specialist was assigned because of demonstrated need, availability, or an administrative default.

## D. Support quality and decision quality

- `case_outcome` does not capture explanation quality, correctness, dignity of treatment, accessibility, or whether the applicant understood the decision.
- A Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review event does not establish that assistance was timely, competent, or sufficient.
- The log does not capture whether support prevented later rework, errors, or appeals.

## E. Appeal and rework quality

- Appeal flags do not show filing assistance, whether the applicant understood the remedy, submission barriers, appeal acceptance, timeliness, success, or reasons for reversal.
- Reopen/rework is not defined in the scenario. It could reflect corrected decisions, missing documentation, system events, or another event type.
- Because Group D has more denials, part of its higher appeal rate may be mechanically related to decision volume. Conditional appeal rates require initial-decision information and careful causal handling.

## F. Log and statistical limitations

- The variant table lists only top paths and truncates V4 and V5.
- Activity timestamps do not necessarily separate queue waiting from productive service. Support-related service may also be recorded outside the main process.
- Open cases and right censoring may bias cycle-time comparisons if incomplete cases are dropped.
- There are no uncertainty intervals for raw percentiles or adjusted estimates.
- Matching and adjustment cannot remove unobserved confounding. Even a well-balanced matched cohort could still differ in unmeasured need or prior institutional treatment.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Option | Implementation | Side effects and safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| **Add language-support and accommodation-review capacity** | Recruit and train qualified bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; create regional reserve pools; cross-train general caseworkers; protect specialist time from unrelated duties. | Higher staffing costs and onboarding time. Demand growth can create new bottlenecks, and specialists may become overloaded. Track skill-matched workload and service quality, not just headcount. |
| **Introduce service-level targets for support steps** | Set separate first-contact, completion, and end-to-end timeliness targets for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review, including p90 thresholds. Allow documented extensions for individual accommodation or complexity without penalizing staff. | Targets can encourage premature closure, inadequate assistance, or reclassification of cases as completed. Pair targets with resolution quality, applicant understanding, rework, and satisfaction measures. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** | Introduce one accountable case owner, consultant sign-off without transferring case ownership, shared case summaries, and clear decision rights. Retain independent fraud review and genuine accommodation authority. | A single owner can become overloaded, and specialist input may become superficial. Measure actual consultation quality, not only handover counts. |
| **Improve decision explanations** | Provide plain-language, translated, and accessible notices stating eligibility reasons, missing requirements, evidence considered, appeal rights, and available support. Record concise reasons for missing-document requests and supervisor escalation. | Additional administrative effort and translation costs; explanations can contain errors or reveal internal decision language. Use templates, quality audits, and reason-code validation. |
| **Proactively assist with documentation** | Offer translated checklists, assisted online completion, and in-person help before classifying a file as incomplete. | Consumes capacity and may be misused to complete low-quality evidence. It should supplement, not replace, necessary verification. |
| **Monitor adjusted disparities** | Monitor approval, cycle-time distributions, missing-document requests, supervisor review, appeals, rework, and resource ratios by group and intersection, conditional on need and route. Include confidence intervals and qualitative review. | Monitoring can distort behavior, stigmatize high-risk groups, create incentives to reject cases, or cause gaming of route classification. Do not set caseworker approval targets; protect small-cell privacy. |
| **Audit `risk_score_band`** | Review features, thresholds, historical validation, calibration, and disparate error rates by language need, accommodation status, and region. Provide human review and an appeal or correction mechanism; version the score. | Recalibration may initially increase review workload and reduce some fraud detection while false positives are corrected. Preserve legally necessary controls but validate them independently. |
| **Audit `documentation_completeness`** | Distinguish missing evidence from inaccessible presentation; review inter-rater consistency; test whether the same file would be judged complete in another channel; train staff on equivalent standards. | Requires case-file evidence not present here. More consistent standards can initially increase requests for all groups, which is equitable if the previous standard was inconsistent. |
| **Address regional workload and priority rules** | Use transparent urgency and aging rules, cross-region backup, and monitor whether support-dependent cases are systematically deprioritized. | Reallocating cases affects other applicants and may require overtime or additional supervision. Measure displacement effects and staff well-being. |

None of these measures should remove or weaken language support, accommodation review, or legitimate fraud review. The objective is to make necessary support timely, coherent, and effective.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The overall assessment is **indeterminate, with a material procedural-fairness warning**.

Raw approval differences are not discrimination findings, and documented complexity explains a substantial part of the gaps—especially for Group D’s fraud routing and the missing-document routes. Nevertheless, the preliminary adjusted results and the waiting, handover, and resource patterns show that measured complexity does not fully account for differential procedural burden.

The strongest concern is not the existence of language or accommodation steps. It is that those rights-preserving steps are associated with long queues, repeated handovers, and additional review without separate timeliness targets. That can make access to a procedural right functionally burdensome.

There is not enough evidence to conclude that the process is procedurally fair, nor enough to find intentional discrimination or unjustified decisions. The approval gaps could reflect unmeasured need, biased risk or documentation measures, inconsistent discretion, or resource allocation. The missing evidence on support quality, explanation quality, appeal effectiveness, and workload prevents a stronger conclusion.

A defensible next step is to preserve all support steps while testing whether, among comparable cases receiving the same legitimate route, support-related delays and outcome differences persist after resource and workload adjustment. If they do, the evidence for unfair treatment would become considerably stronger.
