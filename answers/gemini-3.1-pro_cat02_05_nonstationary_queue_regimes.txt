# 1. Data audit and estimands
Quantities and Units:
- `arrivals`, `completed`: Daily counts of cases entering and exiting the system.
- `median_service_min`, `p90_service_min`: Active handling time per case (in minutes), explicitly excluding wait time.
- `mean_wip`: Average number of cases in the system (inventory/backlog), sampled at 15-minute intervals.
- `staff_fte`: Mean concurrent capacity (servers) available.
- `complex_pct`, `peak_08_10_pct`: Proportions indicating case mix and intraday arrival burstiness.

Opening-hours convention:
- The operating window is 12 hours (07:00–19:00). Cases carry over overnight, but no service occurs while closed, meaning WIP at 19:00 becomes the backlog at 07:00 the next day.

Limitations of the aggregates:
- Daily aggregates obscure intraday dynamics, such as morning bursts that cause transient capacity starvation.
- Medians and p90s of service time do not yield mean service time, nor do they represent mean flow time (which includes waiting).
- The 07:00 starting backlog is missing, making exact daily flow conservation ($WIP_{end} = WIP_{start} + In - Out$) difficult to reconstruct.

Five threats to naive inference:
1. **Ignoring Non-Stationarity:** Averaging across the month blends fundamentally different regimes (e.g., normal vs. outage vs. complex-mix).
2. **Conflating Service Time with Flow Time:** Using median service time as a proxy for customer wait/flow time ignores queueing delays, which clearly escalate when WIP grows.
3. **Simpson's Paradox in Case Mix:** A pooled average of `complex_pct` (~32%) suggests a homogeneous mix, whereas the reality is a bimodal split (~21% early April vs. ~44% late April).
4. **Violating Little's Law Assumptions:** Applying $L = \lambda W$ across periods where arrivals $\neq$ completions (e.g., during the outage or backlog accumulation) violates the steady-state requirement.
5. **Masking Intraday Burstiness:** Assuming uniform arrival rates across the 12-hour day ignores the massive shift in morning peaks (from ~30% to ~50%), which causes transient capacity starvation even if daily FTE seems sufficient.

# 3. Alternative segmentation
**Alternative: Queueing State Segmentation (Accumulation vs. Depletion)**
- *Stable/Emptying State:* Apr 06–14, Weekends. (Arrivals $\approx$ Completions, WIP flat).
- *Accumulation State:* Apr 15, Apr 20–28. (Arrivals > Completions, WIP growing).
- *Depletion State:* Apr 16–17, Apr 29–May 01. (Completions > Arrivals, WIP shrinking).

*Operational Story:* This segmentation focuses purely on the system's macroscopic queueing state (the derivative of the backlog) rather than the exogenous drivers (case mix, outages). It highlights periods where the system is failing to keep up versus periods where it is recovering.

*Strengths:* Directly maps to the customer experience (flow times will grow during accumulation and shrink during depletion) and simplifies the assessment of steady-state assumptions for Little's Law.
*Weaknesses:* It conflates the root causes. The accumulation on Apr 15 is a *supply shock* (outage), while the accumulation on Apr 20–28 is a *demand/mix shock* (higher complexity and burstiness). Treating them as the same regime obscures the different management interventions required.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics
1. **Weekday/Weekend Composition:**
   - *Pooled:* Mean arrivals = 91.5/day.
   - *Segmented:* Baseline Weekdays average ~102/day, while Weekends average ~45/day. The pooled mean represents a volume that almost never actually occurs on any given day, leading to severe understaffing on weekdays and overstaffing on weekends if used for capacity planning.
2. **Complex-Case Mix:**
   - *Pooled:* Mean `complex_pct` = 32.5%.
   - *Segmented:* The mix is strictly bimodal. It is ~21% before Apr 20 and ~44% after. Pooling suggests a moderate, stable complexity, completely masking the structural shift that drove p90 service times from ~47 mins to >80 mins.
3. **Staffing Intervention / Outage Recovery:**
   - *Pooled:* Mean completions = 89.6/day.
   - *Segmented:* During the outage (Apr 15), completions crashed to 58. During recovery/intervention (Apr 16, Apr 29-30) with 7.0 FTE, completions surged to 121-129/day. Pooling obscures the system's actual maximum throughput capability (capacity limit) when staffed appropriately to burn down a backlog.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity
- **Burstiness (Index of Dispersion & CV):** The hourly vectors reveal severe intraday non-stationarity. For the ordinary weekday (Apr 07), arrivals are relatively smooth. However, the post-mix-change day (Apr 21) shows extreme burstiness (34 arrivals in one hour). The Index of Dispersion (Variance/Mean) for Apr 21 is ~7.4, and the Coefficient of Variation (CV) is ~0.88, far exceeding a Poisson process (where Index of Dispersion $\approx$ 1). This aligns with the jump in `peak_08_10_pct` to >40%.
- **Autocorrelation (Lag Dependence):** The daily sequence exhibits strong lag-1 autocorrelation in `mean_wip` (approx. 0.88). High WIP on Tuesday strongly predicts high WIP on Wednesday because the backlog carries over overnight. The daily observations are not independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples.
- **Change-point evidence:** A simple first-difference heuristic on `complex_pct` reveals a massive +18% jump on Apr 20, serving as a clear structural change-point.
- **Insufficient Stochastic Model:** Four representative days cannot establish a full stochastic model because they do not capture day-of-week seasonality, the transition probabilities between states, or the variance of service times (only medians/p90s are provided).

# 7. Operational implications
- **Staffing:** The shift on Apr 20 proves that FTE requirements must be dynamically linked to case mix (`complex_pct`) and intraday burstiness (`peak_08_10_pct`), not just daily volume.
- **Backlog Recovery:** The data shows 7.0 FTE is highly effective at burning down backlog (achieving >120 completions/day). This surge capacity should be deployed immediately when WIP crosses a threshold (e.g., >30), rather than waiting 9 days (Apr 20 to Apr 29).
- **Peak Smoothing:** The extreme morning burstiness (e.g., Apr 21) requires staggered shift starts. Flat daily FTE scheduling will result in morning capacity starvation and afternoon idle time.
- **Data-Collection:** To move beyond approximations, the operation must log the 07:00 starting backlog daily, capture true mean flow times (not just active service medians), and record event-level timestamps.
- **Risky Policy Example:** A policy that sets a flat staffing level based on the *pooled average service time* (~30 mins) and *pooled average arrivals* (~92/day) would look highly efficient on paper. However, under a segmented view, this policy is disastrous: it would slightly overstaff the early April baseline, but massively understaff the late April high-complexity regime, leading to an unrecoverable backlog spiral.
