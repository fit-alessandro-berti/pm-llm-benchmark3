## 1. Analytical assumptions

- **Case notion**: The object type selected as the trace identifier during flattening. It defines the unit of analysis and determines which events are copied into each trace. A case notion is a methodological choice, not an ontological truth; it optimizes for specific analytical lenses at the cost of others.
- **Convergence**: The merging of distinct business entities into a single flattened trace due to shared events or explicit O2O relations. Convergence is valuable for spotting coordination points (e.g., D2 consolidating P2/P3) but distorts independent cycle times and creates phantom sequential dependencies in DFGs.
- **Divergence**: The fragmentation of a single business entity across multiple traces or the truncation of its lifecycle when the chosen object type participates in few event types. Divergence is inherent in object-centric data but becomes problematic when it severs logical dependencies (e.g., items never seeing invoicing events).
- **Treatment of copied shared events**: I treat them as **both evidence and duplication**. They are evidence of cross-cutting coordination (e.g., e16 linking O1, O2, O3 via D2), but they are duplication in the flattened log because the same timestamped activity appears in multiple traces without preserving the original event-object bipartite structure. This duplication inflates event frequencies, creates artificial self-loops or parallel branches in DFGs, and masks true concurrency.
- **Analytical transformations permitted**: I permit explicit relation propagation (e.g., enriching `Add Item` events with the `contains` link so OI is visible) only when clearly documented, as it reconstructs logical dependencies that E2O alone hides. I do not permit silent propagation through the O2O graph, as it introduces phantom paths (e.g., inferring O1O2 from shared P3 without direct event linkage). All transformations will be flagged as post-hoc reconstructions, not native log properties.

   *Impact*: Harmful for bottleneck identification; inflates dispatch counts. Context-dependent: Useful for spotting high-volume consolidation events.

8. **Delivery flattening / Cross-order consolidation D2**  
   *Affected flattening*: Delivery  
   *Events/Objects*: D2 carries P2 (O1, O2) and P3 (O2, O3)  
   *Distortion*: Convergence & lifecycle masking. D2 trace merges O1, O2, O3 events. DFG shows a single delivery lifecycle (e15–e24), but actual order-to-delivery times vary significantly.  
   *Impact*: Harmful for delivery SLA analysis; informative for route optimization and load balancing.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

- **Flattened logs to create**:  
  1. `Order` flattening (primary) for end-to-end business process analysis.  
  2. `Invoice` flattening (secondary) for financial settlement and cash-flow analysis.  
  3. `Package` flattening (tertiary, filtered) for warehouse/logistics throughput.

- **Safe questions for flattened logs**:  
  - *Order*: “What is the median time from order creation to closure?” “How frequently are orders split (O1P1/P2) or consolidated (O2/O3V2)?” “What is the distribution of dispatch-to-confirmation latency?”  
  - *Invoice*: “What is the settlement cycle time for consolidated invoices?” “How often are invoices settled in multiple instalments?”

- **Questions requiring object-centric views**:  
  - “How do items move across orders during consolidation?”  Requires **OC-DFG** to preserve event-object bipartite structure and avoid phantom DFG edges.  
  - “What is the true dependency between packing and invoicing when orders are merged?”  Requires **ET–OT graph** to map which events relate to which objects without copying.  
  - “How do partial payments correlate with delivery confirmation across consolidated orders?”  Requires **Object Type Graph (OTG)** to trace V2M2/M3O2/O3 dependencies without flattening-induced sequence bias.  
  - “Which objects drive bottlenecks in cross-order consolidation?”  Requires **object interaction summary** to quantify cross-cutting event frequency and identify coordination hubs.

- **Reconciling contradictory findings**:  
  When flattened logs show artificial DFG edges (e.g., O2O3 via shared e16), cross-reference with the OTG to verify if a direct E2O link exists or if the edge is purely an artifact of event copying. If frequency metrics diverge (e.g., Order trace shows 2-day cycle, Invoice trace shows 0.5-day), attribute the gap to physical/logistical handling using the ET–OT graph. Use object-centric clustering to group equivalent OCEL fragments before flattening, ensuring that copied events are weighted by their actual coordination role rather than treated as independent process steps.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**Decision**: B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.

- **Decision criteria**: Business centrality (does the object capture the core workflow?), lifecycle completeness (does it span creation to closure?), event coverage (does it participate in the majority of event types?), and distortion tolerance (can copying/fragmentation be mitigated?).
- **Strongest supporting evidence**: Order (O1–O4) satisfies all criteria. It spans e01–e31, captures split packaging (P1/P2), cross-order consolidation (P3/P4, D2, V2), and partial payments (M2/M3). It aligns with standard process mining objectives (cycle time, compliance, bottleneck identification) and allows secondary lifecycles (Invoice, Package) to handle finance and logistics without distorting the primary business narrative.
- **Strongest counterargument**: Order flattening suffers from heavy event copying (e13–e17, e19–e20, e22, e25–e27), creating artificial DFG edges, inflating frequencies, and masking independent processing times. A pure object-centric view (OC-DFG, OTG) avoids these distortions entirely and preserves the true bipartite structure.
- **Residual risk**: Over-reliance on Order flattening may obscure item-level bottlenecks, misattribute delivery delays to specific orders, and inflate coordination costs in performance metrics. Secondary lifecycles may still suffer from truncation (e.g., Package missing financial steps).
- **Conditions to change decision**: I would shift to a pure object-centric approach if: (1) the analysis goal prioritizes inventory turnover or cash-flow optimization over order fulfilment; (2) advanced analytics (graph neural networks, event sequence alignment) are feasible and justified by compute budget; or (3) the process scales to thousands of orders where event copying becomes computationally prohibitive.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Removing/down-weighting copied shared events**: Filter events where the lead object type appears in multiple related objects (e.g., `len([o for o in e['related_objects'] if o['type']=='Order']) > 1`). Compare DFG edges, trace lengths, and performance metrics before/after filtering. High divergence indicates heavy copying; low divergence suggests natural concurrency. Down-weighting can be implemented via event frequency scaling in PM4Py’s DFG parameters.
2. **Comparing direct E2O relations with propagated-relation variant**: Build a custom flattening where `contains`/`bills`/`settles` links are explicitly added to events (e.g., `Add Item` gets O and I; `Issue Invoice` gets O and V). Compare trace completeness and DFG edges. If propagated relations reveal missing steps (e.g., OV via bills), the original E2O flattening is truncating logical dependencies.
3. **Changing the lead object**: Run flattening on Invoice and Package. Compare lifecycle distributions, bottleneck locations, and event frequencies. If Order shows a 2-day cycle but Invoice shows 0.5-day, attribute the gap to physical/logistical handling using the ET–OT graph. If Package shows uniform dispatch times but Order shows variance, the distortion is due to consolidation masking.
4. **Comparing frequency metrics across granularities**: Calculate event frequency per trace, per unique object, and total objects in the OC-DFG. High trace frequency vs low unique-object frequency indicates heavy copying; high unique-object vs low trace frequency indicates fragmentation. Cross-validate with OTG edge weights to confirm whether high-frequency events are genuine coordination hubs or artefacts of flattening.
