## 1. What is observed

Automation availability began in Alpha and Beta in April 2026 and in Gamma in July; Delta and Epsilon remained unavailable through August. Availability is not equivalent to case-level treatment: the automated-fast-path share rose gradually from roughly 25–27% in first rollout months to 48–50% in Alpha/Beta and 32% in Gamma, while a residual 4–6% appears even before availability, likely reflecting coding, legacy tooling, or a definition issue requiring validation.

Rollout was targeted rather than randomized. Alpha and Beta had high volume and historically poor cycle times, while Gamma adopted after a readiness pilot. Thus adopters and nonadopters differ in baseline performance and likely in managerial attention and implementation capability. Several changes coincide with rollout: the centralized fraud desk began in March; routing raised arrivals to Alpha/Beta from April and Gamma after July; staffing changed differently by branch; and a May product change raised recorded complexity in several branches.

Observed outcomes also have important limits. Branch-month medians and p90s summarize heterogeneous case populations and cannot identify whether individual cases used automation, were escalated, or waited at the fraud desk. Rework and escalation are process rates, not necessarily harms: an increase can reflect safer routing. CSAT is a monthly respondent mean with unknown branch-month response counts and changing response propensities. These data therefore describe joint operational changes, not a clean treatment-control experiment.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: staggered-adoption comparative interrupted time series / event study** | Cohort-specific event time: Alpha/Beta adopt April, Gamma July; estimate post-month contrasts separately and pool only transparently | Not-yet-treated branches at each event time: Gamma/Delta/Epsilon for April cohort; Delta/Epsilon for July cohort | Calendar month effects or common time shocks; pre-specified arrivals/FTE, complexity, and load per FTE as descriptive precision variables; do not treat post-treatment mediators as innocuous controls | E1, E2, E3, E7 | In absence of availability, adopter outcome changes would follow comparator changes conditional on modeled common shocks and covariates; no material unmeasured branch-specific shocks; limited interference | Plot pre-levels/slopes; placebo adoption dates; leave-one-control-out comparisons; compare medians and p90 separately; inspect residuals and event-time pattern | Only 5 branches, 3 treated, short pre-period, targeted adoption, and common shocks make parallel trends largely untestable |
| Matched synthetic-control-style trajectory comparison | Branch-specific April or July intervention; create weighted comparator trajectory from nonadopters/not-yet-treated | Delta/Epsilon, and Gamma before July for April cohort | Match pre-cycle levels, pre-slopes, volume/FTE, complexity, rework, and escalation | Branch-specific E1/E2 | Weighted controls approximate each adopter’s untreated trajectory; no donor contamination from fraud-desk spillovers | Pre-period fit RMSPE; placebo gaps for untreated branches; sensitivity to donor removal | Very few donors and poor baseline overlap, especially for high-burden Alpha/Beta |
| Controlled interrupted time series with branch-specific slopes | Level/slope break at rollout, optionally gradual ramp linked to months since availability | Each adopter’s own pre-period plus aggregate nonadopter trend | Season/time trend, arrivals/FTE, complexity; explicit indicators for May product change and March fraud-desk onset where possible | Within-branch dynamic E1/E7 | Pre-period slope predicts counterfactual post slope after common-shock adjustment; no coincident branch-specific intervention | Pre-break residuals; placebo breakpoints; compare to untreated slope changes | Only three pre months for Alpha/Beta and six for Gamma; intervention coincides with routing and staffing shifts |
| Case-level emulation for future rollout | Randomized or randomized-encouragement phase-in; case-level eligibility and assignment timestamp | Concurrent eligible cases assigned to manual versus automation, or randomized branch waves | Registration risk, product, channel, arrival time, queue state, staffing, prior history | E6 and E10; also safer E1 | Random assignment/encouragement, accurate treatment recording, no unmeasured interference or explicitly modeled interference | Balance checks; assignment compliance; instrument strength; event-log conformance; outcome completeness | Requires new instrumentation and may face queue spillovers if assignment changes shared specialist/fraud queues |

The primary approach should be a cohort-aware comparative interrupted time-series/event-study analysis, not a conventional two-way fixed-effects model. With staggered timing and likely dynamic effects, ordinary two-way fixed effects can assign already-treated units as controls and produce difficult-to-interpret weighted averages when effects differ by cohort or event month. Here, Alpha/Beta have five post months but Gamma has two; learning and staffing shocks imply those effects plausibly differ.

The design should estimate each rollout cohort against branches not yet available at that time, retain cohort-specific estimates, and emphasize plots and transparently calculated contrasts before any pooled number. Calendar shocks matter: the March fraud desk, May complexity shift, and common seasonal volume pattern affect all branches, so month effects or control changes are useful. However, post-rollout arrivals, complexity, escalation, and utilization can themselves be affected by availability. They should be reported as mechanisms and used in sensitivity models, not mechanically “controlled away” as though pre-treatment confounders.

This remains weak causal evidence because rollout was targeted to weak branches and local staffing/routing changed at the same time. Its advantage is not definitive identification; it is honest separation of common operational shocks, cohort timing, and dynamic implementation from raw before-after changes.

**Comparison with untreated branches.** Delta and Epsilon combined changed from a January–March mean median of about **37.4 h** to an April–August mean of **43.8 h** (+6.4 h), while their p90 rose from about **77.2 h** to **94.3 h** (+17.1 h). Against that common deterioration, Alpha’s median change (0.4 h) and Beta’s (+1.9 h) look relatively favorable, but their p90 changes (+9.6 and +9.8 h) remain adverse in absolute terms. Gamma’s July–August median was 3.4 h below its January–June average, but this comparison is especially short and follows the common May/June deterioration.

**Treatment intensity.** Across the three adopters, higher post-period automated-fast-path use coincides with late improvement: Alpha moves from 25% in April to 48% in August as median falls from 48.3 to 43.3 h; Beta moves 27% to 50% while median falls 47.3 to 45.0 h; Gamma moves 25% to 32% while median falls 38.4 to 36.0 h. This is a within-rollout association, not a causal dose response: later months also had lower arrivals than May/June and accumulated learning. The corresponding p90 pattern is less favorable: Alpha/Beta p90 remained above April in August, though improved from June peaks.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** Alpha and Beta were chosen partly because of poor historical cycle times. With only three pre months, their pre-trends cannot establish parallel trends. Alpha’s pre median fluctuated 46.9–50.4 h; Beta rose 45.4 to 46.4 h. Selection after poor performance creates a real regression-to-the-mean risk. The broad May/June worsening among untreated branches indicates a common complexity/operational shock, while Alpha’s May staffing loss and routing increase add branch-specific confounding.

**Sensitivity/ranges.** For median cycle time, raw adopter changes range from **3.4 to +1.9 h**; a control-trend-adjusted descriptive comparison could imply roughly **0 to 8 h relative improvement**, but that interval is scenario-based rather than a confidence interval. For p90, raw changes range from **0.9 to +9.8 h**; relative to untreated deterioration there may be a smaller tail worsening than expected, but no secure evidence of tail reduction. For rework, changes are between **0.6 and 0.1 percentage points** among adopters, compatible with a modest reduction, no change, or displacement into escalation; escalation increased **2.0–2.7 points**, so “rework reduction” alone is not a customer-quality conclusion.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**expand with an embedded evaluation**

Expand next to **Delta and Epsilon**, but not as an unstructured simultaneous deployment. They are the remaining branches, have different baseline complexity profiles, and provide the best opportunity to learn about generalizability. Use a randomized phased rollout at the **branch-week or branch-fortnight** level if operationally feasible: randomly assign one branch to early activation and the other to a later activation after a protected baseline, with a pre-specified minimum delay sufficient to observe outcomes. If leadership requires both systems available immediately, randomize an encouragement or workflow-default at case level among clearly eligible low-risk cases, while preserving manual triage as the alternative. Assignment must be recorded independently of actual use.

Collect at least **8–12 weeks of stable pre-period data** and **12–16 weeks post activation** for each phase, with daily or weekly measurement rather than monthly aggregation. Record event-level timestamps for registration, triage assignment, automated decision, specialist queue entry/exit, fraud-desk queue entry/exit, requests for information, rework loops, resolve, close, reopen, and complaint. Capture actual automation exposure, model/version, confidence score, override, eligibility reason, risk class, product/channel, staffing by skill and shift, arrivals, backlog, queue length, and wait-time distributions.

Monitor spillovers at the centralized fraud desk and specialist teams by origin branch and treatment status. The principal operational guardrails should be p90 and p95 cycle time, unresolved backlog, fraud/specialist wait times, rework plus reopen rates, incorrect-routing audits, complaint rate, and representative CSAT response rate. Pause the next phase if p90 exceeds its pre-specified risk-adjusted comparator by, for example, 10% for two consecutive weeks, if reopen/error rates rise materially, or if shared queues breach service levels.

Scale to full default use only if the early phase shows: (1) no guardrail breach, (2) a practically meaningful and reproducible median improvement, (3) no deterioration in tail performance after accounting for shared queues, and (4) stable or improved resolution quality. This policy preserves potential operational benefit while converting the next rollout into evidence capable of distinguishing fast-path substitution from genuine system capacity creation.

print("Branches:", sorted(df["branch"].unique()))
print("\nValidation passed.")

# 2. Print trends for five outcomes. No subplots are used.
outcomes = ["median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h", "rework_pct", "escalation_pct", "csat_1to5"]
for outcome in outcomes:
    print(f"\n=== {outcome}: branch-month trend ===")
    print(df.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values=outcome).round(2).to_string())

# 3. Transparent branch-specific pre/post contrasts.
adoption_month = (
    df.loc[df["automation"].eq(1)]
      .groupby("branch")["month"]
      .min()
      .to_dict()
)
df["adoption_month"] = df["branch"].map(adoption_month)
df["treated_ever"] = df["adoption_month"].notna()

contrast_rows = []
for branch, adopt_date in adoption_month.items():
    d = df.loc[df["branch"].eq(branch)].copy()
    pre = d.loc[d["month"] < adopt_date]
    post = d.loc[d["month"] >= adopt_date]
    row = {
        "branch": branch,
        "pre_months": len(pre),
        "post_months": len(post),
        "pre_fast_path_pct": pre["automated_fast_path_pct"].mean(),
        "post_fast_path_pct": post["automated_fast_path_pct"].mean(),
    }
    for y in outcomes:
        row[f"pre_{y}"] = pre[y].mean()
        row[f"post_{y}"] = post[y].mean()
        row[f"change_{y}"] = post[y].mean() - pre[y].mean()
    contrast_rows.append(row)

contrasts = pd.DataFrame(contrast_rows)
print("\n=== Descriptive adopter-specific pre/post contrasts ===")
print(contrasts.round(3).to_string(index=False))

# 4. Relative-time indicators for staggered adoption.
# Negative values are pre-adoption months; 0 is first available month.
# Never-treated branches have missing event_time and remain valid controls.
df["event_time"] = np.where(
    df["treated_ever"],
    ((df["month"].dt.year - df["adoption_month"].dt.year) * 12
     + (df["month"].dt.month - df["adoption_month"].dt.month)),
    np.nan
)

for k in range(-3, 5):
    # Never-treated units are 0 for all event indicators.
    df[f"event_{k:+d}"] = (
        df["treated_ever"] & df["event_time"].eq(k)
    ).astype(int)

print("\n=== Relative-time counts among ever-treated branches ===")
print(df.loc[df["treated_ever"]].groupby("event_time").size().to_string())

# 5. Descriptive panel models.
# IMPORTANT CAUSAL COMMENTS:
# - These regressions are descriptive associations, not proof of a causal effect.
# - Availability was targeted to weak branches and coincided with routing/staffing/case-mix changes.
# - Calendar effects capture only common shocks, not branch-specific contemporaneous changes.
# - With only five branch clusters, cluster-robust standard errors are fragile.
# - Adding post-rollout variables such as load or complexity can block mechanisms or induce bias
#   if automation itself changed routing or recorded case mix.

n_clusters = df["branch"].nunique()
warnings.warn(
    f"Only {n_clusters} branch clusters are available. Cluster-robust inference is highly unstable; "
    "treat p-values and confidence intervals as descriptive."
)
warnings.warn(
    "Outcomes are branch-month aggregates. A regression on median_cycle_h does not estimate a "
    "case-level average treatment effect, and medians should not be treated as raw individual data."
)

# Descriptive availability model: branch FE + calendar-month FE.
availability_model = smf.ols(
    "median_cycle_h ~ automation + C(branch) + C(month)",
    data=df
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"]})

print("\n=== Descriptive panel model: availability association with median cycle ===")
print(availability_model.summary())

# Descriptive treatment-intensity model.
# Restricting to ever-treated branches asks whether later/higher-use months correlate with outcomes
# within rollout branches; it remains confounded by learning, demand, staffing, and selection.
intensity_df = df.loc[df["treated_ever"]].copy()
intensity_model = smf.ols(
    "median_cycle_h ~ automated_fast_path_pct + C(branch) + C(month)",
    data=intensity_df
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": intensity_df["branch"]})

print("\n=== Descriptive intensity model: fast-path share association with median cycle ===")
print(intensity_model.summary())

# A sensitivity specification adds observed workload and recorded complexity.
# These may be confounders, mediators, or both, so this is not automatically 'more causal.'
sensitivity_model = smf.ols(
    "median_cycle_h ~ automation + load_per_fte + high_complexity_pct + C(branch) + C(month)",
    data=df
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"]})

print("\n=== Descriptive sensitivity model (interpret controls cautiously) ===")
print(sensitivity_model.summary())

print("\nInterpretation warning:")
print("- Do not interpret these coefficients as identified causal effects.")
print("- Inspect branch-specific trends, cohort timing, staffing, routing, complexity, and shared-queue data.")
print("- A future randomized phase-in or case-level randomized encouragement would provide stronger evidence.")

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** Automation availability was followed by gradually increasing automated-fast-path use, higher escalation rates, and mixed branch-level median and p90 cycle-time patterns.
- **Suggestive:** Higher implementation intensity and later rollout months coincide with improved medians in the three adopters, while tail performance does not show a comparably consistent improvement.
- **Conditionally causal:** Under strong assumptions that not-yet-treated branches represent each adopter’s untreated trend and that routing, staffing, case mix, and fraud-desk spillovers are adequately handled, availability may have mitigated a broader deterioration in median cycle time.
- **Not supported:** “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a general, customer-beneficial causal claim for Delta and Epsilon.
