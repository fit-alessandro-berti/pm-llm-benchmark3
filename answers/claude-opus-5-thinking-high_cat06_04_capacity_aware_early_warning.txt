## 1. Decision rule

The rule is a five-gate filter followed by a scored, capacity-priced ranking and a scheduling step. A high breach probability alone never authorises a swarm.

**Step 0 — Robustify the probability before anything else.**
The model output is observational and segment-biased, so I never rank on `p_now`. I compute an adjusted interval and a working point estimate:

| Segment / condition | Adjustment to `p_now` | Working point | Band used for robustness |
|---|---|---|---|
| SME | +0.04 to +0.09 (documented underprediction) | +0.065 | apply both ends |
| Strategic Enterprise | −0.05 to −0.12 (post-redesign overprediction) | −0.085 | apply both ends |
| Public Sector | 0 offset, moderate sample | 0 | widen ±0.05 |
| Non-Native-Language Support (NNLS) | 0 offset (no credible offset estimable from a small sample) | 0 | widen ±0.08 |
| Any case whose alert is driven by an imputed feature | shrink toward the last trusted prefix: `p_adj = (p_prev + p_now)/2` | shrunk value | widen to span `p_prev`…`p_now` |

Decisions must be *sign-stable across the band*. If a case only clears the bar at the favourable end of its calibration band, it is not authorised at that refresh; it is either re-scored after evidence improves or handled in the cheap-action lane.

I also robustify the *effect*, which is the more dangerous unknown. Supplied ranges are priors about a causal quantity, not measured effects, so I shrink them:
- Strategic Enterprise: ×0.8 (effect estimates were fitted on inflated risk baselines, so the achievable reduction is likely overstated).
- NNLS: shrink the midpoint ~25% toward the pooled cross-segment mean (≈0.17), i.e. treat 0.24 as ≈0.22 — shrinkage for uncertainty, *not* exclusion.
- Triage-stage cases: ×0.85. A diagnostic swarm on an unscoped case spends part of its 45 minutes doing scoping the case would have done anyway.
- Post-swarm runway below the assumed 1.5 h of remaining resolution + validation work: ×0.75.
- Alert driven by an unverified imputed feature: effect treated as unknown, case gated (below), not scored.

**Step 1 — Eligibility gates (all must pass; each is a hard veto).**
1. **Stage gate.** Current stage strictly before `Diagnosis Complete`. A case at or past `Diagnosis Complete` is *never* swarm-eligible, regardless of probability or contract value. This is the mechanism gate: after that milestone the treatment has no channel through which to act (0.00–0.03).
2. **Runway gate.** Time to deadline ≥ 45 min (swarm) + 1.5 h (expected post-diagnosis resolution + customer validation) + 15 min (coordination) ≈ **2.5 h at swarm start**. Below that the swarm may finish but the case still breaches, so the money buys a better-diagnosed breach. Cases failing this gate route to the expedite / renegotiate lane.
3. **Effect gate.** Lower bound of the risk-reduction range ≥ 0.05 **and** shrunk midpoint ≥ 0.10. This kills the "high risk, no lever" pattern.
4. **Evidence gate (repeat alerts).** A second-or-later alert requires a *material change* (defined in §4). Alert count is not evidence; re-firing on an unchanged feature vector every 30 minutes is a refresh artefact.
5. **Data-quality gate.** If the alert's principal driver is imputed or a feature pipeline has failed, the case is *held* for verification (target 30–60 min) rather than treated or dismissed. Verification is cheap; a swarm is not.

**Step 2 — Score the survivors, charging them for the queue they damage.**

`Net = (shrunk effect × value of avoiding breach) − €450 − (1 − p_adj) × false-alarm burden − congestion price`

Two deliberate choices. First, the false-alarm burden is weighted by the probability the case would not have breached anyway, `(1 − p_adj)`; charging it in full, as the template formula does, double-penalises genuinely at-risk cases (I report both where it matters). Second, and more important, each slot is charged an internal **congestion price** so that the externality on untreated cases is internalised rather than externalised: base model = 6 untreated diagnosis-stage cases × 0.02 added breach probability × €15,000 typical untreated case value ≈ **€1,800 per slot** (range €600–€4,500). A case must beat the harm it causes, not merely be worth something in isolation. This single term is what makes the policy capacity-aware rather than a dressed-up threshold.

**Step 3 — Authorisation bar and prioritisation.**
- Authorise only if `Net > 0` at the *unfavourable* end of the calibration and effect bands, or if the case carries a non-monetary obligation floor (regulatory / public-sector duty, formal escalation, contractual credit exposure).
- Rank by `Net`, but use **log-spaced value tiers** (≤€10k / €10–35k / >€35k) rather than raw euros in the ranking, so one €100,000 case cannot buy the whole day's capacity on the strength of a 0.05 effect estimate.
- **Schedule by earliest latest-feasible-start**, not by score. The two senior specialists deliver the swarm together, so swarms are **serial**: four slots = four sequential 45-minute windows, not four parallel ones. A high-scoring case with 10 hours of slack has large option value from waiting; a mid-scoring case whose window closes at 12:30 has none.
- Quota floors, applied before ranking, not as tie-breaks: ≥30% of weekly slots to below-median-value cases, and a standing exploration/learning quota (§5) for sparse segments.
- Reserve policy: hold one slot for the possible regulatory incident, but treat the reserve as *preemptible-by-deferral* — if the reserve slot's window arrives with no incident, release it, because a swarm started 45 minutes before a new incident arrives only delays that incident's swarm by 45 minutes, which a freshly opened case can almost always absorb.
- Every decision is logged with inputs, adjusted probability, shrunk effect, score, action, and the runner-up — so the policy is auditable and later usable for effect estimation.

**The load-bearing conclusion.** At the stated spillover magnitudes, the queue externality (≈€1,800/slot base, up to ≈€2,700/slot adverse) is the same order of magnitude as the direct expected benefit of a *typical* case in this portfolio. Three implications follow directly: (i) only high-value **and** high-effect **and** stage-eligible cases should ever consume a slot; (ii) fewer slots is often the better portfolio, so the reserve slot is good economics and not merely a management preference; (iii) measuring the spillover coefficient is the single highest-value analytical investment available, because it determines the sign of the entire programme. One modelling caveat: applying "+0.01–0.03 per slot" to the *same* six untreated cases three times implies a total increment of 0.03–0.09 each, which likely saturates at the top and may be superadditive under heavy congestion. I have used the linear form because that is what was specified, and flagged it as an estimand to test rather than a fact.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

**Principle: key the policy on information change, never on alert count — and never confuse suppressing a notification with withdrawing a case's claim on resources.** Both failure modes are live here: alert fatigue in one direction, silent permanent neglect in the other.

**Material change (the only thing that re-opens an alert).** At least one of:
1. a new recorded process event (stage transition, new attachment or diagnostic log, customer response, reassignment, severity change);
2. `Δp_adj ≥ +0.10` attributable to *non-imputed* features, verified by the feature-attribution diff between refreshes;
3. a runway-boundary crossing (remaining time falls below ~2× expected remaining work, changing feasibility and therefore the decision);
4. a scope or value change (severity upgrade, contract or penalty-clause change, regulatory involvement).
A refresh cycle firing on an unchanged feature vector is explicitly **not** a material change.

**Cool-down.** After an alert is reviewed and a swarm declined, suppress further alerts on that case for `max(90 min, 2 refresh cycles)`, overridden immediately by any material change. The case stays visible on the watchlist during suppression; only the interruption stops.

**Escalation ladder** (an alert enters at the lowest rung that fits, and repeated alerts move *up the ladder*, not around it):
1. automated actions — missing-information request, queue re-prioritisation, customer notification;
2. 10-minute single-specialist scoping read (cheap lane, does not consume a swarm slot);
3. 45-minute two-specialist swarm (scarce, priced);
4. duty-manager escalation, staffing/overtime authorisation, formal deadline renegotiation.
A third alert with no material change escalates to **supervisor review of the case** — not to a swarm and not to silence. This is the release valve that stops a persistently at-risk, low-value case from being declined forever by the same arithmetic.

**Post-swarm rule.** One swarm per case. A second swarm requires all of: a material new event, `p_adj` still above the bar after the first swarm, runway gate still satisfied, and named supervisor sign-off recorded in the decision log. Repeat swarms otherwise compete very poorly against fresh cases, because the cheapest gains have already been taken.

**Re-entry.** When suppression expires with `p_adj` still elevated, the case re-enters alerting but carries a **persistently-at-risk flag**. The flag does not raise its score (that would let noise accumulate into priority) — it grants standing claims: automatic cheap-lane treatment, first claim on unused end-of-shift capacity, and inclusion in the weekly starvation review (§5).

**I-05 versus I-07 — same segment, same alert count, opposite handling.**

Both are SME, both third alerts, both never treated, both nominally eligible on stage. They diverge on the only two things that matter.

*I-05 — repetition without information.* No event has occurred since the first alert; `p_now` moved 0.55→0.57, which is refresh noise, and the model is re-scoring an unchanged feature vector every 30 minutes. Three alerts here are one alert counted three times. Action: **suppress the notification** (de-duplicate at the source so it stops consuming reviewer attention), and decline the slot — €7,000 of value cannot beat a €1,800 congestion price plus €450 cost. But the decline is *not* a judgement that the case is fine: with the SME correction its true risk is ≈0.635 and it has the strongest effect estimate in the portfolio (0.25–0.40). So it receives the persistently-at-risk flag, cheap-lane actions now (queue re-prioritisation, targeted request for the missing information, 10-minute scoping), supervisor review triggered by the third alert, and first claim on the 14:35 slot if the regulatory incident does not materialise and no better claimant appears. It also feeds a specific fix: alert de-duplication should be a platform change, not a per-case decision.

*I-07 — repetition with information.* A new customer diagnostic log has arrived. That is a material change under criterion 1, so the alert is **legitimately raised and must not be suppressed** — even though `p_now` *fell* slightly, 0.75→0.72. The direction of movement is not the test. A probability that moves after genuinely new evidence is more trustworthy than one that sits still, and a small decline is consistent with the log having resolved some uncertainty; adjusted for SME underprediction the case still sits at ≈0.785, the highest in the portfolio.

And yet I-07 gets **no swarm**, for a reason entirely separate from its alert history: with 1.5 h remaining and the case still in Triage, a 45-minute swarm would leave roughly 45 minutes for resolution work plus customer validation. The stated 0.20–0.32 effect was presumably estimated on cases with adequate runway and does not transfer. Actions instead: a 10-minute senior read of the new log (which is what the new evidence actually warrants), resolution prep started in parallel with diagnosis, immediate customer notification, and a formal deadline-extension request.

The contrast is the whole point of the temporal policy: **I-05's alert is wrong to fire and its case still deserves attention; I-07's alert is right to fire and its case still does not deserve a slot.** Alert quality and capacity allocation are separate decisions, and collapsing them is how these systems fail — either by treating alert count as urgency, or by treating suppression as a verdict on the case.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**(a) Value-based prioritisation starving lower-value cases.** The scoring above is transparently value-weighted, and the effect is visible in this very portfolio: every case that clears the bar on score alone is €20k+ and mostly Strategic Enterprise, while I-05, I-07, I-01 and I-10 — including the two with the *largest* estimated effects (0.25–0.40 and 0.20–0.32) — are all declined. Over weeks this is a structural bias, not bad luck: a €7,000 case can essentially never beat a €1,800 congestion price at any plausible effect size. Safeguards: (i) a **hard quota** of ≥30% of weekly slots to below-median-value cases, allocated in a separate lane so it cannot be eroded by tie-breaks; (ii) **log-spaced value tiers** instead of raw euros in the score, so a €100,000 case counts as one tier above a €35,000 case rather than three times it; (iii) a **funded cheap-action lane** with its own protected capacity (single-specialist 10-minute reads, information requests, queue re-prioritisation), so low-value cases receive a real, if smaller, response — that lane is what carries I-05, I-07, I-01 and I-10 today; (iv) a weekly **starvation report**: unserved-alert rate and, critically, *breach rate among alerted-but-unserved cases* by segment and value band, with a standing escalation if the SME rate exceeds the Strategic Enterprise rate by more than 5 points for two consecutive weeks; (v) **non-monetary obligation floors** for public-sector and regulatory cases (I-01, I-06), so statutory duty is not silently traded against contract value.

**(b) Uncertainty for Non-Native-Language Support.** Both calibration and effect estimates are weak here, and the tempting response — down-weight what you cannot verify — is self-fulfilling: fewer treatments produce fewer observations, which keep the estimates uncertain, which justify further down-weighting. Uncertainty is not evidence of low benefit. Safeguards: (i) shrink the effect toward the pooled mean (I applied 0.24→0.22) rather than discounting the case, so the penalty for ignorance is bounded and explicit; (ii) refuse to apply *any* segment calibration offset until `n ≥ 150` outcome events — fitting an offset to a small sample manufactures false precision; (iii) an explicit **exploration quota**: a guaranteed minimum share of slots to sparse segments, which is part of why I-09 takes slot 1 rather than merely surviving the ranking; (iv) **structured outcome capture** on every NNLS swarm (was the bottleneck diagnostic depth, or translation/interpreter availability, or customer-side comprehension?) — because if the real constraint is interpreter access, the swarm is the wrong instrument and no amount of better ranking will help; (v) add an interpreter/translation-escalation cheap action so the segment has a mechanism suited to its likely bottleneck; (vi) report NNLS metrics with confidence intervals and never suppress the row for small `n`.

**(c) Differential calibration across segments.** Uncorrected, the bias compounds in the worst possible direction: Strategic Enterprise cases have inflated probabilities *and* the highest values, so a naive ranking over-serves them twice over, while SME cases are simultaneously under-scored (by 0.04–0.09) and under-valued. Safeguards: (i) segment offsets applied as **intervals**, with decisions required to be sign-stable at both ends; (ii) per-segment reliability curves, ECE and Brier decomposition reviewed monthly, plus per-segment alert→swarm conversion rates; (iii) **freeze** offsets estimated on thin samples and widen intervals instead; (iv) specifically re-estimate the Strategic Enterprise offset on rolling recent data only, since it is an artefact of a recent workflow redesign and will decay as the redesign stabilises — a stale −0.085 will eventually under-serve those cases; (v) publish the offsets in the decision log so an auditor can see that I-02's authorisation rested on 0.575, not 0.66.

**(d) Selective intervention changing future training labels.** This is the most insidious risk. We treat the highest-risk treatable cases; successful swarms prevent breaches; the model then learns that this feature pattern does *not* breach; alerts stop firing on it; breaches return. The policy quietly destroys the signal it depends on, and the failure looks like model improvement (fewer alerts, good calibration on observed labels). Safeguards: (i) record an **intervention flag with timestamp and prefix** on every case, non-optional; (ii) train the target as the **untreated potential outcome** — restrict to pre-intervention prefixes, or include treatment as a feature and predict at treatment = 0, or treat post-intervention outcomes as censored; (iii) maintain a **randomised holdout in the grey band**: where priority scores are statistically indistinguishable, allocate by lottery — this costs almost nothing in expected value precisely because the cases are near-ties, and it is the only clean source of unbiased labels and effect estimates; (iv) propensity/importance weighting in training for non-randomised decisions; (v) a named **drift signature** to watch: falling alert volume in a cohort whose underlying breach rate is stable, which is the fingerprint of this loop and should trigger retraining rather than celebration.

**(e) Spillover harms falling systematically on untreated groups.** The delay caused by swarms lands on the six untreated diagnosis-stage cases. If treated cases are systematically high-value and Strategic Enterprise, the harmed set is systematically low-value and SME — a regressive transfer that is invisible in every report we currently produce, because a case harmed by congestion never appears as "an alert we declined." Per §3 this harm is comparable in magnitude to the direct benefit, so it is not a rounding error. Safeguards: (i) **measure it** — tag every case in the diagnosis queue during a swarm window with an `exposed-to-swarm-congestion` flag, and compare breach rates by exposure, segment and value band; (ii) **price it into the decision** (the €1,800/slot congestion charge), so a slot must beat the harm it causes rather than externalising it; (iii) **congestion circuit-breaker**: when the queue index (queue length, mean and 95th-percentile diagnosis wait) exceeds a threshold, cap the shift at two slots; above a severe threshold, suspend swarms and escalate staffing instead — the intervention that is optimal in a quiet queue is value-destroying in a congested one; (iv) **compensating action** bundled into every authorisation: when a swarm runs, automatically re-prioritise the two most deadline-critical untreated queue cases and consider overtime authorisation, so the harm is partly repaid at source; (v) a quarterly **"who paid for the swarms"** report by segment and value band, reviewed by the same forum that approves the allocation rule — the point being that the people who benefit from the policy should have to look at who funded it.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

**Operational.** Alert volume and alerts-per-case; suppression rate and suppression overrides; alert→swarm conversion rate; time from alert to decision; swarm punctuality and completion within 45 minutes; **gate-violation counters that should read zero** (swarms on cases at or past `Diagnosis Complete`; swarms started inside the runway threshold); reserve-slot utilisation and how often the regulatory reserve was actually needed (if it is never used, the reserve policy is over-conservative and should be relaxed; if it is always used, capacity is the real problem). Every decision logged with inputs, adjusted probability, shrunk effect, score, action, runner-up and decider.

**Predictive.** Per-segment and per-prefix calibration (reliability plots, ECE, Brier decomposition into calibration and refinement), because a model well calibrated overall can be badly calibrated at early prefixes where the decisions actually happen; discrimination (AUC, PR-AUC) tracked separately from calibration, since the two fail independently; feature-distribution drift; and a **data-quality monitor** on imputation rates per feature that alarms when missingness spikes and automatically downgrades any prediction whose principal driver is imputed — I-06 is the case that should have been caught by machine, not by a human noticing. Segment offsets re-estimated monthly with intervals; a minimum of ~150 outcome events per segment before an offset changes; the Strategic Enterprise offset watched with particular care as the workflow redesign matures.

**Causal-effect estimation** (the crux, since the supplied ranges are priors and the whole policy rests on them). Layered, in order of credibility: (i) **grey-band randomisation** — lottery allocation among statistically indistinguishable near-ties, giving a clean randomised contrast at negligible policy cost; (ii) **capacity-driven instrumental variation** — exogenous shocks such as a regulatory incident consuming a slot, or absence of a senior specialist, plausibly shift treatment without affecting case-level breach risk directly, and can instrument for treatment; (iii) **regression discontinuity** at the authorisation bar; (iv) **target-trial emulation with doubly-robust (AIPW) estimation** using the logged decision inputs as the propensity covariates — feasible precisely because the decision log records what was known at decision time. Stratify every estimate by stage at intervention, remaining runway, and segment, because the policy already asserts that effects vary along exactly those dimensions (Triage ×0.85, short-runway ×0.75, NNLS shrinkage) and those assertions are currently untested assumptions. Analyses pre-registered; results reported as intervals; the priors in the score updated by **hierarchical shrinkage**, never replaced wholesale by a noisy point estimate. Rough power target: 50–80 treated cases per stratum before ranges are narrowed materially — which implies the ranges will stay wide for months and the policy must remain robust to that.

**Spillover and capacity.** Estimate the spillover coefficient directly, since §3 shows it determines the programme's sign: compare breach rates for diagnosis-queue cases whose waiting window overlapped a swarm against matched non-overlapping cases, controlling for the congestion index; exploit day-level variation (zero-swarm versus three-swarm days) as a coarse check; estimate the delay-to-breach elasticity so the €1,800/slot congestion price can be **re-derived monthly** rather than assumed. Track queue length, mean and 95th-percentile diagnosis wait, senior-specialist utilisation and overtime, cases waiting beyond threshold, and the composite congestion index that drives the circuit-breaker. Also monitor the human side, which the arithmetic ignores: interruption load per specialist, and diagnosis quality or rework rate on the cases they were pulled off.

**Fairness.** Slot share and cheap-lane share by segment, value band and language-support status; quota compliance (≥30% below-median value; sparse-segment exploration minimum); breach rate among alerted-but-unserved cases by segment; congestion-exposure incidence by segment; NNLS calibration and effect reported with intervals and never suppressed for small `n`; count of persistently-at-risk cases never treated (the I-05 pattern) with a hard review after three cycles. Monthly review, named owner, explicit authority to change quotas.

**When to change the rule — pre-specified triggers, so changes are decisions rather than reactions.**
- A segment calibration offset moves outside its current interval with adequate `n` → update the offset and re-derive the bar.
- An estimated intervention effect's confidence interval excludes the assumed midpoint → update the effect prior and re-derive the bar; if a stratum's interval includes zero, remove that stratum from eligibility pending more evidence.
- The estimated spillover price changes by more than 50% → re-derive both the authorisation bar and the per-shift slot cap. A materially higher price plausibly implies **fewer** slots, not better ranking.
- A fairness metric breaches its threshold for two consecutive periods → adjust quotas before adjusting the score, since quotas are the instrument that actually binds.
- The drift signature from §5(d) appears (falling alert rate in a cohort with stable breach rate) → retrain with treatment-aware labels and audit the holdout.
- Runway or stage discount assumptions contradicted by stratified effect estimates → update the gates, including the possibility that the 2.5 h runway gate is too lax or too strict.

**Change control.** Every candidate rule change runs first in **shadow mode** for two weeks (its would-be decisions computed on live data and logged, without acting), is compared against the incumbent on the metrics above, then rolls out in stages with the grey-band randomised holdout preserved throughout and pre-agreed rollback criteria. Quarterly review of manual overrides, since a high override rate is evidence about the rule and not merely about the reviewers.

**Standing caveat.** Nothing above should be read as claiming the swarm programme is known to be net-positive. At the base spillover estimate it is modestly positive; in the adverse world it is negative until the gates truncate the downside to roughly break-even. The correct posture is therefore to run the programme as a **measured experiment with guardrails** — small slot counts, priced externalities, hard eligibility gates, randomisation among near-ties, and rapid investment in estimating the spillover coefficient and the true intervention effects — rather than as a mature ranking system to be scaled up on the strength of its expected-value table.
