## 1. Temporal Data Semantics

I reconstruct each case as an ordered event sequence, not from its final case attributes.

- **Ordering:** For events \(e,f\) in the same case, \(e \prec f\) means that \(e\) occurred before \(f\), using timestamp first and `event_index` second. Thus an event can see updates from lower-indexed events at the same timestamp, but not from higher-indexed events.
- **State:** \(S(e)\) is the state immediately after applying nonblank attribute cells on event \(e\). A blank cell carries the previous value forward. It does not erase that value. If no value has yet been observed, the attribute remains **unknown**.
- **No backward leakage:** State is forward-filled only within a case. A release snapshot includes only events ordered no later than that release. In particular:
  - TX-305’s settlement amount does not become its release amount.
  - TX-306’s Enhanced_Review basis is EUR 220,000, not the final EUR 230,000.
  - TX-309’s Enhanced_Review basis is EUR 118,000, not the final EUR 145,000.
  - TX-311’s review at index 3 sees US; the jurisdiction update at index 4 changes the state to DE.
- **Review basis and materiality:** A review’s amount basis is the latest known amount at that review. For amount-dependent obligations, an amount after the review greater than \(1.20\times\) that basis invalidates the review under R4. Comparing each new amount with the review basis also catches cumulative increases. A decrease or an increase of 20% or less does not, by itself, invalidate the review. If the basis or new amount is unknown, materiality is **unknown**, not “nonmaterial.”
- **Other material changes:** Materiality is obligation-relative. A jurisdiction change can materially change the R1 review basis; risk and customer-type changes can change the R2 review context. Not every nonblank cell is automatically an invalidating change. A change that takes a case out of R1 scope before release does not retroactively create an R1 violation.
- **Four different times:**
  - **Event time:** when the logged business event occurred.
  - **Effective time:** when an attribute or exemption legally/operationally applies. Attribute updates default to their event time; exemptions have explicit effective and expiry times.
  - **Record time:** when a database or register record was inserted, such as 11:00 for both exemption records.
  - **Evidence time:** when signatures, certificates, hashes, witness evidence, or other proof were created or obtained.
- **Late evidence:** A late record can prove an earlier fact, but record time alone does not backdate approval. Conversely, evidence created after release does not necessarily disprove earlier oral approval. The quality and independence of the evidence must be assessed separately.

## 4. Ambiguities and Alternative Readings

- **TX-302:** If the 11:00 database insertion were the only exemption evidence, EX-77’s approval time might be uncertain. That would not change the primary result because the Enhanced_Review independently satisfies R1. If that review were separately proven invalid, strong EX-77 evidence would instead support **JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION**; if both the review and exemption failed, the case would be **NONCOMPLIANT**. Strong late evidence can resolve substantive validity without proving that the release-time system actually had access to the exemption.
- **TX-305:** Assuming settlement amount equals release amount would be unjustified backfilling. If reliable evidence showed an unchanged amount below EUR 100,000 at release, R1 would not apply. If it showed EUR 150,000 throughout and no post-review material change, the logged Enhanced_Review could satisfy the literal R1 event/order formulation. If the amount became high through a post-review material change, or if organizational policy requires an amount-aware review and that cannot be established, a deviation could result. An immutable release payload and review input snapshot would distinguish these readings.
- **TX-310:** The main classification treats the historical approval fact as unresolved. Under a strict audit burden-of-proof convention—an exemption must be adequately proven to be relied upon—the case can instead be reported **NONCOMPLIANT**, because no valid exception has been established. Independent evidence corroborating Head_Compliance’s oral approval at 08:45 could support **JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION**, notwithstanding later documentation. Evidence affirmatively rejecting timely approval would support **NONCOMPLIANT**. The certificate timeline contradicts simple reliance on the displayed PDF date, but does not logically disprove an earlier oral approval.
- **TX-311:** Reversing the same-timestamp order would change the review snapshot and material-change diagnostics: the review would see DE rather than US. It would not change the logged release state. Merely rejecting the DE update would also not automatically create a violation: with US at release, R1 would apply, but the Enhanced_Review occurred after the EUR 115,000 amount update and before release. A noncompliant result would require additional evidence, such as a subsequent material change invalidating that review or a different foreign release state coupled with an invalid review. Requiring re-review after every jurisdiction update even when R1 is no longer triggered would be a stricter, different policy.
- **TX-306 and TX-308:** A policy requiring re-review after *every* amount update could produce different findings. That is not R4’s stated rule: a 4.55% increase and a decrease do not by themselves invalidate the respective reviews.
- **TX-309:** Alternative cumulative-versus-single-update calculations do not rescue the case. The decisive comparison is EUR 145,000 against the Enhanced_Review basis of EUR 118,000, which is itself greater than 20%.
- **TX-307:** Only evidence that the approval actually concerned a distinct, relevant enhanced review could cure the sequence problem. Treating a Standard_Review or a standard-packet approval as an Enhanced_Review would contradict the supplied semantic facts unless a different policy interpretation were explicitly adopted.

def amount_review_status(review, between):
    """
    T: no invalidating amount increase detected
    F: definitely invalidated under the >20% rule
    U: an amount comparison from an unknown basis is decisive

    """
    basis = review["state_amount"]
    unknown = False
    messages = []
    for _, event in between.iterrows():
        if not event["_amount_update"]:
            continue
        new_amount = event["state_amount"]
        if pd.isna(basis) or pd.isna(new_amount):
            unknown = True
            messages.append(f"unknown amount materiality at {row_time(event)}")
        elif float(new_amount) > 1.20 * float(basis):
            pct = (float(new_amount) / float(basis) - 1.0) * 100.0
            messages.append(
                f"{basis:g}->{new_amount:g} ({pct:.2f}%) at {row_time(event)}"
            )
            return False, messages
    return (None if unknown else True), messages

The `Enhanced_Review < Compliance_Approve < Release_Funds` check illustrates what a trace-order or DECLARE-style check can capture. It does not establish whether R2 was triggered, whether the review remained valid after a data change, or whether an exemption was genuinely approved in time. Accordingly, `REVIEW_REQUIRED` is intentionally used where human evidential judgment is necessary.

## 6. Why Control-Flow-Only Conformance Is Insufficient

1. **Final-state leakage:** A model using final case attributes can evaluate the wrong release or review state. TX-305’s settlement amount and TX-311’s final DE value must not be copied backward.
2. **Post-hoc exemptions:** An activity sequence can show `Release_Funds` followed by `Record_Exemption`, but cannot determine whether timely approval actually existed. TX-302 and TX-310 have similar control flow but materially different evidence.
3. **Material changes after review:** The sequence `Enhanced_Review  Update_Amount  Release_Funds` is not uniformly compliant or noncompliant. TX-306’s increase is non-invalidating, TX-308’s is a decrease, and TX-309’s increase invalidates the review.
4. **Conditional activation:** R1 and R2 do not apply to every release. Identical activity sequences can have different outcomes depending on amount, jurisdiction, risk, and customer type. TX-308 also shows why the inclusive EUR 100,000 threshold matters.
5. **Missing data:** Control-flow conformance normally treats an activity sequence as either matching or not matching. It cannot safely distinguish “amount absent” from “amount below threshold.” TX-305 requires three-valued reasoning.
6. **Same-timestamp ordering:** Timestamp-only analysis can lose the order of TX-311’s review and jurisdiction update. Even where the final classification is unchanged, the review snapshot and material-change analysis can be wrong.
7. **Activity-type semantics:** A Standard_Review is not an Enhanced_Review. Likewise, the mere presence of Compliance_Approve does not prove that it approved the relevant enhanced packet, as TX-307 demonstrates.
8. **Cross-event data dependencies:** R3 depends on the relationship among a screen, an external list update, jurisdiction, and release. “A Sanctions_Screen exists somewhere in the trace” is insufficient.
9. **Exemption scope and evidence quality:** Control flow does not evaluate amount ceilings, jurisdiction/customer scope, expiry, rule coverage, signature time, certificate creation, or witness independence.
10. **Review-basis semantics:** Conformance can establish that a review occurred before release but not which amount or jurisdiction it reviewed. That distinction is central to R4 and to meaningful audit evidence.

## 7. Recommended Logging and Control Improvements

- **Immutable release payloads:** At each release, store a signed or hashed snapshot containing amount, currency, risk, jurisdiction, customer type, state/version identifiers, and the rule version evaluated. Link settlement to release without treating settlement as a substitute for the release payload.
- **Explicit change provenance:** For every attribute change, log `change_id`, old value, new value, event time, effective time, record time, source system, actor, reason, and correction/supersession relationships. Distinguish “unknown,” “feed unavailable,” and “not applicable” from blank cells.
- **Review input snapshots:** Give each review a `review_id`, type, completion time, outcome, reviewer, input-snapshot hash, amount basis, jurisdiction/risk/customer scope, and findings. Link subsequent material changes and any invalidation/re-review decision to that ID.
- **Approval linkage:** Compliance_Approve should identify the exact review and packet approved, the decision, approver, approval time, and signature evidence. A standard-packet approval should be structurally distinguishable from enhanced-review approval.
- **Sanctions versioning:** Log list name/version, affected jurisdiction, list effective time, publication and record times, and the list version used by each screen. A pre-release control should require a screen newer than every applicable list update.
- **Exemption evidence model:** Store structured scope, waived rules, effective/expiry times, approval event, approver, qualified signature time, certificate creation time, evidence hashes, record time, and late-entry reason. Witness evidence should identify the witness, relationship to the transaction, and corroboration.
- **Stable ordering:** Use immutable event IDs and case sequence numbers, unique `event_index` values within equal timestamps, high-precision UTC timestamps, and causal links such as `caused_by` or `supersedes`.
- **Rule and evaluation versioning:** Log the policy/rule version, threshold configuration, evaluation time, code version, and resulting trigger/obligation diagnostics so later audits can reproduce the decision.
- **Preventive release controls:** Evaluate obligations synchronously before release. Block or escalate when decisive state is unknown, a review has been materially invalidated, a sanctions rescreen is missing, or exemption evidence is contested.
- **Separate substantive validity from control operation:** Late evidence may subsequently prove that an exemption was substantively valid, while the inability to verify it at release remains a separate control weakness. Both facts should be retained rather than collapsed into one timestamp or label.
