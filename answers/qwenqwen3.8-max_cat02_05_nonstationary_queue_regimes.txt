def single_mean_shift_split(x, min_len=3):
 x=np.asarray(x,float); n=len(x); best=None
 overall_sd=x.std(ddof=0)
 if overall_sd ==0: return None
 for i in range(min_len, n-min_len):
   left=x[:i]; right=x[i:]
   score=abs(left.mean()-right.mean())/overall_sd
   if best is None or score>best['score']: best={'split_index':i,'date':df['date'].iloc[i] if i<n else None,'left_mean':left.mean(),'right_mean':right.mean(),'score':score}
 return best

def single_mean_shift_split(series, min_len=3):
    x = np.asarray(series, dtype=float)
    n = len(x)

    if sd == 0 or n < 2*min_len:

    best = None

# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Operating-window convention and estimands

The supplied rows are daily aggregates for a 12-hour opening window, 07:00–19:00. I use the following estimands and units:

- **Arrival intensity**:  
  \(\lambda_a\) = arrivals per opening day or arrivals per opening hour.  
  Since the center is open 12 hours, `arrivals / 12` gives a crude hourly arrival rate for a day. This is only a daily-average rate; it does not describe intraday peaks.

- **Completion intensity / throughput**:  
  \(\lambda_c\) = completed cases per opening day or hour. Completed cases may have arrived on a previous day, so completions are a departure measure, not necessarily same-day demand service.

- **Service-time behavior**:  
  `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min` are active handling times, excluding waiting. They are quantiles, not means. The mean active service time \(E[S]\) is not supplied.

- **WIP**:  
  `mean_wip` is the mean number of cases in the system from 15-minute snapshots. The unit is cases. It is not stated whether snapshots cover only opening hours or also overnight carry-over. This matters for Little’s Law.

- **Capacity**:  
  `staff_fte` is mean concurrently available full-time-equivalent capacity. I treat 1 FTE as 60 available staff-minutes per opening hour when forming rough capacity-pressure estimates. This is an assumption, because FTE may include breaks, non-case work, or other availability constraints.

- **Case mix and concentration**:  
  `complex_pct` is the percentage of arrivals following a multi-step complex variant. `peak_08_10_pct` is a coarse intraday concentration measure. The representative hourly vectors give additional intraday shape, but they are only four days and do not fully reconcile with the daily `peak_08_10_pct` field.

## Labeled assumptions

- **A1. Opening-hours clock**: Unless stated otherwise, rates are expressed per 12-hour opening day. If cases carry overnight, calendar-time flow times will be longer than opening-hours flow times.

- **A2. Service quantiles as planning proxies**: Because mean service time is absent, I use the median as a lower planning proxy and the p90 as an upper planning proxy for “typical high-side” load. This is not a mathematical bound; it is a pragmatic assumption needed because only quantiles are supplied.

- **A3. FTE is usable case-handling capacity**: I assume `staff_fte` can be compared with offered case work in staff-minutes. This is needed for rough capacity-pressure estimates.

- **A4. Representative hourly vectors are indicative**: The four hourly vectors are treated as illustrative of regime shape, not as a full sample from the arrival/completion process.

- **A5. Intervention date is treated as 2026-04-29**: The intervention began during that day, but the exact time is unknown. Therefore 2026-04-29 is treated as a transition/intervention day at daily resolution.

## Threats to naive inference

At least five important threats are present:

1. **Intraday nonstationarity hidden by daily aggregates**  
   Daily averages hide peaks, outage windows, and bursty arrival behavior. A pooled queueing model using daily averages would assume a stationary arrival rate that is not supported by the representative hourly vectors.

2. **Median and p90 service times are not mean service times**  
   Queueing capacity formulas usually require mean service time or at least a defensible estimate of it. Substituting median service time for mean service time would understate load if the service-time distribution is right-skewed. The p90 can be used as a conservative planning proxy but not as a direct mean estimate.

3. **Carry-over backlog and unknown boundary conditions**  
   The backlog at 07:00 is not supplied, cases can carry overnight, and completed cases need not have arrived on the same day. Therefore arrivals and completions cannot be treated as equal except in approximately stable regimes.

4. **Multiple structural changes and regimes**  
   The data contain an outage on 2026-04-15, a recovery period, a large case-mix or coding change around 2026-04-20, and a staffing intervention beginning during 2026-04-29. Pooling these periods violates stationarity and can produce misleading average waiting, throughput, and capacity estimates.

5. **Day-of-week composition effects**  
   Weekdays and weekends have materially different arrivals, staffing, and WIP. A pooled average is dominated by weekdays but can still mask weekday overload and weekend underload or vice versa.

6. **Representative vectors are sparse and partially inconsistent**  
   The four representative hourly vectors are not enough to define a stochastic arrival process. Also, the hourly vectors do not exactly reconcile with `peak_08_10_pct`; for example, the 2026-04-21 vector has a large 10:00–11:00 burst, while the daily field says 47% of arrivals were in 08:00–09:59. This suggests either a definitional mismatch or that the vector is only representative, not literal.

7. **Daily observations are not independent**  
   WIP, backlog, case mix, and staffing create serial dependence. Treating daily rows as independent samples would understate uncertainty.

## Notes on outage and recovery treatment

The outage should not be treated as a normal high-WIP queueing regime. During the approximate 11:00–14:00 window on 2026-04-15, arrivals continued while completions stopped. That creates a backlog shock. The following days, especially 2026-04-16 and 2026-04-17, show completions exceeding arrivals, which is consistent with backlog burn-down. Therefore their high completion counts are not direct evidence of normal sustainable capacity.

The staffing intervention beginning during 2026-04-29 is also confounded with recovery. Completions exceed arrivals and WIP declines, but that can occur because extra capacity is applied to an existing backlog. Once the backlog is cleared, completion intensity may fall toward arrival intensity.

Four hourly vectors cannot establish a full stochastic arrival or service model because:

- They cover only four days out of 28.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law, \(L = \lambda W\), can be applied only when the window is stable or when boundary inventory changes are accounted for. Here:

- \(L\) is approximated by `mean_wip`.
- \(\lambda\) is a throughput proxy, preferably completions per opening day when the system is approximately stable.
- \(W\) is implied flow time.
- I use an opening-hours clock: one daily throughput unit corresponds to 12 opening hours. Thus implied opening-hours flow time is  
  \[
  W_{\text{opening hours}} = \frac{L}{\lambda_{\text{per opening day}}} \times 12.
  \]
- The implied flow time is total system time, including waiting and possibly overnight carry-over. It is not active service time. Median service time must not be substituted for this quantity.

# 7. Operational implications

## Staffing by regime

- **Baseline weekdays**: Approximately 6 FTE appears consistent with roughly balanced arrivals and completions, but the true pressure depends on the unknown mean service time. The median-based load looks moderate, while the p90-proxy load suggests limited slack.
- **Baseline weekends**: 2.5 FTE appears adequate for the low-complexity weekend demand, but the p90-proxy load suggests limited slack for long cases.
- **High-complexity weekdays before intervention**: 6 FTE is likely insufficient under the post-2026-04-20 mix. Arrivals are about 114/day, completions remain around 100/day, and WIP rises.
- **High-complexity weekends**: The pre-intervention weekend with 2.5 FTE shows completions below arrivals and rising WIP. The intervention weekend with 3.0 FTE looks more balanced.
- **Intervention weekdays**: 6.5–7.0 FTE coincides with completions exceeding arrivals and WIP declining. This supports the hypothesis that additional capacity or focused backlog work helped, but it does not prove that 7 FTE is the long-run steady-state requirement.

## Backlog recovery

Recovery periods should be managed as distinct operational states:

- Do not treat completion rates of 120–129/day as normal sustainable capacity.
- Track opening backlog, closing backlog, and WIP trend explicitly.
- Consider temporary dedicated recovery capacity rather than assuming ordinary capacity will clear the backlog.
- Prioritization rules may matter: clearing simple cases can reduce WIP quickly, but complex cases may be the source of customer impact.

## Peak smoothing

The bursty post-mix representative vector shows that a large share of arrivals can occur in a small number of hours. Even if daily utilization is acceptable, hourly bursts can create long waits.

Possible actions:

- Use appointments or scheduled arrival windows where feasible.
- Add flexible surge coverage during known burst windows.
- Use intraday dashboards rather than daily averages for staffing decisions.
- Validate the definition of `peak_08_10_pct`, because the representative hourly vectors do not fully reconcile with it.

## Data-collection recommendations

The current aggregates are insufficient for a definitive queueing model. Useful additions would include:

1. Event-level case IDs.
2. Arrival timestamps.
3. Service start and finish timestamps.
4. Variant label: simple versus complex.
5. Resource or staff identifier.
6. Opening and closing WIP each day.
7. Exact outage start/end times.
8. Exact intervention start/end times.
9. Reason codes for complex-case coding changes.
10. Snapshot timing definition: opening hours only or 24 hours.

With event-level data, one could build a proper event log and use process-mining tools to estimate variant-specific service times, waiting times, rework, and throughput.

## Policy that looks beneficial under pooled averages but risky under segmented view

A pooled view might support the following policy:

> “Average arrivals are 90/day and average completions are 86/day, so we can reduce staffing toward the pooled average and rely on the overall average completion rate.”

This looks financially attractive under pooled averages but is risky under the segmented view:

- Weekday high-complexity demand is about 114/day, not 90/day.
- High-complexity service quantiles are much longer than the pooled median.
- The pooled completion average is depressed by the outage and inflated by recovery days.
- Cutting staff based on pooled averages would likely rebuild backlog during high-mix weekday periods.

A related risky policy would be:

> “Use the intervention-period completion rate of about 121 weekday cases/day as the new normal service-level promise.”

That is also risky because the intervention period includes backlog burn-down. Once backlog is cleared, sustainable completions may fall closer to arrivals unless the higher capacity is maintained.

    Fano factor = variance / mean. CV = standard deviation / mean.

# 5. Transparent change-point / segmentation heuristic
#    This is a simple single mean-shift split, not ground truth.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------

def single_mean_shift_split(series, min_len=3):
    """
    For each possible split, compute the absolute difference between
    left and right means, scaled by the overall standard deviation.
    Return the split with the largest score.
    """
    x = np.asarray(series, dtype=float)
    n = len(x)
    sd = x.std(ddof=0)

    if sd == 0 or n < 2 * min_len:
        return None

    best = None
    for i in range(min_len, n - min_len):
        left = x[:i]
        right = x[i:]
        score = abs(left.mean() - right.mean()) / sd

        if best is None or score > best["score"]:
            best = {
                "split_index": i,
                "split_date": daily["date"].iloc[i],
                "left_mean": left.mean(),
                "right_mean": right.mean(),
                "score": score,
            }

    return best

    if b is not None:
