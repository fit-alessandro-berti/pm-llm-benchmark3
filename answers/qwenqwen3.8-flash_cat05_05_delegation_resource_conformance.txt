def network_to_df(net):
    if hasattr(net, 'get_edge_list'):
        rows = []
        for u, v in net.get_edge_list():
            try: w = net.get_edge_weight(u, v)
            except Exception: w = 1
            rows.append((u,v,w))
        return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=['from','to','weight'])
    if isinstance(net, dict):
        rows=[]
        for a, b in net.items():
            if isinstance(b, dict):
                for c, w in b.items(): rows.append((a,c,w))
            else:
                rows.append((a,'',b))
        return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=['from','to','weight'])
    return pd.DataFrame(columns=['from','to','weight'])

## 2. Rule Interpretation

**RR1 — Drafter/approver separation**

For each case, after alias normalization and service-account attribution, the human performing `Draft_Change_Request` must differ from the human performing `Approve_Change`.

- If both activities are attributable to known humans and they are the same human: violation.
- If one activity is attributable only to an unattributed service account: conformance cannot be proven; classify as identity/data problem or indeterminate.
- Closing by the drafter is not covered by RR1, but it may be a control weakness.

**RR2 — Implementer/verifier separation**

For each case, the human performing `Implement_Change` must differ from the human performing `Verify_Implementation`.

- A service-account verification passes only if reliable session evidence attributes it to a human who is not the implementer.
- Workstation ownership, badge entry to a shared room, or absence of CCTV is not enough to attribute a service-account action to a specific human.
- If the verifier is unattributed, RR2 is not technically satisfied even if the implementer is known.

**RR3 — High-risk approval authority**

For non-emergency HIGH-risk `Approve_Change` events, the human actor must have either:

1. standing QM authority at the event timestamp, or  
2. a valid QM delegation at the event timestamp.

A valid delegation requires all of the following:

- The delegate is the human actor.
- The event timestamp is inside the delegation’s effective interval.
- The case scope is inside the delegation scope.
- If the delegation requires the principal to be absent or formally unavailable, that condition is evidenced.
- No prohibited subdelegation occurred.
- The evidence is reliable enough to establish the delegation existed at event time.

**RR4 — Emergency substitution**

For HIGH-risk emergency changes:

- `Approve_Change` may be performed by the standing IC or a valid IC delegate.
- `Implement_Change` may follow that approval if the emergency exception is otherwise justified.
- `Retrospective_QM_Review` must be performed by a human with standing QM authority or a valid QM delegate whose scope covers retrospective QM review for that case.
- The review must occur within two business days.

**Business-day counting for RR4**

I count business days as Monday through Friday, excluding site holidays. The site calendar had no holiday in the period, and 2026-08-15 and 2026-08-16 were weekend days.

Conservative rule: the due date is the end of the second business day after the emergency approval/implementation date.

- Example: emergency approval/implementation on Tuesday 2026-08-11:
  - first business day: Wednesday 2026-08-12;
  - second business day: Thursday 2026-08-13;
  - due: 2026-08-13 23:59 UTC.
- A review on Friday 2026-08-14 is therefore late under this interpretation. If the organization counted inclusively, the due date would be even earlier, not later.

**Action by a principal while on leave**

Annual leave does not automatically erase a standing role unless the role interval or policy says so. If Asha, the standing QM, personally performed an approval while on leave, RR3 may be technically satisfied because she is QM at event time.

However, this creates a control objective problem:

- Delegation design assumes the principal is absent.
- If the principal returns or acts during leave, the delegation should be suspended or reconciled.
- If identity evidence is weak, the action may be unauthorized even though the role assignment is valid.

Thus, standing-role action during leave can be technically authorized but still require identity and availability investigation.

**Later database entry proving an earlier delegation**

A database record created after the event cannot, by itself, prove that a delegation existed at event time, because it may be a migration backfill or late entry.

A later database entry may support event-time validity only if there is independent pre-event evidence, such as:

- a cryptographically signed PDF with a trusted timestamp before the event;
- a controlled approval workflow record with immutable audit trail;
- a registered delegation system entry created before the event;
- corroborating calendar, email, or incident records.

For D-QM-01, the signed PDF timestamp 2026-08-09 18:00 is pre-event evidence, but the QMS database record created 2026-08-12 09:15 during migration is an evidence-timing weakness. For CHG-501, the signed PDF may make the delegation valid, but the migration gap prevents high confidence without additional validation.

**RR5 — Identity evidence**

A service account is not a human identity. It can support conformance only if reliable session evidence maps the action to an authorized human.

Strong attribution examples:

- individual re-authentication;
- MFA/FIDO token tied to a person;
- badge plus terminal session ownership;
- immutable session logs.

Weak attribution examples:

- workstation normally assigned to a person;
- badge showing entry to a shared room;
- shared terminal without individual re-authentication;
- no CCTV or keyboard/session owner evidence.

**RR6 — Suspicion versus proof**

Repeated Clara/Ben pairings, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations, concentrated handovers, and leave conflicts are indicators. They are not proof of collusion by themselves. Collusion requires supporting evidence such as credential sharing, intentional bypass, documented benefit, instruction, override, or correlated identity anomalies.

**Network interpretation**

- Handover networks should show concentrated edges such as Clara  Ben, Clara  Diego, Diego  Eva, and possibly Ben  Diego/Eva. These are indicators of concentration, not proof of collusion.

- The critical question is not whether the pair appears often, but whether the pair’s actions were authorized, attributable, independent, and reviewed.

def network_to_df(net):
    if net is None:
        return pd.DataFrame(columns=["from", "to", "weight"])

    # Graph-like object
    if hasattr(net, "get_edge_list"):
        rows = []
        for u, v in net.get_edge_list():
            try:
                w = net.get_edge_weight(u, v)
            except Exception:
                w = 1
            rows.append((u, v, w))
        return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["from", "to", "weight"])

    # Dictionary-like object
    if isinstance(net, dict):
        rows = []
        for a, b in net.items():
            if isinstance(b, dict):
                for c, w in b.items():
                    rows.append((a, c, w))
            else:
                rows.append((a, "", b))
        return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["from", "to", "weight"])

    return pd.DataFrame(columns=["from", "to", "weight"])

## 6. Control Improvements

**Delegation creation**

- Require delegations to be created and approved in the QMS before the effective start time.
- Require a machine-readable delegation record with immutable audit trail, not only a signed PDF.
- Require explicit activity scope: for example, `Approve_Change`, `Retrospective_QM_Review`, or `Close_Change`; do not assume approval authority covers review.
- Require explicit case-scope mapping, such as production equipment, training documents, cooling-system incidents, or incident ID.
- Prohibit subdelegation by default unless the delegation record explicitly allows it and identifies the permitted subdelegation chain.

**Effective-time recording**

- Store delegation start/end as UTC timestamps with timezone validation.
- Prevent activation of delegations before their effective interval and automatic expiry at the end interval.
- Log every delegation activation, suspension, extension, and cancellation.
- Require migration imports to preserve original creation timestamps and signatures; flag backfilled records.

**Principal availability**

- Integrate leave, sick leave, and on-call calendars with delegation validity.
- Automatically suspend delegations when the principal returns or becomes available.
- Require a formal exception when a principal acts while on leave.
- Treat principal absence as an evidence condition, not merely a policy statement.

**Alias management**

- Maintain a governed alias table mapping user IDs, email aliases, and service identities to a single human identity.
- Require alias changes to be approved and auditable.
- Run segregation-of-duties checks on normalized human IDs, not raw user IDs.
- Flag cases where two aliases appear in the same case for the same activity type.

**Individual authentication**

- Eliminate shared service accounts for human accountability activities such as approval, implementation, and verification.
- Require individual re-authentication for high-risk actions.
- Use FIDO/MFA, session binding, and terminal-level session ownership.
- Where shared workstations are unavoidable, require badge plus individual login, not badge alone.
- Retain keyboard/session owner logs for verification and approval actions.

**Emergency review**

- Require emergency changes to include a structured safety rationale, incident ID, and approver role at creation.
- Automatically calculate two-business-day review due dates using the site calendar.
- Require retrospective QM review to be performed by standing QM or a delegation whose scope explicitly includes retrospective review.
- Escalate overdue reviews and block closure until resolved or formally waived.
- Require independent review of emergency approvals, especially when the approver is also the drafter.

**Pair rotation**

- Implement pair rotation for drafting, approval, implementation, and verification where feasible.
- Track concentration metrics: drafter/approver pairs, implementer/verifier pairs, handover density, and working-together frequency.
- Trigger review when a pair exceeds thresholds, but treat thresholds as indicators, not proof.
- Require independent QM or compliance review for repeated HIGH-risk pairs during absence periods.

**Network monitoring**

- Run handover and working-together networks periodically, not only after incidents.
- Monitor changes in network concentration, especially during leave or delegation periods.
- Correlate network spikes with delegation records, leave records, and service-account usage.
- Use network metrics to prioritize sampling, not to conclude collusion automatically.

**Evidential retention**

- Retain signed delegation artifacts, QMS records, session logs, badge logs, CCTV where lawful, VPN/MFA logs, and IP-to-person mappings for at least the investigation window.
- Preserve original timestamps and hashes for signed PDFs and database records.
- Create a single evidence timeline per case linking role, delegation, session, and activity.
- For migration events, retain both migration audit logs and original source artifacts.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

The apparent nonconformance is mixed, not uniformly misconduct. A meaningful portion is genuine authority failure: CHG-502, CHG-507, CHG-508, and especially CHG-510 are clear technical violations of segregation or approval authority. CHG-505 is also nonconforming because the emergency exception fails on retrospective QM review authority and timeliness, even if Farid’s approval may have been valid under D-IC-03.

Another portion is identity/data quality rather than proven misconduct: CHG-503 is a service-account attribution failure; CHG-506 is an unresolved human-account/credential issue during sick leave; CHG-504 is technically authorized if Asha personally acted, but identity and leave controls are weak. CHG-501 is probably a valid substitution with a migration/evidence-timing defect. CHG-509 is probably a valid delegation, but principal absence and approver/verifier overlap require confirmation.

Organizational-pattern risk is real but not conclusive. The Clara/Ben concentration, reciprocal training approvals, shared workstations, and emergency subdelegation attempt create a coherent investigation hypothesis, but they do not prove collusion without session attribution, credential-sharing evidence, or documented intent/benefit.

The three highest-priority investigations are:

1. **CHG-510 emergency authority bypass**: Clara drafted and approved a HIGH emergency change using an invalid subdelegation, and Ben performed retrospective review without proper scope. This is the clearest authority failure.
2. **CHG-503 and CHG-506 identity/credential integrity**: CHG-503 has an unattributed service-account verification on Diego’s workstation; CHG-506 has Ben’s account used during sick leave while Ben denies the action. These require session, MFA, badge, IP, device, and CCTV evidence.
3. **D-QM-01 and emergency retrospective review controls**: The signed-PDF/migration gap affects CHG-501 and potentially CHG-503/506, while CHG-505 shows that retrospective review authority and business-day timeliness are not enforced. This is a systemic delegation and emergency-review control failure.
