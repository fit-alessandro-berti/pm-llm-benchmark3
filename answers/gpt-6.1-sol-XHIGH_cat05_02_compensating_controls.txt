## 1. Control-Objective Decomposition

FEP-1 separates payment preparation from authorization so that a single person cannot cause an erroneous or fraudulent transfer without effective independent scrutiny. Different user IDs are evidence of separation, but do not establish independent judgment, exclusive credential ownership, or a substantive review. Conversely, an independent approval that can still stop settlement can protect the objective even if Prepare_Payment and Release_Payment have the same actor.

The relevant baseline is an effective second human authorization, with access to the final payment details and a real ability to reject the transfer. It is not an assumption that any two-person process eliminates fraud.

| Objective or threat scenario | AV-2 | MG-4 | RA-7 | ED-1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prevent a lone operator from authorizing their own unauthorized payment | Prevents only transactions failing its specified checks; a plausible-looking fraudulent payment can pass. It supplies no independent human authorization. | Can prevent the transfer through an independent, substantive approval before release or an enforceable veto before settlement. | Detects an unauthorized transaction afterward; can correct only if intervention or recovery remains possible. | Permits a restricted emergency route with independent approval and timely audit. The token alone supplies no second judgment. |
| Prevent bank diversion and recent beneficiary changes | Checks account ownership and changes within seven days; blocking is preventive. An overridden warning weakens that protection. | Can compare final bank instructions with trusted vendor evidence and reject a change. Review of compromised evidence remains vulnerable. | Can detect diversion and initiate recall, subject to the bank's actual recovery window. | Excludes recent bank changes and requires an operational, clean AV-2 pass; it is unavailable when these conditions fail. |
| Prevent duplicates, operator-limit breaches, sanctions problems, and basic invoice/PO inconsistencies | Direct preventive coverage for these checks, provided the relevant configuration, inputs, and enforcement work. | Can independently challenge the invoice and authorization, but is not automatically a replacement for complete sanctions or duplicate screening. | Can detect missed or overridden exceptions, with correction dependent on reversibility. | Requires a clean AV-2 pass without override and an amount below EUR 100,000; it does not authorize bypassing these checks. |
| Prevent payment for nonexistent, unreceived, or commercially unreasonable goods/services | Does not establish receipt or commercial reasonableness. A basic PO match is insufficient. | A substantive review of invoice, bank details, and supporting receipt can prevent unsupported payment. Authentic-looking fabricated evidence may still defeat review. | Can detect false support or non-delivery through independent verification; a late finding does not recover the payment automatically. | Relies on the substantive manager review and audit. Emergency status does not establish that work occurred. |
| Prevent mistakes in amount, recipient, and payment instruction | Covers some inconsistencies and ownership, but a within-limit amount can still be wrong. | Can compare the final instruction with the invoice and supporting evidence before funds become irreversible. | Can detect errors and arrange cancellation, recall, or recovery while feasible. | Combines controls and restricts eligibility; it is not a guarantee against every input error. |
| Preserve actor accountability despite shared or compromised credentials | Does not establish who controlled a compromised account. | Counts only if the manager personally acts through attributable credentials and is genuinely independent. A preparer using a manager credential is not a second actor. | Can detect suspicious access and approval patterns if identity and credential logs are retained; it cannot recreate missing accountability. | Requires an independent manager, not merely a manager account name or token. |
| Resist collusion, conflicts of interest, and routine rubber-stamping | Does not detect collusion. | Independence, conflict assessment, and substantive challenge reduce exposure; a colluding or conflicted manager defeats the control. | Independent auditors can identify fabricated support or patterns, but audit coverage and timing matter. | Requires unresolved conflicts to be absent; does not itself detect hidden collusion. |
| Preserve a practical opportunity to stop or recover a defective emergency payment | Blocks before release when its covered checks fail. | Strongest before release; can still prevent settlement if a post-release approval has an effective stop mechanism. | Detective at review; partly corrective only when a finding leads to an achievable stop, recall, or recovery. | Requires approval before settlement and audit within one business day. Those are separate conditions; timely audit does not necessarily fall within a 24-hour recall window. |

## 2. Equivalence and Sufficiency Test

Assess the package against the relevant threats, the payment's actual circumstances, and an effective independent second authorization. Apply the following test in order:

1. **Establish the decision and exposure points.** Identify preparation, validation, release, manager review, bank settlement, audit start/completion, and actual recall deadlines. A review protects only against losses that remain avoidable when it occurs. Confirm whether an approver could stop the payment, rather than merely record approval.
2. **Verify identity and independence.** Identify the humans controlling each credential. For MG-4, verify L3-or-higher status, separation from the preparer/releaser, unresolved conflicts, and substantive review. A supervisory relationship warrants assessment; it does not, by itself, prove collusion or disqualification.
3. **Map coverage to the plausible loss mechanisms.** Identify which threats AV-2 actually tested and which require receipt verification, commercial scrutiny, or independent human judgment. Do not infer delivery, valid emergency authority, or absence of collusion from a clean automated pass.
4. **Check enforcement and overrideability.** Establish whether validation and approval were mandatory, whether warnings could be overridden, who actually exercised the override, and whether the final payment details changed after review. An approval that can be bypassed is weaker than an enforced gate.
5. **Assess detection and correction separately.** Determine audit completeness, independence, completion time, findings, and ability to act. Use bank-confirmed cancellation/recall capability, not the existence of an Audit_Review event, as corrective evidence. After settlement, reliable internal cancellation is normally unavailable.
6. **Assess evidence quality and adversarial resistance.** Require attributable approvals, trustworthy timestamps, validation results, authentic supporting documents, and coverage of all relevant payments. Consider compromised accounts, fabricated receipts, and collusion. Neither multiple recorded activities nor a correct eventual payment establishes control effectiveness.
7. **Apply permission conditions and residual-risk acceptance.** Evaluate all seven ED-1 conditions conjunctively. A late token, override, recent bank change, excessive amount, or unavailable AV-2 cannot be cured by a legitimate outcome. Any tolerability decision outside an established permission needs a separate, documented basis.

| Sufficiency level | Decision standard |
|---|---|
| Fully equivalent for the assessed objective | Trustworthy evidence shows materially comparable protection against the relevant threats: independent substantive authorization before irreversible exposure, enforceable controls over the final instruction, adequate covered checks, and credible response capacity. Equivalence concerns the specified objective, not immunity to all fraud. Formal FEP-1 or ED-1 compliance must still be reported separately. |
| Partially equivalent | The package preserves significant elements of independent authorization or prevention, but leaves a material coverage, enforcement, timing, or evidence gap. It can support a limited tolerability decision only if the remaining exposure and authority for acceptance are explicit. |
| Merely mitigating | Controls reduce some errors or losses, but do not replace the missing independent authorization. Examples include AV-2 alone, after-settlement ratification, or audit with uncertain recovery. |
| Ineffective for the material threat | The relevant causal path remains substantially open: controls are absent, bypassed, compromised, too late, or irrelevant to that threat. A legitimate payment can nevertheless have an ineffective package. |

Missing decisive evidence produces an indeterminate judgment rather than proof of either effectiveness or failure.

“Same expected loss” is not the only standard. Similar averages can conceal very different catastrophic-loss exposure, unauthorized discretion, collusion vulnerability, accountability, or inability to recover. Some requirements are mandatory constraints rather than risks that can be traded away. A preventive independent veto and a promise of eventual reimbursement also protect different interests, even if someone estimates the same average monetary loss.

For timing below, release plus 24 hours is a conservative **screening proxy** for recall, not a bank-confirmed deadline. The stated bank settlement time ends the ordinary internal preventive window. A subsequent bank recall may still be possible, but needs evidence. “Within one business day” also needs the organization's business calendar and deadline convention; AP-201 is particularly sensitive to that definition, and its recorded audit time is a start, not proven completion.

## 4. Preventive–Detective–Corrective Comparison

I classify a control by the harm it can still avert, rather than solely by its event name. “Release” and irreversible settlement are separate boundaries in these cases.

| Control/action | Preventive contribution | Detective contribution | Corrective contribution | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AV-2 clean validation or block before release | Blocks covered defects; a clean pass supports only the specified checks | Identifies covered exceptions before release | A block avoids transfer; it is not recovery of an already completed loss | No receipt, commercial-reasonableness, credential-control, or collusion assurance; warnings may be bypassed |
| Independent substantive MG-4 before release | Direct second authorization and ability to reject | Finds questionable invoice, beneficiary, or support during review | Can require correction before release | Requires qualifying independence, review quality, and enforceable linkage to final instructions |
| MG-4 after release but before settlement | Prevents irreversible payment if the manager can actually stop or veto settlement | Detects defects in an already released instruction | Can cancel or amend the pending transfer | Hybrid: detective relative to release, preventive relative to loss, with a corrective action on the pending instruction. Without a usable veto, it is only detective/ratifying. |
| MG-4 after settlement | Does not prevent the original transfer | May identify a defective payment or document a concern | May initiate separately feasible recall/recovery | Cannot retrospectively supply independent preventive authorization |
| RA-7 completed promptly | Does not normally prevent the initial release; may avert settlement if reviewed exceptionally early | Independently detects defects in emergency payments | Recall/cancellation is partly corrective only when available and pursued successfully | An audit event, no finding, or next-business-day timing does not establish recoverability |
| RA-7 after the ordinary recall window | No preventive contribution to the original transfer | Detects historical defects and supports investigation | Recovery through other routes may be possible but uncertain | Useful learning and accountability do not neutralize the original exposure |
| ED-1 | Restricts who may use an emergency route and under what safeguards | Requires complete, timely emergency audit | Requires a package with possible intervention/recovery opportunities | Permission, not an independent verification mechanism; all conditions must hold |

Thus m04 and m15 could provide meaningful preventive protection against settlement despite acting after Release_Payment. That judgment depends on an effective stop mechanism. m09 and m21 acted too late to provide that protection. The same Manager_Ratify activity can therefore have materially different control value across cases.

## 6. Evidence and Monitoring Recommendations

Collect evidence that can resolve the material judgments, rather than accumulating more activity counts:

- **Human identity and approval authority:** link preparation, release, overrides, and manager actions to individual sessions and credential owners. Obtain manager L3 status, conflict assessments, and evidence of personal action. Investigate m09 credential use without presuming how access was obtained.
- **Substantive review and authentic receipt:** retain the final invoice, beneficiary details, receipt/service evidence, reviewer challenges, and decisions. Authenticate AP-204's service evidence and establish whether work occurred. A clean AV-2 result must never populate a “receipt verified” field automatically.
- **Enforcement and immutability:** demonstrate validation configuration and coverage, payment holds, manager veto capability, and binding of approvals to the final payment version. Log changes after validation/approval and require renewed checks where relevant.
- **Emergency authority:** preserve token creation, activation, expiry, authorized user/scope, and trustworthy clock information. For AP-206, distinguish an earlier actual activation from delayed logging; outage documentation alone is insufficient. Verify vendor age, amount, bank-change history, clean AV-2, qualifying manager approval, and completed audit for every ED-1 candidate.
- **Bank and audit evidence:** record settlement, payment-rail-specific cancellation/recall cutoffs, audit start and completion, scope, findings, escalation, recall requests, bank responses, and amounts recovered. Confirm the business-day convention and holiday calendar. Review timely audit completion and timely recovery opportunity separately.

| Monitoring rule | Measure or trigger | Interpretation and response |
|---|---|---|
| Same-user rate | Completed payments with the same accountable preparer/releaser divided by all completed payments; also show amounts and breakdown by emergency eligibility | Exclude blocked attempts from the completed-payment denominator. Separate evidenced ED-1 use from unapproved exceptions and unresolved cases. Investigate routine convenience use. |
| Override rate | Overrides divided by warning cases, with a separate count/rate among completed payments; segment by actor, credential owner, warning type, and exposure | Escalate overrides by the preparer using another person's credential, particularly recent bank changes and operator limits. An authorized override still needs substantive independent evidence. |
| Late manager approval | Separately flag review after release, after settlement, missing approval, and inability to stop settlement; measure delays | Post-release/pre-settlement review may be effective under ED-1. Post-settlement approval does not provide equivalent prevention. |
| Audit-before-recall-window | Percentage of required emergency audits completed before the bank-confirmed cutoff, alongside the one-business-day SLA | Use completion, not audit start. Show reviews meeting the business-day SLA but missing recall, and record whether corrective action was still feasible. |
| Repeated resource pairs | Pair frequency, concentration, payment mix, and defect association over a defined rolling window | The embedded log contains two u12/u13 payments; the supplied wider history reports 37 in 30 days. Seek cell-design evidence and compare with peer patterns before drawing conclusions. |
| Shared workstations | Same-device/session activity, overlapping login use, and credential reuse, correlated with repeated pairs | A shared workstation can be normal. Session evidence may instead expose account sharing or bypassed independence. Treat as a signal, not proof. |
| Bank-change proximity | Flag changes within seven days, short change-to-payment intervals, and discrepancies between verified and released details | ED-1 becomes ineligible for recent changes. Require trusted ownership confirmation and independent review; prioritize changed-bank overrides. |
| Control outages | Any release while AV-2 is unavailable, partially operational, or returning stale/unverifiable results | Block ED-1 use. Hold affected payments or route them through a separately authorized, evidenced process meeting the relevant objectives. An unsigned checklist is insufficient. |
| Coverage and threshold manipulation | Missing receipts, incomplete validation, splitting near limits, repeated emergency use, and modification after approval | Detect routes around the safeguards, rather than merely checking whether named events exist. |

An alert is a reason to examine evidence, not proof of misconduct. The AP-204 fabricated receipt is an established document defect; pairing and workstation patterns are suspicion indicators. AP-202 establishes serious bypass and unrecovered loss, while the participation and intent of particular people remain separate investigatory questions. Conversely, absence of an alert or audit finding is not proof that receipt, independence, or authorization requirements were met.

def case_ids(result):

        return set(result[CASE].astype(str))

            raise RuntimeError("Unrecognized PM4Py trace identifier.")
        ids.add(str(value))
    return ids
