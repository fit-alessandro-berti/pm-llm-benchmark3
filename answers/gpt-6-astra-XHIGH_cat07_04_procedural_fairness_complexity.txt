# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness asks whether applicants receive justified, accessible, consistent, and reasonably timely treatment, with a meaningful opportunity to correct errors and challenge decisions. It does not require identical paths or identical approval rates. A necessary support step can preserve a right while its implementation creates an avoidable disadvantage.

| Dimension | Assessment from the evidence |
|---|---|
| Outcome parity | Approval rates range from 58% for D to 78% for A. Relative to A, the raw differences are −15 percentage points for B, −9 for C, and −20 for D. These are descriptive disparities, not evidence by themselves of discrimination. |
| Adjusted outcome disparity | The preliminary model reports approval differences of −5.5 points for B, −2.2 for C, and −3.1 for D. Measured case mix accounts statistically for some of the raw differences, but residual disparities warrant investigation. Adjustment does not establish causation or make the included covariates neutral. |
| Procedural burden | Additional document requests, repeated checks, handovers, contacts, and escalations impose time and effort on applicants. B has adjusted excess probabilities of document requests and supervisor review of 9.5 and 8.0 points; C has excesses of 4.2 and 5.5 points. Whether these reflect necessary assistance, inconsistent demands, or avoidable duplication is unresolved. |
| Timeliness | Relative to A, raw median cycle times are 5.6 days longer for B, 7.8 for C, and 4.0 for D. The corresponding 90th-percentile gaps are 12.8, 17.4, and 8.1 days. Adjusted cycle-time differences remain +2.6, +4.1, and +0.9 days. Timeliness must cover support-dependent applicants and long delays, not just the overall average. |
| Consistency of routing | Risk-triggered fraud review and need-triggered language or accommodation support can justify different paths. Fairness requires consistent triggers, appropriate exceptions, and comparable implementation for comparable needs. It also requires checking whether applicants receive all necessary support. |
| Quality/accessibility of support | A logged support contact does not establish that interpretation was effective or an accommodation was suitable. Assess communication accessibility, successful completion, applicant understanding, and whether support arrived in time to influence the process. |
| Appeal/rework burden | B, C, and D have higher raw appeal and rework rates than A. Appeals may reveal initial errors, unclear explanations, or successful access to challenge; low appeal rates can also reflect access barriers. Distinguish agency-caused rework from necessary updates and applicant-initiated changes. |
| Resource-allocation fairness | Concentration of B cases among seven bilingual workers and overloaded regional officers, and C cases among five specialists, raises questions about capacity relative to demand. Allocation should reflect needs and required expertise while delivering equitable service. Equal case counts per worker would not necessarily achieve this. |

Assess these dimensions together: an approval can follow an unnecessarily burdensome procedure, and a justified denial can follow a fair procedure. Resource shortages can create structural procedural disadvantage without deliberate differential treatment by an individual worker.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

**Define separate questions and endpoints.** Estimate both the total experienced disparity and differences among cases with comparable pre-existing needs. Then examine how routing and resource assignment contribute. Do not control away support queues and handovers in the only fairness analysis: these may be mechanisms of the disadvantage being assessed.

Separate submission-to-first-decision, submission-to-notification, approval-to-activation, appeal resolution, and final closure. Mixing appeals into the primary decision clock can make initial handling appear slower simply because more applicants appeal. Define rework episodes and count repeated requests, applicant contacts, and handovers per case. Report medians, upper quantiles, threshold exceedances, and uncertainty. The preliminary model's cycle-time estimand is unspecified; its adjusted differences cannot automatically be interpreted as adjusted median differences.

**Construct comparable cohorts.** Use submission cohorts with sufficient follow-up, retain open cases for appropriate censored analyses, and audit missing events, duplicate cases, timestamp ordering, and attribute consistency. Use baseline or decision-time snapshots as appropriate; final documentation status or a risk score revised after investigation would leak later process information into a baseline comparison. Derive month from submission time.

1. **Match, stratify, or standardize on relevant case mix.** Begin with risk band, baseline documentation completeness, household size, prior-case count, region, submission month, and channel. Add verified language-support need and accommodation need for comparisons within the relevant service population. Obtain finer measures of substantive eligibility and complexity where possible. Use exact/coarsened matching for key categorical criteria or flexible propensity/outcome models with interactions, followed by standardization to a stated common population.
2. **Check overlap and balance.** Report matched sample sizes, standardized differences, weights, and excluded cases. Group definitions and support needs may be strongly associated, leaving few comparable A applicants. Do not extrapolate confidently from A's small support-using population to all B or C cases. Within support-dependent cohorts, compare equivalent need, regions, queues, and policy targets even when a credible A comparator is unavailable. Report results for the overlap population as such.
3. **Distinguish covariates from mechanisms.** A useful working causal structure is applicant circumstances and earlier barriers → measured risk/documentation/channel → routing → assignment and handovers → delay and decision. Group-associated disadvantage may operate along several of these links. Channel, documentation, and region can reflect access barriers as well as case mix. Support receipt, realized variants, resource assignment, final outcomes, and appeals are generally downstream variables; controlling for them changes the question and can introduce selection bias.
4. **Separate disparity from discrimination.** Raw and adjusted contrasts establish associations under their definitions. An unexplained adjusted gap is not automatically discriminatory treatment, and a gap absorbed by potentially biased covariates is not automatically justified. Audit the correctness and consistent application of routing and decision criteria using appropriately qualified reviewers, alongside the statistical analysis. Causal claims require stronger identification assumptions or credible intervention evidence.

**Compare paths in two stages.** First estimate group differences in the probability of entering a route among cases with the same documented trigger—for example, fraud review within each risk band, or support provision among applicants who need it. Second compare duration, repetitions, handovers, and outcomes within exact variants or clinically/administratively comparable path families, using only additional information relevant to those comparisons. Report both between-route composition and within-route performance differences. These are descriptive decompositions unless causal assumptions are justified: conditioning on a realized variant can select cases on prior treatment or unmeasured difficulty.

Analyze rare, incomplete, and non-top variants as well as common ones. Paths ending at different milestones are not interchangeable. A path's presence does not establish that every step was necessary or properly performed.

**Compare resource assignment.** Within region, time, relevant qualification, support need, risk, and workload strata, estimate assignment to overloaded workers, specialist queues, and supervisor review. Measure case concentration, arrivals per available staff-hour, queue size, transfers, return transfers, and continuity of ownership. Compare similarly situated applicants within workers and similarly situated cases across workers. A model with resource effects can diagnose within-worker differences, but it may absorb unfair assignment into those effects; retain an analysis of assignment itself. Staffing, qualifications, leave, and simultaneous assignments are needed to distinguish expertise requirements from avoidable scarcity.

**Decompose elapsed time carefully.** For each case, separate active processing, queueing for the agency, applicant-response intervals, and other inactive periods where timestamps and status data support the distinction. Identify support and supervisor queues, repeated episodes, and parallel work. Applicant-response time may itself arise from inaccessible instructions or repeated demands and should not automatically be treated as applicant-caused delay.

With only one event timestamp, a transition duration is an elapsed inter-event gap, not a measured queue wait. True service and queue decomposition needs reliable start/completion events and preferably queue-entry/ready-for-work states. For parallel activities, distinguish total labor time from the union of active intervals used in a wall-clock decomposition. Decompose case-level durations before aggregating; the table's median intervals cannot be added to explain median cycle time.

**Run sensitivity and subgroup analyses.** Compare models with and without risk, documentation completeness, and channel, and with independently reviewed alternatives to those measures. Examine finer risk levels, scoring versions, missingness, documentation types, and support availability. These models answer different questions; they do not mechanically bracket a true causal effect. Test interactions between group and region/channel, and examine within-region and within-channel results plus results standardized to a common regional/channel distribution. Pay particular attention to the two backlogged regions and to intersections of language need and accommodation need. Sparse cells require explicit uncertainty and careful pooling.

Use confidence intervals and practically meaningful thresholds, with dependence handled at appropriate worker, office, and time levels where feasible. Avoid interpreting many subgroup tests without accounting for multiplicity. Assess alternative endpoint definitions, censoring, unmeasured severity, and eligibility criteria. The supplied tables provide neither uncertainty intervals nor sufficient joint distributions to perform these analyses; no new adjusted estimates can be calculated reliably from them alone.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Group B: language access and document handling are the strongest signals.** B follows V1 in 19% of cases versus A's 42%, V2 in 34% versus 24%, and V4 in 24% versus 1%. Incomplete documentation is more common in B, and required language support can legitimately add work. However, documentation completeness may already reflect language barriers, and an additional request is not necessarily justified merely because an application was initially incomplete.

B's reported completeness-to-document-request interval is 1.6 days versus A's 0.4 days. The reported language-support-to-eligibility interval is 3.8 days. Concentration among seven bilingual caseworkers, three overloaded regional officers, and repeated generalist → bilingual worker → generalist handovers provides a plausible capacity and coordination mechanism. Adjusted excess document requests, supervisor review, and cycle time strengthen the case for examining avoidable burdens. The data do not establish how much each mechanism contributes or whether particular requests were unwarranted.

**Group C: accommodation provision appears to be a major bottleneck.** C uses V5 in 31% of cases versus A's 2%; that path has a pooled median of 21.6 days. Accommodation need justifies specialist involvement. It does not by itself justify the reported 4.7-day accommodation-to-eligibility interval or repeated generalist → specialist → supervisor transfers. Only five specialists serve the concentrated demand, with two also performing supervisor review, suggesting competing responsibilities and limited capacity. C has the largest adjusted cycle-time difference, +4.1 days, and the highest raw rework rate, 24%. These indicate an important operational fairness concern even though C's adjusted approval difference is smaller than B's.

**Group D: risk-based routing explains a plausible part of the different process.** D has a 51% high-risk share versus A's 18%, and V3 represents 28% of D cases versus 9% of A cases. Under the stated policy, higher measured risk can legitimately require fraud review. The smaller adjusted cycle-time difference, +0.9 days, is consistent with measured case mix accounting for much of the raw delay difference; it does not prove the routing criterion is fair.

D's risk-to-fraud-review interval is 2.8 days versus A's 2.2 days. Compare these intervals within the same risk criteria, period, region, and queue conditions. Audit whether scoring and escalation are accurate and consistently applied. The adjusted appeal excess of 5.7 points—the largest reported—and approval deficit of 3.1 points warrant examination of decision reasons, false positives, and appeal outcomes. Neither high risk nor frequent prior cases is sufficient evidence that adverse decisions are correct.

**Shared routing issues require local analysis.** Supervisor-to-draft intervals are 1.9 days for B and 1.6 for C, compared with 1.0 for A; the two regional backlogs may explain some concentration. Submission-to-verification differences may partly reflect channel, but slower in-person service remains an accessibility issue when online access is constrained. Draft-to-notification differences are smaller, so the evidence points more strongly toward earlier support, documentation, and review stages. Tail delays remain important even at stages with modest median differences.

**The path summaries have important boundaries.** The displayed variants cover 82% of A, 99% of B, 98% of C, and 90% of D cases. Their medians are pooled, not group-specific, and several summaries stop before activation or closure. V4 stops before notification; that is not proof that notification was omitted. The paths may abbreviate activities, so missing risk screening or completeness checks in a summary cannot establish a policy violation. Support need shares cannot be compared directly with V4/V5 shares to infer unmet support, since other variants can contain those activities.

The supplied “step → step” intervals also need clarification: some named activities need not be directly consecutive, as V4 illustrates for language support and eligibility. Distinguish directly-follows edges from intervals spanning intervening work. Aggregate variant shares and medians cannot quantify the proportion of disparity attributable to routing or capacity.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate substantive complexity.** Higher risk can require investigation; missing information can require a request; accommodation and language needs can require specialist work. This predicts different route frequencies and potentially more active service, conditional on valid triggers. It does not establish that all observed waiting or repetition is necessary.
2. **Insufficient support capacity.** Demand concentrated among seven bilingual workers or five accommodation specialists can create queues. Specialists who also conduct supervisor review may face competing queues. This predicts delays that track staffing availability and demand, even among cases with comparable needs.
3. **Fragmented ownership and transfer delays.** Generalist–specialist–generalist or specialist–supervisor handovers can create duplicate checks, lost context, and idle periods. Repeated transfers and document requests should predict longer elapsed time, although difficult cases could also cause both.
4. **Accessibility barriers upstream.** Forms, instructions, or document requests may be hard to understand or use. This can produce incomplete documentation, longer response intervals, and rework. Adjusting for completeness without examining how incompleteness arose can conceal this mechanism.
5. **Biased measurement or discretionary escalation.** Historical risk scoring, prior-case counts reflecting earlier administrative problems, or inconsistent completeness judgments can trigger disproportionate scrutiny. Supervisor referrals may also reflect different interpretations of similar cases. Validate these possibilities through criterion-level review rather than inferring them from group gaps alone.
6. **Regional and channel capacity differences.** Applicants may be concentrated in offices with backlogs or channels with slower intake. Region/month adjustment can miss interactions, short-term surges, and differences within a region. Geographic explanation does not resolve whether allocation of capacity meets needs equitably.
7. **Weak explanations or unequal appeal access.** Unclear decisions can increase appeals and repeat contacts; effective assistance can also increase legitimate appeals. Conversely, inaccessible appeal processes can suppress challenges. Appeal reason, timeliness, resolution, and applicant understanding distinguish these mechanisms.

Several mechanisms can operate simultaneously. Observed association between a handover and delay, for example, cannot by itself distinguish transfer friction from the severity that prompted the transfer.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Coarse and potentially biased measurements.** Risk bands conceal within-band variation and possible historical disparities in labels, enforcement, and data coverage. Documentation completeness may depend on language accessibility, channel, or staff judgment. Prior-case count can record exposure to past administrative burden rather than inherent complexity. Household bands are weak proxies for substantive eligibility complexity.
- **Missing applicant needs and eligibility facts.** Language preference does not establish interpreter need, proficiency, or receipt of an appropriate service. A recorded accommodation request excludes people who needed support but could not request it. The log lacks detail on accommodation type, literacy, digital access, housing instability, urgency, documentary alternatives, and many underlying benefit-eligibility facts. These omissions limit both comparability and judgments of correctness.
- **Unclear measurement timing.** It is not stated whether attributes are frozen at submission or updated later. Controlling for information changed by staff assistance or review can create misleading adjusted results. “Month” must be derived from a defined event and used consistently.
- **Limited workload and organizational data.** Daily workload cannot reveal within-day queues, shift coverage, task duration, staff absences, interruptions, or simultaneous responsibilities. Resource identifiers do not establish qualifications, authority, or responsibility for a delay. Distinct worker counts do not measure available capacity.
- **Insufficient time semantics.** The listed log has no activity start time, lifecycle status, queue-entry time, or reason-coded pause. Event gaps alone cannot identify active work, agency waiting, applicant response, or unlogged assistance. Open cases, missing closure events, timestamp ties, and incomplete histories can bias measured paths and durations.
- **Appeal and rework quality are unobserved.** Appeal flags do not reveal eligibility to appeal, reasons, costs, representation, accessibility, timeliness, withdrawal, success, or whether reversal reflects an initial error versus new evidence. Higher appeal rates can partly reflect more denials. Compare rates among appropriately eligible decision types and retain the overall applicant burden measure; neither denominator is sufficient alone. An activity repetition does not establish unnecessary rework.
- **Outcomes and experience are incomplete.** Approval does not establish accurate benefit amount, timely activation, explanation quality, dignity, or satisfaction. Denial does not establish substantive correctness. Support-contact presence does not establish effective support.
- **Aggregate evidence and model uncertainty.** No individual traces, joint covariate distributions, standard errors, model specification, or overlap diagnostics are provided. The adjusted results may depend on misspecification, omitted variables, or selection into observed/closed cases. Group-level patterns cannot be attributed to particular workers or applicants from these summaries.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Preserve necessary language support, accommodation, and justified review. Improvements should reduce avoidable waiting and effort while maintaining service quality and decision correctness.

| Option | Intended improvement | Side effects or trade-offs and evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Add language-support and accommodation capacity | Expand qualified staffing, cross-training, backup coverage, and protected specialist time according to arrival volume and required work. Address the overloaded offices explicitly. | Training takes time; redeployment can delay other services; general cross-training cannot replace all specialist expertise. Track support quality, queue tails, staff workload, and other applicants' service as well as speed. |
| Set service-level targets for support steps | Measure time to first effective assistance and time through each support queue, with escalation for prolonged waits and urgency. Include these steps in end-to-end targets. | Targets can encourage superficial contacts, queue relabeling, or rushed reviews. Monitor completed, effective support, aged open cases, accessible responses, and justified exceptions. Do not penalize applicants for accommodation-related time needs. |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers | Assign a continuing case owner, enable joint work and shared records, and remove duplicate checks. Where dependencies permit, organize specialist input alongside other work. | Coordination consumes staff time; integrated roles require training and appropriate information access. Preserve expertise and any required independent review. Track repeated transfers, duplicated requests, errors, and applicant contacts. |
| Improve decision explanations and document requests | Provide clear, accessible, translated or appropriately formatted reasons, specific missing-information guidance, and understandable appeal instructions. | Better explanations require production and quality-control effort. Boilerplate or poor translation can mislead. Improved access may initially increase appeals, so assess comprehension, avoidable errors, and resolution quality rather than targeting fewer appeals alone. |
| Monitor adjusted procedural and outcome disparities | Track route entry, support provision, requests, handovers, waiting, tails, activation, appeals, and decisions alongside raw gaps and need-specific targets. Report overlap and uncertainty. | Adjustment can conceal structural barriers; sparse subgroup estimates can be unstable; dashboards can incentivize gaming and create privacy risks. Retain total burden measures and use case review to investigate persistent signals. |
| Audit risk bands and documentation completeness | Review score inputs, historical labels, scoring versions, error patterns, and the justification for review thresholds. Reassess completeness judgments using accessible requirements and independent samples. | Recalibration may alter investigation demand and error trade-offs; historical outcomes may be a biased validation target. Use independently reviewed evidence and evaluate both missed issues and unnecessary scrutiny. Do not assume a residual group coefficient identifies the cause. |
| Improve intake and appeal assistance | Test accessible forms, earlier support, consolidated document requests, status updates, and accessible appeal channels. | Upfront effort and staffing may rise, and improved access can reveal previously unmet demand. Check successful access, repeated requests, abandonment, and appeal resolution rather than only contact counts. |

Prioritize B's language/documentation queues and C's accommodation queue, including the two backlogged regions. Pilot feasible staffing and routing improvements, compare against credible contemporaneous baselines, and measure quality and equity alongside speed. Staggered implementation can help evaluation where appropriate, without withholding required support. Do not reduce burden metrics by suppressing appeals, discouraging accommodation requests, or removing required reviews.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence does not establish discriminatory decision-making, and some different routes are justified by documented risk and support needs. It also does not support affirming that the process is procedurally fair. B and C face substantial support-related delays, concentrated staffing, extra handovers, and residual adjusted burdens; these are credible signals of avoidable structural disadvantage. D's risk-based routing is more plausibly explained by measured complexity, but risk-score validity and its elevated adjusted appeal rate remain concerns.

The defensible conclusion is **serious procedural fairness concerns, with their causal extent and decision-level discrimination indeterminate**. Prioritize support capacity, timeliness, continuity of ownership, and accessible explanations while validating routing criteria and measuring comparable-case burdens. Preserve the rights-protecting support steps themselves.
