## 1. Current-process diagnosis

The process has two different problems that are being conflated: the clean fast path is genuinely efficient for some low-risk claims, while the same automation is unsafe for claims whose value, defect ambiguity, or evidence requirements make a wrong decision expensive. The redesign should therefore optimize the complete claim outcome, not the time to Fast approval or the cost before downstream rework.

**Performance.** The frequent clean route is short: Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen averages 0.8 hours, Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval 1.0 hour, Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement 3.0 hours, and Dispatch replacement -> Quality/compliance close 5.0 hours. The frequency DFG shows 2,550 auto-screen-to-Fast-approval transitions and 2,300 Fast-approval-to-dispatch transitions, so this is not a marginal path. It is attractive on throughput and nominal cost.

The slower routes expose where time is being lost. Intake -> Manual eligibility review averages 7.5 hours, Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote 22 hours, and Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review averages 34 hours, with a p90 of 88 hours. The complaint/reopen -> Manual eligibility review edge averages 40 hours and has a p90 of 100 hours. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort has a 140-hour median, and the DFG contains a large evidence loop: Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review occurs 890 times, alongside 320 abandonments and 220 fraud/risk-review transitions. These are not merely slow activities; they are repeated handoffs and waiting states.

Capacity makes the tail structurally likely. Seven manual reviewers have a sharp queue effect above 82% utilization, two fraud/risk specialists are a bottleneck on Mondays and after campaigns, and the escalation board has only one daily 45-minute slot. The DFG also shows loops rather than a single pass: complaints/reopens return to manual review, and repaired devices can enter Rework repair and return to Repair device.

**Quality and economics.** Fast approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits. Fast approval has a 14.1% complaint/reopen rate, versus 3.3% for the Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device path. This does not prove that repair is universally better: repair is slower and may be inappropriate for a simple accessory or an uneconomic defect. It does show that the apparent fast-path saving is partly an accounting boundary; downstream failure is being excluded.

The high-value segment is especially exposed. Premium high-value claims have 22% complaint/reopen on the fast path versus 5% on the reviewed/repair path; Standard high-value claims have 18% versus 6%. Incorrect replacement selection is particularly costly, with an expected error cost of EUR 410 for high-value devices. Rework repair is 12.5% for repaired high-value devices, versus 4.0% for repaired low-risk devices, so a repair-first design also needs technical quality controls rather than assuming repair is automatically the quality answer.

The repeated-evidence statistic is another important signal: Request missing evidence occurs at least twice in 26% of cases that enter it. This suggests that the evidence request is not functioning as a single clear completion step. The 22% unclear/missing-evidence share, highest abandonment, and 140-hour median make it a distinct redesign target rather than noise in the average.

**Compliance and conformance.** The token-based replay reports log fitness of 0.91 but only 68% fitting traces. In other words, much of the event behavior resembles the normative process, but a substantial minority does not. The low precision of the discovered model is also a warning: mixed low-frequency combinations of auto approval, risk review, and repair are being combined in ways that do not represent allowed policy paths.

The alignment diagnostics identify concrete risks, not just a low score: Fast approval before Evidence completeness, Customer complaint/reopen after close, and Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices. These conflict with R1, R2, and R3 respectively or in combination. The DFG cannot by itself establish which individual claims violated a rule because it is aggregated and contains loops, but it is sufficient to require trace-level controls and segmentation. A redesign that merely accelerates the current fast route would amplify the most frequent nonconformant behavior.

**Customer experience.** Premium customers face high service-credit exposure when cycle time exceeds 24 hours, so moving every claim to the reviewed path is not acceptable. Premium low-risk accessories have an 8-hour median on the fast path and 6% complaints, compared with 46 hours and 3% on the manual path: here speed has real value, and the complaint gap may be tolerable if the claim is policy-safe. Standard low-risk customers tolerate up to 72 hours when status visibility is good, so certainty and communication can substitute for some raw speed. High-value customers have the opposite trade-off: fast handling is attractive, but wrong-part and wrong-replacement failures create much more dissatisfaction than a controlled review. Low digital literacy is a further risk if a new evidence portal simply transfers work to customers and increases abandonment.

The central contradiction is therefore real. The most frequent fast path is the best local answer for eligible, low-risk, complete claims, but it is a poor global answer when applied to high-value or incomplete claims. Its direct edges look fast and cheap, while its later complaint/reopen edges, compliance deviations, and reshipment costs make the end-to-end result worse for particular segments. The reviewed path should not be treated as universally superior either: it carries long queues and high labor cost, and the Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote edge is often too slow for Premium cases. The appropriate response is controlled differentiation and measurement of downstream outcomes.

## 2. Redesign A: Policy-correct risk-and-segment lanes

**Redesigned process flow.**

1. At Intake claim, capture customer segment, device value band, defect type, evidence completeness, and risk indicators. Run an updated auto eligibility screen as a presort, not as permission to bypass policy.
2. Premium low-risk accessories enter a narrowly defined rapid lane. The explicit Premium low-risk exception to R2 may permit replacement dispatch before the normal evidence-completeness point, but the exception is encoded and audited; required evidence must still be collected before Quality/compliance close. A risk flag or ambiguous defect removes the exception.
3. Standard low-risk accessories can use Fast approval only when eligibility and evidence are complete and there is no risk trigger. They receive a clear status and expected completion time. An incomplete claim receives one defined evidence-pending state and a consolidated request before it can proceed.
4. High-value devices have a hard policy gate. Before Fast approval, the case must pass Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review under R1, and evidence completeness must be confirmed under R2. Premium high-value cases receive priority capacity, but priority does not remove the gate. The technical decision then selects repair or replacement based on defect type and policy.
5. Add a lightweight pre-dispatch verification for high-value and otherwise flagged replacements: device model, part number, entitlement, and claim record must agree. Keep Quality/compliance close as a true close event. Any Customer complaint/reopen is routed through Manual eligibility review before close or denial under R3.

**Targets.** This is primarily a routing and policy-control redesign. It targets the 22% high-value population, especially the 14% Premium high-value and 21% Standard high-value segments; the fast-path complaint rates of 22% and 18%; the alignment deviations involving evidence, risk review, and dispatch; and the incorrect replacement tail. It also preserves a rapid route for the 18% Premium low-risk accessory segment and a controlled automated route for the 25% Standard low-risk accessory segment.

**Mechanism of improvement.** The auto screen becomes segment- and value-aware, so high volume is no longer mistaken for low risk. Policy gates directly remove the common Fast approval before Evidence completeness and high-value dispatch-without-risk-review deviations. The pre-dispatch check addresses the 96-hour mean Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen tail associated with wrong replacement parts. Applying manual review only where the policy and risk warrant it should reduce avoidable complaints without imposing the 46- to 120-hour reviewed-path experience on every accessory claim.

The update can be deployed in approximately two weeks, but the expected 15-35% increase in manual-review inflow must be capacity-tested. A simple capacity-aware rule can divert only genuinely ambiguous cases to manual review and send fraud signals to the risk queue, rather than sending every non-perfect auto result to the same seven reviewers.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** High-value claims will usually take longer and cost more up front. The 6-18-hour pre-dispatch gate may reduce the apparent speed advantage, and the manual queue may exceed the 82% utilization danger zone. A static classifier can still misclassify an unusual defect or become stale. This design does not by itself solve low digital literacy, repeated evidence requests, or the 12.5% high-value repair rework rate; those require the other redesigns or separate controls. The Premium accessory exception also remains a consciously accepted residual risk rather than a universal shortcut.

## 3. Redesign B: Repair-first specialist cells for high-value devices

**Redesigned process flow.**

1. Use Intake claim and the auto screen for triage only. High-value and unclear-defect cases are sent to a dedicated case cell containing an eligibility reviewer, access to a fraud/risk specialist, and a technician. The case packet is shared across roles so the claim is not repeatedly re-explained.
2. Enforce R1 and R2 as blocking conditions: technical work can be prepared in parallel, but Fast approval or replacement dispatch cannot occur until the required manual/risk review and evidence-completeness checks are complete. Reopens always return to Manual eligibility review under R3.
3. For repairable high-value defects, make Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device the default technical route. Quote preparation uses pre-authorized price bands and a maintained parts-price catalogue so that part-price confirmation does not hold the whole case for 22 hours or more. Premium high-value cases get a reserved daily pod slot and a promised review/repair milestone; Standard cases use a lower-cost scheduled promise.
4. A replacement is selected only when repair is unsuitable, uneconomic, or disallowed by policy. Before dispatch, verify the replacement part and model. Every repair proceeds through Quality check; rework is recorded by defect and part type, with a feedback loop to the technical decision rules.
5. Low-risk accessories remain on a policy-correct automated route, but they do not consume the specialist cell unless risk or technical ambiguity warrants it.

**Targets.** This design targets Premium and Standard high-value devices, the EUR 410 expected cost of a wrong replacement, the 22%/18% high-value fast-path complaint rates, and the slow Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote and Repair quote -> Customer approval waits. It is also suited to high-value defects for which the product can be repaired reliably. It deliberately does not put the entire 43% low-risk accessory population into a technician-led flow.

**Mechanism of improvement.** The main lever is fewer handoffs and a different technical decision, not a faster eligibility classifier. The repair path already has a much lower observed complaint/reopen rate (3.3%) than the Fast approval population (14.1%), which supports testing repair-first for the high-value cases where replacement mistakes are costly. A shared cell can begin quote preparation and evidence clarification without violating the decision gates, while pre-authorized quote bands reduce waiting for price confirmation. The quality-check loop makes the 12.5% high-value repair rework visible and actionable instead of hiding it in a nominally successful close.

This design can also use the daily escalation slot for cases at risk of missing the Premium promise, rather than for routine handoffs. Its success depends on end-to-end outcomes: fewer complaints, wrong replacements, reshipments, and service credits, even if the initial repair labor line is higher.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** Repair-first increases upfront labor, parts, and customer waiting for claims that could have been safely replaced. It can be worse when parts are unavailable, the defect is not repairable, or the customer values immediate replacement more than repair. The three technicians, parts-price confirmation, and two inspectors may become the new constraint; a cell can also create a single point of failure when a specialist is absent. The design is more operationally complex and does not materially improve the unclear-evidence cohort unless the cell is paired with a better evidence-collection service. It also leaves the simple-accessory path largely unchanged, apart from mandatory policy controls.

## 4. Redesign C: Evidence-first assisted service with a single case packet

**Redesigned process flow.**

1. Immediately after Intake claim, issue an adaptive evidence checklist based on segment, value, and defect type. A new portal validates photos, serial numbers, purchase evidence, and required fields and assembles one reusable case packet. Provide phone, agent-assisted upload, or other assisted handling for customers with low digital literacy.
2. Replace repeated Request missing evidence transitions with an explicit Evidence pending state, one consolidated request, plain-language reason codes, reminders, and a timeboxed customer contact path. A case should not bounce between evidence request and Manual eligibility review simply because different reviewers ask for different items.
3. When the packet is complete, rerun the auto screen against the same evidence. Clear low-risk claims can use the existing fast service, subject to policy-as-code gates. High-value claims remain blocked until the R1 manual/risk requirement and the R2 evidence requirement are met; the Premium low-risk accessory exception is explicitly recorded. Reopened claims return to Manual eligibility review before final close or denial.
4. Show the customer the current state, missing item, owner, next milestone, and expected completion window. Give Premium claims an aging trigger before the 24-hour service-credit boundary and give Standard low-risk customers a credible 72-hour status promise. Reserve the escalation board for cases approaching a promise breach or with a genuine exception.
5. Close only after evidence and compliance fields are complete. Feed complaints, abandonment, wrong-part events, and rework back into the evidence checklist and routing rules; do not judge the portal by upload completion alone.

**Targets.** This design is aimed first at the 22% unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort, the 26% of evidence cases that request evidence at least twice, the 320 DFG abandonments after evidence request, and the 140-hour median. It also targets Standard customers, who can tolerate a 72-hour completion if visibility is good, and Premium customers whose service-credit exposure makes early aging intervention valuable.

**Mechanism of improvement.** The primary improvement is front-door quality and customer clarity. The portal has a stated opportunity to reduce repeated evidence requests by 30-45%; a single packet should also reduce re-reading, inconsistent requests, and the 34-hour mean evidence-to-manual wait. Assisted channels limit the risk that digitization merely increases abandonment. Reusing the packet across eligibility, risk, technical, and close activities removes a common source of rework while preserving the fast path for cases that are genuinely simple.

Policy gates are part of this design, but the core change is not a new repair policy or a fixed segment-routing matrix. It is a customer- and evidence-centered front door with aging management. Therefore it can be tested independently and then combined with either risk lanes or repair cells.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** Portal build, integration, reminders, and assisted support add cost and implementation complexity. A portal can increase abandonment for customers who cannot use it, and a single request can still be wrong if the checklist logic is poor. Better packets may release a wave of cases into Manual eligibility review or the two-person fraud/risk queue, merely moving the bottleneck downstream. This design does not by itself cure wrong replacement selection or high-value repair rework, and it cannot justify violating R1 or R2 in pursuit of a shorter front-door time.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A.**

- The updated auto rules may be too conservative or too broad. A 15-35% increase in manual inflow could push reviewer utilization above 82%, causing Premium cases to miss 24 hours and negating the benefit of segmentation.
- The classifier may miss an unusual high-value defect or incorrectly grant the Premium low-risk exception. That would preserve the current R1/R2 failure pattern while making the violation harder to see because the lane appears intentional.
- The pre-dispatch check may be staffed too thinly. It can catch wrong parts but create a 6-18-hour queue, especially after campaigns, shifting delay from complaint handling to dispatch quality control.
- A static rule set may drift as products and fraud patterns change; a falling average cycle time could hide a new defect-specific complaint cluster.

The main bottleneck migration is from general processing to Manual eligibility review, Fraud/risk review, and pre-dispatch verification. Capacity dashboards must therefore report queue age and utilization by lane, not just total claims completed.

**Redesign B.**

- Repair-first may send nonrepairable or parts-constrained devices into a dead end. Customers then wait for a repair decision and still require replacement, increasing cycle time and dissatisfaction.
- The specialist cell may become a single point of failure. A missing reviewer, technician, or risk specialist can stop the whole packet even when other work could proceed.
- More repair volume may expose the existing 12.5% high-value rework rate. If Quality check is treated as a final inspection rather than a feedback loop, Rework repair will grow and consume the same technicians.
- Pre-authorized price bands can be wrong or stale, creating margin leakage, customer disputes, or inappropriate repair approvals. A repair path can also underperform if customers strongly prefer replacement.

The likely bottleneck migration is toward the three technicians, parts-price confirmation, and two quality inspectors. If those queues grow, the apparent reduction in eligibility handoffs will be offset by longer Repair quote -> Customer approval and Quality check -> Rework repair loops.

**Redesign C.**

- The portal may increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy. A higher upload rate is not success if completion falls or customers switch to complaints because they cannot satisfy the checklist.
- Adaptive logic may request the wrong evidence or generate a technically complete but substantively weak packet. The repeated-evidence loop would disappear from the graph only because the case is abandoned or denied earlier.
- A successful evidence campaign may release many complete cases into the seven-person manual queue or the two fraud/risk specialists. Front-door cycle time will improve while end-to-end cycle time and queue age worsen.
- Reminder and aging rules can overwhelm assisted support or the escalation board, particularly after marketing campaigns. Status messages that promise dates without capacity can damage trust more than silence.

The principal bottleneck migration is to assisted intake/support first and to manual/risk review second. A secondary risk is that unresolved technical decisions continue to generate the Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen tail, so the evidence redesign must be evaluated together with downstream outcomes.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**1. Establish the measurement and safety baseline.** Before changing routing, create a trace-level baseline by segment, value band, defect type, evidence status, and outcome. Track end-to-end elapsed time and p50/p90, time in each queue, first-pass completion, repeat evidence requests, abandonment, complaint/reopen within a fixed post-close window, wrong replacement, reshipment, service credits, repair rework, labor and downstream cost, and explicit R1/R2/R3 conformance. The aggregate DFG should be sliced into case-level traces; otherwise loops and mixed populations can create misleading edge counts. Retain a contemporaneous control group or stepped-wedge cohort.

**2. First pilot: a narrow version of Redesign A on low-risk accessories.** Start with a limited product/channel cohort covering Premium low-risk accessories and Standard low-risk accessories, while holding high-value routing unchanged. Encode the Premium low-risk R2 exception explicitly, require evidence completion before close, and add the basic R1/R3 controls and status events. Test the updated auto rules in shadow mode before allowing them to route cases, then use a stepped rollout so a manual queue surge can be stopped.

Monitor clean-path median and p90, end-to-end close time, complaint/reopen, service credits, abandonment, evidence completeness at dispatch and close, fitting-trace percentage, R1/R2/R3 deviations, manual utilization, risk-queue age, and total cost after a downstream observation window. A go decision requires materially fewer conformance deviations, no meaningful increase in complaints or abandonment, Premium completion consistently inside the 24-hour promise, Standard completion generally within the 72-hour expectation, and no sustained queue utilization above the approximately 82% danger zone. Stop or roll back if a policy violation appears, complaint/reopen rises by more than a small low-single-digit percentage-point range, or manual/risk queues remain above safe capacity for more than a short transient period.

This first step tests whether targeted automation preserves the genuine speed benefit without reproducing the current fast-path failure. It is relatively reversible and avoids exposing high-value customers to an unproven technical policy.

**3. Second pilot: Redesign C for the unclear/missing-evidence cohort.** Introduce the portal for one channel or product family, with assisted handling available from day one and the old process retained as a control. Start with an adaptive checklist and a single consolidated request rather than attempting a large platform transformation. Track the proportion of cases with two or more evidence requests, time from request to complete packet, the 140-hour-cohort median and p90, abandonment by digital-literacy proxy, support contacts, complaint/reopen, and the queue age that follows packet completion.

The opportunity is credible if repeated requests fall toward the stated 30-45% reduction range, but the go threshold must also require no material abandonment increase and an improvement in end-to-end completion, not just faster uploads. Stop or redesign the checklist if abandonment rises materially, evidence-related denials increase, assisted support becomes saturated, or manual/risk queue age increases enough to erase the front-door gain. This stage comes before the high-value repair pilot because it tests whether better evidence reduces noise or simply feeds a bottleneck.

**4. Third pilot: Redesign B on selected high-value, repairable defects.** Begin with Premium high-value cases because their fast-path complaint rate is 22% and their service promise makes quality failures costly, but restrict eligibility to defect types with known repairability and available parts. Keep a reviewed high-value control, enforce R1 and R2 as hard gates, and use the shared specialist cell with a capped work-in-progress limit. Expand to Standard high-value devices only after the cell demonstrates that it can meet a credible promise at lower service-credit exposure.

Measure total cycle time and p90, time to first credible technical decision, complaint/reopen, wrong replacement and reshipment, repair acceptance, Quality check failures, Rework repair, service credits, labor/parts cost, and utilization of technicians, inspectors, manual reviewers, and risk specialists. Go if complaints and wrong replacements move materially toward the reviewed-path outcome (roughly the 5-6% range where comparable reviewed/repair observations exist), rework does not worsen, compliance remains clean, and the total outcome cost is acceptable even if the upfront repair cost is higher. Stop or narrow the defect scope if repair rework rises, parts or technician queues breach the promised window, or customers reject repair often enough that replacement is still required.

**5. Combine only after attribution is clear.** Roll out the designs in a factorial or stepped combination where feasible: policy-correct lanes, evidence service, and repair cells should each have an identifiable comparison. Review outcomes after enough time for complaints, reshipments, credits, and rework to appear; a same-day close metric cannot validate a downstream quality claim. The final operating policy should be segment-specific: retain fast automation for complete low-risk claims, use assisted evidence for the unclear cohort, and use reviewed/repair-first handling for high-value situations where the cost of a wrong replacement dominates the cost of waiting.

At every stage, a claimed improvement is real only if it survives three checks: the end-to-end claim outcome improves, the downstream complaint/rework/compliance measures do not worsen, and the displaced queue has not merely moved to another team. Exact ROI and strict Pareto dominance should be estimated from the pilot data, not inferred from the aggregated DFG alone.
