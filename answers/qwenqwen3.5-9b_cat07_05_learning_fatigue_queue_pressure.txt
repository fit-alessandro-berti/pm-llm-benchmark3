# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed patterns cannot be attributed to a single human factor. Instead, the data reveals a system where **queue pressure** and **case-mix selection** interact with **learning effects** to create a specific performance profile.

The decrease in service time (18.8 min to 10.8 min) is not primarily due to learning, as learning typically plateaus after 20–30 events. The continuous decline correlates strongly with the drop in `case_complexity_score` (0.72 to 0.43) and the rise in `queue_length_at_start`. This suggests that as the shift progresses, easier cases accumulate in the queue, and workers speed up because the cognitive load is lighter, *not* because they have mastered the task.

However, the rise in error rates (peaking at 7.9% in the late shift) contradicts the "learning" narrative. While complexity drops, `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` rise dramatically (62 to 88). This indicates **Queue Pressure** is the dominant driver of late-shift errors. When WIP exceeds 70, workers face high pressure to clear the queue, leading to a speed-quality tradeoff. This is exacerbated by the "High WIP + High Complexity" risk zone (10.9% error), suggesting that when complex cases inevitably arrive late in a high-pressure environment, they become the primary source of error.

**Selection bias** plays a critical role in the early shift. Seniors and experienced resources handle complex carryover cases (0.72 complexity) at the start of the shift. This masks the true learning curve for Junior staff and creates the illusion that service time is stable early on. The "Night Staff" data suggests circadian effects exist (higher error rates), but this is confounded by the fact that Night shifts handle "more urgent exceptions" (higher complexity share), meaning their error rates are driven by case type, not just biological fatigue.

Therefore, the service time decline is a mix of **Case-Mix simplification** (easier cases later) and **Queue Pressure** (speeding up to clear backlog). The error spike is driven by **High WIP** forcing workers to process cases faster than their cognitive capacity allows, particularly when complex cases remain in the queue. Fatigue is present (especially for Juniors and Night staff) but is secondary to the structural pressure of WIP levels.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**1. Selection Bias and Fairness:**
Using raw error rates to evaluate performance is misleading because `case_complexity_score` is not evenly distributed. Seniors are assigned complex cases (high complexity, high error potential) early in the shift, while Juniors may be assigned simpler cases later. Penalizing a worker for errors on a complex case without adjusting for complexity is unfair. Furthermore, if Seniors are selected for complex cases, they are doing the "hard work," but if the metric only counts errors, it might unfairly penalize the resource assigned to the high-risk bucket.

**2. Management-Induced Pressure:**
The data shows that error rates rise when `queue_length_at_start` > 70. This suggests that management decisions (e.g., hiring more juniors without adding seniors, or not hiring enough) create the pressure. Penalizing the worker for the system's inability to handle the queue is ethically questionable. The "Queue Pressure" mechanism is a systemic failure, not an individual performance failure.

**3. Automation Quality:**
The `Bot_Risk` data shows instant service but high downstream correction. If we measure "Service Time" as zero for the bot, the system looks efficient. However, the "Rework" happens downstream. If we attribute bot errors to the human Quality Check resource, we are blaming the human for the bot's output. This requires clear process ownership definitions.

**4. Measurement Error:**
`minutes_since_shift_start` is a proxy for time, but it does not account for breaks. A worker who takes a 15-minute break will have a higher `minutes_since_shift_start` for the same amount of work. If we use this to infer fatigue, we ignore legitimate rest.

**5. Punitive Metrics:**
Intervention #8 (Penalize workers) is explicitly discouraged because it ignores the correlation between WIP and Error. High WIP is a systemic risk factor. Punishing workers for errors that are statistically more likely to occur under high WIP creates a "gaming" behavior where workers might hide cases to lower reported WIP, increasing the risk of errors in hidden queues.
