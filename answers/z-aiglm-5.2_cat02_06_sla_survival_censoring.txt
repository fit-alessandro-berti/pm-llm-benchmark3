# Implement a small non-parametric CIF estimator to avoid library assumption.

def aalen_johansen(enc, causes=(1,2,3,4), horizon=HORIZON):
    # Sort by t_obs; at each unique event time compute cause-specific hazard
    # h_k = d_k / n_at_risk, and CIF_k(t) = CIF_k(prev) + S(prev)*h_k.
    # S(t) = product over event times of (1 - sum h_k).
    enc = enc.sort_values("t_obs").reset_index(drop=True)

    S = 1.0
    cif = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    n = len(enc)
    for tt in times:
        at_risk = (enc.t_obs >= tt).sum()
        if at_risk == 0:
            continue

# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** `t = 0` is the `Submit` event. The single supplied duration field `duration_or_age_h` is the elapsed time since `Submit`; for terminal rows it is the time-to-event, and for `open` rows it is the age at the extract freeze (right-censoring age). No event-level timestamps are supplied, so within-case ordering comes only from the `prefix` field.

**Horizon.** ` = 72` hours. All estimands are evaluated at this fixed SLA horizon. We never estimate tail behaviour beyond 72 using the freeze-time data without explicit extrapolation assumptions, because open cases freeze before the eventual outcome is known.

**Events and states.** A case can end in one of four *terminal* dispositions (`Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`) or remain `open` at the freeze. Only `Resolve` is an operational success; `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are not. `Transfer` is special: the internal system stops observing it, so the *external* outcome is unknown.

**Right censoring vs competing events vs already-observed breaches.** These three concepts must not be conflated:

- *Right censoring*: the freeze prevents us from seeing the eventual outcome for an `open` case. For `open` cases with `age < 72` (e.g., S15 at 35 h, S31 at 23 h) we genuinely do not know whether the case will resolve by 72 h. For `open` cases with `age  72` (S09, S11, S25, S26) we *do* know that no terminal disposition occurred by 72 h, so they are not censored at their full age; they are censored at `t = 72` and contribute the information "still in the initial state at 72."
- *Competing event*: `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer` preclude `Resolve` on the same case within the same process; once a case is withdrawn it cannot later be resolved in this dataset.
- *Already-observed breach*: a resolved case with `duration > 72` (S03, S13, S20, S28) is a confirmed SLA breach, not a censored observation; an `open` case aged 72 (S09, S11, S25, S26) is also a confirmed elapsed-time breach because we already know it was not resolved by 72 h.

**Estimands.** Let `T` be time to terminal disposition and `C  {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}` the cause. Let `F_c() = P(C = c, T  )` be the cause-`c` cumulative incidence at ``, and `S() = P(T > ) = 1  _c F_c()` the probability of still being active at ``.

- **E1 — Probability of not being resolved by 72 h (SLA breach):**
  `_1 = 1  F_Resolve(72)`. This counts withdrawals, rejections, transfers (under the competing-event view) and still-open cases at 72 as breaches. This is the estimand that best matches the **SLA-compliance report** decision: "What fraction of cases failed to reach `Resolve` within 72 h?"

- **E2 — Cause-specific cumulative incidences at 72 h:**
  `(F_Resolve, F_Withdraw, F_Reject, F_Transfer)(72)`. These match the **process-improvement decision** — which failure mode is driving breaches (escalation inefficiency vs withdrawal vs transfer) — and are the components whose sum yields `S(72)`.

- **E3 — Probability of still being active (no terminal disposition) at 72 h:**
  `_3 = S(72)`. This matches the **workload / queue-management decision**: how many cases will still be in flight at the SLA deadline and need active intervention.

- **E4 — Cause-specific risk under competing events** is the joint vector `(F_Resolve(72), F_Withdraw(72), F_Reject(72), F_Transfer(72), S(72))`, which sum to the population total under no censoring before 72 and to a censoring-adjusted total otherwise.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis that keeps only `resolved` rows (S01–S04, S07, S13, S16–S20, S27, S28) and computes, say, the mean or median resolution time, would suffer from at least five distinct biases visible in the supplied extract.

1. **Survivor bias (selection on the outcome).** Completed `resolved` cases are a non-random subset: cases that reached `Resolve` are systematically easier or faster. Dropping withdrawals (S06 at 27, S12 at 15, S22 at 44, S30 at 70), rejections (S05 at 9, S14 at 63, S23 at 11), and transfers (S08, S21, S32) removes the slow and the difficult cases. For example, S03 (76 h) and S13 (110 h) are kept only because they *eventually* resolved; S09 and S11, both `open` beyond 72, are dropped even though they are confirmed breaches. The mean resolution time on the completed subset understates the true SLA-tail risk because it conditions on `C = Resolve`.

2. **Informative censoring.** Treating `transferred` as ordinary right-censoring assumes transfer is independent of the latent resolution time. S08, S21, and S32 all involve `Escalate` followed by `Transfer` — a path that likely reflects cases the internal team could not resolve. Their exclusion is therefore not random with respect to the outcome; censoring is informative. Similarly, `open` cases with large `last_event_gap_h` (S11 at 55 h, S25 at 48 h, S26 at 72 h) may be effectively abandoned, so their freeze-time censoring is not independent of the latent resolution.

3. **Competing risks ignored.** Withdrawal and rejection preclude resolution. The CIF of `Resolve` is not `1  KM_resolve` unless withdrawals and rejections are independent censoring, which they are not — a case withdrawn at 27 h (S06) cannot be counted as still at risk of resolving. Pooling withdrawals into censoring would inflate the apparent `F_Resolve` estimate.

4. **Cohort imbalance.** Cohort B was rolled out after a screening and escalation policy change, assignment was not randomized, and rollout compliance was incomplete. Cohort B has 4/17 AutoCheck-first cases (S16, S18, S19, S21, S24, S27, S28, S29, S31, S32 — actually 9/17) and contains all three P1 transfer cases except S08. Cohort A has 1 AutoCheck-first case (S01). Comparing cohort-level resolution rates on completed cases confounds cohort with policy, priority mix, and transfer rate.

5. **Active open cases below vs above 72 h are fundamentally different.** Open cases aged <72 (S10 at 47, S15 at 35, S24 at 67, S29 at 51, S31 at 23) are genuinely right-censored before the SLA deadline — their eventual outcome is unobserved. Open cases aged 72 (S09 at 92, S11 at 81, S25 at 79, S26 at 104) are *already known* to have breached; they should contribute to the breach estimand as confirmed breaches, not be dropped as "incomplete." Lumping the two groups together (e.g., "open cases") or dropping both biases the breach estimate in opposite directions.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

I compare the methods specifically against the supplied extract, not generically.

**Kaplan-Meier (KM) on time-to-resolve.** Treats `Resolve` as the event and every other status as censoring. Biased here: withdrawals/rejections/transfers are not independent censoring, so KM overstates `F_Resolve`. Useful only as a sanity check against the more principled estimators; not recommended as primary.

**Aalen-Johansen (AJ) cumulative incidence.** Handles competing risks non-parametrically and yields `F_c(72)` for each cause as well as `S(72) = 1   F_c(72)`. This directly produces E1, E2, E3, E4 from a single fit. With only 32 cases it is small-sample noisy but it is the *correct* non-parametric estimator and requires no covariate assumptions. **Recommended as the primary estimator.**

**Cause-specific Cox hazards.** Models `h_c(t) = h_{0c}(t) exp(_c'x)` separately per cause. Useful for understanding *which covariates drive which transition* (e.g., does cohort B reduce time-to-resolve hazard, or increase time-to-transfer hazard?). However, baseline covariates are limited to `cohort` and `priority` without leakage, and the extract is too small for stable Cox fits. Useful as a secondary, hypothesis-generating analysis.

**Subdistribution (Fine-Gray) hazards.** Models the subdistribution hazard of `Resolve` directly, which is convenient for predicting `F_Resolve(72) | x`. Risk of misinterpretation: the subdistribution hazard is hard to translate into process interventions because it is not a transition rate out of an identifiable state. Use only for prediction, not for understanding causality.

**Restricted mean time in state (RMST).** Integrates `S(t)` (or per-state occupancy probabilities in a multi-state model) up to 72. Useful for SLA governance because it has units of case-hours, which converts directly into staffing and capacity planning. Recommended as a secondary *summary* reported alongside AJ.

**Landmarking.** Fixes a landmark time `t_L < 72` (e.g., 24 h or 48 h) and conditions on the state occupied at `t_L`. This is the cleanest way to use time-varying process features (current stage, escalated-by-`t_L`, rework count up to `t_L`, current inactivity gap) without immortal-time bias or leakage. With a 24 h landmark, open cases with `last_event_gap_h` already large at 24 h (S11 had escalated by then and may have been inactive) can be flagged early. **Recommended as the secondary approach** for risk scoring and early-warning alerts.

**Multi-state model.** States derived from prefixes (`Submit, Screen, AutoCheck, Review, Escalate, Rework, Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer`). Transition hazards can be modeled semi-parametrically. It explicitly captures rework (`Review  Rework  Review`), escalation paths, and the cohort-specific `Submit  AutoCheck  Screen` ordering. With 32 cases and many transitions, cell counts are tiny; this is a structural / mechanistic overlay rather than a stable estimator. Use it to *organize* the analysis and to define the landmark state space, but rely on AJ for the headline numbers.

**Recommendation.** Primary: Aalen-Johansen CIF at 72 h with transfers as a competing event (Assumption B), reported overall and stratified by `cohort × priority`. Secondary: a 24 h and 48 h landmark analysis with the current-state and process-derived features in §5, plus a multi-state diagram (§5) to communicate transition structure. RMST reported as a capacity-planning supplement. Cox and Fine-Gray reported as hypothesis-generating only, with caveats about sample size.

- Under A (censoring), transfers leave the risk set silently. With S08 (68 h, P1), S21 (26 h, P1), S32 (96 h, P1) all in P1, this *lowers* the P1 breach estimate substantially.
- Under B (competing), P1 breach rises because all three transfers count as breaches.
- Under C (guaranteed breach), the qualitative conclusion "P1 is the safest priority" — which one might naïvely reach from S04 (29 h) and S18 (12 h) — can reverse, because P1 also has S11 (open 81 h), S25 (open 79 h), and the three transfers. **Conclusion most likely to reverse: P1 vs P2/P3 ranking.**

**Sensitivity 2 — Treatment of withdrawals and rejections.** Default: competing events. Alternative: non-informative censoring (assume withdrawn/rejected cases would have had the same resolution-time distribution as continuing cases). With withdrawals S12 (15 h, P3) and S05 rejection (9 h, P3) all early in P3, treating them as censoring would push P3's apparent `F_Resolve` upward and could make P3 look better than P2. **Conclusion possibly reversed: P3 vs P2 ordering.**

**Sensitivity 3 — Long inactivity gap as silent terminal.** Hypothesis: open cases with `last_event_gap_h`  24 h (S09 gap 31, S11 gap 55, S25 gap 48, S26 gap 72) are effectively abandoned and will not resolve by 72. Recode them as a competing "silent terminal" event at `t = age  gap`. This raises the breach estimate for cohort B (S25, S26) and for cohort A (S09, S11), and lowers `S(72)` in both cohorts. **Conclusion possibly reversed: cohort A vs B comparison**, because cohort B contributes two of the four large-gap cases (S25, S26) and already has the AutoCheck-first policy.

**Sensitivity 4 — Cohort policy effect (stratified vs combined).** Combined AJ assumes exchangeability across cohorts given the same priority. A stratified analysis (AJ within A, within B) does not assume exchangeability but loses power. Because cohort assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, the stratified estimate is the more honest descriptive comparison; the combined estimate should be interpreted as confounded by policy. **Conclusion that could reverse: any claim that cohort B improved SLA performance**, since B's apparent edge in resolved cases (S16 16 h, S18 12 h) is offset by its transfers and long-gap opens if those are counted as breaches.

**Sensitivity 5 — Landmark choice.** Re-run the landmark Cox at 24 h vs 48 h. At 48 h, S10 (open 47) is right at the boundary and S24 (open 67) is still at risk; their inclusion changes the risk set. Conclusions about escalation as a risk factor may be sensitive to whether `Escalate`-by-`t_L` is observed before or after most cases have already been resolved.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**From survival outputs to actions.**

- **SLA governance dashboard.** Report `_1 = 1  F_Resolve(72)` by `cohort × priority` with confidence bands from AJ, using the competing-event transfer assumption (B) as the headline and A/C as bracketing bounds. Do not present a single point estimate; report the bound width as a measure of how much the transfer ambiguity matters.

- **Workload and queue actions.** Use `_3 = S(72)` (still active at 72) to size surge capacity. RMST in the "active" state up to 72 h gives expected case-hours of work, directly convertible into staffing. Stratify by priority: P1 still-active cases at 72 (e.g., S11, S25) are highest-cost interventions.

- **Early-warning alerts.** A landmark Cox at `t_L = 24 h` predicts `P(Resolve by 72 | state at 24, covariates)`. Cases above a calibrated threshold (e.g., 50% predicted breach probability) are flagged for active intervention — pull from the queue, escalate to senior reviewer, or pre-emptively transfer to a known-fast external partner. Crucially, the alert uses only information available at `t_L`: current stage, escalated-by-24 flag, rework count up to 24, current inactivity gap.

- **Process-improvement signals.** Cause-specific CIFs (`F_Withdraw`, `F_Reject`, `F_Transfer`) identify which failure mode dominates which stratum. For example, if `F_Transfer(72)` is concentrated in P1 (S08, S21, S32 are all P1), the action is "improve P1 internal capability before escalation," not "improve SLA monitoring."

- **Do not present risk scores as causal effects.** Cohort B is not a randomized treatment; `autocheck_first` is not a randomly assigned intervention; priority is a triage label that itself predicts difficulty. Report predicted breach probabilities, not "cohort B reduces breach by X%." Any causal claim requires the data in §9 (assignment mechanism, rollout compliance, baseline case-mix).

- **Calibration checks.** Group cases by predicted decile of breach probability and compare to observed breach rate (where outcome is known). Report calibration by `priority` and by `cohort` separately, because a model that calibrates on average can be badly miscalibrated within P1 or within cohort A.

- **Fairness checks.** Examine whether predicted breach probabilities differ systematically by priority *after conditioning on observable case difficulty*; if they do, the model may be encoding triage bias rather than genuine risk. Because `priority` is itself a label assigned by the organization, "fairness" here means transparency about how priority enters predictions, not removal of priority from the model.

#   E3 = S(72)               (still active at 72 = workload)

def encode(df, transfer_assumption="competing", horizon=HORIZON):
    rows = []
    for r in df.itertuples(index=False):
        t, st = r.t, r.status
        if st == "open":
            t_obs, cause = min(t, horizon), 0
        elif st == "resolved":
            if t <= horizon:
                t_obs, cause = t, 1
            else:
                t_obs, cause = horizon, 0   # in initial state at 72; resolved after horizon
        elif st == "withdrawn":
            t_obs, cause = min(t, horizon), 2
        elif st == "rejected":
            t_obs, cause = min(t, horizon), 3
        elif st == "transferred":

                t_obs, cause = min(t, horizon), 4
            elif transfer_assumption == "breach":    # Assumption C
                t_obs, cause = min(t, horizon), 4
            else:
                raise ValueError(transfer_assumption)
        else:
            raise ValueError(st)
        rows.append((r.case, r.cohort, r.priority, t_obs, cause, st, t))
    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["case","cohort","priority","t_obs","cause","orig_status","orig_t"])

# 3. Aalen-Johansen CIF estimator (no survival library assumed).
# If `lifelines` is available, the same quantities can be obtained via
# `lifelines import AalenJohansenFitter`; otherwise this is the fallback.
# ============================================================
def aalen_johansen(enc, causes=(1,2,3,4,5), horizon=HORIZON):
    enc = enc.sort_values("t_obs").reset_index(drop=True)
    event_times = sorted(enc.loc[enc.cause>0, "t_obs"].unique())
    S = 1.0
    cif = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    for tt in event_times:
        at_risk = int((enc.t_obs >= tt).sum())
        if at_risk == 0:
            continue
        d = {c: int(((enc.t_obs==tt)&(enc.cause==c)).sum()) for c in causes}
        d_total = sum(d.values())
        if d_total == 0:
            continue
        for c in causes:
            if d[c] > 0:
                cif[c] += S * (d[c] / at_risk)
        S *= (1.0 - d_total / at_risk)
    return S, cif

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The supplied extract is sufficient to define and structure the analysis but not to produce stable inferences. The most important missing data, in priority order:

1. **Exact event timestamps.** `prefix` gives order and `duration_or_age_h` gives total elapsed time, but we cannot recover the timing of intermediate events (`Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`). Without these, all time-varying features can only be measured at the freeze, not at landmark times `t_L = 24, 48`. This blocks the landmark Cox and multi-state hazard models that are the most operationally useful analyses. *Assumption needed to proceed:* durations between events are roughly uniform, which is implausible.

2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`.** S08, S21, S32 are transferred and never observed again. Without external resolution status and external resolution time, the transfer assumption (A/B/C) cannot be empirically chosen, and any cohort comparison is bracketed rather than point-identified.

3. **Cutoff and case-start dates.** Without calendar dates we cannot correct for seasonality, cohort-age confounding (cohort A cases started earlier and have had more time to be observed terminal), or rollout timing. The cohort B "improvement" could partly be an artefact of cohort B cases being younger on average and therefore over-represented among `open` rows.

4. **Time-varying workload and staffing.** SLA tail risk is likely driven partly by queue length and reviewer availability at the moment each case is in `Review`. Without staffing and arrival-rate data, the model cannot distinguish process design from capacity constraints.

5. **Assignment mechanism for cohort B.** Cohort assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete. Without the assignment rule (which cases were eligible for cohort B's policy) and the compliance log (which eligible cases actually received the new policy), cohort B vs A is a confounded comparison, not an effect estimate. The `autocheck_first` feature is a proxy for compliance, not a clean treatment indicator.

6. **Rollout compliance details.** "Incomplete rollout compliance" means some cohort-B-eligible cases may have followed the cohort A path and vice versa. The `cohort` label may therefore misclassify the actual policy received. A per-case policy-received flag would let us run an as-treated or instrumental-variable analysis.

7. **Reason codes for `Withdraw` and `Reject`.** Withdrawals at 15 h (S12) and 70 h (S30) are operationally very different — the first is an early customer cancellation, the second is a late abandonment that may be a hidden SLA failure. Reason codes would let us split `Withdraw` into informative vs non-informative subcauses.

8. **Right-censoring pattern of `open` cases.** A few open cases (S11 gap 55 h, S25 gap 48 h, S26 gap 72 h) have very long inactivity gaps, suggesting they may be effectively abandoned. Without a "case still being actively worked" flag, the silent-terminal sensitivity in §6 is the best available treatment, but it is a hypothesis, not a measurement.

Until these data are available, the analysis should be reported as a **bracketed descriptive comparison** (AJ under transfer assumptions A/B/C, with the long-gap sensitivity), explicitly framed as non-causal, and used primarily for hypothesis generation and for the design of a follow-up study with randomized or at least well-documented cohort assignment.
