# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let case \(c\) have arrival \(a_c\), fork release \(r_c\), branch \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\) start/end times \(s_{ci}, e_{ci}\), and final activity start/end times \(s_{cF}, e_{cF}\).

Define:

\[
U_c = r_c-a_c
\]

as **upstream elapsed time before fork release**.

For each branch:

\[
Q_{ci}=s_{ci}-r_c
\]

is **branch-specific queue waiting** after the fork has released the branch but before work starts.

\[
S_{ci}=e_{ci}-s_{ci}
\]

is **branch active service time**.

The inferred fork-join readiness time is:

\[
J_c=\max_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} e_{ci}
\]

The branch or branches attaining that maximum are the **critical branch(es)**:

\[
K_c=\{i:e_{ci}=J_c\}
\]

For each branch, synchronization waiting after its completion is:

\[
W^{sync}_{ci}=J_c-e_{ci}
\]

Thus, a critical branch has zero synchronization wait, while a branch finishing early waits for the remaining branch work to complete.

The elapsed fork-join stage, from branch permission to join readiness, is:

\[
T^{FJ}_c=J_c-r_c
=\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})
\]

This is a maximum, not a sum. It can also be expressed for every branch as:

\[
T^{FJ}_c=Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W^{sync}_{ci}
\]

but this equality is a branch-specific representation of the *same elapsed interval*. It must not be added across branches.

For example, summing synchronization waits,

\[
\sum_i W^{sync}_{ci} = \sum_i(J_c-e_{ci})
\]

counts parallel elapsed time multiple times. If A and C both finish before B, both wait over overlapping portions of wall-clock time while B continues. The sum is useful as a measure of aggregate “branch-minutes held at the join,” but is not a case elapsed duration and must not be added into total flow time.

Downstream time after join readiness is:

\[
Q^{down}_c=s_{cF}-J_c
\]

Under the stated model, this is **downstream queueing before the final station**. It is nonnegative in all supplied cases.

Final service is:

\[
S^F_c=e_{cF}-s_{cF}
\]

Total case flow time is:

\[
T_c=e_{cF}-a_c
\]

with non-overlapping elapsed decomposition:

\[
T_c
=U_c+T^{FJ}_c+Q^{down}_c+S^F_c
\]

or equivalently:

\[
T_c
=(r_c-a_c)+(\max_i e_{ci}-r_c)+(s_{cF}-\max_i e_{ci})+(e_{cF}-s_{cF})
\]

Branch queue and service should be reported per branch, but not summed across A, B, and C as if they were sequential.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

All summaries are across 12 cases and are in minutes. The supplied timestamps show no ties in latest branch completion, although the analytical definitions permit them.

| Branch | Mean service | Service range | Mean queue | Queue range | Mean queue + service | Critical cases | Critical frequency | Mean synchronization wait | Max synchronization wait | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A | 50.0 | 50–50 | 0.0 | 0–0 | 50.0 | 2 | 16.7% | 14.3 | 30 | Stable, long technical service; controls only when B/C do not overtake it |
| B | 37.5 | 30–45 | 19.2 | 5–30 | 56.7 | 8 | 66.7% | 5.8 | 17 | Strongest overall completion driver; queueing is materially important |
| C | 20.2 | 18–30 | 10.4 | 5–35 | 30.6 | 2 | 16.7% | 30.2 | 52 | Usually early, but can control completion when administrative queue is extreme |

### Critical-branch frequencies

- B is last-completing in 8 of 12 cases: F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, and F12.
- A is last-completing in 2 cases: F01 and F11.
- C is last-completing in 2 cases: F04 and F08.

Thus, **B is the strongest observed driver of join readiness**. This conclusion is not based solely on B’s mean service time; B’s mean service time is only 37.5 minutes, shorter than A’s fixed 50 minutes. B dominates because its queue delay is large and variable, producing the largest mean elapsed branch time from fork release to completion:

\[
\overline{Q_B+S_B}=56.7
\]

compared with 50.0 for A and 30.6 for C.

### Synchronization-wait interpretation

Mean synchronization wait is not a direct measure of a branch’s intrinsic delay. Rather, it measures how much earlier that branch tends to finish than join readiness.

- C has the highest mean synchronization wait, 30.2 minutes, because it is usually completed well before A or B.
- A has mean synchronization wait of 14.3 minutes because B often overtakes it.
- B has the lowest mean synchronization wait, 5.8 minutes, because it is usually the latest branch.

This reinforces B’s central role: it is least often “waiting for others” and most often the activity others wait for.

### Tail contribution

The largest observed fork-join elapsed time is 75 minutes, in F07 and F12, both controlled by B:

\[
Q_B+S_B=30+45=75
\]

B also creates 65-minute fork-join stages in F03, F06, and F09. In contrast:

- A’s completion contribution is a stable 50 minutes after fork release.
- C’s extreme case is F04: \(35+22=57\) minutes, enough to exceed A’s 50 minutes and become critical.
- C’s F08 elapsed duration is \(25+30=55\) minutes, again just exceeding A’s 50 minutes.

### Influence ranking

1. **B — highest priority.**  
   It is critical in two-thirds of cases, has the largest mean queue-plus-service time, causes both observed 75-minute fork-join tails, and has substantial queue variability. B is therefore the strongest current controller of total completion time.

2. **A — second priority, but a stable baseline constraint.**  
   A is never queued in the supplied data but takes 50 minutes in every case. It is critical in only two cases, yet it establishes a 50-minute floor on join readiness whenever B and C complete sooner. Improvements to A have limited benefit unless they are large enough to move it below B or C in a given case.

3. **C — third overall, but an important exception-risk branch.**  
   C is generally not the dominant elapsed branch, but its queue delay has the largest single observed value, 35 minutes. It becomes critical in F04 and F08. This suggests an administrative queue or release problem may create occasional tail events, even though C has the shortest mean service.

### Interaction with final-station congestion

Observed downstream queueing ranges from 8 to 20 minutes, with mean:

\[
\frac{8+15+15+18+10+15+15+15+15+20+20+10}{12}=14.7
\]

A branch improvement that advances join readiness does not necessarily advance final completion one-for-one if the final station remains constrained. The exact amount depends on final-resource schedules, dispatching, and whether freed capacity is absorbed by other cases; these cannot be fully reconstructed from only the supplied final start/end timestamps.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

The following calculations use explicit simplifying assumptions:

1. Branch starts and all non-targeted durations remain unchanged.
2. If a branch service time is reduced, that branch’s completion moves earlier by the reduction amount.
3. If B queue is reduced, B starts and ends earlier by the reduction amount.
4. Final service remains 20 minutes.
5. For the branch-improvement scenarios, the observed downstream queue is initially held fixed. This gives a direct fork-join benefit estimate. In practice, final-station schedules may partly absorb or amplify the benefit.
6. For the final-station scenario, “eliminating downstream queue” is an upper-bound capacity/release-policy counterfactual, not a claim that a third resource alone necessarily achieves it.

## Counterfactual 1: reduce A service time by 20%

A’s service falls from 50 to 40 minutes, a 10-minute reduction in each case.

A direct total-flow benefit occurs only where reducing A changes the maximum branch completion. Results:

| F07 | B | 0 |
| F08 | C | 0 |
| F09 | B | 0 |
| F10 | B | 0 |
| F11 | A | 10 |
| F12 | B | 0 |

Aggregate direct savings:

\[
15 \text{ minutes across 12 cases} = 1.25 \text{ minutes per case}
\]

Why F01 saves only 5 rather than 10: A ends at 09:00; B ends at 08:55. Moving A to 08:50 makes B the new critical branch at 08:55. The improvement is therefore capped by the 5-minute gap to B.

Why F11 saves the full 10: A ends at 11:30 and B at 11:20. After the reduction, A ends at 11:20, tying B. Join readiness advances by 10 minutes.

**Zero-benefit cases:** all ten cases where B or C remains later than accelerated A.

**Likely secondary effects:** where join readiness moves earlier, cases may reach the final station earlier and potentially encounter a different queue. The fixed-downstream-queue calculation may overstate realized final completion improvement if the final station is already occupied, or understate it if earlier arrival avoids a later busy period.

- The observed final station already has two resources, F1 and F2.

- A true extra resource might not eliminate all waiting if the cause is not resource capacity.

### Comparative interpretation

- **Best branch-stage intervention:** reducing B queueing. It attacks the branch that most often governs join readiness and produces the longest fork-join tails.
- **Largest observed direct waiting pool:** downstream queueing before F. It merits operational investigation because it averages 14.7 minutes per case.
- **Least effective broad intervention:** reducing A service by 20%, because A is frequently masked by B and occasionally by C.

A combined redesign could have interaction effects: B improvements move cases toward the final station earlier, potentially increasing final congestion unless final capacity or dispatching is also improved.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Recommendation 1: Reduce B’s pre-service queue and variability

**Recommendation.** Pilot a B-specific queue-reduction intervention: dedicated or protected B capacity during peak periods, appointment/reservation logic, better dispatching, or a workload-release rule that avoids releasing cases to B when its queue is already growing. The appropriate operational mechanism cannot be selected conclusively from these timestamps alone.

**Why this is prioritized.**

- B is critical in 8 of 12 cases.
- B has the largest mean fork-to-completion time, 56.7 minutes.
- Its mean queue is 19.2 minutes, compared with A’s zero and C’s 10.4.
- B controls both 75-minute fork-join tail cases.
- A modeled 50% B queue reduction produces about 7.7 minutes of direct mean flow-time reduction under fixed downstream waiting.

**Throughput implications.**  
Reducing B queue may increase completed-case throughput if it reflects a genuine B capacity or scheduling constraint. If B capacity is fixed and demand unchanged, queue reduction from better dispatching may primarily smooth waiting and reduce flow time rather than increase long-run throughput. Measuring B utilization and work-in-process would distinguish these possibilities.

**Flow-time-tail implications.**  
Expected tail reduction is strong because the worst observed fork-join times are B-driven. The intervention should be evaluated not only on mean B queue but also on high percentiles of B queue and fork-join elapsed time.

**Resource-utilization risks.**  
Protected B capacity can lower utilization if demand is uneven. Conversely, if B is genuinely overloaded, utilization may already be high and the intervention may require additional capacity rather than better scheduling.

**Potential new bottlenecks.**

- A becomes critical more often once B no longer overtakes it.
- C administrative queues may become more visible, especially cases resembling F04 and F08.
- Earlier join-ready arrivals may increase final-station congestion.

**Synchronization effects.**  
Reducing B delay decreases synchronization waiting for A and C when B is currently critical. It does not eliminate the join: it merely shifts the controlling branch in some cases. Monitoring critical-branch shares before and after the pilot is essential.

**Pilot and measurement.**

1. Measure B resource occupancy, queue length, staffing, and dispatching decisions at finer granularity.
2. Track B queue distribution, not just its mean.
3. Recalculate critical-branch frequency and fork-join tail percentiles after intervention.
4. Observe whether final downstream queue grows as B cases arrive earlier.

## Recommendation 2: Diagnose and reduce final-station delay before committing to capacity expansion

**Recommendation.** Run a short operational study of the final station and pilot a targeted change: improved F1/F2 dispatching, staggered final-release appointments, cross-training, coverage during peaks, or limited additional capacity. Add a permanent resource only if evidence supports a capacity-driven queue.

**Why this is prioritized.**

- Downstream waiting averages 14.7 minutes and reaches 20 minutes in F10 and F11.
- Eliminating it is an upper-bound improvement of 14.7 minutes per case, larger than the modeled B-queue intervention.
- However, the timestamps do not establish that the delay is caused by insufficient final-station capacity.

**Throughput implications.**  
If F is capacity constrained, reducing its queue can increase system throughput and prevent upstream improvements from simply creating a larger final backlog. If the waiting is a release policy, capacity expansion may add little throughput benefit.

**Flow-time-tail implications.**  
Final waiting contributes to many cases, including otherwise relatively short F08 and F11. Reducing it should improve both median flow time and the upper tail, but it will not remove B-driven fork-join tails.

**Resource-utilization risks.**

- An extra F resource may be underutilized because final service is only 20 minutes and arrivals depend on branch synchronization.
- F1/F2 utilization cannot be derived reliably from these complete-event records alone without knowing availability windows and all station work.
- A third resource may not help if queueing is caused by release rules, prioritization, paperwork, or unlogged readiness checks.

**Potential new bottlenecks.**

- Once final waiting falls, B’s long and variable fork-join delay becomes even more dominant.
- Earlier final completions may expose upstream preparation or release constraints.

**Synchronization effects.**  
Final-capacity improvement does not alter branch synchronization. It changes only the time after all three branches have completed. Therefore, it is complementary to B queue reduction rather than a substitute for it.

**Pilot and measurement.**

1. Reconstruct final-resource occupancy from the interval data and compare join-ready arrivals with F1/F2 availability.
2. Record final-station queue reason codes: resource busy, scheduled hold, prioritization, missing approval, or other.
3. Pilot dispatching/cross-coverage before adding permanent capacity.
4. Evaluate actual waiting reduction by case class and by time of day.

A secondary monitoring target is C. It should not be the first broad investment based on these data, but its 35-minute queue in F04 and 25-minute queue in F08 demonstrate that administrative work can become a tail driver.

    df[f"{b}_queue"] = minutes(df[f"{b}_start"] - df["fork_release"])
    df[f"{b}_service"] = minutes(df[f"{b}_end"] - df[f"{b}_start"])
    df[f"{b}_elapsed_from_fork"] = minutes(df[f"{b}_end"] - df["fork_release"])

    latest = row["join_ready"]
    return "+".join([b for b in branches if row[f"{b}_end"] == latest])

df["critical_branch"] = df.apply(critical_branches, axis=1)

# summed into total flow time because parallel waits overlap in elapsed time.

# Verify the non-overlapping elapsed decomposition.

assert np.allclose(df["decomposition_check"], df["total_flow_time"])

# 4. Counterfactuals
# Assumption: unchanged branch starts except where queue reduction explicitly
# moves B start/end; unchanged final service. Baseline downstream queue is held
# fixed for direct-effect calculations. This isolates fork-join benefit but does
# not simulate altered F1/F2 dispatching or resource conflicts.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def recompute_with_modified_ends(base, new_ends, new_final_start=None):
    out = base.copy()
    out["cf_join_ready"] = pd.concat(new_ends, axis=1).max(axis=1)
    if new_final_start is None:
        # Fixed observed downstream queue: direct branch-stage effect only.
        out["cf_final_start"] = out["cf_join_ready"] + pd.to_timedelta(
            out["downstream_queue"], unit="m"
        )
    else:
        out["cf_final_start"] = new_final_start
    out["cf_final_end"] = out["cf_final_start"] + pd.to_timedelta(
        out["final_service"], unit="m"
    )
    out["cf_total_flow"] = minutes(out["cf_final_end"] - out["arrival"])
    out["saving"] = out["total_flow_time"] - out["cf_total_flow"]
    return out

# CF1: A service time reduced 20%; A has a 50-minute service in every case,

a_reduction = (df["A_service"] * 0.20)
cf1 = recompute_with_modified_ends(

cf1["scenario"] = "A service -20%"

cf3["scenario"] = "Final downstream queue -> 0"

    cf1[["case", "scenario", "saving", "cf_join_ready", "cf_total_flow"]],
    cf2[["case", "scenario", "saving", "cf_join_ready", "cf_total_flow"]],
    cf3[["case", "scenario", "saving", "cf_join_ready", "cf_total_flow"]],

counterfactuals["cf_join_ready"] = counterfactuals["cf_join_ready"].dt.strftime("%H:%M")

# 7. Limits of inference

The timestamps support an elapsed-time decomposition under the stated fork-join model, but they do not identify all operational causes.

1. **Causality is not identified.**  
   The data show that B queue delay is associated with later join readiness and that B is frequently critical. They do not prove why B queues occur or prove that a particular intervention will cause the modeled reduction.

2. **Branch resource capacity is unobserved.**  
   There are no branch resource identifiers, calendars, staffing levels, capacities, task assignments, or queue lengths for A, B, or C. A zero observed A queue may indicate abundant capacity, deliberate scheduling, earlier unlogged work, or a timestamp convention rather than guaranteed absence of operational waiting.

3. **Work content and case mix are unknown.**  
   Fixed A service time may reflect standardized work, rounded reporting, or a data construction rule. Variation in B and C service may be caused by case complexity, staff, equipment, rework, or measurement conventions. None can be separated here.

4. **Preemption and interruptions cannot be determined.**  
   A branch interval from start to completion is treated as active service, but it may include setup, pauses, machine downtime, handoffs, or waiting while technically “in progress.” Complete-event data alone do not distinguish uninterrupted processing from elapsed occupied time.

5. **The fork-release interpretation is assumed.**  
   The decomposition assumes `fork_release` is the earliest moment all branches are permitted to begin. If releases are branch-specific, if one branch has additional prerequisites, or if timestamps represent administrative rather than physical release, the queue definitions would need revision.

6. **Join readiness is inferred, not logged.**  
   The analysis defines it as the latest branch completion because the prompt states that F requires all three branches. It cannot detect unlogged quality checks, documentation reconciliation, or decision-preparation work between the branch completions and actual final eligibility.

7. **Final-station waiting may not be a conventional queue.**  
   The quantity

   \[
   final\_start-join\_ready
   \]

   is downstream queueing only under the stated model. It may instead include a scheduled release policy, batching, priority rules, staff breaks, deliberate cooling/holding time, a missing unlogged approval, or an unobserved eligibility condition.

8. **Final capacity cannot be inferred reliably from two named resources alone.**  
   F1 and F2 are recorded, but their availability, shifts, utilization outside these cases, setup times, and dispatching rules are unknown. Adding capacity might reduce waiting substantially, modestly, or not at all.

9. **Counterfactual estimates are direct-effect estimates, not a full queueing simulation.**  
   Holding downstream queue constant isolates branch-level benefit, but real earlier arrivals can change F1/F2 contention. Likewise, eliminating downstream queue is an upper bound rather than a guarantee from adding one resource.

10. **Small-sample and single-day limitations apply.**  
    Twelve cases from one date cannot establish stable long-run averages, weekday patterns, volume effects, seasonality, or the prevalence of rare tail events. The branch ranking is best treated as an evidence-based hypothesis for pilot testing, not a definitive capacity plan.
