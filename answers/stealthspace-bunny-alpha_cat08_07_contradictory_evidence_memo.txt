## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive leadership  
**Subject:** Next decision on Redesign R

**Recommendation: choose E—a guarded hybrid, with D embedded as the learning plan.** Continue the redesign for simple, low-risk houses; restore stronger controls for apartments, old buildings, and other higher-risk cases; and expand only after segment-specific evidence demonstrates that the benefits persist without unacceptable downstream harm.

**The evidence supports retaining R’s benefits, not scaling R unchanged.** Reported median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%, and visible cost fell from €184 to €161. Simple, low-risk houses experienced a six-day median improvement, an eight-day p90 improvement, better first-time-right performance, and fewer complaints.

However, overall p90 and p95 duration worsened to 38.5 and 62 days. First-time-right installation declined from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation rose from 7% to 15%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. Estimated cost including complaints and revisits increased from €198 to €207, but that estimate is incomplete. These are observed associations, not a clean causal comparison: building mix, period, region, attribute completeness, and cost coverage differ.

The process-mining artifacts reinforce caution. Fitness increased to 0.94, while precision fell to 0.51 and generalization to 0.56. High fitness means the model accommodates observed behavior; it does not establish that the redesigned process is desirable or compliant. Alignments involving activation, permits, and downstream complaints require corrective controls.

**Immediate operating decision:**

- **Retain** combined validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership for verified simple, low-risk cases, with shared case records and backup ownership.
- **Pause expansion** of survey skipping in medium- and high-risk buildings until its effects are tested by risk stratum.
- **Suspend billing before successful activation.** Require documented permit checks where applicable and explicitly assign ownership of activation exceptions.
- **Rebalance capacity** toward second visits, where utilization reached 92%, rather than adding load to already highly utilized coordinators.

A full rollback would discard demonstrated simple-house benefits. National scaling would expose more customers to unquantified tail, rework, cost, and activation-control risks. Running experiments without correcting the clearest control gaps would also be inappropriate.

Use concurrent regional controls to evaluate survey skipping, validate the risk classifier, instrument actual work and waiting time, capture exception reasons, and measure complete downstream cost. Review the next expansion decision when each cohort has mature 60-day downstream follow-up. Broader rollout should require noninferior tail duration, quality, complaints, and loaded cost—not merely an improved median.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

1. **High fitness but low precision.**  
   Fitness describes how well a model accommodates observed behavior; precision reflects how narrowly it constrains acceptable behavior. A dominant simplified coordinator path can increase fitness, while a permissive model simultaneously accommodates skipped surveys, manual activation, revisits, and billing before activation. Thus, 0.94 is not “94% correct,” and 0.51 is not a measured failure rate. The lower generalization score further limits confidence in transfer beyond the pilot.

2. **Improved median but worse tail.**  
   Median duration can improve because many straightforward cases become faster while a smaller, riskier group incurs much larger delays. The segment results illustrate this: apartment median duration improved two days, but apartment p90 worsened twelve days; old-building p90 worsened twenty-five days. The growing failed-visit/revisit and post-billing complaint paths provide plausible mechanisms, although their causal contribution is not yet isolated.

3. **Fewer handovers but lower resilience.**  
   A named coordinator reduces formal transfer of ownership. However, exception handling still crosses coordinator, field, activation, and service boundaries. High utilization, centralized expertise, and incomplete case context can turn fewer handovers into queues and single points of failure. Handover efficiency and operational resilience are therefore different properties.

4. **Lower visible cost but possible downstream cost.**  
   The €161 figure excludes downstream complaint handling and may reflect simpler case execution. Additional manual work, revisits, and disputes can shift costs to unmeasured stages or later periods. The incomplete €207 estimate suggests adverse economics but does not establish the full cost either. Workload transfer from field surveys to second visits further complicates comparisons based only on visible case cost.

5. **Simple-house gains but apartment/old-building deterioration.**  
   These are heterogeneous treatment effects, not necessarily contradictory metrics. The redesign may improve coordination for straightforward installations while its survey and activation assumptions fail on older or more complex buildings. Case-mix differences may amplify this pattern, but only concurrent, stratified comparisons can establish its size and cause.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks and conditions under which it is reasonable |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale nationally** | Potentially standardizes ownership, realizes simple-case gains, and simplifies implementation. | Unacceptable now: tail, rework, activation, cost, and capacity risks remain unresolved. Reasonable only after representative evidence meets rollout guardrails across material risk strata. |
| **B. Roll back fully** | Restores baseline process controls and removes exposure to the combined redesign. | Discards demonstrated simple-house benefits and leaves field-survey capacity underused. Could be reasonable for an affected stratum if safe segmentation fails, controls cannot be implemented, or testing shows persistent net harm—not simply because averages worsened. |
| **C. Retain only coordinator ownership** | Removes survey-skipping and post-billing activation changes while preserving accountability benefits. | Coordinator overload and missing-context risks remain; tails and costs need not improve. A useful conservative fallback if the hybrid’s measurement and support infrastructure cannot be deployed, but not a proven complete solution. |
| **D. Run a controlled regional experiment** | Provides stronger causal evidence on survey skipping, measurement gaps, and regional transferability. | Delays simple-case benefits and leaves known activation/permit control gaps uncorrected if used as a substitute for immediate safeguards. Appropriate for uncertain design elements, alongside—not instead of—those safeguards. |
| **E. Guarded hybrid** | Preserves simple-house benefits, bounds exposure, restores higher-risk controls, and generates segment-specific evidence. | Risks include classifier error, limited national scale, and operational complexity. This is the recommended immediate operating model, with D determining which components earn expansion. |

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

The ranking below prioritizes **decision value**, not implementation ease. M7 and basic instrumentation should begin immediately; ranking does not justify waiting for a measurement before applying necessary safeguards.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty resolved and possible decision change |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — M6: Validate the building-risk rule** | Determines whether eligibility can safely identify low-risk cases. Reliable validation supports expansion to verified simple cases; poor calibration confines the redesign to a narrower class or triggers suspension. |
| **2 — M1: Randomize survey skipping by risk stratum** | Establishes the causal effect of skipping on failure, revisits, duration, complaints, and cost while retaining mandatory permit controls. A favorable result enables controlled expansion; harm in a stratum keeps baseline survey controls there. |
| **3 — M4: Measure full downstream cost for 60 days** | Resolves the €161-versus-€207 economic conflict. Persistent savings strengthen rollout; higher complete costs can outweigh duration gains and tighten eligibility despite apparent operational improvement. |
| **4 — M2: Add start timestamps** | Separates execution time from queueing and identifies whether coordinator, field, or activation capacity is driving tails. Queue-driven delays support capacity and backup changes; execution-driven delays point toward training, equipment, or process controls. |
| **5 — M3: Add exception reason codes** | Identifies why visits fail, activation becomes manual, revisits occur, and complaints escalate. This enables targeted corrective action rather than treating all rework as equivalent and can reveal whether M5’s ownership design is the main remedy. |
| **6 — M7: Monitor activation and permit conformance** | Distinguishes actual control failures from logging artifacts and provides immediate release/suspension signals. It is operationally urgent but has lower option value now because existing evidence already justifies pausing unauthorized billing-before-activation. |
| **7 — M5: Test ownership under coordinator absence** | Determines whether coordinator ownership can be retained safely or should become shared-team ownership with better context sharing. It can change the operating model, especially if simple-case rollout increases concentration risk. |
| **8 — M8: Separate customer-experience dimensions** | Prevents faster appointments from masking unreliable service or billing problems. It may change the customer-benefit assessment and remediation priorities, but is less likely than safety or loaded-cost evidence to reverse rollout by itself. |

## 6. Final recommendation

**Select E, implemented through a controlled version of D. Do not nationally scale the complete redesign or fully roll it back.**

**Immediate action**

- Continue the simplified path only for verified simple, low-risk cases, preserving its measured median and p90 benefits.
- Pause wider survey-skipping eligibility, especially for apartments and old buildings.
- Suspend billing before successful activation across redesigned cases.
- Restore documented permit controls where applicable and name an owner for every activation exception.
- Introduce shared case records and tested backup ownership; rebalance some field-survey capacity toward second-visit handling.

**What to scale**

Only the simple-case validation/scheduling improvement and coordinator accountability, with verified risk classification, sustainable capacity, and downstream-quality monitoring. Pilot cases represented a basis for evaluation—not proof of national eligibility or economics.

**What to measure next**

Run M6 and M1 with concurrent, stratified controls; implement M2, M3, and M7 during that work; and use M4 to determine complete economics after each cohort has 60 days of downstream follow-up. M5 and M8 should run as supporting studies.

**Evidence required for broader rollout**

Before expanding any stratum, require:

1. **No unapproved billing before successful activation and documented checks for 100% of applicable permits.** A material breach pauses the affected path pending investigation.
2. **Adequately powered evidence that p90 and p95 are noninferior to matched baseline cases**, using practical margins defined before reviewing results.
3. **First-time-right and complaint performance that meet prespecified noninferiority limits**, while preserving the simple-house gains.
4. **Complete loaded cost that is noninferior and economically acceptable**, preferably lower after complaints, revisits, credits, and disputes mature.
5. **Demonstrated resilience during coordinator absence and sustainable utilization**, rather than performance only under favorable staffing.
6. **Successful replication across relevant building types and regions**, not merely high aggregate model fitness.

If higher-risk cases remain net harmful after valid testing, retain baseline-quality controls there. If simple-house gains disappear under safe capacity limits, roll back the affected simplification rather than scaling on median improvements alone. This preserves demonstrated value while requiring stronger evidence before accepting broader downside risk.
