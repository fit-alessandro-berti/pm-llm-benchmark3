# 1. Disruption timeline

The wholesaler demonstrated selective service resilience, especially for hospitals, but had not achieved complete recovery by T4. Delivery speed improved sooner than traceability, control completeness, and the workload needed to resolve incident orders. The appropriate objective is safe continuity of supply followed by demonstrable recovery of both operations and assurance.

| Period | Operational state and dominant variants | Risk posture |
|---|---|---|
| **T0: Day -30 to -1** | Normal authorization → reservation → pick/pack → release → dispatch → delivery proof → invoice close. Compliance holds, backorders, and rechecks were established exception paths. | A useful reference, not a zero-risk state: traceability and mandatory-control exceptions already existed. Fitness was 0.96 and 91% of traces fitted the normative model. |
| **T1: Day 0 morning** | WMS and parts of order-to-delivery became unavailable. In-flight orders, reservations, and handoffs became uncertain; subsequent work depended on the last available records. | The initial problem was both availability and trust in inventory and transaction state. A cyber incident makes the integrity of an offline snapshot something to establish, not assume. No separate T1 performance figures are provided. |
| **T2: Day 0 afternoon to Day 2** | Emergency triage, hospital fast lanes, manual authorization and inventory checks, spreadsheet picking, temporary release, dispatch without scans, telephone delivery confirmation, and invoice holds. Noncritical work entered deferral queues. | Critical supply was partly protected, but controls became uneven and traceability deteriorated sharply. Fitness fell to 0.71 and fitting traces to 44%. Some deviations represented substitute controls; others represented missing controls. |
| **T3: Day 3 to Day 6** | Order intake returned while inventory synchronization and scanning remained unreliable. Performance suggests an improving but still degraded operation; no T3 DFG is supplied, so its exact variants cannot be established. | The manual/electronic transition creates plausible duplicate-reservation, double-dispatch, and missing-event risks. Hospital fill remained 91% during T2, versus 61% for pharmacies. Together with the cycle-time differences, this supports an interpretation of deliberate protection of critical supply and displacement of delay onto other customers. It does not establish that the fast lane alone caused the outcome or that all protected deliveries were compliant.

By T4, the hospital median had returned to 8 hours, but pharmacy p90 remained 74 versus 49 hours and bulk p90 120 versus 72 hours. Traceability exceptions remained 2.75 times baseline, missing temperature documentation 2.25 times baseline, and missing dual-control 2.5 times baseline. Calling this complete recovery would conceal both service tails and control debt.

The DFGs reinforce that distinction: T2 includes 1,600 transitions into skipping system authorization and 1,800 directly from manual packing to dispatch without scan, while another 1,450 reached that dispatch step through temporary release control. In T4, 1,400 transitions into manual-reservation reconciliation, 1,200 into delivery-proof remediation, and 2,300 from invoice close into audit remediation show continuing recovery work. These frequencies identify important pathways; they do not establish complete case histories or prove that every case on a pathway lacked an equivalent control.

The periods have different lengths, operating conditions, and potentially different case mixes. Their case totals cannot be used as comparable daily throughput rates. DFG edges are aggregate adjacencies, not necessarily unique orders or a flow-conserving ledger. In particular, T2 has 2,100 transitions from spreadsheet invoice hold to END: a recorded endpoint is not evidence of normal financial or compliance completion. Cohort boundaries, delayed recording, and unresolved cases must be checked before treating cycle-time improvements as like-for-like recovery.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is the ability to adapt while preserving essential service and the safety purposes of required controls, then recover without unbounded unresolved obligations. A changed activity name or a paper record can be resilient if it provides adequate authorization, release, identification, custody, and product-condition assurance. Throughput preserved by omitting these functions is a risky shortcut, even if no harm has yet been detected.

**Strong candidates for resilience are B, I, and J**, when their eligibility checks and priority rules are reliable and explicit. **K and L are essential recovery capabilities**, provided they expose and resolve uncertainty honestly. Their existence does not establish that earlier shipments were compliant. A is also a valuable resilience mechanism once hospital priority is separated from permission to bypass required controls.

**C is the clearest risky shortcut as observed.** It replaces verified authorization with an assumption. Undocumented versions of G, release sampling under F that leaves mandatory checks undone, and E or L that backfill fictional events also constitute concealed noncompliance. An authorized emergency operating mode should not turn a missing control into a successful one by relabeling it.

**A, D, E, F, G, and H are conditional adaptations.** Their acceptability depends on the reliability of substitute evidence, elapsed outage time, product risk, and capacity to operate the alternative controls. D can be defensible early in T2 and unsafe later without movement reconciliation. G can be defensible without an electronic scan if a complete manual chain of custody exists. H can provide useful delivery intelligence while remaining insufficient for controlled-product assurance. Risk appetite can govern additional delay, cost, manual effort, and explicitly assessed uncertainty within permitted controls; it cannot establish that an unperformed mandatory check occurred.

Several abductive explanations fit the observations, and recovery should test them:

- **Hospital protection:** smaller hospital delays and 91% fill are consistent with successful prioritization and rerouting. Easier product mix, available stock, or preferential handling of readily fulfillable orders could also explain part of the result. Compare urgent orders with similar products, availability, and clinical need, including canceled and deferred demand.
- **Falling exception rates:** the reduction from 9.5% traceability exceptions in T2 to 2.2% in T4 is consistent with improved control. It could also partly reflect late data entry, changed detection, or a different case mix. Inspect original dispatch and receiving evidence and compare event-occurrence time with recording time.
- **More recovery paths:** elevated holds and rechecks in T4 may reflect accumulated defects or stronger detection. The larger remediation burden is not, by itself, evidence that the recovery controls made operations worse. Link each task to its originating incident phase and defect.
- **Missing records versus missing actions:** missing temperature documentation does not prove a temperature excursion; a manually documented release may have been performed despite a missing electronic event. Conversely, a later-entered event does not prove the original control was performed. Resolve these alternatives from contemporaneous evidence and retain uncertainty where that evidence does not exist.

Conformance findings locate these issues but do not decide them. T2 move-on-log activities such as phone delivery confirmation or invoice hold may be sensible adaptations; skipping authorization may not be. Move-on-model gaps in release, scan, delivery proof, or temperature recording require investigation into whether the control was absent or implemented differently. Assess traces against both the normal model and an approved emergency model, with non-negotiable product-control constraints applied to both. High fitness without genuine control evidence is insufficient; lower fitness with adequate substitute controls need not mean failed resilience.

The temporal deviations are particularly important. Long backlog-to-pick waits reveal displaced service harm; variable dispatch-to-phone-confirmation delays reveal a custody/evidence lag; invoice-close-to-audit-remediation reveals work excluded from ordinary cycle-time reporting. Recovery must address all three rather than optimize invoice close alone.

# 4. Recovery process model

Use an explicit operating-mode state machine: **normal → controlled manual operation → controlled hybrid operation → stabilized operations → incident assurance closure**. Any loss of critical control can move an affected lane back into manual operation or hold. Different warehouses and product lanes may advance at different times. These are readiness gates, not automatic transitions on a calendar date.

**1. Detect, contain, and declare the mode.**

- Trigger incident mode immediately when cyber compromise makes a critical workflow untrusted, or when authorization, reservation, release, or custody recording cannot be completed reliably. Escalate capacity management when forecast queues threaten agreed clinical service limits, even if systems are technically available.
- Appoint an incident commander, a quality/compliance decision owner, an inventory owner, a transport lead, and an IT/security restoration owner. Record the declaration time, affected functions, approved alternatives, and next review time.
- Isolate affected systems under the cyber-response process. Identify in-flight orders and pause affected releases long enough to establish their physical and control status. Quarantine stock whose identity, condition, or custody is unresolved.
- Activate trusted offline reference data and numbered transaction records. If necessary controls cannot be operated locally, route urgent demand to a verified alternate facility or supplier with the required handling capability.

**2. Register demand and triage before allocation.**

Record a unique incident order identifier, original order identifier if available, receipt time, requested delivery time, customer and destination, product/lot requirements, quantity, and clinical urgency. Check for duplicates, including orders resubmitted by phone after an electronic submission failed.

Prioritize by expected harm from delay, time to customer stock-out, available alternatives, and delivery deadline. Hospital-critical status is a strong signal, but permit verified urgent pharmacy needs and bulk consignments supporting critical care to enter the urgent lane. Product risk determines handling controls and specialist routing; it does not automatically justify dispensing with them. Record the reason for every priority override.

Within comparable urgency, use oldest promised deadline and then order age. Reserve capacity for essential pharmacy demand and for aging noncritical orders. Reassess deferred work at each operating review, provide a revised promise, and escalate before the agreed maximum waiting time is breached.

**3. Apply minimum controls at every product/customer intersection.**

| Risk category | Minimum controls before dispatch and through receipt |
|---|---|
| **Every order, including ambient** | Verify customer/product authorization and destination against trusted current evidence; confirm available, eligible stock; identify product, quantity, batch/lot, expiry, and relevant hold/recall status; perform documented pick/pack verification and release; capture custody handoff and attributable receiving evidence. Record who performed each control and when. |
| **Cold-chain** | All common controls plus validated packaging and route duration, a linked temperature-monitoring record/device, appropriate storage during queues, and documented review of available pre-release temperature evidence. Maintain monitoring through transport and verify receipt condition. Hold affected stock when required temperature assurance or suitable transport is unavailable. |
| **Controlled-substance** | All common controls plus applicable customer/product permissions, distinct authenticated dual-control participants, controlled quantities and secure storage/transport, a stock-movement ledger, and documented transfers to authorized recipients. A single release signature or phone confirmation cannot substitute for required dual control or custody evidence. |
| **Recall-sensitive** | All common controls plus verified current recall/hold information, exact lot-to-order-to-recipient linkage, segregation of affected stock, and the ability to locate or intercept shipments promptly. If applicable recall status cannot be established, hold the affected stock or source verified replacement stock. |
| **Hospital-critical or other verified urgent demand** | Expedited access to authorization, pharmacist review, stock verification, and transport; frequent updates and escalation to qualified alternative supply. Apply all product controls above. Customer urgency changes queue position, not the minimum release standard. |

For products with multiple risk attributes, apply the combined controls. Sampling can supplement this minimum for appropriate low-risk checks; it must not replace an order's required authorization, release, temperature assurance, or dual control.

**4. Execute manual fulfillment with contemporaneous evidence.**

Follow: **manual authorization → verified reservation → pick → pack → risk-specific checks → signed release → documented courier handoff → verified receipt**. A failed gate routes to an identified hold owner for resolution, qualified substitution/rerouting, or a reason-coded cancellation.

Use a single controlled ledger or explicitly partitioned ledgers with one reservation authority per stock pool. Treat the last trusted snapshot as an opening balance and record every subsequent movement. An unaccounted movement, conflicting allocation, count discrepancy, or uncertain stock status triggers physical verification before further affected allocations. Do not rely on the original snapshot simply because the outage is still designated T2.

Each evidence packet should contain the order and line identifiers, product/lot/quantity, authorization source and version, decision and operator, reservation and stock movements, pick/pack checks, release decision, required second-person checks, temperature-device linkage, courier and destination, seal where relevant, custody times, and receiving evidence. Use controlled forms or an approved secure offline tool, access controls, and correction history.

Capture both **when the action occurred** and **when it was recorded/imported**. Number and retain original paper records. Later transcription must link to those originals; reconstructed or inferred information must be visibly labeled with its source and uncertainty. Missing evidence triggers an exception immediately rather than first appearing after invoice close.

**5. Control backlog and capacity continuously.**

Maintain separate visible queues for eligible orders awaiting capacity, orders blocked by a control or stock issue, and orders awaiting post-dispatch evidence. Display original age and promised deadline even after reclassification. Review all queues at least each shift and more often for urgent or temperature-sensitive work.

Escalation triggers include an urgent-order deadline at risk, a deferred order approaching its maximum agreed wait, shortages of qualified control staff, or remediation arrivals exceeding sustainable resolution capacity. Responses include qualified surge staffing, verified alternative supply, limited intake with communicated promises, and deferral of lower-urgency work. Do not increase dispatch faster than the capacity to provide required evidence and controls.

**6. Enter hybrid operation only after integrity and transaction checks.**

Order-intake restoration alone is not a failback trigger. IT/security must establish that the restored services and relevant data are trustworthy; operations and quality must verify authorization data, inventory state, scan-to-lot linkage, release gating, and custody recording using representative end-to-end transactions for each affected product lane.

Select a cutover boundary by site, lane, or order range. For each boundary, designate whether the manual ledger or restored system is authoritative. Match incident identifiers to system identifiers, suppress duplicate orders and reservations, import evidence with original timestamps and provenance, and reconcile in-flight work. Avoid independent manual and electronic allocation of the same stock. Unreliable inventory or scanning keeps the affected lane in controlled manual operation.

**7. Reconcile and remediate through separate assurance closure.**

For every affected order and stock movement, match **demand → authorization → reservation → physical movement → release → dispatch/custody → receipt → financial state**. Prioritize gaps with potential patient exposure, controlled stock discrepancies, temperature uncertainty, and inability to locate recall-sensitive lots; then address aging and lower-consequence discrepancies. Assign each item an owner, deadline, required evidence, and disposition.

Route findings as follows:

- **A control was performed and original evidence exists:** validate and import the evidence, retaining source and timestamps; independently review high-risk corrections.
- **Records conflict:** investigate the physical stock, customer receipt, courier record, and financial state; correct with an audit trail and prevent duplicate fulfillment or billing.
- **A control was missed or cannot be evidenced:** retain the exception. Quality assesses product and recipient impact and directs containment, contact, retrieval/recall assessment, reporting where applicable, and corrective action. A later check may help determine current risk but cannot retroactively perform a pre-dispatch control.

Track delivery complete, financial close, and assurance status separately. Invoice closure must not clear the incident exception. Assurance disposition should distinguish verified control performance from an unresolved historical gap whose consequences and corrective actions have been formally addressed; neither category should erase the original deviation.

**8. Exit recovery using evidence-based gates.**

Stabilized operations require reliable systems, reconciled authoritative stock, functioning mandatory controls, and service/backlog measures within agreed limits across customer groups for a pre-agreed observation window. Set that window before failback; two representative operating shifts could be an initial operational test, with longer observation for service tails and evidence delays.

Incident assurance closure requires a complete affected-order register, no unresolved immediate product-safety or controlled-custody exposure, and every exception given a reviewed disposition. Any remaining longer-term corrective work needs named ownership, deadlines, and management visibility. Report it explicitly as residual recovery work. A recovered hospital median or improved fitness alone cannot satisfy either exit gate.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Measure performance by customer group, clinical urgency, product risk, warehouse, operating mode, and order-origin cohort. Also report activity by the phase in which it was performed, so incident orders processed in T4 do not disappear into an apparently clean stabilization cohort. Preserve original receipt dates through deferral, cancellation, and re-entry.

| Dimension | Leading indicators and intervention use | Lagging indicators and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughput and sustainable capacity** | Demand versus capacity for picking, qualified authorization/release, transport, and evidence processing; forecast bottlenecks; proportion of orders ready to pass release gates. | Unique orders/lines dispatched and delivered per staffed operating hour; separately report the proportion with complete required controls. Show raw physical output and control-complete output together. Case totals across unequal periods are insufficient. |
| **Service protection** | Urgent demand expected to miss clinical need-by time; customer stock-out horizon; qualified alternative-supply capacity; high-risk transport availability. | Hospital and other urgent fill rate, on-time-in-full delivery, median/p90 and extreme delays, and cancellations by reason. Use all eligible demand in the agreed fill-rate denominator, including deferred or canceled demand, to avoid hiding unmet need. T2's 91% hospital versus 61% pharmacy fill is a starting comparison, not a complete assessment of clinical harm. |
| **Traceability** | Completeness of order-line-lot-recipient and custody links before release; missing handoff evidence at shift review; age of unrecorded physical movements. | Traceability exception rate, time to locate an affected lot or reconstruct custody from authentic evidence, and shipments whose recipients remain unverified. Separate genuinely missing evidence from delayed import. T4's 2.2% remains materially above the 0.8% baseline. |
| **Control completeness** | Trusted authorization-list freshness; stock-ledger discrepancies; required signer and temperature-device availability; attempted releases blocked for missing controls. | Authorization, release, temperature, dual-control, and receiving-evidence completeness using the appropriate eligible-product denominator. Separate performed and evidenced, reportedly performed but unproven, and known omitted controls. The target at a mandatory release gate is complete compliance; baseline defects are not an acceptable allowance. |
| **Backlog age and fairness** | Oldest age, p90 age, time until deadline, repeated deferrals, capacity allocation, and priority overrides by group and urgency; urgent pharmacy orders trapped in standard queues. | Waiting-time distributions, fill, cancellations, and missed promises across groups, adjusted for clinical urgency and product constraints. Publish both absolute group outcomes and outcomes for comparable-risk orders; equal service is not required where clinical need differs, but unexplained disadvantage needs correction. |
| **Rework and reconciliation** | Unmatched manual/system identifiers, duplicate reservations, physical-count discrepancies, correction arrivals versus resolution capacity. | Unique cases requiring correction, repeat corrections, staff hours per affected case, inventory adjustments, duplicate dispatches, and financial corrections. T4 rework of 5.8% versus 3.5% baseline indicates residual burden even after system restoration. |
| **Assurance debt and recovery duration** | Open exceptions by severity, age and evidence availability; high-risk exceptions past deadline; remediation staffing and projected time to clear. | Time from dispatch or invoice close to reviewed assurance disposition, oldest unresolved case, total remediation hours/cost, unresolved historical gaps, and time to operational versus assurance recovery. Maintain both clocks through the audit tail. |
| **Clinical/product outcome and detection quality** | Temperature excursions awaiting review, custody breaks, new recall matches, and customer reports awaiting investigation. | Confirmed mishandling, unauthorized supply, loss/diversion, recall-location failures, complaints, and substantiated patient impact. No reported harm is weak reassurance if receiving and traceability evidence are incomplete. |

During an incident, review immediate control breaches continuously and publish service, backlog, and exception dashboards at least each shift. A mandatory-control failure triggers a hold for the affected order or lane; rising queue age or unequal service triggers capacity or priority review. Quality, operations, and management should agree numeric service and aging thresholds before an incident, by clinical and product risk, rather than choosing thresholds after seeing performance.

For recovery reporting, include open-order aging and canceled demand alongside completed-order cycle times to address survivor bias and right-censoring. Report denominators and case mix for each rate; cold-chain and controlled-substance percentages should not be compared as if they covered all orders. Audit samples should verify record authenticity and operating-mode classification. Compare normal-model fitness and emergency-model fitness, but never substitute either for direct control-completeness evidence.

Implement the P0 measures first. Build the manual kit, triage rules, and assurance queue together so the process is usable under actual staffing constraints. Start hybrid-recovery engineering early because it takes longer, and test the complete process before relying on it during another incident.

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept explicit, time-limited deferral of lower-urgency demand, higher manual operating costs, and slower dispatch where necessary to sustain verified critical supply. It should not accept assumed authorization, unverified required product controls, undocumented custody, fabricated retrospective events, or recovery declarations that omit unresolved service and assurance work. The evidence supports retaining controlled manual operations and critical-order prioritization while replacing the unsafe variants and funding the recovery tail. Authentic contemporaneous evidence demonstrating equivalent controls could justify wider use of manual dispatch; confirmed product harm, custody failures, unacceptable pharmacy stock-outs, or a remediation queue growing beyond available capacity would justify tighter limits and more alternative sourcing. Expansion and final recovery decisions should depend on verified controls and service outcomes across risk groups, not hospital median cycle time alone.
