# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The apparent contradiction around Mira_Coordinator is not a data error by itself; it is what should be expected when different organizational networks measure different relationships. Handover measures *sequential transfer between successive actors in a trace*; working-together measures *co-presence in the same case*; subcontracting measures *delegation followed by return to the delegator*. A person who performs many brief routing acts for many different people can therefore be central in the first and third networks while being peripheral in the second.

All three networks must also be read with process-mining caveats: an edge does not necessarily mean direct communication, a vendor or bot is not a person, and a short logged service time is not the same as low overall impact. The event log also contains an activity, `Assign Specialist`, attributed to Mira_Coordinator that is not in the initial 14-activity list; this needs clarification before formal role labels are assigned.

## 1.1 Handover-of-work network

In the handover-of-work network, an edge from A to B means that, in a case trace, an event by B directly followed an event by A. It is a sequential work-transfer measure, not proof of conversation, accountability, or endorsement.

For Mira_Coordinator, this network shows a strong switchboard position:

- Handover out-degree centrality is 0.82, in-degree centrality is 0.51, and betweenness is 0.77 — the highest values in the supplied table.
- The most important incoming edge is Bot_Rules  Mira_Coordinator, 920 cases, with a mean gap of 3.5 minutes.
- Her main outgoing edges are to adjusters — Carla 350, Diego 330, Elise 290, Farid 280 — and to specialists — Omar_Medical 210, Priya_Legal 180.
- There are return edges from specialists to her: Omar  Mira 160 and Priya  Mira 140.
- Her 960 events cover 905 distinct cases, a ratio of about 1.06 events per touched case, consistent with brief, shallow touches rather than end-to-end case ownership.

What this implies: she sits on many paths connecting automation, adjusters, and specialists. In particular, Bot_Rules  Mira is far more specific and frequent than an informal emergence would suggest; it looks consistent with designed exception routing.

What it does not imply: it does not prove she is the most productive worker, the most influential decision-maker, or a supervisor. High betweenness can be produced by a star topology in which she links otherwise separate groups, even if each individual contact is short. Betweenness is also inflated when alternative direct links are absent; it is not a measure of quality or legitimacy.

The handover network also shows that she is not end-to-end: Alice_TeamLead  Vendor_Pay (510) and Bruno_TeamLead  Vendor_Pay (480) are the payment-release edges. Bot_Ingest  Bot_Rules (4040) is the dominant automated entry path, and Bot_Rules itself has betweenness 0.41. Mira is therefore a human bridge inside a larger automated and vendor-mediated structure, not the whole structure.

Finally, temporal adjacency can overstate handovers when activities run in parallel or in batches. If a specialist review occurs while an adjuster continues work, the “next” resource in the trace may not actually receive the work from the previous one. Sampling of actual traces and lifecycle transitions is required to distinguish system reassignment, human delegation, and mere ordering.

## 1.2 Working-together network

The working-together network, as supplied, is undirected and based on the percentage of cases in which two resources appear together. It measures co-presence in a case, not direct sequence, collaboration intensity, or communication.

Here Mira looks peripheral:

- Weighted degree centrality is 0.16, compared with Carla_Adjuster 0.49, Diego_Adjuster 0.47, Alice_TeamLead 0.46, and Bruno_TeamLead 0.43.
- Her co-case rates are low: with Carla 8%, Diego 7%, Elise 6%, Farid 6%, Alice 5%, and Bruno 4%.
- Contrast this with stable team cores: Alice–Carla 27%, Bruno–Diego 26%, Alice–Elise 24%, Bruno–Farid 22%, Carla–Elise 20%, Diego–Farid 19%.
- Omar–Priya co-occur in only 4% of cases, confirming that specialists are rarely jointly involved.

This low working-together profile does not contradict high handover centrality; it helps interpret it. Mira’s incoming and outgoing handovers are spread across many partners, teams, specialists, and temporary staff. Each individual pairwise co-occurrence is therefore diluted. She is not embedded in one stable team in the way Alice–Carla or Bruno–Diego are. Her pattern is closer to a cross-cutting exception contact than to a case-team member.

It also does not prove that she works “alone” in a social sense. The operational evidence explicitly notes that many of her events are short routing, assignment, reopen, or clarification events. A three-minute assignment followed by another person’s 22-minute assessment creates a handover but may not create sustained co-working. Conversely, the co-occurrence measure gives one brief touch the same case-level presence as a long joint investigation, so duration and sequencing still have to be examined.

The network also distinguishes her from the team leads. Alice and Bruno combine high handover betweenness with high working-together degree; they look like team anchors. Mira combines high handover betweenness with low working-together degree; she looks more like a broker or dispatcher than a team member.

## 1.3 Subcontracting network

The subcontracting network with `n=2` looks for patterns of the form A  B  A within a short window. It is informative about delegation followed by return, but in a claims process it can also capture consultation, approval loops, or reopening loops rather than legal subcontracting.

Mira has the highest subcontracting betweenness, 0.69, and the two largest listed subcontracting patterns involve her:

- Mira  Omar_Medical  Mira: 126 cases, fragment: Request Missing Information  Medical Review  Assign Specialist/Reopen.
- Mira  Priya_Legal  Mira: 98 cases, fragment: Escalate Complaint  Legal Review  Reopen/Assign Specialist.

This supports the interpretation that she starts specialist involvement and receives the answer back. That is stronger than a one-way referral: the case thread returns to her, at least often enough to generate these patterns.

It does not prove that she formally “outsources” accountability, nor that she selects specialists without rules. The return event may be a reopen, assignment, or clarification logged under her resource ID rather than her personally reviewing the specialist’s work. Similarly, Alice  Carla  Alice (86) and Bruno  Diego  Bruno (82) have fragments involving Approve/Negotiate/Approve; these are probably approval cycles, not subcontracting in an external sense. Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules (62) suggests automation–human–automation exception handling, not ordinary subcontracting. The algorithm’s label should not be taken literally.

The subcontracting result also does not tell us who actually chose the specialist, whether a rule engine suggested the choice, or whether the specialist could be requested directly by an adjuster in cases outside Mira’s queue. Those mechanisms require trace-level and attribute-level tests.

## 1.4 Performance/time statistics

Performance statistics separate into service time, waiting time, and total cycle time; these should not be merged.

Mira’s median service time is 4.2 minutes, short compared with adjusters at 17–24 minutes, Omar at 37 minutes, and Priya at 51 minutes. Vendor_Verify has a 2.1-hour median and Vendor_Pay 1.3 hours, while Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules are measured in seconds. For bots and vendors, the logged “service time” may largely reflect system elapsed time, file transfer, or external turnaround rather than human effort.

Mira is associated with difficult cases: 48% of her cases have exception_flag=True, compared with 9% for Vendor_Pay, 13–14% for the temporary staff, and roughly 21–26% for the team leads. She touches only 22% of all claims but 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. The table also labels her average case-cycle-time contribution as “high variance.”

This does not mean that her 4.2-minute services cause long cycle times. The more plausible statistical interpretation is selection and confounding by complexity: difficult cases are routed to her, then wait for adjusters, specialists, vendors, customers, or approvals. Her service itself is short; the potentially important delay may occur *before* she acts, *after* she hands over, or while the case waits for a specialist or vendor.

The six-day absence is the strongest quasi-experimental signal. Ordinary low-value claims were not materially affected, but the 90th percentile cycle time for exception_flag=True cases rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. That pattern is consistent with a selective exception-handling function rather than a universal bottleneck. It is not a randomized experiment, however: backlog accumulation, case-mix changes, compensatory overtime, and simultaneous operational changes could contribute. It supports dependence for exception cases, but does not by itself prove that she is slow, inefficient, or the sole cause of delay.

## 1.5 Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

No activity-based similarity matrix or organizational-role clustering result is supplied, so any role-discovery statement must be treated as an inference to be tested rather than an observed result.

Her activity profile is unusually broad: Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Assign Specialist. By contrast, Omar and Priya are single-activity specialists; Carla and Diego share Assess Damage/Negotiate Settlement; Elise and Farid share Assess Damage/Request Missing Information; Alice and Bruno share Triage/Approve patterns; Temp_1 and Temp_2 focus on validation, missing information, and closure. A similarity algorithm would therefore be expected to separate Mira from the adjusters, from the single-activity specialists, and probably from Alice/Bruno despite the coordinator label.

That expected distinctness supports a specialized exception role, but activity-profile similarity has limits:

- It measures what activities a resource performs, not authority, skill, judgement, or accountability.
- Specialists with one activity can look artificially similar or dissimilar because their profiles are not normalized for case complexity.
- Bots and vendors must not be clustered with humans as if they were employees. Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules form an automation cluster; Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay are external service actors.
- The unexplained `Assign Specialist` activity could materially change the similarity result if it is a system-generated assignment rather than a human-performed activity.
- Role discovery can identify a descriptive cluster; it cannot validate a formal job title.

Overall, the role-discovery expectation is that Mira forms a small, cross-cutting coordination/exception cluster, distinct from both the North and South adjuster teams and from the specialist pair. It does not establish whether that cluster is formal, informal, designed, or emergent.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

## 2.1 Formal coordinator / exception desk

**Mechanism.** Automation and team leads route exceptions, complaints, reopen requests, and missing-information problems to a designated exception desk. Mira briefly clarifies, reopens, reassigns, or sends the case to a specialist, after which the receiving adjuster or specialist retains the substantive work.

**Evidence supporting it.** Bot_Rules  Mira has 920 handovers. Her exception share is 48%; she touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Specialist answers return to her. The absence results selectively affected exception cases. The process owner’s “queue cleaner” description is consistent with a formal first-line exception queue.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** She works with both teams and all adjusters at similar rates, rather than being clearly subordinated to Alice or Bruno. Her co-occurrence with Alice and Bruno is only 5% and 4%. She does not perform Approve Settlement or Issue Payment. The unexplained `Assign Specialist` activity and the absence of a documented routing rule mean that formality cannot be assumed from the log alone.

**Observable predictions.** If she is a formal desk, filtering to exception_flag=True should preserve her centrality, while filtering to routine cases should sharply reduce it. Her share of handovers should be stable across weeks, regions, and channels after controlling for case mix. Work should arrive through a queue with identifiable assignment rules, and a trained deputy should be able to reproduce most routing decisions.

## 2.2 Organizational boundary spanner or triage broker

**Mechanism.** Mira connects otherwise weakly connected domains: Bot_Rules, the North and South teams, junior and senior adjusters, medical and legal specialists, and temporary staff. She translates an automated exception signal into a human owner and may translate specialist answers back into case action.

**Evidence supporting it.** Her handover betweenness is 0.77 and subcontracting betweenness is 0.69, the highest in the table. She hands over to both teams and both specialists. Her working-together profile is dispersed rather than team-bound. Return edges from Omar and Priya indicate that specialist knowledge is brokered back into the case.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** A boundary spanner usually integrates parties, but her low working-together degree suggests limited sustained joint work. She has no direct handover edge to Vendor_Pay and no listed route to Vendor_Verify, so the brokerage does not span the whole external boundary. Equal handovers to northern and southern adjusters could reflect a generic pool rather than brokerage across distinct organizational cultures.

**Observable predictions.** Traces should show her appearing at transitions between activity clusters: after Fraud Screen or Triage, before Medical/Legal Review, and after specialist answers before reopening or reassignment. Cases crossing from automation to specialists should pass through her more often than cases requiring direct adjuster approval. Removing her from the handover graph should disconnect or lengthen several automation–specialist paths, while direct Alice/Bruno approval paths remain intact.

## 2.3 Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

**Mechanism.** Exception cases depend on one person’s availability, tacit knowledge, or discretionary prioritization. Even if her own service is brief, cases wait for her decision before moving to the next actor, making her a decision bottleneck rather than a processing bottleneck.

**Evidence supporting it.** She has extreme betweenness in both handover and subcontracting networks. The absence period increased the 90th percentile cycle time for exception cases from 8.4 to 13.9 days. She is associated with complaints and reopened claims, where delays are especially consequential. The process owner’s description of a queue cleaner and the adjusters’ “knows where hard cases should go” both imply non-routine knowledge.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** Routine low-value claims were unaffected during her absence, so this is not a general throughput bottleneck. Her service time is short, and many cases have alternative routes through Alice, Bruno, and adjusters. High cycle-time contribution is confounded by exception status and specialist involvement. No data are supplied on her queue length, utilization, working hours, or waiting time specifically attributable to her.

**Observable predictions.** Waiting time after Bot_Rules  Mira and after Mira  next resource should rise with her hourly or daily event load. Queue age should be higher at weekends, holidays, and unusual working hours. Exceptions should clear faster when a deputy or alternate coordinator is active. If delays are instead concentrated after specialist or vendor handover, the bottleneck hypothesis weakens.

## 2.4 Quality gatekeeper and automation monitor

**Mechanism.** Mira checks automated triage and fraud-screen outputs, requests missing information, challenges premature closure, and reopens cases when specialist answers or complaints reveal problems. She acts as a human control point over automation rather than as a general coordinator.

**Evidence supporting it.** Bot_Rules  Mira is the dominant human inflow. The Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules subcontracting fragment, 62 cases, is explicitly described as Fraud Screen  manual exception  Fraud Screen update. Her main activities include Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, and Escalate Complaint. Her exception share is high, and specialist answers return to her before reopening or reassignment.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** She does not personally perform Fraud Screen. Her events are short, which may indicate routing rather than substantive quality review. Temp_1, Temp_2, and vendors also perform validation and closure-related work, so quality control is distributed. There is no supplied evidence that her interventions improve fraud detection, reduce reopenings, or prevent payment errors.

**Observable predictions.** Mira’s events should be concentrated immediately after Fraud Screen, Triage, or exception flags, especially on high priority or high amount_band cases. Her reopen decisions should be followed by measurable changes in the subsequent activity sequence, such as specialist review or additional information requests. If she is only an automation monitor, the Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules loop should be a substantial share of her activity; if she is a broader coordinator, adjuster and specialist loops should dominate.

## 2.5 Data or system artifact / queue-owner attribution

**Mechanism.** The event log may attribute system-generated assignments, queue ownership, or escalation ownership to Mira’s resource ID even when the work is initiated by rules, another person, or a shared mailbox. Her apparent centrality may partly reflect how `org:resource` is populated.

**Evidence supporting it.** Many of her events are very short. `Assign Specialist` is not in the original activity taxonomy. Handover and subcontracting algorithms may treat system reassignment as human transfer. The low working-together rate and frequent disappearance after a touch are compatible with queue-owner metadata.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** A pure artifact would not easily explain the real absence effect on exception-case cycle times, the concentration in complaints and reopenings, or the reciprocal specialist edges. Adjusters’ comments about her knowledge also suggest a real human function, although perceptions are not proof.

**Observable predictions.** Her timestamps may cluster in batches, align with system assignment events, or show lifecycle transitions inconsistent with human work. Certain activities may have identical timestamps or durations across many cases. If a system change reassigns the queue-owner field, her centrality may collapse without a corresponding operational change. Trace inspection should reveal whether the same individual also appears as the human actor in adjacent events.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The evidence is most compatible with a designed or emergent exception-routing function that also creates key-person dependence; it is not sufficient to choose among formal design, informal emergence, and system attribution.

# 3. Risk assessment

## Bottleneck risk

The risk is selective rather than universal. Routine claims do not appear to depend on her, but exception cases may depend on a prompt routing decision. Because her service time is short, the relevant risk is decision latency and queue prioritization, not the 4.2-minute service itself. The absence effect on the 90th percentile supports this risk for complex cases, but the size of the effect is uncertain because case mix and backlog dynamics were not controlled.

## Coordination risk

A brokered process can lose context when work passes through a short-touch intermediary. Mira hands cases to adjusters and specialists, but has low co-presence with them, and the case then disappears from her view for several events. This creates risks of fragmented ownership, repeated missing-information requests, duplicated specialist questions, and unclear responsibility after specialist answers return. The subcontracting loops suggest that she often regains the thread, which mitigates but does not eliminate the risk.

## Key-person risk

This is the clearest organizational risk. High betweenness, specialized exception knowledge, cross-team reach, and the absence effect together indicate dependence on one person’s availability and tacit judgement. The risk is heightened because low working-together degree implies that her knowledge is not broadly embedded in stable teams. It does not automatically mean she is irreplaceable; it means replaceability has not been demonstrated.

## Compliance/control risk

The compliance concern is that Mira may both identify problems and select the next specialist or adjuster, concentrating discretion. That concentration creates a control vulnerability: selection criteria, overrides, and conflicts of interest may be difficult to audit if they are not documented. The evidence does **not** show wrongdoing, favoritism, or manipulation. It shows only that a potentially sensitive decision is concentrated. The proportionate response is an audit trail, explicit criteria, and periodic review, not an accusation.

## Workload fairness risk

There are two fairness issues. First, Mira receives a disproportionate share of difficult, emotionally demanding exception work; this may be invisible if workload is measured only by event count. Second, her assignments may allocate easier or harder cases unevenly to adjusters, specialists, or teams. The supplied data do not show that allocations are unfair: adjuster event counts are broadly similar, and she hands work to all four adjusters. Fairness cannot be assessed without controlling for priority, amount_band, case_type, region, channel, exception status, and expected effort.

## Data-quality risk

Data-quality risk is material. Handover edges may reflect trace ordering rather than actual communication; bots and vendors are recorded as resources; `Assign Specialist` is missing from the activity list; service time for bots and vendors may not be comparable to human service time; exception_flag may be assigned at different process stages; and subcontracting `n=2` can capture approval loops. If these issues are ignored, centrality may be over-interpreted as authority, activity similarity may be mistaken for skill, and cycle-time contribution may be mistaken for causation.

A related automation and vendor risk should also be monitored: Bot_Rules concentrates initial routing discretion, Vendor_Verify may generate long waiting periods, and Vendor_Pay is a terminal sink whose upstream accountability rests with Alice and Bruno rather than Mira. Removing or blaming only Mira would miss these structural dependencies.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

The analyses below should begin with `pm4py.format_dataframe`, with explicit timestamp parsing, case sorting, and checks of lifecycle transitions. Bots and vendors should be analyzed both included and excluded, because omitting them changes betweenness and role clusters.

## Handover-of-work network

Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` on the full log, then repeat after:

- filtering exception_flag=True and exception_flag=False with `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values`;
- filtering by region, priority, amount_band, case_type, and channel;
- splitting the absence period from comparable baseline periods;
- removing Bot_Ingest, Bot_Rules, Vendor_Verify, and Vendor_Pay to examine the human subnetwork.

Use `pm4py.discover_network_analysis` and `pm4py.view_sna` or `pm4py.save_vis_sna` to compare Mira’s in/out degree, reciprocal edges, and betweenness across subsets. The key test is whether her centrality survives in routine cases, in specific regions, or after removing automated nodes. A sharp drop outside exception cases would support the exception-desk hypothesis; persistence everywhere would support a broader coordination role.

## Working-together network

Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` and examine weighted degree, clustering, and community structure. Crucially, recalculate co-occurrence with denominators restricted to cases in which Mira is present, rather than all cases. This corrects for her presence in only 22% of claims.

Compare her partners within exception cases, complaint cases, reopened cases, and specialist cases. Also compare North/South co-working with cross-team pairs. If her conditional co-occurrence with adjusters remains low even when she is present, the broker interpretation strengthens. If it rises substantially, the original low percentages are partly a denominator artifact.

## Subcontracting network

Use `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with `n=2` and sensitivity analyses with larger windows. Separate fragments into specialist consultation, approval loops, missing-information loops, and automation–human–automation loops. Examine whether Mira  Omar/Priya loops end in reopening, reassignment, closure, or payment.

This analysis should test whether she retains responsibility after delegation or merely receives the next system-generated task. It should also compare her return rate with Alice/Bruno approval loops and with Temp_1  Vendor_Verify validation loops.

## Activity-based resource similarity or organizational role discovery

Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`. Run the analysis in three ways:

1. humans only;
2. humans, bots, and vendors separately flagged;
3. excluding the ambiguous `Assign Specialist` activity or recoding it after trace inspection.

Inspect whether Mira clusters with Alice/Bruno, remains unique, or clusters with temporary staff because of Request Missing Information. Compare Carla/Diego and Elise/Farid similarity as positive controls. Role clusters should be interpreted as activity profiles, not jobs or seniority.

## Filtering by exception_flag, activity, variants, or time ranges

Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` for exception_flag, priority, region, channel, case_type, and amount_band. Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to isolate variants containing Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, Medical Review, Legal Review, or repeated Request Missing Information.

Construct time-based cohorts: baseline, Mira’s six-day absence, and recovery. For each cohort, compare case counts, exception share, variant frequencies, median and 90th percentile cycle time, queue accumulation, and the proportion of exception cases receiving any human routing touch. Stratify by routine versus exception cases to test whether the absence effect is specific, as reported.

## Service/waiting-time analysis

Use `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to separate service, waiting, and total cycle time. Analyze:

- waiting before Mira after Bot_Rules;
- waiting after Mira before adjuster or specialist activity;
- waiting after specialist return to Mira;
- service and waiting by exception status, priority, amount_band, and case_type;
- waiting associated with Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay;
- changes during the absence period.

Use medians and high percentiles, not only means, because exception queues are likely to have long tails. Check whether higher Mira workload predicts longer pre-Mira waiting. If waiting is concentrated after specialists or vendors, the bottleneck is downstream rather than Mira herself.

Across all analyses, sample complete traces, check incomplete cases and lifecycle transitions, and report uncertainty. Descriptive network position should not be treated as causal evidence.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

Do not remove Mira_Coordinator. Her exception-routing function appears operationally real, and removal without a replacement would predictably harm complex cases.

Immediate changes should make the function visible and coverable:

- Document the routing criteria she uses for adjusters, Omar, Priya, reopening, and escalation, including when Bot_Rules recommendations are overridden.
- Create a shared exception queue and train at least one deputy; maintain a brief decision log for specialist selection and reopening.
- Clarify accountability: Mira routes and reopens, adjusters own assessment and negotiation, Alice/Bruno approve, specialists own their reviews, and vendors own external verification/payment turnaround.
- Add explicit absence coverage and a short handover note for active complaints and reopened cases.
- Resolve the `Assign Specialist` taxonomy issue and distinguish human actions from system-generated assignments in future logs.

## What should be monitored

Monitor a small exception dashboard rather than her event count:

- exception queue length and age;
- median and 90th percentile waiting before and after Mira;
- exception-case cycle time, separately from routine cases;
- shares of reopened claims and complaints routed through the desk;
- specialist queues for Omar and Priya;
- Vendor_Verify waiting and Vendor_Pay completion times;
- Bot_Rules override rates and Fraud Screen exception loops;
- allocation of high-priority, high-amount, and exception cases across adjusters and teams;
- repeat Request Missing Information and reopen rates after her handovers.

Review these measures by region, case_type, priority, amount_band, and channel to detect fairness or control problems.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

Do not remove, demote, or convert her into an end-to-end case owner based only on centrality. Do not make her a settlement approver without a separate control assessment. Do not set performance targets from handover betweenness or raw cycle-time contribution. Do not redistribute adjusters based solely on working-together co-occurrence. Do not automate her decisions with new Bot_Rules thresholds until the filtered, service/waiting, and role-similarity analyses above show which decisions are rule-based and which require judgement.

## What would replace her if the role were ever changed

Any future redesign should replace the *function*, not merely the person: a staffed exception desk with at least two coordinators, documented routing rules, shared queue visibility, and specialist-selection audit trails. That replacement should be piloted in parallel before any change to her current role.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira Coordinator is a short-touch, high-impact routing point for difficult claims, not a conventional team leader who works cases end to end. Automated systems send her many exceptions; briefly she checks, reassigns, reopens, or sends them to medical or legal specialists, after which others do the substantive work. That explains why handovers through her look frequent while shared case work with any one colleague looks low, and why her absence mainly delayed complex cases rather than routine ones. The risk is dependence on one person’s judgement and availability, with little documented backup. It does not prove poor performance or misconduct. Do not remove her without replacing the exception function. Instead, document her routing rules, train a deputy, separate quality checks from specialist selection where possible, and monitor exception queues, waiting times, and fairness of assignments by case type.
