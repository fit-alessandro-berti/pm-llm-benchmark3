# 1. Objectives and constraints

Use a **constrained, multi-objective, rolling assignment policy**. Safety and qualification rules are checked before scoring; high scores never override them. Assignments should be reviewed when a case, workload snapshot, deadline, or supervision arrangement changes.

The policy should balance:

- **Throughput:** predicted completion time and work actually completed per available hour.
- **SLA protection:** urgency based on remaining time, priority, and predicted service/queue time—not priority alone.
- **Quality:** skill match and predicted rework/appeal risk.
- **Ownership continuity:** avoid unnecessary context loss and repeated diagnosis/rework loops.
- **Fairness:** bounded new assignments, manageable workloads, and protection from repeated exposure to high-overtime conditions.
- **Development:** assignments aligned with documented training priorities.
- **Resilience:** preserve backups for scarce skills and avoid concentrating all work in one team or specialist queue.

### Safety and compliance constraints

These are non-negotiable unless an explicitly authorized emergency procedure is used:

1. **Skill 0 is never assignable**, including through supervision.
2. High- and urgent-priority cases require skill level **at least 2**, unless an appropriately qualified supervisor is committed for the required portion of the work.
3. Security Review requires:
   - skill level **3**, or
   - skill level **1–2 with explicit, available supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo**.
4. Final Approval requires skill level **2–3** for the final decision. A level-1 worker may prepare the approval but cannot finalize it.
5. No resource receives more than **four new assignments in the batch**.
6. A worker’s cumulative estimated effort, handover effort, and supervision time must fit the available hours, with an operational safety buffer.
7. Every decision must record eligible candidates, rejected candidates, reasons, supervision, policy version, and any exception.
8. Achieve at least **20% training-supporting assignments among normal/low-priority cases** when the training is qualified, supervised, and SLA-safe. If that is impossible, record a reviewed deferral rather than forcing an unsafe assignment.

### Preference trade-offs

These influence scoring but do not automatically disqualify a worker:

- Avoid additional work for high-overtime-risk resources when a safer feasible alternative exists.
- Prefer continuity for complex, expensive-to-transfer cases.
- Prefer the least skilled competent worker when that adequately addresses risk, rather than routing everything to specialists.
- Use trainees for suitable normal/low-priority work without weakening urgent-case capacity.
- Limit concentration in specialist queues and preserve an available backup.
- Prefer batching only when its coordination and waiting costs remain acceptable.

Importantly, **fair new-assignment counts are not the same as fair total workloads**. Existing heavy workloads must also be reviewed and, where appropriate, rebalanced.

# 2. Assignment policy

## A. Construct feasible assignment options

For each case \(i\) and resource \(j\):

1. Use the skill, workload, and availability data **as of the decision time**.
2. Determine the minimum required level:
   - Eligibility, Diagnosis, Negotiation, Security, Approval, Quality, or Rework.
   - Apply the special Security and Approval rules.
3. Include a candidate only if:
   - skill is positive;
   - supervision is feasible if required;
   - the four-assignment batch limit remains satisfied;
   - predicted effort fits remaining hours;
   - a feasible queue-and-service forecast does not already make the deadline unattainable.
4. Represent supervised work as a **pair or team**, with the supervisor’s time included in the capacity calculation. A supervisor’s name alone is not evidence of availability.

Process urgent cases first, but check that committing their capacity does not unnecessarily make another known urgent case infeasible.

## B. Score feasible options

Use components scaled to \([0,1]\). Illustrative components are:

| Component | Scoring rule |
|---|---|
| **SLA urgency \(U\)** | Priority, remaining time, predicted queue/service time, waiting caused by transfer, and case age. A larger score means greater urgency. |
| **Skill match \(M\)** | \(M=\text{actual skill}/3\), adjusted for complexity and historically observed error/rework rates. Do not assume skill 3 is always necessary. |
| **Continuity benefit \(C\)** | Reward staying with the current owner, especially for complex cases. Discount continuity for high handover cost or repeated reassignment. |
| **Workload fairness \(W\)** | Evaluate open cases, estimated hours, assignments already received in the batch, and qualified-pool workload—not just raw case count. |
| **Overtime safety \(1-O\)** | Low, medium, and high risk receive progressively lower scores. Apply a stronger preference against new high-risk work when a feasible lower-risk option exists. |
| **Development \(T\)** | Award full credit when the assignment matches the worker’s documented training priority, the work is suitable for development, and qualified supervision is available. |
| **Resilience benefit \(B\)** | Penalize predicted specialist waiting and dependence on an already scarce qualified resource. Credit qualified backups and cross-trained coverage. |

Example scoring functions include:

\[
M_{ij}=\frac{s_{ij}}{3}
\]

\[
C_{ij}
=
I(j=\text{current owner}_i)\,
q(\text{handover band}_i)
-
0.10\min(\text{previous reassignments}_i,3)
\]

where \(q(\text{low})=1.0\), \(q(\text{medium})=0.6\), and \(q(\text{high})=0.2\). An unowned case has no continuity penalty.

A possible urgency score is:

\[
U_i=p_i\left[
0.7\left(1-e^{-h_i/\tau_i}\right)
+
0.3\min\left(1,\frac{\max(0,\hat w_{ij}-h_i)}{h_i+\epsilon}\right)
\right]
\]

where \(h_i\) is SLA time remaining, \(\hat w_{ij}\) is predicted queue-plus-service time, and \(p_i\) is a calibrated priority weight.

An illustrative utility is:

\[
\text{Utility}
=
0.32U+0.20M+0.20C+0.14W+
0.06(1-O)+0.05T+0.03B
\]

These weights are **initial governance choices, not empirically established optima**. The safety constraints and training quota are enforced separately; increasing the training weight must not make an otherwise prohibited assignment permissible.

## C. Select a constrained batch

Enforce these filters in order:

1. **Hard safety and qualification rules**
2. **Available capacity and the four-assignment limit**
3. **Urgent-case protection and look-ahead**
4. **Overtime-risk preference where a safer alternative exists**
5. **Training quota**
6. **Continuity preferences for complex, expensive transfers**
7. **Utility, with Pareto-style review of the resulting trade-offs**

Where possible, optimize the small batch jointly—for example, through constrained assignment optimization or a two-pass heuristic:

```text
urgent_pass:
    assign urgent cases first
    reserve demonstrably required supervision and scarce-skill capacity

development_pass:
    select safe, supervised assignments for documented training priorities
    until the normal/low training quota is met

remaining_pass:
    maximize the utility score subject to all remaining constraints
```

At larger scale, use rolling greedy assignment with look-ahead rather than independently greedily assigning every case.

## D. Tie-breaking and exceptions

- If utilities differ by less than an agreed margin, such as two points:
  1. prefer the smaller predicted completion time;
  2. for non-urgent work, prefer the current owner;
  3. prefer lower workload/overtime exposure;
  4. use a stable resource-ID tie-break.
- Do not use arbitrary score differences to justify unnecessary transfers.
- If no feasible authorized candidate exists:
  - escalate to the responsible manager;
  - add qualified capacity or schedule protection;
  - defer safe lower-priority work where possible;
  - never bypass skill-zero, Security, Approval, or supervision rules merely to clear the queue.
- Replan at batch boundaries, shift changes, material workload changes, or SLA escalation.
- Preserve an assignment while the case remains viable unless there is a documented safety, quality, or continuity reason to change it.

# 5. Explanation to workers

A worker-facing explanation could say:

> “We try to meet urgent customer deadlines, but we do not treat speed as the only goal. First, the work must be safe and within the worker’s recorded qualifications. Some work needs a qualified second reviewer, and the reviewer’s time is reserved before the assignment is made.
>
> We try to keep an existing owner when that protects the customer and avoids losing case context. We may change ownership when a qualified specialist is needed, a repeat handover would cause delay, or another worker can take the work without creating an unsafe workload.
>
> Training assignments are planned work, not extra work hidden after the main assignment. They are used for appropriate cases with enough time and a real supervisor. A training target is never used to justify sending an urgent case to someone who cannot safely perform it.
>
> High overtime risk is treated as a capacity warning, not a judgment about a person’s effort. We aim to avoid adding more work where a reasonable safer option exists. We also review existing heavy workloads rather than simply directing all new work to whoever currently has the fewest cases.
>
> Every assignment has a reason and an audit record. If a worker identifies missing information, unrealistic effort, unavailable supervision, or a quality concern, they can request a review. The assignment can be changed when the evidence supports it.”

The policy should therefore be presented as **decision support with human review**, especially for exceptions, training assignments, and repeated reassignments—not as an automatic ranking of employee performance.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Audit results by policy version, priority, complexity band, team, and skill. Compare outcomes with pre-deployment periods or a matched pilot while accounting for case mix.

| Indicator | Definition and interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Handover frequency** | Number of distinct cases with an owner change divided by 100 assignment decisions; also report changes per 100 completed cases. Break down by handover cost, complexity, priority, and team. |
| **Reassignment count** | Mean, median, and 90th percentile per case; share with more than two reassignments; cross-team reassignment rate. Compare subsequent rework and appeals, particularly for cases already above two reassignments. |
| **Skill mismatch** | Share of work starts where actual skill is below the minimum required level. Report separately from permitted supervised skill-1/2 Security work. Skill 0 is always a rule violation, even if supervision was present. |
| **Qualification/supervision compliance** | Share of high-priority assignments below level 2 without supervision, Security assignments without a qualified level-3 worker or authorized supervision, and level-1 approvals recorded as final decisions. |
| **Workload distribution** | New assignments per resource per day/shift, actual and predicted hours, and distribution within comparable skill/role pools. Report count limits and hours together; also show Gini or coefficient of variation where appropriate. |
| **Overtime-risk exposure** | Share of new assignments and predicted/actual hours directed to medium/high-risk resources, plus actual overtime. A low new-assignment share does not mean their existing workload is acceptable. |
| **SLA misses** | Share of cases completed after their original deadline. Separate urgent, high, normal, and low priorities; distinguish queue time from execution time. Account for unfinished/censored cases using survival analysis where possible. |
| **Rework and appeal after assignment** | Rework and appeal rates within a fixed window, such as 7 or 30 days, linked to the assignment that started the case. Report by skill match, continuity, complexity, and previous reassignments. |
| **Training assignment share** | Normal/low assignments matching a documented training priority, performed at an appropriate level, and actually supported by supervision, divided by all normal/low assignments. Report planned and delivered supervision separately. |
| **Specialist bottleneck indicators** | Qualified-specialist utilization, queue and service waiting times, p90 completion, number of qualified resources available, and dependency on the single busiest specialist. Security and Approval should be reviewed separately. |
| **Continuity indicators** | Percentage retaining the current owner at first assignment, unnecessary owner changes, repeated transfers to the same resource, and outcomes conditional on handover-cost band. |
| **Policy deviations** | Actual owner differs from approved owner; required supervision was absent; an unauthorized exception was used; the selected option was not the best logged eligible option under the recorded policy version; or no reason was recorded. Separate approved exceptions from unexplained deviations. |
| **Data quality** | Missing or invalid skill, owner, workload, deadline, training-priority, and supervision fields; timestamp or timezone inconsistencies; and stale workload snapshots. |
| **Productivity and equity together** | Work completed per labor hour, backlog age, and service-time percentiles by skill pool and shift. Throughput should not be reported without workload and case-mix context. |

Two useful audit safeguards are:

- The formal training share must use the same denominator definition in the pilot and deployment; for this queue it is nine normal/low-priority assignments.
- Handover and resource networks reveal observed relationships, not causal proof that a handover caused rework. Outcome comparisons should control for complexity, priority, and prior reassignments.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

PM4Py supplies the event-log measurements and network features; the assignment engine and policy rules should remain explicit and externally auditable. Function signatures and attribute constants can differ across PM4Py versions.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

CASE = "case:concept:name"
DECISION_ACTIVITY = "assignment_decision"

# events: the supplied event log, preferably augmented with:
# - structured reason codes and score components
# - policy_version
# - eligible candidates and approved alternatives
# - supervision_required / supervision_actual
# - explicit handover events
# - batch_id and training-confirmation identifiers
# Use consistent UTC timestamps for events and SLA deadlines.

events = pd.read_parquet("events.parquet")

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events.copy(),
    case_id=CASE,
    timestamp="time",
    activity="concept:name"
)

# Service and waiting-time perspectives
log_with_waiting = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
service_times = pm4py.get_service_time(log_with_waiting)

# Resource relationships
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)

pm4py.save_vis_sna(handover, "handover_network.html")
# pm4py.view_sna(handover)  # optional interactive display

# Process-route perspectives
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log, variant="sequence")

def filter_cases_by_attributes(df, **equalities):
    for attribute, allowed in equalities.items():
        values = allowed if isinstance(allowed, (list, set, tuple)) else [allowed]
        df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            df,
            {attribute: values},
            case_id_key=CASE
        )
    return df

# Use decision-time case attributes, not later reassignment or outcome fields.
normal_low = filter_cases_by_attributes(
    log_with_waiting, case_priority=["normal", "low"]
)
urgent = filter_cases_by_attributes(
    log_with_waiting, case_priority="urgent"
)
high_complexity = filter_cases_by_attributes(
    log_with_waiting, high_complexity_flag=True
)

# Performance filtering is also useful for investigating delayed routes.
# Exact parameters depend on the PM4Py release:
# slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
#     log, min_performance=pd.Timedelta("8h")
# )
```

Use these outputs for different purposes:

- **Handover network:** identify repeated transfer paths and possible rework loops.
- **Working-together network:** find collaboration or backup relationships.
- **Activity-based similarity:** suggest development opportunities, but never replace the explicit skill matrix.
- **Role discovery:** group comparable work for workload and bottleneck analysis; inferred roles are not proof of legal or technical authorization.
- **Batches:** audit actual batching behavior. The proposed C006/C012 batch may need an explicit batch identifier if the event structure would not otherwise reveal it.
- **Service/waiting times:** estimate execution, queue, and handover-related delays.
- **Performance DFG and variants:** identify process paths associated with delays and repeated diagnosis.
- **Attribute filters:** compare SLA, training, and complexity segments without silently mixing materially different case populations.

A policy-audit layer can then join approved decisions to actual assignment events:

```python
# queue: current queue table
# skills: resource skill matrix
# live: decision-time workload snapshots
# decisions: one row per approved assignment
# actual: case -> actual primary resource and actual supervisor

def hard_rule_ok(row):
    s = int(row["actual_skill"])
    activity = row["required_skill"]
    supervised = bool(row.get("supervision_committed", False))

    if s == 0:
        return False
    if activity == "Security":
        if s == 3:
            return True
        return s in (1, 2) and row.get("qualified_supervisor") in [
            "R7_Gita", "R8_Hugo"
        ]
    if activity == "Approval" and s < 2:
        return False
    if row["case_priority"] in ("urgent", "high") and s < 2:
        if not supervised:
            return False
    return True

# Build a versioned audit table:
# decision_id, case, approved_resource, actual_resource,
# approved_supervisor, actual_supervisor, scores, reason_code,
# skill_at_decision, batch_id, policy_version, exception_id

audit = decisions.merge(skills, left_on="approved_resource",
                        right_index=True)
audit = audit.merge(
    actual[["actual_resource", "actual_supervisor"]],
    left_on=CASE, right_index=True, how="left"
)

audit["authorization_ok"] = audit.apply(hard_rule_ok, axis=1)
audit["actual_owner_deviation"] = (
    audit["actual_resource"] != audit["approved_resource"]
)
audit["supervision_deviation"] = (
    audit.get("supervision_required", False)
    & ~audit.get("supervision_delivered", False)
)

new_counts = (
    decisions.groupby("approved_resource")
    .size()
    .reindex(skills.index, fill_value=0)
)
assert (new_counts <= 4).all()

# Other indicators, calculated with the same denominators as Section 6:
# - cases_with_reassignment / assignment_decisions
# - share with reassignment_count > 2
# - skill_mismatch and supervision violations
# - assignment/workload hours by resource
# - overtime exposure
# - SLA misses by priority
# - rework/appeal after assignment
# - actual supervised training assignments / normal-low assignments
# - specialist queue and utilization percentiles
# - approved vs unexplained deviations
```

For learning or score calibration, use only features available **before** assignment: skill, complexity, priority, workload, previous reassignments, handover band, team, and historical service/queue estimates derived from the PM4Py outputs. Fit calibrated p90 duration and SLA-risk models on earlier cases, validate on later cases, and retain the safety and fairness rules as explicit constraints.

Finally, compare policies through workload replay and a monitored pilot using identical qualification and capacity rules. Report uncertainty and case-mix differences rather than claiming that the proposed policy is universally optimal.
