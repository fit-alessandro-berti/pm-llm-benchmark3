# 1. Fairness dimensions

**Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ substantially (A 78%, B 63%, C 69%, D 58%). This is the weakest evidence of unfairness on its own, since groups differ in documented complexity (risk band, documentation completeness, accommodation needs, prior-case count). Outcome parity is a starting signal, not a conclusion.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk band, documentation, channel, household size, prior-case count, region, and month, disparities shrink but do not disappear (B 5.5pp, C 2.2pp, D 3.1pp approval probability; B/D also show elevated appeal probability). This is stronger evidence than raw parity because it nets out much of the legitimate case-mix variation, but it is still only as good as the covariates used—residual disparity could reflect unmeasured legitimate complexity, unmeasured bias, or both.

**Procedural burden.** Independent of final outcome, the *number and type of steps* a case must pass through (Request Missing Documents, Supervisor Review, Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, Fraud Review) constitutes burden. Groups B, C, D all show elevated probabilities of extra review steps even after adjustment, meaning some of this burden is not fully explained by measured complexity.

**Timeliness.** Median and 90th-percentile cycle times are much higher for B, C, D, and adjusted cycle-time differences remain positive (B +2.6d, C +4.1d, D +0.9d) after controlling for complexity. Waiting-time breakdowns show specific bottlenecks (bilingual staff queue, accommodation specialist queue) rather than generic slowness — this points to resource-capacity fairness, not just case complexity.

**Consistency of routing.** Variant analysis shows Groups B, C, D are disproportionately routed into longer, more complex variants (V3, V4, V5, V6) even accounting for expected legitimate routing (fraud review for D, accommodation review for C, language support for B). The question is whether routing consistently reflects only legitimate triggers or also reflects group-correlated discretion (e.g., supervisor review attached to accommodation review for C, which is not obviously required by policy).

**Quality/accessibility of support.** Rights-preserving steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review) exist to protect access, but they are handled by very few specialized staff (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists) with no service-level timeliness targets. This creates a structural equity gap: using a right-preserving step correlates with worse timeliness, which is itself a fairness concern even if the step's *existence* is legitimate.

**Appeal/rework burden.** Appeal rates (B 13%, C 10%, D 16% vs A 6%) and reopen/rework rates (B 22%, C 24%, D 20% vs A 11%) are elevated, and adjusted appeal probability remains higher for B and especially D (+5.7pp) after controlling for complexity. This suggests either more first-pass errors, less accessible explanations, or lower initial-decision quality for these groups — an important dimension separate from approval rate.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Concentration of B and C cases onto a small number of specialized staff (7 bilingual, 5 accommodation specialists, some double-booked with supervisor review) creates queueing bottlenecks and potential single-point overload, independent of any caseworker's individual behavior. This is a system-design fairness issue, not necessarily an individual-bias issue.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

**Goal:** separate (a) legitimate complexity-driven variation, (b) structural/resource-driven disparity, (c) possible discriminatory or biased treatment, and (d) genuine uncertainty from data limitations.

**Stratification / matching variables:** risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month — as already used — plus, where possible, finer strata combining accommodation_requested × risk_band × documentation_completeness to isolate whether within *matched* complexity cells, group differences persist. Use exact stratification or propensity-score matching on these covariates; report residual disparity per stratum, not just a pooled coefficient, to check for effect heterogeneity (e.g., is the B penalty concentrated in high-documentation-completeness cases, where complexity should not matter?).

**Path/variant comparison:** Use `pm4py.get_variants` / `pm4py.filter_variants` to compare variant frequency by group *within* matched complexity strata. E.g., among cases with the same risk_band and documentation_completeness, do B/C/D still route into Supervisor Review or Request Missing Documents at higher rates than A? If yes, that is evidence of discretion-driven divergence beyond documented complexity.

**Resource-assignment comparison:** Use `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`, `discover_handover_of_work_network`, and `discover_working_together_network` to compare handover patterns and caseworker-load distribution by group. Check whether specialized staff (bilingual, accommodation) are systematically more overloaded (more concurrent cases, longer queues) than general caseworkers, and whether this bottleneck—not group membership per se—explains most of the timing gap.

**Waiting-time decomposition:** Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `pm4py.get_service_time` to split total cycle time into (i) waiting time at legitimate specialized queues (language support, accommodation review, fraud review), (ii) waiting time at generic steps (completeness check, supervisor review, decision draft), and (iii) active processing time. This distinguishes "complexity requires more steps" (legitimate) from "queue capacity is unequal" (structural) from "generic steps take longer for this group for no apparent reason" (concerning).

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates:** Because risk_score_band and documentation_completeness may themselves encode historical bias or language-driven measurement artifacts, run the adjusted model (a) with these covariates included, (b) excluding them, and (c) using alternative proxies (e.g., verified fraud outcomes instead of risk score) to bound how much the adjusted disparity estimate depends on trusting these covariates. Report a plausible range rather than a single adjusted number.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel:** Re-run adjusted comparisons within region and within channel (online vs in-person) to check whether disparities are concentrated in specific regions (e.g., "backlog in two regions" mentioned for Supervisor Review) or specific channels (in-person wait longer at Identity Verification). If disparities vanish within channel/region strata, that points to operational/capacity causes rather than group-targeted treatment; if they persist even within channel/region, that strengthens concern about differential treatment.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Most important disparities in the data:**

1. **V4 (Language Support path) for Group B**: 24% of B cases vs ~1–3% for others, median cycle time 19.2 days, with a 3.8-day wait specifically at Language Support Contact  Eligibility caused by only 7 bilingual caseworkers. This is *partly* legitimate (minority-language applicants need this step) but the *magnitude* of delay reflects a capacity shortfall, not applicant complexity — concerning.

2. **V5 (Accommodation Review path) for Group C**: 31% share vs 2–3% for others, median cycle time 21.6 days, 4.7-day queue wait at a "specialized team queue." Similarly, the accommodation trigger is legitimate; the queue delay and the extra Supervisor Review attached to this path (some accommodation specialists double as supervisors) is a routing/resourcing choice, not an inherent requirement of the accommodation review itself — concerning.

3. **V3 (Fraud Review + Supervisor Review) for Group D**: 28% share vs 8–11% for others, 18.4-day median. Given D's much higher high-risk-band share (51%), this path is largely expected/legitimate, though the adjusted +5.7pp appeal-probability increase suggests decision quality on this path may still be worse for D even accounting for risk.

4. **V6 (Appeal path) over-representation for D and B** (13% and 8% vs 4% for A): consistent with elevated adjusted appeal probabilities; suggests first-pass decisions are less robust or explanations less clear for these groups, independent of routing complexity.

5. **Handover patterns**: B's generalbilingualgeneral handover and C's generalaccommodationsupervisor handover both add coordination steps and potential information loss beyond what A experiences, even when the underlying case complexity (need for translation/accommodation) is comparable across a matched case.

**What may be justified:** the *existence* of extra steps (Fraud Review, Accommodation Review, Language Support Contact) for the groups whose risk/accommodation/language attributes trigger them — this is policy-consistent legitimate routing.

**What remains concerning:** (a) the *duration* of waits at these steps, driven by staffing scarcity rather than case need; (b) the *coupling* of accommodation review with supervisor review for Group C, which may not be policy-required and adds burden; (c) residual adjusted disparities in approval and appeal probability that persist after routing/complexity is accounted for; (d) absence of service-level timeliness targets for rights-preserving steps, which structurally permits these delays to persist unmonitored.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity-driven routing** — higher risk_score_band genuinely requires Fraud Review, accommodation requests genuinely require Accommodation Review, language needs genuinely require Language Support Contact. This explains part of the path and time differences and is policy-consistent.

2. **Resource scarcity / queueing bottleneck** — only 7 bilingual and 5 accommodation specialists serving disproportionate caseloads creates delay that is a capacity-design issue, not necessarily intentional bias, but still a fairness harm because affected applicants bear the cost of a resourcing choice.

3. **Discretionary escalation correlated with group attributes** — e.g., accommodation specialists also performing supervisor review may reflect an informal practice of extra scrutiny for accommodation or language cases beyond what policy requires, which would be concerning discretionary bias.

4. **Measurement/covariate bias propagating into routing** — if risk_score_band itself reflects historical disparities (e.g., proxying for group D's prior contact with the system) or documentation_completeness is degraded by language barriers (Group B), then "legitimate" routing based on these covariates could be indirectly encoding bias, producing disparate paths and outcomes that look justified by the model but are not truly neutral.

5. **Decision-quality/communication gaps** — elevated appeal and rework rates for B, C, D even after adjustment could reflect less effective initial explanations or less time spent per case for groups whose caseworkers are overloaded, rather than differences in true eligibility — a quality-of-process mechanism distinct from routing or resourcing.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in documentation_completeness:** applicants with language barriers or less familiarity with the system (Group B) may be scored as having lower documentation completeness for reasons unrelated to true case complexity, inflating apparent "legitimate" cause for Request Missing Documents and downstream delay.

- **Historical bias in risk_score_band:** if the risk model was trained on past decisions/enforcement that themselves reflected disparate scrutiny of Group D applicants (higher prior-case count, higher measured risk), then "controlling for risk_score_band" partially controls away the very disparity being investigated, understating true bias.

- **Missing applicant needs:** the log only captures disability_accommodation_requested and language_preference as binary/categorical flags; it does not capture severity, exact accommodation type, or unmet needs (e.g., applicants who needed but did not request accommodation), so equity gaps for those who under-request support are invisible.

- **Unobserved resource workload:** daily-granularity data cannot show real-time caseworker overload, multitasking, or fatigue; the finding that few specialists serve many cases is suggestive but not a precise workload measure, limiting causal claims about queueing as the delay mechanism.

- **Appeal quality and dignity of treatment are unmeasured:** case_outcome and appeal_flag say nothing about whether explanations were clear, respectful, or adequate; two cases with identical outcomes and durations could differ greatly in the applicant's experienced fairness, which this log cannot capture.

- **No ground truth for "correct" outcome:** without an external correctness/audit measure, we cannot know whether lower approval rates for B/C/D reflect appropriate eligibility determinations or systematic under-approval; adjusted models only show correlation controlling for observed covariates, not a causal correctness benchmark.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Adding capacity for language support / accommodation review**
 *Benefit:* reduces queue delay for B/C rights-preserving steps.
 *Side effect:* costly; may shift bottlenecks elsewhere (e.g., supervisor review) if new staff still double-book roles; hiring lag means short-term effect limited; must avoid diluting specialist expertise by rushing hiring.

- **Service-level targets for support steps**
 *Benefit:* makes rights-preserving steps subject to the same timeliness accountability as other steps, closing the current monitoring gap.
 *Side effect:* risk of rushing accommodation/language support to meet targets, potentially degrading quality of the very support the step is meant to provide; needs quality safeguards alongside speed targets.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**
 *Benefit:* fewer handovers (e.g., separating accommodation review from supervisor review staff) could reduce delay and coordination loss for Group C.
 *Side effect:* may require more staff or cross-training; could reduce specialization benefits or create new inconsistency if roles are split without adequate training.

- **Decision-explanation improvements**
 *Benefit:* clearer explanations at Decision Draft/Notification could reduce unnecessary appeals and rework, especially for B/D where adjusted appeal probability is elevated.
 *Side effect:* added drafting time could lengthen cycle time slightly; translation needs for explanations add further demand on scarce bilingual staff unless separately resourced.

- **Monitoring adjusted disparities on an ongoing basis**
 *Benefit:* institutionalizes the adjusted-comparison approach used here, allowing early detection of drift or improvement.
 *Side effect:* requires sustained data quality and model maintenance; risk of false reassurance if the underlying covariates (risk score, documentation completeness) are themselves biased and treated as fully neutral controls.

- **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**
 *Benefit:* addresses the possibility that "legitimate" routing variables encode historical bias or language-driven measurement error, which is essential before trusting adjusted estimates.
 *Side effect:* auditing/recalibrating these measures could change eligibility and routing determinations broadly, is resource-intensive, and may face resistance if it appears to alter accepted operational baselines; must be done transparently to maintain trust.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence is **more consistent with a procedurally imperfect but not conclusively discriminatory process, with specific, identifiable areas of concern that warrant remediation and further investigation.** Raw outcome gaps are largely — but not entirely — attenuated by legitimate case-mix and routing differences (fraud review for higher-risk cases, accommodation review for accommodation requesters, language support for minority-language applicants). However, adjusted analyses still show non-trivial residual disparities in approval probability, review/documentation-request probability, and appeal probability for Groups B, C, and D, and the *severity* of delay at rights-preserving steps (language support, accommodation review) appears driven by scarce specialized staffing rather than case need — a structural equity concern independent of any individual caseworker's intent. Some specific patterns (accommodation specialists doubling as supervisors for Group C, elevated adjusted appeal rates for Group D despite risk adjustment) are concerning enough to merit targeted audit and process redesign. At the same time, key covariates used for "control" (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness) may themselves carry historical or measurement bias, and the log lacks any measure of explanation quality or true eligibility correctness, so the residual disparities cannot be confidently attributed to discriminatory treatment versus incompletely-modeled legitimate complexity. The appropriate characterization is **indeterminate-but-flagged**: the data justify targeted mitigation (capacity, routing, monitoring, covariate audits) and continued measurement, not a definitive verdict of fair or unfair process.
