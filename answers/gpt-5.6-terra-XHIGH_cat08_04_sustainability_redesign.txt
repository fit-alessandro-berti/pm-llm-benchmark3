# 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Environmental scope.** The proposed scope is an order-to-delivery operational lifecycle boundary, including the effects that can be observed or reasonably linked from the event log:

- Production energy (`energy_kwh`) by `machine_group`, including repeat production after `Rework production`.
- Production material losses (`material_scrap_kg`), including scrap generated in rework loops.
- Packaging material (`package_material_kg`), packaging rework, and additional packaging caused by partial shipments or replacement deliveries.
- Outbound transport emissions (`transport_kgco2e`) for all shipment legs, including multiple legs for partial shipments, reshipments, and returns where these can be linked.
- Avoidable inventory waste/obsolescence, using the observed proxy that 6% of large-batch items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong.
- Service, quality, and resilience outcomes that can cause environmental displacement: late delivery, cancellation, emergency production, air freight, dedicated vans, return/rework loops, and carrier switching.

**Outside the current boundary.**

- Upstream embodied emissions of purchased raw material, supplier production, and capital equipment manufacture.
- Customer use-phase impacts and end-of-life recycling/disposal, except where an unshipped or returned part is identifiable as likely waste.
- Full emissions from vehicle manufacturing, warehouses, and employee travel.
- Dedicated-van empty-return emissions unless route utilization and return-leg data are added. This is important because the current evidence states that vans often return empty.

**Key assumptions and uncertainties.**

- Outcomes must be analyzed by segment and path, not as portfolio averages. A Critical-downtime Service-contract order has a fundamentally different acceptable trade-off from a Standard-stock Regular order.
- The flattened order log can hide line-item urgency, part mass, and the fact that one line may justify a partial shipment while another does not. Availability and partial-shipment changes should therefore be validated at line-item level where possible.
- Batch energy allocation is uncertain unless `energy_kwh` is allocated consistently across the actual `batch_id` and `batch_size`.
- Existing route choices are not random: air, emergency production, and dedicated vans are likely selected because an order is already urgent or disrupted. Apparent differences between paths therefore should not be interpreted as purely causal without matched or phased evaluation.
- `transport_kgco2e` should be checked for consistency across parcel, truck, and van routes and expanded to cover returns and empty repositioning where feasible.
- A lower operational footprint is not sufficient if it increases cancellations, downtime exposure, unshipped stock, damage returns, or dependency on one carrier.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

The indicators should be calculated per fulfilled item where possible, and per completed order as a secondary view. They should be stratified by `case_type`, `customer_type`, promised-lead-time band, route, machine group, and path variant.

| Dimension | Indicator | Event-log basis and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | **Total production energy per fulfilled order/item** | Sum `energy_kwh` across CNC, heat treatment, inspection, emergency production, and rework loops. Separate first-pass energy from rework energy. |
| Energy | **Energy per shipped item by batch-size band** | Compare CNC_A and heat-treatment energy for batch sizes 1–3, 4–11, and 12–20, while reporting associated waiting time and obsolescence. |
| Transport | **Transport kgCO2e per completed order/item** | Sum `transport_kgco2e` across all shipment legs, including partial shipments and replacement/return legs when linked. Report by `route_mode`. |
| Transport | **High-emission transport share** | Share of orders using parcel air or dedicated van, split into true Critical-downtime, precautionary urgent, Service-contract, and Regular segments. |
| Transport policy | **SP1-eligible ground/consolidation conversion rate** | Among orders for which ground or consolidated truck had an estimated on-time probability above 85% at the decision time, share actually assigned to those modes. |
| Materials | **Total material loss per fulfilled order/item** | `material_scrap_kg + package_material_kg`, reported separately so increased protective packaging is not mistaken for production scrap reduction. |
| Materials | **Obsolescence proxy rate** | Share of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days, by `batch_id`, forecast-error band, and product type. |
| Rework | **Rework-loop rate and burden** | Share and count of traces containing `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production` and packaging-rework loops; associated incremental energy, scrap, packaging, and delay. |
| Batching | **Batch waiting-time versus energy frontier** | Median and p90 time from `Plan production` to `CNC production`, energy/item, late risk, and unshipped-within-14-days rate by `batch_size`. |
| Service | **Cycle time and promise performance** | Median and p90 order cycle time, late-delivery rate, cancellation rate, and complete-order time for partial shipments. Use original customer need date as well as negotiated promise date. |
| Quality | **First-pass yield and damage/return rate** | Rework rate by `machine_group`, part characteristics, inspection queue state, packaging type, and route. Track Q1 violations and downstream returns or packaging rework. |
| Partial fulfillment | **Partial-shipment burden** | Share of orders entering `Partial shipment`, number of shipment legs, backorder waiting time, final completion time, late rate, cancellation, and transport emissions. |
| Resilience | **Alternative-route availability** | For Service-contract orders, share with at least one feasible alternative route/carrier at decision time, as required by R1. Also track carrier/mode concentration and switch time during disruptions. |
| Governance | **Override completeness and quality** | SP1/SP2/Q1 exceptions with reason, approver, customer urgency, available alternatives, predicted on-time probability, actual outcome, and environmental increment. |

# 3. Intervention options

### 3.1 Green shipping recommender and replacement of avoidable Service-contract air freight (S1, S4)

- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Service-contract orders, especially `Pick from stock -> Package standard -> Ship air`. This is the segment with 22% SP1 violations and an average transport footprint of 7.8 kgCO2e/order; the selected stock-air path has 18.5 kgCO2e/order versus 3.6 kgCO2e for stock-ground.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** At the shipping decision, estimate on-time probability for ground, consolidated truck, air, and available alternatives using remaining promised-time slack, pick/pack capacity, carrier reliability, route cut-off, and disruption status. Use priority picking plus ground when ground can meet the promise with probability above 85%; retain air where it cannot.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Cycle time will increase for converted orders because air saves about 24 hours versus ground. However, conversion is limited to orders with sufficient predicted slack. Quality is largely neutral if packaging-route compatibility is checked. Resilience worsens if air is removed as a fallback, so air capacity and at least one alternative route must remain available for Service-contract customers.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Decision-time promised-time slack; predicted and actual on-time probability by mode; `route_mode`; `expedite_flag`; pick queue time; transport time; SP1 compliance; late deliveries by converted versus non-converted comparable orders; air use during carrier disruptions.

### 3.2 Twice-daily consolidated-truck departures for non-urgent stock orders (S5)

- **Target path or segment:** Non-urgent Standard-stock Regular orders and Service-contract orders with sufficient slack currently sent as individual ground parcels. Candidate paths are `Pick from stock -> Package standard -> Ship ground` and some air-eligible orders that can instead wait for consolidation.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Move eligible shipments to two daily consolidated-truck cut-offs. Consolidated trucks reduce transport emissions by about 35–55% versus individual ground parcels, provided load factors are maintained.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Consolidation adds a 12–24 hour wait, so it must be excluded for orders near their delivery promise or with disruption risk. It can reduce parcel dependence but may create dependence on a single departure or carrier if not designed with alternative capacity.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** `route_mode`, dispatch cut-off, consolidation wait, load proxy, promised slack at assignment, late rate by departure window, emissions per shipment, cancellation/expedite after consolidation, and R1 route availability.

### 3.3 Reduce avoidable partial shipments through line-level availability and reservation rules (S11)

- **Target path or segment:** `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Package standard -> ...`, especially the `Partial shipment -> Backorder wait -> Pick from stock` path. This path has a 79-hour median cycle time, 24% late delivery, two shipment opportunities, and 7.2 kgCO2e/order.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Change availability checking from a simple “ship what is available” rule to an order- and line-aware rule. Reserve inventory for complete orders where a short wait is likely to avoid a second shipment; allow partial shipment only when it genuinely protects a priority line, avoids cancellation, or meets a customer-approved split-delivery need.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Fewer split shipments reduce packaging, transport legs, customer contact, and backorder loops. But holding an urgent line for a non-urgent line can worsen service or cause cancellations. Critical and contractually urgent line items must remain eligible for immediate partial shipment.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Line-level availability if available; `Partial shipment`, `Backorder wait`, number of shipment legs, complete-order time, late/cancellation rate, transport emissions, customer segment, and reason for partial release. Compare outcomes for “hold complete” versus “ship partial” decisions with similar item urgency.

### 3.4 Dynamic CNC and heat-treatment batching with explicit waiting-time caps (S2)

- **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order paths through `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production`, particularly the 13% path using CNC_A, heat treatment, inspection, reinforced packaging, and ground shipment.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Batch CNC_A and heat-treatment work when due-date slack supports it. CNC_A energy falls from 2.8 kWh/item at batch sizes 1–3 to 1.3 kWh/item at batch sizes 12–20. Use segment-specific maximum batch-wait caps rather than maximizing batch size alone. Release work before the cap is breached.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Uncapped batching raises median batch wait from 4 to 38 hours and heat-treatment batch waiting has a 1.7-times higher late-delivery risk. Shorter caps are needed for Made-to-order Service-contract orders; Regular customers can use longer caps only when they explicitly accept the promise date. Very large batches also increase the 14-day unshipped/obsolescence risk.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** `batch_id`, `batch_size`, batch-wait duration, machine and heat-treatment timestamps, energy/item, promised slack at release, late delivery, emergency-production escalation, items not shipped within 14 days, forecast error, and cancellation.

### 3.5 Shift suitable Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A (S3)

- **Target path or segment:** The problematic path `Batch wait -> CNC_B -> Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production -> ... -> ground`, which has 160-hour median cycle time, 32% late delivery, 32 kWh/order, and 1.8 kg scrap/order.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Route Made-to-order work to CNC_A when CNC_A queue time and due-date slack allow it. CNC_B has an 18% rework rate versus 9% for CNC_A, so avoiding CNC_B for suitable parts can reduce repeat machining, scrap, inspection effort, packaging disruption, and replacement transport.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** CNC_B is faster, so indiscriminate switching may create CNC_A queues and late deliveries. CNC_B should remain available as an alternative machine during disruption and for orders where its speed is necessary. The decision should use expected first-pass completion time, not machine label alone.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** `machine_group`, queue and processing time, rework rate, inspection result, energy and scrap including rework, due-date slack, late rate, machine utilization, and the availability of CNC_B as contingency capacity.

### 3.6 Risk-based inspection quality gate for high-rework combinations (S7)

- **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order parts, especially CNC_B combinations with elevated rework, and cases with long or rushed `Inspection` queues that subsequently enter `Rework production`. The DFG records 470 `Inspection -> Rework production` transitions.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Add a targeted, not universal, inspection gate before packaging for machine/part/geometry combinations with high rework or damage history. Stabilize inspection staffing and work-in-process limits so inspectors do not rush checks merely to clear queues.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** A well-designed gate increases first-pass yield and reduces hidden energy, scrap, packaging rework, returns, and replacement transport. It may add a small inspection delay and can become a bottleneck if capacity is not protected. It should therefore be applied based on risk and supported by inspection capacity.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Inspection queue and duration, inspection outcome, `rework_flag`, rework-loop count, rework energy/scrap, damage/return events, packaging rework, p90 cycle time, and the relationship between inspector workload and defect escape.

### 3.7 Packaging-route compatibility checks and enforced Q1 logic (S6)

- **Target path or segment:** `Package standard -> Rework packaging` and `Package reinforced -> Rework packaging`, plus Made-to-order parts over 8 kg. There are 620 observed packaging-rework transitions, and Q1 violations correlate with return/rework loops.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Before packaging release, validate part geometry, weight, route mode, and packaging choice. Enforce reinforced packaging for Made-to-order parts above 8 kg unless an inspection-based low-damage-risk certificate exists. Prevent packaging configurations that are incompatible with air, ground, truck, or dedicated-van handling.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Reinforced packaging doubles package material, so it is not automatically the greenest option. For heavy Made-to-order parts, it reduces damage-related returns by 40%; where this avoids return transport, repeat production, and replacement packaging, it can lower total lifecycle impact. The check should reduce packaging rework and delay, while allowing certified lower-risk exceptions.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Part mass and geometry, package type, `package_material_kg`, route mode, Q1 compliance, packaging rework, damage/return rate, replacement shipments, material scrap, and total trace energy and transport through closure.

### 3.8 Critical-downtime triage and shared emergency lanes for precautionary urgent orders (S8, S9)

- **Target path or segment:** `Availability check -> Emergency production -> ... -> Dedicated van` for Critical-downtime Service-contract orders. The dedicated-van path has a 14-hour median cycle time and 3% lateness but 52 kgCO2e/order. The lower-emission stock/ground exception has 23-hour median cycle time and 9% lateness, so it is not an automatic substitute.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Create an explicit urgency classification: (1) verified true downtime, (2) high-risk but not yet downtime, and (3) precautionary urgent. Keep dedicated vans and emergency production for verified downtime. For suitable precautionary cases, use shared emergency courier lanes, priority stock allocation, or available ground/air alternatives that still meet the operational need.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** This reduces unnecessary dedicated-van use and potentially empty returns without sacrificing true downtime protection. The main risk is misclassification: moving a true downtime case to a slower shared lane can be operationally unacceptable. Shared lanes also create carrier-concentration risk unless a dedicated or alternate route remains available.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Urgency classification event, downtime justification, `expedite_flag`, route mode, van occupancy and empty-return data if available, actual customer downtime outcome, delivery time, SP2 documentation, alternative-route availability, carrier disruption performance, and escalation reversals.

### 3.9 Carbon-aware promise-date options for Regular Made-to-order customers (S10)

- **Target path or segment:** Made-to-order Regular orders, especially the immediate-production/air path with 70-hour median cycle time, 24 kWh/order, and 23 kgCO2e/order, compared with the batched-ground path with lower energy and transport but 118-hour median cycle time and higher lateness.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Offer a transparent, customer-approved promise-date choice at order entry: an urgent option or a lower-impact option that permits planned CNC_A/heat-treatment batching and ground or consolidated transport. The agreed date must be operationally realistic, not merely extended to improve a KPI.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** Regular customers may accept a longer lead time in exchange for predictable price, service, or emissions information. This can reduce emergency production and air freight, but only if demand forecasts remain credible and batching is capped to prevent obsolescence. It should not be used for Critical-downtime or mandatory Service-contract commitments without contractual agreement.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Original requested need date, negotiated promise date, option offered/accepted, customer segment, batch size and wait, route, energy, transport emissions, late delivery against both dates, cancellation, repeat ordering, and unshipped-within-14-days rate.

### 3.10 Sustainability exception log and controlled override workflow (S12)

- **Target path or segment:** All paths involving SP1 route overrides, SP2 dedicated-van decisions, Q1 packaging exceptions, and disruption-driven combinations that make the discovered model imprecise.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Make exceptions visible and reviewable rather than trying to eliminate them. At decision time, record the available alternatives, predicted on-time probability, customer/downtime reason, selected mode, packaging certification, approver, and actual outcome. This enables repeated avoidable exceptions to be redesigned.
- **Likely service, quality, and resilience effect:** The log does not directly reduce emissions, but it prevents policy rules from becoming blind constraints. Critical orders can still move immediately through a fast override path, while repeated unjustified air, van, or packaging exceptions become measurable. Excessive approval delay must not be imposed on true emergencies.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Completeness and timeliness of SP1/SP2/Q1 exception events, override reason codes, predicted versus actual service outcome, environmental increment versus feasible alternatives, repeat exceptions by customer/product/carrier, approval latency, and post-event review actions.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Energy-efficient batching can create expedited transport.**  
   Larger CNC_A and heat-treatment batches lower energy per item, but waiting can rise from 4 to 38 hours and heat-batch waiting is associated with a 1.7-times higher late risk. Orders that miss their slack may then be moved to air, emergency production, or dedicated van, offsetting the production-energy gain.

2. **Batching can convert material efficiency into obsolescence.**  
   Large batches may look efficient on kWh/item while producing surplus parts. The observed 6% rate of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days under forecast error is an environmental loss that should not be hidden by favorable production-energy averages.

3. **Consolidation can trigger customer or planner escalation.**  
   A 12–24 hour truck-consolidation wait can cause planners to switch the next order to air, split an order into partial deliveries, or trigger customer complaints and expedited replacements. Consolidation should therefore be based on forecasted promise feasibility, not blanket mode substitution.

4. **Curtailing air can displace pressure to picking and inspection.**  
   Replacing air with ground may induce priority picking and compressed pack/inspection windows. If this creates rushed inspections, it can increase the `Inspection -> Rework production` loop, hidden scrap, and later return transport.

5. **Packaging material reduction can increase total lifecycle waste.**  
   Replacing reinforced packaging with standard packaging lowers `package_material_kg` immediately, but for heavy Made-to-order parts it can violate Q1 and increase damage, return shipments, replacement production, and material scrap. Conversely, applying reinforced packaging to low-risk items can add material without enough damage reduction.

6. **Quality gates can become delay and expedite generators.**  
   More inspection can reduce defects, but an under-resourced gate may lengthen queues. If work is then rushed through inspection or diverted to emergency production, the expected quality and emissions benefit can disappear.

7. **Shifting away from CNC_B can create a new bottleneck and reduce redundancy.**  
   CNC_A has lower rework, but excessive migration can create queues, reduce due-date performance, and leave CNC_B insufficiently maintained as a disruption fallback. The environmental gain from fewer rework loops should not be purchased by a single-machine resilience failure.

8. **Reducing partial shipments can worsen service for critical line items.**  
   Holding an entire order to avoid a second shipment can delay the one part that prevents customer downtime. Customers may respond by placing duplicate orders, requesting more urgent service, or demanding air shipment, increasing emissions rather than reducing them.

9. **Promise-date negotiation can alter customer behavior.**  
   Customers may accept a slower “green” option for low-priority orders but then place later orders as urgent, split demand, or choose competitors for time-critical work. The original requested date and repeat-order behavior must be monitored, not only performance against a revised promise.

10. **Shared emergency courier lanes may shift rather than eliminate transport impacts.**  
   A shared lane can reduce dedicated-van use, but may have poor utilization, additional cross-docking, or provider concentration. It must not eliminate the dedicated-van or alternate-carrier capability needed for verified downtime events.

11. **Exception governance can create metric gaming.**  
   If managers are judged only on SP1/SP2 compliance, cases may be mislabeled as critical or promised dates may be extended artificially. Exception review needs to compare the recorded reason with actual downtime, requested date, route availability, and customer outcome.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a **segmented decision architecture** rather than a blanket “use slower transport” or “make larger batches” program. The redesign should combine transport, production, quality, and exception controls because the major environmental burdens are interconnected through rework and expedites.

1. **Make urgency and promise slack explicit at order intake.**  
   Record original requested need date, contractual promise, customer segment, product type, and whether a Critical-downtime order is verified downtime or precautionary urgent. This is essential before changing routes, batching, or partial-shipment rules.

2. **Use a route decision hierarchy that preserves service and resilience.**  
   - For Standard-stock Service-contract orders, apply priority pick plus ground when the predicted on-time probability is above 85%; this directly addresses the 22% SP1 violation rate without treating all air freight as unjustified.  
   - Use consolidated truck for non-urgent stock orders only where its 12–24 hour wait fits the promise.  
   - Keep air as a controlled fallback for insufficient slack, disruption recovery, or genuinely urgent Service-contract requirements.  
   - Keep dedicated vans for verified Critical-downtime cases. Shift only precautionary urgent cases to shared emergency lanes or other feasible alternatives, and retain at least one alternative route for Service-contract customers.

3. **Reduce avoidable waste before optimizing production energy.**  
   Prioritize the high-impact rework path through CNC_B and rushed inspection. Route eligible Made-to-order work to CNC_A, add a risk-based inspection gate, and prevent packaging-route mismatches. This is likely to reduce repeat CNC work, scrap, packaging rework, and replacement transport simultaneously.

4. **Batch only within a service and obsolescence envelope.**  
   Use dynamic CNC_A and heat-treatment batching with explicit waiting caps by segment. Made-to-order Service-contract work receives tighter caps; Regular Made-to-order customers can choose a longer, lower-impact promise where they agree to it. Do not optimize average kWh/item while ignoring the 1.7-times heat-batch late risk or the 6% unshipped-item signal.

5. **Fix partial shipment decisions at the line-item level.**  
   Replace default partial shipment with a decision based on line urgency, expected replenishment time, and total-order completion risk. This can remove the high-emission, high-lateness partial/backorder path without withholding parts that prevent customer downtime.

6. **Treat reinforced packaging as a lifecycle quality control, not a material target.**  
   Enforce Q1 for heavy Made-to-order parts unless certified low damage risk exists. Minimize packaging material through compatibility and right-sizing where safe, but do not reduce packaging in a way that creates returns, replacement production, or more transport.

7. **Make exceptions fast, visible, and reviewable.**  
   A sustainability exception log should support—not obstruct—emergency response. It should distinguish justified resilience actions from repeated avoidable air, van, or packaging exceptions and feed recurring patterns back into capacity, routing, and product-packaging redesign.

This package avoids the false choice between environmental performance and service. It reduces the clearest avoidable impacts—unnecessary air, multiple shipment legs, repeat production, packaging rework, and unplanned expedites—while preserving critical response capacity and route alternatives.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

### Baseline and implementation design

- Establish a pre-change baseline by the five reported case segments and by the main path variants, including stock-ground, stock-air, partial/backorder, CNC_A batch-ground, immediate CNC-air, CNC_B-rework-ground, critical dedicated-van, and critical stock/ground exception.
- Pilot changes in staged cohorts rather than implementing all interventions at once. For example: first packaging/inspection controls, then shipping recommender, then batching/promise-date controls.
- Compare treated orders with comparable untreated orders matched on `case_type`, customer type, product characteristics, promised lead time, machine availability, route reliability, and disruption period. Avoid comparing all air orders directly with all ground orders because urgency drives route selection.
- Use a sufficiently long observation window to capture backorders, rework, customer returns, replacement shipments, and the 14-day unshipped-item obsolescence proxy.

### Event-log data requirements

Extend or verify the log to retain:

- Decision-time timestamps for route assignment, production release, batch release, inspection release, and packaging release.
- Original requested need date, agreed promise date, predicted on-time probability by feasible route, actual delivery time, and reasons for promise changes.
- Order-line linkage for partial shipments, including line criticality, quantity, inventory reservation, and final complete-order date.
- `batch_id`, `batch_size`, batch energy allocation method, machine queue time, processing time, and rework linkage.
- Part mass, geometry, damage-risk certificate, packaging type, packaging material, route compatibility result, and return/damage reason.
- Transport leg identifiers, carrier, mode, emissions, consolidation wait, shipment utilization where available, and dedicated-van return-leg/empty-kilometre data.
- Criticality classification, downtime evidence, route alternatives considered, carrier-disruption state, and exception approver.

### Conformance monitoring

| Policy | Automated conformance test | Governance response |
|---|---|---|
| **SP1** | At route-decision time, where ground or consolidated truck had a documented probability above 85% of meeting the promise, confirm that one was selected or that an approved exception exists. | Review repeated air overrides by customer, product, planner, carrier reliability, and pick-delay cause. Correct capacity or promise design rather than merely denying overrides. |
| **SP2** | Every dedicated-van case must contain a Critical-downtime classification and explicit justification; distinguish verified downtime from precautionary urgency. | Missing justification is a compliance failure. Review whether the selected route was necessary, whether a shared emergency lane existed, and whether the customer outcome supports the classification. |
| **Q1** | For Made-to-order parts above 8 kg, confirm reinforced packaging or an inspection-based low-damage-risk certificate before shipping. | Investigate violations with subsequent packaging rework, damage, return, replacement production, and transport. Update part-route packaging rules where patterns recur. |
| **R1** | For Service-contract orders, record whether at least one alternate feasible route/carrier existed at the decision time and during disruption. | Escalate carrier or mode concentration, unavailable fallback lanes, and slow switch times. Do not report emissions savings as success if R1 coverage falls. |

### Segment-level service and quality guardrails

Monitor weekly and monthly, with both median and p90 outcomes:

- Late delivery, cycle time, cancellation, and complete-order time for all five case segments.
- Service outcomes specifically for converted air-to-ground orders, consolidated-truck orders, held-versus-partial orders, and promise-date-option orders.
- Critical-downtime outcomes separately from precautionary urgent outcomes; no aggregation that hides deterioration in true downtime protection.
- Rework-loop rate, first-pass yield, damage/return rate, Q1 violations, packaging-rework rate, and rework-attributable energy and scrap.
- Batch wait, batch-size distribution, energy/item, heat-treatment lateness, emergency-production share, and 14-day unshipped-item rate.
- Route/carrier concentration, alternative-route availability, disruption switch time, and emergency-mode utilization.

Predefine escalation and rollback conditions before pilots begin. For example, a route or batching rule should be paused if its eligible segment shows a sustained material increase in p90 lateness, cancellation, damage returns, or loss of R1 alternative-route coverage, even if transport emissions fall.

### Environmental accounting and review cadence

- Report energy, transport, material/scrap, and rework impacts as **trace totals**, summing all loops and shipment legs rather than only the first production or first shipment event.
- Report results both per fulfilled item and per order, with canceled orders and unshipped batch output shown separately to avoid denominator improvements that hide waste.
- Review environmental and service outcomes jointly at an operations, quality, transport, and customer-service forum. A reduction in air or vans is accepted only when accompanied by stable service and resilience evidence.
- Review exceptions monthly for recurring root causes: inaccurate promise dates, stock allocation, inspection capacity, packaging design, carrier reliability, forecast error, or inappropriate urgency classification.
- Use the exception log to update routing probabilities, batching caps, machine-assignment logic, packaging compatibility rules, and customer promise options on a controlled basis.
