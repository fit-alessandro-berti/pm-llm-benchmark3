# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The evidence is most consistent with **selective, episodic coordination**: Mira is positioned between automated routing and several human specialist groups, but she is not embedded in one stable production team. The different networks measure different relationships, so the apparent centrality contradiction is largely structural rather than substantive. 

## Handover-of-work network

A handover-of-work network represents directed transitions between resources that act successively on the same case. It is therefore particularly sensitive to routing, assignment, approval, and escalation events.

Mira’s out-degree centrality of 0.82, in-degree centrality of 0.51, and betweenness of 0.77 support several interpretations:

* She receives a substantial flow from `Bot_Rules`, especially after automated exception detection.
* She redistributes cases across medical, legal, senior-adjuster, and junior-adjuster resources.
* She connects otherwise separated parts of the organization: automation, temporary staff, adjusters, and specialists.
* Many resource-to-resource paths in the constructed graph pass through her.

This supports the description of Mira as a **transition broker or routing hub**. It does not by itself show that she performs most of the substantive claims work, owns the cases, has formal managerial authority, makes good routing decisions, or causes the observed delays. A single short administrative event can create both an incoming and an outgoing handover while consuming little service time.

The automation layer matters. `Bot_Rules` already has substantial handover betweenness, and the very large `Bot_Rules → Mira` flow means that Mira’s position is partly produced by the design and thresholds of the rules engine. An overly sensitive rules engine could make Mira appear overloaded; an overly narrow one could conceal exceptions from her.

There is also a numerical issue that should be reconciled before treating the edge frequencies as literal directly-follows counts. The listed outgoing edges from Mira sum to 1,640 occurrences, while she has only 960 recorded events. Listed incoming edges likewise sum to more than 960. Under a conventional single-sequence, adjacent-event handover definition, each Mira event can have at most one immediate predecessor and successor. Possible explanations include an indirect handover horizon, multiple successors created by tied timestamps, lifecycle duplication, differently filtered tables, or inconsistent aggregation. The overall topology may still be informative, but the absolute frequencies require validation.

## Working-together network

A working-together network measures whether two resources participate in the same case. It captures **case-level co-participation**, not temporal transfer, supervisory authority, communication intensity, or the importance of a brief intervention.

Mira’s weighted degree of 0.16 is low relative to the stable North and South team clusters. This indicates that she is not a conventional embedded team member like:

* Alice with Carla and Elise;
* Bruno with Diego and Farid;
* the senior and junior adjusters within each regional team.

It does not imply that Mira is unimportant. She touches only 22% of all claims, and her 960 events across 905 cases amount to approximately 1.06 recorded events per touched case. That is consistent with sparse interventions rather than sustained joint work.

The reported pair rates should also be interpreted relative to their denominator. If the percentages use all approximately 4,100 claims, an 8% Mira–Carla co-case rate represents roughly 328 claims—about 36% of Mira’s 905 touched cases. Thus, a pair can look peripheral in the all-claims network while being common within Mira’s selected exception cohort.

The statement that Mira’s short event duration explains the low working-together score is only partly correct. Event duration is not normally part of a co-case metric. The relevant mechanisms are that she:

1. participates in a minority of claims;
2. often participates only once;
3. spreads those interventions across many different partners;
4. is outside the dense North and South team cores.

The reconciliation is therefore:

> **Handover and subcontracting centrality reward brokerage across transitions; working-together centrality rewards repeated membership in cohesive case teams. Mira exhibits the first pattern, not the second.**

## Subcontracting network

With the supplied (n=2) configuration, the subcontracting network identifies short return structures of the form (A \rightarrow B \rightarrow A), possibly with a bounded number of intervening events. In organizational mining, “subcontracting” is a technical name; it does not necessarily mean a contractual outsourcing relationship.

The patterns:

* `Mira → Omar → Mira`, 126 times;
* `Mira → Priya → Mira`, 98 times;

suggest that Mira initiates specialist work and then resumes coordination after the specialist response. Her subcontracting betweenness of 0.69 is therefore consistent with:

* consultation management;
* temporary delegation with retained coordination ownership;
* interpreting a specialist response and deciding the next destination;
* reopening or reassigning the case after specialist input.

This is stronger evidence of a **send-and-return coordination loop** than the handover network alone. It is nevertheless not proof that Mira supervises Omar or Priya, formally approves their conclusions, or creates the need for specialist review. The same pattern could arise from a system convention in which specialist results are always returned to a central queue.

Results are also sensitive to the chosen (n). A short horizon emphasizes tight consultation loops; a wider horizon may count unrelated later returns and inflate the appearance of control.

## Performance/time statistics

Mira’s median service time of 4.2 minutes is consistent with brief routing, clarification, reopening, or assignment events. Her high-variance cycle-time contribution is not inconsistent with this: a short intervention can sit at the entrance to a long queue or determine whether a case enters a slow specialist path.

The strongest evidence of operational dependence is the six-day absence:

* ordinary low-value claims were largely unaffected;
* exception cases accumulated;
* the exception-case 90th percentile increased from 8.4 to 13.9 days.

This suggests a **localized capacity or knowledge dependency in exception handling**, particularly in the tail of the distribution. It does not establish that Mira is a steady-state bottleneck for the whole process. The absence may have exposed inadequate backup coverage rather than insufficient capacity during normal operations.

Case-mix selection is a major limitation. Mira handles disproportionate shares of reopened claims and complaints, and 48% of her cases carry an exception flag. Cases involving Omar, Priya, or repeated missing-information work are inherently likely to have longer durations. High duration among Mira-touched cases cannot therefore be attributed causally to Mira without comparing equivalent cases.

The vendor resources must be separated from Mira’s contribution. `Vendor_Verify` and `Vendor_Pay` have long time components, and delays at those boundaries may dominate some claims. Likewise, the rules engine’s classification quality may determine both Mira’s arrival rate and the complexity of the cases she receives.

Finally, service time requires valid start and completion information. Since the stated schema includes a timestamp and lifecycle transition rather than a separate start timestamp, the analysis should verify that start/complete events are correctly paired. Inter-event elapsed time must not be mislabeled as service time; it generally includes queueing and calendar delay.

## Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

Mira’s activity profile is qualitatively distinct from those of the team leads, adjusters, specialists, bots, temporary staff, and vendors. Her concentration in missing-information requests, reopenings, complaints, and specialist assignment would likely produce a separate organizational role such as:

* exception coordinator;
* escalation dispatcher;
* specialist scheduler;
* case-recovery coordinator.

Activity-based similarity could also reveal a limited functional resemblance to `Bot_Rules`: both route exceptional work, but one does so automatically and the other through human judgment. That would support a **human-in-the-loop exception-routing architecture**, not the conclusion that Mira and the bot have the same organizational authority.

Role discovery does not recover the formal organizational chart. A unique set of activity labels can force Mira into a singleton cluster even when she is operationally similar to team leads. Conversely, broad labels such as `Request Missing Information` could make her look similar to junior adjusters despite very different decision rights.

Automated and vendor resources should not be silently removed. They should first be retained as distinct resource classes, followed by a human-only sensitivity analysis. Otherwise, their highly specialized activity profiles may dominate the similarity structure.

There is also a label inconsistency to investigate: `Assign Specialist` appears in Mira’s activity profile and subcontracting fragments but is absent from the enumerated list of 14 process activities. It may be a valid administrative activity omitted from the list, a derived label, or a logging inconsistency.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

The following hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Mira could simultaneously be a formal exception coordinator, an organizational broker, and a key-person dependency.

## Hypothesis A: Formal coordinator / exception desk

**Mechanism.** `Bot_Rules` identifies cases requiring manual handling; Mira clarifies the problem, requests missing information, reopens claims when necessary, and selects an appropriate specialist or adjuster.

**Evidence supporting it.** The large `Bot_Rules → Mira` flow, the short median event duration, the broad outgoing handovers, and her concentration in reopenings and complaints fit a formal exception-desk design. The process owner’s phrase “queue cleaner” also describes an operational queue-management function, even though it minimizes its importance.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** Only 48% of Mira-touched cases are marked `exception_flag=True`. Either the flag is incomplete or time-dependent, or her role includes substantial non-exception coordination. She also has little working-together overlap with team leads, which suggests that her desk may operate parallel to, rather than inside, the formal team structure.

**Observable predictions.** Future logs should show Mira shortly after a rule-engine exception, followed by a bounded set of routing outcomes. Assignment reasons should map to case characteristics and specialist type. A trained substitute following the same decision rules should preserve routing accuracy and exception throughput during Mira’s absence.

## Hypothesis B: Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

**Mechanism.** Exception cases require Mira’s tacit knowledge, queue access, or informal authority. Cases can enter the exception queue without her, but cannot be reliably classified or released from it.

**Evidence supporting it.** The absence-period accumulation and rise in exception-case tail duration provide direct operational evidence that her availability matters. High handover and subcontracting betweenness indicate that many exception routes return to or pass through her.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** Her recorded service time is short, and routine claims did not slow during the absence. This argues against an organization-wide capacity bottleneck. The observation covers only one six-day period and may coincide with unusual arrivals, staffing, system conditions, or specialist availability.

**Observable predictions.** Queue age should rise before Mira’s events when her workload is high. Her return from absence should be followed by a release surge or backlog-clearing burst. Comparable absences without trained cover should reproduce the tail increase, while an effective backup should substantially reduce it.

## Hypothesis C: Organizational boundary spanner or triage broker

**Mechanism.** Mira bridges structural boundaries: automated versus human work, North versus South teams, routine adjusters versus medical/legal specialists, and temporary versus permanent staff.

**Evidence supporting it.** She has outgoing edges to both teams’ adjusters and to both specialist functions. Temporary staff escalate to her, and specialists return work to her. Her high handover betweenness combined with low team embeddedness is a classic brokerage pattern. Adjusters’ statement that she knows “where hard cases should go” directly supports this interpretation.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** Her co-occurrence with the team leads is surprisingly low, and the top edges do not show her managing the external payment or verification providers. Her brokerage may therefore be narrower than an enterprise-wide boundary-spanning role: she may primarily span the internal exception-to-specialist boundary.

**Observable predictions.** Mira-touched claims should cross more resource groups than routine claims, but with fewer events per participant. Her routing should distribute work based on specialist suitability or capacity. Removing her without replacement should increase fragmented handovers, bounced assignments, formal escalations, or specialist overload.

## Hypothesis D: Quality gatekeeper and discretionary control point

**Mechanism.** Mira not only routes cases but determines whether a problem warrants reopening, complaint escalation, or specialist intervention. She may therefore act as a practical quality gate even without formal approval authority.

**Evidence supporting it.** She touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. The specialist return loops suggest that she acts again after receiving expert input. The compliance reviewer’s concern identifies a plausible concentration of detection and routing discretion.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** Concentration is not evidence of misuse. These activities may simply be assigned to the exception desk by design. No outcome, assignment-reason, override, audit, or comparable-case evidence is provided, so there is no basis for claiming biased, improper, or unauthorized decision-making.

**Observable predictions.** After controlling for case type, priority, amount, region, channel, and exception characteristics, comparable cases may show systematic differences in specialist assignment or reopening. High-discretion cases may have missing reason codes, frequent rule overrides, or greater variation than cases processed by documented rules.

## Hypothesis E: Data artifact or automation-monitor account

**Mechanism.** Some events attributed to Mira may be system-generated routing records, completion clicks, or administrative updates rather than distinct human decisions. Her network position could consequently be inflated.

**Evidence supporting it.** Her events are short, often immediately follow `Bot_Rules`, and are followed by several events in which she is absent. `Assign Specialist` is missing from the master activity list, and the reported handover frequencies do not reconcile straightforwardly with her event count.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.** The absence-period backlog and staff descriptions indicate that at least part of the function is genuinely human and operationally consequential. A pure logging-artifact explanation is therefore weak.

**Observable predictions.** Suspect events would have tied or mechanically spaced timestamps, occur outside Mira’s working hours, lack valid start/complete pairs, or correspond exactly to system-field changes. Reattributing or removing such events would reduce her calculated centrality, but a material human-routing signal should remain.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

## 4.1 Validate the event semantics before interpreting centrality

Use `pm4py.format_dataframe` after verifying case identifiers, resource identifiers, timestamps, and lifecycle transitions. Then test:

* whether every start event has a corresponding completion;
* whether tied timestamps create multiple possible direct successors;
* whether `Assign Specialist` is an actual activity;
* whether the summarized tables use the same 12-week cohort and filters;
* whether bot, vendor, and temporary-resource identifiers represent individual actors or shared accounts;
* whether handover edge counts can be reproduced from the event-level log.

This step should precede management conclusions because some of Mira’s apparent centrality may depend on ordering and aggregation choices.

## 4.2 Cohort-specific handover-of-work networks

Apply `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` separately to:

1. exception and non-exception cases;
2. complaints, reopenings, and ordinary claims;
3. North- and South-routed cases;
4. pre-absence, absence, and post-absence windows;
5. the full resource graph and a clearly documented human-only analysis.

Do not merely delete bots and vendors and allow previously nonadjacent human events to become direct handovers without labeling that projection. Report both the original multilayer structure and any projected human network.

For every cohort, compare:

* `Bot_Rules → Mira` volume;
* Mira’s destinations;
* return flows from specialists;
* edge-level elapsed-time distributions;
* centrality with case-level bootstrap intervals.

`pm4py.discover_network_analysis` can be used for customized resource edges with performance attributes, while `pm4py.view_sna` or `save_vis_sna` can visualize changes. `networkx` can calculate weighted centralities and bootstrap stability.

## 4.3 Correctly normalized working-together analysis

Run `pm4py.discover_working_together_network`, but publish multiple denominators:

* percentage of all claims;
* percentage of Mira-touched claims;
* percentage of exception claims;
* Jaccard overlap between each resource’s case set;
* observed co-occurrence relative to that expected from case size and activity volume.

This will distinguish true social or operational isolation from a denominator effect. Compare Mira’s broad, shallow participation with the dense North and South team clusters. Also test whether Mira co-occurs with many resource groups on the same complex claims or instead routes each claim to only one destination.

## 4.4 Subcontracting sensitivity and fragment inspection

Run `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with several horizons, such as (n=1,2,3,4). For every major (A \rightarrow B \rightarrow A) pattern, inspect the actual activities and timestamps.

For `Mira → Omar/Priya → Mira`, classify returns as:

* normal specialist consultation;
* request for additional information;
* reopened work;
* misrouting or bounced assignment;
* administrative recording only.

Measure the elapsed time to the specialist, specialist service time, return delay, subsequent rework, and final outcome. A high return frequency with low rework would support successful coordination; repeated returns and reassignment would be more consistent with routing instability.

## 4.5 Activity-based similarity and organizational role discovery

Use both:

* `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity`;
* `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`.

Perform at least three versions:

1. all resources, retaining bots and vendors as explicit organizational actors;
2. humans only;
3. humans with activity frequencies normalized within resource so that high-volume resources do not dominate.

Then test whether Mira:

* forms a singleton exception-coordination role;
* clusters with Alice and Bruno because of routing and reopening;
* clusters with junior adjusters because of missing-information work;
* has a complementary profile to `Bot_Rules`.

The analysis should not equate a discovered cluster with formal authority. Add exception share, case complexity, and lifecycle information only as descriptive annotations rather than silently changing the role definition.

## 4.6 Filtering, variants, and temporal comparisons

Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to isolate:

* `exception_flag=True`;
* `Reopen Claim`;
* `Escalate Complaint`;
* specialist-review activities;
* claims involving Mira;
* claims involving temporary or vendor resources.

Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to compare variants such as:

* `Bot_Rules → Mira → specialist → Mira`;
* `Bot_Rules → team lead → adjuster`;
* exception claims that bypass Mira;
* missing-information loops involving temporary staff or `Vendor_Verify`.

Use pandas time filtering for the absence period and comparable before/after windows. Compare case mix by `case_type`, `priority`, `amount_band`, `region`, and `channel`; otherwise, variant-duration differences will largely reflect selection.

## 4.7 Service-time and waiting-time decomposition

Use `pm4py.get_service_time` only after validating lifecycle pairing. Apply `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` or an equivalent timestamp-based decomposition to separate active work from waiting.

The most informative intervals are:

* `Bot_Rules → Mira`: exception-queue entry delay;
* Mira event → first adjuster or specialist event: routing and downstream queue delay;
* specialist completion → next Mira event: result-return delay;
* Mira return → next operational activity: post-consultation release delay;
* team lead → `Vendor_Pay`: external payment delay;
* temporary staff → `Vendor_Verify` → next human event: verification waiting and rework.

Report medians, 90th and 95th percentiles, and queue-age distributions rather than relying on means. This would establish whether Mira’s risk is service-capacity, queue ownership, specialist availability, or external waiting.

## 4.8 Absence-period quasi-experiment and compliance analysis

Treat the six-day absence as a quasi-experiment, not as definitive causal proof. A difference-in-differences design could compare:

* exception versus non-exception claims;
* before, during, and after the absence;
* matched weekdays and similar arrival volumes;
* adjusted case-mix strata.

Outcomes should include queue age, specialist assignment delay, total duration, reopenings, complaints, and bounced handovers.

For compliance analysis, model specialist destination or reopening as a function of documented case characteristics. Examine unexplained variation only after adjustment for case type, priority, amount, region, channel, and exception characteristics. The objective is to identify decisions requiring review, not to infer misconduct from raw group differences.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

1. **Recognize and define the coordination function.** Whether Mira is called a coordinator, exception-desk operator, or queue cleaner, the log and absence evidence indicate that the function is operationally real. Its scope, decision rights, and ownership boundaries should be written down.

2. **Establish resilient coverage.** Designate more than one trained backup, preferably with coverage across both teams. Backups need the same queue access, specialist directory, routing rules, and authority required to reopen or reassign appropriate cases. Coverage should be tested operationally rather than assumed.

3. **Make routing auditable.** Record at least the incoming exception reason, target resource or specialty, assignment reason, rule recommendation, human override, queue entry and exit times, and actor responsible. High-impact overrides or unusual complaint/reopening decisions can receive a second review without imposing dual control on every routine assignment.

4. **Correct the measurement problems.** Reconcile the handover counts, formal activity inventory, lifecycle semantics, and exception-flag timing before using network metrics for performance evaluation.

5. **Protect capacity for coordination work.** Do not estimate Mira’s workload solely from her 4.2-minute median events. Queue monitoring, interruptions, specialist selection, follow-up, and tacit problem solving may consume time that is not represented as event service time.

## What should be monitored

Management should monitor:

* exception arrivals, completions, backlog, and oldest-case age;
* median and tail delay from `Bot_Rules` to a human decision;
* specialist queue load and bounce-back rates;
* frequency and outcome of Mira–specialist–Mira loops;
* reopened and complaint-case durations;
* coverage performance during Mira’s absence;
* `Bot_Rules` exception volume and apparent false-positive routing;
* vendor waiting separately from internal waiting;
* assignment and override patterns after adjustment for case mix;
* monthly network drift, accompanied by actual volume and time metrics.

High centrality should be treated as a diagnostic signal, not a target or performance score.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

Management should not:

* remove Mira or dissolve the exception desk based on low working-together centrality;
* conclude that she is a bottleneck solely from high betweenness;
* infer noncompliance from her concentration in complaints and reopenings;
* automate all specialist routing before the tacit decision logic is understood;
* force Mira into routine team co-working merely to raise a network score;
* redistribute work based only on event counts or unadjusted cycle times;
* impose organization-wide segregation of duties without first identifying which decisions are sufficiently material to justify it.

Any later removal or major redesign of Mira’s position must explicitly replace **queue ownership, exception interpretation, specialist selection, follow-up after specialist review, absence coverage, and auditable escalation authority**. Simply distributing her events among team leads would not necessarily replace that coordination system.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira appears to be a selective exception coordinator rather than a conventional team member. Her high handover and subcontracting centrality is consistent with many short routing events between Bot_Rules, adjusters, and medical or legal specialists; her low working-together score reflects sparse, episodic involvement across only 22% of claims, not necessarily low importance. The six-day absence provides the strongest evidence of operational dependence: ordinary claims remained stable, while exception queues accumulated and the exception-case 90th percentile rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. This supports a localized key-person risk, but it does not prove that Mira is a steady-state bottleneck or that her discretion is improper. Management should preserve the coordination function, appoint trained backups, document routing criteria, and record assignment reasons and overrides. Monitor exception backlog, tail cycle time, specialist load, return loops, and routing differences after adjusting for case mix. Do not remove Mira, force additional co-working, or automate all exception routing based on centrality alone. Any redesign must explicitly replace queue ownership, specialist triage, and escalation knowledge.
