## 1. What is observed

Automation availability is coded at the branch-month level: Alpha and Beta change from 0 in January–March to 1 in April, Gamma changes in July, and Delta and Epsilon remain at 0. Availability is not a case-level routing indicator. Actual completed automated-fast-path share was already 4–6% before the coded rollout and rose after availability to averages of 38% in Alpha, 40% in Beta, and 28.5% in Gamma. Thus system availability, triage assignment, successful automated fast-path completion, and the version of work actually performed are distinct exposures.

Rollout was not randomly assigned. Alpha and Beta were selected because of volume and historically poor cycle times; Gamma followed a readiness pilot. The rollout period also includes a centralized fraud desk beginning in March, routing-driven arrival growth at adopters, FTE changes, a May product-driven complexity increase, and possible local work-practice changes. These changes differ by branch and month.

The outcomes are branch-month aggregates rather than case records. Median and p90 cycle times cannot show which process variant a case followed or whether delays occurred in registration, specialist review, rework, or the fraud desk. Rework and escalation are recorded branch-wide, and fraud-desk waits are absent. CSAT is a monthly respondent mean with varying response rates and unavailable respondent counts, so its precision and representativeness cannot be assessed.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: cohort-specific stacked group-time ATT/event study** | Event month 0 is April for Alpha/Beta and July for Gamma; estimate cohort-by-calendar-month effects rather than a pooled two-way fixed-effect coefficient | For Alpha/Beta: Gamma before July plus Delta/Epsilon; after July: Delta/Epsilon only. For Gamma: Delta/Epsilon only. Never use already-treated branches as controls | Branch baseline outcomes and pre-slopes, calendar-month effects, registration complexity; arrivals/FTE only in sensitivity models because they may be policy-affected | E1, E2, E5, E6, E7, E8 | Conditional parallel untreated trends, no anticipation, no fraud-desk spillovers, no unmeasured local co-interventions, and comparable outcome measurement | Event-time leads, placebo launch dates for Delta/Epsilon, leave-one-control-out estimates, cohort-specific estimates, workload and complexity balance | Only five branches, two treated cohorts, short pre-periods, selected adoption, and simultaneous routing/staffing changes |
| Controlled interrupted time series centered on Gamma | June-to-July level change and May–June versus July–August slope/level comparison | Delta and Epsilon, optionally with Alpha/Beta excluded because already treated | Calendar timing, complexity, staffing, arrivals/FTE, and pre-period outcome level/slope | Immediate Gamma availability effect on median and p90 | In absence of launch, Gamma would have changed like its controls after accounting for measured covariates | Compare Gamma’s May–June to July–August change with control change; placebo breakpoints in April/May | Two post months; July seasonal recovery, delayed product effects, and limited pre-trend information |
| Outcome-specific synthetic control or augmented synthetic control | Separate April counterfactuals for Alpha/Beta and July counterfactual for Gamma | Weighted untreated donor branches available at each launch date | Pre-rollout outcomes, complexity, workload, and FTE paths | Branch-specific availability effect | Donor combination reproduces treated branch’s untreated trajectory; no donor contamination | Pre-period fit, donor weights, placebo gaps, leave-one-donor-out analysis | Three to six pre-months are too few; donor pool is tiny and Gamma becomes contaminated after July |
| Prospective randomized saturation or encouragement rollout in Delta/Epsilon | Randomize branch-week automation saturation and case-level automation offers among pre-specified eligible cases | Concurrent manually routed cases and lower-saturation branch-weeks | Randomization strata, risk class, arrival day, staffing, queue state | E3, E4, E5, E6, E7, and queue spillovers | Random assignment is implemented; logged route assignment is accurate; interference is measured rather than ignored | Assignment balance, compliance/use rates, override reasons, queue-state balance, fraud-desk monitoring | Spillovers within branch-week and ethical/operational limits on withholding a potentially useful tool |

The primary approach is preferable for the supplied panel because it respects staggered timing and avoids the known problem with a naïve two-way fixed-effects regression: with heterogeneous effects, early-treated Alpha/Beta can improperly serve as controls for later-treated Gamma. A cohort-specific design instead compares each treated cohort only with branches not yet treated in the same calendar month. It can report separate Alpha/Beta and Gamma estimates, event-time patterns, and explicitly show whether effects evolve as actual fast-path use rises.

It is still a weak observational design here, not a source of a definitive causal estimate. There are only five branches, Delta and Epsilon differ in baseline performance from the adopters, and the key rollout months include changes in demand, staffing, case mix, routing, and a shared fraud queue. Calendar effects absorb shocks common to all branches, but not branch-specific routing or staffing shocks. Complexity is measured at registration and is useful for sensitivity analysis, whereas arrivals and FTE may partly lie on the pathway from rollout policy to outcomes. Therefore, the primary estimand should be described as a conditional, cohort-specific availability effect, with alternative specifications reported rather than collapsed into one universal percentage.

**Calendar-aligned comparison.** For the April Alpha/Beta cohort, compare January–March with April–August for Alpha/Beta versus the never-treated Delta/Epsilon average over the same months.

These are arithmetic difference-in-differences, not identified causal effects. Alpha/Beta workload rose about 7.5 arrivals/FTE versus roughly 3.0 for Delta/Epsilon, while complexity and staffing also changed. That creates a plausible masking mechanism for operational benefit, but it is also possible that differential recovery, branch selection, or unmeasured managerial interventions create an apparently favorable contrast.

A second, narrower comparison uses Gamma’s May–June versus July–August change against Delta/Epsilon. Gamma’s median fell 8.5 hours versus a 4.3-hour fall for controls, an unadjusted relative change of about 4.2 hours. Gamma p90 fell 15.6 hours versus 8.0 hours for controls, or about 7.7 hours relative. Rework changed 2.0 pp in Gamma versus about +0.1 pp in controls, while escalation changed +1.0 pp versus about +0.1 pp. This is more temporally local but rests on only two post months.

**Treatment-intensity evidence.** Among Alpha/Beta, the first available month (April) had mean automated-fast-path use of 26%, median 47.8 hours, p90 96.5 hours, and rework 10.8%. In July–August, use averaged 48%, median was 46.2 hours, p90 was 103.9 hours, and rework was 13.6%. Gamma increased from 25% use in July to 32% in August while median fell from 38.4 to 36.0 and p90 from 87.1 to 82.7. These are inconsistent raw dose patterns: higher use coincides with modestly lower medians but worse Alpha/Beta tails and rework. Actual use is endogenous because case suitability, staff confidence, overrides, and queue conditions affect it.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** Before launch, Alpha’s median moved 50.446.949.4, Beta’s moved 45.445.946.4, and Gamma’s moved 36.436.539.6 before worsening further in May–June. The selected branches were historically weak, so a subsequent improvement may partly reflect regression to the mean. The March escalation increase is also visible in every branch, consistent with a systemwide process change. Three to six pre-periods are insufficient to establish parallel trends.

**Sensitivity scenarios, not confidence intervals.**

| outcome | descriptive anchors | observation-compatible scenario interval | reason for range |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Median cycle time | April cohort relative change 4.5 h; Gamma local relative change 4.2 h; raw Alpha/Beta changes mixed | roughly 8 to +4 h | Benefit could be masked by routing and FTE pressure; apparent benefit could be explained by recovery, selection, or unmeasured branch practices |
| p90 cycle time | April cohort relative change 6.3 h; Gamma local relative change 7.7 h; Alpha/Beta raw p90 worsened about 10 h | roughly 12 to +12 h | Tail outcomes are especially vulnerable to unrecorded specialist/fraud congestion and composition changes |
| Rework | Relative changes about 1.9 pp and 2.1 pp in the two comparisons | roughly 3 to +3 pp | A recorded reduction could be genuine or could represent displacement to specialist/fraud loops |

No formal finite bound is available because key confounders and queues are unmeasured. A single statement such as “automation reduces cycle time by X%” would therefore overstate what the panel can support.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma had a readiness pilot | Regression to the mean can exaggerate benefit; difficult implementations can mask benefit | Longer pre-history, adoption decision records, placebo adoption dates | Observational effect cannot be generalized to Delta/Epsilon |
| Nonparallel trends | Pre-launch medians and p90s do not move similarly across branches | Ambiguous | Event-time leads, pre-period slope comparisons, placebo gaps | Difference-in-differences interpretation fails |
| Time-varying confounding | Fraud desk, routing campaign, product change, and local work practices overlap rollout | Ambiguous and branch-specific | Dated intervention log, branch-week covariates, negative-control outcomes | Cannot separate automation from the rollout environment |
| Binary availability versus treatment intensity | `automation=1` does not imply that a case used automation; use grew gradually | ITT may be diluted toward zero; selected fast-path use can falsely look beneficial | Assignment, acceptance, override, and completion-route logs | No credible “effect of actual use” from current data |
| Treatment-version heterogeneity | Managers could change local work practices; model use and exception handling may differ | Ambiguous | Version records, training dates, protocol audits, override reasons | “Automation” is not one stable intervention |
| Fraud-desk spillovers | Automated triage sends more borderline cases to a shared centralized desk | Can mask benefit through congestion or contaminate controls | Fraud arrival, queue, service, wait, and branch-origin data | SUTVA/no-interference assumption fails |
| Staffing changes | Alpha lost two FTEs, Gamma lost one, Beta and Delta gained FTE | Usually masks a beneficial automation effect at understaffed adopters | Productive hours by role, shifts, overtime, absenteeism, WIP/FTE | Outcome changes may be staffing effects |

| Local management co-interventions | Managers could alter staffing priorities, triage thresholds, or work allocation during rollout | Ambiguous | Implementation diary, policy audit, role-level workflow logs | Estimated effect is of a local bundle, not automation alone |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**expand with an embedded evaluation**

Delta and Epsilon should be the next cohort, but expansion should mean a controlled saturation rollout rather than turning both branches to 100% at once. Use the existing monthly history for context, then collect at least eight weekly pre-period observations with the new instrumentation. For a 16-week evaluation, randomize each branch-week in balanced blocks to 25%, 50%, or 75% automation saturation among pre-specified eligible low- and medium-risk arrivals. Within each saturation week, randomly assign the stated share of cases, stratified by branch, risk class, and arrival day, to an automated offer; the remainder receives manual triage. High-complexity, fraud-borderline, and safety-sensitive cases should remain on the manual/specialist protocol initially.

The primary analysis should be intention-to-treat by randomized offer and branch-week saturation. Actual use, acceptance, overrides, and fast-path completion should support secondary compliance analyses. Collect case-level timestamps for registration, triage assignment, automated recommendation, override, specialist queue entry/exit, fraud queue entry/exit, information request, rework, resolution, reopening, and closure. Record risk, product, source channel, staffing hours by role, WIP, backlog age, service capacity, and fraud-desk queue state. Track all branches sharing the fraud desk so that local gains are not purchased through off-site delay.

Guardrails should stop or throttle a saturation level if p90 exceeds its matched manual or baseline comparator by more than 10% for two consecutive weeks, rework rises by more than 2 percentage points, fraud waits materially increase, safety misrouting occurs, or CSAT declines with a stable response process. Complaints, reopenings, and manual overrides should be reviewed weekly.

Scale eligible automation to 100% only if the randomized evidence shows at least a 5% median-cycle improvement or a clearly favorable operational margin, p90 is non-inferior within 5%, rework and safety outcomes are non-inferior, and fraud-desk waits do not worsen. Scale separately by risk stratum rather than assuming that a low-risk fast-path result transports to high-complexity cases.

```python
Epsilon,2026-07,0,935,27,38.0,4,44.8,98.9,16.2,10.8,3.83
Epsilon,2026-08,0,867,27,38.0,4,40.8,91.4,16.2,10.8,3.9
"""

# 1. Load and validate the balanced branch-month panel.
panel = pd.read_csv(StringIO(csv_text), parse_dates=["month"])
panel = panel.sort_values(["branch", "month"]).reset_index(drop=True)

required = {
    "branch", "month", "automation", "arrivals", "fte",
    "high_complexity_pct", "automated_fast_path_pct",
    "median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h", "rework_pct",
    "escalation_pct", "csat_1to5"
}
assert required.issubset(panel.columns)
assert panel.shape[0] == 40, "Expected 5 branches x 8 months."
assert panel[["branch", "month"]].duplicated().sum() == 0
assert panel.isna().sum().sum() == 0
assert set(panel["automation"].unique()).issubset({0, 1})
assert panel.groupby("branch").size().eq(8).all()
assert panel["month"].nunique() == 8
assert (panel["arrivals"] > 0).all() and (panel["fte"] > 0).all()
for col in ["high_complexity_pct", "automated_fast_path_pct", "rework_pct", "escalation_pct"]:
    assert panel[col].between(0, 100).all(), f"{col} must be a percentage."
assert panel["csat_1to5"].between(1, 5).all()

panel["arrivals_per_fte"] = panel["arrivals"] / panel["fte"]

print("Validated balanced panel:", panel.shape)
print("Branches:", sorted(panel["branch"].unique()))
print("Months:", panel["month"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m").unique().tolist())

# 2. Print and plot branch trends for the five requested outcomes.
# Each plot is intentionally a separate figure; no subplots are used.
outcomes = [
    "median_cycle_h",
    "p90_cycle_h",
    "rework_pct",
    "escalation_pct",
    "csat_1to5",
]

for outcome in outcomes:
    print(f"\nBranch trend table: {outcome}")
    print(panel.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values=outcome).round(2))

    plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
    for branch, branch_df in panel.groupby("branch"):
        plt.plot(branch_df["month"], branch_df[outcome], marker="o", label=branch)
    plt.title(f"{outcome} by branch")
    plt.xlabel("Month")
    plt.ylabel(outcome)
    plt.legend()
    plt.xticks(rotation=45)
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

# 3. Compute transparent adopter-specific pre/post contrasts.
# "Pre" means every observed month before the first automation=1 month.
# "Post" means every observed month from that month onward.
adoption_month = (
    panel.loc[panel["automation"].eq(1)]
    .groupby("branch")["month"]
    .min()
)
panel["adoption_month"] = panel["branch"].map(adoption_month)
panel["ever_treated"] = panel["adoption_month"].notna()

contrast_metrics = [
    "arrivals", "fte", "high_complexity_pct", "automated_fast_path_pct",
    "median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h", "rework_pct", "escalation_pct", "csat_1to5"
]

contrast_rows = []
for branch, adopt_month in adoption_month.items():
    d = panel.loc[panel["branch"].eq(branch)].copy()
    pre = d.loc[d["month"] < adopt_month]
    post = d.loc[d["month"] >= adopt_month]

    row = {
        "branch": branch,
        "adoption_month": adopt_month.strftime("%Y-%m"),
        "n_pre": len(pre),
        "n_post": len(post),
    }

    for metric in contrast_metrics:
        row[f"{metric}_pre"] = pre[metric].mean()
        row[f"{metric}_post"] = post[metric].mean()
        row[f"{metric}_change"] = post[metric].mean() - pre[metric].mean()

    # Ratio of average arrivals to average FTE, matching a transparent workload definition.
    row["arrivals_per_fte_pre"] = pre["arrivals"].mean() / pre["fte"].mean()
    row["arrivals_per_fte_post"] = post["arrivals"].mean() / post["fte"].mean()
    row["arrivals_per_fte_change"] = (
        row["arrivals_per_fte_post"] - row["arrivals_per_fte_pre"]
    )
    contrast_rows.append(row)

contrasts = pd.DataFrame(contrast_rows)
print("\nAdopter-specific descriptive pre/post contrasts:")
print(contrasts.round(3).to_string(index=False))

# 4. Construct relative-time indicators for staggered adoption.
# Event time is measured in months relative to first branch availability.
month_number = panel["month"].dt.year * 12 + panel["month"].dt.month
adopt_number = panel["adoption_month"].dt.year * 12 + panel["adoption_month"].dt.month
panel["event_time"] = month_number - adopt_number

for k in range(-3, 5):
    label = f"event_m{abs(k)}" if k < 0 else f"event_p{k}"
    panel[label] = (
        panel["ever_treated"] & panel["event_time"].eq(k)
    ).astype(int)

event_cols = ["branch", "month", "automation", "adoption_month", "event_time"] + [
    c for c in panel.columns if c.startswith("event_")
]
print("\nRelative-time indicators for treated branches:")
print(panel.loc[panel["ever_treated"], event_cols].to_string(index=False))

# 5. Descriptive panel models.
# IMPORTANT CAUSAL COMMENT:
# These regressions do NOT prove causality. A causal availability interpretation would require,
# at minimum, conditional parallel untreated trends, no anticipation, no fraud-desk spillovers,
# no unrecorded branch-specific co-interventions, and a stable treatment version.
# Those assumptions are doubtful in this setting. The binary model is deliberately labeled
# descriptive and should not replace a cohort-specific staggered-adoption analysis.

warnings.warn(
    "Only five branch clusters are available. Cluster-robust standard errors and p-values "
    "are highly unstable with so few clusters; do not treat them as reliable inferential evidence."
)
warnings.warn(
    "Median and p90 outcomes are aggregated branch-month summaries. They are not individual "
    "case outcomes and can change because of composition, censoring, or queue displacement."
)

descriptive_model = smf.ols(
    "median_cycle_h ~ automation + C(branch) + C(month)",
    data=panel
).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": panel["branch"], "use_correction": True}
)

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE binary-availability panel model:")
print("Formula: median_cycle_h ~ automation + branch fixed effects + calendar-month fixed effects")
print(descriptive_model.summary().tables[1])

# IMPORTANT CAUSAL COMMENT:
# The intensity coefficient is even less causal than the availability coefficient unless
# automated fast-path use is randomly assigned or validly instrumented. Actual use can depend
# on eligibility, case difficulty, staff confidence, overrides, and congestion. Complexity and
# arrivals/FTE may themselves be affected by rollout policy, so this is an adjusted association,
# not a controlled direct causal effect.
intensity_model = smf.ols(
    "median_cycle_h ~ automated_fast_path_pct + high_complexity_pct + arrivals_per_fte "
    "+ C(branch) + C(month)",
    data=panel
).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": panel["branch"], "use_correction": True}
)

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE treatment-intensity model:")
print(
    "Formula: median_cycle_h ~ automated_fast_path_pct + high_complexity_pct "
    "+ arrivals_per_fte + branch FE + calendar-month FE"
)
print(intensity_model.summary().tables[1])

print("\nReminder: use cohort-specific not-yet-treated comparisons, placebo checks, "
      "and process/queue data for causal assessment; do not interpret these regressions "
      "as proof that automation caused the estimated associations.")
```

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** Availability coincided with substantial increases in completed automated-fast-path share, but median and p90 outcomes moved differently across Alpha, Beta, and Gamma.
- **Conditionally causal:** If untreated trends would have been parallel after accounting for measured differences, and if fraud-desk spillovers and local co-interventions were negligible, the descriptive comparisons are consistent with roughly 4–5 fewer median-cycle hours and 6–8 fewer p90 hours.
- **Suggestive:** Gamma’s immediate post-July improvement, alongside reduced rework and higher fast-path use, suggests automation may be beneficial in a branch where staffing and workload are relatively stable.
- **Not supported:** “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a general causal statement for all branches, including Delta and Epsilon, is not supported by these data.
