## 1. Materiality Framework

Use a two-dimensional assessment:

1. **Business severity** — the materiality of what happened, or may have happened, to the claim payment process.
2. **Confidence** — how strongly the supplied alignment and context support that interpretation.

### Severity scale

| Severity | Meaning | Typical characteristics |
|---:|---|---|
| **1** | Negligible / technical noise | No control impact; harmless telemetry, duplicate transport event, fitting hidden move, or minor model artifact. |
| **2** | Low materiality / model or evidence friction | Process-model mismatch, label mismatch, or weak evidence, but no clear payment-control failure or customer harm. |
| **3** | Moderate materiality / evidence or control-gap concern | High-value or high-risk case with missing core evidence, weak cross-channel evidence, or timing uncertainty, but compensating controls may exist. |
| **4** | High materiality / control failure | Payment or approval control bypassed, data-aware policy violated, or fraud-relevant condition ignored; exposure is material even if amount is modest. |
| **5** | Critical / severe control failure | High exposure, irreversible or hard-to-reverse payment, missing required fraud/manual control, payment before applicable approval, or strong fraud red flag. |

### Severity dimensions

- **Control objective**: fraud prevention, approval authority, assessment completeness, payment sequencing, notification evidence.
- **Amount / exposure**: higher EUR amounts increase severity, but low-value fraud red flags can still be high severity.
- **Reversibility**: bank-accepted payments are harder to reverse than pending or unpaid claims.
- **Timing**: payment before approval or fraud screening is more severe than late or missing notification.
- **Customer / regulatory harm**: duplicate notifications, denied-claim handling, notification proof, auditability.
- **Recurrence**: one-off anomalies may be incidents; repeated patterns indicate systemic control or logging weaknesses.
- **Evidence reliability**: immutable system evidence is stronger than verbal assertions, mutable CRM text fields, or external documents without stable case linkage.
- **Model scope**: if the model is payable-claims-only, denied or withdrawn cases may generate high alignment cost without operational failure.

### Confidence versus severity

- **Severity** asks: *If the interpretation is correct, how bad is it?*
- **Confidence** asks: *How reliable is the interpretation given the available evidence?*

A case can be **severity 5 but medium confidence** where a major control failure is suspected but evidence is incomplete. Conversely, a case can be **severity 1 with high confidence** where the deviation is clearly harmless telemetry.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

Raw alignment cost is an edit-distance measure under a chosen cost function. In this dataset, every visible log/model move costs 1. That technical cost does not represent compliance risk, financial exposure, customer harm, or evidential confidence.

1. **Cost-function design can equalize trivial and severe deviations.**  
   CL-601 has raw cost 3 because of three UI telemetry events. CL-602 has raw cost 1 because only `Fraud Screen` is missing. Technically, CL-601 appears “worse” than CL-602, but business reality is the opposite: CL-601 is harmless noise, while CL-602 is a EUR 480,000 HIGH-risk control failure.

2. **Zero-cost alignments can hide data-aware policy failures.**  
   CL-609 and CL-610 are fully synchronous and have raw cost 0. However, CL-609 violates the EUR 5,000/LOW-risk auto-approval condition, and CL-610 violates the recent-bank-change rule. The alignment engine sees the activity sequence but not the business attributes that make the sequence non-compliant.

3. **Hidden moves may be technically fitting and business harmless.**  
   CL-605 contains a silent `tau` move with cost 0. This is not a deviation; it is a Petri-net routing artifact. A low or zero cost can be correct for technical fit, but it does not by itself prove policy compliance.

4. **Model scope can create high cost without operational failure.**  
   CL-607 and CL-612 have raw cost 4 because the payable-claims model expects assessment, approval, payment, or notification after denial/withdrawal. The business process legitimately stopped before payment. The cost reflects model incompleteness and scope dispute, not financial loss.

5. **Missing events are not proof that activities did not occur.**  
   CL-606 has raw cost 1 because `Assess Loss` is missing from the core log, but an external licensed adjuster signed a report. The issue may be evidence integration, not absence of assessment. Similarly, CL-608 lacks core notification but has a CRM/portal trace with weak linkage.

6. **Irreversibility and timing are invisible to raw cost.**  
   CL-604 has raw cost 3, but the bank accepted payment before manual approval. Once money leaves, severity rises sharply because recovery is difficult. Alignment cost does not encode irreversibility.

7. **Activity granularity and label mismatch can inflate cost without control failure.**  
   CL-611 has raw cost 2 because `Assess Damage` and `Assess Loss` are treated as different labels. If they represent the same business activity, the cost reflects naming/ontology friction rather than a missing control.

8. **Duplicated telemetry can create cost without business meaning.**  
   CL-603 has raw cost 1 from a duplicate notification event with the same message ID. The underlying business notification likely occurred once. The cost reflects transport retry behavior, not claim-payment risk.

Therefore, raw cost is useful as a diagnostic clue, but it must be translated into business severity using policy overlay, evidence quality, amount, timing, reversibility, and recurrence.
