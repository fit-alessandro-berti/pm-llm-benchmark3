# 2. Integrated explanation

The pattern is over-determined. At least four real mechanisms and two measurement artifacts overlap, and they push service time and error rates in partly *opposite* directions at different points of the shift. A single-cause story ("people warm up and then get tired") fits the curve superficially but is contradicted by the stratified tables.

**Start of shift (positions 1–15): mostly case mix, handover cost and some junior warm-up.** Workers begin with the backlog left by the previous shift. It is complex (0.72), high-priority (41%) and often carryover, requiring re-orientation on cases someone else started. Seniors deliberately take these cases. A rough adjustment shows how much this alone explains. Assume, for illustration, that the low and high complexity bands average about 0.4 and 0.8 on the score scale, so the complexity gap is worth about 24 min per score unit (22.4 − 12.7 = 9.7 min per 0.4). Then the complexity drop from 0.72 (positions 1–5) to 0.50 (positions 31–50) predicts about 5 of the 5.9 minutes of decline. Little is left for "warm-up" in aggregate. Errors tell the same story. A bin that is about 80% high-complexity would be expected to show 3.8–5.1% errors at low-to-medium WIP, yet positions 1–5 show only 3.4%. Seniors handle the hard work and there is no aggregate error warm-up penalty. The one place warm-up seems real is among juniors (5.1% early vs 4.4% later at low WIP). This fits unfamiliarity plus, possibly, juniors getting complex carryover they are not equipped for.

**Mid-shift (positions 16–50): the "sweet spot" is a combination of conditions, not peak human performance.** Queues are at their lowest (31–33), complexity is moderate and carryover has been cleared. Errors are at their minimum (2.5–2.7%), matching the low- or medium-WIP, mostly-low-complexity cells of the WIP table (about 2.6–3.6%). Calling this "learning" would misread a favourable operating state as a skill improvement.

**Late shift (positions 51+): queue pressure dominates, with likely fatigue amplification and measurement artifacts.** In the Day shift, demand peaks late (queue 38 → 75). The Late shift inherits that backlog, and the last hours run at WIP 62–88. Under this load, people compress their work: they skip optional verification, call customers back less, accept bot classifications or re-check them hastily, and batch similar items. Service time falls further (11.4 → 10.8 min). This late speed is not learning. It is roughly what the WIP table's within-band pressure effect predicts (−25%), partly offset by other factors. Errors rise sharply (4.6%, 7.9%) even though complexity keeps falling (0.46 → 0.43), so after case-mix adjustment the late deterioration is *larger* than it looks.

The WIP × complexity cells do not fully account for it. For a bin that is mostly low-complexity, they predict about 3.4% at WIP 62 (observed 4.6%) and about 6.2% at WIP 88 (observed 7.9%). The mid-shift bins, by contrast, are predicted almost exactly. Candidate explanations for this 1–2 percentage point late residual are:

1. **Dose-response within the ">70" band.** Mean WIP of 88 is deeper overload than the band average.
2. **Time-on-task fatigue interacting with pressure.** The same WIP is harder to absorb after 6–7 hours. This does not contradict the resource table, because "later under low WIP" is mostly mid-shift, and late low-WIP events are rare.
3. **Quality Check deferred to shift end.** Errors made earlier are detected and recorded in the last 45 minutes, and Rework Correction events themselves occupy late positions.
4. **Junior batching and bulk logging.** This concentrates short, possibly lower-quality Document Reviews in late bins and inflates event counts.
5. **Bot misclassifications under surge conditions.** These are corrected, or missed, by humans at the worst possible moment.

**Shift-level view.** Last-two-hour error rates rank exactly by queue at shift end: Early 42 → 4.2%, Night 55 → 5.1%, Late 66 → 6.8%, Day 75 → 7.5%. This is strong ecological evidence for pressure over circadian timing. Night's lowest-risk-by-clock hours (04:00–06:00) are not its worst for errors, though they are the slowest (15.2 min). That slowness is consistent with circadian slowing, but it is not separable from Night's urgent-exception mix and its two-person staffing. Early's 4.2% at a modest queue is a hint that end-of-shift effects (fatigue, Quality Check bursts) exist independently of pressure, but only a hint.

**Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions.** "Faster" has at least four different meanings in this log:

1. **Easier cases.** Service time falls and errors should also fall. This is the case-mix effect, dominant early.
2. **Removal of slack.** Seniors on routine cases at moderate WIP work faster by cutting idle time and deliberation they did not need (Parkinson's law in reverse). Errors stay flat: Seniors show 2.1% vs 2.2%.
3. **Compression below the task's verification needs.** When WIP exceeds about 70, and especially for complex cases, juniors and late hours, speed comes from dropping checks. Errors rise roughly 2.6–2.8× (a multiplicative effect), so the *absolute* risk concentrates in the complex × high-WIP × junior cells (10.9% overall, 12.4% for juniors).
4. **Measurement artifacts.** Bulk logging produces apparent speed with no real change in effort. Quality Check deferral produces apparent late errors that were made earlier.

Errors rise only when mechanism 3 is active, or when mechanism 4 reallocates detected errors to late positions.

**The speed gain is probably illusory at system level.** Compare positions 31–50 with 76+: 2.1 minutes saved per event, but 8.7 percentage points more 7-day rework (5.0% → 13.7%). If a rework episode costs R minutes (Rework Correction, a new Quality Check, possibly a new callback), the break-even point is R ≈ 2.1 / 0.087 ≈ 24 min. Given the service times in this log, a rework loop almost certainly costs more than 24 minutes, before counting customer harm and the next shift's inherited backlog. There is also a reinforcing loop: pressure → compression → errors → rework → more WIP → more pressure. This helps explain why the Late shift inherits about 70 queued items and ends with about 66: the backlog regenerates partly from the system's own errors.

**Bottom line.** The falling service-time curve is mainly case mix early and queue pressure late, with a modest warm-up component for juniors and a bulk-logging artifact. The rising error curve is mainly queue pressure, which is strongly moderated by experience and complexity. It is plausibly amplified by time on task and inflated in its timing by deferred Quality Check and bot corrections. Circadian effects are not identifiable from the aggregates because Night is confounded with specific staff and case types. None of this can be settled from these tables; Section 3 describes how to separate the mechanisms.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## 3.1 Data preparation and validity checks (do these before any modelling)

- **Timestamp semantics.** Check whether `start_timestamp` is genuinely logged or reconstructed. Look for chained starts (`start` = previous `complete` for the same resource), zero or negative durations, and completion timestamps clustered at shift end (a bulk-logging signal).
- **Error semantics.** Determine whether `error_flag` marks the event where the error was *committed* or the event where it was *detected* (Quality Check / Rework Correction). Re-attribute errors to the commission event where possible.
- **Queue semantics.** Establish whether `queue_length_at_start` is system-wide, team-level or per resource, and whether `wip_at_start` is personal WIP.
- **Complexity semantics.** Establish when and by whom `case_complexity_score` is set (before or after triage, human or model), and test whether it drifts with WIP for the same claim types.
- **Censoring.** For "rework within 7 days", events in the last 7 days of the extract are right-censored. Exclude them or use survival methods.
- **Carryover flag.** Mark cases whose first event precedes the current shift start. Also check for events whose start and complete fall in different shifts, which inflates service time with off-shift pauses.

## 3.2 Core pipeline (PM4Py + statistics)

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

raw = pd.read_csv("claims_event_log.csv",
                  parse_dates=["time:timestamp", "start_timestamp", "shift_start_time"])

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(raw, case_id="case:concept:name", activity_key="concept:name",
                             timestamp_key="time:timestamp", start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")

log["service_min"] = (log["time:timestamp"] - log["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
log["clock_hour"] = log["start_timestamp"].dt.hour
log["weekday"] = log["start_timestamp"].dt.dayofweek
log["human"] = log["org:resource"] != "Bot_Risk"
log["exp_group"] = log["org:resource"].str.split("_").str[0]          # Senior / Mid / Junior / Night / Bot
shift_map = {6: "Early", 9: "Day", 14: "Late", 22: "Night"}
log["shift_type"] = log["shift_start_time"].dt.hour.map(shift_map)
log["res_shift"] = log["org:resource"] + "|" + log["shift_id"].astype(str)

first_seen = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["start_timestamp"].transform("min")
log["carryover"] = (first_seen < log["shift_start_time"]).astype(int)

log = log.sort_values(["org:resource", "start_timestamp"])
g = log.groupby("res_shift")
log["busy_cum_min"] = g["service_min"].cumsum() - log["service_min"]   # workload already done this shift
log["gap_prev_min"] = (log["start_timestamp"] - g["time:timestamp"].shift()).dt.total_seconds() / 60
log["new_block"] = (log["gap_prev_min"].fillna(999) >= 15).astype(int)  # idle >= 15 min ~ break
log["block_id"] = log.groupby("res_shift")["new_block"].cumsum()
log["min_since_break"] = (log["start_timestamp"]
                          - log.groupby(["res_shift", "block_id"])["start_timestamp"].transform("min")
                          ).dt.total_seconds() / 60

log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log, start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")
log = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(log, start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")

hourly = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log, grouper_freq="H",
                                                   start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
                                                   resource_key="org:resource")

m = log[log["human"]].rename(columns={
    "case:concept:name": "case", "concept:name": "act", "org:resource": "res",
    "minutes_since_shift_start": "mins", "wip_at_start": "wip",
    "queue_length_at_start": "queue", "case_complexity_score": "cx",
    "sequence_position_within_shift": "pos", "resource_experience_months": "exp_m"})
m["log_st"] = np.log(m["service_min"].clip(lower=0.1))
```

## 3.3 Within-resource comparisons

Compare each worker only with themselves. Use resource fixed effects, or better, resource-shift fixed effects so that a worker's good or bad day is absorbed. Cluster standard errors by resource-shift. The central question: *for the same person, on comparable cases, does performance change with minutes on shift once WIP is held fixed, and vice versa?*

```python
fe_st = smf.ols("log_st ~ bs(mins, df=5) + bs(wip, df=4) + cx + C(priority) + C(activity_family)"
                " + carryover + C(clock_hour) + C(weekday) + C(res_shift)", data=m
               ).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": m["res_shift"]})

fe_err = smf.logit("error_flag ~ bs(mins, df=5) * I(wip > 70) + cx + C(priority)"
                   " + C(activity_family) + carryover + C(clock_hour) + C(res)", data=m
                  ).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": m["res_shift"]})
```

A high-dimensional fixed-effects estimator (e.g., `pyfixest` with `| res_shift + act`) is preferable at scale. The interaction `bs(mins) * I(wip > 70)` directly tests "fatigue as an amplifier of pressure".

## 3.4 Within-shift position effects

- Use **minutes since shift start**, **cumulative busy minutes** and **minutes since last break** as the primary time-on-task measures. Treat `pos` only as a secondary descriptor, because position is endogenous: fast workers and short or batched tasks reach high positions. Showing that the "76+ effect" shrinks when you switch from position to minutes reveals this selection.
- Estimate the warm-up curve on a clean subsample (non-carryover, low WIP, low and high complexity separately), by experience group.
- Test post-break recovery: compare errors and service time in the 30 minutes after an idle gap of 15 minutes or more with the 30 minutes before it, within resource-shift.
- Tabulate bin composition (share of each resource, shift type, activity family and batched events per position bin) to expose survivorship in the 76+ bin.

## 3.5 WIP / queue stratification

- Stratify at WIP < 30 / 30–70 / > 70, and also fit a dose-response spline to locate thresholds (is 70 the right alert level?).
- **Address reverse causality.** Use lagged WIP (queue 30–60 minutes before the event start), and instrument WIP with exogenous arrival surges (arrival rate from `insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`, aggregated by hour) or unplanned colleague absence.
- **Event study.** Align events on the moment the queue first crosses 70 within a shift and plot service time, error rate and variant mix from −60 to +90 minutes.
- **Task-reduction check.** Compare control-flow between low- and high-WIP sub-logs. Look for fewer Customer Callbacks, fewer or more Specialist Referrals, and shorter Document Review → Risk Classification handoffs.

```python
hi = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "wip_bin", ["high"], level="event", retain=True)
lo = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "wip_bin", ["low"], level="event", retain=True)
dfg_hi, sa_hi, ea_hi = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(hi)
dfg_lo, sa_lo, ea_lo = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(lo)
st_hi = pm4py.get_service_time(hi, start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp", aggregation_measure="median")
st_lo = pm4py.get_service_time(lo, start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp", aggregation_measure="median")

rushed = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(log, ("Document Review", "Risk Classification"),
                                        min_performance=0, max_performance=120, keep=True)
callback_share_hi = hi.groupby("case:concept:name")["concept:name"].apply(
    lambda s: "Customer Callback" in set(s)).mean()
```

## 3.6 Complexity adjustment

- Adjust for complexity as a continuous spline plus its interactions with WIP and experience. Do not rely on two coarse bands, because residual confounding within a band is large.
- Use inverse-probability weighting to reweight each position bin to a common case mix (complexity, priority, activity family, carryover), then re-plot the curves. The reweighted service-time curve should be much flatter early. The reweighted error curve should be steeper late.
- Use trace clustering to capture case-mix dimensions that the score misses:

```python
clusters = list(pm4py.cluster_log(log))                    # sub-logs by trace similarity
for k, sub in enumerate(clusters):
    log.loc[log["case:concept:name"].isin(sub["case:concept:name"].unique()), "trace_cluster"] = k
mix = pd.crosstab(log["pos_bin"], log["trace_cluster"], normalize="index")
```

- Check whether complexity scoring is itself affected by pressure. For the same claim type, compare the score distribution under high vs low WIP. Also check whether cases scored low under high WIP have higher downstream rework.

## 3.7 Shift and time-of-day controls

- Include clock hour, weekday and shift type as separate factors. Identify circadian effects vs time on task from **overlap windows**:
  - At 09:00–14:00, Early workers are 3–8 h into their shift and Day workers 0–5 h.
  - At 14:00–17:00, Day workers are 5–8 h in and Late workers 0–3 h.
  - Within an overlap window, the shared system queue is roughly identical, so this design also partly controls for queue pressure.

```python
ov = m[m["clock_hour"].between(9, 13) & m["shift_type"].isin(["Early", "Day"])]
ov_err = smf.logit("error_flag ~ mins + wip + cx + C(priority) + C(activity_family) + C(clock_hour) + C(res)",
                   data=ov).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": ov["res_shift"]})
```

- For Night, report the resource and shift confounding explicitly. Estimate circadian effects only if some staff rotate across shifts, or through case-matched comparisons.

## 3.8 Batching detection

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log, merge_distance=5 * 60, min_batch_size=3,
                                 resource_key="org:resource")
for (act, res), n_batches, by_type in batches:
    print(act, res, n_batches, {t: len(b) for t, b in by_type.items()})   # e.g. "Sequential batching"

m["same_act_prev"] = m["act"].eq(m.groupby("res_shift")["act"].shift())
m["chained"] = m["gap_prev_min"].abs() < 0.2
m["very_short"] = m["service_min"] < 2
m["run_break"] = ~(m["same_act_prev"] & m["chained"] & m["very_short"])
m["run_id"] = m.groupby("res_shift")["run_break"].cumsum()
m["run_len"] = m.groupby(["res_shift", "run_id"])["act"].transform("size")
m["bulk_suspect"] = (m["run_len"] >= 5) & m["very_short"]
```

## 3.11 Automation interaction

- Define a bot correction as a human Risk Classification or Rework Correction that follows a Bot_Risk Risk Classification in the same case.
- Model correction probability on queue, complexity, claim attributes, and upstream Document Review duration and batching status. The key question is whether the "high-queue" effect is really about inputs.
- Validate corrections against a blinded audit sample (was the bot or the human right?). Compare downstream Eligibility Decision error rates for bot-kept vs bot-corrected vs human-only classifications.
- Use the bot as a negative control. It has no fatigue, learning or circadian rhythm, so any pattern in *its* error rate by hour or position reflects case mix or input quality, and that pattern should be subtracted from the human-side interpretation.

## 3.12 Robustness and inference

- Pre-specify primary estimands, for example: the effect of WIP > 70 on errors within resource-shift, and the effect of hour 7–8 vs hour 3–4 at fixed WIP.
- Cluster standard errors by resource-shift, and correct for multiple comparisons across resources.
- Sensitivity analyses: position vs minutes, excluding batched events, excluding carryover, commission-time vs detection-time error attribution, alternative WIP definitions.
- Prefer prospective designs for decisions: stepped-wedge rollout of breaks or extra seniors, and constrained randomization of assignments within qualified pools.

# 4. Intervention options

**1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers**
- *Expected benefit.* Addresses fatigue from overtime or extended shifts, if such shifts exist. It helps if analyses show an error rise with hours on shift *at fixed WIP*, or a fatigue × pressure interaction beyond hour 8.
- *Risk.* Shifts are already nominally 8 h. If the main driver is queue pressure, a cap removes capacity exactly when demand peaks and hands a larger backlog to the next shift (the Late shift already inherits about 70 items). This raises pressure and handovers. Handover itself creates re-orientation cost and carryover complexity. A blanket rule also ignores differences between roles.
- *Monitor.* Events with `minutes_since_shift_start` > 480; error rate by hour on shift stratified by WIP; queue at shift end and next shift's start; carryover share and handover count per case; first-hour service time of the next shift.
- *Verdict.* Useful only as a guardrail against overtime, not as a main lever.

**2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift**
- *Expected benefit.* It would make the start-of-shift curve look better and protect juniors from complex carryover during warm-up.
- *Risk.* The first hour is when seniors are available, the queue is not yet at peak and complex backlog is already waiting. Delaying these cases increases their waiting time and SLA risk and pushes them into the late, high-WIP window, which is the strongest risk zone (high complexity × high WIP: 10.9% errors). Early slowness is largely a *correct* response to harder cases, not a defect.
- *Better variant.* Route complex carryover to seniors or mid-level staff at shift start, give juniors a short warm-up on routine cases, and *avoid* scheduling complex work into the high-WIP late window.
- *Monitor.* Complexity mix by hour and by experience group; waiting time and SLA breach rate of complex cases; error rate of complex cases by WIP band; share of complex cases started when WIP > 70.
- *Verdict.* Reject as stated; adopt the routing variant.

**3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70**
- *Expected benefit.* Targets the mechanism with the strongest evidence. It is useful if the alert *triggers a capacity or quality response*: pull in a floater or senior, protect Quality Check capacity, route complex cases to experienced staff, pause non-urgent low-value work, or increase bot-output review.
- *Risk.* An alert aimed at workers ("the queue is high") may *increase* speed pressure and errors. Alert fatigue is likely if the threshold is poorly calibrated (it should come from the dose-response analysis and may differ by team or activity). It can also shift blame onto the workers who received the alert.
- *Monitor.* Share of working time with WIP > 70; time from alert to response; error, rework and bot-correction rates under WIP > 70 before and after; queue recovery time after crossing the threshold; number of alerts per shift.
- *Verdict.* Adopt, but direct the alert to supervisors and planners and tie it to a predefined response playbook.

**4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias**
- *Expected benefit.* Makes worker comparisons and causal estimates of experience, time and WIP effects credible, and removes cherry-picking.
- *Risk.* Pure random assignment destroys skill matching: juniors receive complex or urgent cases, seniors spend time on routine work, and priority handling suffers. Errors and SLA breaches would likely rise, possibly harming customers. It also removes workers' legitimate discretion.
- *Better variant.* Use constrained randomization within eligible pools and strata (e.g., randomize among qualified resources within complexity and priority bands), apply it only during defined evaluation windows or to a subset of cases, and obtain ethics and works-council agreement.
- *Monitor.* Covariate balance across resources; error rate by experience × complexity; SLA and waiting times of high-priority cases; worker-reported workload.
- *Verdict.* Use as a research design, not as a permanent operating rule.

**5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours**
- *Expected benefit.* Addresses fatigue and attention decline, and could blunt the fatigue × pressure interaction. Breaks are low-cost and supported by general human-factors evidence.
- *Risk.* The final two hours of the Day shift coincide with peak demand. Unstaggered breaks reduce capacity when the queue is highest, which could raise WIP and pressure on those still working. Post-break re-entry costs can also occur. If late errors are mainly pressure-driven or artifacts (Quality Check bursts), breaks alone will show little effect.
- *Implementation.* Stagger breaks, cover them with overlap staff, and evaluate with a stepped-wedge rollout across teams.
- *Monitor.* Error rate and service time in the 30 minutes after vs before breaks; last-two-hour error rate by WIP band before and after; queue growth during break windows; share of breaks actually taken; reported fatigue.
- *Verdict.* Worth piloting under evaluation, combined with capacity cover.

**6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift**
- *Expected benefit.* Smooths the end-of-shift Quality Check correction burst and reduces last-45-minute pressure.
- *Risk.* It delays error detection, lets faulty outputs propagate (to customers, decisions and payments), and shifts load onto the next shift. The Early shift already clears the night backlog, and the Late shift already inherits the day backlog, which raises start-of-shift complexity and queue. It must never mean closing cases before Quality Check or silently dropping checks. Removing quality checks to improve throughput is not acceptable.
- *Better variant.* *Distribute* Quality Check continuously through the shift (e.g., a check after every N cases, or a dedicated Quality Check slot mid-shift), prioritized by risk (complexity, batched outputs, bot-classified cases produced under high WIP) rather than by priority label alone.
- *Monitor.* Quality Check waiting time; detection latency; count of cases closed without Quality Check (target zero); errors found downstream or by customers; next-shift first-hour queue and carryover share; Quality Check completion rate.
- *Verdict.* Reject plain deferral; adopt continuous, risk-based scheduling of Quality Check.

**7. Add an extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks**
- *Expected benefit.* Directly relieves the worst pressure window (Day queue rises to 75; last-two-hour errors 7.5%). It adds the most pressure-robust resource type (Seniors: 4.9% under high WIP vs 12.4% for juniors), allows complex cases to be routed away from juniors under load, and provides real-time review of batched junior outputs and bot corrections. It also reduces the backlog inherited by the Late shift.
- *Risk.* Cost. Seniors may be pulled from other valuable work. Seniors may end up taking the *simple* cases, which wastes the intervention. There is a dependency risk on a few people. The benefit may be misattributed if demand also changes.
- *Evaluation.* Difference-in-differences or stepped rollout across days or teams.
- *Monitor.* Queue and WIP from 14:00 to 17:00; share of time with WIP > 70; junior error rate under high WIP; complexity routed to the extra senior; the Late shift's starting queue; rework within 7 days for Day-shift cases.
- *Verdict.* Most promising capacity lever given the evidence.

**8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates**
- *Expected benefit.* None that survives scrutiny.
- *Risk.* Raw late-shift error rates mostly reflect conditions management and the system create: queue pressure, case mix, shift assignment, Quality Check timing, bot errors and detection-time attribution. Penalties would punish people for being rostered into the Day peak or for being junior. They would incentivize under-reporting of errors (degrading `error_flag` itself), avoidance of complex cases, slowdowns that worsen queues, and gaming of logging. They damage psychological safety and are likely unfair and legally problematic.
- *Better variant.* Risk-adjusted, system-level feedback; coaching and training for juniors under pressure; just-culture review of error clusters.
- *Monitor (if feedback of any kind is introduced).* Error-reporting rates and near-miss reports (a sudden drop signals suppression); complex-case refusal or deflection rates; staff turnover.
- *Verdict.* Reject.

**Summary.** The evidence favours **7** (extra senior capacity at peaks), **3** (WIP alert tied to an operational response), continuous risk-based Quality Check instead of **6**, a routing variant of **2**, and a piloted, staggered **5**. Use **4** only as a constrained evaluation design and **1** only as an overtime guardrail. Reject **8** outright.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias makes raw worker comparisons misleading.** Seniors choose complex cases early. Night staff receive urgent exceptions. Juniors may be given batchable Document Reviews. Workers in the Day shift face the demand peak. A worker's raw error rate is largely a function of *what* and *when* they were assigned. Even "within-worker" comparisons are biased if assignment responds to that worker's state (e.g., supervisors give easier cases to someone who looks tired). High sequence positions are reached only by some workers, so position-based comparisons carry survivorship bias. Night results rest on two identifiable individuals, so statistical noise and person-shift confounding are severe, and any reporting risks exposing them personally.
- **Measurement error is substantial and non-random.**
  - Bulk logging distorts service time, making batchers look fast and possibly careless.
  - Errors may be timestamped at detection (end-of-shift Quality Check), not at commission.
  - Errors may be attributed to the last human touching a case, even when the cause was upstream (rushed Document Review, bot misclassification).
  - The complexity score may itself be distorted under pressure.
  - Service time of carryover work may include off-shift pauses.
  - Rework within 7 days is censored near the end of the extract.
  - Error detection depends on Quality Check coverage, which varies by shift and time, so better-checked workers look worse.
- **Queue pressure is often created by management, not by workers.** Staffing levels, shift design (the Day shift ends while demand is still peaking; the Late shift inherits the backlog), deferring Quality Check to shift end, routing rules and throughput targets determine WIP. When speed-quality trade-offs appear under WIP > 70, the responsible unit of analysis is the *system design*. Attributing these errors to individual carelessness misplaces accountability.
- **Automation quality shapes human performance.** Bot_Risk's classifications appear to need more correction under high queue. Human error rates may partly be inherited bot errors, and correction work adds invisible load. The bot must be evaluated on its own error rate, including under surge conditions and input drift, rather than treated as ground truth against which humans are measured. Automation complacency and automation-induced workload are system properties.
- **Punitive use of error rates backfires.** It encourages under-reporting (the log becomes less truthful), cherry-picking of easy cases, refusal of complex or urgent work, defensive slowdowns and gaming of timestamps. It erodes the reporting culture that quality management depends on. Performance data should be used for learning and system improvement. Any individual-level review should rely on risk-adjusted metrics with uncertainty intervals, minimum sample sizes, context review and the worker's right to explain. It should never rest on raw late-shift error rates.
- **Surveillance and governance.** Event-level monitoring of named individuals is personal-data processing. It requires purpose limitation, data minimisation, transparency to staff, involvement of worker representatives, and compliance with data-protection and labour law. Prefer team-level and pseudonymized dashboards. Limit access to individual views, and document how and why each indicator is used.
- **Statistical humility.** With many resources, shifts and metrics, some workers will look bad by chance (multiple comparisons, regression to the mean). Correlations in aggregate tables do not identify mechanisms. Interventions should be piloted and evaluated before they are scaled.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

**Design principles**
- Every quality indicator is shown **risk-adjusted** (complexity, priority, activity family, carryover) and **stratified by WIP band and hour on shift**.
- The default level is team, shift or process. Individual views are restricted, pseudonymized and used only for coaching and fairness monitoring.
- Each tile shows sample size and a confidence interval.
- The data source is the event log, refreshed hourly for operations and weekly for analytics.
