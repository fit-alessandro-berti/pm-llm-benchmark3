# 2. Integrated explanation

Service time and error rates do not move together monotonically because multiple mechanisms operate simultaneously and change dominance across the work period.

At the start of a shift (positions 1–15) cases are systematically more complex and higher-priority. This arises from backlog carry-over (especially night-to-early and day-to-late handovers) and from preferential selection: seniors tend to claim or receive the harder cases early. Consequently mean service times are elevated (18.8 min) even though queues are already non-trivial. Error rates remain moderate (≈3.4 %) because workers are relatively fresh, seniors absorb much of the complexity, and deliberate pacing is still feasible.

In the middle window (positions 16–50) two processes run in parallel. The residual case mix becomes simpler (complexity falls toward 0.50, high-priority share declines) while individual resources warm up: the same workers process subsequent cases faster (seniors 15.9 → 11.8 min; mid-level and juniors show comparable proportional gains). The net result is a clear decline in service time to ≈12.9–13.6 min while error rates stay low or improve slightly (2.5–2.7 %). This phase therefore mixes genuine learning/warm-up with mechanical case-mix improvement; neither alone accounts for the full pattern.

Later (positions 51+) queue pressure becomes dominant. Demand rebuilds (especially on Day and Late shifts), WIP and queue length rise sharply (to 62–88), and workers respond with a classic speed–quality tradeoff: service times fall further (to 10.8–11.4 min) while error rates and subsequent rework climb steeply (4.6–7.9 % errors, 8.2–13.7 % rework). The effect is strongest for remaining high-complexity cases and for less-experienced resources (juniors reach 12.4 % under high WIP). Batching and workarounds amplify the picture: juniors form sequential short-service batches on Document Review, Quality Checks are deferred to the final 45 minutes producing correction bursts, and human overrides of Bot_Risk classifications increase when queues exceed 70. These behaviors further compress measured service times while elevating downstream error and rework flags.

Circadian and pure fatigue effects are present but secondary. Night staff exhibit higher baseline service times and somewhat elevated errors, and the last two hours of Day/Late shifts show higher error rates, yet the strongest gradients track sequence position jointly with WIP rather than absolute clock time. Fatigue likely contributes to the late error rise, especially for juniors after many events, but it cannot explain the continued shortening of service times (pure fatigue would typically lengthen them) nor the tight coupling with queue length.

Thus the overall pattern—falling service times throughout, with errors first stable then rising—is produced by the sequential dominance of case-mix + warm-up early, followed by queue-pressure-driven speed–quality tradeoffs (plus batching and automation side-effects) later. Learning is real but limited; fatigue and circadian factors modulate vulnerability; selection and pressure are the largest movers of the observed aggregates. Any explanation that attributes the entire trajectory to a single mechanism is inconsistent with the joint movement of complexity, WIP, resource-experience differentials and activity-specific batching signals.

# 4. Intervention options

1. **Strictly cap shift length for all workers**  
   Benefit: truncates exposure to late-sequence high-WIP conditions and cumulative time-on-task, potentially lowering the sharp error/rework rise after position 50–75.  
   Risk: increases handovers, may leave residual queue at artificial ends, reduces capacity during demand peaks (especially Day), can simply shift the pressure spike earlier.  
   Monitor: sequence_position distribution, max minutes_since_shift_start, error/rework rates in final hours, queue_length and complexity at shift boundaries, overall throughput and waiting times.

2. **Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift**  
   Benefit: allows resources to warm up on lower-complexity work, reducing elevated early service times driven by case-mix; may protect juniors.  
   Risk: high-complexity/high-priority cases age in queue, increasing customer waiting times; seniors currently absorbing them may be the safer allocation; backlog of complex work can create later spikes.  
   Monitor: mean case_complexity_score and high-priority share by sequence bin (especially first hour); waiting times for high-complexity cases; error rates stratified by complexity and position; resource assignment probabilities.

3. **Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70**  
   Benefit: makes the strongest risk zone visible in real time; enables temporary staffing, prioritization or slowdown of non-urgent work before error rates climb (especially juniors and high-complexity).  
   Risk: alert fatigue if thresholds poorly tuned; may induce gaming (delaying event starts); does not add capacity by itself.  
   Monitor: frequency of alerts; error rate and service time in the 30–60 min after alerts versus matched high-WIP periods without response; WIP trajectory post-alert; Bot correction rates; downstream rework.

4. **Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias**  
   Benefit: eliminates preferential loading of complex cases onto seniors early and residual easy cases later; produces cleaner within-resource estimates and fairer load distribution.  
   Risk: may slow overall throughput if juniors receive complex cases they handle less efficiently; loss of expertise matching; possible increase in early errors or specialist referrals.  
   Monitor: complexity and priority distributions by resource_experience_months and sequence_position; service-time and error differentials between experience groups; overall cycle time and rework; assignment entropy / fairness metrics.

5. **Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours**  
   Benefit: interrupts continuous high-WIP work, potentially mitigating residual fatigue contribution to the late error rise.  
   Risk: temporary queue growth during breaks; workers may rush before/after; scheduling friction across overlapping shifts; reduces capacity exactly when queues rebuild.  
   Monitor: residualized error and rework rates in final two hours; service-time variance around break windows; queue_length recovery; compliance (gaps in resource event streams); total throughput.

6. **Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift**  
   Benefit: removes the end-of-shift QC burst that currently compresses last-45-min capacity; may smooth late-shift service times.  
   Risk: delays detection of errors, allowing defective cases to progress further and increasing downstream rework cost or customer impact; shifts burden to the following shift. Does not improve quality, only timing.  
   Monitor: timing distribution of Quality Check relative to shift end; lag between potential error events and QC; rework_flag rates and case cycle times for deferred versus non-deferred; queue and complexity at next-shift start. (Do not treat pure throughput gain as success.)

7. **Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks**  
   Benefit: directly addresses the Day-shift pattern of rising queue, high late errors (7.5 %) and the experience × pressure interaction; seniors remain relatively stable under high WIP (error only to 4.9 %).  
   Risk: cost; possible under-utilization if peaks are mis-predicted; new handover bottlenecks.  
   Monitor: Day-shift WIP/queue trajectories and last-two-hours error rates; share of high-complexity cases handled by seniors; overall Day-shift rework and service times; utilization of the added senior.

8. **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates**  
   Benefit: none that is robust; raw late-shift error rates are heavily confounded by queue pressure, residual case mix, experience and assignment.  
   Risk: high. Punishes juniors and those assigned high-WIP periods disproportionately; incentivizes avoidance of complex or late work, under-reporting or further rushing; damages trust and may increase turnover.  
   Monitor: Do not implement. Any quality metric used for feedback must be residualized on WIP, complexity, priority, sequence position and experience; track assignment fairness and gaming indicators instead. Prefer system-level interventions (3, 7).

Priority order by expected value versus risk: 3 and 7 first, then carefully designed versions of 1/5/6 with strong monitoring, 2 and 4 only after testing the value of current selection, 8 avoid entirely.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions drawn from raw sequence-position or late-shift error rates are frequently unfair and misleading.

Selection bias is pervasive: seniors systematically receive or claim higher-complexity and high-priority cases early, especially after night or weekend backlogs. Juniors therefore face a different mix and, later, a higher share of residual pressure. Comparing raw error rates across individuals or across early versus late periods without conditioning on case_complexity_score, priority and the assignment process confounds skill with case mix.

Measurement error and lag further complicate attribution. error_flag and rework_flag (within 7 days) are imperfect and delayed; some errors surface only at Quality Check or downstream. Deferred QC further decouples the timestamp of the original work from the recorded error. High-WIP periods may also change detection probability.

Queue pressure is largely management-created. Late-shift WIP spikes (especially Day shift rising from 38 to 75) reflect arrival patterns, staffing levels and shift-overlap design. Holding individuals accountable for error rates that rise under system-generated load is operationally incoherent.

Automation quality interacts with load: Bot_Risk classifications require more human correction precisely when queue_length > 70. Workers absorbing those corrections under pressure appear to have higher error rates that partly originate in the automation layer.

Experience and role differences matter. Juniors show larger degradation under high WIP (12.4 %) than seniors (4.9 %). Night staff face different case urgency and circadian load. Uniform benchmarks ignore these structural differences.

Punitive use of raw late-shift error rates creates perverse incentives: avoidance of high-WIP periods, selective acceptance of easy cases, under-recording of problems, or excessive caution that harms throughput. It also risks demoralizing the workers (juniors, night staff) who already operate under harder conditions.

Any monitoring or intervention system must therefore emphasize system-level indicators (WIP trajectories, complexity-adjusted residuals, batching prevalence, handover volumes, fairness of case assignment) over individual raw rates. Feedback, if given, should be residualized and framed as process-improvement support rather than personal fault. Interventions that add capacity or reduce pressure are preferable to those that merely reallocate or punish.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

A process-mining dashboard should surface the interacting mechanisms rather than single aggregates. All indicators must be filterable by shift, resource-experience group, activity_family and time window.

1. Mean / median service time (and complexity-adjusted residual service time) by sequence_position bin and by minutes_since_shift_start, further stratified by experience group.
2. Waiting time (case-level and activity-level) from insert_case_service_waiting_time; track queue aging and high-complexity waiting times.
3. WIP at start / queue_length_at_start distribution and percentage of events occurring under WIP > 70 (pressure-exposure rate); trajectories from shift start to end by shift type.
4. Error rate (error_flag) and rework rate (rework_flag or get_rework_cases_per_activity) stratified by WIP bin × complexity band × experience group.
5. Case-complexity mix: mean score and high-complexity / high-priority share by sequence bin, by first hour of shift, and by resource group.
6. Batching indicators: share of events belonging to discovered batches (pm4py.discover_batches), mean run length of consecutive same-resource short-service events, especially for Document Review and Quality Check near shift end.
7. Handover / queue-ownership changes: volume and complexity of cases carried across shift boundaries; mean queue at shift start versus end by shift type.
8. Worker-load fairness: distribution of high-complexity cases and high-WIP exposure across experience groups (Gini or simple share ratios); cumulative events per resource per shift.
9. Automation health: Bot_Risk correction / override rate by queue_length bin; downstream error rate after Bot-assisted versus pure-human Risk Classification.
10. Speed–quality frontier: binned plot of service time versus error rate within complexity strata, colored by WIP; alert when operating in the high-WIP high-error region.
11. Downstream impact: fraction of cases with rework within 7 days, linked back to originating activity, WIP at that activity, and whether an error_flag was raised.
12. Circadian / shift overlay: error and service curves by clock hour with sequence-position and WIP controls visible.

Visuals: dotted charts colored by error_flag or complexity; events-distribution graphs by minutes_since_shift_start; performance DFGs conditioned on WIP > 70 versus < 30; trend lines with confidence bands for residual metrics.

Alerts: sustained WIP > 70 for > 30 min; junior error rate under high WIP exceeds residualized threshold; QC burst volume in last 45 min; first-hour complexity share > 45 %; Bot correction rate > baseline + 2 SD.

The dashboard enables continuous monitoring of whether interventions improve residual (pressure- and complexity-adjusted) performance rather than merely shifting case mix or gaming raw throughput.
