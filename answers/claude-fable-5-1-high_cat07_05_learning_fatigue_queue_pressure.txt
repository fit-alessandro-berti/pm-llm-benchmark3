# 1. Competing mechanism table

Before the table, three facts from the aggregates anchor everything that follows:

- **Service time falls monotonically** across the shift (18.8 → 10.8 min), but **error rate is U-shaped** (3.4% → 2.5% at positions 16–30 → 7.9% at 76+). Any single mechanism that predicts a monotone trend in both is already falsified.
- **Complexity, priority share and queue length are not constant across positions.** Complexity falls 0.72 → 0.43, high-priority share 41% → 19%, while queue length is itself U-shaped (46 → 31 → 88). The position curve is therefore a composition curve, not a clean time-on-task curve.
- **In the experience table, the error rate of late events under low WIP is essentially equal to the error rate of the first 20 events** for every group (Seniors 2.2% vs 2.1%; Mid 2.9% vs 2.8%; Juniors 4.4% vs 5.1%; Night 3.7% vs 3.6%). Late-shift error excess appears only when WIP is high. That is the single most discriminating observation in the data.

# 2. Integrated explanation

The pattern is best read as three overlapping regimes within a shift, each driven by a different dominant mechanism, on top of a stable experience gradient and a set of measurement artefacts.

**Start of shift (positions 1–15, roughly the first 75 minutes).** Workers inherit the previous shift's backlog. That backlog is not a random sample of work: the previous shift, working under end-of-shift pressure, tended to clear easy cases and leave complex, high-priority, exception-laden cases behind (complexity 0.72, 41% high priority, 48–56% high complexity in the first hour of Early and Night). Service time is long mainly because the cases are hard, not because workers are slow. Error is somewhat elevated (3.4%) for the same reason, plus the information loss inherent in handing over half-processed cases. Seniors self-select into these complex carry-overs, which keeps the early error rate from being worse than it is and simultaneously makes seniors look slower than they are in raw statistics. A small genuine warm-up effect may exist, but the data cannot separate it from the complexity gradient, and its similarity across experience levels argues against a skill-based interpretation.

**Middle of shift (positions 16–50, roughly hours 2–5).** Complexity and priority are declining, queue length is at its minimum (31–33), and error reaches its floor (2.5–2.7%). This is the regime in which workers are operating at their "true" capability: enough time per case, no backlog pressure, ordinary case mix. Service time continues to fall, but that is mostly the easier case mix, with at most a modest contribution from settling into the work.

**End of shift (positions 51+, hours 6–8).** Two things happen at once and they must not be conflated. Case mix continues to get easier (0.46 → 0.43), which would by itself lower both service time and error. But the queue nearly triples (31 → 88) because demand peaks late in the Day shift and inherited backlog persists in the Late shift. Under high WIP, workers shift along the speed–accuracy frontier: at fixed complexity they are 25–30% faster and two to three times more error-prone. The response is multiplicative with complexity — a complex case processed at high WIP has a 10.9% error rate — and strongly moderated by experience: seniors give up little accuracy, juniors give up a lot. Workers also adapt through workarounds: Junior_H and Junior_I batch Document Reviews; Quality Check is deferred to the last 45 minutes and then done in a hurry; easier cases are cherry-picked, which is why the 76+ bin has the lowest complexity and why the *next* shift inherits a complex backlog. The cherry-picking closes a loop: end-of-shift pressure manufactures the start-of-shift complexity that the "warm-up" story misreads as cold-start slowness.

**Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions.** Service time falls for four stacked reasons, all pointing in the same direction: easier case mix through the shift; pressure-induced speed-up; batching and end-of-shift correction bursts, which insert many very short events into the late bins; and a small warm-up component. Errors, by contrast, are driven by a condition, not by a clock: they rise when pressure is high, when the case is complex, and when the worker is inexperienced, with the effects compounding. When those conditions are absent — late events under low WIP, or the Early shift's final two hours after it has cleared the night backlog — late errors are indistinguishable from early errors. This is why the late-shift error excess is confined to the Day and Late shifts and to mid-level and junior staff, and why the last-two-hours error rate ranks across shifts exactly as the end-of-shift queue does.

**What fatigue and circadian rhythm contribute.** Neither is excluded, but neither is identified by these aggregates. If fatigue were the primary driver, late low-WIP events would be worse than early events; they are not. If circadian troughs were primary, the Early shift's post-lunch window would not be the safest late period in the data, and the Night shift's last hours would be worse than the Day shift's afternoon; the reverse is observed. The most defensible reading is that time-on-task may reduce a worker's *tolerance* for pressure — an interaction that would explain why early-shift high queues (Early 61, Late 70 at shift start) produce only 3.4% error while late-shift high queues produce 7–8% — but this must be tested with the overlapping-shift natural experiment rather than assumed.

**Measurement artefacts that inflate the late-shift picture.** Sequence position is an outcome, not just an exposure: fast processing under pressure and batching push workers into the 76+ bin, so that bin over-represents high-WIP Day shifts and batchers. Deferred Quality Check means errors are *detected* late even when they were *committed* earlier, and rushed end-of-shift QC may both flag more and miss more. Some flagged errors in Rework Correction originate from Bot_Risk output. Each of these must be measured and removed before the residual late-shift effect is interpreted as human deterioration.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The analytic strategy is to (a) compute clean event-level measures, (b) remove composition and artefact effects, and (c) estimate the remaining position, WIP, hour-of-day and experience effects jointly, within resources, with interactions.

## 3.1 Preparation and event-level measures

```python
import pandas as pd, numpy as np, pm4py

df = pd.read_csv("claims_events.csv")
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id="case:concept:name", activity_key="concept:name",
                            timestamp_key="time:timestamp", start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
df["start_timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"], utc=True)

# Event-level service time (minutes)
df["svc_min"] = (df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
df["hour"] = df["start_timestamp"].dt.hour
df["dow"] = df["start_timestamp"].dt.dayofweek
df["is_human"] = df["org:resource"] != "Bot_Risk"
df["exp_group"] = df["org:resource"].str.split("_").str[0]  # Senior / Mid / Junior / Night / Bot

# Case-level service, waiting and sojourn time; arrival and finish rates
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df, service_time_column="case_service",
        sojourn_time_column="case_sojourn", waiting_time_column="case_waiting",
        start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp", timestamp_key="time:timestamp")
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df, arrival_rate_column="arr_rate",
        finish_rate_column="fin_rate", start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp")

# Hourly temporal features (events, cases, resources active) to characterise load per hour
temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df, grouper_freq="H",
        start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp", timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
        resource_key="org:resource", case_id_key="case:concept:name")

# Position bins and WIP bands matching the management tables
df["pos_bin"] = pd.cut(df["sequence_position_within_shift"], [0,5,15,30,50,75,np.inf],
                       labels=["1-5","6-15","16-30","31-50","51-75","76+"])
df["wip_band"] = pd.cut(df["wip_at_start"], [-1,29,70,np.inf], labels=["low","med","high"])
df["cx_band"] = pd.qcut(df["case_complexity_score"], 5, labels=False)  # deciles/quintiles, finer than 2 bands

# Carry-over flag: case had events in a previous shift
first_shift = df.sort_values("start_timestamp").groupby("case:concept:name")["shift_id"].first()
df["carried_over"] = df["shift_id"] != df["case:concept:name"].map(first_shift)
```

## 3.2 Batching detection (do this first, because it contaminates everything else)

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(df, merge_distance=15*60, min_batch_size=6,
        activity_key="concept:name", resource_key="org:resource",
        start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp", timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
        case_id_key="case:concept:name")
# batches: list of ((activity, resource), n_events, {batch_type: [(start, end, events), ...]})

batched_idx = set()
for (act, res), n, by_type in batches:
    for btype, intervals in by_type.items():
        for start, end, events in intervals:
            for ev in events:
                batched_idx.add(ev)          # event identifiers/timestamps per pm4py output
df["in_batch"] = df.index.isin(batched_idx)   # adapt keying to the returned identifiers

# Visual confirmation: bursts by resource, end-of-shift QC/rework spikes
pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(df, "dotted_by_resource.png", attribute="org:resource")
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(df, "events_by_hour.png", distr_type="hours")
qc = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "concept:name", ["Quality Check", "Rework Correction"],
                                         level="event", retain=True)
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(qc, "qc_rework_by_hour.png", distr_type="hours")
```

Report: share of events in batches by resource, shift and position bin; mean service time and error rate with and without batched events; QC latency (time from the checked activity to its Quality Check) by shift. Re-run every later analysis on `df[~df.in_batch]` and on the full data.

## 3.3 Within-shift position effects, adjusted for composition

```python
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

hum = df[df.is_human & (df["concept:name"] != "Rework Correction")].copy()
hum["log_svc"] = np.log(hum["svc_min"].clip(lower=0.1))
hum["resource"] = hum["org:resource"]

# Service time: resource fixed effects, bins for position/WIP, hour and shift dummies,
# complexity & priority controls, batching and carry-over flags
m_svc = smf.ols("log_svc ~ C(pos_bin) + C(wip_band) + case_complexity_score + C(priority) "
                "+ C(activity_family) + C(hour) + C(shift_id) + carried_over + in_batch "
                "+ C(resource)", data=hum).fit(cov_type="cluster",
                cov_kwds={"groups": hum["resource"] + "_" + hum["shift_id"].astype(str)})
raw_curve = hum.groupby("pos_bin")["svc_min"].mean()
adj_curve = m_svc.params.filter(like="pos_bin")      # position effect net of composition
```

Compare the raw position coefficients (18.8 → 10.8 min) with the adjusted ones. Whatever position effect survives complexity, priority, WIP, hour and batching controls is the upper bound on warm-up plus fatigue.

## 3.4 WIP/queue stratification and the speed–accuracy trade-off

```python
# Error model with interactions; cluster SE by resource-shift
m_err = smf.logit("error_flag ~ wip_at_start * case_complexity_score + wip_at_start * C(exp_group) "
                  "+ minutes_since_shift_start * wip_at_start + C(pos_bin) + C(hour) + C(shift_id) "
                  "+ C(priority) + C(activity_family) + carried_over + in_batch",
                  data=hum).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": hum["resource"] + hum["shift_id"].astype(str)})

# Non-parametric check: 3-way table early-vs-late × WIP band × complexity quintile
tab = hum.assign(late=hum.sequence_position_within_shift > 50) \
         .groupby(["late","wip_band","cx_band"]).agg(err=("error_flag","mean"),
                                                     svc=("svc_min","mean"), n=("error_flag","size"))
```

Key contrasts: early high-WIP (Early/Late shift starts) vs late high-WIP at matched complexity — a positive `minutes_since_shift_start × wip_at_start` coefficient supports a fatigue × pressure interaction; a null supports pure pressure. Also distinguish static backlog from rising intake using `arr_rate`/`fin_rate` — pressure from a growing queue may behave differently from a large but shrinking one.

Early complex cases are handled by seniors at low pressure with only mildly elevated error (3.4%); moving them later pushes them toward the high-WIP zone where complex-case error is 10.9%. The real early-shift issue is handover information loss and the previous shift's cherry-picking. | Complex/high-priority cases age; the constraint "do not ignore that early cases are complex" is about attribution, not about relocation. Better variant: protect complex cases from *pressure* whenever they are worked, and stop the end-of-shift cherry-picking that creates the complex carry-over. | Complexity by position bin; waiting time of high-complexity/high-priority cases; error of high-complexity cases by WIP band and by position; carried_over flag error vs fresh-case error at same complexity. |
| 3 | Queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70 | Targets the dominant mechanism. Effective if the alert triggers *actions* (re-route to available seniors, stop batching, pause bot auto-acceptance, protect QC) rather than just informing workers. Threshold should probably be lower: error already rises at medium WIP (30–70). | An alert with no capacity response increases felt pressure and may worsen the speed–accuracy shift and cherry-picking; alert fatigue if it is on most of the Day afternoon. | Minutes per shift with WIP > 70 (and > 50); error and service time within alert windows vs matched non-alert windows; complexity of completed work during alerts (cherry-picking); bot-correction rate during alerts. |
| 4 | Force random assignment of cases | Analytically valuable: it breaks the senior→complex selection and makes worker comparisons fair. Operationally it wastes senior skill and exposes juniors to complex cases under pressure (12.4% error zone). | Higher junior error and rework, more Specialist Referral, slower complex cases. Acceptable only as a limited pilot: randomise within complexity band, or randomise a small fraction, or randomise which senior rather than whether a senior. | Complexity distribution per resource (should converge under randomisation); junior error on high-complexity cases; referral and rework rates; case sojourn for high-priority cases. |
| 5 | Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours | Targets fatigue, which is weakly supported; breaks might still help by interrupting "rush mode". Uncertain. | Breaks reduce capacity during the demand peak, so queue grows and pressure rises unless coupled with added capacity; may produce the opposite of the intended effect. Run as a controlled pilot on some shifts/teams. | Break detection via gaps in resource event sequences; error and service time in the 30 min after a break vs before, at matched WIP; queue at shift end and next-shift start queue for pilot vs control shifts. |
| 6 | Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift | Superficially raises throughput and lowers the *measured* in-shift error rate. Rejected as framed: it removes or delays the detection mechanism, shifts work onto a shift that already starts with complex carry-over, and converts visible errors into 7-day rework and customer-facing failures. | Longer detection lag, more downstream rework, distorted error statistics, violation of the constraint against removing QC for throughput. Acceptable alternative: spread QC through the shift (end the end-of-shift burst), and use risk-based QC intensity rather than deferral. | QC latency (activity → QC); share of errors found in-shift vs via rework; 7-day rework rate; next-shift start queue and its complexity; QC service time (very short QC under pressure signals rubber-stamping). |
| 7 | Extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks | Directly addresses the mechanism: more capacity at the peak lowers WIP, and a senior can take complex cases or review junior work, reducing the compounding of pressure × complexity × inexperience. Also reduces the backlog inherited by the Late shift (70 at start). | Cost; added capacity absorbed by intake growth; the senior becoming a review bottleneck (handover delays); seniors' own capacity elsewhere. | Day shift queue at end (baseline 75) and Late shift queue at start (70); minutes of WIP > 70; junior error under high WIP; 15:00–17:00 error rate (7.5%); complexity of completed work in late Day (should rise if cherry-picking stops). |
| 8 | Penalise workers with higher late-shift error rates | None. Raw late-shift error rates are dominated by assignment (who gets complex cases), WIP (a management-controlled state), shift (Day/Late vs Early), batching, bot corrections, and detection timing. Punishment would target juniors and Day/Late staff for conditions they do not control. | Goodhart effects: hiding errors, avoiding complex cases, rubber-stamping QC, gaming rework flags, attrition, legal/labour exposure. Directly violates the constraint against punishing on raw late-shift error. | If worker-level review is ever done: use adjusted rates (within-resource, matched WIP and complexity) with confidence intervals, and only as a development conversation, never as a sanction trigger. Monitor error-reporting rates and rework logging for signs of suppression. |

Overall ranking: 7 and a well-designed 3 (alert coupled with capacity/re-routing actions) address the identified mechanism; 6-as-spread-QC and a limited pilot of 4 and 5 are worth testing; 1 and 2 are low-value; 8 is rejected.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Selection bias makes worker-level rankings unfair.** Seniors volunteer for complex carry-overs, so their raw service time and error rates are inflated relative to their skill; juniors who batch Document Reviews look fast and, in some periods, accurate. Night staff face 56% high-complexity work and urgent exceptions with two people. Any league table that does not condition on what each worker was given will reward the people who received easy work and penalise those who took hard work. The high-WIP high-complexity cell holds only 1800 events spread over many workers; individual estimates there are very noisy.

**Measurement error is systematic, not random.** `error_flag` depends on detection: Quality Check is deferred to the last 45 minutes, so errors made at any time surface late, and QC done in a hurry both flags and misses differently from QC done calmly. `rework_flag` within 7 days conflates the original worker's error, downstream misinterpretation, bot misclassification, and customer-driven changes. `sequence_position_within_shift` is partly a consequence of speed and batching, not an independent exposure. `queue_length_at_start` may be defined per pool and appears inconsistent across overlapping shifts (Early end 42 at 14:00, Late start 70 at 14:00), so any threshold-based rule inherits that ambiguity. Complexity bands are coarse and leave residual confounding (high-complexity cases done under high WIP are the easier ones).

**Queue pressure is created by management decisions.** WIP is a system state produced by staffing levels, shift design (Day demand peaks with no additional capacity), intake policies, handover practices, and the decision to run QC as an end-of-shift burst. Attributing the resulting error to individual workers inverts responsibility. The same worker is accurate at low WIP and error-prone at high WIP; that is information about the system, not the person.

**Automation quality must be assessed separately.** Corrections of Bot_Risk output are human events, and any error or rework arising from a wrong bot classification can land on the human who touched the case next. Higher correction frequency at high queue may indicate the bot performs worse on peak case mix, or that rushed humans over-correct, or that calm humans under-detect; without an audit of correction correctness, neither the bot nor the humans should be blamed. Automation also changes what "service time" means: a human "finishing" a Risk Classification in 30 seconds may be a rubber stamp.

**Punitive use of error rates corrupts the data and the process.** Once error rates carry consequences, workers stop logging rework, avoid complex or high-priority cases, and rubber-stamp QC, which removes the very signal management needs. The constraint against punishing raw late-shift error is not only ethical; it is a data-quality requirement. Individual-level monitoring should be aggregated to roles and shifts by default, with individual views used only for supportive purposes with the worker's knowledge, with confidence intervals shown, and with adjusted rather than raw measures. Involve workers in interpreting batching and workaround patterns: they usually reflect rational adaptation to load, and they know why.

**Operationally**, avoid interventions that reduce capacity at peak (caps, breaks without cover) or remove detection (deferred QC), and avoid one-variable fixes: the loop between end-of-shift cherry-picking and next-shift complex carry-over means a change in one shift's behaviour shows up in another shift's statistics.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

All indicators are computed from the event log, stratified by shift, hour of day, WIP band, complexity quintile and resource group (individual resource views restricted and adjusted). Each indicator has a raw and an adjusted version (adjusted = residual from the model in section 3.3/3.4 with complexity, priority, activity family, WIP, hour and resource fixed effects).

| # | Indicator | Definition / computation | Display and stratification | Alert or interpretation rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Service time (event and case)** | Event: `time:timestamp − start_timestamp`; case: `insert_case_service_waiting_time` (`case_service`). Raw and complexity/WIP-adjusted. | Median and P90 by activity, position bin, WIP band, shift; trend per week. | Adjusted event service time falling > 20% below the low-WIP baseline at the same complexity flags speed–accuracy risk, not efficiency. |
| 2 | **Waiting and sojourn time** | `case_waiting`, `case_sojourn` from `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; waiting time by priority and complexity. | Distribution by priority; ageing curve of high-complexity cases; waiting time of carried-over cases. | High-complexity or high-priority waiting time growing while low-complexity waiting time shrinks indicates cherry-picking. |

Governance rules for the dashboard: default views are at role/shift level; individual views require adjusted metrics with intervals, minimum sample sizes, and are used for support and scheduling, not sanctions; every error metric is shown next to its detection-lag and QC-burst indicators so that changes in detection are not misread as changes in quality; and every late-shift metric is shown next to WIP so that pressure created by scheduling is visible as such.
