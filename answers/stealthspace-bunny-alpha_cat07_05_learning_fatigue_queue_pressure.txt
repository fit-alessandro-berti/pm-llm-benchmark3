## Key interpretive cautions

The strongest evidence is for a **conditional speed–quality trade-off**, not a universal fatigue curve. Falling case complexity explains some early-to-middle improvement. Later, however, complexity continues to fall while errors and rework increase alongside pressure.

At the same time, the data do not establish that pressure alone causes the deterioration. Learning, resource experience, batching, automation follow-up, detection delays, and unmeasured case characteristics could all contribute.

# 2. Integrated explanation

### Early positions: difficult inherited work, followed by favorable selection

The first events are not a clean baseline. They have the highest complexity, priority share, queue length, and rework rate. Early shift staff are also clearing Night carryover, and experienced staff may deliberately take the difficult inherited cases.

Three processes can therefore produce the initial improvement:

1. **Staff become familiar with the work and systems.**
2. **Backlog and case difficulty decline.**
3. **Resources are assigned progressively easier work.**

The error rate reaches its minimum around positions 16–30, when complexity and queues are relatively favorable. That pattern is consistent with warm-up, but it is not an individual learning curve: the aggregate mixes resources, activities, shifts, and case types.

### Middle positions: apparently efficient, lower-complexity work

By positions 31–50, mean service time is 12.9 minutes, complexity is 0.50, and queue length is 31. The modest error increase from 2.5% to 2.7% is small enough that sampling variation or unobserved differences could matter.

This is the point at which favorable selection, learning, and manageable pressure may coexist. It should not be labeled a stable “good performance” regime without separating activity and case composition.

### Late positions: lower complexity does not mean lower risk

Late positions have the lowest average complexity, yet their error and rework rates are highest. Mean service time falls to 10.8 minutes while errors reach 7.9% and seven-day rework reaches 13.7%.

The WIP/complexity table reinforces this conditional pattern:

- Low-complexity cases: service falls from 12.7 to 9.4 minutes, while errors rise from 2.1% to 5.8%.
- High-complexity cases: service falls from 22.4 to 16.8 minutes, while errors rise from 4.2% to 10.9%.

Thus, later work is not merely “harder.” Even within the same coarse complexity band, pressure is associated with faster handling and worse outcomes.

A shorter event time need not mean greater productivity. It may reflect:

- More narrowly defined work or fewer nonessential steps.
- Less checking or documentation.
- Deadline-driven prioritization.
- Batching that moves work into later bursts.
- Greater specialization in simpler activities.
- Case closure or recording conventions that omit downstream work.

The complete-case duration, waiting time, and rework must therefore accompany event service time.

### Why the speed–quality trade-off is conditional, not universal

The resource table argues against a simple “everyone gets worse late in the shift” explanation. Under low WIP, later error rates are approximately stable for Seniors, Mid-level staff, and Night staff. Juniors actually improve from 5.1% early to 4.4% later under low WIP, although their initial case allocation must be accounted for.

Under high WIP, however, later error rates rise for every group. This is more consistent with a **pressure-dependent change in behavior or work execution**, interacting with experience and case difficulty, than with elapsed time alone.

The experience table is also descriptive. Seniors may be faster because of expertise, easier allocations, activity mix, or selective handling—not necessarily because a given Senior learned during a shift. The comparison does not separate experience from resource identity or assignment practices.

### Shift patterns introduce backlog, demand, and circadian hypotheses

Early shift clears a large, complex inherited queue and has comparatively favorable late error rates. Day’s queue grows from 38 to 75, with a 7.5% last-two-hour error rate. Late inherits that demand. Night has fewer cases but a 56% first-hour high-complexity share, long service times, and urgent exceptions.

These patterns are compatible with circadian vulnerability, but they are also compatible with:

- Different demand by clock time.
- Inherited backlog and handoffs.
- Night-specific case selection.
- Different staffing and supervision.
- Different opportunity to batch or defer work.
- Small-sample uncertainty.

The pooled sequence pattern could therefore partly reflect **which shifts contribute most to the late-position events**, not simply how individual workers change over time. A position near 459 minutes in Early shift is not the same physiological state as a Night worker late in a shift after several previous duty periods.

### Overall interpretation

A useful causal representation is:

> Incoming demand, backlog, staffing, and routing determine case mix, WIP, and assignment. Experience, repetition, and elapsed work time affect execution. Pressure, batching, and automation alter how that work is performed and recorded. Errors then generate rework, additional handling, and potentially additional queue pressure.

The current summaries cannot assign a numerical share of the observed change to each mechanism. In particular, fatigue remains plausible but is not demonstrated, while the WIP-stratified results make pressure an especially important candidate.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Data preparation and outcome definitions

Use the **event-level log**, not the aggregated tables, for the requested comparisons. First establish:

- Whether `time` is completion time and `start_timestamp` is handling start.
- Whether service time means `time - start_timestamp` or another convention.
- Whether `error_flag` identifies an error occurrence or its detection.
- Whether `rework_flag` is an event flag or a case-level seven-day outcome.
- Whether each case contributes multiple errors, corrections, and closures.
- Whether all cases have complete seven-day follow-up.
- Whether “cases/events” in the WIP table means cases, events, or mixed units.

Use the shift’s start timestamp to assign the business day across midnight. Validate timezone handling, duplicate events, missing timestamps, missing resources, and missingness in error and rework labels.

Estimate these outcomes separately:

1. **Event service time**, preferably with a lognormal, Gamma, or generalized model.
2. **Error occurrence**, using a binary or event-history model.
3. **Seven-day rework**, using a case-level or time-to-rework model with censoring.

Do not reconstruct service time from gaps between activity completions unless unobserved work and overlapping work can be ruled out.

## Comparison and model design

| Required analysis | Design and interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Within-resource comparisons** | Compare each resource across early and later events, holding activity, complexity, priority, and WIP as constant as possible. Exact or nearest-neighbor matching within resource and activity can make early–late comparisons more credible. Also compare the same case handled by different resources. Repeated events within a case are not independent observations. |
| **Within-shift position effects** | Estimate service and error curves separately by shift using sequence position and actual minutes since shift start. Use splines rather than assuming a linear learning or fatigue curve. Distinguish sequence/repetition from elapsed shift time, cumulative service minutes, and time since awakening. The first 20 events versus later events is a useful summary, but not the only analysis. |
| **WIP/queue stratification** | Compare low, medium, and high WIP at the same activity and complexity band, while controlling for resource group, shift, hour, priority, and date. Also examine continuous and lagged WIP: a threshold of 70 should not be assumed to be a causal breakpoint. WIP is endogenous to staffing and demand, so its coefficient is not automatically a causal effect. |
| **Complexity adjustment** | Model complexity continuously and nonlinearly, by activity and priority, rather than only using broad bands. Include new versus carryover cases, customer or policy type if available, and indicators for unusual exceptions. Test WIP-by-complexity and elapsed-time-by-WIP interactions. |
| **Shift and time-of-day controls** | Use shift, date, calendar hour, weekday/weekend, start-of-shift queue, arrivals, and inherited backlog. Compare elapsed shift time at the same clock time across dates. Shift identifiers and clock hour are not interchangeable. Where rostering allows, estimate worker-specific time-of-day patterns. |
| **Batching detection** | Detect candidate batches and compare them with non-batched events within the same resource, activity, shift, and day. Examine batch size, service duration, gaps, and downstream rework. Quality Check bursts should be analyzed both as possible batching and as possible **delayed error detection**. |
| **Resource-experience interactions** | Model experience months where informative, but recognize that experience is resource-specific and not randomly assigned. Test experience-by-position and experience-by-WIP interactions. The group summary cannot separate tenure from resource identity, assignment, shift, or activity. |
| **Rework/error downstream analysis** | Determine whether errors precede rework, whether rework cases consume additional service, and whether pressure changes that relationship. Analyze cases that reach an activity, not all completed cases indiscriminately. Distinguish error occurrence from later detection, and count repeated defects per case appropriately. |

Use mixed-effects or generalized additive models, for example:

```text
log(service time) ~
    activity
  + complexity
  + priority
  + log1p(WIP)
  + complexity × log1p(WIP)
  + elapsed-time spline
  + sequence-position spline
  + elapsed-time × log1p(WIP)
  + experience × sequence-position
  + shift/date/time-of-day controls
  + random case, resource, and resource-day effects
```

Use a logistic mixed model for error occurrence and a survival model for rework within seven days. Include random slopes only where supported by the number of resources and repeated observations. Report uncertainty intervals and resource/date effects; do not rely on very small Night subgroups.

Pre-specify the estimand. A model without WIP describes the total elapsed-time association; adding WIP asks a different question about the association holding pressure fixed. A change in coefficients after adjustment is **not itself a decomposition into causes**.

batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)
# Inspect the returned batch identifiers and merge them into d.
# Verify batching against consecutive events, resource, activity,
# service duration, and gaps. Same queue is only a proxy unless
# a queue-entry or batch identifier is actually recorded.

# 5. Fit inferential models outside PM4Py.
service_model = fit_mixed_lognormal_model(

For each resource and shift, produce matched summaries by activity, position, WIP, complexity, and priority. Also test whether later-position effects remain after adjustment, and plot **predicted service and error curves at common WIP and complexity values**. # 5. Ethical and operational cautions

## Selection bias can make a worker appear unsafe or ineffective

Juniors may receive more difficult cases, Night staff may receive more urgent exceptions, and Seniors may deliberately take complex inherited work early. A raw error rate therefore measures a worker **plus their assigned portfolio and operating context**.

Case-mix adjustment helps but cannot remove unmeasured differences, such as severity, customer vulnerability, policy exceptions, or case novelty. Worker-level conclusions require comparable work opportunities, not merely a lower pooled average.

## Queue pressure may be created by management

A worker cannot be held responsible for errors caused by assignment ratios, insufficient coverage, unrealistic deadlines, or upstream delays. WIP is not an exogenous personal characteristic. Managers should distinguish controllable individual behavior from system-generated pressure before drawing conclusions.

## Measurement error can change the apparent pattern

Event durations, waiting times, and errors require consistent definitions:

- Bot_Risk’s instant processing is not comparable to human handling.
- A short activity may omit review, documentation, or handoff work.
- A long event may contain pauses or waiting that are not active service.
- Case-level duration cannot be reconstructed safely from event gaps when work overlaps.
- Errors detected at delayed Quality Check may have originated earlier.
- A seven-day rework flag is not equivalent to a verified corrective event.
- Incomplete follow-up can make a late case appear to have no rework.
- Filtering to completed or resolved cases can introduce survivor bias.

A high late-position error rate can partly reflect **when errors are detected**, not only when they were created. Error origin and detection timestamps would be valuable additions.

## Fatigue and circadian effects require data that are absent

Elapsed shift time is not a direct measure of fatigue, and Night shift is not a direct measure of circadian phase. Hours since awakening, previous duty periods, rest breaks, sleep timing, and actual active work are needed. Without these, fatigue remains a plausible competing explanation rather than an established conclusion.

## Automation quality must be assessed explicitly

Instant processing does not establish classification accuracy. Human override frequency is also not a perfect error label: overrides can represent justified human judgment, policy changes, or different risk tolerances.

Automation should be evaluated through confirmed same-case corrections, downstream impact, model/version context, and human correction workload. A bot may improve speed while worsening quality or transferring substantial work to people.

## Raw error rates must not become a punitive ranking system

Error labels can be affected by reporting behavior, supervision, case exposure, and whether checking was performed consistently. Using these labels for punishment can suppress reporting and disproportionately penalize workers assigned difficult or urgent work.

Worker-level reporting should use comparable case and workload adjustments, uncertainty intervals, minimum sample sizes, and a route to challenge the underlying record. Process ownership should not be replaced with individual blame. The appropriate questions are whether the system is assigning work fairly, providing sufficient capacity, detecting defects promptly, and helping workers resolve them safely.

Use `insert_case_service_waiting_time` for exploration, but validate its timestamp assumptions against observed start/end events. |
| **3. WIP and queue pressure** | Active WIP, pending queue length, arrivals versus available capacity, workload per available worker, and the share of time above the validated pressure threshold. | Resource role, activity, shift hour, and skill group. Display medians and upper quantiles over rolling intervals, not one instantaneous snapshot. Do not combine active work and waiting into an unexplained single number. |
| **4. Error rate** | Errors divided by eligible completed events or decisions, with a fixed definition of error occurrence and detection. | Activity, shift/time, complexity, priority, WIP, and resource group. Show counts and denominators. Separate confirmed errors from overrides and delayed detections. |
| **5. Seven-day rework rate** | Share of eligible cases with verified rework within seven days; separately show number of Rework Correction events. | Case complexity, activity of origin, priority, WIP, and shift. Exclude or flag immature follow-up windows. A case with multiple corrections must not become multiple independent case outcomes. |
| **6. Complete-case cycle time and quality** | First arrival to final valid closure, with rework and waiting visible separately; also show defects escaping beyond the original activity. | Original activity, priority, complexity, and whether the case was reworked. Do not substitute partial event duration for total case duration. |
| **7. Complexity and priority mix** | Mean and distribution of complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, new versus carryover cases, and unusual-case indicators. | Activity, resource assignment, shift, and sequence/time band. A stable quality target should not be achieved merely by routing difficult work elsewhere. |
| **8. Batching indicators** | Share of events in detected batches, median and upper-tail batch size, short-service proportion, between-event gaps, and share of Quality Check events in the last 45 minutes. | Resource, activity, shift, queue/batch identifier where available, and case complexity. Use `discover_batches` and temporal/event-distribution views. Validate that the batch definition is not simply a workload-dependent proxy. |
| **9. Handover and queue-ownership changes** | Number of assigned-resource changes, unresolved or reopened work at handoff, carryover age, queue ownership changes, and orphaned pending work. | Shift boundary, queue, priority, activity, and case complexity. The current `org` field is only a proxy for queue ownership; add explicit `case_owner` and `queue_entry_id` fields if possible. |
| **10. Worker-load fairness** | Distribution of active service minutes per available hour, caseload, WIP, consecutive work, high-complexity share, and comparable-case error outcomes. | Comparable skill groups, shift, day, and workload band. Show Gini or percentile ranges across a rolling period, not just averages. Do not target identical minutes across cases or create individual error rankings. |
| **11. Shift/time-of-day context** | Start/end queue, incoming rate, carryover, first-hour complexity, staffing, last-two-hour service and error, and hours since awakening if collected. | Actual clock hour and business date derived from shift start. Display Night uncertainty and do not interpret every clock-time difference as circadian. |
| **12. Automation quality and correction burden** | Risk Classification correction rate, correction delay, human correction time, and downstream rework for the same case. | Bot version, case complexity, priority, WIP, shift, and human reviewer. Record override reasons and bot confidence if available. Instant bot duration must not be counted as equivalent human productivity. |

The dashboard should also provide drill-down from a case to its activities, assignments, waiting intervals, bot decisions, errors, and corrections. PM4Py dotted charts and performance DFGs are useful for this trace-level investigation.

Every quality metric should display its numerator, denominator, observation window, and maturity status. A service improvement accompanied by unchanged or worse **correctly resolved cases** is not a success. Conversely, a short-term service increase caused by appropriately detecting more defects is not necessarily deterioration.

The most important guardrail is to monitor the **whole system**: case mix, workload, queues, batching, handovers, and automation must remain visible beside worker performance.
