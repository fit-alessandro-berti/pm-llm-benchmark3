| **T3, Day 3 to Day 6** | Intake returned, but inventory synchronization and scans remained unreliable. Normal validation resumed alongside backlog and manual-reservation reconciliation; some manual deviations therefore persisted. | In 11,400 cases, Hospital-critical median/p90 improved to 9h/18h, Pharmacy-standard to 38h/90h, and Wholesale-bulk to 84h/160h, still slower than baseline. # 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is the ability to continue or restore the essential service while adapting the means of work and preserving the outcome of required controls. A different route through the process is not automatically a failure: manual authorization (B), a bounded inventory snapshot (D), documented paper picking (E), explicit deferral (I), principled prioritization (J), and disciplined reconciliation (K) can all be resilient adaptations. Their validity depends on whether the substitute control is effective, recorded at the time, and appropriate to the product risk. Hospital fast-laning (A) is also resilient when it changes queue order while retaining authorization and product safeguards.

A shortcut becomes concealed noncompliance when required evidence or a control is omitted, presumed, or fabricated as if it had occurred. Skipping authorization (C) is the clearest example. Dispatch without custody evidence (G), relying on phone POD alone for controlled products (H), using a pharmacist sample instead of required per-order checks (F), and treating later audit work (L) as proof of real-time compliance cross that line when mandatory controls were absent. The conformance decline in T2 (0.71 fitness; 44% fitting traces), move-on-log deviations, and 9.5% traceability exceptions corroborate that this was more than harmless procedural variation. Reconstructed scans can improve the record, but they must be labeled as reconstructed and cannot establish that the original handoff control occurred.

Some choices are ambiguous and depend on risk appetite and operating rules. A hospital fast lane can justify a shorter queue, not automatic eligibility. A morning inventory snapshot can be adequate briefly for ambient stock with movement tracking, but not after its freshness limit or for high-risk lots. Deferring noncritical orders can be a safer choice than uncontrolled fulfillment, but only with transparent criteria, time limits, and attention to pharmacy access. Temporary release checks may add resilience but only if they cover each mandatory control. Management may accept a documented, time-limited substitute control for low-risk ambient orders; it should not accept silent waiver of authorization, custody, temperature, controlled-substance, or recall safeguards.

# 4. Recovery process model

The following is a degraded-mode process with explicit gates. “Hold” means the order or affected product cannot progress until the named evidence or authorized disposition exists.

1. **Detect and declare.** Trigger the incident process when WMS is unavailable or data integrity is in doubt, or when scanning/inventory synchronization fails often enough that a normal event cannot be trusted. The incident commander records start time, systems affected, data cut-off, cyber containment status, and the degraded-mode tier. Set an explicit owner and review cadence; do not keep using an unverified system as if it were authoritative.
2. **Establish a controlled operating set.** Select the latest known-good order, authorization, inventory, lot/recall, and customer reference data; label each source with version and timestamp. Issue numbered manual order, pick, dispatch, temperature, and exception records. Restrict who may allocate stock and approve exceptions. Set freshness limits per data type and product risk; when a source expires, refresh or physically verify rather than silently extending it.
3. **Triage each order.** Capture customer, requested items, quantity, clinical/service urgency, product class, lot/expiry where applicable, available evidence, and required-by time. Assign an explicit priority using (a) patient/service criticality, (b) product risk and expiry/temperature exposure, (c) order age and promised service, and (d) available stock and capacity. Require a logged reason and named approver for overrides. Notify deferred customers of status and next review time.
4. **Apply minimum control gates before pick/release.**
   - **All orders:** verify customer identity and authorization using a current offline source or verified callback; confirm product is eligible and not subject to a hold/recall; check stock and record its source; capture order-to-item/lot and quantity identity; retain a release decision and named operator.
   - **Hospital-critical:** expedite queue position, with the same authorization and product gates as other orders. If normal electronic proof is unavailable, use the approved manual evidence path; urgency alone never waives product safeguards.
   - **Ambient, standard-risk items:** a documented offline eligibility check and fresh enough stock evidence may support manual processing. If authorization cannot be established, place on hold or use a narrowly defined, pre-approved emergency exception with named approval and follow-up—not an unrecorded skip.
   - **Cold-chain:** document the required pre-dispatch temperature, use a functioning logger/approved temperature evidence through transit, record custody and delivery conditions, and define hold/reject limits and disposition. Missing or out-of-range evidence is a product-quality decision and must not be cured by later paperwork.
   - **Controlled-substance:** verify authorized customer and product, maintain the required dual control at pick/release, record quantity and custody at each transfer, and obtain strong authenticated delivery evidence. No shipment on phone assurance alone or without the required security controls.
   - **Recall-sensitive:** verify current recall/hold status and lot identity against a sufficiently current source before allocation and dispatch. If lot-level verification cannot be made, hold the item.
5. **Pick, pack, release, dispatch, and prove delivery.** Use a numbered paper or offline form linked to the order; record picker/checker, timestamps, SKU, lot/serial, quantity, substitutions, pack seal, release checks, temperature evidence, carrier, and recipient/custody signatures. A manual dispatch is allowed only if equivalent handoff evidence is captured contemporaneously. For low-risk deliveries, verified phone POD may be a temporary supplemental record with callback identity, time, items, and exceptions; it is not a substitute for controlled-substance custody evidence. Hold any shipment for which required evidence cannot be captured.
6. **Control the backlog throughout the disruption.** Maintain one prioritized queue, not separate informal lists. Re-score at a defined cadence and on new clinical need, stock, expiry, or customer information. Apply age escalation and minimum service floors to Pharmacy-standard as well as Hospital-critical demand; assess Wholesale-bulk deferrals against age and contractual commitments. Publish the rules internally, notify customers, log overrides, and review cancellations and service disparities daily.
7. **Restore systems and reconcile before declaring recovery.** Establish a trusted recovery point. Import manual records using unique IDs and duplicate-safe replay; reconcile each order, reservation, physical stock movement, lot/serial, dispatch, POD, and required product record against the system and source evidence. Count or quarantine discrepant stock, prevent duplicate reservations or shipments, and resolve authorization/recall/temperature exceptions before the affected order or inventory is treated as compliant. Keep unresolved records in an owned exception queue.
8. **Separate evidence repair from control failure.** Classify each gap as (a) control performed and contemporaneous evidence recovered, (b) evidence reconstructed later, or (c) control absent/failed. Never backdate or represent categories (b) or (c) as contemporaneous compliance. A qualified reviewer determines release, customer/product notification, regulatory escalation, and corrective action where required. Track remediation to closure with risk-based deadlines and a retained audit trail.
9. **Exit degraded mode by evidence, not by uptime alone.** Resume standard flow only when system integrity and inventory sync are verified, scan and authorization functions are reliable, manual records are reconciled to a defined threshold, high-risk exceptions are resolved or formally controlled, and backlog service is recovering. Continue incident governance for the remediation queue after operational close; declare audit recovery separately from order-flow recovery.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Measure by phase, customer segment, and product-risk class. Report both counts and rates, with denominators and the number of unresolved cases; do not let a high volume of low-risk ambient orders mask sensitive-product failures.

| Type | Indicator | How it should be used |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** | WMS, inventory synchronization, scan, and authorization-source availability and data age | Detect a need to enter or leave a degraded tier before staff resort to improvised bypasses. Track failed scans/syncs and age of each offline snapshot. |
| **Leading** | Minimum-control readiness and completion at each gate | Show whether offline authorization, dual control, temperature logging, recall/lot verification, custody forms, and trained coverage are available before release. Escalate any high-risk order lacking a required control. |
| **Leading** | Backlog size, age distribution, priority class, and predicted time to service | Identify queue growth and work older than its service limit; trigger added capacity, customer notice, or reprioritization. Monitor deferred and near-expiry orders. |
| **Leading** | Manual evidence completeness at pick, release, dispatch, and delivery | Track in-period completeness of IDs, timestamps, operators, lots, temperature, carrier, and recipient evidence. Missing required fields should stop progression at the relevant gate. |
| **Leading** | Priority overrides and fairness by segment | Review override rate, deferral age, fill, and projected service by Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk. Detect inconsistent treatment before cancellations accumulate. |
| **Lagging** | Throughput and cycle time, including median and p90 | Compare orders/cases and cycle-time distributions with baseline by segment and phase. Include the time spent in holds and remediation, not just operational close. |
| **Lagging** | Service protection | Track fill rate, on-time/in-full delivery, cancellations, and unmet demand separately by customer group and product risk. Hospital-critical performance alone is not evidence that the whole service is resilient. |
| **Lagging** | Traceability and control completeness | Measure traceability exceptions, missing scan/POD, authorization evidence, cold-chain temperature records, and controlled-substance dual-control separately. Distinguish a missing record from a confirmed missing control. |
| **Lagging** | Rework and reconciliation quality | Track corrections, duplicate reservations/shipments, inventory variance, wrong-item/lot events, and repeat exceptions after system restoration. |
| **Lagging** | Remediation burden and tail | Track open audit-remediation count, oldest age, risk tier, closure time, reopened cases, and share classified as recovered, reconstructed, or control failed. Publish it alongside operational recovery metrics. |

Use the supplied baseline as the initial comparison (for example, traceability 0.8%, cold-chain missing 0.4%, dual-control missing 0.2%, rework 3.5%, cancellations 2.1%, and segment-specific median/p90 cycle times). Set incident-specific alert and exit thresholds in the continuity plan, with stricter zero-tolerance gates for mandatory high-risk controls. These data support elevated alerting when the incident rates rise; they do not by themselves establish a safe universal threshold.

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower service, transparent deferrals, and documented manual variants when they protect urgent hospital supply while retaining the minimum authorization, product-quality, security, and custody controls. It should not accept unrecorded authorization skips, missing mandatory temperature or dual-control checks, undocumented dispatch, or after-the-fact entries presented as real-time compliance. The recommendation to allow any exception should change only when an approved control assessment shows that a specified substitute produces reliable, contemporaneous evidence for that product and order, and the data demonstrate improved service without worsening control completeness, segment fairness, or the remediation tail.
