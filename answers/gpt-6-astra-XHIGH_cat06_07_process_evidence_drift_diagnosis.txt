## 1. Drift diagnosis table

The categories overlap. Covariate drift concerns the distribution of inputs actually available at the prediction time, P(X); concept drift concerns P(breach | X). Process drift concerns changes in paths or their execution times. A population shift can cause both covariate and concept drift, particularly when relevant population attributes are omitted. Pipeline corruption changes recorded inputs without necessarily changing the underlying process. Completed variants and downstream edge durations are diagnostic evidence, but are not automatically valid features immediately after diagnosis.

## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

- **January:** Establish the reference process: AUROC 0.81, Brier 0.132, ECE 0.025, complete labels, and very low activity/feature defects. V3 is intrinsically riskier than V1 or V2; aggregate performance therefore depends on mix even without a changing conditional relationship.
- **February:** Performance remains stable: AUROC 0.82 and predicted/observed mean risk both 0.19. Parts time rises from 28 h to 31 h, but this alone does not demonstrate material concept drift.
- **March:** AUROC remains 0.81, and predicted and observed risk are both 0.18. Parts time reaches 35 h while the matured V3 breach rate is 0.30. Use January–March distributions and variability as the reference, rather than treating every movement in a median as an incident.
- **April:** This is primarily a schema and feature-pipeline incident. The scoring event was renamed, so some cases were scored at the wrong landmark or not scored. Unknown activities reach 17%, unmapped variants 18%, and missing queue features 14%. AUROC falls to 0.67, ECE rises to 0.110, and mean risk rises to 0.28 while the fully mature observed breach rate remains 0.19. Parts time is essentially unchanged at 34 h. Small increases in variant-specific breach rates leave room for some operational differences, but the strongest triangulated explanation is corrupted inputs and scoring eligibility. The reported metrics may also omit cases that never received a score.
- **May:** The mapping repair coincides with partial recovery: AUROC 0.78, ECE 0.050, unknown activities 2%, and missing queue features 3%. This supports the April diagnosis but does not prove complete repair. April's cached event-count feature remains wrong for some records, and deduplication was inconsistent. Separate May before and after 6 May, source-specific behavior, and the dates of backfill and feature recomputation; a monthly average hides these distinctions.
- **June:** This is a real routing change. V2 increases from 34% to 52%, while its matured breach rate is only 0.09 and remote-fix time remains stable. A larger low-risk path share plausibly explains much of the aggregate decline to 0.15. Mean risk remains 0.20 and ECE rises to 0.060, but AUROC remains relatively strong at 0.79. Mix shift or calibration under the new routing policy is more immediately plausible than a general collapse in ranking. Concept drift remains possible if the policy changes future outcomes among otherwise identical diagnosis-time cases and the model cannot observe the new assignment mechanism.
- **July:** The supplier incident begins on 10 July. Parts time rises to 65 h, the matured V3 breach rate rises to 0.46, and V3 share reaches 22%. AUROC falls to 0.76. This is early evidence of deteriorating execution and a changing risk relationship, with modest label incompleteness at 98%. Compare pre/post-incident exposure by when a part was ordered, as well as by case-start month: cases started before 10 July may still encounter the supplier disruption.
- **August:** The supplier explanation becomes substantially stronger. Parts time doubles from July to 130 h, matured V3 breach reaches 0.64, and V3 share grows to 35%. V1 and V2 rates remain much lower, at 0.18 and 0.11. Observed breach is 0.28 versus mean prediction 0.19; AUROC is 0.68 and ECE 0.120. Both increased exposure to V3 and deterioration within V3 matter. Label maturity of 93% makes aggregate estimates provisional, but the process evidence and supplied matured variant rates argue against a purely reporting-driven failure.
- **September:** The supplier bottleneck persists at 148 h, and V3 remains high risk at 0.61. That V3 rate is slightly below August's 0.64 even though aggregate observed breach rises to 0.31: this specifically argues against explaining the entire increase by worsening risk within V3. V3 exposure rises to 42%, and Partner Z contributes a new, older-device, more-rural population. Its larger V3 share helps explain the composition change. September combines persistent execution problems, likely conditional-risk mismatch, population shift, and substantial label selection at 82% maturity. Partner Z's observed 0.45 versus 0.27 elsewhere must be compared at equal follow-up before drawing partner-specific conclusions.
- **October:** The reported breach drop to 0.16 is not established recovery. Only 55% of labels are available, and the missing cases disproportionately have long resolution paths. Meanwhile, parts time reaches 155 h, V3 remains 41%, and Partner Z reaches 27%. All three indicate continuing exposure to difficulty. AUROC 0.64, Brier 0.205, and ECE 0.130 describe the available-label sample; neither those values nor apparent overprediction of 0.23 versus 0.16 reliably measure the eventual cohort. Under the simplifying assumption that maturity is the fraction labeled in this same cohort and available labels are final, the eventual breach rate could lie anywhere from 0.55 × 0.16 = 0.088 to 0.088 + 0.45 = 0.538. This is a logical bound, not a forecast; it illustrates why 0.16 is inadequate evidence for recovery.

April needs semantic and feature repair; June needs assessment of changed routing and calibration; August–September need operational and modeling responses to overlapping changes; October first needs trustworthy outcome observation. These are different mechanisms with different remedies.

Aggregate mean prediction must also be recomputed on exactly the cases used for each observed outcome metric. The supplied table does not establish that these denominators match. Variant shares and matured variant rates likewise should not be multiplied into an exact decomposition until their cohorts, maturity definitions, and unmapped cases are reconciled.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

1. **Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting.** Maintain case start, diagnosis/scoring, promise deadline, outcome-finalization, and outcome-ingestion timestamps. Plot the fraction labeled against days since scoring and since the promised deadline, by month, source, and observed path. Distinguish unresolved cases from finalized outcomes stuck in the API. Archive label vintages and compare cohorts at common follow-up ages, such as 30, 60, and 90 days, extending the horizon until each relevant segment reaches a plateau. Score all comparisons using the original immutable predictions and the same eligibility rules. October's later vintages cannot be manufactured at the current cutoff; backfill the evaluation as they arrive.
   - Under **selective label delay**, October's initially labeled subset differs in path/source mix; the backlog appears in ingestion delay, and metrics and breach prevalence change as long-path labels arrive. The apparent recovery should weaken if delayed cases have higher breach rates.
   - Under **genuine risk reduction**, the lower breach rate persists in mature, comparable strata at fixed follow-up, despite backlog reconciliation. Under **persistent concept drift**, mature errors remain after the reporting distortion is removed.
   - Equal calendar age alone does not eliminate unequal maturity. Weighting by estimated label-observation probabilities is only a sensitivity analysis unless conditional observation assumptions and overlap are credible. Ordinary survival treatment addresses unresolved outcomes, but does not automatically repair selectively undelivered finalized labels.

2. **Controlled pipeline replay.** Preserve original events, predictions, features, and model versions. Replay January–May with a canonical diagnosis mapping, source-aware duplicate handling, a correct diagnosis landmark, and complete dependency-driven recomputation of caches. Compare mapping-only, mapping-plus-deduplication, and full-feature-recompute versions. Verify case inclusion, score coverage, event counts, queue values, and feature hashes. Reconstruct information available at the historical prediction timestamp; do not add later events or outcomes to the prefix.
   - Under **pipeline failure**, April's unknown/unmapped shares and prefix errors collapse and same-model replay metrics materially recover; full recomputation improves beyond mapping-only repair where stale caches matter. May residuals track affected lineage or sources.
   - Under **real process or concept drift**, correct inputs and coverage do not restore mature performance, and process/outcome differences remain after repair.
   - Repaired replay estimates a counterfactual healthy pipeline. It must not overwrite the historical record of what production actually predicted. Cases originally never scored require a separate coverage analysis and counterfactual evaluation.

3. **Calibration and discrimination by variant, source, and month.** On common eligible, mature cohorts, calculate AUROC, precision–recall metrics, Brier score, calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, reliability curves, and ECE with fixed bin definitions and adequate bin counts. Include score coverage and uncertainty intervals, using case-level or appropriate operational-cluster resampling. Compare variant × source strata where sample sizes allow; standardize the aggregate metrics to a common composition.
   - Under **mix shift with stable conditional predictions**, within-stratum calibration and ranking are broadly stable; common-mix comparisons remove much of the aggregate change. AUROC and Brier can change merely because the evaluation population changes.
   - Under **mainly calibration drift**, ranking remains useful within comparable strata while intercept/slope or reliability curves move; recalibration improves held-out Brier/calibration.
   - Under **conditional/ranking deterioration**, residual errors or lost discrimination persist within comparable V3 or partner strata after maturation, repair, and mix adjustment. Recalibration alone cannot restore lost ordering information.
   - Completed variants are permissible retrospective audit strata. Repeat deployable analyses using only routing information known at diagnosis; a retrospective path-specific result does not establish that a path-specific predictor can be deployed at that landmark.

4. **Process variants and frequency/performance DFGs.** Compare canonical full-trace variants for adequately observed case-start cohorts, and compare event-calendar workload separately. Report transition counts normalized for volume, case counts, completed/open shares, and edge-duration distributions, including median and upper quantiles. Inspect duplicates, loops, unknown activities, missing diagnosis events, and timestamp ordering. Stratify parts delivery by supplier, source, rurality, and device/case type where available. Measure open orders separately because an Order Part → Part Received duration only exists after receipt.
   - Under **logging corruption**, April's apparent structural deviations mostly disappear after normalization and cleaning, without a corresponding change in true parts execution.
   - Under **routing-policy drift**, June shows a sustained shift toward the remote branch, while its duration distribution remains approximately stable.
   - Under **supplier execution drift**, July onward shows a rightward shift in parts durations within comparable supplier/case strata and an accumulation of outstanding orders. If aggregate slowing disappears after matching composition, a changed mix of orders is a stronger explanation than universal within-type slowing.

5. **Outcome relationships conditional on the same path.** Within V3, compare pre-incident and post-incident mature cohorts matched or adjusted for diagnosis-time severity, product, deadline slack, calendar effects, source, and geography/device age when recoverable. Examine frozen-model residuals and estimate period effects and period-by-feature interactions. Repeat for V1 and V2 as comparison groups. Check promise-date and outcome-definition lineage; measure exposure by order date as well as start month.
   - Under **pure path-mix shift**, within-path, comparable-case breach relationships remain stable and the change is reproduced by changing path weights.
   - Under **supplier-associated concept drift**, a post-July excess remains among comparable V3 cases, is much smaller on unaffected paths, and tracks supplier exposure. Stable conditional rates after repair and maturation would falsify that version of the explanation.
   - Under **within-path population change**, adjustment for severity, rurality, device age, or deadlines absorbs the period effect. Because some of these were omitted from the deployed model, this can still induce concept drift relative to its limited X, even without a changed physical relationship conditional on richer information.
   - Same-path conditioning is diagnostic, not a randomized causal experiment: the path is post-diagnosis and may itself be affected by policy or case severity. Actual future delivery duration can help examine the mechanism retrospectively, but must not enter diagnosis-time predictions. Comparison-path analyses need plausible common trends and still cannot exclude all confounding.

6. **Partner Z transport and reweighting checks.** First compare September Partner Z and contemporaneous non-Z cases at equal maturity, which limits confounding by the shared supplier period. Then compare historical non-Z cohorts transported to September's observed diagnosis-time covariate distribution. Use density/propensity weighting or matching on supported attributes, inspect overlap and effective sample size, and repeat an explicitly retrospective decomposition including realized path. Collect distance and device age before claiming adjustment for them.
   - Under **measured composition shift**, reweighted non-Z data reproduce much of Partner Z's mature breach rate and error profile, and residual partner gaps shrink. The supplied 58% versus 36% V3 shares make this plausible.
   - Under **unmeasured population differences or partner-specific execution/conditional change**, gaps remain after credible weighting, motivating richer features and direct partner/process investigation.
   - Under **differential label delay**, the apparent partner gap changes substantially when follow-up and availability are aligned, even before covariate adjustment.
   - Poor common support makes transport inconclusive; extreme weights do not provide evidence of successful generalization. A remaining source coefficient alone does not identify discrimination or prove that partner identity causes breaches.

7. **Feature missingness, lineage, and scoring-landmark audit.** Track schemas, activity dictionaries, event IDs, ingestion/event-time lag, timestamp ties, feature transformation/cache versions, source-specific deduplication, queue snapshots, and expected-versus-actual scoring coverage around 1 April, 20 April, and 6 May. Inspect prefixes at the diagnosis landmark, including every eligible case that lacks a score. Compare missingness and stale feature hashes by source, period, and outcome maturity.
   - Under **pipeline failure**, error discontinuities align with migration/patch boundaries and specific transformations or sources; normalized recomputation restores expected values and landmark alignment.
   - Under **valid operational covariate change**, correctly computed values change without lineage violations and survive replay. Under **concept drift**, lineage remains sound while conditional prediction errors persist.
   - A missingness increase can itself be operational, for example if the queue genuinely does not exist under a new route; compare against source semantics before imputing it as a technical defect.

8. **Intervention and deployment attribution.** Join prediction/model versions, threshold changes, routing-policy versions, intervention assignments, supplier actions, and promise revisions to the audit. Use approved randomized or phased comparisons where feasible; otherwise state the limitations of observational adjustment.
   - Under **deployment or intervention effects**, errors or outcomes change at the relevant rollout/exposure boundary, potentially because high-risk cases receive more help or routing changes future risk.
   - Under **supplier-driven deterioration**, parts-exposed cases worsen across unchanged model versions or untreated comparison groups after the supplier incident. If only a new model version deteriorates under common repaired data, investigate that deployment instead.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

1. **Immediate containment:** Repair scoring-event recognition and all affected feature dependencies, then verify that every eligible case is scored once at the intended landmark. Use a validated fallback or human review for cases with unreliable prefixes. Reconcile the outcome API and flag recent evaluation as provisional. Investigate and mitigate supplier delays using operational evidence without waiting for October labels; enhanced review for parts-exposed cases can be temporary and explicitly logged. Preserve production predictions and version every repair. Do not lower October risks because its incompletely observed breach rate looks lower.

2. **Short-term recalibration:** After feature repair, use recent, mature, representative holdout data to assess whether a simple intercept/slope correction suffices. June is a plausible candidate if within-stratum ranking remains useful and the main residual problem is overprediction under the new routing policy. Prefer modest, regularized corrections when data are limited; do not infer one common adjustment for June's apparent overprediction and August's underprediction. Evaluate calibration and decision costs prospectively. If ranking has deteriorated within comparable mature groups, a monotone recalibrator will not recover it.

3. **Feature repair and extension:** Recompute April's event counts and all downstream dependencies, validate queue lineage, and correct source-specific duplicates. Then add justified features available by diagnosis: intended route if already decided, expected parts dependency, supplier reliability calculated only from information received before scoring, current known supplier alerts/backlog, deadline slack, travel distance, device age, and operational workload. Distinguish when a fact occurred from when the system learned it. Future Part Received times, eventual variants, and the case's future repair duration are prohibited diagnosis-time features. Offline replay must use historical availability, not today's complete data.

4. **Full retraining:** Retrain after mature, repaired analyses establish persistent conditional error or inadequate ranking, especially on V3 and underrepresented new-population cases. Use chronological training/calibration/test splits with outcome-availability cutoffs; a label must have been available at the simulated training date. Include repaired historical data for stable paths and sufficient recent mature data for supplier and population conditions, with weighting justified by the expected deployment population. Compare against the frozen model and recalibration-only alternatives on calibration, ranking, segment error, and operational utility. Deploy through shadow/canary evaluation with rollback and logged interventions. Retraining cannot itself fix late parts delivery.

Training readiness at the supplied cutoff is as follows:

- **January–March:** Suitable clean, mature reference data, subject to ordinary point-in-time checks. They do not by themselves represent the later operating regime.
- **April:** Unsafe as currently represented despite 100% label maturity. Require corrected landmarks, source-consistent duplicate handling, complete feature recomputation, and recovered eligibility before admitting records.
- **May:** Conditionally usable after record-level lineage verification. The deployment of a mapping patch is not proof that all May records or historical caches are correct; quarantine affected versions until repaired.
- **June:** Generally suitable once feature lineage is verified; it is mature and represents the new routing policy. Reconstruct features and routing knowledge available at scoring.
- **July–August:** Valuable evidence of supplier change, but not automatically safe as entire cohorts at 98% and 93% maturity. Prefer adequately followed, reconciled cohorts. Naively selecting only currently labeled cases may bias training even though their individual labels are correct; any interim correction needs supported observation assumptions and sensitivity analysis.
- **September:** Not ready for naive full-cohort binary training at 82% maturity, particularly with slower Partner Z maturation and new feature-support gaps. Prepare features now and admit appropriately matured, representative data as it becomes available.
- **October:** Unsafe for ordinary supervised retraining or recalibration from the current available-label subset. Resolve backlog and maturity first. A censoring-aware formulation is a separate modeling choice and still requires an observation model for API delivery failures.

A shared model with suitable features and regularized segment interactions is the initial preference. A Partner Z-specific component or calibrator is justified only by enough mature examples, persistent residual heterogeneity, and prospective improvement. A variant-specific model is deployable only if its routing assignment is known at diagnosis; otherwise use available predictors, a validated route-probability mixture, or explicitly introduce a later prediction landmark. Separate models based on eventual paths would leak future information and fragment already limited samples.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

Use a versioned event and prediction ledger keyed by case and scoring timestamp. Retain raw and normalized activity, source, event identity, event time, ingestion time, diagnosis landmark, feature/cache version, model version, score, promise-date version, actions, outcome-finalization time, and outcome-arrival time. Keep production evaluation distinct from repaired replay. Monitoring denominators must include eligible unscored cases as well as successfully scored cases.

| Monitor | Measures and views | Dependencies and response |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive | Mature-cohort AUROC, precision–recall performance, threshold precision/recall, Brier, operational costs, and score coverage; stratify by month, source, and available routing indicators | Publish provisional and mature series separately with counts and uncertainty. Mature residual degradation can initiate model investigation after data and label gates pass. |
| Calibration | Mean score versus outcome on the identical sample, calibration intercept/slope, fixed-bin reliability curves and ECE; common-mix and segment views | Consider recalibration when mature calibration worsens while discrimination remains adequate. Avoid acting on an aggregate gap caused by sample selection. |
| Label maturity | Availability curves, unresolved versus undelivered outcomes, delay quantiles, backlog age, and availability by score/path/source | Route delivery failures to outcome ingestion owners. Mark affected metrics provisional and prevent automatic recalibration/retraining from them. |
| Data quality | Schema/activity mapping, unknown share, duplicate/conflict rate, timestamp ties/order, missing features, cache freshness, feature lineage, landmark alignment, score coverage | Quarantine affected data, repair and replay, then reassess predictive alerts. Track the backlog of recomputations, not merely patch deployment success. |
| Process variant | Normalized observed/completed variants, routing shares, rare/unmapped paths, loops, prefix coverage, open-case share | Compare like cohorts and maturity. A path-frequency alert triggers process investigation; it is not itself proof of concept drift. |
| Performance DFG | Edge counts, duration medians and tails, outstanding-order ages, diagnosis-to-remote-fix times, source/supplier strata | Supplier alerts can initiate operational containment independently of labels. Check timestamp validity and completion selection before interpreting duration changes. |
| Population | Partner/source share, rurality, device age, available-feature support, missingness and subgroup scoring coverage | Trigger representativeness and transport analysis. Track data acquisition for absent attributes and unsupported regions of feature space. |
| Intervention/deployment | Models, mappings, caches, policies, thresholds, interventions, supplier actions, and promise-definition versions | Annotate every series; monitor canary/rollback criteria and separate changed outcomes caused by assistance from changed predictive quality. |

Alert logic should be explicit:

- **Data-quality failure → data incident and repair/replay.** Predictive alerts remain visible, but their interpretation is qualified; they do not automatically trigger retraining around corrupt features.
- **Label-maturity failure → outcome-recovery workflow and provisional metrics.** Use older mature cohorts for model assessment. Continue unlabeled process/population monitoring and warranted operational containment.
- **Healthy lineage + adequate maturity + persistent calibration-only degradation → recalibration evaluation. Healthy lineage + adequate maturity + persistent conditional/ranking degradation → feature/model investigation and retraining evaluation.** Multiple incidents can remain active simultaneously.

Set alert thresholds using clean reference variability, practical effect sizes, minimum segment counts, and persistence rather than single noisy values. Use source-specific checks so that a large healthy source cannot conceal a failing smaller one. Run data/backlog checks frequently and publish frozen monthly audit vintages. Compare calendar workload with calendar workload, and mature case-start cohorts with comparable cohorts.

The following extraction sketch assumes CSV columns `case_id`, `activity`, `timestamp`, and `source`; optional `event_id` identifies immutable source events, and optional `ingested_at` supports historical availability filtering. It returns Python dictionaries/DataFrames for inspection or serialization, without fitting a model. A snapshot without ingestion timestamps cannot reconstruct what was historically available merely by filtering event time. Use a pinned, compatible PM4Py version.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from numbers import Integral

DIAG = "Initial Diagnosis Completed"
CASE, ACT, TIME = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"


def monthly_evidence(as_of, path="field_service_events.csv"):
    cutoff = pd.Timestamp(as_of)
    cutoff = (cutoff.tz_localize("UTC") if cutoff.tzinfo is None
              else cutoff.tz_convert("UTC"))
    raw = pd.read_csv(path, dtype={"case_id": "string", "event_id": "string"})
    required = ["case_id", "activity", "timestamp", "source"]
    if raw[required].isna().any().any():
        raise ValueError("Quarantine records with missing required event fields")
    raw["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(raw["timestamp"], utc=True, errors="raise")
    available = raw["timestamp"].le(cutoff)
    if "ingested_at" in raw:
        raw["ingested_at"] = pd.to_datetime(raw["ingested_at"], utc=True,
                                            errors="raise")
        if raw["ingested_at"].isna().any():
            raise ValueError("Missing event-availability timestamps")
        available &= raw["ingested_at"].le(cutoff)
    raw = raw.loc[available].copy()
    raw["activity"] = raw["activity"].replace(
        {"Diagnostic Assessment Completed": DIAG})

    # Remove only verified repeated deliveries of the same immutable event.
    # Repeated activities can be legitimate rework; do not delete them by label.
    drop = pd.Series(False, index=raw.index)
    if "event_id" in raw:
        identified = raw["event_id"].notna()
        keys = ["source", "event_id"]
        identified_rows = raw.loc[identified]
        conflicts = identified_rows.groupby(keys)[
            ["case_id", "activity", "timestamp"]].nunique().gt(1).any(axis=1)
        if conflicts.any():
            raise ValueError("Conflicting event IDs require reconciliation")
        drop.loc[identified] = identified_rows.duplicated(keys, keep="first")
    clean = raw.loc[~drop].copy()
    suspected = clean.duplicated(
        ["source", "case_id", "activity", "timestamp"], keep=False)
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(clean, case_id="case_id",
                               activity_key="activity", timestamp_key="timestamp")
    df = df.sort_values([CASE, TIME], kind="stable")
    # case_id must already identify a case globally across source systems.
    starts = df.loc[df[ACT].eq("Case Created")].groupby(CASE)[TIME].min()

    def variants(frame):
        if frame.empty:
            return {}
        return {path: int(v) if isinstance(v, Integral) else len(v)
                for path, v in pm4py.get_variants(frame).items()}

    def summarize(frame, calendar_clipped):
        if frame.empty:
            return {"events": 0, "cases": 0}
        dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(frame)
        perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(frame)
        grouped = frame.groupby(CASE, sort=False)
        edges = frame.assign(
            next_activity=grouped[ACT].shift(-1),
            hours=(grouped[TIME].shift(-1) - frame[TIME]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600)
        medians = (edges.dropna(subset=["next_activity"])
                   .groupby([ACT, "next_activity"])["hours"]
                   .agg(["count", "median"]).reset_index())
        closed_ids = frame.loc[frame[ACT].eq("Case Closed"), CASE].unique()
        diagnosis_counts = (frame[ACT].eq(DIAG)
                            .groupby(frame[CASE]).sum())
        return {
            "events": len(frame), "cases": frame[CASE].nunique(),
            "unknown_share": float(frame[ACT].eq("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY").mean()),
            "timestamp_tie_rows": int(frame.duplicated([CASE, TIME], False).sum()),
            "dfg": dfg, "starts": start_activities, "ends": end_activities,
            "performance_dfg_native": perf_dfg,
            "adjacent_edge_hours": medians,  # Explicit medians match this audit.
            "diagnoses_per_case": None if calendar_clipped else diagnosis_counts,
            "observed_variants": None if calendar_clipped else variants(frame),
            "closed_cases": None if calendar_clipped else len(closed_ids),
            "closed_variants": None if calendar_clipped else variants(
                frame.loc[frame[CASE].isin(closed_ids)]),
            "temporal": pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
                frame, grouper_freq="M", start_timestamp_key=TIME),
        }

    months = {}
    if not df.empty:
        first = df[TIME].min().normalize().replace(day=1)
        for begin in pd.date_range(first, cutoff, freq="MS"):
            end = begin + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)
            calendar = pm4py.filter_time_range(
                df, begin.to_pydatetime(), end.to_pydatetime(), mode="events")
            # Enforce half-open months independently of library endpoint rules.
            calendar = calendar.loc[calendar[TIME].ge(begin) & calendar[TIME].lt(end)]
            ids = starts.index[starts.ge(begin) & starts.lt(end)]
            cohort = df.loc[df[CASE].isin(ids)]  # All available events, across months.
            months[begin.strftime("%Y-%m")] = {
                "event_calendar": summarize(calendar, True),
                "case_start": summarize(cohort, False),
                "duplicates_removed_in_event_month": int(
                    (drop & raw["timestamp"].ge(begin) & raw["timestamp"].lt(end)).sum()),
            }
    return {
        "as_of": cutoff.isoformat(),
        "availability_filtered": "ingested_at" in raw,
        "duplicates_removed": int(drop.sum()),
        "remaining_duplicate_candidate_rows": int(suspected.sum()),
        "cases_without_creation": int(df[CASE].nunique() - len(starts)),
        "months": months,
    }
```

Event-calendar summaries describe work observed during each month, including older cases. Their traces are clipped, so start/end activities are window boundaries, and edges crossing a month boundary are omitted. For a production edge-completion calendar view, compute adjacency on the full available trace first and assign each edge to its destination-event month. Case-start summaries follow cases started that month through the chosen snapshot and can include events months later. Their observed variants remain provisional for open cases; closed-case variants have completion-selection bias and must be reported with open-case counts. Neither view alone removes outcome censoring.

The sketch deliberately retains uncertain duplicate candidates. Reconcile cross-source copies through verified identity mappings, and resolve timestamp ties with source sequence information before interpreting affected edge durations or prefix counts. Audit repairs by source and retain the original raw snapshot. `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` supplies descriptive temporal aggregates; these full-trace outputs are monitoring artifacts, not diagnosis-time model features. PM4Py discovers process evidence here; drift decisions require the cohort comparisons and statistical checks described above, not an invented PM4Py drift-detector API.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

1. **Retraining or recalibrating on immature October labels.** Treating absent outcomes as negatives, or using only the fast-labeled subset, can teach the model that long, parts-dependent cases are safer than they are. Mitigation: reconcile delivery failures, use fixed-vintage mature cohorts, retain unknown labels explicitly, and test any observation-weighting assumptions rather than assuming missingness is random.

2. **Calibrating around April's pipeline bug.** An adjustment may compensate for malformed prefixes temporarily but become wrong once mappings, duplicates, and caches are repaired; it also cannot recover missed scores. Mitigation: repair the landmark and all feature dependencies first, replay the frozen model, and recalibrate only residual errors on validated inputs.

3. **Calling all deterioration a reporting artifact and ignoring supplier-driven risk.** This misses mature V3 breach escalation and the increase in parts time from 42 h in June to 155 h in October, leaving operational failures and underprediction untreated. Mitigation: maintain an independent process-performance alert, address supplier execution promptly, and test the model on mature comparable parts cases.

4. **Treating June's routing mix change as proof that the model must be replaced.** Unnecessary retraining can destabilize otherwise useful ranking and obscure a policy-induced change in aggregate prevalence. Mitigation: inspect within-stratum calibration, standardize composition, and compare a modest recalibration against retraining on a chronological holdout. Also test whether the policy changes outcomes for the same observable prefix.

5. **Attributing Partner Z's higher observed breach rate to unfair model behavior without stratified evidence.** Outcome disparity, prediction error, and intervention disparity are different quantities; rurality, device age, routing, and label delay confound comparisons. Conversely, explaining everything by composition could conceal harmful errors. Mitigation: use equally mature groups, transport checks with overlap diagnostics, subgroup calibration and threshold-error analysis, and review intervention allocation. Do not infer protected characteristics from partner or rural status.

6. **Using completed variants or future delivery times to fix diagnosis-time predictions.** This creates leakage and unrealistic offline improvements. Mitigation: enforce feature availability at the scoring landmark, preserve ingestion timestamps, and use eventual paths only for retrospective diagnosis unless a later prediction task is explicitly defined.

7. **Destroying real process behavior through indiscriminate deduplication or clipped-trace analysis.** Removing repeated activities can erase legitimate rework, while comparing incomplete recent variants with complete historical variants can manufacture drift. Mitigation: deduplicate by verified immutable event identity, quarantine conflicts, audit timestamp ties, and report full-cohort observation/completion status separately from calendar workload.

8. **Using a single global correction or unlogged intervention for all periods and segments.** June's apparent overprediction and August's underprediction require different explanations; helping high-risk cases can itself change the observed score–outcome relationship. Mitigation: version policies and interventions, evaluate mature segment-specific residuals, prefer regularized shared models where possible, and use prospective comparisons with rollback criteria.
