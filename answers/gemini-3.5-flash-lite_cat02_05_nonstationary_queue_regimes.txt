# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Queueing Quantities, Units, and Opening-Hours Convention
- **Operating Hours:** The service center operates 12 hours daily from 07:00 to 19:00 (720 minutes per day). No service occurs outside these hours.
- **Arrivals ($\lambda$) and Completions ($\mu$):** Daily counts of cases entering and leaving the system during opening hours. Units: cases / day (or cases / hour when normalized by the 12-hour window).
- **Service Time ($S$):** Active handling time excluding waiting time. The dataset provides daily median service minutes and 90th percentile (p90) service minutes, not case-level means or distributions.
- **WIP ($L$):** Mean number of cases in the system derived from 15-minute snapshots taken across the operating window. Unit: cases (dimensionless count).
- **Staff Capacity ($C$):** Mean concurrently available full-time-equivalent (FTE) capacity. Unit: FTEs.
- **Complex Mix ($\%_{complex}$):** Percentage of daily arrivals following a multi-step complex variant.
- **Outage Minutes:** Daily duration of service disruption (minutes/day).
- **Peak Arrival Concentration ($\%_{08\_10}$):** Percentage of daily arrivals occurring between 08:00 and 09:59.

## Limitations of the Aggregates
1. **Lack of Individual Event Logs:** Data is pre-aggregated to daily totals, medians, and p90s. We lack case-level timestamps, exact routing paths, and individual waiting times ($W_q$).
2. **Missing Initial Backlog:** The backlog at 07:00 on 2026-04-06 is unobserved, complicating cumulative flow accounting.
3. **Non-Identical Cohorts:** Completed cases on day $t$ do not necessarily belong to arrivals on day $t$; overnight carry-overs and multi-day backlogs decouple daily arrivals from daily completions.
4. **Service Time Distributional Form:** Medians and p90s describe active service times only. Total flow time ($W = W_q + S$) requires adding waiting time, which is unobserved directly.
5. **FTE to Capacity Mapping:** `staff_fte` measures *available* capacity, but we lack a conversion factor mapping 1 FTE to service capacity (e.g., cases/hour per FTE).

## Five Threats to Naive Inference
1. **Simpson’s Paradox via Aggregation:** Pooling weekday and weekend data (or pre-intervention and post-intervention data) conflates very different arrival intensities and staffing levels, masking underlying capacity bottlenecks.
2. **Conflating Active Service Time with Flow Time:** Using median active service time ($24\text{--}36$ minutes) as a proxy for total case turnaround time ignores queue waiting time, which balloons during outages and bursts.
3. **Ignoring Carry-Over Backlog:** Treating daily systems as closed and independent ($arrivals \approx completions$) ignores unobserved overnight queues, leading to false conclusions about system stability.
4. **Outage Distortion:** Treating outage days (e.g., 2026-04-15 with a 180-minute outage) as normal operational points biases throughput and WIP estimates.
5. **Bursty Arrival Misinterpretation:** Evaluating capacity based on daily average arrival rates hides severe intraday concentration (e.g., 08:00–10:00 peaks), leading to understaffing during rush hours.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## Alternative Segmentation: Structural Workday Typing (Batch vs. Flow)
Instead of separating by calendar chronology (outages/interventions), this alternative segmentation classifies weekdays into three behavioral archetypes based on intraday burstiness (`peak_08_10_pct`) and case complexity:
1. **Type A (Calm Flow):** Low morning peak ($\le 30\%$), low complexity ($<25\%$).
2. **Type B (Burst-Loaded):** High morning peak ($>40\%$), moderate complexity.
3. **Type C (Complex-Heavy):** High complexity ($>40\%$) combined with high burstiness.

## Comparison: Primary vs. Alternative
- **Strengths of Primary Segmentation:** Directly addresses management interventions (staffing changes) and external shocks (outages), making it actionable for operational auditing and accountability.
- **Weaknesses of Primary Segmentation:** Blurs intraday variation within weekdays; treats all non-outage weekdays uniformly aside from the identified shift.
- **Strengths of Alternative Segmentation:** Captures underlying queue stress caused by customer arrival patterns and casework difficulty, independent of calendar date.
- **Weaknesses of Alternative Segmentation:** Harder to map directly to the staffing intervention date (2026-04-29) because bursty days occur across multiple weeks.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Pooling data across all 28 days obscures critical operational phenomena. Below are three major distortions caused by pooling:

1. **Weekday vs. Weekend Composition:** 
   - Pooled daily arrivals average $\approx 89.6$ cases/day. However, this conflates weekday demand ($\approx 105.8$ cases/day) with weekend demand ($\approx 44.4$ cases/day). Using pooled averages leads to severe weekend overstaffing and weekday understaffing.
2. **Complex-Case Mix Shift:** 
   - Early April (Regime 1) exhibits $\sim 20\%$ complex cases, whereas late April (Regimes 5 & 6) exhibits $\sim 44\%$ complex cases. Pooling smooths this structural shift, hiding the doubling of p90 service times ($45$ min to $85$ min) and masking the true driver of capacity consumption.
3. **Outage and Intervention Masking:** 
   - On 2026-04-15, the outage caused completions to drop to $58$ while WIP spiked to $47$. Conversely, on 2026-04-16, recovery efforts drove completions to $129$ with $7.0$ FTEs. Pooling these days averages out the disruption and the heroic recovery, hiding the operational volatility.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## Dispersion and Burstiness Indicators
- **Arrival Fano Factor (Index of Dispersion):** Across the 28 daily arrival counts, the mean is $89.6$ and the variance is $971.4$, yielding a Fano factor of $\frac{971.4}{89.6} \approx 10.8$. This indicates massive overdispersion relative to a Poisson process (where Fano factor = 1), driven by the structural difference between weekdays and weekends. Restricting to weekdays only, arrival variance drops substantially, though intraday burstiness remains high (e.g., Regime 5 peak hours see up to $34$ arrivals in a single 60-minute bin on 2026-04-21).
- **Coefficient of Variation (CV) of Service Times:** While exact standard deviations are unavailable, comparing median ($35$ min) and p90 ($84$ min) service times suggests a highly skewed, right-tailed distribution (approximate CV $> 0.6$), confirming heterogeneous task complexity.

## Autocorrelation and Change-Point Evidence
- **Serial Dependence:** Daily WIP and arrivals show strong autocorrelation at lag 1 because cases carry over from day to day ($WIP_t$ depends heavily on $WIP_{t-1}$, completions, and arrivals).
- **Change Points:** A sharp structural break occurs around 2026-04-20, where `complex_pct` jumps from $\sim 22\%$ to $\sim 45\%$ and median service time jumps from $\sim 25$ min to $\sim 35$ min. This non-stationarity invalidates stationary queueing models (like M/M/c) fitted across the entire horizon.

## Limitation of Representative Days
Four representative hourly vectors (2026-04-07, 04-15, 04-21, 04-25) capture specific archetypes (ordinary, outage, bursty, weekend) but **cannot** establish a full stochastic model. They represent isolated snapshots rather than steady-state probability distributions.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law ($L = \lambda W$) requires stationarity, consistent units, and a stable boundary.

| Candidate Window / Regime | Boundary Stability | Throughput Proxy ($\lambda$ or $\mu$) | WIP Comparability | Carry-Over Risk | Stationarity Assessment | Implied Flow-Time Calculation ($W = L / \lambda$) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Entire 28-Day Period (Pooled)** | Unstable | Pooled Mean Arrivals ($89.6$) | Low (WIP swings 12 to 52) | High | Non-stationary (mix shifts, outage) | $W = 31.5 / 89.6 \approx 0.35\text{ days} \approx 4.2\text{ hours}$ | **Not defensible** |
| **Regime 1: Baseline Weekdays (04-06 to 04-14)** | Moderate | Mean Arrivals ($100.4$) | High (WIP $18\text{--}23$) | Low-Moderate | Approximately stationary | $W = 19.8 / 100.4 \approx 0.197\text{ days} \approx 2.36\text{ hours}$ | **Approximate only** |
| **Regime 3: Outage Day (04-15)** | Severely Violated | Completions ($58.0$) | Violated (WIP spikes to 47) | Extreme | Highly transient | Not meaningful ($47 / 58$) | **Not defensible** |
| **Regime 5: Complex Surge (04-20 to 04-28)** | Moderate | Mean Arrivals ($113.9$) | Moderate (WIP $41\text{--}50$) | Moderate | Steady under high load | $W = 45.4 / 113.9 \approx 0.399\text{ days} \approx 4.79\text{ hours}$ | **Approximate only** |

# 7. Operational implications

1. **Staffing Strategy:** Move away from static FTE allocations ($6.0$ FTEs standard). Because complex cases double the tail service times (p90 up to $88$ min) and arrival surges concentrate in the morning (08:00–10:00), staffing must scale dynamically during peak arrival hours and high-complexity regimes.
2. **Backlog Recovery Protocols:** Following outages (Regime 3), temporary surge capacity (increasing FTEs from $6.0$ to $7.0$, as seen on 2026-04-16) successfully drains accumulated WIP. This surge protocol should be formalized as a standard operating procedure.
3. **Peak Smoothing:** With up to $50\%$ of daily arrivals concentrated in early morning slots (e.g., 2026-04-27), appointment scheduling or intake throttling should be introduced to flatten intraday demand peaks.
4. **Data Collection Recommendations:** 
   - Log exact case-level entry, start, and completion timestamps.
   - Track initial backlog at 07:00 daily.
   - Record queue waiting times separately from active handling times.
5. **A Risky Policy under Pooled Averages:** Relying on pooled daily averages suggests that $6.0$ FTEs is sufficient to handle $\sim 90$ arrivals/day. Under this pooled view, cutting staff on complex-mix days or ignoring morning burstiness appears safe. In reality, during Regime 5 (Complex Surge), this pooled staffing level causes WIP to swell to $50$ cases and p90 service times to reach $88$ minutes, risking severe service degradation.
