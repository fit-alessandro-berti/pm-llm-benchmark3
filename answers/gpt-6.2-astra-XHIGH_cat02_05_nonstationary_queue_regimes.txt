# 1. Data audit and estimands

The center recorded 2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions in 336 open hours. The difference of **+110 cases** measures an imbalance in recorded flows. Interpreting it as growth of one physical backlog requires a stock-flow assumption that fails a check later in the series.

For a set of *n* days, let *A* and *C* be total arrivals and completions. The arrival and completion intensities below are A/(12n) and C/(12n), in cases per open hour. The operating clock runs from 07:00 to 19:00, includes outages during those hours, and pauses overnight. Mean WIP, denoted by L, includes cases waiting and in service. Because days have equal exposure, the average of their snapshot means estimates time-average occupancy **assuming** the snapshots represent all open hours comparably. Active service, waiting, and total time in the system are distinct quantities.

Three measurement checks affect the interpretation.

- **Inventory boundary.** Assume every arrival enters the measured WIP, every completion removes one case, and there are no other entries, exits or overnight adjustments. Then closing stock equals opening stock plus A − C. From Apr 6 through Apr 24, cumulative A − C is +88. Even with zero initial stock, Apr 25 must open with at least 88 cases. With only 43 completions that day, stock at *every* snapshot must be at least 45, yet reported mean WIP is 23. The same check gives lower bounds of 57, 59 and 65 against reported means of 20, 22 and 19 on Apr 26, May 2 and May 3. Even restarting the accounting at zero on Apr 20 yields a minimum of 35 cases on Apr 26. An unknown positive initial stock increases the discrepancy. Possible explanations include transfers, other exits, or a WIP extract that excludes carried or inactive cases. The data do not identify which. Below, “accumulation” and “drain” describe recorded flow balances.
- **Time bins.** All four hourly vectors sum to their respective daily arrivals and completions. All four disagree with the daily 08:00–09:59 arrival percentage by more than rounding permits (section 5). The particular hours at which to add staff depend on resolving this discrepancy.
- **Service and staffing denominators.** Only daily service medians and p90s are available. Their ranges describe shifts in typical and upper-tail handling times. Neither a pooled case quantile, mean service demand, nor mean waiting time is identified. Multiplying each day’s mean FTE by 12 and summing across days estimates available staff-hours. Arrivals per staff-hour and the signed flow balance are pressure indicators. Actual utilization would require busy time or compatible mean labor demand and effective capacity, including skill restrictions and downtime.

Additional threats include completions drawn from earlier arrival cohorts, quarter-hour sampling that can miss short peaks, unknown opening stock and ages, a possible complex-variant coding change, and unknown intervention timing on Apr 29. A weekday, an outage, and a day spent clearing old work expose different demand and service opportunities. Four weeks supply few comparable repetitions, and adjacent days share both calendars and unfinished work.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

For next-day dispatch, classify each day by **net flow relative to its own arrivals**. As an explicit operational choice, call a day accumulating when (A − C)/A ≥ 5%, draining when (A − C)/A ≤ −5%, and intermediate values near balance. Scaling by arrivals lets a few cases matter on a low-volume weekend. Five percent is a tolerance for triage, with no claim of a statistical threshold.

| Observed state | Days | Arrivals/h | Completions/h | Total net | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Accumulating | 11 | 7.89 | 6.70 | +156 | Includes Apr 11, the outage, and all nine days Apr 20–28 |
| Near balance | 11 | 7.30 | 7.29 | +1 | Includes Apr 29 despite the staffing change |
| Draining | 6 | 7.15 | 7.81 | −47 | Includes Apr 16–17 and Apr 30–May 1, also two low-volume Sundays |

This groups distant days by whether the next shift faces a surplus or deficit in recorded completions. It can trigger a review of outstanding work before the next shift, including on an otherwise ordinary day. It loses the distinction between an outage that interrupts completions and a persistent change in arriving work. State membership is known only after counting completions, so the separation in rates is partly definitional. Apr 30 lies exactly at −5%, illustrating threshold sensitivity. The primary segmentation is better suited to advance scheduling and comparing like calendar days. Both require an independent stock measure to establish how much old work remains.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

- **Calendar composition.** Across all days the rates are 7.50 arrivals/h and 7.17 completions/h, with 29.9 WIP and 5.14 FTE. The 20 weekdays average 9.00 and 8.60/h, with 34.9 WIP and 6.15 FTE. The eight weekend days average 3.74 and 3.60/h, with 17.4 WIP and 2.63 FTE. Weekends account for 29% of open hours but only 14% of arrivals. The pooled rate is a poor forecast for either shift type.
- **Mix and workload.** Weighting each day's complex percentage by its arrivals gives about 33.8% overall (reported percentages are rounded). Between ordinary and high-mix pre-intervention weekdays it rises from 20.9% to 44.6%. Mean staffing is 5.93 FTE in both groups. Arrivals per available staff-hour rise from 1.41 to 1.60, while completions per staff-hour change only from 1.40 to 1.41. The daily p90 ranges move from 44–50 to 78–88 minutes, and reported mean WIP from 20.3 to 44.3. The concurrence supports a change in work presented to the queue. Separating a genuine complexity effect from coding, routing or selection of cases for completion requires case-level linkage.
- **Incident cancellation.** Pool Apr 15–17 and the rates are 8.78 arrivals/h and 8.31 completions/h, with net +17. That completion rate is almost the ordinary weekday 8.30/h. It averages a three-hour stoppage with two high-output recovery days. A single “normal” service rate estimated from these three days would miss both the disruption and the extra capacity used to recover.
- **An observed Simpson reversal.** Compare Apr 20–28 with Apr 30–May 3, omitting the transition. Completions fall from **7.28 to 7.08/h pooled**, yet rise from **8.38 to 10.08/h on weekdays** and **3.42 to 4.08/h on weekends**. Weekend weight rises from 2/9 to 2/4 of open days. Applying the earlier 7:2 weekday/weekend weights to the later rates gives **8.75/h**. Even on matched Thursdays and Fridays (Apr 23–24 versus Apr 30–May 1), completions rise from 8.13 to 10.08/h, mean FTE from 5.75 to 6.75, and net swings from +22 to −13 with about 44% complex arrivals in each pair. Starting stocks are unobserved, service distributions differ, and there are only two matched pairs. A causal staffing effect cannot be isolated.

Likewise, an hourly Fano above one across different clock hours can arise from a deterministic daily profile even with conditionally Poisson arrivals. Estimating the CV of interarrival times would require individual timestamps.

In chronological daily data the arrival correlation is 0.41 at lag 1 and 0.99 at lag 7 (21 pairs at lag 7). WIP has lag-1 correlation 0.61, and recorded complexity 0.93. Weekly scheduling, persistent mix levels and carry-over can all induce dependence. An exploratory two-level fit to daily complex percentage, minimizing absolute deviations with at least five days per side, places the split at Apr 20. Segment medians are 22.5% and 43.5%, and absolute loss falls from 292 to 56 percentage points summed across days. Both weekends and weekdays move to the higher recorded level. Apr 29 is an externally known but partially exposed day.

Short windows may be approximately repeatable **conditional on** calendar, clock hour, mix, backlog and available staff. Four selected profiles provide no replicated distribution for a given hour in a given state. They cannot distinguish a weekly intensity schedule from extra random clustering, establish independent increments or exponential service, or support precise change-point intervals. With more weeks, estimate expected counts by clock bin and day type, then examine dispersion and lag correlations of residuals across comparable bins. Retain whole days or weeks together when assessing uncertainty.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

For a common queue boundary, L = XW relates mean number present, throughput and mean **total sojourn**. For these finite windows, 12 times the sum of daily mean WIP values approximates the area under WIP in case-hours. On a consistently measured operating clock, that area equals the sum of open-hour sojourns of departures **plus the total age of cases present at the end minus their total age at the start**. This follows by letting each present case accrue one hour of age per open hour and removing its age at exit. Equal endpoint counts do not guarantee equal total ages. A long-run or repeating-cycle interpretation requires stable flow, comparable boundaries and negligible or cancelling endpoint terms. Time-varying daily rates alone do not invalidate the area identity.

The only ratios calculated below are for two contiguous early windows, conditional on complete, comparable WIP and count coverage there. Departures are the primary proxy because the target is time per case leaving the system. Arrivals give a sensitivity check when counts nearly balance. Outage time is on the operating clock, closed hours are off it.

# 7. Operational implications

1. **Staff to day type and work content.** Keep separate weekday and weekend baselines, and plan skill coverage for multi-step cases. The higher-mix weekdays recorded about 13.4 excess arrivals per day at the earlier weekday staffing level. Test extra coverage against comparable weekdays with similar starting stock, variant mix and arrival volume. Track age of outstanding cases and upper-tail waits alongside completions. A flat target of 7.50 completions/h would cover pooled mean arrivals if achieved. Against 9.50 arrivals/h on high-mix weekdays it would leave about 24 excess arrivals per day, before allowing for peaks within a shift.
2. **Give recovery its own staffing rule.** At outage end, enumerate open cases and their ages, reserve staffed time for old work, and protect enough capacity for incoming cases. Check at handover how many pre-outage cases remain. The two recovery days offset 30 cases of net inflow, but that tally cannot identify which cases cleared. Use verified inventory and the observed excess of completions over new entries to revise the recovery horizon each shift.
3. **Smooth the observed bursts.** For arrivals the center can schedule, trial staggered appointment or batch-release times. For unscheduled work, stagger breaks and make a cross-trained responder available when short-bin arrivals exceed completions and the oldest wait is rising. On the Apr 21 profile, coverage limited to 08:00–10:00 would end just before the 34-arrival hour. Reconcile bin definitions and observe repeated comparable days before fixing permanent shift times.
4. **Instrument one queue boundary end to end.** Capture case and queue-visit IDs, queue entry, assignment, start, pause, resume, transfer, abandonment, reopen and final exit timestamps, with resource and activity. Record the original and versioned variant classification. Log actual staff availability by skill, outages and intervention start/end by timestamp. At 07:00 and 19:00 enumerate outstanding case IDs and ages, including suspended and overnight cases. Reconcile opening stock plus all entries less all exits to closing stock and the next opening. Rebuild WIP from visit intervals, compare it with snapshots, and reconcile hourly arrival shares to the source timestamps. Those records would support direct open-time and elapsed-time sojourns, waiting versus handling by variant, and a staffing evaluation over repeated matched days.
