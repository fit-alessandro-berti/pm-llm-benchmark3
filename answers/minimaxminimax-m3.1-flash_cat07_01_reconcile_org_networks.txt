# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

**Handover-of-work network**

- The handover network represents direct succession between resources: an event by resource A is followed by an event by resource B. Mira has very high out-degree centrality (0.82), in-degree centrality (0.51), and betweenness (0.77). This indicates that she:
  - receives work from the rules engine, temporary staff, and specialists;
  - routes work to medical, legal, and four adjusters;
  - often lies on shortest organizational paths between those resources.
- The edges `Bot_Rules  Mira` (920), `Mira  Carla/Diego/Elise/Farid` (1,250 combined), and `Mira  Omar/Priya` (390 combined) support a routing-hub interpretation. The return edges from the specialists to Mira are consistent with receiving specialist answers and deciding how to continue the case.
- This does **not** establish that Mira is a formal manager, that she makes correct decisions, or that she performs the substantive specialist work. It establishes organizational brokerage: many paths pass through her.
- Edge counts should not be interpreted without case normalization. Repeated transitions within a small number of difficult cases can produce many edges. Likewise, mean elapsed time on an edge, such as 42–51 minutes from Mira to specialists, includes downstream waiting and specialist handling, not just Mira’s own work.
- Bot_Rules is also structurally central, with betweenness 0.41, but its events take seconds and have minimal cycle-time contribution. This illustrates why centrality is not inherently problematic: automation can be highly central and fast at the same time.
- Vendor_Pay has substantial in-degree but no outgoing edge because it is a terminal external stage. Its 1.3-hour median duration may represent a payment gate or external queue. Its relatively low betweenness therefore does not make it operationally unimportant.

**Working-together network**

- The working-together network measures whether resources co-occur in the same cases. Mira’s low pair rates of 4–8% contrast with the 19–27% rates of the regional lead–adjuster pairs.
- This is not contradictory to her handover centrality. Mira generally makes an episodic intervention: she receives or routes a case and then disappears from that case. She has about 1.06 events per touched case (960 events over 905 cases), compared with roughly 1.17–1.28 for the leads and adjusters. Her work connects resources without jointly processing the claim.
- Her low co-occurrence is also expected where she touches 22% of all claims but primarily handles one-off routing, reopening, clarification, and assignment events. Many of the resources with whom she interacts are different from one another, so there are few repeated co-case pairings.
- The metric does not imply that she has little influence, poor collaboration, or low workload. It says only that the log contains little sustained, same-case collaboration with her.
- Conversely, the co-case rate of Omar and Priya—4%—can be low because they appear only on the most complex cases, while still representing substantive specialist collaboration. Pair co-occurrence is therefore not directly comparable with a resource-level centrality score.
- Possible measurement limitations remain: a minimum-support threshold, the way simultaneous or assignment-only events are represented, and unlogged conversations could all reduce measured co-working.

**Subcontracting network**

- In an \(n=2\) subcontracting pattern, resource A delegates or routes to B and reappears soon afterward. Mira’s patterns with Omar (126) and Priya (98) therefore suggest that she sends work to specialists and then resumes control of the case.
- Her subcontracting betweenness of 0.69 reinforces the broker interpretation. She connects resources that would otherwise have weak or no direct paths.
- The pattern does not establish a formal employment relationship, actual outsourcing, specialist supervision, or successful consultation. It can also arise because an assignment event and a later “reopen” or “assign” event are both recorded under Mira.
- The comparable Alice–Carla and Bruno–Diego patterns show that task delegation followed by resumption is not unique to Mira. Her distinctive feature is that her delegation occurs in the exception and specialist-routing path rather than the routine settlement path.
- The Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules pattern suggests a possible human-in-the-loop exception or rules-update loop, but it is not proof of an unauthorized override.

**Performance/time statistics**

- Mira’s 4.2-minute median service time is short relative to adjusters, medical specialists, legal specialists, and vendors. The evidence therefore points more toward a **coordination-capacity or decision-bottleneck** hypothesis than toward slow individual task execution.
- Her 960 events and 905 distinct cases place her among the higher-volume resources by case count. Her 48% exception rate among touched cases is consistent with role selection rather than proof that she causes exceptions.
- Her concentration of 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints makes her central to rework and complaint handling, but it does not establish that her decisions caused those outcomes.
- The absence evidence is the strongest operational signal: during six days without her, ordinary claims were unaffected, while the 90th-percentile cycle time for exception cases rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days—an increase of 5.5 days, or about 65%. This supports a localized dependency on the exception path. It does not prove that Mira personally caused the delays; the same pattern could arise from a single-role queue with no backup.
- “High variance” in cycle-time contribution is consistent with concentrated tail delay, but the metric needs a defined denominator and uncertainty estimate before being interpreted quantitatively.
- Other waits must remain in view: Omar and Priya have 37- and 51-minute median service times, Vendor_Verify has 2.1 hours, and Vendor_Pay has 1.3 hours. These may be external processing times rather than queue delays, so active work and waiting should be separated.

**Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity**

- No actual result from `discover_organizational_roles` or `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` has been supplied. It would therefore be premature to assign Mira a definitive role label.
- Her activity mix—missing information, reopening, complaint escalation, and specialist assignment—is behaviorally distinct from the routine triage/approval and damage/settlement profiles of the regional teams. A role-discovery analysis might identify an “exception coordination” or “triage brokerage” role.
- If she is placed in a singleton cluster, that should be interpreted as a distinct behavioral profile, not automatically as a formal organizational position. If she clusters with leads, that would support a coordination interpretation but still not establish managerial authority.
- These algorithms are sensitive to activity granularity. There is also a schema inconsistency: “Assign Specialist” appears in Mira’s activity profile and the sequence fragments but is absent from the stated list of 14 activities. That should be resolved before clustering or variant analysis.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. For example, the best-supported current interpretation is a combination of an episodic exception coordinator and a boundary broker, possibly with some discretionary gatekeeping authority. The “hidden bottleneck” hypothesis is principally a failure mode of that design, while the data-artifact hypothesis challenges the measurements themselves.

**1. Formal coordinator / exception desk**

- **Mechanism:** Bot_Rules or staff place flagged cases in an exception queue. Mira owns clarification, reopening, complaint escalation, and assignment to specialists or adjusters.
- **Supporting evidence:** The 920 incoming handovers from Bot_Rules; her exception-focused activities; her 48% exception rate; and her handling of 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. The fact that she touches approximately 22% of claims is consistent with a queue intended for a defined subset.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** There is no explicit queue identifier, formal mandate, service-level ownership, or evidence that she has authority to approve specialist selection. The owner’s description of her as a “queue cleaner” suggests operational rather than formally managerial responsibility.
- **Future predictions:** Exception traces should show a stable queue-owner field or event, consistent Bot_Rules/temporary-staff predecessors, and documented routing dispositions. If the role is formal, qualified backup resources should receive traces in her absence rather than traces simply waiting.

**2. Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency**

- **Mechanism:** All exception work funnels through one short-duration but non-substitutable decision point. Even if each interaction is quick, lack of capacity or authority at that point creates queues and delays downstream specialists.
- **Supporting evidence:** Exceptional out-degree, in-degree, and betweenness; high subcontracting betweenness; broad routing responsibilities; and the rise in exception-case tail time during her absence.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** Her median service time is only 4.2 minutes, and ordinary cases were unaffected. Centrality alone does not prove limited capacity. Specialist availability, vendor processing, and missing backup staffing could explain the absence result without classifying her as an inefficient individual.
- **Future predictions:** Exception queues should build before or after her events, and longer absence periods should increase exception wait time, age, reopen frequency, and specialist imbalance. If a trained backup takes over with no deterioration—or if queues form only downstream of her—then she is a broker rather than the primary bottleneck.

**3. Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker**

- **Mechanism:** Mira translates machine-generated flags, customer information, and specialist advice into assignments across regional teams and specialist functions. She bridges organizational boundaries without remaining in the case for substantive processing.
- **Supporting evidence:** Incoming links from automation, temporary staff, and specialists; outgoing links to every adjuster and both specialists; the return edges to her; and the A–B–A subcontracting patterns. Her low working-together rate is exactly what an episodic boundary-spanning role would produce.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** She does not perform the medical, legal, or damage assessment herself. Some routing may be fully prescribed by Bot_Rules, in which case she is executing rather than brokering decisions.
- **Future predictions:** Her cases should contain more transitions across inferred role clusters, more diverse specialist assignments, and fewer sustained same-team episodes than ordinary cases. If a documented rules engine predicts nearly every assignment, the discretionary “spanner” component is weaker, though the coordination function remains.

**4. Quality gatekeeper or human-in-the-loop automation monitor**

- **Mechanism:** Mira identifies problematic or ambiguous cases and selects whether and where they should continue, potentially modifying the rules engine’s triage.
- **Supporting evidence:** Her exception role, the 62 Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules recurrence pattern, the compliance reviewer’s concern, and her ability to choose among medical, legal, and adjuster resources.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** The log does not show an explicit override, rejection, approval, or decision field. “Manual exception” may simply mean that a standard workflow was completed by a person rather than evidence of unauthorized discretion.
- **Future predictions:** Decision logs should contain reason codes, rule identifiers, original and final routing, approvers, and override flags. Specialist choices and outcomes should show meaningful variation where discretion exists; near-deterministic assignments would support a queue-execution rather than gatekeeping role.

**5. Informal team lead / specialist scheduler**

- **Mechanism:** Mira uses tacit knowledge to identify which adjuster or specialist is best suited to a hard case, functioning as a de facto lead without formal authority over settlement approval.
- **Supporting evidence:** Adjusters describe her as knowing where hard cases should go. Her routing, reassignment, reopening, and specialist-selection responsibilities fit a scheduler or informal lead role.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** She has little working-together overlap with Alice or Bruno, does not approve settlements, and has no reported direct-management relationship. Her function may be knowledge-intensive without being managerial.
- **Future predictions:** Her involvement should cluster around exceptional cases rather than routine ones, and cases might show referral, coaching, or reassignment events not captured by the current activity catalog. During her absence, suitable cases should shift to team leads or a backup scheduler if the informal authority is person-dependent.

**6. Recording or data-artifact explanation**

- **Mechanism:** The network may be measuring administrative routing rather than actual coordination—for example, a shared queue account, an assignee field copied into `org:resource`, or isolated assignment events being treated as work.
- **Supporting evidence:** “Assign Specialist” is not in the declared activity list; most of Mira’s activities are short administrative events; and she has roughly one event per touched case. These patterns can generate high handover degree but little working-together connectivity.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** Her activity profile is substantively coherent, and the absence-period association is difficult to explain as a purely graphical artifact. Nevertheless, a shared account or coverage design could still explain both.
- **Future predictions:** Reconciliation with source task assignments, agent identities, and work-item histories should show whether another person performed the routing. Correcting those resource labels, timestamps, or activity definitions should materially reduce Mira’s centrality if the artifact hypothesis is correct.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk — moderate and localized to exceptions.** Mira’s handover and subcontracting centralities make a capacity constraint at her decision point potentially consequential. The absence-period result strengthens this concern for the exception path. Her short median service time argues against “slow work” as the explanation, but queueing can still arise before or after a fast router. The whole process is not dependent on her because ordinary cases were unaffected. Specialist, vendor, payment, and automated-rule waits must be analyzed as alternative or complementary bottlenecks.
- **Coordination risk — moderate.** Her matching of hard cases to specialists may be valuable, particularly if informal knowledge improves routing quality. The same concentration creates priority inversion, information loss, and fragmented ownership if her assignments are undocumented. The risk is not that she performs too many tasks; it is that too much exception coordination depends on one routing point.
- **Key-person risk — moderate-to-high for the exception path.** The 5.5-day increase in the 90th-percentile exception cycle time during her absence is direct evidence of continuity risk. It does not establish that no substitute could work; it establishes that the current substitution mechanism was inadequate.
- **Compliance/control risk — uncertain but material as a governance issue.** Combining problem detection and specialist selection may create discretion that is difficult to audit, especially if routing overrides are not logged. There is no direct evidence here of wrongdoing, biased specialist choice, or policy violation. High exception, reopen, or complaint rates should not be treated as evidence of misconduct. Appropriate controls include approved routing criteria, reason codes, an audit trail, and risk-based second review rather than indiscriminate dual approval.
- **Workload fairness risk — unknown to moderate.** Mira’s 960 events and 905 cases exceed the event volumes of the two team leads, and 48% of her cases are exceptional. Her median event time is much lower, so event count alone does not establish overload. Specialist exception rates may also reflect case mix rather than poor performance. Workload should be compared using active minutes, repeated touches, complexity, interruption frequency, and queue exposure.
- **Data-quality risk — material.** The missing “Assign Specialist” activity in the catalog must be resolved. Reported “service time per event” should be checked against the exact PM4Py definition, since service time may include idle gaps. Edge frequencies are not case-normalized, centrality depends on graph pruning and normalization, and absence effects are based on one short period without a formal control group. Resource labels may also represent teams or shared accounts rather than individual actors.
- **Automation and vendor dependency — contextual operational risk.** Bot_Rules is a central routing component despite its short processing time, while Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay are external stage gates with long durations. A governance response focused only on Mira would miss these dependencies.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

**1. Prepare, validate, and stratify the log**

- Use `pm4py.format_dataframe` to standardize timestamps, activity names, resource labels, and case identifiers.
- Resolve “Assign Specialist” and determine whether resource labels identify people, queues, bots, or service accounts.
- Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to create exception and non-exception views, and filter separately on `Reopen Claim`, `Escalate Complaint`, `Request Missing Information`, and `Assign Specialist`.
- Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to compare routing paths for ordinary, reopened, complaint, medical, and legal cases. Verify whether the PM4Py version and filter semantics retain whole traces or only matching events.
- Examine pre-absence, absence, post-absence, and rolling monthly time windows.
- Stratify by priority, amount band, case type, region, and channel so that differences in Mira’s case mix are not mistaken for routing differences.

**2. Handover-network analysis**

- Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` on the full log, exception cases, ordinary cases, and time slices.
- Calculate predecessor/successor frequencies per 100 eligible cases, not just raw edge counts, and separate one-time transitions from repeated transitions.
- Use `pm4py.discover_network_analysis` for the same metrics and `pm4py.view_sna` or `pm4py.save_vis_sna` for visual comparison.
- Examine whether Bot_Rules  Mira concentrates all exception entry or whether direct adjuster-to-specialist paths also exist.
- Compare elapsed edge times with resource service times to identify whether elapsed time is waiting before, during, or after the next resource’s activity.
- Include Bot_Rules, Bot_Ingest, Vendor_Verify, and Vendor_Pay in the same comparison rather than treating human centrality in isolation.

**3. Working-together analysis**

- Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` for the full and exception-specific logs and test several minimum-support or threshold settings.
- Add event-count-per-case and same-episode duration statistics. A pair with one short co-occurrence is structurally different from a pair handling a case over several days.
- Compare whether Mira’s low co-working is stable across months and whether she collaborates more with specialists after excluding assignment-only events.
- Supplement the log with interviews or system data about unlogged advice, since the working-together network cannot observe telephone or informal discussions.

**4. Subcontracting analysis**

- Use `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with \(n=2\) and, as a sensitivity check, \(n=3\).
- Compare Mira–Omar and Mira–Priya patterns with Alice–Carla and Bruno–Diego to determine whether recurrence is an exception-routing mechanism or a general delegation-and-resumption mechanism.
- For each fragment, measure waiting before the specialist event, the time until Mira reappears, and subsequent reopen or complaint behavior.
- Inspect whether the Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules pattern represents a rule update, exception clearance, repeated evaluation, or a logging artifact.

**5. Activity-similarity and organizational-role analysis**

- Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` to quantify behavioral similarity between Mira, team leads, adjusters, specialists, temporary staff, bots, and vendors.
- Use `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` to infer behavioral clusters, first on all cases and then on exception cases only.
- Test cluster stability across thresholds, time periods, and weighting by active service time rather than event count.
- Inspect whether the inferred clusters align with the regional teams or instead reveal a cross-team exception role. Do not equate an inferred behavioral role with a formal job title.

**6. Service and waiting-time analysis**

- Use `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to distinguish active handling from time spent waiting between events.
- Compare median, 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles by resource, activity, exception status, case type, and region.
- Specifically examine waiting before Mira’s routing event, between her assignment and specialist start, and after specialist completion.
- Apply the same analysis to Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay so external waiting is not mislabeled as internal coordination delay.
- Use confidence intervals or case-level bootstrapping because highly skewed cycle times can make means unreliable.

**7. Absence-period evaluation**

- Construct matched pre-, during-, and post-absence cohorts, using ordinary low-value cases as a comparison group.
- Test pre-trends and post-absence backlog recovery rather than relying only on the change in the 90th percentile.
- If possible, measure queue age and accumulation by exception type. An interrupted time-series or difference-in-differences model with ordinary cases as controls would be more credible than a simple before/after comparison.
- Treat the current result as strong but preliminary evidence of exception-path dependency, not definitive causal attribution.

**8. Routing-quality and outcome analysis**

- Trace individual exception cases to determine whether specialist selection follows documented attributes such as claim type, amount band, region, or detected problem.
- Compare routing accuracy, cycle time, reopen rate, complaint rate, and downstream service time across routes. Reliable routing-quality labels may require additional outcome data or expert review.
- Examine whether temporary staff and junior adjusters are disproportionately routed to Mira and whether that reflects appropriate escalation or poor initial triage.
- Compare Bot_Rules recommendations with Mira’s final assignments to quantify genuine human judgment separately from rule execution.

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately**

- Retain and formally define the coordination function currently embodied by Mira. Provisionally describe it as an exception-coordination and triage role, while recognizing that its formal authority and boundaries are not yet established.
- Establish trained backup coverage, a queue-ownership rule, and a routing/escalation runbook. Temporary coverage should not consist merely of redirecting work to adjusters, who may already be capacity-constrained.
- Instrument the process with explicit events and fields for exception detection, reason code, decision maker, selected specialist, original and final routing, priority, disposition, override, and return-to-owner. Reconcile the “Assign Specialist” activity definition.
- Introduce proportionate controls: approved routing criteria, complete audit trails, and second-line review for defined high-risk decisions. Do not impose dual review on every exception without first measuring its delay cost.
- Protect capacity and review workload using normalized active minutes, complexity, repeated touches, and specialist queues rather than raw event counts.

**Monitor**

- Exception queue depth, age, wait time, p50/p90/p95 cycle time, reopen and complaint rates, rework, routing accuracy, and workload distribution.
- Rolling handover, working-together, and subcontracting networks, with case-normalized measures and confidence intervals.
- Coverage performance during planned leave or absence, including queue recovery after the coordinator returns.
- Bot_Rules exception rates and routing loops, as well as waiting around Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay.
- Whether backup staff can make comparable routing decisions and whether the presence of two trained coordinators reduces tail delay without duplicating work.

**Do not change yet**

- Do not remove Mira or reduce her role until an alternative demonstrably provides all of the following: queue ownership, exception detection, specialist matching, escalation, return-to-case ownership, documentation, and tested backup staffing. A shadow trial should demonstrate comparable routing quality and exception-cycle performance first.
- Do not fully automate specialist selection or exception disposition merely because Bot_Rules is centralized; the existing human-in-the-loop behavior may be protecting quality.
- Do not reorganize the regional teams or reallocate adjusters based only on centrality or co-case rates.
- Do not treat her exception concentration, reopen rate, or complaint rate as evidence of poor performance or misconduct.
- Do not infer that the 6.5-day increase in the 90th percentile of exception cycle time proves a personal performance problem. It establishes a continuity and queue-design risk that requires systemic investigation.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira appears to be the process’s exception coordinator and triage broker, although the evidence does not establish that she is a formal manager or that she causes delays. Her high handover and subcontracting centrality means many claims pass through her for routing, reopening, complaint handling, or specialist assignment. Her low working-together score is not contradictory: she usually has one short event in a case and then leaves, so she connects roles without co-handling the claim. Her own median event time is short, but exception claims accumulated during her absence and their 90th-percentile cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days; ordinary claims were unaffected. This makes her a likely dependency for the exception path, not necessarily the whole process. The immediate response should be to retain and formalize her function, establish documented routing rules and audit trails, train backup coverage, and balance specialist queues. Monitor exception waits, rework, complaints, workload, and bot/vendor delays. Do not remove her until equivalent coordination is demonstrably replaced, and do not infer misconduct or weak performance from centrality alone.
