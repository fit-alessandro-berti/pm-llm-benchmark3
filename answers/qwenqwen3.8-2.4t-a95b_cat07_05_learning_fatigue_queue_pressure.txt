# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed work-period curve is not explained by a single mechanism. It is a layered result of case selection, learning, queue pressure, batching, automation behavior, and possible fatigue/circadian effects interacting with resource experience.

**Early shift: slow because the work is hard, not simply because workers are cold.**  
Positions 1–5 have the highest mean complexity, highest high-priority share, and backlog from the previous shift. Service time is 18.8 minutes and rework is 6.1%. This should not be read as poor warm-up or fatigue. Senior resources appear to take complex carryover cases early, especially after night or weekend backlog. Therefore, early slowness is at least partly a case-mix and selection effect. The first hour also contains many complex cases in Early and Night shifts, so early metrics are biased toward difficult work.

**Middle shift: apparent learning, but also easier work.**  
From positions 6–30, service time falls from 15.2 to 13.6 minutes, error rate falls slightly, and complexity/queue decline. This is consistent with learning or warm-up, but also with stabilization of case mix. Workers have cleared some backlog, queue pressure is lower, and cases are less complex. Seniors and mid-level staff show stable later error rates under low WIP, which supports genuine stabilization rather than fatigue. However, we cannot attribute all improvement to learning because the later cases are easier.

**Late shift: faster service does not mean better performance.**  
Positions 51–75 and 76+ show service times falling to 11.4 and 10.8 minutes while error rises to 4.6% and 7.9%, and rework rises to 8.2% and 13.7%. This is the key pattern that resists a simple fatigue-only explanation. Pure fatigue would usually predict slower and more variable service time, not sustained speedup. The more plausible interpretation is that high queue pressure creates a speed-quality tradeoff. Workers may rush, batch, use simpler pathways, or rely on automation to keep up. High WIP is associated with faster service but worse quality, especially for high-complexity cases. Late speed may therefore reflect pressure and workarounds, not proficiency.

**Errors rise only under certain combinations.**  
The tables show that later errors are not universal. Seniors under low WIP maintain an error rate around 2.2%. Mid-level staff also remain near baseline under low WIP. Juniors have higher early errors and very high later errors under high WIP. This suggests that errors emerge when pressure, lower experience, complexity, and potentially fatigue intersect. Low WIP later-shift work does not show the same error explosion, so elapsed time alone is not sufficient.

**Fatigue is plausible but conditional.**  
Fatigue may contribute to late-shift errors, especially in final hours, during Quality Check bursts, and among junior staff under sustained pressure. However, fatigue is not the primary explanation for falling service time. The evidence suggests fatigue interacts with queue pressure: when workers are tired but still required to clear a high queue, they may maintain speed while making more mistakes. This is a classic speed-accuracy tradeoff, not a simple slowdown.

**Circadian and shift-design effects are present but confounded.**  
Night staff have slower service and more urgent exceptions. Day shift demand peaks late, and Late shift inherits Day backlog. These patterns could reflect circadian troughs, but they also reflect staffing, arrival rates, shift handover, and case mix. To identify circadian effects, we must compare performance at the same clock hour across shifts and control for queue, complexity, and worker experience.

**Batching and automation distort the apparent curve.**  
Late-day short-service Document Review batches by juniors can reduce average service time while potentially increasing downstream rework. Bot_Risk instantly classifies cases but may create extra human corrections when queues are high. Quality Check delayed to the end of shift can generate bursts of corrections in the last 45 minutes. These mechanisms can make late throughput look better while quality problems appear later as rework.

In short: early metrics are dominated by complex backlog and selection; middle metrics reflect warm-up plus easier case mix; late metrics reflect queue pressure, batching, automation interaction, and possibly fatigue. Service time falls because workers are handling easier cases, becoming familiar with the rhythm, batching tasks, and rushing under pressure. Errors rise only when pressure, complexity, experience level, automation corrections, and late-shift control work combine.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair or misleading because the event log does not represent a randomized experiment. The cases assigned to each worker, the queue state at the moment work starts, the shift schedule, the automation output, and the timing of quality control are all system conditions largely outside the worker’s control.

**Selection bias is a major risk.**  
Senior resources appear to take complex cases early, especially after backlog periods. Juniors may receive different case mixes or may be exposed to high-pressure periods differently. Comparing raw error rates across workers or across shift positions can therefore reflect assignment rules rather than individual capability.

**Raw service time is not pure effort.**  
Event service time calculated from timestamps can include system delays, batch processing, parallel work, idle time, automated processing, or timestamp artifacts. Very short service times may indicate batching or automation, not exceptional efficiency. Very long service times may indicate complex cases, interruptions, missing documents, or system issues, not low effort.

**Error flags are not neutral.**  
Error detection depends on Quality Check timing, review coverage, and downstream rework discovery. If Quality Check is delayed to the end of shift, errors may appear concentrated late even if the underlying work occurred earlier. If some workers or activities are checked more heavily, their observed error rate will be higher.

**Queue pressure is often management-created.**  
WIP and queue length arise from staffing, shift design, arrival rates, backlog carryover, automation quality, and triage policy. Penalizing workers for high-WIP error rates can be inappropriate if the organization creates the pressure. A better response is to manage queue pressure, add capacity, adjust assignment rules, and protect complex-case handling.

**Automation can shift blame.**  
Bot_Risk may reduce recorded service time while increasing human correction work under high queue pressure. If human corrections are counted as human errors, the log can misattribute automation-induced rework to workers. The organization should distinguish original human errors from corrections of automated classifications.

**Fatigue and circadian effects require humane treatment.**  
If fatigue or circadian effects are present, the appropriate response is schedule design, breaks, workload smoothing, and staffing—not punishment. Workers may differ in sleep patterns, health conditions, caregiving responsibilities, and shift tolerance.

**Punitive use of error rates harms measurement.**  
If workers are penalized for raw error rates, they may avoid complex cases, underreport errors, delay quality checks, or shift work to others. This can reduce visible error rates while increasing hidden risk, customer harm, and later rework.

**Use adjusted, system-level metrics first.**  
Before making worker-level decisions, adjust for case complexity, priority, WIP, activity family, automation exposure, shift, and time of day. Use worker effects only as diagnostic signals within a fair process that includes context, appeal, coaching, and system improvement.
