# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness is not only about equal approval rates. It concerns whether similarly situated applicants experience similar processes: consistent routing, comparable procedural burden, timely service, accessible support, meaningful appeal, and fair allocation of scarce staff. The evidence shows disparities on several of these dimensions.

- **Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ substantially: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, Group D 58%. This is a disparity, not proof of discrimination. The groups also differ in case mix, so raw approval gaps cannot be equated with unfair treatment.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, residual approval differences remain: Group B 5.5 percentage points, Group C 2.2 points, Group D 3.1 points relative to Group A. These adjusted differences are concerning, but they are not definitive evidence of discrimination because key covariates may be biased or endogenous, and unmeasured complexity remains.

- **Procedural burden.** The simple path V1 is much less common for Groups B, C, and D than for Group A: 19%, 22%, and 18% versus 42%. Groups B, C, and D are more likely to experience document requests, support steps, fraud review, supervisor review, and appeals. Reopen/rework rates are also higher: Group B 22%, Group C 24%, Group D 20%, versus Group A 11%. Extra steps, handovers, and rework loops are burdens even if final approval rates were equal.

- **Timeliness.** Median cycle times are longer for Groups B, C, and D: 13.7, 15.9, and 12.1 days versus 8.1 days for Group A. The 90th percentile is also much worse for B and C: 34.2 and 38.8 days versus 21.4 days. Adjusted cycle-time differences remain: Group B +2.6 days, Group C +4.1 days, Group D +0.9 days. The largest single waits are in rights-preserving support steps: Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment takes 3.8 days for Group B, and Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment takes 4.7 days for Group C.

- **Consistency of routing.** Applicants with similar documented complexity should not experience systematically different routing. Some routing differences are legitimate: higher risk_score_band requires Fraud Review, disability accommodation requests require Accommodation Review, and minority-language requests require Language Support Contact. But the adjusted model still shows higher probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents for Groups B, C, and D. That suggests routing differences are not fully explained by the documented case mix.

- **Quality/accessibility of support.** Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving steps, but they are associated with long waits and few specialists: only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists. Service-level targets do not include separate timeliness thresholds for those steps. A support step that is formally available but slow and overloaded can still undermine accessibility and dignity.

- **Appeal/rework burden.** Appeal rates are higher for Groups B and D: 13% and 16% raw versus 6% for Group A. Adjusted appeal probability is +4.4 points for Group B and +5.7 points for Group D. The appeal/rework variant V6 is also much more common for Group D (13%) and Group B (8%) than Group A (4%), with a median cycle time of 30.7 days. This suggests a heavier dispute and correction burden for some groups.

- **Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers. Group B cases are disproportionately assigned to 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. Group C cases are disproportionately assigned to 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also do supervisor review. Group D cases are routed to the fraud-review queue more often. In two regions, Group B has more handovers of the form general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker, while Group C has more handovers of the form general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor. This concentration of work is a structural fairness issue.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to distinguish four things:

- **Disparity:** an observed difference between groups.
- **Legitimate routing:** a difference explained by policy-relevant case complexity or applicant needs.
- **Discrimination:** a residual difference after accounting for legitimate complexity that is not justified by policy and may reflect bias.
- **Uncertainty:** the possibility that the data cannot separate the above because of measurement bias, unobserved needs, or endogenous covariates.

Applicant group is not a treatment; it is a proxy for language, disability, risk, and historical disadvantage. The analysis should estimate group-associated differences after accounting for legitimate case complexity, while avoiding over-adjustment for mediators or biased covariates.

**Matching/stratification variables**

| Variable | Role in main analysis | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| risk_score_band | Control, but sensitivity-exclude and audit | Legitimate routing, but may encode historical disparities |
| documentation_completeness | Control, but sensitivity-treat as mediator | May be affected by language barriers |
| channel | Control | In-person applications may take longer |
| household_size_band | Control | Case complexity |
| prior_case_count | Control, but sensitivity-exclude | May reflect past discrimination |
| region, month | Control | Context and seasonality |
| language_preference, disability_accommodation_requested | Stratify, not control in main model | These are support needs and mechanisms, not neutral confounders |
| Request Missing Documents, Supervisor Review, Fraud Review, Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review | Mediators/outcomes | Do not control in the main model; analyze as procedural burden |

The main model should not adjust for downstream process steps such as Request Missing Documents or Supervisor Review when estimating the total group-associated difference. Those steps are part of the process that may themselves be unfair. Mediation analysis can then decompose how much of the group difference operates through extra document requests, support steps, or supervisor review.

**Path/variant comparison**

Compare variant shares across groups within strata of risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. Focus on:

- V1: simple path without extra steps.
- V2: document-request loop.
- V3: risk/fraud/supervisor path.
- V4: language-support path.
- V5: accommodation-review path.
- V6: appeal/rework path.

For each variant, compare median cycle time, approval rate, and appeal rate. The key question is whether group differences in variant shares remain after stratification. If they do, the difference is not fully explained by documented case mix.

**Resource-assignment comparison**

Compare:

- number of distinct caseworkers per group;
- cases per resource;
- concentration of cases in a small number of resources;
- handover counts per case;
- handover-of-work networks;
- working-together networks;
- activity-based resource similarity;
- organizational roles.

This reveals whether Groups B and C are structurally assigned to a small set of overloaded specialists, and whether that drives waiting times.

**Waiting-time decomposition**

Use service/waiting-time separation to decompose total cycle time into:

- service time within activities;
- waiting time between activities;
- waiting time by specific activity pair;
- waiting time by resource.

Then estimate how much of the group cycle-time difference is explained by:

- Submit  Identity Verification;
- Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents;
- Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment;
- Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment;
- Risk Screening  Fraud Review;
- Supervisor Review  Decision Draft;
- Decision Draft  Applicant Notification.

A mediation-style decomposition can show, for example, how much of Group B’s adjusted +2.6-day cycle-time difference is attributable to the 3.8-day Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment wait versus the 1.9-day Supervisor Review  Decision Draft wait.

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates**

Run the adjusted comparison under several specifications:

1. Full controls as in the preliminary model.
2. Exclude risk_score_band.
3. Exclude documentation_completeness.
4. Treat documentation_completeness as a mediator rather than a confounder.
5. Exclude prior_case_count.
6. Audit risk_score_band for disparate impact, predictive validity, and calibration across groups.
7. Use bounding or E-value-style sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding.

If adjusted disparities persist across these specifications, concern increases. If they disappear when risk_score_band or documentation_completeness is removed, then the preliminary adjustment may have been over-controlling for biased or endogenous variables.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel**

Run the adjusted comparisons within:

- each region;
- each channel;
- region × channel cells;
- language_preference groups;
- disability_accommodation_requested groups;
- caseworker groups.

This is important because the data note backlogs in two regions and because in-person applications wait longer. It is also important to compare Group B language-support users with Group A language-support users, even if the latter cell is small, to isolate group-associated differences independent of support need.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most important procedural disparities are not only the raw approval gap. They are the lower share of the simple path, the concentration of Groups B and C in slow support paths, the extra document requests and supervisor reviews after adjustment, the appeal/rework path, and the concentration of Groups B and C in a small number of resources.

| Language-support path V4 | Group B 24% versus Group A 1%; median 19.2 days; Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment wait 3.8 days | Legitimate routing, but concerning delay and burden |
| Accommodation path V5 | Group C 31% versus Group A 2%; median 21.6 days; Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment wait 4.7 days | Legitimate routing, but concerning delay and burden |
| Risk/fraud path V3 | Group D 28% versus Group A 9%; median 18.4 days | Justified by higher risk_score_band, but risk score requires audit |

**What may be justified by case complexity**

- Group D’s higher share of V3 is consistent with its higher risk_score_band: 51% high-risk versus 18% for Group A.
- Group B’s higher share of V4 is consistent with its language-support need: 57% versus 2% for Group A.
- Group C’s higher share of V5 is consistent with its accommodation requests: 62% versus 4% for Group A.
- Group B and C’s higher share of V2 is partly consistent with higher incomplete documentation: 46% and 36% versus 21% for Group A.
- Group B’s longer Submit  Identity Verification wait is partly consistent with its higher in-person share: 38% versus 9% for Group A.

**What remains concerning after adjustment**

- The adjusted model still shows lower approval probabilities for Groups B, C, and D.
- The adjusted model still shows higher probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents for Groups B, C, and D.
- The adjusted model still shows higher appeal probabilities for Groups B and D.
- Group C has the largest adjusted cycle-time difference, +4.1 days, and its Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment wait alone is 4.7 days.
- Group B has a 3.8-day wait after Language Support Contact, and only 7 bilingual caseworkers are available.
- Groups B and C are concentrated in a small number of specialists, and both experience more handovers.
- The policy treats language support and accommodation review as rights-preserving, but there are no separate timeliness thresholds for those steps.
- Group D’s adjusted cycle-time difference is small, +0.9 days, but its adjusted appeal difference is the largest, +5.7 points. That pattern is more consistent with decision quality, risk-score bias, or unmeasured complexity than with simple cycle-time burden.

The adjusted model does not control for language_preference or disability_accommodation_requested. Therefore, some residual differences for Groups B and C may reflect unmeasured support needs. But that does not make the process fair. The fairness concern is precisely that rights-preserving support steps are associated with longer waits, more handovers, and fewer specialists.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available attributes are insufficient to support a strong conclusion of discrimination, but they are sufficient to identify serious procedural-fairness concerns.

- **Measurement bias in risk_score_band.** The risk score may encode historical disparities. It should not be treated as unquestionably neutral. Controlling for it may over-adjust and mask discrimination, while ignoring it may leave legitimate or illegitimate risk routing unexamined. It requires a formal audit.

- **documentation_completeness may be endogenous.** It may be affected by language barriers, disability, channel, or applicant support. If it is a consequence of communication problems, controlling for it may absorb part of the very disadvantage being measured. It should be sensitivity-tested as both a confounder and a mediator.

- **prior_case_count may reflect past discrimination.** A high prior-case count may be the result of past barriers, not an independent measure of complexity. Controlling for it may absorb historical disadvantage.

- **Missing applicant needs.** language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested are captured, but not their intensity: interpreter quality, accommodation complexity, disability type, language proficiency, literacy, digital access, or cultural needs. A binary accommodation flag does not show whether the accommodation was granted, denied, delayed, or adequate.

- **Unobserved resource workload.** Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. There is no real-time capacity, absence, skill mix, or caseworker-level load data. It is difficult to distinguish understaffing from inefficiency or from deliberate routing.

- **Appeal quality is missing.** appeal_flag only indicates that an appeal occurred. It does not show whether the appeal was justified, the reason, the outcome, or the quality of the appeal review.

- **case_outcome does not capture decision quality.** Approval rate is binary. It does not capture the quality of the explanation, the dignity of treatment, the accuracy of the decision, the benefit amount, or the timeliness of benefit activation.

- **No decision-rationale or decision-maker data.** There is no information on why a document was requested, why a supervisor review occurred, what the decision-maker concluded, or whether similarly situated cases were treated similarly by the same caseworker.

- **No applicant-experience data.** There is no measure of comprehension, satisfaction, trust, or perceived fairness.

- **Channel and region are proxies.** In-person channel may reflect digital access, transportation, or office location. Region may capture backlog but not individual office conditions.

- **Cross-sectional event log.** The log appears to cover one period. Month is controlled, but longer trends, seasonality, and policy changes cannot be assessed.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

None of these options removes language support or accommodation review. The goal is to preserve and strengthen rights-preserving steps while reducing their procedural penalties.

| Option | Description | Expected benefit | Possible side effects/trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Add capacity for language support and accommodation review | Hire or train more bilingual caseworkers, interpreters, and accommodation specialists | Reduce waits at Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment and Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment; reduce handovers | Cost, recruitment time, possible dilution of specialized skills, may reduce general capacity if specialists are pulled, requires quality monitoring |
| Service-level targets for support steps | Set timeliness thresholds for Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and related handovers | Make support-step delays visible and actionable | May incentivize rushing or gaming; could reduce quality; may shift bottlenecks elsewhere; pair with quality metrics |
| Routing redesign to reduce handovers | Assign bilingual or accommodation specialists as primary caseworkers, or create integrated teams, to avoid general  specialist  general handovers | Reduce waiting, errors, and procedural burden | May reduce specialization; may increase generalist workload; requires training; may disrupt continuity; pilot first |
| Decision-explanation improvements | Plain-language, translated notices; clear reasons for decisions; clear appeal rights | Reduce appeals and rework; improve trust and procedural fairness | More staff time; legal review needed; may increase initial cycle time; requires testing |
| Monitoring adjusted disparities | Regular dashboard by group, region, channel, with case-mix adjustment and support-step SLAs | Detect emerging disparities; support accountability | Perverse incentives; stigmatization; privacy concerns; gaming; needs governance, confidence intervals, and qualitative review |
| Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness | Validate risk score for bias, predictive validity, and calibration; audit documentation completeness for language and disability bias | Reduce biased routing; improve fairness of controls | May remove a useful risk signal; may increase fraud risk; requires model redevelopment; legal and policy challenges; needs fairness constraints |
| Resource rebalancing and cross-training | Shift cases away from overloaded bilingual and accommodation specialists; cross-train generalists | Reduce bottlenecks and handovers | May reduce quality of specialized support; requires training and supervision |
| Appeal quality review | Review appeal reasons, outcomes, and decision explanations | Identify decision-quality problems and reduce rework | Resource intensive; may require legal and policy changes |

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **not demonstrably procedurally fair**, but the evidence does not establish intentional discrimination.

There are substantial raw disparities, and more importantly, procedural disparities that persist after documented case-mix controls. Groups B and C experience slower, more handover-intensive, more document-request-heavy, and more supervisor-review-heavy processes. Group D experiences more fraud review and a higher adjusted appeal probability. Some of these patterns are consistent with legitimate routing: higher risk scores, language-support needs, and accommodation requests. But the disparities cannot be dismissed solely because groups differ in case mix.

The most serious fairness concern is that rights-preserving support steps—language support and accommodation review—are associated with long waits, few specialists, and extra handovers. Group B waits 3.8 days after Language Support Contact before Eligibility Assessment, and Group C waits 4.7 days after Accommodation Review before Eligibility Assessment. These steps should not become a penalty.

However, the evidence is not sufficient to conclude that the process is intentionally discriminatory. risk_score_band may encode historical disparities, documentation_completeness may be affected by language barriers, and unmeasured needs, resource workload, and decision quality are not fully observed. The adjusted model also does not control for language_preference or disability_accommodation_requested, so some residual differences may reflect unmeasured support needs.

**What can be inferred:** disparities in outcomes, timeliness, procedural burden, routing, resource allocation, and appeal/rework burden; residual adjusted differences for Groups B, C, and D; and concentration of support-step delays in Groups B and C.
