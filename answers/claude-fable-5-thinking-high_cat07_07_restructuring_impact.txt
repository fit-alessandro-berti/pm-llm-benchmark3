# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring is a set of structural interventions. Each intervention triggers causal mechanisms that surface as observable changes in event-log measures. The map below links **action → mechanism → observable PM signal**.

**A. Nominal handover reduction (merging embedded specialists into a shared service)**
- Mechanism: local caseworker↔embedded-tech loops (36 + 31 per 100 cases today) are replaced by caseworker↔shared-queue arcs. The *count of distinct handover arcs* in the social network shrinks (fewer resource pairs), but the *frequency per case* may not fall — management itself forecasts 41 outbound referrals vs. 36 today. Handovers are consolidated, not eliminated.
- Observable: `discover_handover_of_work_network` shows fewer edges but higher edge weights concentrated on shared-service nodes; degree centrality of Technical Shared Service resources spikes. Nominal handover count per case (transitions where `org:resource` changes) may be flat or slightly higher, with `handover_reason` shifting from "absence cover"/"local loop" to "routing rule".

**B. Central queue formation (single shared queue for Technical, Legal, Risk, hard Quality Check)**
- Mechanism: pooling reduces idle time (classic M/M/c pooling benefit) but the pooled queue now serves heterogeneous demand: technical referrals, legal/risk triage, and difficult Quality Checks. Legal/Risk are already at 91–93% utilization; adding routed demand (9 → 12 per 100 cases) pushes them past the utilization knee where waiting time grows non-linearly. The wait after handover to the shared service is forecast by management at 4.0h vs. 3.2h locally — an admitted degradation even in their own optimistic model.
- Observable: `insert_case_service_waiting_time` shows waiting time concentrating before Specialist Review; performance DFG (`discover_performance_dfg`) shows the caseworker→shared-service edge becoming the slowest edge; waiting-time distribution develops a heavy right tail; queue-length proxy (number of open cases enqueued at shared-service activities per day, from `extract_temporal_features_dataframe`) trends upward.

**C. Specialist utilization (+15% target)**
- Mechanism: pooling raises utilization of the ex-N/S/E techs (currently 71%, 68%, 82%). But utilization above ~85% converts directly into queueing delay and removes slack that today absorbs demand spikes and informal consultation. E_Tech at 82% benefits from pooling; N_Tech/S_Tech slack — which currently funds informal early advice — disappears.
- Observable: `get_service_time` per shared-service resource rises toward saturation; ratio of waiting to service time at Specialist Review climbs; the "informal advice" signal (short caseworker–tech interactions that today prevent referrals) disappears from the working-together network.

**D. Rework**
- Mechanism: two opposing forces. Standardized decisions can cut inconsistency-driven rework; but loss of local context (regional customer conventions, log line: "local teams know regional customer conventions; this reduces rework in complex cases") raises rework on complex/regional cases. Today the local-loop rework rates are 8–9%; scarce-specialist and cross-team paths already run 14–18%, showing what "distance from context" does to quality. Routing more work through context-distant resources drags average rework toward the higher band.
- Observable: `get_rework_cases_per_activity` split by `case_complexity` and `local_expertise_flag`; `rework_flag` rates on cases whose specialist advice came from a non-home-team resource; frequency of Rework activity after shared-service advice returns.

**E. Local expertise loss**
- Mechanism: routing all hard cases away means local caseworkers stop practicing complex work; skill atrophies over months, then simple-case quality also degrades and referral rates creep up further (a self-reinforcing loop: less skill → more referrals → central load grows → more delay).
- Observable: `local_expertise_flag` proportion trends down; `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` shows local caseworkers converging into a narrow "simple work only" profile; `discover_organizational_roles` shows local roles losing activities (Technical Assessment disappears from local role definitions); reassignment_count rises on cases bounced back for missing local context.

**F. Role clarity**
- Mechanism: removing a supervisor and creating a shared approval queue detaches approval from case ownership. "Who owns this case" becomes ambiguous when advice, approval, and implementation involve four organizational units. Standardization can *improve* clarity of decision criteria while *reducing* clarity of accountability.
- Observable: `discover_organizational_roles` shows overlapping, blurred role clusters; `reassignment_count` and ping-pong variants (A→B→A→B patterns in `get_variants`) increase; `handover_reason` values like "unclear ownership"/"wrong queue" appear; subcontracting network (`discover_subcontracting_network`) shows work bounced out and back without resolution.

**G. Resilience to absence**
- Mechanism: pooling improves resilience to *individual* absence (any pooled specialist can cover) but creates a single point of failure at the shared service (systemic backlog if the queue stalls) and removes local fallback. Cross-team handover falls (6 → 3 per 100), which is good only if the shared queue actually absorbs the cover need.
- Observable: variance of waiting time across calendar weeks; backlog recovery time after a specialist absence (temporal features); whether absence of one pooled resource still produces case-level delay spikes.

**H. Fairness across teams and customer segments**
- Mechanism: a shared FIFO/priority queue arbitrates between teams. East (currently worst-served, E_Tech at 82%) likely gains access to capacity; North/South lose dedicated capacity and may wait behind East's backlog. Customers needing language/regional accommodation face more handovers and context loss. Priority rules can systematically starve low-priority or particular `customer_segment` cases.
- Observable: waiting time and cycle time percentiles segmented by `team`, `customer_segment`, `location`, `priority`; SLA miss rate per segment; share of shared-service capacity consumed per team vs. their case volume.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

**Optimistic scenario (pooling works; routing rules are accurate; ~25–30% probability)**
- Handover pattern: arcs consolidate onto the shared service; cross-team ad hoc transfers drop to ~3/100; local loops disappear cleanly; `handover_reason` dominated by rule-based routing.
- Queue/waiting pattern: shared-service wait stabilizes near management's 4.0h median with a modest tail; Legal/Risk wait held flat by better triage; no persistent backlog growth.
- Cycle-time distribution: median improves 5–10% overall, East improves most (8.6d → ~7.3d); 90th percentile improves or holds because pooled capacity absorbs spikes better than E_Tech alone did.
- Rework/quality: standardization cuts rework to ~7% overall; complex-case rework holds flat because advice quality compensates for lost local context.
- Role clarity: routing rules are unambiguous; shared approval queue has explicit ownership rules; reassignment_count falls.
- Fairness: East converges toward North/South performance; segment-level SLA gaps narrow; no team consumes disproportionate shared capacity.
- Early warning signs (that it is on track): shared-queue waiting time flat over first 4–6 weeks; no rise in ping-pong variants; `local_expertise_flag` stable on simple cases kept local.

**Central scenario (mixed; most likely, ~45–50%)**
- Handover pattern: nominal per-case handovers roughly flat (management's own table sums to slightly *more* handover volume); the network shows a hub-and-spoke topology centered on the shared service; occasional bounce-backs for missing local context.
- Queue/waiting pattern: shared-service median wait 4–6h with a growing 90th percentile; Legal/Risk wait worsens (they were at 91–93% utilization and now receive 12/100 instead of 9/100); periodic weekly backlog oscillation.
- Cycle-time distribution: median roughly flat to slightly better (East improves, North/South degrade slightly — offsetting in the average); 90th percentile worsens 10–20%, driven by high-complexity cases stacking in the central queue.
- Rework/quality: overall rework flat (~9%), but composition shifts — standardization-driven rework falls while context-loss rework on complex/regional cases rises to 12–15%.
- Role clarity: measurable ambiguity at the approval step; reassignment_count up ~20% in months 1–3, partially recovering as conventions form.
- Fairness: East gains; North/South complex cases and language/regional-accommodation customers wait longer; averages hide a redistribution of delay from East to North/South and from simple to complex cases.
- Early warning signs: 90th-percentile wait at Specialist Review rising week over week; rework concentrated in `case_complexity=high` with non-home-team advice; North/South satisfaction slipping while East improves.

**Adverse scenario (central bottleneck + expertise spiral; ~25% probability)**
- Handover pattern: handovers per case *increase* — routing errors, bounce-backs, and "wrong queue" transfers create A→shared→A→shared ping-pong variants; the shared service becomes the highest-betweenness node with saturated in-flow.
- Queue/waiting pattern: Legal/Risk and Technical queues cross the saturation knee; waiting time grows super-linearly; backlog compounds week over week; high-priority preemption starves normal-priority cases entirely.
- Cycle-time distribution: median may still look acceptable for weeks (simple local cases dilute it) while the 90th/95th percentiles blow out (22.8d East tail becomes the *system-wide* tail, 25–30d+); bimodal distribution: fast simple-local cases vs. very slow shared-service cases.
- Rework/quality: rework rises to 12–15% overall; complex cases hit ~18% (matching today's cross-team rate, which is the correct analogue for context-distant work); Quality Check failures increase because difficult QC sits in the same congested queue.
- Role clarity: chronic ambiguity — approvals owned by nobody, `reassignment_count` doubling, staff routing around the official process (shadow handovers visible as undocumented resource switches).
- Fairness: systematic — low-priority segments, regional-accommodation customers, and whichever team's cases arrive behind the backlog suffer persistent SLA misses; specialist attention allocated by queue politics rather than need.
- Early warning signs: shared-queue inflow persistently exceeds outflow (backlog slope positive for 3+ consecutive weeks); referral rate to shared service climbing above the forecast 41/100 (the expertise-loss spiral starting); informal-advice interactions vanished from the working-together network; staff feedback flags "can't find anyone to ask."

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency.** The average is dominated by the ~55–60% of cases that stay local and fast. A blown-out 90th/95th percentile at the shared queue (high-complexity, legal/risk cases) can coexist with a *falling* mean. East already shows how this hides: 8.6d median vs. 22.8d P90. Monitor P90/P95 and the full waiting-time distribution per edge, not the mean.

2. **High-complexity case degradation.** Management's own model forecasts *longer* waits into the shared service (4.0h vs. 3.2h) and to Legal/Risk (10.5h vs. 9.6h). Complex cases pass through these edges multiple times, compounding delay. Average cycle time weighted by mostly-simple volume will not show it; only complexity-segmented analysis will.

3. **Local knowledge loss (slow-burn).** Skill atrophy has a lag of months. Early averages will look fine while the referral rate quietly climbs above the forecast 41/100 and `local_expertise_flag` erodes. By the time cycle time moves, the caseworker population has deskilled and the effect is expensive to reverse. Track referral-rate drift and role-scope narrowing as the real signal.

4. **Fairness redistribution.** Averages net out winners and losers: East improves, North/South degrade, and the mean is flat. Similarly, priority rules in a shared queue can systematically starve low-priority customers and regional-accommodation segments while high-priority cases keep the average respectable. Only per-team / per-segment percentiles and SLA-miss rates reveal it.

5. **Learning and development collapse.** No cycle-time metric captures that junior local staff never see hard cases anymore. The observable proxies are organizational-mining signals: resource-activity matrices narrowing, resource similarity collapsing into "simple work" clusters, and rising dependence of every complex variant on shared-service resources.

6. **Accountability diffusion.** Shared approval with no owner produces decisions that are on-time but unowned: when something goes wrong, no one traces or fixes it. Visible not in cycle time but in `reassignment_count`, ping-pong variants, subcontracting patterns without resolution, and later in outcome/complaint data.

7. **Exception and edge-case handling.** Standardized routing handles the standard 80% well and the exceptional 20% badly (wrong queue, bounced cases, informal workarounds). Exceptions are rare enough not to move averages but are often the highest-stakes cases. Detect via variant analysis of rare paths, `handover_reason="wrong queue"`, and shadow-routing patterns.

8. **Resilience loss.** With slack pooled away (target utilization +15%), the system runs closer to saturation. Averages in calm weeks look great; a demand spike or one specialist absence now causes a backlog that takes weeks to drain instead of being locally absorbed. Only visible in week-to-week waiting-time variance and post-shock recovery curves.

# 5. Pilot design

**Pilot scope.** Phase the change rather than switching all three teams. Recommended: pilot the Technical Shared Service with **East + one of North/South (say South)** for 3 months. East is included because it is the team most likely to benefit (E_Tech at 82% utilization, worst cycle times) — if the model fails even for East, that is decisive. South provides the "does a well-functioning local team get worse?" test. **Defer** the Legal/Risk queue expansion and the supervisor removal to a later phase: Legal/Risk are at 91–93% utilization and adding load there during the pilot confounds everything; the approval change is a separate mechanism and should be tested separately.

**Comparison group.** North continues under the current model as a concurrent control. Use difference-in-differences: compare South's pre/post change against North's pre/post change over the same calendar window, so seasonality and demand shifts are netted out. East's change is evaluated against its own baseline plus North as the trend control.

**Pre/post observation window.** Freeze a 3-month pre-period baseline from the existing event log (all metrics in Section 3, segmented). Run the pilot ≥3 months post-cutover, excluding a 2–4 week transition burn-in from the formal comparison but *monitoring* it for guardrail breaches. Skill-atrophy and learning effects need a 6-month extended observation before full rollout is approved.

**Segmentation.** All metrics computed per: `team` (North/South/East), `case_complexity` (low/medium/high), `required_specialty` (technical/legal/risk/security/none), `customer_segment` (including language/regional-accommodation segments), and `priority`. Report distributions (median, P90, P95), never means alone.

**Guardrail metrics (pilot continues only if all hold):**
- P90 waiting time before Specialist Review ≤ 1.3× baseline for the pilot teams.
- Shared-queue backlog slope not positive for >2 consecutive weeks.
- High-complexity rework rate ≤ baseline + 2 percentage points.
- Referral rate to shared service ≤ 48/100 (forecast 41 + tolerance) — drift above signals the expertise spiral.
- No customer segment's SLA miss rate rises >3 points above its baseline.
- Legal/Risk utilization stays <92% (they must not absorb spillover).
- `reassignment_count` P90 ≤ baseline + 1.

**Staff feedback collection.** Biweekly structured pulse from local caseworkers and pooled specialists: "Can you get informal technical advice when needed?", "Is case ownership clear at approval?", "Are you routing around the official queue?" Plus a lightweight mechanism to tag cases where the shared advice lacked local context (feeds the `handover_reason`/`local_expertise_flag` fields so it is minable). Triangulate feedback against the log: if staff report workarounds, look for shadow handovers in the resource data.

**Rollback / adjustment triggers.**
- *Hard rollback*: shared-queue backlog grows ≥4 consecutive weeks, or high-complexity P95 cycle time >1.5× baseline, or complex-case rework >15% for a month.
- *Adjustment (before rollback)*: re-tune routing thresholds (send medium-complexity back to local handling); add a "local liaison" specialist rotation returning pooled specialists to sites part-time; introduce team-fairness weighting in queue discipline; cap Legal/Risk intake.
- *Preplanned reversibility*: keep local specialist desks/access rights intact for 6 months so rollback is operationally cheap.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, in a modified form — do not proceed to full rollout as proposed, and do not reject.**

Reasoning. The proposal has one clearly plausible benefit backed by the current data: East is capacity-starved (E_Tech 82%, worst cycle times, 11.5% rework) and pooling genuinely helps capacity-starved units. But three elements of the proposal are high-risk on the current evidence and should be modified before any pilot: (a) routing Legal/Risk *more* volume (9 → 12 per 100) into specialists already at 91–93% utilization is queueing-theoretically reckless — that queue will saturate; (b) bundling difficult Quality Check into the same shared queue couples quality assurance to the bottleneck; (c) removing a supervisor while simultaneously creating a new shared approval step changes two accountability mechanisms at once and makes attribution impossible. Pilot the Technical Shared Service alone (East + South vs. North control, per Section 5), phase Legal/Risk and approval changes separately and only after adding Legal/Risk capacity.

The recommendation is **conditional on monitoring**: continuation requires the guardrails in Section 5 to hold for the full pilot window, the leading indicators in Section 3 to be reviewed weekly, and the 6-month expertise-drift check (referral rate, `local_expertise_flag`, role scope) to pass before rollout to North.

**What management must not infer from early averages.** A flat or improved average cycle time in the first 4–8 weeks proves almost nothing, for four reasons. First, the average is volume-weighted toward simple local cases that the change barely touches; the affected minority (high-complexity, legal/risk, regional-accommodation cases) can degrade badly inside a stable mean. Second, the costliest failure modes — skill atrophy, accountability diffusion, resilience loss — have lags of months and, in the case of deskilling, initially *reduce* apparent local workload, flattering early numbers. Third, early weeks benefit from a honeymoon: backlogs start empty, attention is high, and pooled specialists still carry their local knowledge; the steady state is worse than week four. Fourth, an improved mean can be pure redistribution — East improving while North/South and specific customer segments quietly deteriorate — which is a fairness failure, not a success. The decision rule should be: judge on segmented percentile distributions, backlog slopes, referral-rate drift, and rework composition — never on the headline average.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# ---------- 0. Load and format before/after logs ----------
def load_log(path):
    df = pd.read_csv(path)
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    return df

df_before = load_log("log_before.csv")   # pre-restructuring window
df_after  = load_log("log_after.csv")    # post-restructuring window

# ---------- 1. Handover-of-work network: consolidation vs. concentration ----------
how_before = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df_before)
how_after  = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df_after)
# Compare: number of arcs, weight on shared-service nodes, degree/betweenness
# centrality of Technical Shared Service resources. Expect fewer arcs but a
# hub-and-spoke topology after; a saturated hub is the bottleneck signature.

# Nominal handovers per case (resource switches within a case)
def handovers_per_case(df):
    d = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
    switch = d.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"] \
              .apply(lambda s: (s != s.shift()).sum() - 1)
    return switch.describe()
print(handovers_per_case(df_before), handovers_per_case(df_after))

# ---------- 2. Working-together network: loss of informal local ties ----------
wt_before = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df_before)
wt_after  = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df_after)
# Check whether caseworker–specialist co-working ties within a team disappear
# (informal early advice channel) and whether all ties now route via the hub.

# ---------- 3. Subcontracting network: ping-pong / bounce-backs ----------
sub_before = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(df_before)
sub_after  = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(df_after)
# Rising subcontracting from local caseworkers to shared service with work
# returning unresolved indicates routing errors / unclear ownership.

# ---------- 4. Variants and performance DFG ----------
var_before = pm4py.get_variants(df_before)
var_after  = pm4py.get_variants(df_after)
# Track: share of ping-pong variants (... Specialist Review ... Specialist Review ...),
# rare exception paths, and variant explosion at the approval step.

dfg_f, start_f, end_f = pm4py.discover_dfg(df_after)
perf_before = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df_before)
perf_after  = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df_after)
# Compare edge times: 'Local Technical Assessment'->'Specialist Review' before
# vs. caseworker->'Technical Shared Service' after; use median AND p90 stats.

# ---------- 5. Service vs. waiting times: queue formation ----------
df_b_wait = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df_before)
df_a_wait = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df_after)
svc_before = pm4py.get_service_time(df_before, aggregation_measure="median")
svc_after  = pm4py.get_service_time(df_after,  aggregation_measure="median")
# Key ratio: waiting/service time at Specialist Review and shared approval.
# Rising ratio at flat service time = pure queueing degradation.

# Temporal features for backlog slope / resilience analysis
tf_before = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df_before)
tf_after  = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df_after)
# Weekly inflow vs. outflow at shared-service activities; variance of waiting
# time across weeks; recovery time after specialist-absence weeks.

# ---------- 6. Resource similarity and role discovery: deskilling signal ----------
sim_before = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df_before)
sim_after  = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df_after)
roles_before = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df_before)
roles_after  = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df_after)
# Expect after: local caseworker profiles collapsing into a narrow simple-work
# cluster; technical activities vanishing from local role definitions.

# ---------- 7. Segmented comparisons: fairness and complexity ----------
def segment_metrics(df, attr, values):
    out = {}
    for v in values:
        sub = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            df, attr, [v], level="case", retain=True)
        durations = (sub.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
                       .agg(lambda s: (s.max() - s.min()).total_seconds()/86400))
        out[v] = durations.quantile([0.5, 0.9, 0.95])
    return pd.DataFrame(out)

for attr, vals in [("team", ["North", "South", "East"]),
                   ("case_complexity", ["low", "medium", "high"]),
                   ("required_specialty", ["technical", "legal", "risk", "security"]),
                   ("customer_segment", sorted(df_after["customer_segment"].unique()))]:
    print(attr, "BEFORE\n", segment_metrics(df_before, attr, vals))
    print(attr, "AFTER\n",  segment_metrics(df_after, attr, vals))
# Fairness test: did East improve while North/South or specific customer
# segments degraded? Compare P90/P95 gaps between segments before vs. after.

# ---------- 8. Rework and SLA misses ----------
rew_before = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(df_before)
rew_after  = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(df_after)

def rework_rate(df, mask_cols=None):
    cases = df.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        rework=("rework_flag", "max"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "first"),
        team=("team", "first"))
    return cases.groupby(["team", "complexity"])["rework"].mean()
print(rework_rate(df_before), rework_rate(df_after))
# Also: rework rate conditional on whether specialist advice came from a
# non-home-team resource (context-loss effect), and on local_expertise_flag.

def sla_miss(df, sla_days=10):
    dur = df.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        days=("time:timestamp", lambda s: (s.max()-s.min()).days),
        team=("team", "first"),
        segment=("customer_segment", "first"),
        priority=("priority", "first"))
    dur["miss"] = dur["days"] > sla_days
    return dur.groupby(["team", "segment", "priority"])["miss"].mean()
print(sla_miss(df_before), sla_miss(df_after))

# ---------- 9. Referral-drift monitor (expertise-loss early warning) ----------
def referral_rate(df):
    n_cases = df["case:concept:name"].nunique()
    d = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
    to_shared = ((d["org:resource"].shift(-1).astype(str).str.contains("Shared|TSS")) &
                 (d["case:concept:name"] == d["case:concept:name"].shift(-1))).sum()
    return 100 * to_shared / n_cases
# Weekly trend; alert if it drifts above the forecast 41/100 toward 48/100.
```

Decisions in the pilot (Section 5) key off these measures, not the headline average.
