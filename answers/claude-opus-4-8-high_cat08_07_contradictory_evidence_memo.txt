# Decision Memo: Fiber-Installation Redesign R

## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Operations & Customer Experience Leadership
**Re:** What to do next with Redesign R (coordinator ownership, task merge, survey skipping, post-billing activation)
**Recommendation: Adopt Alternative E — a controlled, segment-gated rollout — and do not scale nationally yet.**

Redesign R is not a win or a loss; it is a redesign that helps one population and harms others while shifting cost and risk from the visible front of the process to a less-measured back end. The evidence supports this directly. Median cycle time improved (14.2 → 10.8 days) and cases with 3+ handovers fell sharply (34% → 12%). But the tail worsened materially (p90 31.0 → 38.5; p95 45.0 → 62.0), first-time-right fell (82% → 76%), 30-day complaints rose (6.1% → 9.8%), manual activation and second visits roughly doubled, and the fuller cost estimate flipped the visible saving into a likely loss (EUR 184 → 161 visible, but EUR 198 → 207 all-in on an admittedly incomplete estimate).

The segment view explains the averages and drives the recommendation. Simple, low-risk houses are strong beneficiaries (median −6 days, p90 −8, first-time-right +3pp). Apartments/medium-risk and old/permit-sensitive buildings deteriorate badly (p90 +12 and +25 days; first-time-right −8pp and −11pp; complaints +5pp and +7pp), because the survey-skip rule is frequently wrong and permit checks are being missed. In other words, R's core mechanism (skip work, centralize ownership) is well-matched to easy cases and mismatched to hard ones.

Two of the harms look compliance-like, not merely quality-related: billing starts before a successful activation test, and permit checks are absent for older buildings. These are the alignment deviations most likely to create regulatory, financial-dispute, and trust damage, and they should be stopped regardless of the scaling decision.

**The central trade-off:** R buys speed and accountability for simple cases by removing checkpoints and concentrating work on coordinators (utilization 93%, resilience down when a coordinator is absent). For complex cases, those same removals convert a slower-but-correct path into a faster-but-wrong one, pushing failures downstream where they cost more and are least measured. We are partly "improving" a metric (median, handovers) by relocating the problem into the tail and into complaint handling.

The measurement base is too weak to justify a national bet: start timestamps are sparse (so we cannot cleanly attribute durations), risk/segment attributes are missing before week 3, downstream cost is truncated after week 4, and pilot regions are skewed toward apartments/old buildings (confounding the comparison against baseline). The low model precision (0.51) is itself a symptom: the process now permits too many uncontrolled variants.

**What we will do now.** (1) Keep and scale the clearly-good part: coordinator ownership and the validate/schedule merge for simple, low-risk houses. (2) Immediately suspend the two risky mechanisms everywhere: survey skipping for medium/high-risk addresses and post-billing activation; reinstate permit checks and enforce "no billing before successful activation" via conformance monitoring. (3) Add a coordinator-absence backup so cases do not stall. (4) Fix measurement before widening: instrument start timestamps, add reason codes, and track full 60-day downstream cost. (5) Run a randomized, risk-stratified experiment to learn where survey skipping is safe.

**Threshold to scale wider:** on properly instrumented data, for a target segment, R must show non-inferior p90/p95, first-time-right within ~1pp of baseline, complaints not worse, zero systematic billing-before-activation and missing-permit deviations, and all-in cost ≤ baseline. Segments meeting the bar graduate; segments failing it stay on the controlled baseline path.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness asks "can the model replay the traces we saw?"; precision asks "does the model forbid behavior we never intend?" R made most cases follow one simplified coordinator path, so the discovered model replays them well (fitness 0.94). But because survey-skip, manual activation, second visit, and billing-start now combine freely, the model must allow a large space of variants to stay fit — so it also permits many combinations that shouldn't happen (precision 0.51). High fitness + low precision is the fingerprint of an under-controlled process: everything is allowed, so everything fits.

**Improved median but worse tail.** The median moved because the large, easy majority (e.g., simple houses, 34% of pilot) got genuinely faster. The tail moved because the hard minority got worse: skipped surveys and missing permits turn into failed/second visits and manual activation, and the new Billing→Complaint escalation is a long-tail relation. Median measures the typical case; p90/p95 measure the failure modes. R shifted mass from the middle down and pushed the failures further out — improving one statistic while degrading the other.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handovers were a cost (delay, dropped context) but also redundancy: multiple roles held context and could cover. Concentrating ownership in one coordinator removed the handover cost (34% → 12% with 3+ handovers) but also removed the redundancy. At 93% utilization with no backup, a single absence stalls cases because context lived in one head. Efficiency and resilience traded against each other.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost.** Visible cost fell (184 → 161) because front-end steps and handovers were cut. But the removed checks did work that prevented downstream failures; those failures now appear as second visits, manual activation, complaints, and billing disputes — captured only partially, and after the cost-tracking window closes at week 4. So the saving is real but local; the all-in estimate (198 → 207) suggests cost was relocated, not eliminated. We optimized the measured segment of the cost curve and exported cost to the unmeasured segment.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings.** R's mechanisms assume the case is simple enough to skip survey and centralize ownership. That assumption holds for low-risk houses (median −6, p90 −8, FTR +3pp) and breaks for buildings that structurally need a survey or a permit (old buildings: p90 +25, FTR −11pp, complaints +7pp). Same intervention, opposite effect, because the intervention removes exactly the steps the hard segment depends on. Averaging across segments hides this and produces the "contradictory" overall picture.

## 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
- *Advantages:* Fastest capture of the median/handover gains; single operating model; simplest to manage.
- *Risks:* Scales the tail blow-up, rework, and the compliance-like failures (billing-before-activation, missing permits) to the whole book; all-in cost likely rises; decision rests on confounded, under-instrumented data. Violates the "don't scale without addressing tail/rework/compliance" constraint.
- *Reasonable only if:* the same gains held across segments with no tail/compliance regressions on clean data — which is not the case.

**B. Roll back to baseline.**
- *Advantages:* Restores known compliance behavior and first-time-right; eliminates the new risky variants immediately.
- *Risks:* Throws away a large, verified benefit for simple houses (the majority) and the genuine handover/accountability improvement; over-corrects on the basis that "some metrics worsened," which the constraints warn against.
- *Reasonable only if:* the compliance/complaint exposure were judged so severe and un-fixable that even partial R is untenable — not supported, since the harms localize to specific mechanisms and segments.

**C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
- *Advantages:* Keeps the best-evidenced structural win (fewer handovers, clearer accountability) while removing the two mechanisms most tied to defects and compliance issues; likely improves precision by re-adding controls.
- *Risks:* Coordinator bottleneck/resilience (93% utilization, absence stalls) remains unaddressed; doesn't yet exploit the strong simple-house speed gains from the validate/schedule merge; doesn't fix measurement gaps.
- *Reasonable when:* leadership wants a fast, low-risk consolidation and is willing to add coordinator backup and staffing. This is essentially the safe core of E.

**D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
- *Advantages:* Directly attacks the root problem — the data can't support a national decision (confounded regions, sparse start timestamps, truncated cost, missing risk attributes). Produces clean causal evidence.
- *Risks:* Delays value capture for the segments already proven to benefit; costs time and instrumentation effort; risk of "analysis paralysis" if not time-boxed.
- *Reasonable when:* combined with capturing known wins now (so it isn't pure delay) — again pointing to E.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, measure before wider scale. (Recommended)**
- *Advantages:* Captures the proven simple-house benefit immediately; contains harm to the segments that suffer; fixes compliance issues by keeping surveys/permits and billing-after-activation for risky cases; builds the measurement base for a defensible scale decision; likely restores precision by constraining variants.
- *Risks:* Operational complexity of running two paths; needs a reliable segment/risk classifier at intake (currently unvalidated); requires investment in instrumentation and coordinator backup.
- *Reasonable when:* segments are separable at intake and leadership accepts a staged, evidence-gated rollout — which matches this situation.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by how much each measurement can change the decision (highest first).

1. **M4 — Full 60-day downstream cost after billing.** Resolves the pivotal cost uncertainty (198 vs 207 is incomplete and could flip the entire business case). If all-in cost is clearly worse, it hardens the case against skipping/post-billing activation; if neutral, it strengthens wider rollout. Highest leverage because cost sign determines go/no-go.
2. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule vs. survey outcomes.** The survey-skip rule is the single mechanism most responsible for apartment/old-building harm. Knowing its true accuracy tells us whether skipping can ever be safe and for which strata — directly gating a large part of R.
3. **M2 — Instrument start timestamps (visit, activation, manual activation).** Without start times, every duration claim (including the median win) is partly an artifact. This makes all other metrics trustworthy and de-confounds the tail analysis; it is a prerequisite for a defensible national decision.
4. **M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit absence.** Turns the two compliance-like risks into continuously enforced controls. High value because it both quantifies exposure and prevents recurrence; low cost relative to impact.
5. **M1 — Randomized survey-skipping trial by risk stratum.** The clean causal test of the most contested mechanism; removes the region/case-mix confound. Slightly below M6/M2 only because it depends on them (a validated rule and clean timestamps) to interpret results.
6. **M3 — Structured reason codes (failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit).** Converts "rework rose" into "why it rose," enabling targeted fixes and distinguishing redesign-caused from region-caused failures. Enables the above analyses to be actionable.
7. **M5 — Coordinator vs. shared-team ownership under absence.** Resolves the resilience/bottleneck uncertainty (93% utilization, stalls on absence). Important for scaling the coordinator model safely, but the ownership benefit is already fairly clear; this optimizes rather than decides.
8. **M8 — Split customer experience into "speed to appointment" vs. "working service."** Explains the NPS dip and aligns the metric with the real trade-off (fast appointment, worse working service). Valuable for framing and monitoring, lower direct decision impact than the cost/rule/measurement items.

*Sequencing note:* M2, M3, and M7 are enabling instrumentation and should be stood up first (cheap, unlock everything else); M4 and M6 produce the decisive economic and mechanism evidence; M1 provides the causal confirmation to graduate segments.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E — segment-gated hybrid rollout with mandatory controls and measurement (incorporating the safe core of C and the rigor of D). Do not scale nationally (A); do not roll back wholesale (B).**

**Immediate action (now):**
- Deploy R for **simple, low-risk houses** only: coordinator ownership + merged validate/schedule. This segment is a proven beneficiary.
- Stand up conformance monitoring (M7) and hard-block **billing before a successful activation test**; reinstate **permit checks** for older/permit-sensitive buildings.
- Add a **coordinator-absence backup / shared context** mechanism to remove the resilience risk at 93% utilization.

**What to pause (everywhere, until evidence justifies otherwise):**
- **Survey skipping** for medium/high-risk addresses.
- **Post-billing activation** (activation must precede billing for any non-trivial case).
- Any **national scaling** decision.

**What to scale:**
- Coordinator ownership and the validate/schedule merge **for the simple low-risk segment** (~34% of volume), with staffing to keep utilization sustainable.

**What to measure next (in order):**
- Enabling instrumentation first: **M2** (start timestamps), **M3** (reason codes), **M7** (conformance) — already partly triggered above.
- Decisive evidence: **M4** (full 60-day downstream cost), **M6** (validate the risk rule).
- Causal confirmation: **M1** (randomized, risk-stratified survey-skip trial). Support with **M5** (resilience) and **M8** (split CX).

**Threshold / evidence that would justify broader rollout (per segment, on clean instrumented data):**
- **p90 and p95 non-inferior** to baseline (tail not worsened).
- **First-time-right within ~1pp** of baseline and **30-day complaints not higher**.
- **Zero systematic** billing-before-activation or missing-permit deviations (conformance clean).
- **All-in cost (incl. full downstream) ≤ baseline.**
- For survey skipping specifically: **M1/M6 show the risk rule is accurate enough** that skipping does not reduce first-time-right for the stratum.

Segments that clear the bar graduate to R; segments that fail remain on the controlled baseline path. This captures the real, segment-specific gains without pretending R dominates, addresses the worsened tail/rework/compliance risks head-on, and buys the information needed to make the national decision defensibly rather than prematurely.
