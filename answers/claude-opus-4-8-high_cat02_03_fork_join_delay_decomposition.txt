# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let `c` index cases and `i ∈ {A, B, C}` index the three parallel branches. All quantities are in minutes. For every case the observed timestamps are `arrival_c`, `fork_release_c` (abbreviated `fork_c`), branch start/end `s_{c,i}` / `e_{c,i}`, and `final_start_c` / `final_end_c`.

**Primitive components**

- **Upstream elapsed** (before fork): `upstream_c = fork_c − arrival_c`.
- **Branch queue wait**: `queue_{c,i} = s_{c,i} − fork_c`. This is the branch's own queueing after the fork permits work; it is *not* synchronization waiting.
- **Branch active service**: `service_{c,i} = e_{c,i} − s_{c,i}`.
- **Join-ready time** (the fork-join synchronization point, not logged directly): under the stated "F may start only after all three branches complete" rule,
  `join_ready_c = max_i e_{c,i} = max(e_{c,A}, e_{c,B}, e_{c,C})`.
- **Critical branch**: `crit_c = argmax_i e_{c,i}` (the last-completing branch). Ties broken deterministically (see below).
- **Branch synchronization wait** (idle wait a *completed* branch spends until the join fires):
  `sync_{c,i} = join_ready_c − e_{c,i} ≥ 0`. By construction `sync_{c,crit_c} = 0`.
- **Fork-join stage elapsed** (wall-clock length of the parallel stage):
  `stage_c = join_ready_c − fork_c`.
- **Downstream queue** (wait between join-ready and the final station actually starting):
  `downstream_c = final_start_c − join_ready_c`. This is only "queue" if F's resources were busy; it could also be a release policy (see §7).
- **Final service**: `final_service_c = final_end_c − final_start_c`.
- **Total flow time**: `flow_c = final_end_c − arrival_c`.

**Why summing branch synchronization waits double-counts elapsed time**

The three branches run *concurrently*, so their waits overlap the same wall-clock interval. The stage's true elapsed length is governed only by the last finisher:

`stage_c = join_ready_c − fork_c = queue_{c,crit} + service_{c,crit}` (the critical branch's own queue+service, since `sync_{c,crit}=0`).

But `Σ_i sync_{c,i} = Σ_i (join_ready_c − e_{c,i}) = 3·join_ready_c − Σ_i e_{c,i}`, which can exceed `stage_c` several times over (it counts idle-wait on parallel timelines as if it were serial). Concretely for F07, `stage = 75` min, yet `sync_A + sync_B + sync_C = 25 + 0 + 37 = 62` min of *overlapping* idle — adding these to elapsed time would count the same wall-clock window multiple times.

The only additive, non-double-counting flow-time identity is therefore built along the **critical path**, not by summing branches:

`flow_c = upstream_c + stage_c + downstream_c + final_service_c`
`       = upstream_c + (queue_{c,crit} + service_{c,crit}) + downstream_c + final_service_c.`

Synchronization waits and non-critical branch queue/service are *diagnostic* quantities (slack), not additive terms in flow time. This is the central discipline of fork-join accounting.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

**Branch service and queue summaries (min), completion, sync**

| branch | service mean | service max | queue mean | queue max | (queue+service) mean | end-after-fork mean | end-after-fork max | crit frequency | Σ sync (min) | mean sync/case |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|:--:|--:|--:|
| A (Lab Assay) | 50.0 | 50 | 0.0 | 0 | 50.0 | 50.0 | 50 | 2/12 | 132 | 11.0 |
| B (Stress Test) | 37.5 | 45 | 19.2 | 30 | 56.7 | 56.7 | 75 | 8/12 | 52 | 4.3 |
| C (Doc Review) | 20.2 | 30 | 10.4 | 35 | 30.6 | 30.6 | 57 | 2/12 | 365 | 30.4 |

**Reasoning about influence (not service alone)**

- **A** has the *largest and perfectly rigid* service (always exactly 50, zero queue). It is a strong, low-variance "floor": the stage can never be shorter than ~50 min. But because it is constant, A is the *last* finisher only when B and C are both fast (F01, F11), since B's queue+service (mean 56.7) usually overtakes it. A sets a hard lower bound but is seldom the binding constraint at the margin.
- **B** has smaller mean service (37.5) than A, yet the **highest queue** (mean 19.2, up to 30) and the **highest total completion** (end-after-fork mean 56.7, max 75). It is critical in **8 of 12** cases. B is the dominant controller of `join_ready` — and crucially, its dominance comes from **queue + service**, not service alone (its service is *below* A's).
- **C** has the smallest service (mean 20.2) but the **longest average synchronization wait** (30.4 min): it usually finishes early and sits idle. It becomes critical only in the two cases (F04, F08) where its queue balloons (35 and 25 min). C is normally slack, occasionally a tail risk.

**Sync-sum caveat**: the Σ sync column is *diagnostic idle*, not additive delay (see §1). C's huge 365-min total sync means C is chronically over-provisioned relative to the join; it is the branch with the most removable idle, but removing C idle does *not* shorten flow.

**Influence ranking (honest about near-ties)**

1. **B — dominant.** Critical 8/12, highest completion mean and tail, and its criticality is driven by queueing (a fixable operational lever). This is the branch that most strongly controls total completion time.
2. **A — strong secondary / floor.** Critical in 2 cases (F01, F11), but its rigid 50-min service is the binding constraint whenever B happens to be fast, and it is a *near-tie* with B in several cases (e.g., F02: A_end 09:15 vs B_end 09:25 — only 10 min apart; F09: 11:00 vs 11:15). Small worsening of A or improvement of B would flip several cases. A and B are close competitors for the critical role.
3. **C — usually slack, occasional tail.** Critical only when its queue spikes (F04, F08). Otherwise pure synchronization idle.

Ties/near-ties to report honestly: F02, F03, F05, F09, F10 all have A finishing within ~10–20 min of B; these are the cases where the A/B ranking is fragile and where a counterfactual on either branch could change the critical branch.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

Modeling convention (stated as an assumption, A1): I hold `downstream_c` (final-station availability) fixed and let flow-time savings equal the reduction in `join_ready_c`, capped so `join_ready` cannot fall below the second-latest branch completion (fork-join max logic). This gives a *first-order* estimate; §4.3 revisits the fixed-downstream assumption. Savings are floored at 0.

**Counterfactual 1 — reduce A service by 20% (50 → 40 min).**

- New A_end = A_start + 40. A drops to at most 40 min after fork.
- **Direct benefit only where A is critical.** A is critical only in **F11** (saves 10 min) and, after re-checking near-ties, **F01** (A_end 09:00 vs B 08:55, C 08:38 → A critical, new A_end 08:50 but B_end 08:55 becomes the new max → saves 5 min).
- Per-case join_ready reduction: F01 = 5, F11 = 10, **all other 10 cases = 0** (B or C remains the last finisher; e.g., F07 A_end 10:30 → 10:20 but B_end still 10:55, zero effect).
- **Total stage saving ≈ 15 min across 12 cases (~1.25 min/case).** Despite A having the *largest* service, cutting it is almost worthless because A is rarely critical — the canonical fork-join lesson: **speeding a noncritical branch has no case-level effect.**
- Secondary effects: freeing A resource capacity could shorten A queues elsewhere, but A already has zero queue, so no secondary gain.

**Counterfactual 2 — reduce B queue delay by 50%.**

- New B_start = fork + 0.5·B_queue; B_service unchanged; new B_end earlier by 0.5·B_queue.
- **Direct benefit in the 8 cases where B is critical.** Per-case join_ready reduction (capped by the next branch): F02 = 10, F03 = 10, F05 = 10, F06 = 15, F07 = 15, F09 = 12.5, F10 = 5, F12 = 15; F01/F04/F08/F11 = 0 (A or C critical).
- **Total stage saving ≈ 92.5 min (~7.7 min/case)** — roughly **6× the benefit of CF1** while touching the *smaller-service* branch. This is the decisive evidence that B's **queue**, not any branch's service, is the primary lever.
- **Zero-benefit cases:** F04, F08 (C critical), F11 (A critical), F01 (A critical). Also, in near-tie cases the saving is *capped* when A becomes the new critical branch (e.g., F02: B falls to 09:15, ties A → only 10 of the 20-min queue cut converts to flow-time).
- Secondary effects: reducing B queue implies more B capacity/faster dispatch; this could raise B resource utilization variance but has no synchronization downside (it only pulls the critical branch in).

**Counterfactual 3 — add final-station capacity / reduce downstream queue.**

- Downstream queue mean = 14.7 min (range 8–20), present in **every** case regardless of which branch is critical. Total downstream = 176 min.
- Adding an F resource (or a faster release policy) that halves downstream queue saves ~7.4 min/case *uniformly* and is **immune to the fork-join max problem** — it benefits all 12 cases, including the ones where CF1/CF2 give zero.
- **This is the key masking observation:** because `downstream ≈ 15 min` sits after every join, branch improvements upstream can be partly *absorbed* if F remains a bottleneck. If F1/F2 are the constraint, pulling `join_ready` earlier just lengthens the downstream wait rather than shortening flow. Evidence of possible F contention: downstream queue is large (18–20 min) precisely in later cases (F04, F10, F11) when both F resources are likely busy with the backlog — consistent with a real queue, not just a fixed policy (see §7).
- Secondary effects: more final capacity raises throughput and shrinks the flow-time tail (F07 = 130 is inflated by 15 min of downstream). But it does nothing for the stage itself; combined with CF2 it compounds.

**Summary comparison**

| CF | lever | total saving (min/12) | cases benefiting | zero-benefit cases | notes |
|---|---|--:|:--:|---|---|
| 1 | A service −20% | ~15 | 2 | 10 | largest service, least useful — A rarely critical |
| 2 | B queue −50% | ~92.5 | 8 | F01,F04,F08,F11 | best per-branch lever; capped by A near-ties |
| 3 | halve downstream | ~88 (≈7.4×12) | 12 | none | uniform, unmasks upstream, needs F capacity |

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Redesign R1 — attack B's queue (dispatch/capacity for the Machine Stress Test).**

- *Rationale*: B is critical in 8/12 cases and its criticality is driven by queueing, the single most convertible delay. CF2 shows ~7.7 min/case flow reduction.
- *Throughput*: shorter B queues raise stage throughput; the fork-join stage no longer waits on a queued B.
- *Flow-time tail*: directly cuts the worst cases (F07, F12), compressing the right tail.
- *Resource utilization*: requires added B capacity or better scheduling; watch for B resource idling between arrivals (utilization may drop even as flow improves — that is acceptable).
- *New bottleneck risk*: **A becomes the binding constraint** in the near-tie cases (F02, F05, F09) once B is pulled in — the 50-min rigid A service is the next floor. Benefit is capped there.
- *Synchronization effects*: reduces C's and A's idle sync waits (good), but does not create new sync problems.
- *Pilot/measure first*: measure B queue drivers (is it resource count, batch release, or upstream arrival bursts?) and confirm A does not immediately re-become critical; pilot on a subset of shifts and track `critical_branch` frequency and stage length.

**Redesign R2 — add/rebalance final-station capacity (or convert release policy to pull-on-ready).**

- *Rationale*: downstream queue (~15 min) is present in *every* case and is immune to the fork-join masking effect; it also caps the payoff of any upstream fix.
- *Throughput*: a third F resource (or faster F service) raises end-of-line throughput and prevents the backlog that inflates late-case downstream waits (F10, F11 = 20 min).
- *Flow-time tail*: uniform ~7 min/case cut; combined with R1, F07 could drop from 130 toward ~100.
- *Resource utilization*: F1/F2 utilization would fall; justify only if the tail/throughput gain matters, or pursue the cheaper "release policy" variant first (let F start the moment join_ready fires rather than on a batched cadence).
- *New bottleneck risk*: if F capacity is added but B queue is untouched, the stage remains the constraint and the extra F sits idle — **do R1 and R2 together, sequenced.**
- *Synchronization effects*: none at the fork-join; this is purely post-join.
- *Pilot/measure first*: **first determine whether downstream is a true queue or a release policy** (§7). If it is a fixed 15-min administrative gate, adding F resources will not help and the policy should be changed instead. Measure F resource busy-fraction at each `join_ready`.

Recommended sequencing: pilot R1 (cheapest, biggest stage lever), remeasure critical-branch frequency, then apply R2 only if downstream proves to be a genuine queue.

    ("F08","09:35","09:55","09:55","10:45","10:00","10:30","10:20","10:50","11:05","11:25","F2"),
    ("F09","09:50","10:10","10:10","11:00","10:35","11:15","10:15","10:35","11:30","11:50","F1"),
    ("F10","10:05","10:25","10:25","11:15","10:45","11:20","10:30","10:50","11:40","12:00","F2"),
    ("F11","10:20","10:40","10:40","11:30","10:50","11:20","10:45","11:03","11:50","12:10","F1"),
    ("F12","10:35","10:55","10:55","11:45","11:25","12:10","11:00","11:18","12:20","12:40","F2"),
]
cols = ["case","arrival","fork_release","A_start","A_end","B_start","B_end",
        "C_start","C_end","final_start","final_end","final_resource"]
df = pd.DataFrame(raw, columns=cols)

# 2. Parse timestamps and calculate every decomposition component -------------
tcols = [c for c in cols if c not in ("case","final_resource")]
for c in tcols:
    df[c] = pd.to_datetime(DATE + " " + df[c])

def mins(a, b):  # minutes from a to b
    return (b - a).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

df["upstream_elapsed"] = mins(df.arrival, df.fork_release)
for br in ["A","B","C"]:
    df[f"{br}_queue"]   = mins(df.fork_release, df[f"{br}_start"])
    df[f"{br}_service"] = mins(df[f"{br}_start"], df[f"{br}_end"])

# Fork-join JOIN is a MAX over branch completions -- NOT a directly-follows edge.
df["join_ready"] = df[["A_end","B_end","C_end"]].max(axis=1)

# 3. Critical branch with deterministic tie handling -------------------------
# Tie rule (deterministic): if two branches share the max end time, prefer the
# one with the larger (queue+service), then alphabetical order A<B<C.
def critical(row):
    ends = {"A": row.A_end, "B": row.B_end, "C": row.C_end}
    mx = max(ends.values())
    tied = [b for b, e in ends.items() if e == mx]
    if len(tied) == 1:
        return tied[0]
    load = {b: row[f"{b}_queue"] + row[f"{b}_service"] for b in tied}
    mxload = max(load.values())
    tied2 = sorted(b for b in tied if load[b] == mxload)
    return tied2[0]
df["critical_branch"] = df.apply(critical, axis=1)

for br in ["A","B","C"]:  # synchronization idle (DIAGNOSTIC, not additive)
    df[f"{br}_sync"] = mins(df[f"{br}_end"], df.join_ready)

df["stage_elapsed"]   = mins(df.fork_release, df.join_ready)
df["downstream_queue"]= mins(df.join_ready, df.final_start)
df["final_service"]   = mins(df.final_start, df.final_end)
df["total_flow_time"] = mins(df.arrival, df.final_end)

# Additive identity check (critical-path accounting, no double counting):
df["flow_check"] = (df.upstream_elapsed + df.stage_elapsed
                    + df.downstream_queue + df.final_service)
assert (df.flow_check == df.total_flow_time).all(), "critical-path identity broken"

show = ["case","upstream_elapsed","A_queue","A_service","B_queue","B_service",
        "C_queue","C_service","stage_elapsed","critical_branch",
        "downstream_queue","final_service","total_flow_time"]
print("== Case-level decomposition ==")
print(df[show].to_string(index=False))

# 4. Counterfactuals ---------------------------------------------------------
# Assumption A1: downstream availability held fixed; flow saving = reduction in
# join_ready, capped by the second-latest branch end (fork-join max logic).
def join_saving(row, A_mul=1.0, Bq_mul=1.0):
    a_end = row.A_start + pd.to_timedelta(row.A_service * A_mul, unit="m")
    b_start = row.fork_release + pd.to_timedelta(row.B_queue * Bq_mul, unit="m")
    b_end = b_start + pd.to_timedelta(row.B_service, unit="m")
    new_join = max(a_end, b_end, row.C_end)
    sav = (row.join_ready - new_join).total_seconds() / 60.0
    return max(0.0, sav)

df["cf1_saving"] = df.apply(lambda r: join_saving(r, A_mul=0.8), axis=1)   # A svc -20%
df["cf2_saving"] = df.apply(lambda r: join_saving(r, Bq_mul=0.5), axis=1)  # B queue -50%
# CF3: reduce downstream queue by 50% (uniform, no fork-join cap)
df["cf3_saving"] = 0.5 * df.downstream_queue

print("\n== Counterfactual per-case savings (min) ==")
print(df[["case","critical_branch","cf1_saving","cf2_saving","cf3_saving"]].to_string(index=False))
print("\n== Counterfactual aggregate ==")
agg = pd.DataFrame({
    "counterfactual": ["CF1 A_service -20%","CF2 B_queue -50%","CF3 downstream -50%"],

    "cases_benefiting": [(df.cf1_saving>0).sum(), (df.cf2_saving>0).sum(), (df.cf3_saving>0).sum()],
    "mean_per_case":   [df.cf1_saving.mean(), df.cf2_saving.mean(), df.cf3_saving.mean()],
})
print(agg.to_string(index=False))

inf = pd.DataFrame({

                    (df.critical_branch=="B").sum(),

# 7. Limits of inference

- **Causality is not identifiable.** The timestamps show *when* things happened, not *why*. We cannot prove that B's queue *causes* long flow times versus B queue and long flow being co-driven by a hidden arrival surge. The counterfactuals are structural (fork-join arithmetic), not causal guarantees; real interventions could shift arrival patterns.
- **Branch resource capacity is unobserved.** We see one A/B/C interval per case but not how many servers each branch has, nor whether B's queue is due to one busy machine or a scheduling policy. A_queue = 0 everywhere could mean A is over-provisioned *or* that A is simply released first; we cannot distinguish.
- **Work preemption / multitasking is invisible.** Complete-event, one-minute data cannot reveal whether a resource paused a branch to serve another case, split attention, or batched work. Service times may embed hidden interruptions.
- **Downstream "queue" vs release policy is genuinely ambiguous.** `final_start − join_ready` is 8–20 min every case. This could be true F1/F2 contention (a queue) *or* a fixed administrative release gate (policy) *or* travel/handoff time. The fairly uniform ~15 min, and the fact that it appears even in early low-load cases (F01 = 8, but F02/F03 already 15), is *weak* evidence of a policy component; the larger 20-min waits in busy later cases (F10, F11) suggest a real queue component. Both likely coexist. This directly conditions R2: if it is a policy, adding F capacity is wasted (§5).
- **Only 12 cases, one day, one time window.** All from a single morning ramp-up; queues generally grow through the morning (downstream rises F01→F11). Tail estimates and critical-branch frequencies are low-confidence and may not generalize to steady state or other shifts.
- **Ties and near-ties** (A vs B within 10–20 min in ~5 cases) mean the critical-branch attribution is fragile; small measurement error (±1 min rounding) could reassign criticality, so influence rankings should be read as central tendencies, not certainties.
- **No rework, exceptions, or abandonment** are observable; every case completes cleanly. Whether the process behaves the same under error conditions cannot be inferred.
