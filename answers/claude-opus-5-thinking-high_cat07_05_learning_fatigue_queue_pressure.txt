# 1. Competing mechanism table

Before the table, three structural facts about this log make every naive reading unsafe:

- **Collinearity.** `sequence_position_within_shift`, `minutes_since_shift_start`, clock time, and `queue_length_at_start` all move together within a shift. Time-on-task (fatigue) and load (queue pressure) are nearly the same regressor unless we exploit shifts where they *diverge*.
- **Monotone case-mix drift.** Mean complexity falls from 0.72 to 0.43 and high-priority share from 41% to 19% across the position bins. The early-shift service time is therefore partly a *harder work* effect, not a *cold worker* effect.
- **Divergent directions late in the shift.** Between bins 16–30 and 76+, service time keeps falling (13.6 → 10.8 min) while complexity also falls (0.54 → 0.43), yet error rate nearly triples (2.5% → 7.9%) and 7-day rework nearly triples (4.8% → 13.7%). Errors rise while the work gets *easier*. Whatever drives late-shift errors, it is not case difficulty.

# 2. Integrated explanation

The single-mechanism stories all fail. "Workers learn then get tired" cannot be right, because the early improvement coincides with the work becoming systematically easier, and the late degradation coincides with the work becoming *easier still* while errors triple. What the log actually shows is at least four processes superimposed on one shared time axis, plus two measurement artifacts that amplify the apparent pattern.

**The first half of the shift is mostly case-mix and load unwinding, with a real but smaller readiness component.** Positions 1–5 arrive with a 46-deep inherited queue, 0.72 mean complexity, and 41% high-priority share — the accumulated hard residue of the previous shift, which seniors then deliberately absorb ("senior resources take complex cases early"). An 18.8-minute mean service time on that mix is not a symptom of a cold worker; it is largely the correct amount of time for genuinely difficult work under contention. As complexity drifts down to 0.54 and the queue drains to 33, service time drops to 13.6 minutes almost mechanically. But not entirely: every resource group, including seniors with years of experience, shows a 4–5 minute first-20-events penalty, and — decisively — the error rate falls *with* service time over this range (3.4% → 2.5%). Speed and quality improving together is the one pattern queue pressure cannot produce, because pressure buys speed by spending accuracy. So there is a genuine warm-up effect, but it is better read as **state-dependent readiness** (context reload, re-establishing case knowledge, absorbing the handover) than as skill acquisition, and it is confounded upward by the hard early case-mix and the start-of-shift backlog.

**The second half is dominated by load, not by hours.** From position 51 onward the queue reverses direction, climbing 31 → 62 → 88 while service time continues to fall and error rate and 7-day rework climb steeply. The strongest evidence that this is load rather than fatigue is a natural experiment sitting in the shift table: the Early shift and the Day shift are the same length, so their workers reach the last two hours with the same time-on-task, but the Early shift's queue *falls* (61 → 42) while the Day shift's queue *rises* (38 → 75). Their last-two-hours error rates are 4.2% and 7.5%. Time-on-task held constant, error tracks the queue. The cross-sectional WIP table says the same thing without any shift-position involvement at all: within the *same* complexity band, going from low to high WIP cuts service time by roughly a quarter and multiplies error by 2.5–2.8×. That is a speed–accuracy tradeoff purchased under pressure, and it is the main engine of the late-shift picture.

**But fatigue is not zero, and refusing to name it would be as wrong as blaming everything on it.** The Early shift's late error rate of 4.2% is still 1.7 points above the mid-shift trough of 2.5%, on *easier* cases and a *falling* queue. Something degrades with hours worked independent of load. The Night shift's slow late service time (15.2 min, the slowest of any shift's final hours) on modest volume points the same way, though Night is so confounded with two-person staffing and an exception-heavy case mix that it cannot carry the argument. The honest reading is that fatigue is a real but second-order contributor that becomes *dangerous mainly in interaction* with load: it is when tired workers meet a rising queue that the error rate goes from 4.2% to 7.5%.

**Circadian effects are present but largely unidentifiable from these aggregates.** The Early shift opens at 06:00 in the tail of the circadian trough with a 48%-high-complexity backlog; the Night shift spans the trough entirely with 56% high complexity, two resources, and no senior or bot backup. Every claimed circadian effect here has a staffing or case-mix twin. Only the clock-time overlaps between shifts (14:00–17:00 shared by Day and Late; 09:00–14:00 shared by Early and Day) can separate clock hour from shift position, and only for staff who rotate across shift types.

**Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions.** Because "faster" has three different causes here, and only one of them is benign.

1. *Faster because easier.* Complexity falls 0.72 → 0.43 across the shift. Part of the speed-up is simply a lighter workload and carries no quality cost — visible in bins 16–30 and 31–50, where service time is low and error rate sits at its 2.5–2.7% floor.
2. *Faster because rushed.* Under high WIP, workers truncate optional verification, skim documents, accept the bot's risk classification without checking, and shorten callbacks. This buys speed by spending accuracy, which is why the WIP table shows falling service time and rising errors *at fixed complexity*, and why the effect is graded by experience: seniors, who have automated the core judgment, hold at 4.9% under high WIP; juniors, who still need deliberate effort for the same judgment, blow out to 12.4%. This is where the risk actually lives, and the worst cell in the entire dataset is high WIP × high complexity at 10.9% error — pressure and difficulty *together*.
3. *Faster because not actually done, or not actually measured.* Junior_H and Junior_I batch 9–18 Document Reviews late in the Day shift; a batch stamped in a few minutes produces a dozen implausibly short "service times" that do not represent a dozen real reviews. Simultaneously, cases whose work does not finish before the shift ends are handed over, so the longest, hardest events are systematically censored out of the tail of every shift, dragging measured late-shift service time down. Both make the late speed-up look larger than the true acceleration.

**And why errors rise more than they really do — partly.** Quality Check is deferred into bursts in the final 45 minutes. If `error_flag` is stamped when a defect is *detected* rather than when it is *committed*, then errors committed at position 20 get charged to position 78. Detection timing alone could manufacture much of the late spike. Two things stop us from dismissing the late degradation as pure artifact: 7-day rework, which is measured outside the shift and outside the QC-timing window, also nearly triples (4.8% → 13.7%); and the WIP table shows the same speed–error tradeoff cross-sectionally, with no shift-position axis involved. So the late degradation is real — but its *size* is inflated by detection timing, and its *attribution* to individuals is contaminated by batching, by handover censoring, and by bot-originated defects landing on whichever human touched the case next.

**Finally, causality between load and performance runs both ways.** `wip_at_start` is not an exogenous treatment. A rushing worker who commits errors generates rework, which lengthens the queue, which raises pressure on the next worker. Deferred Quality Checks park work that reappears as a burst. Understaffing the Day-shift peak converts a demand pattern into a quality problem. The queue is, in substantial part, produced by the organization's own routing, staffing, and QC-scheduling choices — which is exactly why the intervention and fairness discussion below cannot be framed as a question about individual worker stamina.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## 3.0 Preparation and data hygiene

```python
import pandas as pd, numpy as np, pm4py

df = pd.read_csv("claims_triage.csv", parse_dates=["time:timestamp", "start_timestamp",
                                                    "shift_start_time"])

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(df,
                             case_id="case:concept:name",
                             activity_key="concept:name",
                             timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
                             start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")

# Event-level service time (the unit of analysis for every model below).
log["svc_min"] = (log["time:timestamp"] - log["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
log["log_svc"] = np.log1p(log["svc_min"])

# Case-level service / waiting time and arrival-finish rates (the exogenous load driver).
log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)          # @@service_time, @@waiting_time, ...
log = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(log)           # @@arrival_rate, @@finish_rate

# Clock-time features, kept strictly separate from shift-position features.
log["clock_hour"]   = log["start_timestamp"].dt.hour + log["start_timestamp"].dt.minute / 60
log["dow"]          = log["start_timestamp"].dt.dayofweek
log["hours_on_task"]= log["minutes_since_shift_start"] / 60
log["is_bot"]       = log["org:resource"].eq("Bot_Risk")

feat, feat_names = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)   # case-level temporal features
```

Three hygiene checks that must run **before** any inference, because each one can invalidate a headline number:

```python
# (a) Shift-end right-censoring: hard events get handed over, not completed.
shift_end = log.groupby("shift_id")["time:timestamp"].transform("max")
log["near_shift_end"] = (shift_end - log["time:timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60 < 30
log["spans_shift_end"] = log.groupby("case:concept:name")["shift_id"].transform("nunique") > 1
censor_rate = log.groupby("sequence_position_bin")["spans_shift_end"].mean()

# (b) Implausible service times => batching / recording artifacts, not real speed.
floor = log.groupby(["concept:name"])["svc_min"].transform(lambda s: s.quantile(0.02))
log["suspicious_fast"] = log["svc_min"] <= np.maximum(floor, 0.5)

# (c) Unbalanced panel: which resources actually appear in the 76+ bin?
panel = log.pivot_table(index="org:resource", columns="pos_bin",
                        values="case:concept:name", aggfunc="count")
balanced = panel.dropna().index          # restrict late-vs-early comparisons to these
```

## 3.1 Within-resource comparisons

Every position, WIP, and shift effect must be estimated **within person**, because senior–complex matching and unbalanced participation across bins otherwise dominate.

```python
# Within-resource demeaning: removes all time-invariant worker ability.
for col in ["log_svc", "error_flag", "case_complexity_score", "wip_at_start"]:
    log[col + "_wr"] = log[col] - log.groupby("org:resource")[col].transform("mean")

# Even tighter: within resource x activity_family x shift-type cell.
cell = ["org:resource", "activity_family", "shift_type"]
log["log_svc_cell"] = log["log_svc"] - log.groupby(cell)["log_svc"].transform("mean")
```

Mixed-effects specification (statsmodels / `pymer4`), resources as fixed effects for identification and shift as a random intercept to absorb day-level shocks:

```python
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

svc = smf.mixedlm(
    "log_svc ~ bs(sequence_position_within_shift, df=4)"      # warm-up shape
    " + bs(hours_on_task, df=4)"                              # fatigue shape
    " + bs(wip_at_start, df=4) + queue_length_at_start"       # pressure
    " + case_complexity_score + I(case_complexity_score**2)"  # continuous complexity
    " + C(priority) + C(activity_family) + C(org:resource)"
    " + bs(clock_hour, df=5) + C(dow)",
    groups="shift_id", data=log[~log.suspicious_fast]).fit()

err = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ bs(sequence_position_within_shift, df=4) + bs(hours_on_task, df=4)"
    " + bs(wip_at_start, df=4) + case_complexity_score + C(priority)"
    " + C(activity_family) + C(org:resource) + bs(clock_hour, df=5)",
    data=log[~log.suspicious_fast]).fit(cov_type="cluster",
                                        cov_kwds={"groups": log.loc[~log.suspicious_fast,
                                                                    "shift_id"]})
```

**Decisive within-resource contrast:** for each resource, compare *their own* event #3 to *their own* event #80, matched on complexity, priority, activity family and WIP band. If the gap collapses under matching, the "learning curve" was case-mix.

## 3.2 Within-shift position effects, separated from time-on-task and clock time

The identification problem is that position, minutes-since-start and clock hour are collinear *within* a shift. The solution is the **shift-overlap design**: because Early/Day/Late/Night overlap in clock time, the same clock hour is observed at different shift positions.

```python
# Day (hours 5-8) and Late (hours 0-3) both cover 14:00-17:00.
overlap = pm4py.filter_time_range(log, "2024-01-01 14:00:00", "2024-12-31 17:00:00",
                                  mode="events")
# Same clock hour, opposite ends of the shift => position effect net of circadian.
overlap.groupby(["shift_type", pd.cut(overlap.hours_on_task, [0,2,4,6,8])]).agg(
    svc=("svc_min", "median"), err=("error_flag", "mean"),
    cx=("case_complexity_score", "mean"), wip=("wip_at_start", "mean"), n=("svc_min", "size"))
```

Then fit the cross-classified model `outcome ~ f(hours_on_task) + g(clock_hour) + resource FE`, restricted to rotating staff so the same person contributes to multiple (clock hour, position) cells. Visual companions:

```python
pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(log, "dotted_by_resource.png",
                            attributes=["time:timestamp", "case:concept:name", "org:resource"])
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(log, "dist_hours.png", distr_type="hours")
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(log, "dist_days.png",  distr_type="days_week")
```

The dotted chart makes batch stripes and end-of-shift Quality Check bursts immediately visible, which is faster than any statistic for spotting workarounds.

## 3.3 WIP / queue stratification — and instrumenting endogenous load

Stratify first (transparent), then instrument (causal).

```python
log["wip_band"] = pd.cut(log.wip_at_start, [-1, 15, 30, 50, 70, 90, np.inf])

# Fatigue test with load held constant: does time-on-task still hurt inside the LOW-WIP stratum?
low_wip = log[log.wip_at_start < 30]
fatigue_net_of_load = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ bs(hours_on_task, df=4) + case_complexity_score + C(priority)"
    " + C(activity_family) + C(org:resource) + bs(clock_hour, df=4)", data=low_wip).fit()
```

Because a slow or error-prone worker *creates* WIP, use exogenous inflow as an instrument:

```python
from linearmodels.iv import IV2SLS
# Instruments: arrival rate in the prior 60 min, prior-shift carryover, holiday/backlog shocks,
# and other-team WIP -- all shift demand without being caused by this worker's own speed.
iv = IV2SLS.from_formula(
    "log_svc ~ 1 + case_complexity_score + C(activity_family) + C(org:resource)"
    " + bs(clock_hour, df=4) + [wip_at_start ~ arrival_rate_60m + prior_shift_carryover"
    " + is_holiday + other_team_wip]", data=log).fit(cov_type="clustered", clusters=log.shift_id)
```

Also test whether 70 is a genuine breakpoint or a smooth dose–response, using segmented regression / change-point search over candidate thresholds and comparing BIC. This matters directly for intervention #3: an alert at a false threshold is worse than a continuous load indicator.

## 3.4 Complexity adjustment

Coarse two-band complexity control is not enough — the tables themselves flag this as the "interpretation issue."

```python
# Match early-shift and late-shift events on the full covariate profile.
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
X = pd.get_dummies(log[["case_complexity_score", "priority", "activity_family",
                        "resource_experience_months"]], drop_first=True)
ps = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(X, (log.sequence_position_within_shift > 50)).predict_proba(X)[:, 1]
log["ipw"] = np.where(log.sequence_position_within_shift > 50, 1/ps, 1/(1-ps))
# Re-estimate the position profile with these weights: early and late samples now have
# identical complexity/priority/activity/experience distributions.
```

Complement with unsupervised case-mix profiling, so that "complexity" is not reduced to one engineered score:

```python
clusters = pm4py.cluster_log(log)     # variant/behaviour-based case clusters
for k, sub in enumerate(clusters):
    print(k, pm4py.get_service_time(sub, aggregation_measure="median"))
# Then compare position/WIP effects WITHIN each cluster -- a case-mix-free replication.
```

# 4. Intervention options

Reading of the evidence that drives these evaluations: **queue pressure is the dominant modifiable driver**, case-mix explains most of the early-shift profile, fatigue is real but second-order and mainly matters in interaction with load, and a meaningful share of the measured pattern is batching and detection-timing artifact. Interventions that add capacity or smooth load where it peaks should therefore outperform interventions that constrain hours or discipline individuals.

### 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers
**Expected benefit:** Bounds the time-on-task component and protects against extreme overtime. Legitimate as a floor-level safety rule.
**Risk:** Poorly targeted. The Early shift shows that eight hours with a *falling* queue ends at 4.2% error while the Day shift's identical eight hours with a *rising* queue ends at 7.5% — the binding constraint is load, not duration. Worse, a hard cap converts unfinished work into handovers, which inflates the *next* shift's start-of-shift backlog and complexity spike (already the worst cell for service time), increases shift-boundary censoring, and can degrade quality through lost case context. Applied uniformly, it also removes flexibility where it is harmless.
**Monitoring:** Handover count and `spans_shift_end` rate per shift boundary; queue length at shift start/end; start-of-shift complexity share for the *receiving* shift; complexity-adjusted error by `hours_on_task` within WIP strata (the cap should shrink the fatigue slope, not just truncate the x-axis); total cases closed per day to detect throughput loss.

### 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift
**Expected benefit:** If warm-up is real, it avoids matching the hardest cases to the least-ready moment, and would reduce the 18.8-minute opening service time.
**Risk:** Plausibly *net negative* as stated, and the data hint at why. Complex cases are early because seniors deliberately take them early, and early error rate (3.4%, on complexity 0.72) is far better than late error rate (7.9%, on complexity 0.43). Pushing complex work later means colliding it with the high-WIP window — and high WIP × high complexity is the single worst cell in the dataset at 10.9% error. It also delays a 41%-high-priority cohort, which has SLA and customer-harm consequences the error metric does not capture, and it strands night-shift exceptions (56% high complexity in the first hour) that are urgent by nature.
**Monitoring:** If piloted, track complexity-adjusted error and service time for complex cases by the hour they are *started*; waiting time and SLA breach rate for high-priority cases (`@@waiting_time`); the high-WIP × high-complexity event count (this must not grow); senior utilisation in hour 1. **Preferred variant:** keep complex cases early but guarantee senior/experienced assignment and add a short warm-up buffer (2–3 simpler events) before the first complex case — this targets readiness without deferring urgent work into the pressure window.

### 3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70
**Expected benefit:** Best-aligned with the strongest evidence. Crossing into high WIP raises low-complexity error from 2.1% to 5.8% and high-complexity error from 4.2% to 10.9%, and is also the regime where Bot_Risk corrections spike. Making load visible in real time is a precondition for any load management.
**Risk:** An alert is information, not capacity. Without a bound playbook it produces alarm fatigue and may even *worsen* behaviour by signalling "go faster." The 70 threshold should be validated as a real breakpoint rather than assumed; if the relationship is a smooth dose–response, a continuous load index with graded triggers is better. Alerts must never be routed as individual performance signals.
**Monitoring:** Time-in-high-WIP minutes per shift; alert-to-mitigation latency and which mitigation fired; complexity-adjusted error in the 30 minutes after an alert versus matched un-alerted high-WIP periods; batching rate and `suspicious_fast` share after alerts (to detect the alert being answered with corner-cutting rather than help). Escalation actions should be pre-specified: pull in the on-call senior, defer *low-priority non-quality* work, stop routing complex cases to juniors, throttle bot routing.

### 4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias
**Expected benefit:** Removes the confound that makes all current worker comparisons unfair, and would deliver clean causal estimates of position, WIP and experience effects.
**Risk:** As a *production policy* this is close to reckless. Skill matching is protective: under high WIP, juniors err at 12.4% versus seniors at 4.9%, so randomly handing complex high-WIP cases to juniors predictably multiplies harm to real claimants. It also removes the senior-absorbs-the-backlog mechanism that keeps early-shift error low despite complexity 0.72, and it ignores skill/licensing constraints on activities such as Eligibility Decision and Specialist Referral.
**Monitoring / better design:** Randomise only *within eligible skill pools* (randomised tie-breaking among qualified, available resources), or randomise a small, low-risk stratum (low complexity, low priority) as a standing measurement experiment with a pre-registered stopping rule. Monitor error rate by (experience × complexity × WIP) cell with an automatic halt if any junior cell exceeds a pre-set threshold; track the assignment-complexity correlation to confirm selection has actually been reduced; verify covariate balance across resources.

### 5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours
**Expected benefit:** Directly targets the residual fatigue component that the Early shift isolates (4.2% versus a 2.5% trough on easier work). Short recovery breaks are among the better-evidenced interventions for sustained-attention error.
**Risk:** Timing conflict. The final two hours are exactly when the Day shift's queue peaks (38 → 75), so removing capacity there could raise WIP and trade a fatigue gain for a pressure loss — plausibly net-negative if unstaggered. Breaks can also be nominal in practice (workers keep working) or displaced into a post-break rush.
**Monitoring:** Run it as a **stepped-wedge** rollout across shifts/teams so the effect is identified rather than assumed. Track complexity-adjusted error and service time in 30-minute windows before and after each break; WIP trajectory during break periods (must not spike — stagger breaks and backfill); break compliance measured as actual inter-event gaps; end-of-shift carryover. Pair with intervention 7 so the capacity is replaced rather than removed.

### 6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift
**Expected benefit:** Removes the end-of-shift Quality Check burst that both consumes capacity in the peak window and clusters error detection into the last 45 minutes. It would also de-bias the late-shift error statistic.
**Risk:** This is the intervention closest to a false economy, and it must not become quality-check *removal*. Deferral lengthens detection latency, so defects escape further downstream where they are costlier and may reach the customer; it pushes work onto a receiving shift whose start-of-shift period is already the most loaded and complex; and low priority is not the same as low risk — a low-priority case can still carry a large eligibility error. There is also a moral-hazard channel: if checks are known to be deferrable, upstream care may erode.
**Monitoring:** Quality Check latency distribution and the share of checks older than a hard age cap; escaped-defect rate (errors found after Close Case) and 7-day rework; receiving shift's first-hour queue and complexity; Quality Check backlog age p90. **Preferred variant:** smooth quality checks continuously through the shift (a small WIP cap on pending checks), use risk-based sampling intensity rather than priority-based deferral, and impose a hard maximum deferral age. Never defer checks on high-complexity, high-WIP, or bot-classified cases — precisely the cohorts with 10.9% error.

### 7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks
**Expected benefit:** Likely the highest-value intervention on the list. It targets the worst-documented condition (Day shift, queue 38 → 75, 7.5% late error) with the resource whose quality is most pressure-robust (seniors 2.2% → 4.9% versus juniors 4.4% → 12.4%). It attacks the causal driver — load — rather than a symptom, reduces the high-WIP minutes that harm everyone, shields juniors from high-WIP complex work, and adds capacity for bot-classification verification exactly when bot corrections spike.
**Risk:** Cost, and senior availability is finite — pulling seniors from Early may re-create the problem elsewhere (Early clears the night backlog with 48% high-complexity first-hour load). Concentrating hard work on a few seniors risks their own fatigue and creates a single point of failure. Extra capacity can also mask chronic understaffing, and Late shift (queue 70 → 66, 6.8% error) has a similar need.
**Monitoring:** High-WIP minutes and peak queue length on the Day shift; complexity-adjusted error in the last two hours; junior exposure to high-WIP high-complexity events (should fall); senior utilisation and *their* late-shift error trend (watch for displaced fatigue); queue handed to the Late shift; cost per prevented rework case. Roll out as a staggered/difference-in-differences trial across days rather than a permanent change on faith.

### 8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates
**Expected benefit:** Essentially none, and it should be rejected outright.
**Risk:** The raw late-shift error rate is close to an unbiased measure of *management-created conditions*, not of worker effort. It confounds queue pressure that workers do not control, detection timing from deferred Quality Checks (errors committed early are recorded late), batching artifacts, bot-originated misclassifications attributed to the next human, and unbalanced exposure — only workers doing fast, simple work even reach position 76+, so the high-error bin is a selected population. Penalising it also creates predictable gaming with real customer harm: under-reporting errors, avoiding or rubber-stamping Quality Check, cherry-picking easy cases, dumping hard cases into handover at shift end, and refusing exception work. Juniors and night staff would be penalised hardest for structural exposure, which raises equity and possibly legal exposure. It would additionally destroy the data quality that every analysis above depends on.
**Monitoring (of the risk, if any variant were ever attempted):** self-reported error rate versus independently sampled audit error rate (divergence = suppression); Quality Check coverage and duration (falling durations = rubber-stamping); case selection patterns per resource (complexity of accepted versus declined/handed-over cases); shift-end handover spikes; escaped defects found post-closure. **Preferred alternative:** report only condition-adjusted, within-resource, batch-excluded, root-cause-attributed error metrics, use them exclusively for coaching, staffing and routing decisions, and pair individual feedback with the system indicators that show what conditions the person was working under.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker-level conclusions from this log are unfair by construction.** The two workers with the "worst" numbers in any naive ranking would be Junior_H and Junior_I — but they are the ones documented as batching Document Review late in the Day shift, i.e. improvising under a queue that management let climb from 38 to 75 while staffing stayed flat. Their behaviour is a symptom of a capacity decision, not a character trait. Night_L and Night_M would rank badly too, on two-person coverage of an exception-heavy, 56%-high-complexity caseload at the circadian nadir with no senior or bot support. In both cases the metric is measuring the assignment, not the person.

**Selection bias runs in both directions and neither is intuitive.** Seniors take complex cases early, so early-shift statistics blend the hardest cases with the strongest workers — this makes seniors look slow (service time inflated by complexity) while making the early period look high-quality (error suppressed by skill). Late in the shift the mix inverts: easier cases, but a selected subset of *fast* workers, since only they reach position 76+. Any comparison that does not use within-resource contrasts on a balanced panel with complexity, priority and activity-family adjustment is comparing different jobs and calling it different people. Handover censoring compounds this: unfinished hard work leaves the shift and reappears elsewhere, so the workers who take on the hardest cases lose credit for effort that was never completed under their name.

**Measurement error is large, systematic, and mostly points the same way.** Service time derived from `start_timestamp` to `time:timestamp` is not effort time: it absorbs multitasking under high WIP, interruptions, and — critically — batching, which can generate a dozen 20-second "reviews" that never happened as separate acts. Meanwhile `error_flag` is very likely stamped at *detection*, and Quality Check is deliberately clustered into the final 45 minutes, so a defect committed at position 20 can be booked against position 78. Any late-shift error statistic is therefore a mixture of when errors were made and when the organisation chose to look for them. Attributing errors to the last human who touched a case, rather than tracing them to the originating activity, mislabels root causes as individual failures. And volume differences mean small denominators: the 76+ bin and the Night shift will show wide swings that look like performance signals and are partly noise.

**A large share of the "performance problem" is queue pressure that management created.** The Day shift's error rate does not rise because workers deteriorate; it rises as the queue it was given grows past 70. The queue itself is the product of staffing choices against a known late-day demand peak, routing rules, the decision to defer Quality Checks into a burst, and the rework loop that pressure-induced errors feed back into the inflow. Holding individuals accountable for `wip_at_start` — a variable determined by arrival rates, upstream shifts and staffing levels — is holding them accountable for someone else's decision. There is also a reverse-causality trap in the other direction: because a struggling worker's own slowness feeds the queue, any correlational WIP–error estimate mixes cause and effect, which is exactly why the instrumented design in §3.3 matters before anyone acts on it.

**Automation quality is an organisational variable being charged to humans.** Bot_Risk classifies instantly, so it flatters every pooled service-time average while shifting the verification burden onto people. Human corrections of its output rise when the queue exceeds 70 — but a deterministic classifier's accuracy cannot depend on queue length. The rise must come from routing (more, or less suitable, cases pushed to the bot under load), from reduced human capacity to catch bad output, or from attribution. All three are design decisions. Framing the resulting downstream errors as human error inverts responsibility, and it also creates a real safety risk: under pressure, workers rationally accept bot output uncritically, and automation bias makes silent bot failure modes especially costly.

**Punitive use of error rates is both unjust and self-defeating.** Beyond the fairness problem, it changes the data. The predictable responses — not flagging errors, shortening or skipping Quality Check, avoiding complex and exception cases, handing hard work off at shift end — all harm claimants directly and simultaneously destroy the measurement system that would let anyone detect the harm. Error data gathered under threat becomes error data about willingness to report. The disparate impact falls on juniors, night staff, and anyone assigned high-WIP complex work, i.e. on exposure rather than conduct, which carries equity and legal risk in an employment context.

**Practical safeguards.** Use condition-adjusted, within-resource, batch-excluded, root-cause-attributed metrics only; never rank individuals on raw rates. Report uncertainty intervals and minimum volume thresholds, and suppress cells that are too small. Keep individual-level analytics separated from disciplinary processes by policy, and use them for coaching, staffing, routing and training. Aggregate to team level for accountability. Publish the case-mix and load conditions alongside any performance figure, so a number can never be read without the conditions that produced it. Involve workers in defining the indicators — they know which "errors" are recording artifacts. And apply the same scrutiny to Bot_Risk that is applied to people: automation gets a performance review too.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Design principles: every performance indicator is reported **condition-adjusted** (complexity, priority, activity family) and **within-resource** where individuals are involved; every indicator has an artifact-detection twin (an indicator whose job is to tell you the first indicator is lying); nothing individual-level feeds a disciplinary process. Refresh: near-real-time for the operational tiles (1–5), daily for quality (6–9), weekly for fairness and drift (10–13).

**1. Complexity-adjusted service time.** Median and p90 event service time by activity, resource group, shift, and shift-position bin, reported as a residual from the expected time given complexity, priority and activity family. *Source:* `svc_min`, `pm4py.get_service_time`, `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg`. *Why adjusted:* raw service time falling from 18.8 to 10.8 min is mostly case-mix, and reading it as productivity is the core error this dashboard exists to prevent. *Alert:* residual service time falling while error residual rises — the signature of rushing, not improvement.

**2. Waiting time and flow efficiency.** Case waiting time versus service time (`pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`: `@@waiting_time` / `@@service_time`), broken out by priority and by shift boundary, plus SLA breach rate for high-priority cases. *Why:* it is the customer-facing cost that throughput metrics hide, and it catches interventions (such as deferring complex cases or quality checks) that improve worker-side numbers by making claimants wait.

**3. WIP and queue pressure, with time-in-danger.** Live `wip_at_start` / `queue_length_at_start` curves per shift, plus **minutes per shift spent above the high-WIP threshold**, peak queue, and queue at shift start/end. *Source:* raw columns plus `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` (`@@arrival_rate` vs `@@finish_rate`) to show whether inflow is outrunning capacity. *Why:* the dominant modifiable driver; the Early-versus-Day contrast lives here. *Alert:* WIP crossing threshold, and arrival rate exceeding finish rate for a sustained window — the leading indicator of the whole late-shift syndrome.

**4. Error rate, adjusted and decomposed by detection latency.** Condition-adjusted error rate by resource group, WIP band, shift and position — reported **twice**: by the time of the event that *caused* the error (root-cause attribution via the case's DFG) and by the time of detection. Alongside it, the **error detection latency** distribution. *Why:* the gap between the two curves quantifies how much of the late-shift spike is deferred Quality Check rather than degraded work. Any error figure without its detection-latency twin is uninterpretable here.

**5. Rework rate and rework latency.** 7-day rework share and Rework Correction volume by originating activity and resource group (`pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity`), plus escaped defects found after Close Case, and rework-driven inflow as a share of total inflow. *Why:* rework is measured outside the shift, so it is the artifact-resistant quality signal that validates or refutes the in-shift error spike; rework-driven inflow also exposes the feedback loop where today's pressure errors become tomorrow's queue.

**6. Case-mix and complexity drift index.** Mean and distribution of `case_complexity_score`, high-priority share, and activity-family mix by shift hour and position bin, plus a single multivariate drift score measuring how far each hour's case mix departs from baseline. *Why:* it is the guardrail on every other tile — when drift is high, raw comparisons for that window are automatically flagged as non-comparable in the UI. It also directly monitors the "early-shift cases are more complex" fact that naive readings ignore.

**7. Batching and recording-artifact indicators.** Share of events inside a discovered batch by type (`pm4py.discover_batches`), longest consecutive same-activity run per resource per shift, share of events at the service-time floor (`suspicious_fast`), and the batch rate as a function of WIP. *Why:* separates real speed from stamped speed, and makes the Junior_H/Junior_I pattern visible as a **pressure symptom** rather than a personal infraction. *Alert:* rising batch share concurrent with rising WIP — escalate capacity, not discipline.

**8. Quality Check timing and coverage.** Distribution of Quality Check events across the shift, share occurring in the final 45 minutes, Quality Check coverage rate by risk stratum, pending-check backlog age (p50/p90), and mean check duration over time. *Why:* it monitors both the end-of-shift burst that biases error timing and the corner-cutting failure mode; falling check durations with stable coverage is the rubber-stamping signature. A hard floor on coverage for high-complexity, high-WIP and bot-classified cases is enforced here.

**9. Handover and queue-ownership changes.** Number of resource handovers per case, shift-boundary carryover count and complexity, `spans_shift_end` (censoring) rate, ping-pong/rework loops between resources, and a handover-of-work social network per shift pair. *Source:* `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` on the resource dimension. *Why:* handovers are where context and accountability are lost; this tile is also the early-warning system for shift-cap and deferral interventions, whose main failure mode is exporting work across a boundary.

**10. Worker-load fairness.** Per-resource event count, complexity-weighted load, high-priority share, exposure minutes to high-WIP conditions, and share of complex work — summarised as inequality measures (Gini or ratio of p90 to p10) across resources within a shift, plus junior exposure to the high-WIP × high-complexity cell. *Why:* the "worst performers" in this log are largely the most adversely exposed workers; this tile makes exposure itself the managed quantity and is the primary check that routing changes have not simply moved risk onto juniors.

**11. Automation quality and routing.** Bot_Risk share of Risk Classification, human correction rate for bot output **by WIP band**, downstream rework for bot-touched versus matched bot-free cases, and bot routing share as a function of load. *Why:* it holds automation to the same standard as people and prevents bot-originated defects from being charged to the next human. *Alert:* correction rate rising with WIP — that is a routing or verification-capacity problem to fix in the system.

**12. Time-on-task versus clock-time panel.** A cross-classified heatmap of adjusted error and adjusted service time by `hours_on_task` (rows) × `clock_hour` (columns), populated only where shifts overlap, plus break-recovery deltas (performance in the 30 minutes after gaps > 20 min). *Source:* `pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph` for the raw temporal distributions; the adjusted panel from the §3.2 model. *Why:* this is the single tile that keeps fatigue, circadian and position effects visually distinguishable instead of collapsing them into "later is worse."

**13. Intervention tracking and gaming watch.** For each active intervention: pre-registered primary indicator, rollout arm (stepped-wedge/DiD cohort), effect estimate with uncertainty, plus a fixed panel of gaming counter-indicators — self-reported versus audit-sampled error divergence, Quality Check duration trend, resource case-selection profile, shift-end handover spikes, and escaped defects. *Why:* each intervention above has a plausible way to look successful while making things worse; this tile is where that is caught.

**Cross-cutting dashboard rules.** No individual-level tile displays a raw rate without its condition-adjusted counterpart, its confidence interval, and its exposure profile. Cells below a minimum volume are suppressed rather than shown as noisy point estimates. Comparisons default to within-resource and to a balanced panel of resources. Adjusted views exclude batched and floor-service-time events, with the excluded volume shown so nobody mistakes exclusion for absence. And every quality indicator is paired with a throughput indicator, so that no improvement in speed can be booked as a win without checking what it cost in defects, waiting time, and downstream rework.
