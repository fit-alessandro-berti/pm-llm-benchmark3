# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

Here, **core** means more than “the largest count.” A core activity should normally be sustained outside exceptional substitution periods, form a meaningful part of the resource’s regular workload, align with the resource’s specialization or authority, and represent responsibility for an operational outcome. A peripheral activity may be a handoff, consultation, rework contribution, learning assignment, emergency substitution, or administrative completion. 

**Automated intake and precheck.** For Nora, A1 and A2 are unambiguously core by frequency and operating design. The qualification “precheck” is important: the notes describe automated entitlement prechecking, while human agents also perform A2. Unless the log distinguishes automated precheck from final human validation, the same activity name is covering different semantics. Nora has no evidenced peripheral work, but the handoff from Nora to human agents is a critical organizational boundary.

**Permanent front office.** A1, A2, A4, and A12 are core for Amira and Ben. Their activity profiles and formal titles support one broad role, but Amira represents an experienced-generalist tier within it: she has more diagnosis, pricing, negotiation, implementation, quality, and high-complexity exposure. Those activities are peripheral to the shared front-office role, yet may be core to Amira’s informal escalation function. The data do not by themselves establish a separate formal role for her.

**Temporary routine-capacity pool.** The same four activities are operationally core for Kai and Lina, but their scope is narrower. Their lack of certifications, near-exclusive low-complexity workload, and absence from critical work indicate restricted assignment rather than full interchangeability with permanent agents. The 17 reopens linked to incomplete validation notes are evidence of a control or training weakness, not proof that every temporary-agent validation was defective. Performing A2 frequently under a routing policy does not establish the same judgment authority as a certified permanent agent.

**Technical resolution.** A3, A5, and A10 are strongly core for both Chen and Dina. Their occasional A6, A11, A13, and A14 work is plausibly peripheral integration around technical cases. Chen’s higher critical-case exposure, security adjacency, and median complexity support either a senior tier or a specialized assignment pattern. Dina’s lower-complexity mix could indicate developing competence, deliberate routing, or both. Similarity of activity proportions therefore supports a common functional family, not necessarily identical responsibility.

**Security specialist.** A6 is Erik’s core even though it appears only 96 times. Its importance comes from exclusivity, certification, and risk exposure rather than volume. His diagnostic, configuration, implementation, quality, reopen, and knowledge activities are best understood as peripheral work needed to integrate security decisions into the resolution process. The A6 events performed by Chen, Dina, Mateo, and Ivan may represent preparation, low-risk screening, consultation, or genuine substitution; the summarized log does not identify which of them has final security authority.

**Commercial specialist.** A7 and A8 are Fatima’s core activities. Her A2, A9, A12, and small technical counts are peripheral to commercial exception handling. The 16 A9 events should not automatically be read as final approval: they might be recommendations, preparation, co-processing, or system-recording actions. Hana’s much larger A9 concentration and approval certification provide stronger authority evidence.

**Senior front-office/commercial bridge.** Giorgio has two layers. His primary core remains front-office resolution, but A7 and A8 are too substantial to dismiss as isolated noise. During Fatima’s six-day absence he performed 31 A7 and 18 A8 events; over all ten weeks he performed 72 and 88 respectively. Thus, commercial-facing work existed outside the explicitly described absence episode, although not every A8 negotiation necessarily concerned a pricing exception. A7 is best treated as a standing shadow competence that became a temporary substitute function during Fatima’s absence.

**Supervisory authority.** A9 is Hana’s core. A13, A8, A11, and her limited participation elsewhere are peripheral manifestations of escalation oversight. Her case mix and formal approval certification matter more than pure activity similarity. Mateo’s preliminary A9 work during Hana’s workshop is explicitly temporary and partly subject to recheck; it should not be converted into permanent approval authority by a clustering algorithm.

**Quality and knowledge stewardship.** A11 and A14 are jointly core for Ivan and Julia. Their A10, A12, A5, and A3 events are peripheral technical or closure support. The sequence fragments place quality checking after configuration and implementation, which makes the role a process gate, while A14 represents organizational learning. The data support a gatekeeping function but do not specify the exact power to reject, return, or formally sign off work.

**Incident coordination.** Mateo’s core cannot be defined by a single activity count. It is the combination of reopen ownership, cross-functional routing, referral filtering, preliminary escalation processing, and participation across specialist boundaries. A13 and escalation-related A9 are visible anchor activities, but his role is relational. The sequence fragments use the label `Coordinate`, even though no separate coordination activity appears in the A1–A14 activity dictionary. That inconsistency should be resolved in the event schema before exact activity-level role mining: `Coordinate` may be an omitted activity, a coarse description of several recorded activities, or an unlogged coordination action.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

## R07_Giorgio as a commercial shadow role

Giorgio is not merely a front-office agent who happened to touch pricing once. He has pricing certification, 72 A7 events, 88 A8 events, and a 0.71 activity similarity with Fatima. The absence episode confirms that management already treats him as a substitute: he absorbed 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations over six working days.

The evidence nevertheless does not justify calling him fully equivalent to Fatima. Median pricing duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes during the substitution period. That change may reflect lower specialization, heavier workload, more difficult cases, or disruption caused by the absence; the summary does not isolate the cause. It is therefore evidence of a performance difference under substitution, not a clean estimate of Giorgio’s competence.

This shadow role has clear benefits: commercial resilience, continuity for customers already known to front office, and a development path for senior agents. Risks include slower handling, inconsistent exception reasoning, hidden delegation of commercial authority, and overloading a person whose primary role remains front-office service. The role is not inherently improper; it should be made explicit through bounded authorization, training, substitution flags, and post-review rules.

## R13_Mateo as a cross-functional escalation broker

Mateo’s activity distribution is unusually broad, and his case mix is among the most complex. He appears in reopen, approval, security, pricing, negotiation, technical, quality, validation, and closure contexts. This pattern is consistent with an incident coordinator whose value is to connect functions rather than execute one specialist activity repeatedly.

The Enterprise Security episode illustrates both sides of brokerage. Filtering low-risk referrals was associated with fewer false-escalation complaints, suggesting that Mateo may have reduced unnecessary demand on Erik. At the same time, two critical cases waited more than two days. The evidence does not prove that triage caused either outcome, but it identifies a risk frontier: aggressive filtering can improve specialist focus while increasing the danger of delayed or misprioritized critical cases.

His preliminary approval work during Hana’s workshop is also a bridge, not automatically a transfer of authority. Thirteen of 21 approvals were rechecked. A defensible interpretation is that Mateo could perform triage or provisional clearance while Hana retained final accountability. Management should distinguish `preliminary_approval`, `final_approval`, and `recheck` in the event data instead of recording all three as A9.

## R05_Erik as a rare but critical specialist

Erik is the clearest example of why event frequency is not organizational importance. He has only 170 events, but 96 are security reviews, his median case complexity is 0.86, and 55 cases are critical. His role protects against low-frequency, high-consequence risks.

His limited technical and quality activity is not evidence that security is peripheral. It more plausibly reflects the end-to-end work required to understand, implement, and document a security decision. Nor do the A6 events of other resources necessarily make those resources security substitutes: they could perform screening or supporting checks while Erik retains formal sign-off.

The benefits of specialization are expertise and consistent risk judgment. The corresponding risks are single-person dependency, day-shift coverage gaps, queues during incident surges, and the possibility that automated or coordinator filtering hides urgent work. Resilience should therefore focus on a qualified second line, not on distributing security reviews indiscriminately to anyone with adjacent activity counts.

## R14_Nora as an automation-boundary role

Nora forms a bridge between system automation and the human organization. Because the bot performs more registration events than any human and also records A2, it can heavily shape mined roles and handover networks.

The key ambiguity is semantic. The notes call Nora’s A2 an entitlement precheck, whereas human agents appear to perform substantive validation. A bot and a human can therefore execute the same logged label while exercising very different discretion. The analysis should be run both with and without Nora, and the source system should preferably distinguish `Automated Entitlement Precheck` from `Human Entitlement Validation`.

## R11_Kai and R12_Lina as a temporary capacity bridge

Kai and Lina bridge seasonal demand and the permanent front-office workforce. Their nearly identical profiles and low-complexity routing show a deliberate capacity strategy rather than accidental clustering.

This arrangement is useful when demand is high, but the reopen episode indicates that closure throughput cannot be considered separately from documentation quality. Their work should remain explicitly marked as temporary or supervised until competence is demonstrated across case outcomes, not inferred from event volume. A checklist, sampled review of entitlement notes, and escalation rules for uncertain cases would preserve the capacity benefit without treating substitution as permanent competence.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

PM4Py’s organizational-role miner should be interpreted carefully: it discovers **sets of activities performed by similar multisets of resources**, and returns resource importance within those activity roles. Its similarity, handover, and working-together functions return social-network objects rather than formal authority assignments. Current PM4Py interfaces accept explicit activity, resource, timestamp, and case-column keys; `Role` objects expose `activities` and `originator_importance`, while SNA objects expose `connections` and `is_directed`. 

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from pathlib import Path
from typing import Any

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py


CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

COMPLEXITY = "case_complexity"
CUSTOMER_SEGMENT = "customer_segment"
PRODUCT_LINE = "product_line"
SUBSTITUTION = "temporary_substitution"
ESCALATION = "escalation_level"


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load, validate, and format the event log
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

raw = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution.csv")

required_columns = {
    CASE,
    ACTIVITY,
    TIMESTAMP,
    RESOURCE,
    COMPLEXITY,
    CUSTOMER_SEGMENT,
    PRODUCT_LINE,
    SUBSTITUTION,
    ESCALATION,
}
missing = required_columns.difference(raw.columns)
if missing:
    raise ValueError(f"Missing required columns: {sorted(missing)}")

raw[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(
    raw[TIMESTAMP],
    errors="raise",
    utc=True,
)

# Normalize the substitution indicator without silently treating unknown
# values as False.
if not pd.api.types.is_bool_dtype(raw[SUBSTITUTION]):
    truth_map = {
        "true": True,
        "1": True,
        "yes": True,
        "y": True,
        "false": False,
        "0": False,
        "no": False,
        "n": False,
    }
    normalized = (
        raw[SUBSTITUTION]
        .astype("string")
        .str.strip()
        .str.lower()
        .map(truth_map)
    )
    if normalized.isna().any():
        bad_values = raw.loc[normalized.isna(), SUBSTITUTION].unique()
        raise ValueError(
            f"Unrecognized temporary_substitution values: {bad_values}"
        )
    raw[SUBSTITUTION] = normalized.astype(bool)

# Keep the original complexity value. Create a numeric/ordinal helper only
# for summaries; it is not a competence score.
numeric_complexity = pd.to_numeric(raw[COMPLEXITY], errors="coerce")
ordinal_complexity = (
    raw[COMPLEXITY]
    .astype("string")
    .str.strip()
    .str.lower()
    .map({"low": 1.0, "medium": 2.0, "high": 3.0, "critical": 4.0})
)

if numeric_complexity.notna().sum() >= ordinal_complexity.notna().sum():
    raw["_complexity_score"] = numeric_complexity
else:
    raw["_complexity_score"] = ordinal_complexity

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
)

# case_complexity is described as a case attribute. Verify that the export
# does not assign contradictory values within one case.
complexity_values_per_case = (
    log.groupby(CASE, observed=True)[COMPLEXITY]
    .nunique(dropna=False)
)
if (complexity_values_per_case > 1).any():
    inconsistent = complexity_values_per_case[
        complexity_values_per_case > 1
    ].index.tolist()
    raise ValueError(
        "case_complexity varies within these cases; resolve lineage before "
        f"role analysis: {inconsistent[:10]}"
    )


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Run PM4Py organizational mining
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

role_kwargs = {
    "activity_key": ACTIVITY,
    "resource_key": RESOURCE,
    "timestamp_key": TIMESTAMP,
    "case_id_key": CASE,
}
network_kwargs = {
    "resource_key": RESOURCE,
    "timestamp_key": TIMESTAMP,
    "case_id_key": CASE,
}

mined_roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log,
    **role_kwargs,
)

activity_similarity = (
    pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
        log,
        **role_kwargs,
    )
)

handover_network = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    log,
    beta=0,
    **network_kwargs,
)

working_together_network = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log,
    **network_kwargs,
)

# Optional: useful for detecting A -> B -> A patterns in which one resource
# temporarily performs work between two events by another resource.
subcontracting_network = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    log,
    n=2,
    **network_kwargs,
)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Convert PM4Py role and network results to tabular form
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

def roles_to_frame(roles: list[Any]) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Flatten PM4Py roles to resource-role rows.

    'mined_importance' is an observed contribution to an activity role.
    It must not be renamed 'competence', 'authority', or 'formal role'.
    """
    rows: list[dict[str, Any]] = []

    for role_id, role in enumerate(roles):
        # Current PM4Py Role objects:
        if hasattr(role, "activities"):
            activities = list(role.activities)
            importance = dict(role.originator_importance)
        # Compatibility with older nested-list output:
        else:
            activities = list(role[0])
            importance = dict(role[1])

        total_importance = float(sum(importance.values())) or 1.0

        for resource, weight in importance.items():
            rows.append(
                {
                    "candidate_role_id": role_id,
                    "role_activities": tuple(sorted(activities)),
                    RESOURCE: resource,
                    "mined_importance": float(weight),
                    "within_role_importance_share": (
                        float(weight) / total_importance
                    ),
                }
            )

    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def sna_to_frame(sna: Any, metric_name: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Convert a PM4Py SNA object's edge dictionary to a DataFrame.
    """
    rows = [
        {
            "source": source,
            "target": target,
            "weight": float(weight),
            "metric": metric_name,
            "directed": bool(sna.is_directed),
        }
        for (source, target), weight in sna.connections.items()
    ]

    result = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    if not result.empty:
        result = result.sort_values("weight", ascending=False)
    return result


role_membership = roles_to_frame(mined_roles)
similarity_edges = sna_to_frame(
    activity_similarity,
    "activity_profile_similarity",
)
handover_edges = sna_to_frame(
    handover_network,
    "handover_of_work",
)
working_together_edges = sna_to_frame(
    working_together_network,
    "working_together",
)
subcontracting_edges = sna_to_frame(
    subcontracting_network,
    "subcontracting",
)

# For directed handovers, inspect the share of each source resource's
# outgoing handovers rather than only raw counts.
if not handover_edges.empty:
    outgoing_total = handover_edges.groupby("source")["weight"].transform("sum")
    handover_edges["source_normalized_weight"] = (
        handover_edges["weight"] / outgoing_total
    )


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Build case-mix and substitution context without event-length bias
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

def mode_or_na(series: pd.Series) -> Any:
    modes = series.dropna().mode()
    return modes.iloc[0] if not modes.empty else pd.NA


# A long case creates more events. Therefore, summarize complexity once
# per resource-case combination rather than once per event.
resource_case_context = (
    log.groupby([RESOURCE, CASE], as_index=False, observed=True)
    .agg(
        case_complexity=(COMPLEXITY, "first"),
        complexity_score=("_complexity_score", "first"),
        substitution_case=(SUBSTITUTION, "max"),
        substitution_event_share=(SUBSTITUTION, "mean"),
        escalation_mode=(ESCALATION, mode_or_na),
    )
)

resource_case_summary = (
    resource_case_context
    .groupby(RESOURCE, as_index=False, observed=True)
    .agg(
        distinct_cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
        median_case_complexity=("complexity_score", "median"),
        p90_case_complexity=(
            "complexity_score",
            lambda values: values.quantile(0.90),
        ),
        substitution_case_share=("substitution_case", "mean"),
    )
)

resource_event_summary = (
    log.groupby(RESOURCE, as_index=False, observed=True)
    .agg(
        event_count=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
        substitution_event_share=(SUBSTITUTION, "mean"),
        distinct_activities=(ACTIVITY, "nunique"),
    )
)

resource_context = resource_case_summary.merge(
    resource_event_summary,
    on=RESOURCE,
    how="outer",
)

enriched_candidate_roles = role_membership.merge(
    resource_context,
    on=RESOURCE,
    how="left",
)

# This table joins mined functional-role evidence with complexity and
# substitution exposure. It still does not contain formal authority.
print(
    enriched_candidate_roles.sort_values(
        ["candidate_role_id", "mined_importance"],
        ascending=[True, False],
    ).to_string(index=False)
)


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Keep formal authority in a separate HR/RACI source
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

# Example external master-data fields:
# org:resource, formal_title, home_team, certifications,
# approval_authority, security_signoff_authority, employment_type
master_path = Path("resource_master.csv")

if master_path.exists():
    resource_master = pd.read_csv(master_path)
```

Several specific actions follow from the evidence:

1. **Commercial resilience should be formalized, not assumed.** Giorgio already provides meaningful commercial support, but the duration increase during Fatima’s absence indicates that backup capacity is not identical to specialist capacity. A bounded deputy model, with thresholds for mandatory Fatima or supervisor review, is more defensible than either prohibiting Giorgio’s role or treating him as fully interchangeable.

2. **Security capacity should be protected from frequency-based cuts.** Erik’s volume is low relative to Nora or front-office staff because security interventions are selective. Reducing his capacity because of event counts would ignore his critical-case concentration. A second qualified reviewer or an on-call arrangement is preferable to broad, unqualified redistribution.

3. **Mateo’s brokerage should have service-level controls.** Referral filtering can reduce unnecessary escalation, but critical referrals need explicit maximum waiting times and override paths. His preliminary approval role should be logged as provisional so that the organization can distinguish useful delegation from unauthorized final decisions.

4. **Temporary throughput should be evaluated with downstream consequences.** Kai and Lina add substantial simple-case capacity, but closure counts alone overstate success when incomplete notes later create reopen work. Management should track first-time-right closure, reopen severity, rework ownership, and review effort.

5. **Automation should be treated as a different actor class.** Nora’s productivity cannot be compared directly with humans, and its A2 events should not automatically contribute to the same entitlement role. Separate semantic labels will produce more interpretable roles and handovers.

At least four cautions apply before changing staffing:

* **Mined roles are descriptive, not grants of authority.** Assignment policy determines who gets opportunities to perform activities. A resource may lack an activity because work is never routed to them, not because they are incapable; conversely, frequent execution may occur under supervision without independent authority.
* **Case mix is endogenous.** Chen, Erik, Hana, and Mateo receive harder cases by design. Raw speed, volume, or similarity comparisons would confound role, workload, escalation, and complexity.
* **Substitution periods are abnormal operating conditions.** Fatima’s absence and Hana’s workshop reveal resilience mechanisms, but do not establish permanent equivalence. Workload pressure and unfamiliar cases may explain part of the observed performance.
* **Network centrality is not automatically organizational value.** A highly connected resource may be a valuable broker, an avoidable bottleneck, a mandatory control point, or simply the person through whom the information system routes cases. Management must examine outcomes and decision rights before redesigning the network.

The appropriate managerial model is therefore a **layered role architecture**: stable functional roles, documented proficiency tiers, explicit decision authority, and controlled shadow roles for resilience. It explains the observed behavior without forcing Giorgio, Mateo, Erik, the temporary agents, or Nora into misleadingly clean activity-frequency clusters.
