# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

For the following **literal endpoint calculations**, assume: **A1**, queue arrival means the case is ready for this activity; **A2**, relevant clocks are aligned; and **A3**, the recorded fields are interpreted at face value solely to expose their consequences. A1 and A2 are required for cross-system queue waiting, but are not established by the fragments. A3 is deliberately questionable for several clusters.

Define recorded waiting as `start − queue_arrival`, recorded elapsed service as `complete − start`, and recording lag as `recorded_at − complete`. Actual touch time additionally requires evidence of active work and pauses. Sum of case elapsed times and union of their intervals measure different things; neither automatically equals labor consumption.

| Cluster | Mean recorded waiting, min | Mean recorded elapsed service, min | Sum of case elapsed times, min | Union of positive case intervals, min | Observed completion-to-insertion lag |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| G1 | 4.25 | 3.00 | 12 | 3 | 2–3 seconds |
| G2 | 12.25 | 17.50 | 70 | 27 | 1 minute each |
| G3 | 19.50 | 16.75 | 67 | 28 | 1 minute each |
| G4 | 13.00 | 4.00 | 20 | 20 | 3–6 seconds |
| G5 | 15.25 | 17.50 | 70 | 30 | 1 minute each |
| G6 | 103.43 | 0.00 | 0 | 0 | 1 minute each |
| G7 | −4.20 | 15.00 | 75 | 15 | 1–5 minutes |
| G8 | 48.20 | 7.00 | 35 | 35 | 2–6 seconds |

**G1.** If these are genuine fixture-cycle endpoints, the waiting values describe waiting until loading/cycle release, and three minutes is each item’s residence in that cycle. The cell is occupied for three minutes, not twelve. Twelve item-minutes can correctly describe four occupied fixture slots; using them against a one-cell, three-minute denominator would falsely imply 400% utilization. Conversely, assuming serial operation with three minutes of exclusive cell effort per item would understate batch capacity. Four items per three-minute cycle is an observed within-cycle output rate, not an estimate of sustained shift throughput. If the labeling head visits items serially, common start understates later items’ pre-label waiting and cycle duration overstates their individual touch times. Common completion is still meaningful for fixture release. Bottleneck analysis needs load/unload, fixture availability and upstream/downstream observations. Conformance should distinguish a shared cycle from impossible overlap on an exclusive one-item machine.

**G2.** A group-start timestamp moves waiting inside the group into apparent service. Consequently, it likely understates waiting to individual inspection and overstates individual touch time. As a **conditional serial scenario**, assume the first item truly starts at 10:00, each completion is accurate, and the next item starts immediately after the previous one finishes, without interruption. Durations would then be 8, 6, 7 and 6 minutes: mean 6.75 rather than 17.50. Waiting would be 18, 22, 25 and 27 minutes: mean 23.00 rather than 12.25. These are scenario calculations, not recovered observations. Both decompositions retain the same arrival-to-completion elapsed times. Summing 70 case-minutes over the 27-minute span falsely suggests 259% occupation of a single exclusive inspector if starts denote group opening. Completion-based output timing can remain usable if those buttons reflect actual finishes. A model may otherwise identify slow inspection or excessive multitasking when the issue is within-group queuing, and resource conformance may wrongly flag four simultaneous exclusive tasks.

**G3.** If individual starts are genuine, the 19.50-minute mean waiting to first review is not necessarily biased by later bulk completion. Deferred finishes instead inflate elapsed review time and completed-review WIP, shift physical completions toward 11:30, and can make review appear to be the service bottleneck. For illustration only, if work proceeds serially and each review finishes at the next review’s start, with the last finishing at 11:30, durations would be 8, 7, 7 and 6 minutes, averaging 7.00. The log’s 16.75-minute mean would then include post-review holding. The 67 case-minutes cannot simply be interpreted as 67 worker-minutes within 28 elapsed minutes. However, if the activity is defined as review-through-authorized-release, the common finish may correctly represent a business gate; the delay then belongs in case flow time while remaining distinct from reviewer effort. Conformance needs separate review-finished and released events: a successor could validly begin after physical review but appear premature relative to a deferred finish.

**G4.** Under validated endpoint and clock semantics, waiting of 7, 10, 13, 16 and 19 minutes and four-minute service intervals are defensible. The resource is continuously occupied for the observed twenty-minute run if the intervals denote exclusive execution. This does not establish shift utilization: the available-time denominator and work outside the fragment are missing. All five items arrive before processing begins, so initial backlog alone does not prove a batching policy. Treating the run as one twenty-minute operation per item would inflate service, while moving every start to 12:05 would suppress later queue waiting. Five items finish over the run; physical batch intent does not change their count. The growing waits reflect serial clearance of this burst, not proof of a persistent pack-station bottleneck. Serial resource conformance is supported, but a mandatory group rule is not.

**G5.** The 15.25-minute mean waiting is usable only as waiting to whatever each `start` denotes; if it means opening a case before active attention, active-service waiting is understated. The 17.50-minute mean interval length may include pauses, information waits and work switching. If accounts are mistaken for people, analysts appear overutilized: 70 open-case minutes occur in a thirty-minute union. Even dividing by three analysts gives only a conditional 77.8% interval-based ratio, not validated labor utilization. In particular, all four cases are open from 13:12 to 13:20, so three continuously working exclusive analysts cannot account for every open interval as active effort. Identity splitting alone is insufficient; inactive segments or nonexclusive activity semantics are required. Case completion counts may remain valid, but per-person throughput and bottleneck attribution are not identifiable. An apparent resource-conformance violation can be caused by pooling and open-case semantics rather than employee behavior.

G5 also contains an ordering discrepancy worth investigating: source sequence 3051→3052 corresponds to insertion time 13:26→13:21, and 3053→3054 to 13:31→13:23. These are two reversals in `recorded_at` relative to source insertion order. They could reflect transport reordering between source and destination, differing clock domains or an incorrect field mapping. They are not evidence that a worker executed cases in sequence-number order. If both fields claim ordering in the same database/clock domain, their stated meanings conflict.

**G6.** The recorded waits are 175, 148, 122, 110, 76, 58 and 35 minutes, averaging 103.43, while every recorded service duration is zero. This combination is a direct consequence of overwriting both lifecycle endpoints with closure time. Suppose, as a **hypothesis**, actual work starts at `a`, finishes at `b`, and is bulk entered at `T`, with `queue_arrival ≤ a ≤ b ≤ T`. Then:

`recorded wait = T − queue_arrival = actual queue wait + actual service + post-work entry delay`, while `recorded service = T − T = 0`.

Thus entry delay and even real service are charged to the queue, while observed service utilization collapses toward zero. Dividing output by this duration gives an undefined or meaningless service rate; seven closures at 17:00 create an artificial output spike relative to earlier physical finishes. A one-minute `recorded_at − complete` lag does not disprove delayed work entry because `complete` itself may be the entry timestamp. Bottleneck analysis can simultaneously invent a severe waiting bottleneck and an almost instantaneous closure activity, concealing the actual handling constraint. Resource overlap based on positive intervals is undefined here, not evidence of no work. Temporal conformance may show zero-duration activities and synchronized closures despite earlier work. If “Close ticket” truly denotes only the common administrative transition, its timing may be valid for closure reporting; it still cannot measure underlying ticket handling. Nothing supplied establishes that 17:00 is shift end.

**G7.** Literal waits are 2, −1, −4, −7 and −11 minutes. Four violate arrival-before-start under aligned clocks, so neither their mean nor a queue reconstructed from these starts is a valid physical estimate. Clipping negative waits to zero would hide the defect and create a new bias. Apparent fifteen-minute service and 75 case-minutes over fifteen minutes can inflate duration and utilization if starts are rounded down and completions up. The opposite endpoint rules can shrink durations, so the unconditional bias direction is not identifiable. Throughput is concentrated into a quarter-hour boundary; the five cases remain five, but finer completion-rate claims are unsupported. Coarsening can fabricate a simultaneous batch, make one worker appear five-fold occupied, misplace the bottleneck and generate temporal-conformance violations. Even a true physical batch could not start all five at exactly 14:00 if some only become available afterward. Arrival-to-completion differences avoid the negative-start arithmetic but still use a rounded endpoint, so they also need interval treatment.

**G8.** Literal waiting is 38, 43, 48, 54 and 58 minutes; service is seven minutes per item. After recovery, the intervals show serial work for thirty-five minutes. Attributing the long waits to slow triage service alone would confuse a delayed start of capacity with processing speed; treating the whole 15:00–16:13 span as five long service intervals would similarly inflate service effort. Under the **additional assumptions** that clocks align, the safety check fully removes this worker from repair service until 15:35, and no other work intervenes, waiting partitions into mean pre-15:35 exposure of 31.2 minutes and mean post-15:35 queue waiting of 17.0 minutes, totaling 48.2. This is a conditional decomposition, not a causal estimate. Waiting after 15:35 includes a three-minute gap before the first start and serial clearance of earlier cases. A safety assignment is lost repair capacity but may be productive worker activity, so low repair utilization need not mean worker idleness. The observed output spacing is one completion every seven minutes during the run; initial lack of completions does not establish a batching policy. Calendar/resource conformance requires synchronized safety and repair events before deciding whether work overlapped a safety restriction.

Across clusters, count case completions separately from batch or submission events. The 38 rows represent 38 distinct case-activity instances, not 38 complete process traces or eight necessarily physical batches. Grouping does not itself change item counts. It changes the interpretation of when output occurred, which capacity produced it, and which reporting window contains it. Daily physical completion counts are also conditional on finish semantics; G6 supplies closure counts, not observed handling finishes. These fragments do not establish stable arrival rates, long-run bottlenecks, shift utilization or whole-process fitness. In particular, adjacency between different cases is not a directly-follows relation within one case, and cross-case overlap does not establish an AND-split in a process model.

The overlap rate is the proportion of unordered pairs of positive, usable intervals for a resource that strictly overlap. Half-open intervals make touching serial jobs nonoverlapping. G6 has no eligible positive-duration pairs, so its rate is undefined.     spans = sorted(zip(g["start"], g["complete"]))

def tie_sizes(series):
    sizes = series.dropna().value_counts()
    return ",".join(map(str, sorted(sizes[sizes.ge(2)].tolist(), reverse=True))) or "-"

def grid_share(series, seconds):
    s = series.dropna()
    if s.empty:
        return math.nan
    offsets = (s - s.dt.normalize()).dt.total_seconds()
    return float(offsets.mod(seconds).eq(0).mean())

def queue_signature(g):
    q, s = g["queue_arrival"], g["start"]
    # Reconstruct only the fragment's queue, assuming arrival means ready.
    # A negative wait invalidates the literal queue; never clip or jitter it.
    coherent = q.notna().all() and s.notna().all() and s.ge(q).all()
    peak = math.nan
    if coherent:
        events = pd.concat([
            pd.DataFrame({"t": q, "delta": 1}),
            pd.DataFrame({"t": s, "delta": -1})], ignore_index=True)
        levels = events.groupby("t")["delta"].sum().sort_index().cumsum()
        peak = int(max(0, levels.max()))
    return {
        "arrival_span_min": (q.max() - q.min()).total_seconds() / 60,
        "first_start_minus_first_arrival_min": (s.min() - q.min()).total_seconds() / 60,
        "arrived_by_first_start": int(q.le(s.min()).sum()) if s.notna().any() else math.nan,
        "literal_queue_peak": peak,
    }

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

**Resource identity assumptions.** If an identifier means one exclusive worker, the literal intervals imply four open tasks for G2, G3 and G5, and five for G7. G1 is an equipment identifier with potential four-item capacity, so the same numerical overlap has a different meaning. “One worker” does not by itself make an open-case overlap impossible: the worker may switch attention or wait for equipment. A single-worker capacity violation follows only if every interval means uninterrupted exclusive labor.

If identities are instead pooled, G2, G3 and G7 could conceal several performers, and person-level utilization or handover analysis becomes unidentified. Their UI and precision evidence still remains; shared accounts do not automatically explain common starts or ends. G5 must already be treated as pooled because the context explicitly says so. With up to three analysts, its eight-minute four-way overlap still requires at least one open case without exclusive active analyst attention at each instant, or inaccurate interval semantics. G4 and G8 retain their serial recorded pattern under either identity interpretation, but attribution of that run to one person becomes uncertain. Shared identity does little to resolve G6’s missing handling times. Equipment identity should be separated from worker identity before estimating either machine or labor capacity.

**Exact timestamps versus interval-censoring.** Under literal exactness, G1 has identical positive intervals, G6 identical point events, and G7 an impossible arrival/start chronology for four cases under A1–A2. Their equality is the same algebraic fact with different implications. Minute-valued starts and completions occur throughout the supplied table, including sources documented at one-second precision. The extract therefore cannot independently demonstrate one-second execution resolution or accuracy.

Under a **quantization-only sensitivity assumption**, let a local endpoint be recorded as `t` at granularity `g`:

- Rounding down places the actual endpoint in `[t, t + g)`.
- Rounding up places it in `(t − g, t]`.
- Rounding to nearest places it approximately in `[t − g/2, t + g/2)`.
- If the choice among these rules is unknown, `[t − g, t + g]` is a conservative envelope, not a confidence interval.

The last envelope applies only if rounding is the relevant error mechanism and clocks are aligned. Documented precision does not bound unknown clock offsets, copied timestamps or delayed data entry. In particular, applying a ±one-minute envelope to G6 would not recover ticket handling that occurred hours earlier. Do not apply local-source precision automatically to upstream queue arrivals or database insertion times; those require their own metadata.

For start bounds `[Ls, Us]` and completion bounds `[Lc, Uc]`, feasible elapsed duration is bounded by `max(0, Lc − Us)` and `Uc − Ls`, subject to a nonempty feasible set and completion after start. With exact queue arrival `q` and work forbidden before arrival, intersect the start interval with `[q, infinity)` first. If `Us < q`, the assumptions are inconsistent; clipping a negative point estimate is not a substitute for resolving that inconsistency. These are bounds on elapsed intervals, not on active effort when pauses are unobserved.

At one-second resolution, G4 and G8’s four- and seven-minute durations remain approximately stable under small quantization errors, although exact adjacency can become a tiny gap or overlap. Multi-minute overlaps in G2, G3 and G5 also survive ordinary one-minute rounding. Their exact start/end equality may be uncertain physically, but rounding alone does not make every interval an uninterrupted serial task. For example, even independently allowing each G5 endpoint to shift by a full minute leaves a common overlap from 13:13 to 13:19. G1’s common cell interval remains strong evidence of common cycle recording, even if exact physical boundary equality is uncertain.

G7 is substantially different. Under the **specific scenario** that starts are rounded down and completions up, each actual start can lie between 14:00 and 14:15, and each completion between 14:00 and 14:15, subject to arrival and lifecycle order. Short serial measurements, overlapping measurements or a later common batch can all fit. More conservatively, under the unknown-rule ±fifteen-minute envelope and aligned clocks, the recorded 14:00 starts allow nonnegative waits no greater than 17, 14, 11, 8 and 4 minutes respectively, giving a loose mean bound of 0–10.8 minutes. This is an outer bound, not a point estimate or a claim that every joint configuration is feasible. The specific start-down scenario has a higher lower bound for M01 because its start cannot precede 14:00. Unknown clock offsets can invalidate these numerical bounds altogether.

**Observational equivalence remains in several places.** G1’s common endpoints can represent four items processed together, serial labeling inside a shared fixture cycle, or copied group lifecycle fields. G2’s common start with staggered finishes can represent common setup plus parallel execution, serial inspection after one button press, or time-sharing. G3’s common end can represent a true release gate, bulk reporting after serial work, or multiple open reviews. G6’s point events can represent real mass administrative closure or fabricated handling endpoints. G7’s equal coarse intervals can hide serial or parallel execution. G8’s delayed serial run can follow a safety restriction, other assigned work, ordinary scheduling or deliberate holding.

Do not break ties by random jitter or assign physical order from `sequence_no`. Sequence numbers preserve source insertion order, not execution order. Random jitter would invent evidence for a serial/concurrent classification. Instead, report patterns at native resolution, test feasible endpoint intervals, retain uncertainty flags and validate the highest-impact competing explanation operationally. If sampling feasible times for sensitivity, label the sampling distribution as an assumption and do not treat the resulting label frequencies as posterior probabilities.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

1. **Fix event meaning before adding timestamp digits.** Preserve immutable raw fields and separately capture actual execution start, actual finish, administrative release, group-button action and submission time. Define whether “Close ticket” is handling, approval or an administrative transition. Keep `recorded_at` as insertion time; also retain source emission/capture time and ingestion provenance when these differ. Never overwrite missing execution times with submission time. Begin with G2, G3 and G6 because their semantics directly distort waiting/service allocation.
2. **Establish clock comparability and precision metadata.** Record clock-domain ID, timezone/UTC offset, synchronization status and measured offset/uncertainty for the queue, workstation, safety and database systems. Document rounding direction separately for start and completion, original resolution, any display truncation and whether values are user-entered, defaulted or device-generated. Preserve both raw and corrected times with correction provenance. Prioritize the G7 MES rule and the G8 queue/safety alignment; investigate G5’s source-order/insertion-time reversals without treating one field as a universal ordering authority.
3. **Separate worker, account, workstation and equipment identity.** Capture authenticated badge/session-to-worker mappings with appropriate access controls; retain account ID as a separate provenance field. Log assignments and handovers. Record the number of analysts actually on duty, rather than assuming the maximum three. This makes G5 resource attribution assessable and prevents machine capacity from being confused with worker capacity elsewhere.
4. **Record operational batch and fixture membership explicitly.** Capture batch/group/cycle IDs, member cases, fixture positions, group-ready time, setup interval, execution cycle and release rule. Distinguish an operational batch ID from a database transaction or submission ID. For RBT-7, record whether one cycle acts on all positions together or visits them serially. For T-B and T-C, link UI group actions to individual execution and release events.
5. **Measure available capacity and interruptions.** Maintain workstation capacity, fixture slots, worker calendars, equipment availability and changes over time. Record safety checks, downtime, setup and recovery with the affected resource and reason. Capture pause/resume and active-work segments, including waits for equipment or information. This separates open-case elapsed time from labor effort and capacity loss from ordinary queue discipline.
6. **Validate with a small linked operational sample.** Match device cycles, badge assignments, UI actions and independently observed execution for representative groups. Reconcile source sequence and database arrival order. Add checks for arrival-after-start, impossible exclusive-resource occupancy, unexplained zero durations and changes in timestamp grid. Preserve and flag questionable records, rather than silently dropping or repairing them. Continue retaining queue arrivals and queue-exit/service-start events so that complete backlog trajectories, including cases outside these fragments, can be reconstructed.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High-confidence findings are primarily about recorded patterns and data semantics.** G4 and G8 show clear serial intervals; intentional sequential batching is unproved. G2 has a documented common-start mechanism. G5 has a shared-account attribution problem and open intervals exceeding the maximum exclusive analyst capacity. G6 strongly indicates bulk administrative closure or collapsed lifecycle fields, and G7 has documented coarsening incompatible with literal queue-start interpretation. G1 is the strongest physical-batch candidate, but fixture-level batching does not prove simultaneous item-level labeling. G3 strongly supports common completion/submission semantics, with genuine common release still a defensible alternative. The safety check is a plausible explanation for G8’s backlog, not an established cause.

For **queue analysis**, exclude G6 from point estimates of underlying handling wait and service; retain it for valid administrative-closure reporting and explicitly unknown execution times. Exclude G7’s literal waits and use interval-censored estimates only after stating the rounding and clock assumptions. Treat G2’s starts as group starts, so its waits estimate time to group initiation, not individual inspection. G3 waiting-to-first-review and G5 waiting-to-case-opening are provisional until start semantics are validated. G1 waiting-to-cycle-start and G4/G8 waiting-to-recorded-execution-start are the strongest candidates for descriptive queue metrics, still conditional on aligned upstream and local clocks. In particular, G8’s downtime decomposition requires synchronized safety evidence.

Operational validation is necessary before translating G1/G2/G3/G5/G6/G7 into physical touch-time or labor-capacity claims, before assigning G8’s delay to safety work, and before calling either G4 or G8 an intentional batch policy. None of these small fragments alone supports a sustained-throughput, shift-utilization or whole-process-bottleneck conclusion. Preserve the detected PM4Py category as a temporal descriptor alongside the operational hypothesis, data-quality flags and estimable performance measure.
