Redesign R produced a real but narrow benefit. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%, visible cost fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161, and simple low-risk houses improved on duration, first-time-right, and complaints. The same redesign created material tail and quality risk. p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days; first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%; complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%; manual activation rose from 7% to 15%; second visits rose from 11% to 18%; and the estimated all-in cost rose from EUR 198 to EUR 207. The process-mining evidence points to why: survey skipping and post-billing activation produced paths such as installation visit repeated without field survey, billing start before successful activation test, missing permit checks for older buildings, and complaint escalation after billing start. The pilot model’s high fitness but low precision indicates that the simplified path is common, but the process now permits many undesirable combinations.

The trade-off is therefore not “faster versus slower.” It is “faster simple cases versus slower, more expensive, less reliable complex cases.” The pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings, so the aggregate results may understate the benefit for simple houses and overstate the harm for the whole population. Conversely, incomplete segment attributes, sparse start timestamps, and missing downstream cost data mean the pilot may also understate true rework and compliance risk. We cannot infer national dominance from this evidence.

Management should act as follows:

1. **Scale selectively:** deploy coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling only for simple low-risk houses, where median, p90, first-time-right, and complaints all improved.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

Fitness measures how well the discovered model can replay the observed pilot traces. Pilot R has high fitness because many cases follow the intended simplified path: validation and scheduling are merged, a coordinator owns the case, and some surveys are skipped. Precision measures how much the model allows behavior that was not actually observed. Pilot R has low precision because the discovered model permits too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, billing start before activation, and complaint escalation. The combination of sparse start timestamps, incomplete segment attributes, and exception-heavy paths can make the model look flexible rather than controlled.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

The median improves because simple low-risk houses benefit from merged validation/scheduling, fewer handovers, and coordinator ownership. The tail worsens because complex cases are pushed into exception paths: wrong survey skips, missing permits, failed visits, second visits, manual activation, and billing before activation. Temporal diagnostics show that Installation visit  Activation test worsened for apartments and Failed visit  Second visit delay increased where coordinator utilization was high. Thus, the central tendency and the extreme values are driven by different mechanisms.

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

The metric “cases with 3+ handovers” improved because the coordinator owns more of the case. But resilience depends on redundancy, shared context, and backup capacity. Coordinator utilization reached 93%, and cases stalled when a coordinator was absent. The handover network became simpler, but the working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. Fewer handovers can therefore mean less visible coordination but also less organizational slack.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

Visible cost improved because the redesign reduced some survey and handover activity. But first-time-right fell, second visits rose, manual activation rose, complaints rose, and the estimated all-in cost worsened. The process-mining evidence shows cost shifting: work that was previously done through field surveys, permit checks, and activation tests may now appear later as failed visits, manual activation, billing disputes, and complaint escalation. Because downstream complaint handling after week 4 is missing, the true cost may be worse than the visible data suggest.

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

Simple low-risk houses are well matched to R’s assumptions: address validation and scheduling can be merged, survey risk is low, and activation is less complex. Apartments and old buildings are not well matched: they need field surveys, permit checks, building access, and reliable activation. The segment table shows simple houses improved on duration, first-time-right, and complaints, while apartments and old buildings worsened sharply on p90, first-time-right, and complaints. The pilot regions also had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline average, so aggregate results mix a strong benefit for simple cases with a strong harm for complex cases.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Adopt Alternative E as the immediate operating design, combined with a D-style controlled experiment before any broader rollout.**

## Immediate action

Freeze national scaling of Redesign R. Implement a segment gate for fiber-installation cases:

- **Simple low-risk houses:** apply coordinator ownership and merged address validation/appointment scheduling.
- **Medium-risk apartments, old buildings, permit-sensitive cases, business customers, and rural long-distance installs:** apply baseline survey, permit, and activation controls unless they are enrolled in a monitored experiment.

Stand up a conformance dashboard for billing before activation, missing permit checks, second visits, manual activation, complaint escalation, coordinator utilization, and segment-level p90/p95.

## What to pause

Pause the following outside controlled experiments:

1. Predictive field-survey skipping for medium-risk apartments and old buildings.
2. Moving activation checks after billing start for business customers and high-risk cases.
3. Broad coordinator ownership without backup, shared context, or utilization limits.
4. National rollout of R until downstream cost and segment-specific tail evidence are complete.

## What to scale

Scale selectively:

1. Coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses, with a utilization cap and documented backup.
2. Merged validation/scheduling for simple low-risk houses.
3. Reduced handovers where they do not remove required surveys, permit checks, or activation tests.
4. Field survey and permit checks for apartments, old buildings, and other high-risk cases.
5. Activation test before billing for business, old buildings, and any case where signal quality or permit status is uncertain.

## What to measure next

Prioritize in this order:

1. M1 randomized survey-skip experiment by building-risk stratum.
2. M6 validation of the predictive survey-skip rule.
3. M7 conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and missing permit checks.
4. M4 full 60-day downstream cost.
5. M3 structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.
6. M5 coordinator-absence resilience test.
7. M2 start-timestamp instrumentation.
8. M8 separate customer-experience measurement for speed versus working service.

## Threshold or evidence that would justify broader rollout

Broader rollout should be approved only if the controlled experiment shows, with a representative segment mix:

1. **Tail duration:** segment-specific p90 and p95 no worse than baseline by more than 10%; overall p90 34 days and p95 50 days when normalized to the pilot mix.
2. **Quality:** first-time-right at or above baseline for each segment; simple houses 85%; overall 82% if the mix is representative.
3. **Customer impact:** 30-day complaints 6.5% overall and 7.5% for apartments/old buildings; NPS not materially worse than baseline.
4. **Economics:** 60-day all-in cost EUR 198 overall and EUR 184 for simple houses.
5. **Exception control:** manual activation 8%; second visits 12%; SLA breach 13%.
6. **Compliance-like controls:** billing-before-activation <1%; permit checks observed for 98% of old buildings.
7. **Process model health:** model precision 0.75 and alignment violations for skipped survey, missing permit, and post-billing activation materially reduced.
8. **Resilience:** coordinator utilization 85% with backup coverage; no segment shows long stalls due to coordinator absence.

If these thresholds are met, scale R segment by segment, starting with simple low-risk houses and then expanding only to validated medium-risk cases. If they are not met, keep the hybrid design, roll back the risky elements, and preserve the coordinator-ownership benefits where they are proven.
