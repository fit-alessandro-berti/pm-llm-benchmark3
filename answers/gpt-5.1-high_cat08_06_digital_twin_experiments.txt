## 1. Model scope and state variables

### 1.1 Boundary and time scale
- **Process boundary**: From `Submit application` to `Issue permit` or `Reject application`, including all internal reviews, public-notice period, inspections, legal checks, and rework loops. Applicant and third‑party response times (e.g., providing missing documents, responding to objections, external consultant reports) are modeled explicitly but as external to municipal resources.
- **Time scale**: Continuous time in minutes, over horizons of 1–3 simulated years to capture seasonality (non‑stationary demand across months and weekdays) and policy changes. Calendars (working hours, holidays, council weeks) are modeled at least at day and hour resolution.
- **Digital-twin role**: A calibrated discrete-event simulation (DES) that mirrors the current process and can be re‑parameterized with alternative routing rules, calendars, and resources to evaluate interventions D1–D10 under different demand scenarios.

### 1.2 Entities and attributes
- **Case entity (permit application)**  
  Key attributes (many observable in the log, some latent/derived):
  - `case_id` (e.g., `P001`)
  - `case_type` ∈ {Simple renovation, New residential, Commercial, Complex variance}
  - `district` (D1–D5, etc.)
  - `architect_id` (used for bursty arrivals and cross-case bundling)
  - `application_value` (drives risk and some routing, e.g., D2 threshold)
  - Flags and derived indicators:
    - `requires_fire` (log flag plus inferred for large area if/when available)
    - `requires_environment`
    - `requires_public_notice`
    - `is_low_risk_simple` (Simple renovation, value < 50,000, no prior violations)
    - `objection_flag` (latent, inferred from public-notice durations, not directly observed)
  - **Process-state variables per case**:
    - Current activity status (e.g., `waiting_for_planning`, `in_planning`, `completed_planning`, etc.)
    - Timestamps for activity start/end when available; otherwise completion timestamp and inferred start
    - Counts of rework loops per activity (planning, inspection, legal, appeal)
    - Flags that prerequisite reviews are complete (Planning, Fire, Environmental, Public notice, Inspection)
    - Whether the case is currently in a bundle (e.g., inspection tour id)
    - SLA due date and slack (e.g., days remaining until municipal SLA)
    - Final outcome (`permit_issued`, `rejected`) and total lead time.

- **Resource entities**
  - **Clerks**: 5 FTE, with roles for portal support and completeness checks.
  - **Planners**: 8 FTE; two specialized in Simple renovation.
  - **Fire reviewers**: 2 FTE (or equivalent), part-time, Tue–Thu.
  - **Environmental reviewers**: 3 FTE with 40% reserved for non-permit work.
  - **Inspectors**: 4 FTE, field visits Tue–Fri; include a location/district attribute to model travel.
  - **Legal officers**: 2 FTE, with activity suppressed during council-meeting weeks.
  - **System resources**: Infinite capacity for portal, automated notifications, and `Issue permit`.
  - **State variables per resource**:
    - Status (`off_shift`, `idle`, `busy`)
    - Current case (if busy)
    - Next shift start/end, including exceptional closures
    - For inspectors: current district / travel position and tour plan.

- **Queue entities**
  - Activity-specific queues for:
    - Completeness check
    - Planning review
    - Fire-safety review
    - Environmental review
    - Inspection scheduling
    - Site inspection (per inspector or pooled with routing logic)
    - Decision drafting
    - Legal check
    - Public-notice related administrative work (setup, closure) as needed
  - **Queue state variables**:
    - Queue length over time (per activity and sometimes per case type/district)
    - Queue discipline (FIFO with priority rules where necessary, e.g., aging cases or urgent/emergency)
    - Priority parameters (e.g., case age, case type, district, D6 “aging cases” flag).

### 1.3 Calendars and global environment
- **Resource calendars**:
  - Explicit weekly schedules with working days, working hours, and breaks:
    - Clerks: Mon–Fri 08:00–16:00; one clerk reserved for portal support on Monday but can be used for backlog clearing in other days if modeled.
    - Planners: Mon–Fri 09:00–17:00; two planners restricted to Simple renovation cases.
    - Fire reviewers: Tue–Thu, defined working hours; off other days.
    - Environmental reviewers: Mon–Fri, but only 60% of nominal hours available for permit work (modeled via effective capacity or blocked time intervals).
    - Inspectors: Field visits Tue–Fri; Monday for preparation/administrative work if desired.
    - Legal officers: Exclude council-meeting weeks (no standard work except for urgent tasks that may be prioritized separately).
  - **Calendar state**:
    - Public holidays (no work for most human resources, but portal remains active).
    - Council-meeting weeks affecting legal checks and possibly Complex variance decisions.

- **Demand environment**
  - Time-varying arrival rate:
    - Monthly/seasonal pattern: March–May and Sept–Nov at 1.7× Jan–Feb baseline.
    - Weekly pattern: Monday portal submissions at 2.2× Wednesday.
    - Architect bursts: top 20 architects generating clustered arrivals within the same week.
  - Global state variables:
    - Time-dependent arrival intensity λ(t) by case type.
    - Demand scenario multipliers for stress tests (e.g., 2× overall demand).

### 1.4 Cross-case dependencies and interactions
- **Architect-based clusters**:
  - Define “application clusters” as sets of cases with same `architect_id` and `district` arriving within a rolling window (e.g., 7 days).
  - Cluster-level state variables:
    - Cluster id, cluster size, case ids
    - Preferred inspection bundling window (e.g., earliest feasible date when all cluster cases are ready for inspection).

- **Inspection bundling and routing**
  - Inspectors operate tours by district; when scheduling inspections, the simulation:
    - Identifies ready-for-inspection cases in each district.
    - Batches geographically close (same district, potentially same architect cluster) into tours.
    - Reduces per-case travel time at the cost of added waiting for early-ready cases in the batch.
  - State variables:
    - For each tour: list of cases, planned start time, estimated travel and service durations.

- **Neighborhood objections and public notice**
  - Objections are not observed directly; instead, the digital twin:
    - Represents a latent binary/ordinal variable `objection_severity` per case.
    - Uses a mixture distribution for the public-notice duration:
      - Short mode for “no/low objections” (around the 14-day minimum plus modest delays).
      - Long mode for “objections present” approximated from observed long public-notice durations.
  - Cases in the same area or cluster (e.g., same district and similar time window) can share elevated probability of objections, capturing neighborhood effects in aggregate but not individual objection events.

### 1.5 Event types and simulation mechanics
- **Event types**:
  - Case arrival
  - Case entering an activity queue
  - Start and completion of activities (when resource available and calendars allow)
  - Applicant/third-party response events (e.g., `Receive missing documents`, external reports)
  - Public-notice start and end (with objection-driven extension)
  - Rework loop initiation (e.g., after failed inspection or legal conflict)
  - Policy checkpoints (e.g., skipping inspection for D2, activating extra inspector for D4)
  - Calendar state changes (start/end of shifts, holidays, council weeks).

- **Simulation style**:
  - Discrete-event simulation where state transitions result from:
    - Arrivals drawn from a non-homogeneous process calibrated to the log.
    - Service times and waiting times drawn from empirical/parametric distributions conditioned on case attributes and resources.
    - Routing through the process tree and rework loops with probabilities derived from variants and routing summaries.
    - Resource calendars enforcing no work outside allowed time windows.

## 2. Parameter-estimation plan

### 2.1 Data ingestion and PM4Py usage
- **Log ingestion**:
  - Use `pandas.read_csv` (or equivalent) to load the raw event log.
  - Use `pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id='case:concept:name', activity_key='concept:name', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')` to standardize column names and types.
  - Convert the DataFrame to an event log with `pm4py.objects.conversion.log.factory.apply(formatted_df)`.

- **Model artifacts from PM4Py**:
  - Parse the provided process tree string using `pm4py.parse_process_tree(process_tree_str)` (or an equivalent process-tree importer), acknowledging that real PM4Py may require a specific syntax/importer.
  - Derive:
    - Variants and their frequencies from the event log (e.g., `pm4py.statistics.variants.log.get.get_variants`).
    - Directly-follows graph (DFG) and performance DFG (`pm4py.algo.discovery.dfg.factory.apply` with `variant="frequency"` and `variant="performance"`).
    - Per-activity performance statistics (service/waiting approximations).
  - These artifacts parameterize routing probabilities, concurrency, and bottlenecks in the simulation.

### 2.2 Arrivals and non-stationary demand
- **Case arrivals**:
  - From the formatted DataFrame, compute per-case start times as the minimum timestamp per `case:concept:name`.
  - Aggregate case counts by:
    - Calendar day and hour-of-day,
    - Day-of-week,
    - Month (or season: Jan–Feb, Mar–May, Jun–Aug, Sep–Nov, Dec),
    - Case type and architect segment (top-20 vs others).
  - Fit a **non-homogeneous Poisson process** (NHPP) or piecewise-constant rate model:
    - For each case type, estimate a baseline hourly rate and multiplicative factors for:
      - Months/season (enforcing the observed ~1.7× in Mar–May and Sep–Nov).
      - Day-of-week (enforcing ~2.2× on Mondays vs Wednesdays).
      - Architect bursts: model top-20 architects with elevated cluster probabilities (e.g., Poisson cluster or “batch arrival” process within weeks).
  - Implementation:
    - Use regression (e.g., Poisson GLM) or direct empirical rate tables for `(season, day-of-week, hour-of-day, case_type)`.
    - In simulation, generate arrivals via thinning or by sampling inter-arrivals from exponential distributions with time-varying rate λ(t).

### 2.3 Service-time and waiting-time estimation
- **Separation of service and waiting**:
  - Where start and completion timestamps exist, compute:
    - Service time = `end_time - start_time`.
    - Waiting before activity = `start_time - previous_end_time_in_case`.
  - For activities with only completion timestamps:
    - Use records where start timestamps are present (for some resources/periods) to estimate typical service-time distributions per activity and case segment.
    - For records without start times, infer waiting time from:
      - Time since previous event in the case, minus an imputed service time sampled from the calibrated service-time distribution.
    - Explicitly maintain separate random variables for service and waiting in the simulation, even if both are partly inferred.

- **Distributional choices**:
  - For human service activities (planning, fire, environmental, inspection, legal, decision drafting):
    - Fit **lognormal** or **Weibull** distributions to match empirical medians and p90s per activity, segmented by case type and district where relevant.
    - Check for heavy tails and, if visible, consider mixture distributions (e.g., short vs long environmental reviews).
  - For short, near-automated tasks (completeness check, request missing docs, scheduling, issue permit):
    - Use light-tailed distributions (Gamma or lognormal with low variance) calibrated to the provided medians/p90s.
  - For applicant-driven steps:
    - Derive distributions for `Receive missing documents` and long tail responses (5+ days) directly from event log where possible.
    - Use heavy-tailed models (e.g., lognormal with large variance, Pareto-like tail) to replicate long applicant delays.

### 2.4 Routing, branching probabilities, and rework
- **Routing probabilities**:
  - From the PM4Py event log and process tree:
    - Estimate probabilities for optional paths (e.g., Fire-safety review, Environmental review, Public notice) by case type and district.
    - Compare with current routing rules (requires_fire flag, environmentally sensitive districts, Complex variance, New residential with public notice).
  - Use logistic or decision-tree models for routing where log attributes are incomplete:
    - E.g., model `requires_fire` as a function of case type, application value, and district where the explicit flag is missing.
    - Similarly model `requires_environment` where environmental sensitivity data is incomplete.

- **Rework**:
  - Treat repeated occurrences of activities (planning, inspection, legal, completeness) within the same case as rework loops.
  - Estimate:
    - Probability of at least one rework loop per activity, conditioned on case attributes.
    - Distribution of number of loops (e.g., geometric distribution) and added service time per loop.
  - For appeals (particularly in Complex variance), map rework to extended legal/public-notice durations and extended end-to-end lead times rather than explicit appeal events (not always structured).

### 2.5 Resource calendars and productivity
- **Calendars from HR data**:
  - Use provided calendars as the primary source of working hours and closures (holidays, council weeks).
  - Validate calendars against the event log:
    - Check that almost no human events occur outside modeled working hours; where they do, treat them as overtime or exceptional events and either ignore or parameterize separately.
  - Convert calendars into:
    - Per-resource (or per-role) weekly schedules.
    - Lists of non-working days/weeks (holidays, council-meeting weeks).

- **Effective capacity and utilization**:
  - Calibrate capacity by matching historical throughput and utilization:
    - Compare number of completed activities per resource per week against available work hours.
    - Adjust effective service-time distributions or “utilization caps” if necessary to obtain realistic simulated utilizations (e.g., 60–85%).
  - For Environmental reviewers with 40% time reserved for non-permit duties:
    - Either block time explicitly in their calendar or scale down available hours by 40%.

### 2.6 Cross-case dependencies and bundling
- **Architect and district clusters**:
  - Identify clusters of related cases in the log:
    - Same `architect_id`, same `district`, arrivals within X days (e.g., 7).
    - Compute empirical distributions of cluster sizes and within-cluster arrival spreads.
  - For site inspections:
    - Using the log, compute time gaps between inspections in the same district/architect cluster to infer bundling patterns (how often inspections were done in the same tour).
  - Parameterization:
    - Probability that cluster cases are inspected in the same tour vs separate visits.
    - Travel-time savings factor when K inspections are bundled in the same tour vs K single visits.

- **Neighborhood objection influence**:
  - Use public-notice durations as a proxy:
    - Fit a mixture model for the `Public-notice period` duration:
      - Component 1: no/low objections (~14–20 days).
      - Component 2: objections present (long tail; up to ~60+ days).
    - The mixture proportion and distribution parameters may depend on case type and district.
  - For cross-case correlation:
    - If one case in a small area under public notice experiences the long-duration component, increase the probability that neighboring cases within the same time window also draw from the long component.

### 2.7 Public holidays, council weeks, and policy parameters
- **Calendar shocks**:
  - From HR and municipal calendars, build a list of:
    - Public holidays and partial days.
    - Council-meeting weeks during which Legal officers are largely unavailable.
  - Calibrate the effect on queues:
    - Compare waiting times for Legal check and Public notice around these periods vs “normal” weeks.
  - Policy parameters for interventions (D1–D10):
    - For each intervention, add specific parameters (e.g., document-checker success rate at catching missing documents, inspector capacity increments for peak months, triage accuracy for Environmental review) to be estimated from historical proxies or expert judgment.

## 3. Assumptions

### 3.1 Arrivals
- Arrivals per case type follow a **non-homogeneous Poisson process** with rate λ(t) that is piecewise constant over `(season, day-of-week, hour-of-day)` and adjusted for architect-specific clustering.
- The 18-month patterns (1.7× seasonal peaks, 2.2× Monday effect) are assumed to be **structurally stable** in the medium term (no strong long-term trend), though scenario analysis will vary overall demand.
- Architect bursts are modeled by short intra-week clusters (batch arrivals), not by explicit modeling of architect decision processes.

### 3.2 Service times
- Conditional on case attributes, activity type, and (where relevant) district, **service times are independent between cases and activities**, aside from rework loops.
- For each activity, a lognormal or Weibull distribution is assumed adequate to match the empirical median and p90; explicit multi-modal distributions are only used where strongly indicated (e.g., Environmental review).
- For activities missing start timestamps, we assume the missingness is mostly at random within each activity/resource group; we calibrate service times using subsets with complete data and expert information.

### 3.3 Routing
- Routing decisions (e.g., need for Fire or Environmental review, Public notice) depend on case attributes (type, district, flags) and are captured via probabilistic rules estimated from the log plus simple predictive models.
- The simplified process tree adequately represents the main control-flow structure (sequence, choice, parallelism, and loops), even though some rare variants and emergency paths are omitted.
- Policy interventions change routing (e.g., skipping inspection for low-risk Simple renovation) by adjusting these probabilistic rules or adding/skipping activities, while preserving the overall process tree semantics.

### 3.4 Resources and calendars
- Resource availability strictly follows the provided **role-level calendars**, except for:
  - Occasional overtime or weekend work, which is either neglected or modeled via simple extensions in sensitivity analyses.
  - Legal officers’ exceptional urgent work during council weeks, which is modeled as a small prioritized capacity.
- No explicit modeling of sickness or unplanned absences is included in the base model; these effects are implicitly captured in effective service-time distributions and explored in stress tests.
- Resource skills are assumed fixed: planners limited to certain case types, inspectors generally interchangeable within district constraints.

### 3.5 Rework
- The probability and number of rework loops at Planning, Inspection, Legal, and Completeness are modeled per case segment and **do not depend on unobserved qualities** beyond what is proxied by case attributes and past events.
- Each rework loop adds an independent additional service-time draw for the corresponding activity plus waiting time based on the same queue.
- Appeals in Complex variance cases are modeled as extended legal and/or public-notice durations rather than explicit additional structured activities (due to unstructured rework reasons).

### 3.6 Applicant and third-party response
- Applicant response times (e.g., to missing-doc requests, objections) and third-party reports are modeled as **external stochastic processes** independent of municipal resource load, though they may depend on case attributes (value, type, architect).
- Heavy-tailed behavior (very slow responses) is captured via long-tail distributions but not attributed to specific behavioral models of applicants.

### 3.7 Cross-case dependencies
- Cross-case dependencies are limited to:
  - Architect- and district-based clustering for arrivals and inspection bundling.
  - Correlated probabilities of public-notice extensions within the same area and window.
- Other potential dependencies (e.g., planner specialization by architect, shared consultant workloads) are neglected unless discovered in further analysis.

### 3.8 Non-stationary demand
- Seasonal and weekly patterns are assumed periodic and repeatable across simulated years, except where explicit stress scenarios change them.
- There is no long-run secular trend assumed in case volumes; overall demand can be scaled up/down as scenario parameters.

### 3.9 Missing data and biases
- Missing start timestamps, unstructured rework reasons, and omitted emergency decisions introduce uncertainty; we treat them as **parameter uncertainty**, not as known truths.
- Objection events are **not treated as directly observed**; they are inferred from public-notice durations and proxied via mixture distributions without modeling individual objection events or timestamps.
- The flattened per-case log may underrepresent dependencies across related applications; our approximations (architect/district clusters) are assumed to capture the main operational impacts, but not all legal or political nuances.

## 4. Experiment matrix

### 4.1 Factors and levels
- **Policy/intervention factors**:
  - D1: Pre-submission document checker (on/off; effectiveness level).
  - D2: No-inspection rule for low-risk Simple renovation (on/off).
  - D3: District-batched inspection scheduling (none/basic/advanced bundling).
  - D4: Temporary peak-month inspector (0 or +1 FTE in peak months).
  - D5: Parallel legal pre-check for Commercial and Complex variance (off/on with pre-check coverage rate).
  - D6: Reserved planner capacity for aging cases (0 or 1 dedicated planner per day).
  - D7: Architect-facing appointment slots to smooth Monday bursts (off/on, smoothing strength).
  - D8: Environmental-review triage (off/on with triage accuracy for fast lane).
  - D9: Public-notice objection early-warning (off/on with risk-detection accuracy).
  - D10: Combined D1 + D2 + D3 + D4 (as defined).
- **Stress factors**:
  - Demand level: baseline, +30%, +60%, 2× in peak months.
  - Objection intensity: baseline vs high (larger probability mass on long public-notice durations).
  - Resource shocks: loss of one inspector or planner, or increased absenteeism.

## 5. Warm-up, replications, and uncertainty treatment

### 5.1 Warm-up period
- **Ongoing system**: The permit process operates continuously, so initial conditions matter (existing backlog, in-progress cases).
- **Warm-up options**:
  - **Snapshot initialization**: Use a recent period of the log to reconstruct the current backlog (distribution of cases by stage and age) and initialize the simulation close to this real state; then use a shorter warm-up (e.g., 1–2 months) to smooth transient effects.
  - **Classic warm-up**: Start empty and let the model evolve under baseline demand for a long period (e.g., 1 year), then discard statistics from the first X months (e.g., 6–9 months) identified via Welch’s method on key performance indicators (WIP, mean cycle time).
- For policy comparisons, it is usually preferable to:
  - Use the same **calibrated initial state** for all scenarios.
  - Apply a modest warm-up (e.g., 3 months) before collecting results over an analysis horizon (e.g., 12–24 months).

### 5.2 Run length and replications
- **Run length**:
  - At least one full year of simulated time after warm-up to capture seasonal variations.
  - For tail metrics (p90/p95 of lead times), consider 2–3 simulated years per replication to reduce estimation noise, especially for rare Complex variance and rework-heavy variants.
- **Replications**:
  - Use multiple independent replications (e.g., 30–50 per scenario) with different random seeds.
  - Use **common random numbers** across policy scenarios (same seeds and random streams for arrivals, service times, objections, etc.) so differences primarily reflect policy effects rather than random noise.

### 5.3 Uncertainty quantification
- **Confidence intervals**:
  - Compute 95% confidence intervals for means and quantiles (e.g., median, p90) of:
    - End-to-end lead times by case type.
    - Waiting times per key activity (Planning, Environmental, Inspection, Legal).
    - Resource utilizations.
  - For heavy-tailed measures (e.g., Complex variance p95), use:
    - Quantile-based intervals (order statistics) or
    - Bootstrap resampling across replications.

- **Parameter uncertainty and sensitivity**:
  - Identify key uncertain parameters (e.g., objection probability, rework probabilities, triage accuracy, inferred service times where starts are missing).
  - Perform **one-at-a-time** and **factorial** sensitivity analyses:
    - Vary each uncertain parameter ±10–30% from its calibrated value.
    - Observe impacts on decision metrics and whether intervention rankings change.
  - This prevents overconfidence in a single calibrated parameter set.

### 5.4 Heavy-tailed durations
- **Applicant response and public notice**:
  - For `Receive missing documents` and long public-notice durations, explicitly model heavy tails (lognormal with high variance or Pareto-type tails).
  - Use robust metrics (median, p75/p90) and avoid relying solely on means.
- **Reporting**:
  - For each scenario, report both central tendency and tail indicators, and highlight where heavy tails dominate (e.g., Complex variance with appeals).

## 6. Validation strategy

### 6.1 Face validation
- Conduct workshops with clerks, planners, inspectors, and legal officers:
  - Walk through typical and exceptional cases end-to-end in the simulation.
  - Confirm that key behaviors are realistic:
    - No inspections scheduled on non-working days.
    - Legal checks respecting council-meeting blackout weeks.
    - Public-notice periods and inspection tours behaving as expected.
  - Collect feedback on routing logic, priority rules, and whether modeled rework patterns feel plausible.

### 6.2 Event-log replay and temporal validation
- **Replay baseline scenario with historical arrivals**:
  - Feed the model with actual arrival times and case attributes from the log (for a chosen calibration period).
  - Run the simulation with calibrated parameters, using stochastic service and waiting times.
  - Compare simulated vs observed:
    - End-to-end lead time distributions per variant group.
    - Activity waiting times and service times where start times are known.
    - Frequency and pattern of rework loops.

### 6.3 Distributional validation
- Compare empirical and simulated distributions for:
  - Lead times for key variant groups (as in the variant summary table, e.g., Simple clean path, Simple with missing docs, New residential with fire+notice, etc.).
  - Per-activity performance: medians and p90s for service and waiting times.
  - Public-notice durations, including the proportion of cases in the long-duration mixture component.
- Use graphical tools (histograms, QQ-plots) and statistical tests (e.g., Kolmogorov–Smirnov, where appropriate) to quantify discrepancies, understanding that perfect fits are neither expected nor required.

### 6.4 Resource-utilization and bottleneck validation
- From the event log:
  - Estimate approximate utilizations per role (clerks, planners, fire, environmental, inspectors, legal) based on counts and inferred workloads versus calendar capacity.
  - Identify historically congested stages (e.g., Environmental review and Legal for Commercial cases, inspections in D3/D5).
- From the simulation:
  - Compare average utilizations and queue lengths with those inferred from the log.
  - Check that the **same activities** appear as bottlenecks under baseline.
  - Adjust capacity or service-time parameters to better align with observed bottlenecks if necessary.

### 6.5 Out-of-sample validation
- Split the log into:
  - Calibration period (e.g., first 12 months) to estimate parameters.
  - Validation period (e.g., last 6 months) to test predictive performance.
- Use calibration-period parameters to simulate the validation period (with observed arrival pattern as input) and compare:
  - Lead-time distributions and SLA compliance.
  - Variants distribution and routing frequencies.
  - Resource workloads.
- If deviations are systematic (e.g., consistent underestimation of Environmental delays), recalibrate or adjust assumptions.

---

## 7. Decision criteria

### 7.1 Lead times and SLA compliance
- **Primary metrics**:
  - Median and p90 end-to-end lead times by case type and variant group.
  - Activity-specific waiting times for key bottlenecks (Planning, Environmental, Inspection, Legal).
- **Target ranges** (example, to be agreed with stakeholders):
  - Simple renovation:
    - Median < 20 days, p90 < 45 days.
  - New residential:
    - Median < 60 days, p90 < 110 days.
  - Commercial:
    - Median < 80 days, p90 < 140 days.
  - Complex variance:
    - Median < 150 days, p90 < 260 days (aim to reduce tail without unrealistic expectations).
- **SLA metrics**:
  - Proportion of cases meeting statutory or internal SLAs (e.g., 90% of Simple renovation under 40 days).
  - Policy scenarios are considered acceptable only if they do not materially worsen SLA compliance for any case type, unless explicitly justified.

### 7.2 Fairness and equity
- **Across districts**:
  - Compare median and p90 lead times and backlog levels across districts, particularly D3 and D5 vs others.
  - Set thresholds such as: p90 lead times in D3 and D5 no more than X days longer than average across other districts, unless justified by environmental constraints.
- **Across architects**:
  - Compare lead times for large architects vs smaller ones:
    - Ensure that smoothing policies (D7) do not unfairly prioritize or penalize particular architect segments.
  - Avoid policies that systematically disadvantage applicants in less-resourced districts or with smaller architects.

### 7.3 Resource utilization and operational feasibility
- **Utilization targets**:
  - Aim for typical utilization ranges:
    - Clerks and planners: 60–85%.
    - Specialized reviewers and legal officers: 50–80%, recognizing bursty demand.
    - Inspectors: 60–85%, including travel time.
  - Reject policies that push any key resource consistently above ~90% utilization, as they are likely to cause unstable queues and burnout.

### 7.4 Rework, backlog, and robustness
- **Rework metrics**:
  - Average and distribution of rework loops per activity and case type.
  - Interventions like D1 (pre-checker) and D2 (no-inspection rule) should not lead to large increases in rework or post-issuance issues.
- **Backlog metrics**:
  - Average and p90 number of cases in the system and in each key queue.
  - Maximum queue lengths during peak months.
- **Robustness and stress scenarios**:
  - Under stress scenarios (S1–S3, S4), assess:
    - Whether SLA compliance remains acceptable.
    - Whether fairness gaps remain within agreed bounds.
    - Whether key resources remain within acceptable utilization limits.
- **Decision rule**:
  - Prefer interventions or combinations that:
    - Improve lead times and SLA compliance for targeted segments.
    - Do not significantly worsen performance for others.
    - Maintain fairness and robustness under stress.
    - Are operationally feasible given calendars and staffing.

def prepare_event_log(df: pd.DataFrame):
    """Format the DataFrame and convert it to an event log for PM4Py."""
    df_formatted = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    event_log = log_converter.apply(df_formatted)
    return event_log, df_formatted


def analyze_log(event_log, df_formatted: pd.DataFrame) -> Dict[str, Any]:
    """Compute variants, DFG, performance DFG, arrivals, and waiting/service-time columns."""
    # Variants
    variants = variants_get.get_variants(event_log)

    # Directly-follows graph (frequency)
    dfg = dfg_factory.apply(event_log)

    # Performance DFG (e.g., average time between activities)
    performance_dfg = dfg_factory.apply(event_log, variant="performance")

    # Case arrival characteristics
    case_starts = (
        df_formatted.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
        .min()
        .sort_values()
    )
    inter_arrival_hours = case_starts.diff().dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    avg_case_arrival_interval_h = inter_arrival_hours.dropna().mean()

    # Waiting and (where possible) service times
    df_sorted = df_formatted.sort_values(
        ["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]
    ).copy()
    df_sorted["prev_time"] = (
        df_sorted.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].shift(1)
    )

    # When start timestamps exist, separate waiting vs service.
    # In this illustrative log, only completion timestamps are present,
    # so we can at best approximate waiting from inter-event times and
    # leave service times as externally calibrated or NaN.
    if "time:timestamp_start" in df_sorted.columns:
        df_sorted["service_time_h"] = (
            df_sorted["time:timestamp"] - df_sorted["time:timestamp_start"]
        ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
        df_sorted["waiting_time_h"] = (
            df_sorted["time:timestamp_start"] - df_sorted["prev_time"]
        ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    else:
        df_sorted["service_time_h"] = pd.NA
        df_sorted["waiting_time_h"] = (
            df_sorted["time:timestamp"] - df_sorted["prev_time"]
        ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0

    return {
        "variants": variants,
        "dfg": dfg,
        "performance_dfg": performance_dfg,
        "avg_case_arrival_interval_h": avg_case_arrival_interval_h,
        "df_with_times": df_sorted,
    }
