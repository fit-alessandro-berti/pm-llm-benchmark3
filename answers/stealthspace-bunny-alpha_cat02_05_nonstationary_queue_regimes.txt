# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Queueing quantities and units

The opening-hours convention is **07:00–19:00**, giving \(H=12\) hours per day. The supplied hourly vectors confirm 12 one-hour bins. Counts and case durations refer to different processes:

| Quantity | Estimand from the supplied data | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival intensity | \(\lambda_d=A_d/12\), where \(A_d\) is daily arrivals | New cases entering the system per **open hour** |
| Completion intensity | \(\gamma_d=C_d/12\), where \(C_d\) is daily completions | Departures per **open hour**, including cases that arrived earlier |
| Daily net flow | \(B_d=A_d-C_d\) | A count difference, **not** the change in opening backlog |
| Active service behavior | Daily median and p90 service durations | Handling-time distribution summaries, **not flow time or waiting time** |
| Mean WIP | Mean number of cases in the system across 15-minute snapshots | Includes waiting and handling, and possibly any other retained case states |
| Reported capacity | Mean concurrently available FTE | Staffing-capacity measure, not necessarily a count of independent parallel servers |
| WIP loading | Period mean WIP divided by period mean FTE | A comparative inventory/capacity ratio; not a utilization estimate |
| Diagnostic workload index | \(\displaystyle q_d=\frac{A_d\,s_{50,d}}{60\,FTE_d}\) | Median arrival-handling minutes per reported staffed hour |

Here \(s_{50,d}\) is the reported daily median service time. **Calculation assumptions, not additional observations:**

1. Daily means are equally weighted within a regime. Thus, unless weekdays and weekends are separated, a small weekend segment receives the same weight as a larger weekday segment.
2. The workload index assumes that the reported service median is a per-case active-handling duration. If it is instead an individual activity duration within a multi-step case, this index is not a valid case-workload measure.
3. The index is deliberately called a *median-minute workload index*, not utilization. It ignores the unknown service mean, service-time dispersion, outages, and compatibility of work with particular staff or servers.
4. The Little’s-Law calculations below use **open-hour time**, equal-weight daily averaging, and the supplied WIP definition. They do not assume that service medians equal mean flow times.

## Audit limitations and threats to naive inference

At least ten important threats are present:

1. **Completion cohorts differ from arrival cohorts.** A completion on a given day may concern a case that arrived days earlier. Daily \(C_d\) therefore cannot be paired with that day’s \(A_d\) as if they were a cohort.
2. **Opening and closing backlog are missing.** We cannot determine whether a day started with empty queues, whether its net surplus persisted overnight, or whether apparent output cleared earlier arrivals.
3. **The mean is a supplied service quantile.** Replacing mean service time with the median would generally bias capacity and waiting-time reasoning, especially when the median, p90, and complex-case share change together.
4. **Case-mix and selection effects are unresolved.** A p90 increase can reflect a larger complex share, routing changes, coding changes, or a different composition of completed cases. Service behavior is not reported separately by variant.
5. **WIP is not queue length.** It includes active handling. The 15-minute sampling can also miss short-lived peaks.
6. **FTE is not a server-count observation.** One FTE need not correspond to one parallel server, and the outage may affect availability without being fully reflected in the daily FTE mean.
7. **Arrival demand is highly nonstationary.** Daily means, the two-hour morning peak share, and the representative hourly profiles conceal within-day concentration.
8. **Daily observations are serially dependent.** Consecutive days share staffing schedules, carry-over cases, and a common four-week trend. Weekday/weekend composition also induces dependence.
9. **The intervention time is unknown.** Even a full-day comparison beginning on April 29 contains an unknown amount of pre-intervention exposure.
10. **The sample is short and unbalanced.** There are 28 days, only eight weekend days, and only one identified outage episode. Serial dependence and the short follow-up preclude treating daily rows as independent replications.
11. **The representative vectors are not exact reconstructions of their daily rows.** For example, April 21’s vector has \(42/115=36.5\%\) of arrivals from 08:00–09:59, while its daily row reports 47%. The weekend vector similarly gives \(6/51=11.8\%\), versus 22% in the table. They should be treated as illustrative profiles, not additional exact observations.
12. **The service-duration sampling cohort is unspecified.** We do not know whether service summaries concern all arrivals or only observed completions, which matters when backlog and right-censoring differ across regimes.

Consequently, the observations support **descriptive regime comparisons and conditional flow-time diagnostics**, but not a definitive stochastic queue, a causal staffing effect, or a calibrated staffing formula.

- **April 15 is an outage state, not a high-throughput regime.** The next day’s 129 completions should not be pooled with ordinary days as if they represented normal capacity. The increased WIP on April 16 indicates that the recovery episode extended beyond the outage day.
- **April 20 is a joint discontinuity.** Relative to Sunday April 19, arrivals rise from 39 to 108, complex share from 23% to 41%, morning peak share from 18% to 42%, and WIP from 13 to 37. Calendar day, demand concentration, and case mix change together.
- **April 29–May 3 is a post-intervention/partial-drain window, not demonstrated normal operation.** Complex share is approximately unchanged, but WIP is falling and completions exceed arrivals. This can be a recovery state rather than a stable equilibrium.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A serious alternative is **calendar first, with context and incident overlays**:

1. Use two stable calendar strata: **Monday–Friday** and **Saturday–Sunday**.
2. Within each, distinguish baseline April 6–14, the higher-demand/mix period April 20–28, and post-intervention April 29–May 3.
3. Retain April 15–19 as an explicit **outage/recovery disturbance block**, rather than interpreting it as a separate stationary queue.
4. Model complex share, peak share, staffing, and outage indicators as covariates or interactions rather than hard class labels.

The operational story is that the center is the same resource pool operating under changing demand, composition, and availability—not necessarily a sequence of fundamentally different queueing processes.

This alternative preserves more data in the weekday comparisons:

- Baseline weekdays April 6–14: **103.0 arrivals/day, 99.7 completions/day**, median service 25 minutes, median daily p90 48 minutes, and mean WIP 25.4.
- Higher-demand/mix weekdays April 20–28: **114.0 arrivals/day, 106.1 completions/day**, median 35 minutes, p90 83 minutes, and mean WIP 42.3.
- Post-intervention weekdays: **115.0 arrivals/day, 121.0 completions/day**, median 33 minutes, p90 74 minutes, and mean WIP 37.0.

Compared with baseline, higher-demand weekdays have about 11% more arrivals but only about 7% more completions. The post period has almost the same arrival count as the higher-demand pre-intervention weekdays but approximately 14% more completions.

**Strengths**

- Reduces the number of tiny weekend cells.
- Makes weekday comparisons fair with respect to calendar composition.
- Treats mix and peak concentration as potentially continuous explanatory variables.
- Avoids turning one observed recovery episode into a permanent “recovery regime.”

**Weaknesses**

- Can hide the distinct mechanics of the outage and subsequent catch-up.
- Assumes a stable relationship between covariates and flow within broad periods, which may be false.
- Mix, time trend, and intervention are strongly entangled over only four weeks.
- Still cannot reconstruct queue waiting time or identify a service-rate distribution from daily aggregates.

Neither segmentation is uniquely correct. The first is more useful operationally; the second is more conservative statistically.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## Representative hourly vectors

For hourly counts, I use population variance across the 12 bins:

\[
CV=\frac{\operatorname{sd}(x)}{\operatorname{mean}(x)},\qquad
F=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(x)}{\operatorname{mean}(x)}.
\]

A Poisson reference has variance equal to mean. The Fano factor is useful descriptively here, but it is **not a Poisson goodness-of-test**: a time-varying mean alone can generate a large factor, and a fixed daily total also changes the relevant probability model.

| Representative day | A/hour | Arrival CV | Arrival Fano | Vector’s 08–10 share | C/hour | Completion CV | Completion Fano |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apr 7, ordinary weekday | 8.42 | **0.593** | **2.96** | 31.7% | 8.25 | **1.033** | **8.81** |
| Apr 15, outage | 8.75 | **0.658** | **3.79** | 33.3% | 4.83 | **0.654** | **2.07** |
| Apr 21, bursty weekday | 9.58 | **0.984** | **9.28** | 36.5% | 8.42 | **0.327** | **0.90** |
| Apr 25, weekend | 4.25 | **0.527** | **1.18** | 11.8% | 3.58 | **0.448** | **0.72** |

Interpretation:

- **Even the ordinary weekday is concentrated**, with 32 of 101 arrivals from 08:00–09:59 and a maximum hourly count of 20 versus a mean of 8.42.
- **April 15 is structurally different.** Completions are zero in the 11:00, 12:00, and 13:00 vector bins, consistent with the approximate 11:00–14:00 interruption. Its positive completion Fano must not be interpreted as ordinary service variability.
- **April 21 has multiple bursts**, not merely a morning peak. The 09:00–10:00 bin contains 34 arrivals, or 29.6% of that day; the 13:00–14:00 bin contains another 24. Arrival Fano is much higher than completion Fano, consistent with buffering and lagged output—but not proof of a particular queue.
- **The weekend is less dispersed**, with a weekend arrival Fano only slightly above one. A completion Fano below one does not establish genuine underdispersion; queueing, finite-bin quantization, and a relatively smooth service schedule can all produce it.

The daily morning percentages for April 21 and April 25 do not match their representative vectors. Therefore, neither the vectors’ exact hourly shapes nor their inferred burstiness should replace the daily peak observations in statistical fitting.

## Daily dependence and change-point evidence

Across all 28 daily arrival counts:

- Mean arrivals/day: **89.96**.
- Population SD: **29.67**.
- Daily coefficient of variation: **0.330**.
- Daily Fano factor: **9.78**.

That large Fano mainly combines weekday/weekend composition, the outage, and the rising later-period demand. It is not evidence that a stationary, homogeneously rate-varied Poisson arrival process has variance 9.78 times its mean.

Simple Pearson lag correlations of daily arrivals are approximately:

- **Lag 1: \(r_1=0.39\)**
- **Lag 7: \(r_7=0.72\)**

These are descriptive indicators, not independent-sample significance tests. Lag seven compares the same weekday one week apart and captures the common high later-period level. Lag one spans weekends, outages, mix changes, and staffing changes. Neither isolates carry-over autocorrelation.

Evidence for useful change boundaries is stronger than evidence for a single stochastic regime:

- **April 15:** explicitly known interruption, with output loss and elevated WIP.
- **April 20:** simultaneous demand, peak concentration, complex share, service summaries, and WIP change.
- **April 29:** reported capacity increases; service and WIP behavior changes afterward. The exact intervention hour is unknown.

The reproducible code uses a robustly standardized, contiguous-segment SSE heuristic with a complexity penalty. It does not force April 29 as a break, and it deliberately does not treat the single outage row as a segmentation truth label. Such a heuristic describes multivariate level changes; it cannot identify arrival distributions, causal interventions, or queueing mechanisms.

Four representative days and 28 daily aggregates cannot establish stationarity, independence, a renewal arrival process, a service-time distribution conditional on case mix, or a state-dependent queue. Those claims require substantially more event-level history and explicit modeling assumptions.

# 6. Little’s-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law in its stationary form is

\[
L=\lambda_W T,
\]

where \(W\) must be the same case population used for \(L\), and \(\lambda_W\) must be a stable long-run throughput.

The aggregates permit a **finite-window diagnostic**:

\[
\overline L \approx \frac{N_{\text{arrivals}}}{N_{\text{open hours}}\,T_A}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\overline L \approx \frac{N_{\text{completions}}}{N_{\text{open hours}}\,T_C}.
\]

These are not automatically the same quantity. If opening and closing backlog are \(B_0\) and \(B_1\),

\[
B_1-B_0=N_{\text{arrivals}}-N_{\text{completions}}.
\]

Thus, when \(A\ne C\), using arrivals or completions can shift the result. Neither rate is unconditionally correct for an unknown initial backlog.

The 144-minute baseline diagnostic is deliberately **not** replaced by its 25-minute service median. It is an in-system time diagnostic, potentially including waiting and carry-over. The high-mix values of 272 and 308 minutes are not estimates of queue waiting alone, and the difference between them is not a confidence interval for mean flow time.

“Open minutes” also require care: the ratio uses accumulated open time. It is not automatically mean elapsed calendar time for cases that experience overnight closure.

# 7. Operational implications

## Staffing

- **Protect service and recovery capacity, not just daily throughput.** On the first two recovery weekdays, completion intensity averages 10.04/hour while WIP averages 43.5. Treating this as normal productive capacity would remove the very capacity needed to clear the outage backlog.
- **Do not infer required parallel servers from the workload index alone.** Higher-demand weekdays have an index of 11.23 versus 7.04 in baseline weekdays, suggesting substantially more median-minute work per reported staffed hour. Actual staffing requires active-server availability, true mean service time, variant-specific work, and blocking information.
- **Use flexible capacity where the load occurs.** April 21’s largest arrivals are not confined to 08:00–10:00. A flat allocation across the whole day, or a plan based only on the morning peak, may leave late-day bursts unhandled.
- **Separate service improvement from backlog clearance.** Lower WIP and higher completions after April 29 are operationally favorable, but they do not show that one added FTE caused the full change.

## Backlog recovery

- Measure **07:00 opening WIP, closing WIP, case age, and cases carried from previous open days**.
- Separate completions of same-day arrivals from completions of prior-day arrivals. Without this, output can falsely appear to validate the current day’s staffing level.
- Track backlog clearance against explicit recovery objectives rather than classifying high output as a permanently higher-throughput regime.
- Avoid interpreting the outage’s 180 unavailable minutes as a fixed number of “lost cases”: the staffing effect, case eligibility for service, and work actually deferred are not supplied.

## Peak smoothing and routing

- The ordinary weekday already places approximately one-third of arrivals in two hours. Smoothing should target arrival timing, queue assignment, or flexible starts—not simply an across-the-day capacity average.
- Investigate the separate 09:00–10:00, 13:00–14:00, and 17:00–18:00 bursts on April 21. Morning-only smoothing would miss much of that day’s concentration.
- If complex routing is intended to protect long cases, collect service and waiting distributions **by variant**. The aggregate p90 cannot identify whether complex cases, simple cases, or both drive the tail.

## A policy that looks favorable when pooled but is risky when segmented

A pooled weekend view might support reducing weekend staffing because weekends have only **44.9 arrivals/day** and **17.4 mean WIP**. However:

- Baseline weekends already have higher WIP/FTE than baseline weekdays: **5.20 versus 3.42**.
- High-mix weekends have **8.60 WIP/FTE** and p90 service of **81–85 minutes**.
- Under the explicitly conditional median-minute index, reducing 2.5 to 2.0 weekend FTE would change the high-mix weekend index from about **11.14 to 11.60**, versus about **10.66** at the pooled baseline-weekend service level.

That is not proof that exactly two servers are insufficient, but it demonstrates why a pooled weekend cut can conceal a materially different service mix.

## Data collection and evaluation

The highest-value additions are:

1. Case-level arrival, activity start/end, resource, variant, priority, and disposition timestamps.
2. Exact staffing availability, breaks, and the April 29 implementation time.
3. Opening and closing backlog, including case age and prior-day origin.
4. Variant-specific service and queue distributions.
5. Outage start/end, affected resources, and whether cases were blocked or actively serviced.
6. Follow-up beyond May 3 for arrivals near the end of the observation period.

Raw event data would support case-flow medians and percentiles, variant-specific cycle times, hourly and daily overdispersion tests, resource-state comparisons, and interrupted time-series or difference-in-differences designs. A causal staffing estimate would still require a credible comparison strategy and adjustment for serial dependence, mix, peak demand, and prior backlog.

def lag_correlation(values, lag):
    x = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    centered = x - x.mean()
    numerator = np.dot(centered[:-lag], centered[lag:])
    denominator = np.sqrt(
        np.dot(centered[:-lag], centered[:-lag])
        * np.dot(centered[lag:], centered[lag:])
    )
    return numerator / denominator

# 4. Transparent contiguous segmentation heuristic.
#    This is descriptive, not a definitive change-point test.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

features = ["arrivals", "completed", "median_service_min", "wip_per_fte"]
x = daily[features].to_numpy(dtype=float)

# Robust standardization prevents minutes from dominating counts.
center = np.median(x, axis=0)
mad = np.median(np.abs(x - center), axis=0)
scale = 1.4826 * mad
scale[scale < 1e-12] = 1.0
z = (x - center) / scale

n, p = z.shape
max_segments = 7

# Dynamic programming minimizes within-segment multivariate SSE.
# A complexity penalty discourages excessive fragmentation.
cost = np.full((n + 1, max_segments + 1), np.inf)
back = np.full((n + 1, max_segments + 1), -1, dtype=int)
cost[0, 0] = 0.0

for segments in range(1, max_segments + 1):
    for end in range(segments, n + 1):
        for start in range(segments - 1, end):
            piece = z[start:end]
            sse = np.sum((piece - piece.mean(axis=0)) ** 2)
            candidate = cost[start, segments - 1] + sse
            if candidate < cost[end, segments]:
                cost[end, segments] = candidate
                back[end, segments] = start

penalty_per_segment = 2 * p * np.log(n)
candidate_segment_counts = range(3, max_segments + 1)
chosen_k = min(
    candidate_segment_counts,
    key=lambda k: cost[n, k] + penalty_per_segment * k
)

# Recover the chosen start indices.
starts = [0]
end = n
while end > 0:
    start = back[end, chosen_k]
    starts.append(start)
    end = start
starts = sorted(set(starts))

for start, end in zip(starts[:-1], starts[1:]):
    g = daily.iloc[start:end]

    g = daily[daily.date.between(start, end)]
    open_hours = 12 * len(g)

    arrivals_total = g["arrivals"].sum()
    completions_total = g["completed"].sum()
    arrival_rate = arrivals_total / open_hours
    completion_rate = completions_total / open_hours

    # Approximate mean in-system population from supplied daily WIP means.
    # All listed days have equal 12-hour opening windows.
    mean_wip = g["mean_wip"].mean()

    t_from_a = 60 * mean_wip / arrival_rate
    t_from_c = 60 * mean_wip / completion_rate
    t_reported = (
        t_from_a if report_proxy == "A"
        else t_from_c if report_proxy == "C"
        else t_from_a
    )

    ll_rows.append({
        "A_total": arrivals_total,
        "C_total": completions_total,
        "mean_WIP": mean_wip,
        "A_per_open_h": arrival_rate,
        "C_per_open_h": completion_rate,
        "T_from_A_open_min": t_from_a,
        "T_from_C_open_min": t_from_c,
        "reported_proxy": report_proxy,
        "reported_T_open_min": t_reported,
