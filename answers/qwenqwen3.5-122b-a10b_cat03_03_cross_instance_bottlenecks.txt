## 1. Interaction-graph description

The manufacturing system functions as a dynamic typed interaction graph where processes emerge from the convergence of independent object instances. The graph is defined by node types representing physical and logical entities, and directed edges representing dependencies, ownership, or temporal precedence.

**Important Node Types:**
*   **Process Objects:** `Job` (J1–J6), `Component` (C1–C6). These represent the work items flowing through the system.
*   **Resource Objects:** `Machine` (CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1), `Tool` (FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3). These represent capacity constraints.
*   **Grouping Objects:** `ComponentLot` (L7, L8), `Batch` (B1, B2), `Shipment` (S1–S3). These represent synchronization policies that couple otherwise independent jobs.

**Important Edge Types and Qualifiers:**
*   `requires`: Links Job to Component (e.g., J1  C1).
*   `contains`: Links Lot to Component (e.g., L7  C2).
*   `batches`: Links Job to Batch (e.g., J1  B1).
*   `consolidates`: Links Job to Shipment (e.g., J1  S1).
*   `uses` (Inferred): Links Job/Machine to Tool based on acquire/release timestamps (e.g., J3  FIX-A).
*   `waits_for` (Inferred): Derived from timestamp gaps where an object is ready but cannot proceed due to another object's state (e.g., J1 waits for B1 start).

**Higher-Order Motifs:**
1.  **The Batch Gate:** A motif involving `Job` + `Batch` + `Machine` + `Tool`. Here, three Jobs (J1, J2, J3) converge on a Batch (B1), which requires a specific Machine (OVEN-1) and Tool (RACK-7). The availability of the Tool dictates the Machine's ability to process the Batch, creating a multi-object dependency chain.
2.  **The Lot Lock:** A motif involving `ComponentLot` + `Component` + `Job`. Two Jobs (J2, J5) depend on Components belonging to the same Lot (L7). A status change in the Lot (Quarantine) propagates constraints to both Jobs, even if they are in different production stages.
3.  **The Shipment Buffer:** A motif involving `Job` + `Pack` + `Shipment`. A Job (J1) completes its process but enters a holding state because its partner Job (J4) has not finished. The `Shipment` object acts as a sink that buffers early completions.

## 4. What flattening misses

**Job-flattened View:**
*   **Visible:** Total duration, sequence of steps (Stage -> Process -> Inspect).
*   **Hidden:** Synchronization waits (Batch/Ship) appear as generic "processing time."
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "J1 takes 10 hours total." In reality, J1 spends 6 hours waiting for J4 to pack, not working. Managers may optimize the wrong step.

**Machine-flattened View:**
*   **Visible:** Utilization rates, event sequences (Setup -> Process).
*   **Hidden:** Why the machine is idle (e.g., waiting for RACK-7 cleaning or B2 readiness).
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "OVEN-1 has spare capacity." Managers might schedule more jobs, worsening congestion without addressing the root cause (Rack cleaning policy).

**Batch-flattened View:**
*   **Visible:** Batch start/end times, member list.
*   **Hidden:** Individual job readiness variance (J1 ready 08:10 vs J3 ready 11:55).
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "Batching is efficient." It hides that two-thirds of the batch arrived 4 hours early.

**Tool-flattened View:**
*   **Visible:** Tool usage frequency (if logged).
*   **Hidden:** Which jobs are competing (J3 vs J4 for FIX-A).
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "Tools are sufficient." Without job linkage, we miss the contention queues.

**ComponentLot View:**
*   **Visible:** Lot lifecycle (Release/Quarantine).
*   **Hidden:** Impact on specific downstream Jobs (J5 blocked, J2 overridden).
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "Lots move smoothly." We miss the divergent paths caused by overrides.

**Shipment View:**
*   **Visible:** Dispatch times.
*   **Hidden:** Packing-to-dispatch lag (J1 packed 13:30, shipped 19:25).
*   **Misleading Conclusion:** "Shipping is fast." It ignores the inventory holding cost of packed goods.

**Why Low Utilization Coexists with Long Waiting:**
Traditional theory suggests low utilization implies free capacity. However, in object-centric systems, capacity is not just about the machine running; it is about the *convergence* of resources. OVEN-1 is idle not because there is no work, but because RACK-7 is unavailable and B2 is incomplete. The "resource" required is a composite of Machine + Tool + Batch. When any component of that composite is missing, the effective capacity drops to zero, regardless of the machine's raw availability.

**Observations that would weaken the top-ranked explanation (Batch Synchronization):**
1.  **Material Shortage Logs:** Evidence that J1 was actually waiting for C1 delivery at 08:10, not just sitting ready.
2.  **Oven Maintenance Records:** Proof that OVEN-1 was under maintenance during the 08:10–12:05 window.

5.  **Priority Overrides:** Logs showing J1 was deprioritized intentionally for higher-value orders.
6.  **External Quality Checks:** Evidence that components required external inspection before batching, adding valid time.

**Additional fields or event types to discriminate among hypotheses:**
1.  `Job.Ready_Time`: Explicit timestamp when a job is physically available for the next step.
2.  `Batch.Max_Wait_Time`: Policy parameter defining how long a batch waits for latecomers.
3.  `Tool.Availability_Status`: Real-time status of FIX-A/CAL-3 (Free, Busy, Cleaning).
4.  `Lot.Quality_Result`: Specific reason for L7 Quarantine (e.g., "Contamination" vs "Pending Test").
5.  `Supervisor.Override_Log`: Record of when and why J2 bypassed L7 quarantine.
6.  `Packing.Completion_Time`: Precise moment a job is sealed, separate from Shipment Creation.

These fields distinguish between *systemic policy delays* (Batch Max Wait), *operational constraints* (Tool Status), and *exception handling* (Override Logs), allowing the model to pinpoint whether the delay is a bug or a feature of the current design.
