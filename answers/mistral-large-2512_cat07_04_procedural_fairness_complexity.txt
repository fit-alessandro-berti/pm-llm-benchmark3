# Procedural Fairness Assessment of Public-Benefits Case-Management Process

## 1. Fairness dimensions

### Outcome parity
Raw approval rates differ substantially (78% for Group A vs 58-69% for others), but this alone doesn't indicate unfairness as groups differ in case complexity.

### Adjusted outcome disparity
After controlling for documented complexity factors, Group B still shows a 5.5pp lower approval probability and Group C 2.2pp lower. These adjusted disparities suggest potential procedural unfairness beyond case mix differences.

### Procedural burden
- **Variant complexity**: Groups B and C experience more complex paths (V4, V5) with more steps and handovers
- **Documentation requests**: Group B has 9.5pp higher probability of "Request Missing Documents" even after adjustment
- **Supervisor reviews**: Groups B (+8pp) and C (+5.5pp) more likely to require supervisor review
- **Appeal rates**: Group B (13%) and D (16%) have higher appeal rates than Group A (6%)

### Timeliness
- **Cycle time disparities**: Group B (+2.6 days) and C (+4.1 days) have longer adjusted cycle times
- **Waiting time disparities**: Language support (3.8d wait) and accommodation review (4.7d wait) create bottlenecks
- **90th percentile times**: Group B (34.2d) and C (38.8d) have much longer tails than Group A (21.4d)

### Consistency of routing
- **Legitimate routing**: Higher risk cases (Group D) appropriately routed to fraud review
- **Concerning patterns**:
  - Group B disproportionately routed through language support (24% in V4 vs 1% for others)
  - Group C disproportionately routed through accommodation review (31% in V5 vs 2-3% for others)
  - These rights-preserving steps appear to create procedural burdens

### Quality/accessibility of support
- **Language support**: Only 7 bilingual caseworkers serve 57% of Group B, creating bottlenecks
- **Accommodation review**: Specialized team queue creates 4.7d wait for Group C
- **Policy gap**: No separate timeliness targets for support steps despite being rights-preserving

### Appeal/rework burden
- **Appeal rates**: Group B (13%) and D (16%) have higher appeal rates than Group A (6%)
- **Rework rates**: Group B (22%) and C (24%) have higher rework rates than Group A (11%)
- **Adjusted disparities**: Group B (+4.4pp) and D (+5.7pp) more likely to appeal after adjustment

### Resource-allocation fairness
- **Concentration**: Group B cases handled by 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers
- **Specialization**: Group C cases handled by 5 accommodation specialists (2 also do supervisor review)
- **Workload**: In-person applications (more common for Groups B/C) have longer initial waits
- **Handover patterns**: Groups B/C experience more handovers between generalist and specialist staff

## 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

**1. Matching/stratification variables:**
- Primary: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month
- Secondary: language_preference, disability_accommodation_requested (to test if these create additional burden beyond their legitimate routing)

**2. Path/variant comparison:**
- Compare variant distributions within matched strata
- Test whether Groups B/C are disproportionately routed through more complex variants (V4, V5) after matching
- Compare cycle times and waiting times within variants

**3. Resource-assignment comparison:**
- Compare resource assignment patterns within matched cases
- Test whether Groups B/C are more likely to be assigned to specialized/overloaded resources
- Compare handover patterns (number and type of handovers) within matched cases

**4. Waiting-time decomposition:**
- Insert case service/waiting times using `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`
- Decompose total cycle time into:
  - Service time (actual processing time)
  - Waiting time (queue time)
  - Handover time (time between resource assignments)
- Compare each component within matched cases

**5. Sensitivity analysis:**
- Test whether risk_score_band or documentation_completeness might be biased:
  - Compare approval rates for cases with similar risk scores but different groups
  - Test whether documentation completeness is lower for Group B due to language barriers
- Test alternative matching specifications (e.g., excluding risk_score_band)

**6. Subgroup checks:**
- By region: Test whether disparities are concentrated in certain regions
- By channel: Test whether in-person vs online differences explain disparities
- By risk band: Test whether disparities exist within low-risk cases

## 3. Path and resource disparities

**Potentially justified disparities:**
- Group D's higher share in V3 (fraud review path) is appropriate given their 51% high risk_score_band share
- Group C's higher share in V5 (accommodation review path) is appropriate given 62% accommodation requests
- Group B's higher share in V4 (language support path) is appropriate given 57% language support needs

**Concerning disparities (even after adjustment):**
- **Waiting times for support steps**:
  - Language Support Contact  Eligibility: 3.8d wait (only affects Group B)
  - Accommodation Review  Eligibility: 4.7d wait (only affects Group C)
  - These create additional burden beyond the legitimate routing

- **Handover patterns**:
  - Group B: general  bilingual  general caseworker handovers
  - Group C: general  accommodation specialist  supervisor handovers
  - These create additional coordination delays and potential for information loss

- **Documentation requests**:
  - Group B has 9.5pp higher probability of "Request Missing Documents" after adjustment
  - Suggests either:
    - Language barriers make documentation harder to complete
    - Caseworkers are more likely to request additional documents from Group B applicants

- **Supervisor reviews**:
  - Groups B (+8pp) and C (+5.5pp) more likely to require supervisor review after adjustment
  - Could indicate:
    - More complex cases (but we've controlled for complexity)
    - More conservative decision-making for these groups
    - More frequent quality checks for these groups

- **Appeal rates**:
  - Group B (+4.4pp) and D (+5.7pp) more likely to appeal after adjustment
  - Suggests either:
    - Poorer quality decisions/explanations for these groups
    - Higher dissatisfaction with process

## 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Legitimate mechanisms:**
1. **Complexity-based routing**: Higher risk cases appropriately routed to fraud review; accommodation/language needs appropriately routed to support steps
2. **Resource specialization**: Bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists handle appropriate cases
3. **Documentation challenges**: Some groups may genuinely have more difficulty completing documentation
4. **Regional variation**: Some disparities may reflect regional differences in workload or capacity

**Concerning mechanisms:**
1. **Support step bottlenecks**: Rights-preserving steps (language support, accommodation review) create waiting times that aren't counted against service-level targets
2. **Handover inefficiencies**: Cases requiring support steps experience more handovers, creating delays and potential for information loss
3. **Risk score bias**: risk_score_band may encode historical disparities, leading to more conservative treatment of certain groups
4. **Documentation bias**: Caseworkers may be more likely to request additional documents from certain groups, even when documentation is complete
5. **Decision-making bias**: Caseworkers may require more supervisor reviews for certain groups, even after controlling for complexity
6. **Resource allocation**: Specialized resources (bilingual caseworkers, accommodation specialists) may be under-resourced relative to demand

## 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias:**
- **risk_score_band**: May reflect historical disparities in how risk is assessed rather than true risk
- **documentation_completeness**: May be affected by language barriers or caseworker subjectivity
- **case_outcome**: Doesn't capture quality of explanation or dignity of treatment

**Missing applicant needs:**
- Unobserved barriers (e.g., digital literacy, transportation access)
- Quality of interaction with caseworkers
- Understanding of decision rationale
- Perceived fairness of process

**Unobserved resource workload:**
- Daily granularity may hide peak workload periods
- No data on caseworker experience or training
- No data on caseworker workload beyond assignment counts

**Appeal quality:**
- No data on why appeals are filed
- No data on quality of appeal decisions
- No data on whether appeals correct errors or reflect applicant persistence

**Historical bias:**
- Prior_case_count may reflect past discrimination
- risk_score_band may be trained on historically biased data
- Documentation standards may be biased against certain groups

## 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**1. Add capacity for language support/accommodation review**
- *Benefits*: Reduce waiting times for rights-preserving steps, improve timeliness
- *Side effects*: Increased costs, may increase demand for these services
- *Implementation*: Hire more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists

**2. Service-level targets for support steps**
- *Benefits*: Ensure rights-preserving steps don't create undue delays, improve accountability
- *Side effects*: May require additional resources, could create perverse incentives to rush
- *Implementation*: Set separate timeliness targets for language support and accommodation review

**3. Routing redesign to reduce handovers**
- *Benefits*: Reduce coordination delays, improve efficiency
- *Side effects*: May reduce specialization benefits, could increase training needs
- *Implementation*: Allow bilingual caseworkers to complete entire cases, not just language support

**4. Decision-explanation improvements**
- *Benefits*: Reduce appeals, improve perceived fairness
- *Side effects*: Increased caseworker time per case
- *Implementation*: Standardized templates, training on clear communication

**5. Monitoring adjusted disparities**
- *Benefits*: Track whether disparities persist after controlling for complexity
- *Side effects*: May create gaming incentives, requires careful specification
- *Implementation*: Regular reporting on adjusted approval rates, cycle times, appeal rates

**6. Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**
- *Benefits*: Identify and correct potential biases in these measures
- *Side effects*: May reveal uncomfortable truths, requires statistical expertise
- *Implementation*: Regular audits of risk scoring model, documentation standards

**7. Workload balancing**
- *Benefits*: Reduce disparities in waiting times, improve efficiency
- *Side effects*: May require caseworker flexibility, could reduce specialization
- *Implementation*: Dynamic case assignment based on real-time workload

## 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence suggests **probable procedural unfairness** in the case-management process, though the exact nature and causes remain somewhat uncertain due to data limitations.

**Key findings supporting unfairness:**
1. **Adjusted disparities persist** after controlling for documented complexity, particularly for Group B (-5.5pp approval, +2.6d cycle time) and Group C (+4.1d cycle time)
2. **Rights-preserving support steps** (language support, accommodation review) create **additional procedural burdens** through:
   - Long waiting times (3.8-4.7 days)
   - Additional handovers between resources
   - No separate timeliness targets
3. **Higher rates of documentation requests and supervisor reviews** for Groups B and C even after adjustment suggest potential bias in caseworker behavior
4. **Higher appeal rates** for Groups B and D after adjustment suggest potential issues with decision quality or explanation

**Important caveats:**
1. **Risk score and documentation completeness** may themselves be biased, meaning our "adjusted" comparisons may still understate disparities
2. **Unobserved complexity factors** could explain some remaining disparities
3. **Data limitations** prevent assessing quality of interactions, decision explanations, or applicant perceptions
4. **Some disparities** (e.g., fraud review for high-risk cases) are appropriate

**Overall assessment:**
The process appears to create **disproportionate procedural burdens** for Groups B and C through:
- Bottlenecks in rights-preserving support steps
- More complex routing with more handovers
- Longer waiting times at multiple points
- Higher rates of documentation requests and supervisor reviews

While some disparities may reflect legitimate complexity differences, the **pattern of adjusted disparities** and the **specific mechanisms** (support step bottlenecks, handover inefficiencies) suggest **systemic procedural unfairness** that warrants mitigation. The evidence is **strong enough to justify intervention** but not definitive enough to conclude intentional discrimination.
