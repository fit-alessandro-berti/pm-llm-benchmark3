# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let case index be \(c\), and branch index be \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\). All durations below are in minutes unless stated otherwise.

Observed timestamps:

- `arrival_c`
- `fork_release_c`
- `start_{c,i}`, `end_{c,i}` for branches A, B, C
- `final_start_c`, `final_end_c`

Definitions:

1. **Upstream elapsed time before fork release**

\[
upstream_c = fork\_release_c - arrival_c
\]

2. **Branch-specific queue waiting**

Because `fork_release_c` is the earliest time branch work is permitted, branch queue waiting is:

\[
q_{c,i} = start_{c,i} - fork\_release_c
\]

3. **Branch active service**

\[
s_{c,i} = end_{c,i} - start_{c,i}
\]

4. **Branch elapsed time from fork release to completion**

\[
T_{c,i} = q_{c,i} + s_{c,i} = end_{c,i} - fork\_release_c
\]

5. **Join readiness**

Under the stated rule, `join_ready` is the latest branch completion:

\[
join\_ready_c = \max_i end_{c,i}
\]

6. **Elapsed fork-join stage time**

\[
fork\_join_c = join\_ready_c - fork\_release_c
\]

Equivalently:

\[
fork\_join_c = \max_i T_{c,i}
\]

7. **Branch-specific synchronization waiting after completion**

For branch \(i\):

\[
sync_{c,i} = join\_ready_c - end_{c,i}
\]

Equivalently:

\[
sync_{c,i} = fork\_join_c - T_{c,i}
\]

8. **Downstream queueing before final activity F**

Under the stated model assumption:

\[
downstream_c = final\_start_c - join\_ready_c
\]

This is observed downstream delay. It may be a true queue, but it could also include release policy, setup, or other unobserved waiting.

9. **Final service**

\[
final_c = final\_end_c - final\_start_c
\]

10. **Total case flow time**

\[
total_c = final\_end_c - arrival_c
\]

The additive decomposition is:

\[
total_c = upstream_c + fork\_join_c + downstream_c + final_c
\]

## Why summing all branch synchronization waits double counts

For every branch \(i\):

\[
q_{c,i} + s_{c,i} + sync_{c,i}
= (end_{c,i} - fork\_release_c) + (join\_ready_c - end_{c,i})
= join\_ready_c - fork\_release_c
= fork\_join_c
\]

Therefore:

\[
\sum_i (q_{c,i} + s_{c,i} + sync_{c,i})
= 3 \times fork\_join_c
\]

The fork-join stage is a calendar interval from `fork_release_c` to `join_ready_c`. It is already fully represented by the latest-completing branch’s queue plus service, or by `fork_join_c`. The synchronization waits of the other branches are not additional elapsed case time; they are idle waiting of branches that finished earlier.

For example, in F02:

- `fork_join` = 60 minutes
- A sync wait = 10 minutes
- B sync wait = 0 minutes
- C sync wait = 35 minutes
- Sum of sync waits = 45 minutes

Adding the 45 minutes of synchronization waiting to the 60-minute fork-join stage would imply 105 minutes of fork-join elapsed time, which is false. The case did not wait 105 minutes between fork release and join readiness; it waited 60 minutes.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

For counterfactuals, I distinguish two quantities:

1. **Join-ready savings**: reduction in `join_ready` relative to the observed case. This is the direct fork-join benefit.
2. **Estimated total-flow savings**: an estimate of how much `final_end` would improve if the earlier `join_ready` could actually translate into an earlier `final_start`.

The estimated total-flow savings use a conservative assumption:

- Final-station resource availability is inferred from the previous case on the same `final_resource`.
- The inferred resource-free time is held fixed.
- Any unexplained downstream delay after the later of `join_ready` and resource-free time is held constant.
- Therefore, branch acceleration cannot save time if the final resource is still busy after the new `join_ready`.

This is an estimate, not an observed causal effect.

| case | critical_branch | CF1 A service 20%: join savings | CF1 A service 20%: est. total savings | CF2 B queue 50%: join savings | CF2 B queue 50%: est. total savings | CF3 eliminate resource-induced downstream wait: savings | CF3 eliminate all downstream queue: savings |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| F01 | A | 5.0 | 5.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 8.0 |
| F02 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 10.0 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 |
| F03 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 10.0 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 |
| F04 | C | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 8.0 | 18.0 |
| F05 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 10.0 | 0.0 | 10.0 | 10.0 |
| F06 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 |
| F07 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 | 15.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 |
| F08 | C | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 15.0 | 15.0 |
| F09 | B | 0.0 | 0.0 | 12.5 | 0.0 | 15.0 | 15.0 |

| F11 | A | 10.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 20.0 | 20.0 |

## Counterfactual 1: Reduce A service time by 20%

A service is 50 minutes in every case, so a 20% reduction makes A service 40 minutes and moves `A_end` earlier by 10 minutes.

Direct fork-join benefits:

- F01:
  - Original `A_end` = 09:00.
  - New `A_end` = 08:50.
  - B completes at 08:55, so `join_ready` moves from 09:00 to 08:55.
  - Join-ready savings = 5 minutes.
- F11:
  - Original `A_end` = 11:30.
  - New `A_end` = 11:20.
  - B completes at 11:20, so `join_ready` moves from 11:30 to 11:20.
  - Join-ready savings = 10 minutes.
- All other cases:
  - A is not the latest branch even after being accelerated.
  - Join-ready savings = 0.

Estimated total-flow benefits:

- F01: 5 minutes under the conservative final-resource assumption.
- F11: 0 minutes because F11 is on F1, and F1 is already occupied until 11:50 by F09. Even if F11 becomes ready at 11:20 instead of 11:30, it cannot start before 11:50 under the fixed-resource-floor assumption.
- All other cases: 0 minutes.

Zero-benefit cases:

- F02 through F10 except F11, and F12, have zero fork-join benefit because another branch remains critical.
- F11 has fork-join benefit but likely zero total-flow benefit under the conservative final-resource assumption.

Likely secondary effects:

- A becomes less critical; B becomes more likely to be the controlling branch.
- A synchronization wait may increase in cases where A no longer finishes last.
- A resource may become idle earlier, but because A queue is zero in all observed cases, A does not appear to be the main queueing bottleneck.
- This counterfactual has limited aggregate benefit: 15 minutes of join-ready savings, and only 5 estimated total-flow minutes under the conservative assumption.

## Counterfactual 2: Reduce B queue delay by 50%

B queue is reduced by half. B service is unchanged, so `B_end` moves earlier by 0.5 × `B_queue`.

Direct fork-join benefits:

- F02: B queue 20  10; `join_ready` reduces by 10 minutes.
- F03: B queue 20  10; `join_ready` reduces by 10 minutes.
- F05: B queue 25  12.5; B would finish at 09:57.5, but A finishes at 10:00; `join_ready` reduces by 10 minutes, not 12.5.
- F06: B queue 30  15; B and A both finish at 10:15; `join_ready` reduces by 15 minutes.
- F07: B queue 30  15; `join_ready` reduces by 15 minutes.
- F09: B queue 25  12.5; B finishes at 11:02.5, A at 11:00; `join_ready` reduces by 12.5 minutes.
- F10: B queue 20  10; B finishes at 11:10, A at 11:15; `join_ready` reduces by 5 minutes, not 10.
- F12: B queue 30  15; `join_ready` reduces by 15 minutes.
- F01, F04, F08, F11: zero fork-join benefit because B is not critical and remains noncritical after acceleration.

Estimated total-flow benefits:

- F02: 10 minutes.
- F03: 10 minutes.
- F07: 15 minutes.
- F12: 10 minutes, even though join-ready savings are 15 minutes, because the inferred F2 resource-free time is 12:00 and the original `join_ready` is only 10 minutes after that.
- F05, F06, F09, F10: zero estimated total-flow savings under the conservative final-resource assumption, even though they have join-ready savings.
- F01, F04, F08, F11: zero join-ready savings and therefore zero total-flow savings.

Zero-benefit cases:

- F01, F04, F08, F11 have zero benefit because another branch remains critical.
- F05, F06, F09, F10 have fork-join benefits but likely zero total-flow benefit under the conservative final-resource assumption because final-station resource availability or downstream delay masks the earlier readiness.

Likely secondary effects:

- B synchronization wait decreases substantially.
- A synchronization wait may increase in cases where B no longer finishes last, especially F05 and F10.
- If B queue is caused by capacity, reducing it may require additional B resources or better scheduling.
- If B queue is caused by release or scheduling policy, the improvement may be achievable without adding hardware.
- This is the strongest branch-level counterfactual: 92.5 minutes of join-ready savings, and 45 estimated total-flow minutes under the conservative assumption.

## Counterfactual 3: Add final-station capacity or otherwise reduce downstream queueing

Observed downstream delay totals 176 minutes across 12 cases, with a mean of 14.7 minutes.

I split observed downstream delay into two inferred components:

- Resource-induced waiting: waiting because the assigned final resource appears to still be busy from the previous case on that resource.
- Unexplained downstream delay: delay beyond the later of `join_ready` and inferred resource-free time. This could be release policy, setup, batch release, or unobserved constraints.

Inferred resource-induced downstream waiting:

|---|---:|

| F02 | 0 |
| F03 | 0 |
| F04 | 8 |
| F05 | 10 |
| F06 | 0 |
| F07 | 0 |
| F08 | 15 |
| F09 | 15 |

| F12 | 0 |

If added final-station capacity eliminates only resource-induced waiting, the estimated aggregate saving is 73 minutes, or 6.1 minutes per case.

If all downstream queueing is eliminated, the aggregate saving is 176 minutes, or 14.7 minutes per case.

Direct benefits:

- Cases with resource-induced waiting benefit from added capacity or better resource scheduling: F04, F05, F08, F09, F10, F11.
- Cases with zero inferred resource-induced waiting do not benefit from capacity alone: F01, F02, F03, F06, F07, F12.
- Those cases would benefit only if the unexplained downstream delay is also reduced, for example by changing release policy.

Zero-benefit cases:

- There are no zero-benefit cases due to another branch remaining critical, because this counterfactual acts after `join_ready`.
- There are zero-benefit cases under the “capacity only” interpretation if the downstream delay is not resource-induced.

Likely secondary effects:

- Adding final-station capacity may reduce final-station utilization per resource.
- If the downstream delay is mainly release policy, adding capacity may have little effect.
- Reducing downstream delay makes branch delays more visible; B and A become more clearly the upstream controllers.
- Earlier final starts may change final-resource schedules and could reduce tail cases such as F10 and F11.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Redesign 1: Reduce B-branch queue delay

**Rationale**

B is the strongest controller of completion time:

- B is critical in 8 of 12 cases.
- B has the largest mean branch elapsed time: 56.7 minutes.
- B has the largest tail: 75 minutes in F07 and F12.
- A 50% reduction in B queue produces 92.5 minutes of join-ready savings, the largest branch-level counterfactual.

**Throughput**

- If B queue is caused by capacity, adding B capacity or rebalancing work can increase B throughput.
- If B queue is caused by scheduling, priority rules, pre-staging, or better release sequencing may reduce queue without adding capacity.
- Because B queue is variable and often large, reducing it should improve the effective throughput of the fork-join stage.

**Flow-time tail**

- The worst fork-join cases are B-critical: F07 and F12 both have 75-minute fork-join elapsed time.
- Reducing B queue by 50% would reduce those from 75 to 60 minutes.
- F06 would reduce from 65 to 55 minutes, and F09 from 65 to 52.5 minutes.
- This directly attacks the upper tail of total flow time.

**Resource utilization**

- Branch resource identities are not observed, so B utilization cannot be measured directly.
- If B queue is due to an underutilized resource being released late, utilization may rise.
- If B queue is due to a saturated B resource, reducing queue may require additional B capacity.
- If B queue is reduced without adding capacity, B may simply process work earlier; the system-wide effect depends on what caused the queue.

**Possible new bottlenecks**

- A has a constant 50-minute service and zero queue. If B is accelerated below 50 minutes in some cases, A becomes the floor.
- F05 and F10 are examples where B acceleration makes A the effective join-ready driver.
- C may also become more relevant if B is improved, especially in cases where C queue is high, such as F04 and F08.

**Synchronization effects**

- B synchronization wait will decrease.
- A and C synchronization waits may increase in cases where B no longer finishes last.
- The total case flow improves only when B was the critical or near-critical branch.

**What to pilot or measure first**

- Instrument B resource availability, if identifiable.
- Measure why B starts after `fork_release`: resource busy, scheduling delay, material readiness, operator availability, or release policy.
- Pilot a priority rule or pre-staging for B in a subset of cases.
- Track:
  - `B_start - fork_release`
  - `join_ready`
  - critical-branch frequency
  - `final_start - join_ready`
  - total flow time

## Redesign 2: Diagnose and reduce final-station downstream queueing

**Rationale**

Downstream delay is large enough to mask branch improvements:

- Mean downstream delay is 14.7 minutes.
- Total downstream delay is 176 minutes.
- Some branch improvements, especially for F11, F05, F09, and F10, are likely masked by final-station availability or release policy.

**Throughput**

- Final service is constant at 20 minutes.
- There are two final resources, F1 and F2.
- Observed final-station busy time is 12 × 20 = 240 minutes.
- Average final-station utilization over the observed day is moderate, but local resource constraints still appear in the data.
- If downstream delay is resource-induced, adding capacity or improving scheduling can increase throughput.
- If downstream delay is release-policy-driven, capacity may not help; scheduling or policy change is needed.

**Flow-time tail**

- Downstream delay contributes directly to the tail.
- F10 and F11 have 20 minutes of downstream delay.
- F04 has 18 minutes.
- Eliminating resource-induced waiting saves 73 minutes aggregate.
- Eliminating all downstream delay saves 176 minutes aggregate.

**Resource utilization**

- Adding a third final resource would reduce utilization per resource.
- If the current final resources are already underutilized on average, added capacity may be idle.
- A policy or scheduling change may be more cost-effective than added hardware.
- The key diagnostic is whether `final_start - join_ready` is driven by resource unavailability or by release/setup policy.

**Possible new bottlenecks**

- If downstream delay is reduced, B and A become more visible as the main upstream controllers.
- Upstream elapsed time is 15–20 minutes and could become relatively more important.
- C queue may become more relevant in cases where C is unusually delayed.

**Synchronization effects**

- Reducing downstream delay does not change branch synchronization waits directly.
- It does reduce total flow time after `join_ready`.
- Earlier final starts may alter final-resource schedules and could reduce congestion later in the day.

**What to pilot or measure first**

- Log the final-station release rule:
  - Is F started immediately when `join_ready` occurs?
  - Is there a batch release time?
  - Is there setup or handoff delay?
- Measure final-resource idle time and resource-free times.
- Compare observed `final_start` with the later of `join_ready` and inferred resource-free time.
- Pilot one of the following:
  - Release F as soon as `join_ready` occurs.
  - Add a temporary third final resource.
  - Rebalance F1/F2 assignments.
- Track:
  - `final_start - join_ready`
  - resource-induced wait versus unexplained delay
  - final-station utilization
  - total flow time

    ("F12", "10:35", "10:55", "10:55", "11:45", "11:25", "12:10", "11:00", "11:18", "12:20", "12:40", "F2"),
]

df = pd.DataFrame(
    raw,
    columns=[
        "case",
        "arrival",
        "fork_release",
        "A_start",
        "A_end",
        "B_start",
        "B_end",
        "C_start",
        "C_end",
        "final_start",
        "final_end",
        "final_resource",
    ],
)

time_cols = [
    "arrival",
    "fork_release",
    "A_start",
    "A_end",
    "B_start",
    "B_end",
    "C_start",
    "C_end",
    "final_start",
    "final_end",
]

for col in time_cols:
    df[col] = pd.to_datetime(date + " " + df[col])


def minutes_between(start, end):
    """Return minutes from start to end as a pandas Series."""
    return (end - start).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Calculate every decomposition component with pandas
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
df["upstream_elapsed"] = minutes_between(df["arrival"], df["fork_release"])

for b in ["A", "B", "C"]:
    df[f"{b}_queue"] = minutes_between(df["fork_release"], df[f"{b}_start"])
    df[f"{b}_service"] = minutes_between(df[f"{b}_start"], df[f"{b}_end"])

# join_ready is max-based fork-join logic.
df["join_ready"] = df[["A_end", "B_end", "C_end"]].max(axis=1)

for b in ["A", "B", "C"]:
    df[f"{b}_sync"] = minutes_between(df[f"{b}_end"], df["join_ready"])

df["fork_join_elapsed"] = minutes_between(df["fork_release"], df["join_ready"])
df["downstream_queue"] = minutes_between(df["join_ready"], df["final_start"])
df["final_service"] = minutes_between(df["final_start"], df["final_end"])
df["total_flow_time"] = minutes_between(df["arrival"], df["final_end"])


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Identify critical branches with deterministic tie handling
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
def critical_branch(row):
    """
    Deterministic tie handling:
    - If multiple branches end at the same latest time, choose A, then B, then C.
    - In the supplied data there are no exact ties in join_ready.
    """
    latest = max(row["A_end"], row["B_end"], row["C_end"])
    for b in ["A", "B", "C"]:
        if row[f"{b}_end"] == latest:
            return b
    return "TIE"


df["critical_branch"] = df.apply(critical_branch, axis=1)
df["critical_tie_count"] = df[["A_end", "B_end", "C_end"]].eq(df[["join_ready"]]).sum(axis=1)


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# Infer final-resource availability for conservative total-flow estimates.
# This is an assumption: the previous case on the same final_resource defines
# the earliest time that resource could become available.
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------

df_sorted["resource_free"] = (
    df_sorted.groupby("final_resource")["final_end"]
    .shift(1)
    .fillna(df["arrival"].min())
)
df = df.merge(df_sorted[["case", "resource_free"]], on="case", how="left")

df["resource_wait"] = np.maximum(
    0.0,
    minutes_between(df["join_ready"], df["resource_free"]),
)
df["unexplained_downstream"] = df["downstream_queue"] - df["resource_wait"]

# Maximum total-flow saving from an earlier join_ready, holding resource_free
# and unexplained downstream delay fixed.
join_minus_free = np.maximum(
    0.0,
    minutes_between(df["join_ready"], df["resource_free"]),
)


# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Run the three counterfactuals
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# CF1: reduce A service time by 20%
df["cf1_new_A_end"] = df["A_start"] + pd.to_timedelta(df["A_service"] * 0.8, unit="m")
df["cf1_new_join"] = df[["cf1_new_A_end", "B_end", "C_end"]].max(axis=1)
df["cf1_d_join"] = minutes_between(df["cf1_new_join"], df["join_ready"])
df["cf1_est_total"] = np.minimum(df["cf1_d_join"], join_minus_free)

# CF2: reduce B queue delay by 50%

# 7. Limits of inference

The timestamps support a decomposition, but they do not identify all causal or operational mechanisms.

1. **Causality is not identified**

   - `B_start - fork_release` is observed queue waiting, but the data do not show why B waited.
   - It could be resource unavailability, scheduling delay, material readiness, operator availability, or release policy.
   - Counterfactual savings are therefore estimates under assumptions, not proven causal effects.

2. **Branch resource capacity is not observed**

   - The data do not identify which resources perform A, B, or C.
   - We cannot measure branch resource utilization, capacity, or whether a branch is truly capacity-constrained.
   - A branch with high queue may be constrained by a single resource, or it may be released late by policy.

3. **Work preemption is not observable**

   - The data show `start` and `end` timestamps only.
   - If work was interrupted, paused, or preempted, the computed service time is total elapsed service time, not pure active processing time.
   - The decomposition assumes continuous activity between `start` and `end`.

4. **`join_ready` is inferred, not directly logged**

   - The stated rule defines `join_ready` as the latest branch completion.
   - If the real system uses a different join rule, hidden hold, or partial release, the inferred `join_ready` may be wrong.

5. **Final-station waiting may not be a true queue**

   - `final_start - join_ready` is labeled downstream queueing under the stated model, but it may include:
     - release policy,
     - setup time,
     - handoff delay,
     - batch release,
     - unobserved resource constraints,
     - or administrative hold.
   - The inferred split into resource-induced waiting and unexplained delay uses the previous case on the same final resource. That assumes no unobserved cases and stable resource assignment.

6. **Counterfactual total-flow savings are bounded, not exact**

   - Join-ready savings are exact under the stated counterfactual changes to branch timestamps.
   - Total-flow savings depend on how final-station start responds to earlier `join_ready`.
   - The conservative estimate assumes fixed final-resource availability and constant unexplained downstream delay.
   - The upper bound assumes downstream queue remains unchanged and final start moves earlier one-for-one with `join_ready`.

7. **Small sample and single-day limitation**

   - There are only 12 cases on one day.
   - Tail estimates, such as maximum queue or maximum fork-join time, are sensitive to individual cases.
   - Aggregate averages are descriptive for this sample, not stable population estimates.
