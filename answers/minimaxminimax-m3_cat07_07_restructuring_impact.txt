# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail-latency masking.** M/M/c queues grow non-linearly above ~80% utilization. Average cycle time can fall while p90 rises sharply. Need p90, p95, and max-case-duration tracked by complexity and segment. `pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe` exposes skew and bimodality that means hide.

2. **High-complexity case degradation.** Routing rule sends all high-complexity cases to TSS, so they now share a queue with mid-complexity work. Average may improve because mid-complexity cases dominate volume, but the long tail — the cases where delay matters most for SLA and revenue — worsens. Filter `case_complexity == "high"` and inspect the variant distribution.

3. **Local knowledge attrition (slow-burn).** Today, local caseworkers absorb informal technical triage that prevents ~30% of unnecessary specialist referrals (visible as the 67/100 local caseworker  tech specialist loops). Once those loops are removed, the *invisible* work that prevented specialist demand disappears over months. Average looks fine in the first quarter; specialist-referral rate creeps up thereafter.

4. **Fairness across customer segments.** Regional-language and small-business segments today rely on the local caseworker to compensate for handovers. Adding queue hops disproportionately extends their p90. Filter by `customer_segment` and compute cycle-time variance — averages across segments will mask this.

5. **Skill atrophy and learning loss.** Local caseworkers stop doing technical work; over 12–18 months they lose the ability to recognize when specialist advice is wrong. This produces a *delayed* rise in rework that does not appear in the first year of any pilot. Track `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` over time — narrowing caseworker profiles is the early signal.

6. **Accountability diffusion in shared approval.** Removing a supervisor and creating a shared approval queue removes the named owner. Cases that stall are no longer anyone's fault. This shows up not in cycle time but in `reassignment_count`, in time-to-first-action after approval queue entry, and in qualitative staff surveys.

7. **Single point of failure in TSS.** Local cross-team cover (currently 6/100 cases) acts as resilience insurance during absences. Centralization converts N_Tech/S_Tech/E_Tech into one logical node whose absence stops the whole system. Not visible until the first holiday season or pandemic wave — but the `cross-team handover` edge should be monitored because its *collapse* below 3/100 is exactly when resilience has been lost.

8. **Exception handling erosion.** Exceptional cases (security review, regulatory edge cases) currently rely on tacit routing by senior local staff. Once routing is rule-based, exceptions fall through. `pm4py.get_variants` filtered by `required_specialty == "security"` and unusual `handover_reason` values reveals whether exception paths remain viable.

9. **Hidden rework from queue bouncing.** Cases that arrive at TSS missing context get sent back, retried, or reassigned. This shows up as growing `reassignment_count` and as new variants appearing in `pm4py.get_variants` with low frequency but long duration. Averages exclude them; tails expose them.

10. **Specialist over-specialization paradox.** Pushing all tech/legal/risk/QC into one queue sounds like efficiency, but `discover_organizational_roles` may show that specialists begin to sub-specialize informally within the queue, recreating silos — without the local context to compensate.

# 5. Pilot design

### 5.1 Pilot scope
**Phase 1 (months 0–2): shadow run.** All routing logic executed in parallel with current organization. No real cases are actually rerouted; the proposed routing is computed and stored as a *predicted* path, then compared with the actual path. Measures: agreement rate, predicted-vs-actual cycle time, predicted rework.

**Phase 2 (months 3–5): partial live pilot in East only.** East is selected because (a) E_Tech is already overloaded at 82% so the bottleneck is most acute there, (b) East has the weakest baseline (CSAT 3.8, p90 22.8d), so the headroom for improvement is largest, and (c) North/South act as control teams preserving local expertise for comparison.

**Phase 3 (months 6–9): expanded pilot.** Add South's high-complexity caseload to TSS but keep North as control. Legal/Risk shared queue is introduced in phase 3 only, after phases 1–2 prove tech routing.

### 5.2 Comparison group
- **Control:** Team North (strongest baseline; stable local loop). Preserves local-specialist model.
- **Treatment A:** Team East (full restructure).
- **Treatment B:** Team South (technical-routing only, no Legal/Risk centralization in phase 2).

### 5.3 Pre / post observation window
- **Pre-period:** 6 months of historical event log for all teams (baseline already partly given in the scenario).
- **Post-period:** minimum 3 months per phase; 9 months for full pilot before deciding on broader rollout. *At least 2 full monthly cycles are needed to capture seasonality.*

### 5.4 Segmentation
Every analysis must be segmented by:
- `team` (North/South/East, and later cross-team)
- `case_complexity` (low/medium/high)
- `required_specialty` (legal, risk, security, technical)
- `customer_segment` (regional-language, small business, enterprise, standard)
- `priority` (because shared queue risks reordering priority)
- `local_expertise_flag` (to track whether cases that needed local expertise are still served locally)

Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` for each segmentation cut.

### 5.5 Guardrail metrics (pre-registered; rollback if breached)

| Guardrail | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|
| TSS p90 wait | >6.0h for 2 consecutive weeks | Pause new routing to TSS; route overflow back to local team |
| Legal/Risk p90 wait | >14.0h | Add temporary Legal/Risk capacity or restrict queue to required_specialty only |
| Cross-team handover frequency | rises above 5/100 | Investigate local fallback; consider restoring cross-team cover |
| Handover-rework rate (TSS  caseworker) | >10% for 4 weeks | Mandatory context packet attached to every handover |
| Cycle-time p90  p50 gap | widens >2× vs control | Roll back to phase 1 |
| CSAT (regional segment) | drops >0.3 | Roll back or carve out exemption for regional segments |
| Staff turnover intent | >20% | Mandatory pause and redesign |
| Median cycle time | worsens >5% | Automatic review board |

### 5.6 Staff feedback collection
- **Weekly pulse surveys** (3 questions, <2 min) to local caseworkers and specialists.
- **Bi-weekly focus groups** rotating through North/South/East.
- **Daily stand-up notes** mined for sentiment via simple keyword tagging.
- **Customer NPS** captured per segment monthly.
- **Handover-rework root-cause tagging** in event log (`handover_reason` enriched with taxonomy: missing context, wrong priority, wrong specialty, queue bouncing).

### 5.7 Rollback / adjustment triggers
- Any **red** guardrail breach triggers automatic rollback.
- Two **amber** breaches in different guardrails within 30 days trigger mandatory design review.
- After phase 2 (East only) at month 5, a **go/no-go gate** reviews all guardrails; only green or improving amber proceeds to phase 3.
- Rollback design must be reversible within 2 weeks (keep old routing rules operational, maintain dual-write capability).

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommend: pilot first, with the East team, in three gated phases, with pre-registered guardrails and explicit rollback triggers. Do not recommend full rollout.**

### Reasoning
The restructuring has plausible upside (median cycle-time improvement of 0.5–1.5 days, +15% specialist utilization, fewer nominal handovers) and plausible downside (p90 worsening by 5–10 days, hidden bottleneck in TSS, multi-month local expertise loss, fairness erosion for regional segments). The expected value of *not piloting* is high because the dominant risks — local atrophy, accountability loss, queue blow-up — are slow-burn and not detectable in the first quarter's averages. The expected value of *piloting in East* is also high because East is where the current model is already weakest, so the marginal benefit is largest and the cost of a wrong call is contained.

**Conditions to proceed beyond East pilot:**
- All Phase 2 guardrails at green or improving amber.
- Demonstrated improvement in East p90, not just median.
- No fairness regression in any `customer_segment` filter.
- Staff pulse 3.6/5 sustained.

**What management should NOT infer from early averages:**

1. *A falling median cycle time in the first 8 weeks is not evidence of success.* It is partly an artifact of low-complexity cases flowing faster through the new queue; the high-complexity tail may already be worsening. Always inspect p50, p90, and p95 by `case_complexity`.

2. *A falling nominal handover count is not a quality signal.* Handovers replaced by queues are not "eliminated" — they are converted to waiting time. Compare `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` with `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` together.

3. *Rising specialist utilization is not, by itself, good.* Above ~85% it becomes a leading indicator of queue collapse. Treat utilization as a two-sided metric: target 75–82%, flag at 85%, alarm at 90%.

4. *Stable rework rate is not stability.* If rework is shifting from local-loop rework to queue-rebound rework, the *cause* has changed even if the *rate* has not. Track rework *per activity* and rework *per handover edge*, not just the case-level rate.

5. *Stable CSAT in the first 3 months is not proof of fairness.* Regional and small-business segments are smaller and slower to register changes. Segment CSAT by `customer_segment` and by `location` from month 1.

6. *A short pilot will not reveal local-expertise loss.* This is a 9–18 month effect. Plan a 12-month longitudinal check using `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` to detect role narrowing.

7. *Specialists reporting "fewer interruptions" early on is misleading.* They are seeing fewer but harder cases; the queue will eventually saturate. Monitor specialist service-time variance, not just volume.

8. *Cross-team handovers falling to ~3/100 is a resilience warning, not a win.* It means local fallback is being abandoned. Monitor that the *capability* to do cross-team handovers is preserved, even if frequency drops.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The following Python sketch shows how to compare pre- and post-restructuring event logs. The pre log and post log have the same schema (the columns listed in the scenario).

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.log.importer.xes import importer as xes_importer
from pm4py.statistics.variants.pandas import get as variants_get

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. Load and format
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
def load(path):
    log = xes_importer.apply(path)         # or pd.read_csv and pm4py.format_dataframe
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        log,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
    )
    return df

pre  = load("eventlog_pre.xes")
post = load("eventlog_post.xes")

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Handover-of-work network (compare structure and edge weights)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
def handover_view(df, label):
    net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df)
    # Edge weights: number of handovers between resource pairs
    edges = (df.groupby(["org:resource", df.groupby(["case:concept:name"])
                  .cumcount().rename("step")])...)
    # Practical shortcut: pm4py returns a NetworkX DiGraph
    edges = [(u, v, d.get("weight", 1))
             for u, v, d in net.edges(data=True)]
    return pd.DataFrame(edges, columns=["from", "to", f"count_{label}"])

pre_edges  = handover_view(pre,  "pre")
post_edges = handover_view(post, "post")
merged = pre_edges.merge(post_edges, on=["from", "to"], how="outer").fillna(0)
merged["delta"] = merged["count_post"] - merged["count_pre"]
# Inspect: edges that shrank (nominal handover reduction) vs new edges (queue formation)

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Working-together network (colocation of activities on the same case)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
wt_pre  = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(pre)
wt_post = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(post)
# Compare density and clustering of local caseworker  tech specialist

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Subcontracting network (escalation / hand-back patterns)
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
sub_pre  = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(pre)
sub_post = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(post)
# Diagnose: are local caseworkers handing cases back-and-forth to shared queue?
# This indicates queue bouncing / context loss.

# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Variants and performance DFG
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
def variant_summary(df, label):
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)
    rows = []
    for v, cases in variants.items():
        rows.append({
            "variant":  "  ".join(v),
            "frequency": len(cases),
            f"median_dur_{label}": df[df["case:concept:name"].isin(cases)]
                .groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
                .apply(lambda s: (s.max() - s.min()).total_seconds() / 86400)
                .median()
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows).sort_values("frequency", ascending=False)

v_pre  = variant_summary(pre,  "pre")
v_post = variant_summary(post, "post")
# Compare top-N variants: did the long-tail "stalled-in-shared-queue" variant emerge?

# Performance DFG
perf_pre  = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(pre)

# 10. Statistical comparison
# -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
from scipy import stats
def compare_cycle_time(df_pre, df_post, by=None):
    # Compute per-case duration
    def dur(df):
        return (df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
                  .agg(lambda s: (s.max() - s.min()).total_seconds() / 86400))
    a, b = dur(df_pre), dur(df_post)
    if by is None:
        return stats.mannwhitneyu(a, b, alternative="two-sided")
    # segmented comparison
    out = {}
    for k in df_pre[by].unique():
        cases_k = df_pre[df_pre[by] == k]["case:concept:name"].unique()
        a_k = a[a.index.isin(cases_k)]
        cases_k2 = df_post[df_post[by] == k]["case:concept:name"].unique()
        b_k = b[b.index.isin(cases_k2)]
        out[k] = stats.mannwhitneyu(a_k, b_k, alternative="two-sided")
    return out
```

**Recommended analysis order:**

1. **Pre vs post handover-of-work network** — confirm nominal reduction and identify new shared-service nodes.
2. **Variants before and after, segmented by complexity and segment** — detect emergence of stalled-in-queue variants.
3. **Performance DFG + service/waiting time** — measure whether handovers were converted into waiting time (the central diagnostic).
4. **Subcontracting network** — detect queue bouncing.
5. **Resource similarity + role discovery** — track skill atrophy and role-clarity loss over time.
6. **Filtered rework per activity per segment** — detect where rework is migrating.
7. **Temporal features** — detect tail-latency masking of average.

The critical discipline is to *never* report a single number. Every result must be reported segmented by team, complexity, specialty, and customer segment — and must include p50, p90, and p95, not only averages.
