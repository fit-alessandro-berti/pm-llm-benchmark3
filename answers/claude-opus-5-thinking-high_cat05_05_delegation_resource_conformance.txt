## 1. Actor–Role–Delegation Matrix

**Calendar anchor used throughout.** The site calendar states 2026-08-15 and 2026-08-16 were weekend days and no holiday fell in the period. That fixes the weekdays: 2026-08-09 = **Sunday**, 08-10 Mon, 08-11 Tue, 08-12 Wed, 08-13 Thu, 08-14 Fri, 08-15 Sat, 08-16 Sun. Two consequences matter: CHG-507 was executed end-to-end on a Sunday, and CHG-509 was executed end-to-end on a Saturday.

**Identity normalization.** Three identity classes are treated differently:

1. *Human accounts* are mapped to a canonical human key (`u_bk` and `b.koch` → `H_BEN`; all others are 1:1). Normalization happens **before** any segregation-of-duties (SoD) test, because a four-eyes test compares resource strings and will silently pass when one human holds two identifiers. This single step changes the RR1 verdict on CHG-508 from "compliant" to "self-approval".
2. *Service accounts* (`svc_qms`) are never merged into a human by default. Each service action becomes a unique pseudo-identity (`SVC::svc_qms@<case>/<activity>`) so it can neither satisfy a control nor create a spurious SoD collision. A human is substituted only when session evidence is asserted, and the substitution carries a strength label (STRONG = individual cryptographic/badge factor at the event; WEAK = circumstantial only). Every SoD test is then run in three modes — strict (no attribution), strong-only, and all-attributions — so that each finding's dependence on contested identity evidence is visible rather than buried.
3. *Roles* are resolved by interval containment at the event timestamp, never from the current user master. Eva is the clearest trap: she is Independent Verifier only through 2026-08-11 23:59 and Quality Engineer from 2026-08-12, and Quality Engineering is not QM at any point.

## 2. Rule Interpretation

Let `H(e)` be the canonical human of event `e` after alias normalization, undefined when a service account cannot be reliably attributed. Let `role(h, t)` be the set of roles held at instant `t`, and `scope(c)`, `risk(c)`, `emerg(c)` the case facts.

**RR1 (drafter/approver separation).** Violated for case `c` iff `∃ h : h ∈ H(Draft_Change_Request ∈ c) ∧ h ∈ H(Approve_Change ∈ c)`. Evaluated on canonical humans, so aliases collapse first. An event whose human is undefined can neither create nor clear a violation; it is reported as *undecidable* rather than *compliant*. RR1 has no risk threshold and no emergency exception.

**RR2 (implementer/verifier separation).** Same predicate over Implement_Change and Verify_Implementation. Note what RR2 does *not* say: it does not require the verifier to hold a verifier role, and it does not prohibit approver = verifier or drafter = implementer. Those are genuine control-objective gaps in the rule set, and I report them separately from rule violations so that "technically conformant" is never confused with "adequately controlled".

**RR3 (high-risk approval authority).** For `risk(c) = HIGH`, Approve_Change is conformant iff `QM ∈ role(H(e), t(e))` **or** a delegation `d` exists with all six conjuncts true: (i) `t(e) ∈ [d.start, d.end]`; (ii) `scope(c) ∈ d.scopes`; (iii) the activity is within `d.activities` — a delegation of "QM approval" does not confer other QM acts; (iv) the principal actually held the delegated role at `t(e)` — you cannot delegate authority you lack; (v) `d.requires_absence → principal is absent/formally unavailable at t(e)`; (vi) `d` is not a prohibited subdelegation. Failing any conjunct means no valid authority; it does not by itself mean misconduct.

**RR4 (emergency substitution).** Two limbs. The *substitution limb*: for HIGH-risk emergency cases, Approve_Change may come from IC or a valid IC delegate under the same six conjuncts, and Implement_Change must follow the approval in time. The *compensating limb*: Retrospective_QM_Review must be performed by QM or a valid QM delegate — the same scope/activity tests apply to the reviewer — within two business days. Both limbs must hold; a valid emergency approval whose retrospective review fails is still a control failure, and arguably the more serious one, because the retrospective review is the only quality gate the emergency path preserves.

*Business-day counting.* Business days are Monday–Friday minus site holidays (none in this period), in UTC. The clock is anchored to the **emergency implementation** timestamp, the day of the event is day 0, and the deadline is 23:59:59 of the second following business day. For an 08-11 (Tuesday) implementation, the deadline is end of Thursday 08-13. CHG-510's review on 08-12 10:00 is comfortably timely. CHG-505's review on 08-14 16:00 is one business day late; it stays late under the stricter reading that counts the event day as day 1, and under a 48-business-hour elapsed-time reading (08-13 13:10). The only reading that rescues it anchors the clock to *incident closure* rather than to implementation, so the site SOP's stated trigger should be confirmed before the finding is closed — I flag the sensitivity rather than hide it.

*Action by a principal while on leave.* Authority is appointment-based, not attendance-based. Asha's QM authority does not lapse because she is on annual leave, so her CHG-504 approval is authorized as to authority; leave conflicts raise an **identity assurance** question, not an authority question. The converse matters for delegations that require absence. I treat a leave record as creating a *presumption of absence* for the whole recorded interval, rebutted only for the window in which the principal demonstrably acted (plus a short reachability margin), unless there is evidence she was generally reachable for approvals. On that rule Asha's 09:15–09:40 VPN session on 08-13 does not restore her availability at 13:00, so Ben's CHG-506 approval remains inside a presumptively absent window. A stricter auditor could argue that a QM who demonstrably worked that morning was reachable all day and that the D-QM-01 precondition therefore lapsed on 08-13; I record that as a residual, not as my primary reading, and the discriminating evidence is simple — was any escalation to Asha attempted before Ben approved?

*Can a later database entry prove an earlier delegation?* No, not on its own. A record created at T2 is a present-tense assertion about a past state, and accepting it as proof would make every control retro-fittable. It can *corroborate* effectiveness at T1 < T2 when four conditions hold: a contemporaneous independent artifact exists; the late creation has an innocent, documented cause; the record's content matches the contemporaneous artifact; and the record sits in a validated system with an immutable audit trail. D-QM-01 satisfies this pattern reasonably well — a signed PDF timestamped 08-09 18:00 pre-dates the whole interval, and a migration explains the 08-12 09:15 row — so I accept D-QM-01 as effective from 08-10 with MEDIUM-HIGH confidence, conditional on two checks: that the PDF timestamp is a trusted RFC-3161 token rather than typed text, and that the 08-12 09:15 row belongs to a genuine migration batch containing many unrelated records. A lone record created in isolation at 09:15 would flip this to INDETERMINATE. Note the asymmetry across cases: the backfilled row post-dates CHG-501's approval (08-11 09:00) but pre-dates CHG-503's (08-12 11:30), so CHG-501 rests entirely on the PDF while CHG-503 has database support as well. And no amount of corroboration reaches CHG-507 on 08-09, which sits before the delegation's own effective interval.

**RR5 (identity evidence).** A service account is not a human identity. `H(e)` is defined for a service action only when session evidence maps it to an authorized human with reliable, individual factors. Absent that, the action is *unattributed*: it cannot discharge a control (so a HIGH-risk verification performed by an unattributed account leaves the verification objective unmet regardless of who "really" did it), and it cannot be scored as a violation of a same-person rule either. Aliases are normalized before all SoD checks.

**RR6 (suspicion versus proof).** Repeated pairs, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations and concentrated handovers are *indicators*. They raise investigation priority and may justify compensating controls, but they never upgrade a classification to misconduct without corroborating evidence. Conversely — and this is the symmetric error — a benign explanation for a pattern does not clear a hard authority or SoD failure that stands on documentary evidence alone.

**Severity scale.** CRITICAL = HIGH-risk change executed with no valid approval authority at all. HIGH = HIGH-risk control breached or unattributable, or the emergency compensating control failed. MEDIUM = LOW-risk breach, or a breach of a control whose objective was partly met by other means. LOW = documentation/assurance gap only.

## 4. Collusion and Alternative-Explanation Analysis

Before any pattern reading, two cautions. First, this extract holds 10 cases and 7 humans, so network weights are unstable and near-uniform edges carry no information. Second, the roster is small and there is only one implementer in every case, which mechanically manufactures "concentration". Structural sparsity is not evidence of conspiracy.

**Indicator 1 — Clara drafted and Ben approved 18 of 23 HIGH-risk production changes in 60 days (Asha approved 3).**
*Benign:* Clara and Ben are assigned to the same production cell, Clara is the designated frequent drafter, Ben is the cell's quality engineer, no pair-rotation policy exists, and the QMS may route work by cell rather than by person. A single-QM site with a QM who takes leave will inevitably concentrate approvals on one delegate.
*Adverse:* Work was preferentially routed to a compliant approver, or an unauthorized standing practice let a Quality Engineer act as QM as a matter of routine.
*Discriminating evidence:* the complete delegation register for the whole 60 days is the decisive item. The only QM delegation on record covers 08-10 to 08-14, which cannot support 18 approvals across 60 days. Either many further delegations exist and were not disclosed, or a large population of HIGH-risk approvals was made without authority — a far bigger finding than anything in this 10-case log. Also compare Ben's and Asha's rejection and comment rates, seconds-from-submission-to-approval, and the downstream deviation rate of changes each approved. A delegate who never rejects and approves in seconds looks different from a diligent one.

**Indicator 2 — Reciprocity: Ben drafted four LOW-risk training changes that Clara approved, and CHG-508 shows Ben approving his own alias-drafted document.**
*Benign:* A two-person cell covering routine, low-consequence paperwork for each other; no financial benefit is known.
*Adverse:* A reciprocity norm ("you sign mine, I sign yours") that erodes the independence of the same pair's HIGH-risk approvals and, in CHG-508, degenerates into self-approval.
*Discriminating evidence:* substantive review artifacts on those four documents (comments, redlines, dwell time), whether other qualified approvers were available and bypassed, whether the documents relate to procedures governing Clara's or Ben's own work, and whether the reciprocity appears in the HIGH-risk stream too.

**Indicator 3 — Shared workstation and service account (CHG-503 on WS-DM-04).**
*Benign:* A shared terminal with a queue account is normal on a plant floor; the verification may even have been generated by a QMS interface or automated test rather than a person, and another qualified operator may have been at the keyboard.
*Adverse:* The implementer self-verified through a service account to defeat RR2, which is precisely the bypass RR5 exists to catch.
*Discriminating evidence:* the QMS session table linking the `svc_qms` token to an interactive OS user, OS logon/logoff on WS-DM-04 around 12:30, Diego's badge egress time, other badge reads for that room, and the shape of the verification payload (hand-typed values versus machine-generated). **What none of this establishes.** Per RR6, no combination of the above proves collusion. Equally, the benign explanations above do not clear the documented failures.

## 5. PM4Py and pandas Checking Code

Service-account attribution is represented explicitly rather than assumed. `svc_qms` events are rewritten to a unique pseudo-identity `SVC::svc_qms@<case>/<activity>` in strict mode, so they can never accidentally satisfy or violate a same-person test; a human is substituted only under an attribution record carrying a strength label, and every SoD test runs in three modes (strict / strong-only / all-attributions) so each verdict's dependence on contested evidence is printed. The script also runs the four-eyes filter on the un-normalized log to demonstrate the alias blind spot, cross-checks every `pm4py.filter_four_eyes_principle` result against an independent pandas computation so that a change in the filter's polarity across pm4py versions cannot silently invert a conclusion, and ends with an explicit list of judgments it deliberately does not automate. Verified on pm4py 2.7.23, pandas 3.0.4, numpy 2.5.0.

HOLIDAYS = np.array([], dtype="datetime64[D]")   # site calendar: none in period

def bd_deadline(trigger_ts, n=2):
    """End of the n-th business day strictly after the trigger day."""
    d = np.datetime64(trigger_ts.date(), "D")
    got, cur = 0, d
    while got < n:
        cur = np.busday_offset(cur, 1, roll="forward", holidays=HOLIDAYS)
        got += 1
    return pd.Timestamp(str(cur), tz="UTC") + pd.Timedelta(hours=23, minutes=59, seconds=59)

def sna_edges(obj):
    """pm4py >=2.7 returns an SNA object; older versions a (dict, bool) tuple."""
    if hasattr(obj, "connections"):
        g = obj.connections
    elif isinstance(obj, tuple):
        g = obj[0]
    else:

## 6. Control Improvements

**Delegation creation.** Require delegations to exist as approved records in the validated QMS *before* their effective interval starts, with mandatory structured fields for principal, delegate, role, effective interval, object scope, permitted activity list, absence precondition, and an explicit subdelegation flag. Reject any activation whose record was created after its own start time. Make the permitted-activity list mandatory and enforced: the CHG-505 and CHG-510 retrospective reviews happened because an approval-scoped delegation was treated as general QM authority.

**Effective-time recording.** Store delegation intervals in UTC with an immutable, append-only audit trail, and hold the signed source document with an RFC-3161 trusted timestamp so that contemporaneity can be proven without relying on a database row. Any record created during a migration must be tagged with the migration batch ID so that late creation is self-explaining rather than suspicious, and migrated delegations that were used before their record existed should be listed for retrospective quality review.

**Principal availability.** Integrate the leave/roster system with the workflow so that a delegation requiring absence activates only when the principal has a matching absence record, and the system logs the escalation attempt (call, message, timeout) that established unavailability. Define "absent or formally unavailable" explicitly, including whether non-working hours and weekends qualify — the CHG-509 verdict turns entirely on that definition. Where a principal on leave nevertheless acts, the system should either suspend the dependent delegation or record an explicit override with justification.

**Alias management.** Reconcile identity: one person, one credential, with historical aliases mapped to a durable person ID in a governed identity store. Enforce SoD checks on the person ID, never on the account string, and re-run the last 12 months of four-eyes checks on normalized identities to find the other self-approvals that the `b.koch`/`u_bk` split will have hidden. Deprovision duplicates and add a periodic duplicate-detection report.

**Individual authentication.** Prohibit service accounts from signing GxP-significant records. Where a shared terminal or interface account is technically necessary, require an individual re-authentication (FIDO/badge plus PIN) that is written into the record itself, and store the mapping from service session to human in the same audit trail rather than in separate infrastructure logs. Terminals in shared rooms need short auto-lock timeouts. The contrast between WS-IV-02 (badge plus FIDO, strong attribution) and WS-DM-04 (no re-authentication, unattributable) shows the capability already exists and simply is not applied uniformly.

**Emergency review.** Configure the retrospective QM review as a hard workflow task with an automatic, calendar-aware due date computed from an unambiguous trigger, escalation to a named alternate QM before expiry, and a block on closing the change until a validly authorized QM review is recorded. The reviewer's authority must be checked against the delegation's activity and scope list at execution time. Emergency invocations should also require the incident ID to be captured on the change record so that scope checks like "is this within CI-44?" are automatic rather than investigative.

**Pair rotation.** Introduce a documented approver-rotation or load-balancing policy for HIGH-risk changes with a monitored cap on any single drafter–approver pair's share, plus a mandatory second reviewer when the cap is exceeded. Prohibit approver = verifier and drafter = implementer for HIGH-risk changes: RR1 and RR2 as written permit both, and CHG-509 exploited exactly that gap.

**Network monitoring.** Run the handover and working-together analyses monthly on normalized identities with statistical baselines that account for roster size, and treat outliers as triggers for review rather than as findings. Add specific detectors for self-loops in the draft→approve relation, approvals on non-business days, actions by accounts whose holder is on recorded leave, service-account signatures, and delegation use in the last hour of an effective interval.

**Evidential retention.** Retain badge, workstation logon, VPN, MFA and QMS session logs for a defined, GxP-appropriate period with synchronized clocks and a single time source, and retain CCTV for shared GxP terminals long enough to support investigations — its absence is the reason CHG-503 cannot be resolved. Preserve delegation source documents, absence records and escalation attempts with the same rigor as the batch record, and make sure the resource field in the workflow captures the authenticated actor rather than the case owner.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

The dominant story is **genuine authority failure**, not bad data. Of the ten cases, four contain breaches that stand on the organization's own paperwork with no identity question at all: CHG-507 (HIGH-risk approval by a non-QM before any delegation existed), CHG-510 (self-approval of a HIGH-risk emergency change resting on a subdelegation that D-IC-03 expressly forbids), CHG-502 (self-approval) and CHG-508 (self-approval concealed by an alias). Both retrospective QM reviews also fail, because Ben's delegation covered *approval* of *production-equipment* changes and was used for *review* of *cooling-system* changes, with CHG-505's review additionally one business day late. Roughly half the apparent nonconformance is therefore real authority failure. About a quarter is **identity and data quality**: CHG-506, where a HIGH-risk approval was made through Ben's account during certified sick leave from an office IP he denies using, and CHG-503, where an unattributed service account performed a HIGH-risk verification on the implementer's own shared workstation. The remaining quarter is **definitional and organizational**: CHG-509 turns entirely on whether a weekend satisfies "principal absent", and the Clara–Ben concentration is a pattern risk rather than a finding. The genuinely clean outcomes are CHG-501, CHG-503's approval leg and CHG-505's emergency approval — all valid substitutions — plus CHG-504, where the QM's own approval while on annual leave is authorized because authority follows appointment, not attendance.

Three caveats bound this. The sample is ten cases across seven people, so network metrics are indicative only. Two verdicts are conditional on documents I cannot inspect: D-QM-01's acceptance depends on the signed PDF carrying a trusted timestamp, and CHG-509 depends on the site's definition of principal absence. And the two identity cases are currently undecidable in either direction — I decline to call them either misconduct or clean, because the evidence that would settle them (workstation logons, MFA factors, badge egress) exists and simply has not been retrieved. Nothing here establishes collusion, and nothing here is excused by the fact that the Clara–Ben pairing has an innocent structural explanation.

**Three highest-priority investigations.** First, determine who operated `u_bk` at 13:00 on 08-13 (CHG-506) and at 16:00 on 08-14 (CHG-505's review), both inside certified sick leave, using workstation logon records, badge data and MFA factors; if the operator was not Ben, a HIGH-risk change was implemented with no valid approval and the site has a credential-compromise event, and if the operator was the drafter it is also a concealed self-approval. Second, reconcile the 60-day baseline: Ben approved 18 of 23 HIGH-risk production changes, yet the only QM delegation on record runs from 08-10 to 08-14, so either a body of undisclosed delegations exists or a large population of HIGH-risk approvals was made without authority — this is the finding with real product-quality and regulatory exposure, and it dwarfs the individual cases. Third, examine the CI-44 emergency governance: whether CHG-505 and CHG-510 fall within the incident, why a second change was raised at 14:10 rather than routed to Farid or Diego, who instructed Clara to self-approve, why D-IC-04 was attempted in the face of an explicit prohibition, and why both retrospective reviews were assigned to someone whose delegation covered neither the activity nor the scope.
