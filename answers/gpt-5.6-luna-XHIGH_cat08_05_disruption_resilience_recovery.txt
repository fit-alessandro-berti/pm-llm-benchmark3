# 1. Disruption timeline

| Period | Operational state and dominant variants | Performance and risk posture |
|---|---|---|
| T0, Day -30 to Day -1 | Normal electronic flow: authorization, reservation, pick, pack, release, dispatch, proof of delivery, and invoice close, with ordinary holds, backorders, and rechecks. | The 42,000-case baseline is the control point: median cycle time was 8 hours for Hospital-critical, 23 hours for Pharmacy-standard, and 36 hours for Wholesale-bulk. p90 times were 15, 49, and 72 hours respectively. Traceability exceptions were 0.8%, missing cold-chain documentation 0.4%, missing controlled-substance dual control 0.2%, rework 3.5%, and cancellations 2.1%. The process was not perfect, but its 0.96 model fitness and 91% fitting traces indicate broadly conformant execution. |
| T1, Day 0 morning | The warehouse management system became unavailable. The normal reservation, scanning, authorization, and event-capture path could not be trusted or used. | This was the decision point at which continuity had to be balanced against authorization, inventory, temperature, security, and traceability obligations. A formal contingency mode was needed; otherwise each local workaround would become an ungoverned process variant. |
| T2, Day 0 afternoon to Day 2 | Teams triaged orders, used offline authorization or phone confirmation, checked a printed inventory snapshot, created manual pick lists, used temporary release controls, dispatched some orders without scans, captured some proof of delivery by phone, and deferred noncritical work. | Throughput and hospital protection were partly preserved, but control quality deteriorated sharply. In 5,600 cases, Hospital-critical median time rose to 10 hours and p90 to 21 hours; Pharmacy-standard rose to 54 hours and 118 hours, and Wholesale-bulk to 96 hours and 180 hours. Hospital-critical fill remained 91%, while Pharmacy-standard fill fell to 61%. Traceability exceptions rose to 9.5%, missing temperature records to 4.8%, missing controlled-substance dual control to 2.9%, rework to 12.5%, and cancellations to 7.8%. Model fitness fell to 0.71 and only 44% of traces fitted; the major move-on-log and move-on-model deviations were skipped authorization, dispatch without scan, phone POD, and missing release, scan, POD, or temperature events. |
| T3, Day 3 to Day 6 | Order intake returned, but inventory synchronization and scanning were unreliable. The process combined normal authorization and reservation with reconciliation of manual reservations and continued backlog handling. | Metrics improved but remained materially worse than baseline: Hospital-critical median/p90 was 9/18 hours, Pharmacy-standard 38/90, and Wholesale-bulk 84/160. Traceability exceptions were 5.4%, missing temperature records 2.3%, missing dual control 1.4%, rework 9.0%, and cancellations 5.2%. Fitness improved to 0.82 with 59% fitting traces, which indicates recovery without full control restoration. |
| T4, Day 7 to Day 21 | Normal flow mostly returned. Backlog prioritization, manual-reservation reconciliation, POD remediation, and post-invoice audit remediation remained visible variants. | Operational cycle times approached baseline for Hospital-critical but tails remained elevated: median/p90 was 8/16 hours, 28/74, and 58/120. Traceability exceptions were still 2.2%, missing temperature records 0.9%, missing dual control 0.5%, rework 5.8%, and cancellations 3.0%. Fitness reached 0.90 and 76% of traces fitted, but that did not mean complete recovery: 2,300 invoices entered audit remediation, only 2,100 of those cases reached END in the observed flow, and the remediation queue persisted. The hidden compliance and administrative workload outlasted apparent operational recovery. |

The incident therefore had two recovery curves: physical order flow recovered faster than evidence quality and control completeness. A future design should report both curves and should not declare recovery solely because median cycle time has returned to normal.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is the ability to preserve the purpose of the process under changed conditions while making the change, uncertainty, and residual risk visible. It is not mere adherence to the exact baseline path, and it is not simply a high dispatch count. A paper pick list can be resilient if it preserves identity, quantity, lot, operator, and time; a system scan can be non-resilient if it records an event that did not actually occur.

In this case, B is the clearest resilient adaptation: it changes the mechanism for authorization while retaining the authorization decision. I is also resilience when deferral is deliberate capacity protection with communication and fair safeguards. J is resilient when prioritization is explicit and risk-based. K is necessary resilience in the recovery phase because manual reservations must be brought back into a consistent state. L is a legitimate resilience function for recovering evidence, provided that it is treated as exception remediation rather than as a substitute for contemporaneous control. F is resilient for controlled substances to the extent that its all-order review and dual-control intent were actually performed and recorded.

A, D, E, and H are conditionally resilient. A protects patient supply, but becomes a shortcut when emergency priority is used to waive authorization. D is reasonable while the snapshot is fresh and verified, then becomes unsafe as stock changes. E preserves physical flow only if the paper or spreadsheet record is authoritative, uniquely identified, and backfilled transparently. H can be acceptable for low-risk ambient delivery evidence under an approved protocol, but is not equivalent to secure or temperature-aware proof for controlled or cold-chain products. I and J also require fairness controls: protecting hospitals does not justify making pharmacies an invisible residual queue indefinitely.

C and G, as observed, are risky shortcuts. C omits authorization and relies on an assumption. G omits the custody event and creates the largest traceability hole. The elevated T2 exceptions, missing temperature and dual-control records, rework, cancellations, and the 0.71 fitness/44% fitting-trace result are evidence that the workaround phase changed more than the transport mechanism; it weakened process controls. The T4 improvement to 0.90 fitness and 76% fitting traces is encouraging, but the remaining exceptions and audit tail show that operational normalization did not erase the consequences.

The key test for every contingency variant is: which control objective is being preserved, what is the contemporaneous evidence, who can authorize an exception, how long is the exception valid, and what event closes it? If those answers are absent, the adaptation should be reported as a control deviation, not counted as resilience merely because an order moved.

# 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned process should operate as an explicit contingency sub-process with controlled entry and exit. “Manual” should describe the execution channel, not an absence of policy or evidence.

**Trigger and activation**

Enter contingency mode when any of the following occurs:

- WMS or scanning is unavailable beyond a defined threshold, such as 15 minutes during operating hours, or the incident commander declares an earlier activation for a patient-safety event.
- Authorization, inventory synchronization, event timestamps, or release records are unavailable or suspected to be corrupted.
- Cold-chain temperature capture, controlled-substance security records, or recall/lot information cannot be reliably retrieved.
- A capacity or backlog forecast shows that Hospital-critical orders will miss their agreed threshold without controlled prioritization.

The incident commander, warehouse lead, compliance lead, quality lead, and customer-service lead declare the mode, record the start time, freeze or isolate unreliable system writes, issue a contingency batch/ID range, and appoint a reconciliation owner. A clear message must state which controls are unavailable, which substitutes are approved, and what products are prohibited from dispatch.

**Triage and routing**

Every order is assigned a customer-criticality tier and a product-risk tier before picking. The higher tier governs. A practical routing policy is:

- **Tier 1: patient-critical or regulated/high-consequence.** Hospital-critical orders, controlled substances, cold-chain items, recall-sensitive lots, and combinations of these receive priority, but still require verified authorization and their full mandatory handling controls. Use the emergency lane only after the offline decision is recorded.
- **Tier 2: standard regulated or clinically important.** Pharmacy orders containing controlled, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive products follow the same high-control path and are not treated as ordinary ambient work.
- **Tier 3: lower-risk ambient.** Pharmacy-standard ambient orders may use approved offline authorization and controlled paper execution. They should not be silently displaced; allocate a stated minimum capacity and process by risk-adjusted age.
- **Tier 4: bulk and deferrable work.** Wholesale-bulk ambient orders may be deferred when capacity is constrained, with a reason, next review time, customer notification, and aging escalation. They remain in a visible queue rather than disappearing from the process.

No tier permits unverified controlled-substance release, loss of cold-chain evidence, shipment of a recall-held product, or an undocumented authorization bypass. Where a mandatory control cannot be performed, the order is held, escalated to the compliance/quality lead, or handled under a specifically approved emergency exception with the risk recorded.

**Minimum control matrix**

| Risk class | Minimum controls before dispatch |
|---|---|
| Controlled-substance | Verified customer/product authorization; current inventory and physical count; unique contingency/order and lot identifiers; two-person pick/count; pharmacist or qualified release; secure custody and courier identity; complete handoff and POD. No phone-only POD or retrospective-only dual-control record. |
| Cold-chain | Verified authorization; physical inventory and lot/expiry capture; qualified pack/release; temperature evidence at the required points; packaging/condition record; courier handoff and delivery evidence. If temperature cannot be recorded, hold or obtain an approved quality exception before release. |
| Recall-sensitive | Authorization; lot/expiry and recall-status check from a current offline source; independent pick verification; release approval; complete shipment and POD linkage. |
| Hospital-critical ambient | Verified authorization through the contingency method; controlled reservation; unique pick and dispatch record; qualified release; POD. Prioritize service, but do not remove authorization or identity controls. |
| Pharmacy-standard or Wholesale-bulk ambient | Verified customer/product authorization; unique order and package record; inventory check with age/variance control; release check; structured dispatch and POD. Defer transparently when capacity is insufficient. |

**Manual evidence capture**

Use pre-numbered forms or a controlled offline application. Each contingency record must capture the order and contingency IDs, customer and product authorization source/version, approver and timestamp, product lot/expiry and quantity, inventory source and count, picker and verifier, pack/release decision, temperature and security fields where relevant, courier/package/handoff details, POD, exception reason, and later-entry indicator. Paper corrections require a single-line correction, initials, timestamp, and reason; spreadsheet edits require version control and an audit log. Every physical label and document must carry the same contingency ID so that later backfill is a join, not a guess.

**Backlog prioritization**

Calculate a visible priority score from patient/customer criticality, product risk, age, expiry or recall exposure, contractual SLA, order completeness, and the capacity required. Reserve capacity for each customer group and review allocations at fixed intervals. The queue owner may override the score only with a reason and approver. Publish the rule to customer service, give deferred customers a next-action time, and monitor whether Pharmacy-standard or Wholesale-bulk orders are being repeatedly deferred. Priority should protect the greatest consequence of delay, not merely the easiest orders or the largest account.

**Recovery and reconciliation flow**

When systems return, first establish a trusted cutover time and stop uncontrolled dual entry. Import or enter each contingency record once, matching the contingency ID to the order, inventory movement, lot, pick, pack, release, dispatch, POD, and invoice. Reconcile the physical stock ledger to the electronic inventory, perform targeted counts for high-risk SKUs, and investigate duplicates, omissions, substitutions, negative stock, and unexplained movements.

Use a three-way check for each completed order: the authorized order, the physical shipment, and the delivery/invoice evidence must agree. A high-risk order cannot return to ordinary closure until its authorization, release, custody, temperature, and POD records are complete or a named quality/compliance authority has approved an exception. Preserve the original event time and label a later-entered record as late evidence; do not backdate it.

Create a separate remediation queue for missing or contradictory evidence. Give each case a risk class, owner, due date, required action, and closure approver. Track it independently from cycle time and invoice close. Material gaps trigger product/customer notification, shipment quarantine or recall review, regulatory assessment, and management escalation as appropriate. The incident closes only when operational backlog, inventory reconciliation, and compliance remediation each meet their exit criteria.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Indicators should be split into leading signals, which support intervention during the incident, and lagging outcomes, which establish whether the process actually recovered. Report both totals and rates by Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk, and by product risk class.

| Dimension | Leading indicators during the incident | Lagging indicators after each phase |
|---|---|---|
| Throughput | Orders received, picked, packed, released, and dispatched per hour versus demand; capacity consumed by each tier; percentage of orders in a contingency route. | Median and p90 cycle time by segment; throughput recovered versus baseline; proportion of orders that required a manual variant. |
| Service protection | Hospital-critical fill/on-time forecast; number and age of Tier 1 orders; protected capacity; projected SLA misses. | Hospital-critical fill and on-time delivery; service-level misses; cancellations and customer complaints by group. |
| Traceability | Percentage of orders with a contingency ID, lot/expiry, picker/verifier, dispatch handoff, and POD before release; age of the oldest unlinked event; scan-gap rate. | Traceability exception rate; unresolved scan/POD gaps; recall or custody investigations; percentage of late-entered evidence. The 9.5% T2 traceability exception rate should be treated as a serious breach, even though flow continued. |
| Control completeness | Percentage of releases with authorization, quality/release, temperature, dual-control, and secure-handoff fields complete; number of orders stopped for missing mandatory evidence; stale authorization/inventory source age. | Missing cold-chain documentation, missing controlled-substance dual control, unauthorized or incorrectly authorized shipments, quality deviations, and conformance/model-fitness results. |
| Backlog age | Count and p90/max age in each queue, including deferred, backorder, reconciliation, and remediation queues; rate at which old work is being bypassed. | Residual backlog, oldest case age, time to clear each queue, and number of cases operationally closed while compliance remains open. |
| Rework and data quality | Duplicate reservation rate, inventory variance, manual correction volume, exception reopen rate, and percentage of records requiring later reconstruction. | Rework/correction rate, inventory adjustments, invoice disputes, failed deliveries, and cancellation rate. Compare with the baseline 3.5% rework and 2.1% cancellation rates. |
| Fairness across customer groups | Fill, delay, deferral, and capacity allocation by segment in the current shift; ratio of service to demand; count of repeated deferrals; documented priority overrides. | Segment-level fill, median/p90 cycle time, cancellation, complaint, and backlog-age distributions. A hospital fast lane is not fair if it produces an avoidable, unreviewed collapse in pharmacy service. |
| Recovery burden | Staff hours spent on manual capture, reconciliation, and audit remediation; number of high-risk cases awaiting a qualified reviewer. | Remediation queue size and aging, audit findings, compliance closure time, and total recovery effort after operational metrics normalize. |

Useful gates are more important than a single composite score. For example, “operationally recovered” should require normal-range throughput and service; “control recovered” should additionally require near-baseline traceability, temperature, dual-control, and release completeness; and “incident closed” should require an acceptably aged remediation queue with documented approval. Baseline values should be the benchmark, not merely the pre-incident median: p90 and exception tails exposed the continuing problem in T3 and T4.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Establish and rehearse a formal contingency playbook with activation thresholds, incident roles, prohibited actions, tiered routing, exit criteria, and an emergency-authorization protocol. | Medium | Fast, consistent decisions; less local improvisation; stronger protection of hospitals without normalizing bypasses. | Staff may still make judgment errors during severe outages; exceptions remain possible and must be logged. |
| 2 | Build a versioned offline control pack: authorization and recall-status caches, customer contacts, product/lot risk data, pre-numbered forms, labels, dispatch ledger, and structured POD templates. | Medium | Preserves authorization, traceability, and product controls when core systems are unavailable. | Cached data can become stale; enforce expiry, refresh, and source-version checks. |
| 3 | Implement a risk-based minimum-control matrix in the operating procedure and training, with 100% controls for regulated/high-consequence products and explicitly bounded sampling for low-risk ambient work. | Low to medium | Prevents a pharmacist sample or hospital priority from accidentally omitting temperature, recall, custody, or authorization controls. | Additional control time can reduce throughput; capacity plans must include qualified reviewers. |
| 4 | Provide an offline transaction and identity mechanism with unique IDs, controlled synchronization, idempotent replay, timestamps, lot capture, and an immutable audit trail. | High | Reduces missing scans, duplicate reservations, retrospective reconstruction, and inventory divergence; improves recovery speed. | Technology itself can fail or be compromised; test offline security and reconcile independently. |
| 5 | Create a dedicated reconciliation and remediation workcell with compliance, quality, inventory, and finance ownership, separate from the operational throughput team. | Medium | Makes the hidden T4 workload visible, prevents invoice close from masking missing evidence, and shortens the audit tail. | It consumes post-incident capacity; prioritize cases by product and regulatory consequence. |
| 6 | Use a transparent backlog allocator with customer-segment fairness constraints, aging escalation, minimum capacity floors, customer communications, and override logging. | Medium | Protects patient-critical supply while limiting the 61% pharmacy fill and concentrated cancellation effect seen in T2. | A mathematically fair rule can still be unpopular; management must set and disclose the service priorities. |
| 7 | Add dashboards and drills that measure p90 tails, control completeness, inventory variance, backlog age, remediation aging, staff load, and segment fairness—not just cases dispatched or median cycle time. | Low to medium | Detects concealed noncompliance and declares recovery only when operational and control curves both normalize. | More metrics can create reporting burden; automate capture and assign clear response thresholds. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower or deferred low-risk work, documented manual handling, and a temporary reduction in aggregate throughput to protect hospital-critical supply and preserve mandatory controls. It should not accept silent authorization bypasses, unrecorded dispatch, missing cold-chain or controlled-substance evidence, or an “operational recovery” declaration that leaves a material audit queue. The recommendation would change only if reliable evidence showed that an emergency substitute preserved the relevant control objective in real time—for example, complete authenticated authorization, custody, temperature, and release records—and that service losses and deferrals were governed fairly across customer groups; throughput alone is not such evidence.
