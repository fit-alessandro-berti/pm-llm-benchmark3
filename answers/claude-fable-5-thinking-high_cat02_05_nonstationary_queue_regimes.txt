# 1. Data audit and estimands

**Quantities and units.**

- Arrival intensity λ: cases per opening hour. Opening window is 07:00–19:00, i.e. 12 h/day, so a day with 96 arrivals has λ ≈ 96/12 = 8.0 cases/h *averaged over the open window*. The hourly vectors show the intraday rate is strongly time-varying, so daily λ is a window average of λ(t), not a stationary rate.
- Completion intensity (throughput) μ_eff: completed cases per opening hour, same convention. This is *realized* throughput, which equals min(demand-limited, capacity-limited) rate; it is not the service rate of a single server.
- Service time S: active handling time in minutes, excluding waiting. We are given the median and p90 only. Service-time distributions in service centers are right-skewed, so **mean(S) ≥ median(S)**; the mean is not identified by the table. Any Erlang/utilization calculation using median(S) is a lower bound on offered load.
- L (WIP): mean number of cases in system from 15-minute snapshots taken (presumably) during opening hours. Includes both in-service and waiting cases, and includes carry-over cases that arrived on earlier days.
- Capacity: staff_fte × 12 h = available FTE-hours/day. A useful derived quantity is completions per FTE-hour, c = completed/(staff_fte × 12), which mixes utilization and speed: c ≈ utilization × 60/mean(S in min).
- Flow time W: time from arrival to completion. If computed via Little's Law as L/λ, the unit here is **open-hours**, because no service (and, we must assume, no WIP measurement) occurs while closed. Converting open-hours to clock time requires knowing how cases straddle the overnight closure; that mapping is not supplied.

**Assumption A1 (labeled):** WIP snapshots are taken only during 07:00–19:00, so mean_wip is the open-hours time-average of L. Needed because Little's Law in open-hour units requires L and λ measured over the same clock.

**Assumption A2:** staff_fte is effective serving capacity (breaks already netted out). Needed for any utilization estimate; if it is rostered rather than effective capacity, utilization is underestimated.

**Assumption A3:** The complex variant has materially longer handling time than the simple variant. Needed to interpret the joint jump in complex_pct and service quantiles; the data are consistent with it but cannot prove causality (caution in the prompt: coding change vs real mix shift).

**At least five threats to naive inference:**

1. **Flow-conservation failure.** Over the 28 days, Σarrivals = 2519 but Σcompleted = 2409: a net inflow of 110 cases. Yet mean_wip starts at 18 (Apr 6) and ends at 19 (May 3). Within Apr 18–28 the cumulative net inflow is 106 cases while mean_wip rises only from 35 to 50. So roughly 90–100 cases are unaccounted for: abandonment, rerouting to another queue, cancellation, right-censoring at the end of the window, or simply inconsistent aggregation. Until resolved, any Little's-Law number is soft, because L, λ, and μ do not describe the same conserved population.
2. **Internal inconsistency in peak_08_10_pct.** For Apr 7 the hourly vector gives (bin 08-09 + bin 09-10) = 5+12 = 17 arrivals = 16.8% of 101, but the daily table says 31%. Shifting one bin (09-10 + 10-11 = 12+20 = 32 → 31.7%) matches the table for Apr 7, Apr 15 (33.3% vs 32) and Apr 25 (21.6% vs 22), but **not** for Apr 21 (36.5% either way vs 47%). Either the hourly bin labels are offset by one hour, or the vectors are stylized rather than exact. This matters because "peak 08–10" drives staffing conclusions.
3. **Only quantiles of S, no mean; no flow-time measurements at all.** The table's 24–36 min values are handling medians. Mean case flow time (what Little's Law returns) is on the order of hours here (see §6) — conflating the two understates congestion by roughly a factor of 5–8.
4. **Non-stationarity and serial dependence.** Lag-7 autocorrelation of daily arrivals is ≈ 0.72 (weekly seasonality) and weekday-only lag-1 autocorrelation is ≈ 0.51 (trend + regime persistence). The 28 daily rows are nowhere near 28 independent draws; naive standard errors are badly overconfident.
5. **Endogenous staffing.** staff_fte responds to backlog (7.0 FTE appears exactly on the recovery and intervention days). Pooled correlation between staff and WIP is **+0.65**, which naively reads as "more staff, more WIP." Treating staffing as an exogenous regressor inverts the causal sign.
6. **Unknown 07:00 backlog and overnight carry-over.** Completed cases need not have arrived that day; day-level arrival/completion differences are contaminated by an unobserved opening backlog state.
7. **Outage day contaminates every pooled statistic.** Apr 15 (180-min outage) drags pooled completions down and pooled WIP up; its neighbors (Apr 16–17) are transient backlog-drain days, not a "high-performance" regime.

**Outage and recovery treatment, explicitly:** Apr 15 is excluded from any rate estimation and treated as a boundary-condition shock (≈ 47 cases pushed into the opening backlog of Apr 16). Apr 16–17 are modeled as a transient with initial condition L(07:00 Apr 16) ≈ high-40s, drain rate ≈ (completions − arrivals) ≈ 17 and 13 cases/day. Their high throughput measures surge capacity under full utilization, not typical service speed — their *service quantiles* are biased up (aged and complex cases cleared first), and their *throughput* is biased up (no idleness). Pooling them into "Thursdays/Fridays" would flatter capacity estimates.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

**Alternative A — backlog-state segmentation (two states crossed with day type):** classify every day by whether it operates **balanced** (|arrivals − completed| small and WIP flat: Apr 6–14, Apr 18–19, May 2–3), **backlog-building** (arrivals > completions and WIP rising: Apr 15, Apr 20–28), or **backlog-draining** (completions > arrivals and WIP falling: Apr 16–17, Apr 29–May 1). The operational story: the center is a system that alternates between equilibrium and disequilibrium episodes, and what matters for prediction is the *state* (current backlog and sign of net flow), not the calendar cause. Under this view the outage recovery and the staffing intervention belong to the **same** regime ("drain"), because both show ≈ 10 completions/h at ≈ 7 FTE and ≈ 1.48–1.49 completions/FTE-h — an almost identical capacity fingerprint.

- *Strengths:* directly aligned with Little's-Law validity (balanced windows are where it applies); parsimonious (3 states); robust to whether the mix shift is real or a coding artifact; naturally handles the unlogged intervention time (the data themselves say when draining started).
- *Weaknesses:* it conflates two causally different drains (post-outage clearing of *old-mix* cases vs intervention-driven clearing of *heavy-mix* cases with different service times); it does not forecast, because it conditions on outcomes (net flow) rather than drivers (mix, staffing); and it hides the fact that the Apr 20–28 build-up would persist indefinitely without action, whereas Apr 15 was a one-day impulse.

**Alternative B — pure calendar-week segmentation** (week 1 baseline, week 2 outage week, week 3 mix-shift week, week 4 intervention week) is simpler for reporting but is dominated by the primary segmentation: it buries the Apr 16–17 recovery inside "week 2" and the Apr 20 change point mid-week transitions are actually clean, so nothing is gained.

**Comparison:** the primary (cause-based) segmentation is better for staffing decisions and root-cause work; Alternative A (state-based) is better for validity assessment of queueing laws and for a control policy ("if in drain state, hold surge staffing until WIP < threshold"). A serious analysis should carry both.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Pooled over 28 days: mean arrivals 90.0/day (λ ≈ 7.5/h), mean WIP 29.9, mean staff 4.9 FTE, "pooled Little" W ≈ 29.9/7.5 ≈ 4.0 open-hours. Each of these is a fiction no actual day resembles:

1. **Weekday/weekend composition (bimodality).** Pooled mean arrivals = 90.0, but the pooled *median* is 103.5 and the distribution is bimodal: weekdays 92–121, weekends 36–53. No day has ≈ 90 arrivals. A staffing plan sized to λ = 7.5/h under-provisions every weekday (true weekday λ ranges 8.3 → 9.6/h across regimes) and over-provisions weekends by ~80%. Weekends are 8/28 = 29% of days but only ~16% of volume.
2. **Complex-case mix / service-time drift.** Pooled median service ≈ 31 min hides a step: weekday medians average 25.0 min (complex ≈ 21%) before Apr 20 and 35.0 min (complex ≈ 45%) after — a ~40% increase in the median and a ~77% increase in the p90 (47 → 83 min). A pooled "average handle time" of ~31 min describes neither period and would mis-size capacity in both (over-staffing April 6–17 by ~20%, under-staffing April 20 onward by ~13% on the median alone, more on the tail).
3. **Outage and recovery cancel out in pooled throughput.** Week-2 weekday completions (101+105+58+129+112 = 505) are almost identical to week-1 (491), so a pooled weekly view says "week 2 was normal." Segmented: Apr 15 delivered 4.8 completions/h and stranded ~47 cases; Apr 16–17 ran at 10.0/h on surge staffing. Pooling averages a capacity failure with an unsustainable sprint and reports "fine." Similarly, pooling Apr 16 into "Thursdays" inflates the apparent Thursday capacity by ~25%.
4. **Simpson's-paradox-like reversal on staffing.** Pooled corr(staff_fte, mean_wip) = **+0.65**: naive reading — adding staff *increases* WIP. The pooling artifact is composition (weekends: low staff, low WIP) plus reverse causality (staff was raised *because* WIP was high). Within-regime the sign is the correct negative: at ~6 FTE in the overload regime WIP climbs 37→50; at ~7 FTE in the drain regime it falls 43→32.
5. **Reversal on service time vs throughput.** Across weekdays, corr(median service, completions) is *positive* (recovery/intervention days have both the longest-mix cases and the highest completions, thanks to extra staff). A pooled regression would "find" that slower service increases throughput — pure confounding by staffing and backlog state.
6. **Pooled Little's W ≈ 4.0 open-hours** blends ≈ 2.4 h (baseline weekdays) with ≈ 4.7–5.3 h (overload) and ≈ 5.4–9.7 h (outage day, itself not interpretable) — a ~2× range collapsed to one number, and the pooled figure is further undermined by the 110-case conservation gap (§1, threat 1).

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

**Hourly index of dispersion (Fano factor, var/mean across the 12 hourly bins) and CV:**

| Day | Fano | CV | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apr 7 (ordinary) | 2.96 | 0.59 | Overdispersed vs Poisson (Fano 1), but the shape is a smooth deterministic morning peak — this is **time-varying-rate (Mt) overdispersion**, not stochastic burstiness |
| Apr 15 (outage) | 3.79 | 0.66 | Arrival side similar to ordinary; the anomaly is on the completion side (three zero bins) |
| Apr 21 (post-shift) | 9.28 | 0.98 | Genuinely bursty: spikes of 34 (10–11), 24 (13–14), 14 (16–17) with troughs between — batch-like arrivals, plausibly upstream batching or the complex variant generating correlated arrivals |
| Apr 25 (weekend) | 1.18 | 0.53 | Nearly Poisson-consistent around a gentle hump |

Key distinction: a Fano factor of ~3 computed across bins of a day with a *known* peak profile mostly measures the profile, not randomness. To measure burstiness proper one needs the Fano factor of counts *around* the time-varying mean (or across multiple days per bin), which four representative days cannot supply. What we *can* say: the post-shift day is qualitatively different (CV ≈ 1.0 at hourly resolution, multiple spikes), so an M(t)/G/s model calibrated on April 7's smooth profile would understate queue peaks after April 20.

**Daily counts:** baseline weekday arrivals (n=7) have mean 100.1, variance ≈ 22.7, Fano ≈ 0.23 — **underdispersed** relative to Poisson (Poisson would give variance ≈ 100). Day totals are more regular than independent random arrivals, suggesting appointment-like smoothing or bounded daily demand; another reason not to assume Poisson daily noise.

**Serial dependence:** raw daily arrivals have lag-7 autocorrelation ≈ 0.72 (weekly cycle) and lag-1 ≈ 0.39; weekday-only lag-1 ≈ 0.51, driven by the level shift at Apr 20 plus regime persistence rather than by day-to-day propagation of randomness. WIP is even more persistent (it integrates net flow). Consequences: (i) effective sample size per regime is far below the row count; (ii) tests comparing "before/after Apr 20" means must treat days as dependent; (iii) confidence statements below stay qualitative.

**Change-point evidence:** three change points stand out and none needs sophisticated detection: complex_pct weekday sequence (19,20,18,21,22 | 22,24,25,27,25 | **41**,44,46,43,45,47,46 | 44,45,43) jumps ≈ +16 points between Apr 17 and Apr 20 with no overlap in ranges; staff_fte steps 6.0→7.0 at Apr 29 (and 2.5→3.0 for the following weekend); outage_min flags Apr 15 exactly. A simple heuristic (|x_t − median of prior 5 weekdays| > 3×MAD for the mix; within-day-type staffing diffs; the outage flag) recovers all three, plus the legitimate Apr 16 surge-staffing step (implemented in §8). Whether the Apr 20 jump is demand mix or a **coding change** cannot be resolved from these aggregates: both would move complex_pct and recorded quantiles together; however, the *throughput* deterioration (completions/FTE-h stays ≈ 1.4 while medians rise, and WIP genuinely accumulates) indicates real extra work content, not just relabeling — a coding change alone would not create a 13-case/day physical deficit.

**Why four representative days cannot establish a stochastic model:** one realization per regime gives no estimate of between-day variance of the intraday profile, no within-bin distribution, no arrival-process correlation structure (e.g., whether Apr 21's spikes recur at fixed hours or move), and the peak_08_10 inconsistency (§1, threat 2) shows the vectors may not even be exact. They support qualitative statements (peak location, outage window, burstiness increase) only.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

L = λW requires a conserved flow, a defined boundary, and either stationarity or start/end WIP equality over the window. Units below: W in **open-hours** (12 open-hours = 1 day). Where computed, W uses regime-mean L divided by regime-mean rate; arrivals are the demand-side proxy (valid when the window is balanced), completions the capacity-side proxy (what the system actually pushed through).

| Window / regime | Boundary stability (L start vs end) | Throughput proxy | WIP comparability | Carry-over risk | Stationarity | Implied flow time (if defensible) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline weekdays Apr 6–14 | Good: L ≈ 18–23 throughout | λ = 8.3/h (≈ μ; balanced) | Consistent snapshots, stable regime | Modest overnight carry-over, but stable → cancels in steady state | Best in dataset | **W ≈ 20.3/8.3 ≈ 2.4 open-h** (≈ 147 min; vs ~25 min median handling → waiting-dominated). | **applicable** (as open-hours average; conservation gap adds ±10–15% haze) |

**Why medians must not stand in for mean flow time:** Little's Law relates L to the *mean* flow time including waiting. Median handling (24–36 min) excludes waiting and understates even mean handling (skew). The gap is roughly 2.4 open-h vs 0.4–0.6 h — congestion, not handling, dominates the customer experience in every regime.

# 7. Operational implications

- **Staffing to regimes, not to the pooled average.** Pooled λ = 7.5/h with ~31-min pooled handling suggests ≈ 4–5 FTE suffices "on average" — this is the flagship policy that looks fine pooled and fails segmented: it under-staffs every post-shift weekday (offered load ≈ 6.4–7.1 erlangs → needs ≥ 7 FTE with any waiting-time target) and wastes weekend capacity. Under the heavy mix, weekdays need the intervention level (≈ 7 FTE) *permanently*, not temporarily, unless the mix reverts: at 6 FTE the system was demonstrably unstable (deficit ≈ 13 cases/day, WIP +2/day).
- **A second pooled-average trap: withdrawing the surge on schedule.** Because completions/FTE-h is flat (~1.4) across regimes, a pooled productivity report says the intervention "changed nothing per capita" and invites ending it early. Segmented, the +1 FTE is exactly what flipped net flow from −13 to +6 cases/day; removing it before WIP returns to ≈ 20 restarts accumulation within days. Rule: hold surge until mean_wip < ~22 for 3 consecutive weekdays.
- **Backlog recovery discipline.** Treat outages as boundary shocks: after Apr-15-type events, plan drain capacity explicitly via ΔL/(μ_surge − λ) (≈ 2–3 weekdays at +1 FTE for a 45–50-case backlog) rather than assuming it "washes out." Do not let recovery-day throughput (10/h) enter capacity baselines.
- **Peak smoothing.** In all consistent readings, 30–50% of daily arrivals land in a two-hour late-morning window. Shifting even 10–15% of the peak (appointments, staggered intake, triage of simple variant to a fast lane) reduces peak WIP more cheaply than an FTE. But first resolve the bin-labeling inconsistency (§1, threat 2) — smoothing the wrong two hours is worse than nothing. The Apr-21-style multi-spike pattern suggests upstream batching; if an upstream party releases work in batches, negotiating dribbled release is the cheapest intervention available.
- **Split the complex variant.** With complex ≈ 45% and p90 ≈ 83 min, one pooled queue makes simple cases wait behind complex ones. A two-class view (fast lane / complex lane with cross-trained overflow) should be evaluated; the current aggregates cannot size it (no per-class service quantiles) — a concrete data need.
- **Data-collection recommendations (ranked):** (1) log case-level events (arrival, start, complete, abandon/reroute) with timestamps — this resolves the 110-case conservation gap, which is currently the single biggest inference risk, and enables true flow-time and PM4Py analysis; (2) record 07:00 opening backlog daily; (3) log intervention/roster changes with timestamps (the Apr 29 intra-day ambiguity forced regime-boundary guessing); (4) fix the peak-window definition vs hourly-bin alignment; (5) capture per-variant service quantiles to separate mix shift from coding change (audit a sample of April cases re-coded under both schemes); (6) keep 15-min WIP snapshots but store them raw, not just the daily mean, to expose intraday transients.

# ---------- 4. transparent change-point / segmentation heuristic ----------
# Heuristic only (not ground truth): on weekdays, flag day t if complex_pct
# deviates from the median of the previous 5 weekdays by > 3*MAD (MAD floored
# at 2 pts); staffing steps are detected WITHIN day-type (weekday vs weekend
# separately, so the weekly roster cycle is not flagged); outage by outage_min.
wk = daily[~daily.is_weekend].reset_index(drop=True)
flags = []
for i in range(5, len(wk)):
    prev = wk.loc[i-5:i-1, "complex_pct"]
    mad = max(2.0, (prev - prev.median()).abs().median())
    if abs(wk.loc[i, "complex_pct"] - prev.median()) > 3 * mad:
        flags.append((wk.loc[i, "date"].date(), "complex_pct jump"))
for sub in (daily[~daily.is_weekend], daily[daily.is_weekend]):
    step = sub.loc[sub.staff_fte.diff().abs() >= 0.9, "date"]
    flags += [(d.date(), "staffing step") for d in step]
flags += [(d.date(), "outage") for d in daily.loc[daily.outage_min > 0, "date"]]
print("change-point candidates (heuristic; trailing complex_pct flags are")
print("within-regime persistence, not new breaks):", sorted(set(flags)))

def regime(d):
    ds = str(d.date()); wknd = d.weekday() >= 5
    if ds == "2026-04-15": return "outage"
    if ds in ("2026-04-16", "2026-04-17"): return "recovery"
    if d <= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-19"): return "base_wknd" if wknd else "base_wkday"
    if d <= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-28"): return "over_wknd" if wknd else "over_wkday"
    return "drain_wknd" if wknd else "drain_wkday"
daily["regime"] = daily["date"].apply(regime)

def autocorr(x, lag):
    x = np.asarray(x, float) - np.mean(x)
    return float(np.sum(x[:-lag] * x[lag:]) / np.sum(x * x))
print(f"daily arrivals lag-1 ac={autocorr(daily.arrivals,1):.2f}, "
      f"lag-7 ac={autocorr(daily.arrivals,7):.2f}, "
