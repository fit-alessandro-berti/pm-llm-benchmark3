## 1. Materiality Framework

Alignment cost answers a narrow question: "how many edits are needed to make this trace replayable in this model under this cost function?" Business severity answers a different one: "how much control failure, money, customer harm and regulatory exposure does this case represent?" The two coincide only when the model is a faithful, complete and correctly-labelled encoding of the control objective and the cost function is risk-weighted. Neither condition holds here (costs were configured uniformly at 1, the tree encodes only payable-claim control flow, and policies P1–P4 are data-aware and therefore invisible to control-flow conformance). The framework below therefore scores severity independently of raw cost.

**Severity dimensions**

- **D1 — Control objective at stake.** Which control does the deviation defeat? Segregation of duties / four-eyes approval and fraud screening are payment-authorization controls; notification is a customer-treatment and conduct control; assessment is a quantum-accuracy control; telemetry is not a control at all. A deviation is only as severe as the control it disables.
- **D2 — Amount and financial exposure.** Exposure is the amount at risk of irrecoverable loss, not the claim amount alone. EUR 480,000 paid without fraud evidence is a materially different event from EUR 1,200 paid with a stray autosave record. Exposure should also be read *systemically*: a EUR 4,500 case that proves an interlock is missing implies exposure equal to the whole population that interlock was supposed to protect.
- **D3 — Reversibility.** Money that has left the institution and been accepted by a receiving bank is, in practice, recoverable only with counterparty cooperation. A missing log entry is fully reversible (it can be reconstructed); an unauthorized payment is not. Reversibility is the single biggest severity multiplier, and it is exactly what alignment cost ignores — the aligner will "repair" an irreversible payment for cost 1 or 2.
- **D4 — Timing and ordering.** Doing the right activities in the wrong order can nullify the control entirely. An approval recorded 17 minutes after the bank accepted the payment is not an approval; it is a rationalization. P3 codifies this: order violations on Pay Claim are severe regardless of repair cost.
- **D5 — Customer and regulatory harm.** Duplicate notification is an annoyance; absent notification beyond 24 hours is a conduct-rule breach; wrongful denial or suppressed claims are conduct and litigation risk; payment to a freshly-changed beneficiary account is potential customer detriment plus financial-crime reporting obligations.
- **D6 — Recurrence and systemic signature.** A one-off is an incident; a pattern that appears 74 times with a single common connector, or 4 times with the same policy breached, is a broken control or a broken interface. Recurrence raises severity when the deviation is a control failure and *lowers* per-case severity (while raising remediation priority) when the deviation is a measurement artifact, because a systematic artifact is cheap to fix at source and should not be triaged case-by-case.
- **D7 — Evidence reliability.** How trustworthy is the record on which the finding rests? Independent, immutable, third-party-timestamped evidence (a bank acceptance timestamp, a signed adjuster report) is strong. Self-reported recollection ("a verbal fraud check"), a mutable free-text CRM link, or a mapping table edited after the fact are weak. Weak evidence does not make a control satisfied; it makes the *conclusion* uncertain in one direction and the *audit trail* deficient in another.
- **D8 — Model scope validity.** If the case is out of the model's intended population (P6: denied pre-screen leads, withdrawn claims), the alignment is measuring model misfit, not process misbehaviour. Cost accumulated purely from scope mismatch carries near-zero severity but high *measurement* priority, because it pollutes every aggregate statistic.

**Severity scale (1–5)**

| Level | Meaning | Typical signature |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Critical control failure with irreversible financial exposure or a live financial-crime indicator; requires immediate escalation and investigation | Payment made without the mandated fraud/approval control, or before it, on a high-value or high-risk claim |
| 4 | Serious control failure; the control was bypassed or never invoked, but exposure on this case is bounded, or the harm is prospective rather than realized | Policy-forbidden auto-approval; data-aware interlock demonstrably absent |
| 3 | Material weakness in evidence or execution of a real control; substantive outcome probably correct but not demonstrable to an auditor | Mandated activity performed outside the system with no reliable case linkage |
| 2 | Audit-trail / evidential deficiency with low realized harm; compliance-in-substance likely satisfied | Notification evidenced only by a mutable field; assessment evidenced by a signed external document with sound linkage |
| 1 | No control impact: technical noise, scope mismatch, label drift, or hidden-move artifact | Telemetry moves, retry duplicates, tau moves, denial/withdrawal misfit, renamed activity |

**Confidence scale**

- **High** — corroborated by independent or immutable evidence (bank acceptance timestamp, signed third-party document, message-ID plus transport log, explicit structured attributes such as amount/risk/bank-change date).
- **Medium** — single-source or reconstructable evidence with a plausible benign alternative that has not yet been excluded (external document without stable case key; alternate-channel notification behind a mutable link).
- **Low** — evidence is self-reported, mutable, or the finding rests only on absence (a missing event).

**How confidence differs from severity.** Severity is a property of the *worst credible state of the world consistent with the evidence*, weighted by exposure and reversibility. Confidence is a property of *our knowledge* about which state obtains. They are orthogonal and must not be multiplied into one number:

- High severity + low confidence does **not** mean "medium". It means "escalate and investigate now": CL-602 is severity 5 whether or not the verbal fraud check happened, because either the control was skipped (fraud exposure) or it was performed without any record (evidential failure on a EUR 480,000 irreversible payment). Uncertainty selects the *type* of response (investigation, funds recall attempt, forensic reconstruction) rather than lowering the priority.
- Low severity + high confidence means "fix at source and stop reporting it": CL-603's duplicate notification is certainly a retry, and certainly harmless.
- A key discipline, applied throughout: **absence of an event is not proof that the activity did not occur.** A missing `Fraud Screen` event is proof that fraud screening is *not evidenced*. For an institution that must demonstrate its controls, non-evidenced equals non-effective for control-reporting purposes — but it does not equal "fraud screening was skipped" as a factual finding, and the two must be reported differently.

**Deviation taxonomy used in Section 2**

1. **Process-model mismatch** — the model does not describe a legitimate real-world path (CL-607, CL-612).
2. **Event-label mismatch** — the same activity under a different name (CL-611).
3. **Missing evidence** — the activity plausibly occurred but left no in-scope record (CL-606, CL-608, and the evidential half of CL-602).
4. **Actual operational deviation** — the real process genuinely departed from the intended one (CL-604; CL-602 under the adverse hypothesis).
5. **Data-aware policy failure** — control flow conforms perfectly, but a data condition in the policy overlay is breached (CL-609, CL-610).
6. **Harmless technical noise** — telemetry, retries, transport artifacts, hidden moves (CL-601, CL-603, CL-605).

**Explicit notes required by the task**

- **Silent moves are fitting.** CL-605's `T(tau, c=0)` is a routing transition manufactured by the process-tree-to-Petri-net conversion of the exclusive choice. It has no log counterpart by construction, costs nothing, and is **not a deviation**. It should be filtered out of every diagnostic view and excluded from every deviation count; presenting it alongside real moves invites false findings.
- **Zero-cost policy failures.** CL-609 and CL-610 both align perfectly (cost 0) and both breach P1. This is not a defect of the aligner — it is a scope statement. The process tree encodes *ordering and choice*; P1 and P2 are guards over *amount, risk grade and beneficiary-change recency*. A control-flow model with an unguarded XOR will happily certify every branch of that XOR for every case. Until the guards are encoded (data-aware conformance, decision mining on the XOR, or an out-of-band rule check), a cost of 0 on an auto-approved claim carries **no assurance whatsoever** about approval-policy compliance. The four cases identified in the pattern evidence with "fully synchronous Auto Approve despite violating P1 or P2" are, collectively, the most dangerous finding in this dataset precisely because they are invisible.

## 3. Repeated Deviation Patterns

**Pattern A — Retry duplicates on notification (harmless technical noise)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-603, plus 74 cases in the month exhibiting `L(Notify Claimant)` immediately after a synchronous notification with an identical message ID, **all** associated with a single retrying connector.
- *Business risk:* Very low. Substantively the claimant was notified once. Two residual risks: (i) if the connector genuinely re-transmitted rather than merely re-logging, a minority of claimants received duplicate messages — a minor conduct/annoyance issue; (ii) 74 spurious deviations inflate the deviation count, degrade every conformance KPI, and consume triage capacity that should go to CL-602 and CL-610.
- *Likely cause:* Non-idempotent messaging connector using at-least-once delivery, combined with an ingestion layer that emits one business event per transport attempt instead of per logical message. The perfect correlation with one connector makes this near-certain.
- *Confidence:* High. Identical message IDs, corroborating transport logs, single-connector concentration, and 74 consistent instances.
- *Recommended owner:* Integration / messaging platform engineering, with the event-log ETL owner for the deduplication rule.

**Pattern B — External-adjuster assessment gaps (missing evidence)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-606, plus nine high-value cases in the month with `M(Assess Loss)` accompanied by an external-adjuster document; **two of the nine lack a stable document-to-case identifier**.
- *Business risk:* Moderate and concentrated in the highest-value segment. The substantive control (professional loss assessment) is very likely operating, but it is not demonstrable from the system of record. For the two cases without a stable identifier the risk is materially higher: the linkage rests on inference, so an auditor or regulator could reasonably conclude the assessment control is unevidenced for high-value claims, and a fabricated or misattributed report could not be detected.
- *Likely cause:* Loss assessment for large claims is outsourced; adjuster work products land in a document store with no write-back integration to the claims core, and document metadata does not reliably carry the claim identifier.
- *Confidence:* High on the mechanism (the pattern is coherent and the documents exist); Medium on per-case linkage, dropping to Low for the two cases with no stable identifier.
- *Recommended owner:* Claims operations (large-loss) for the process, jointly with integration engineering for the document-to-case write-back.

**Pattern C — Activity-label mapping drift (event-label mismatch)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-611 explicitly; the paired-L/M signature (`L(X)` immediately followed by `M(Y)` at the same sequence position) should be swept for across the period, since the mapping table was updated only *after* the case, meaning every earlier case is potentially affected.
- *Business risk:* Low in substance, high in measurement integrity. Zero operational impact, but each occurrence generates two units of cost and can, at scale, dominate the cost distribution and obscure genuine deviations.
- *Likely cause:* Uncontrolled naming divergence between system-emitted labels, the signed procedure and the UI, plus an unversioned mapping table applied prospectively only and never back-applied to extracted logs.
- *Confidence:* High. The adjacency of the L/M pair, the identical sequence position, the documented mapping update, and the fully-conformant surrounding trace all agree.
- *Recommended owner:* Data governance / process-mining CoE, with the claims system product owner for the canonical activity dictionary.

**Pattern D — Out-of-scope denials and withdrawals (process-model mismatch)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-607 and CL-612; the pattern evidence states that denied and withdrawn cases **account for most alignments with raw cost ≥ 4**.
- *Business risk:* Near-zero as a payment-control matter — both cases represent correct outcomes with no money at risk. The real risk is analytic: these cases dominate the top of any cost-ranked worklist, systematically crowding out low-cost/high-severity findings and creating a metric that rewards paying claims and penalizes not paying them. A secondary, genuine risk sits *outside* this model: denial correctness, appeal rights, and whether withdrawals are truly claimant-initiated are all currently unmeasured.
- *Likely cause:* The model (M0) was designed for the payable happy path (P6) and has no denial, withdrawal or cancellation branches, while the conformance population filter admits all claim cases regardless of outcome.
- *Confidence:* High on the cause and the artifact; the scope question itself is a governance decision between the process owner and the model owner, not an evidential one.
- *Recommended owner:* Process owner and model owner jointly, escalated to the process-governance forum to settle P6 with a documented, dated decision.

**Pattern E — Data-aware auto-approval failures (zero-cost policy breaches)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-609 and CL-610; the pattern evidence confirms **four cases with a fully synchronous Auto Approve path despite violating P1 or P2**. Because these cases are invisible to control-flow conformance, four is a floor, not an estimate — the true count is whatever a data-aware re-scan of the period finds.
- *Business risk:* **The highest in this dataset.** Every instance is an approval control that did not operate on a payment that has already been made. CL-610 additionally carries a live payment-diversion signature. Because the failure mode is silent, it can persist indefinitely with a green conformance dashboard, and its aggregate exposure is bounded only by the volume routed through the auto-approval path.
- *Likely cause:* The auto-approval decision logic does not implement all three P1 guards. Most plausibly: the amount threshold is mis-configured, stale, or evaluated on the wrong monetary field; the risk grade is read before the scoring model refreshes it or MEDIUM is treated as passing; and the seven-day beneficiary-bank-change lookback is either unimplemented or queries a customer-master system the engine cannot reach synchronously. The model's unguarded XOR then certifies the result.
- *Confidence:* High that the breaches occurred (amount, risk grade and bank-change timestamps are structured, unambiguous attributes). Medium on which specific engine defect produced each one — that requires the decision traces.
- *Recommended owner:* Underwriting/claims-authority policy owner accountable for the rule set, with the rule-engine product owner for the fix and second-line risk/compliance for the assurance sweep.

**Pattern F — Missing or late authorization on high-exposure payments (severe operational and evidential failures)**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-602 (fraud screen unevidenced on EUR 480,000 with a fresh bank change) and CL-604 (payment 17 minutes before approval, plus no fraud screen, on EUR 90,000). Two cases here, but both above EUR 50,000 and both irreversible; the population should be swept for (i) `M(Fraud Screen)` on any case where P2 is triggered and (ii) any case where the payment timestamp precedes the approval timestamp.
- *Business risk:* Critical. These are direct failures of the payment-authorization control on the institution's largest outflows, with realized and irrecoverable financial exposure, financial-crime reporting implications, and — if they recur — a reportable control-environment deficiency.
- *Likely cause:* Two distinct mechanisms that happen to share a symptom. CL-602 points to a screening step that can be bypassed or that occurs in an unlogged external tool; CL-604 points to a payment-release path that does not require a prior approval token (straight-through release, or a batch cut on an independent schedule). They must not be remediated as one item.
- *Confidence:* High on CL-604 (independent bank timestamp). Medium-to-High on CL-602 — high that the control is unevidenced, medium on whether any screening occurred at all.
- *Recommended owner:* Financial crime / fraud investigations for the case work; payments engineering for the release interlock; second-line control owner for the authorization control design.

**Pattern G — Notification evidence held outside the core with weak linkage**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-608; likely broader wherever the secure portal or CRM is the delivery channel.
- *Business risk:* Low-to-moderate. P4 explicitly permits alternate channels, so this is not a conduct breach where the message genuinely went out. The exposure is evidential: a mutable free-text link cannot support a regulatory assertion that the 24-hour notification obligation was met, and it is trivially alterable after the fact.
- *Likely cause:* Multi-channel customer communication without a system-of-record integration; the CRM link was built as an operational convenience field rather than a controlled, immutable reference.
- *Confidence:* Medium. The notification probably occurred within three hours; the artifact relied upon is weak.
- *Recommended owner:* Customer-communications platform owner, with records-management/compliance for the evidence-retention standard.

**Pattern H — UI telemetry contaminating the business event log**

- *Cases and frequency:* CL-601 (three moves in one case); by nature this affects any case handled through the workbench, so the true frequency is likely large.
- *Business risk:* None operationally (P5 is explicit). Purely a signal-to-noise problem: telemetry generates the highest raw costs in the set on the most compliant cases.
- *Likely cause:* Log extraction pulls the front-end interaction stream alongside the business event stream, with no event-type whitelist.
- *Confidence:* High.
- *Recommended owner:* Event-log ETL / process-mining CoE.

**Pattern I — Cost-function design (a finding in its own right)**

- *Evidence:* "Alignment costs were configured uniformly at 1 for every visible log/model move, with no business-specific weighting." The consequence is visible across the whole table: raw cost ranges 0–4 while severity ranges 1–5, and the two are close to *inversely* related at the extremes (cost 0 → severity 4 twice; cost 4 → severity 1 twice).
- *Business risk:* High, indirectly. A uniform cost function turns the conformance output into a worklist that is actively misleading, and any downstream automation, KPI or management report built on it inherits the inversion.
- *Likely cause:* Default configuration, never revisited after the pilot.
- *Confidence:* High — stated directly and confirmed by the case data.
- *Recommended owner:* Process-mining CoE with risk/compliance, to define risk-weighted move costs and a separate business-severity layer.

## 4. Causal Hypotheses and Evidence Tests

A standing principle for all of the below: **a missing event is evidence about the log, not about the world.** Every hypothesis set therefore includes at least one "the activity happened but wasn't recorded" branch, and the tests are designed to discriminate rather than to confirm.

**1. CL-602 — no `Fraud Screen` event on a EUR 480,000 HIGH-risk claim with a one-day-old bank change**

- *H1 (control bypass):* Screening was genuinely skipped, whether under value/time pressure, via an override, or deliberately.
- *H2 (out-of-system execution):* Screening was performed in a dedicated financial-crime platform whose events never reach the claims log — the same integration gap as Pattern B, applied to a different activity.
- *H3 (informal check only):* The approver performed an unstructured verbal/manual check that was never a system activity and never met the definition of the mandated control.
- *H4 (collusion or compromise):* The approval was procured fraudulently and the absence of screening is deliberate rather than accidental — the amount, HIGH risk grade and one-day-old bank change together make this a hypothesis that must be explicitly tested, not assumed away.
- *Discriminating evidence:* Query the fraud-screening platform directly for any case reference, alert, disposition or analyst assignment in the relevant window — a hit supports H2 and largely exonerates the process. Pull the approver's session and workstation logs: was the screening tool even opened? Check whether an override/emergency-payment code was used and whether it required a reason. Inspect whether *other* high-value cases from the same approver, team or period also lack screening events — a broad pattern favours H2 (systemic integration gap), an isolated instance on the single largest claim favours H1 or H4. Interview the approver on the specifics of the verbal check (with whom, what was checked, what was seen) and test those specifics against independent records; a check that cannot be described in verifiable detail supports H3. Verify the beneficiary account independently and check whether the changed details resemble known mule patterns — a match sharply raises H4.
- *Decision rule:* H2 alone would reduce this to a Pattern-B-class evidential finding. Until the screening platform is queried and returns a hit, the case must be handled at severity 5.

**2. CL-604 — payment accepted by the bank at 10:03, manual approval recorded at 10:20**

- *H1 (clock skew / logging latency):* The approval actually preceded the payment and the timestamps are unreliable.
- *H2 (straight-through release):* The payment path constructs and releases instructions without checking for an approval token; the approval queue is decoupled from the release queue.
- *H3 (retrospective papering):* The payment was made out-of-band (manually, or under an emergency path) and the approval was entered afterwards to close the case cleanly.
- *H4 (batch timing):* The payment sat in a scheduled batch that cut on its own cadence, independent of approval status.
- *Discriminating evidence:* H1 is already weak because the 10:03 timestamp is the *receiving bank's* acceptance, an external clock — but confirm by comparing the payment-instruction creation time, the file transmission time and the approval time across a sample, and check NTP synchronization. For H2 versus H4, inspect the payment instruction record: does it carry an approval reference field, and is that field populated, empty, or populated with a reference created after the instruction? Does the release mechanism enforce the check, or merely display it? For H4, check whether the payment time coincides with a recurring batch window and whether other payments went out in the same batch. For H3, examine the approval record's creation and modification metadata, whether the approver's UI showed the payment as already made, and whether any comment or reason code was entered.
- *Note:* H2, H3 and H4 are all severity 5. Only H1 would downgrade the case, and the external bank timestamp already makes it improbable.

**3. CL-609 and CL-610 — fully synchronous auto-approvals that breach P1**

- *H1 (threshold mis-configuration):* The rule engine's amount limit or risk-grade condition is wrong, stale, or absent.
- *H2 (wrong data field):* The engine evaluates the correct rule against the wrong value — net-of-deductible instead of gross payable, reserve instead of settlement, or a pre-adjustment amount that later rose to EUR 18,000.
- *H3 (timing / staleness):* The engine read the risk grade or beneficiary-change status before it was updated — the grade was LOW at decision time and became MEDIUM later; the bank change was recorded in the customer master after the auto-approval decision was taken.
- *H4 (unimplemented condition):* The seven-day beneficiary-change lookback was simply never built, so CL-610 was never evaluated against it at all.
- *H5 (manual override):* A handler forced the auto-approval route.
- *Discriminating evidence:* Retrieve the rule-engine decision trace for both cases — it should show every condition evaluated, the values used and the outcome, which separates H1, H2 and H4 immediately (an unevaluated condition is decisive for H4). Compare the amount the engine saw against the amount finally paid to test H2. Reconstruct the risk grade and beneficiary-change records *as at the decision timestamp* using their own change history to test H3; for CL-610 the bank change was one hour before payment, so establish whether it preceded the approval decision as well. Check for override flags or elevated-authority markers for H5. Then back-test: replay the whole period's claims through the current rule configuration and count how many auto-approvals should have been blocked — a large count supports H1/H4 (systemic), a handful supports H3/H5 (incidental).
- *Note:* H3 is the only hypothesis that partially excuses the decision *at the time it was made*, and even then it identifies a real design defect: a control that reads stale risk data is not a control. It does not reduce the need for a compensating post-decision check before payment release.

**4. CL-606 and Pattern B — `M(Assess Loss)` with an external adjuster report**

- *H1 (out-of-system execution):* Assessment was genuinely performed by the licensed adjuster and simply never written back as an event.
- *H2 (assessment skipped, report is boilerplate or late):* The report was produced pro forma, or after the fact, without a real assessment informing the settlement.
- *H3 (document belongs to a different case):* Especially for the two cases lacking a stable document-to-case identifier, the report may have been mis-linked.
- *Discriminating evidence:* Check the report's signature and timestamp integrity, and confirm 10:05 precedes the fraud screen and approval (as stated) — sequencing consistent with a real input supports H1. Compare the report's assessed quantum against the amount actually paid: a close match supports H1; a settlement unrelated to the report's figure supports H2. Verify the adjuster's licence, engagement record, invoice and assignment date for this specific claim. For H3, match policy number, claimant name, loss date and loss location between the document and the case; where these are absent, the linkage is inference and must be recorded as such. Interview the handler on how the report was received and used.
- *Note:* Do not conclude from `M(Assess Loss)` that no assessment occurred. The signed external document is affirmative evidence *for* the activity; the alignment merely shows the core system did not witness it.

**5. CL-608 — no notification event, CRM link to a secure-portal message at +3 hours**

- *H1 (genuine alternate-channel notification):* The claimant was notified via the portal within three hours; only the core-system record is missing.
- *H2 (link is inaccurate or fabricated):* The mutable text field was populated incorrectly, optimistically, or deliberately to close a compliance gap.
- *H3 (message generated but not delivered):* A portal message was created but never actually reached the claimant (delivery failure, dormant portal account, no email/SMS alert).
- *Discriminating evidence:* Go to the portal's own system of record and retrieve the message object, its creation and delivery timestamps, and any read receipt — this is independent of the CRM field and settles H1 versus H2 outright. Check the CRM field's audit history: when was it written, by whom, and was it edited after the case closed? Late or post-hoc population supports H2. For H3, check the portal account's activity: was the claimant logged in, was an out-of-band alert sent, was the message opened, and did the claimant subsequently contact the insurer as if uninformed (a complaint or "what's happening with my claim" call would be strong evidence of non-delivery).
- *Note:* H1 and H3 look identical in the CRM. Only the portal's delivery telemetry separates them, which is precisely why the mutable link is inadequate evidence.

**6. CL-611 and Pattern C — `L(Assess Damage)` paired with `M(Assess Loss)`**

- *H1 (pure rename):* One activity, two names; the mapping table lagged.
- *H2 (two genuinely different activities):* `Assess Damage` is a preliminary damage estimate and `Assess Loss` is a formal loss adjustment; the case performed the former and skipped the latter. This would convert a severity-1 finding into a real gap on a EUR 62,000 HIGH-risk claim.
- *H3 (system-version divergence):* A particular release or regional instance emits the old label, so the pattern is bounded to that population.
- *Discriminating evidence:* Read the signed procedure and the activity dictionary for both terms — if only one is defined, H1. Inspect the emitting code path or configuration: does `Assess Damage` write to the same table, with the same payload schema, same required fields and same role permissions as `Assess Loss`? Identical schema and handler strongly support H1. Check whether *any* case in the period contains **both** labels — if none does, they are the same activity; if some cases contain both, they are distinct and H2 becomes live. Review the mapping-table change record for a stated rationale. For H3, chart the label's occurrence by system version, release date and region.
- *Note:* This test is cheap and must be run before the mapping is back-applied; back-applying a mapping that conflates two distinct activities would permanently erase a real control gap from the record.

**7. CL-603 and Pattern A — duplicate notification with an identical message ID**

- *H1 (log-only duplication):* The connector retried at the transport layer and the ingestion pipeline logged each attempt; the claimant received exactly one message.
- *H2 (genuine double send):* The retry actually re-transmitted and the claimant received two identical messages.
- *H3 (two distinct notifications coincidentally sharing an ID):* An ID-generation collision or reuse — very unlikely, but it would mean the duplicate is a real second business event.
- *Discriminating evidence:* Inspect the transport log for the retry's outcome — was the first attempt acknowledged (H1: the retry was spurious) or did it fail before acknowledgement (H2: the retry genuinely delivered)? Query the downstream channel (email/SMS gateway or portal) for the number of delivery events against that message ID. Check inbound contacts for "I received two copies" complaints. For H3, verify that the ID-generation scheme is collision-resistant and that the two events carry identical payloads and recipients.
- *Note:* Either way this is severity 1, but H2 warrants a small customer-experience fix and possibly an apology workflow, while H1 needs only an ingestion dedupe.

**8. CL-607, CL-612 and Pattern D — denials and withdrawals scoring cost ≥ 4**

- *H1 (scope artifact):* The model legitimately excludes these outcomes; the cost is pure model misfit and carries no risk signal.
- *H2 (genuine model incompleteness):* Denials and withdrawals are in-scope business behaviour that the model should represent, and their absence means a whole class of process behaviour is unmanaged and unmeasured.
- *H3 (masked misconduct):* Some "withdrawals" or "denials" are not genuine claimant decisions — pressure to withdraw, premature denial to hit cycle-time or loss-ratio targets, or reclassification to avoid the approval controls entirely. This is the one hypothesis in which these cheap-looking cases would be genuinely severe.
- *Discriminating evidence:* For H1 versus H2, this is a governance decision — obtain a documented ruling on P6 from the process-governance forum, and check whether denial and withdrawal outcomes are subject to any other control framework today. For H3, the tests are behavioural: check whether withdrawals cluster by handler, team, period-end, or claim size band; look for withdrawals immediately following an unfavourable assessment; check whether withdrawn or denied claims are later re-opened, appealed, complained about, escalated to an ombudsman, or re-submitted; verify that withdrawal requests are evidenced by a claimant-originated artifact (signed form, recorded call, portal action) rather than a handler-entered flag; and confirm denials cite a specific policy clause. Compare denial and withdrawal rates against portfolio norms.
- *Note:* Under H1 or H2 these cases are severity 1 and the remediation is a model change. Under H3 they are a conduct issue of high severity that the *current* model structurally cannot detect — a second, complementary example (alongside CL-609 and CL-610) of risk hiding where the model is not looking.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

Alignment cost is the minimum total weight of edits required to transform an observed trace into a trace the model accepts, under a chosen cost function. Every element of that definition — *minimum*, *edits*, *the model*, *the cost function* — is a modelling choice, and none of them was chosen to represent risk. The supplied cases demonstrate the resulting divergence along eight distinct axes.

**1. Cost-function design.** Costs here were "configured uniformly at 1 for every visible log/model move, with no business-specific weighting." That single configuration decision asserts that one stray autosave record equals one missing fraud screen on a EUR 480,000 payment. **CL-601** (three telemetry moves, perfectly compliant, cost 3) therefore scores three times **CL-602** (unevidenced fraud screen on the largest claim in the set, cost 1). The metric is internally consistent and externally meaningless: it is counting edit operations, and edit operations are not fungible with control failures. Risk-weighting the function narrows the gap — moves on `Pay Claim`, `Fraud Screen` and `Manual Approve` should cost far more than moves on peripheral activities — but it cannot close it, because weights are per-activity constants while risk is a function of amount, timing, reversibility and evidence quality that varies case by case.

**2. Scope: the model only knows what it was built to know.** The tree encodes ordering and choice for payable claims. Anything outside that scope produces cost that is a statement about the model, not the process. **CL-607** (denial, cost 4) and **CL-612** (withdrawal, cost 4) are the joint-most-expensive alignments in the set and represent, respectively, a correctly denied claim and a claimant who withdrew — the two cases in which the insurer paid nothing and carried the least financial risk. The bias is systematic, not incidental: because the model charges for every unexecuted downstream activity, cost *increases the earlier a case legitimately terminates*. The metric literally penalizes not paying claims. This is why "denied and withdrawn cases account for most alignments with raw cost ≥ 4" — the top of the cost distribution is populated by the safest outcomes in the portfolio.

**3. Hidden moves: zero cost that genuinely means conformance — and the reader must know the difference.** **CL-605**'s `T(tau, c=0)` is a routing transition manufactured by converting the tree's exclusive choice into a Petri net. It is invisible by construction, fitting by definition, and not a deviation. Here zero cost is *correct*: the case is genuinely clean. The problem is that the very same zero appears on **CL-609** and **CL-610**, where it certifies nothing of the sort. A reviewer looking at a cost column cannot distinguish "conformant and safe" from "conformant and in breach", because the number is identical. Worse, a reviewer unfamiliar with alignment semantics may do the opposite and log the tau move as a finding. The scalar destroys exactly the information needed to act.

**4. Data conditions: the zero-cost policy failure.** This is the central lesson of the dataset. **CL-609** (EUR 18,000, MEDIUM risk, auto-approved) and **CL-610** (EUR 4,500, LOW risk but the beneficiary bank changed one hour before payment, auto-approved) both align **perfectly, at cost 0**. Both breach P1. CL-610 additionally triggers P2 and displays the textbook signature of payment diversion. The reason conformance is silent is structural: the process tree's XOR has no guard on it, so *every* case that takes the `Auto Approve` branch is fitting by construction, whatever its amount, risk grade or beneficiary history. Control-flow conformance can only ever answer "was this ordering permitted?" — it cannot answer "was this branch permitted *for this case*?" A cost of 0 on an auto-approved claim therefore carries **zero assurance** about approval-policy compliance. The pattern evidence confirms four such cases, and because they are invisible, four is a floor. In a cost-ranked worklist, these cases sort to the very bottom and are never opened.

**5. Activity granularity and labelling.** **CL-611** is a fully compliant EUR 62,000 HIGH-risk claim in which the core system emitted `Assess Damage` while the procedure says `Assess Loss`. One naming inconsistency generates **two** moves — a log move and a model move — so a metadata problem scores cost 2 and outranks both CL-602 and CL-608 at cost 1. Two compounding distortions are at work: a single conceptual discrepancy is double-counted because the aligner sees two unmatched transitions, and the discrepancy is not a process fact at all but an artifact of the naming convention and an unversioned mapping table. Change the label and the cost vanishes without a single thing changing in the real world — a decisive demonstration that alignment cost is a property of the *representation*, not of the *behaviour*.

**6. Duplicated telemetry and transport artifacts.** **CL-603** carries cost 1 for a second `Notify Claimant` that is a connector retry with an identical message ID, corroborated by transport logs and replicated across 74 cases traced to one component. **CL-601** carries cost 3 for workbench and autosave events that P5 explicitly declares non-business. In both, cost measures the *plumbing of the log pipeline*: at-least-once delivery semantics, ingestion granularity, and the absence of an extraction whitelist. Aggregated, these artifacts do carry information — 74 identical duplicates is a precise, actionable data-quality finding — but that information is about the integration estate, not about claims handling. Treating it as risk both wastes triage capacity and dilutes the deviation statistics that management relies on.

**7. Missing-but-external evidence: absence of an event is not absence of an activity.** **CL-606** shows `M(Assess Loss)` on a EUR 75,000 HIGH-risk claim, yet an externally licensed adjuster signed an assessment report at 10:05 and the fraud screen and approval both followed it. The substantive control almost certainly operated — outside the system of record. Cost 1 reads as "one deviation"; the accurate reading is "zero handling deviation, one integration gap". **CL-608** is the mirror image at the same cost: the notification was almost certainly delivered via the secure portal within three hours, but the only linkage is a mutable free-text field. Both score 1, and the alignment cost cannot express that one is backed by a signed third-party document and the other by an editable note. **Alignment cost is blind to evidence quality**, which is precisely the dimension that determines whether a finding is a control failure, an audit-trail gap, or nothing at all. The same blindness runs the other way in **CL-602**: cost 1 for a missing `Fraud Screen` does not tell us whether screening happened elsewhere or never happened — and the correct response (investigate) depends entirely on which.

**8. Irreversibility and timing.** The aligner treats every move as an abstract, freely-reversible edit. Reality does not. In **CL-604**, the receiving bank accepted EUR 90,000 at 10:03 and the manual approval was recorded at 10:20. The alignment expresses this as `L(Pay Claim, c=1)` early plus `M(Pay Claim, c=1)` in the legal position — cost 2 to "move the payment back where it belongs". No such operation exists in the world. The money is gone, and the approval that arrived seventeen minutes later could not have prevented anything; it is documentation, not control. P3 recognizes this explicitly: payment before approval is a severe control failure *even if the model alignment can repair it cheaply*. The aligner is also indifferent to *which* activity moved — reordering `Pay Claim` costs exactly what reordering a notification would — whereas the business distinguishes them absolutely. Timing and reversibility are the two dimensions where edit distance and business risk diverge most sharply, and they are also the two that matter most for payment controls.

A ninth point falls out of reproducing CL-604, and it sharpens all of the above. Recomputing the alignment from the tree finds a **cheaper** optimum than the supplied one: instead of explaining the trace along the manual branch (early payment, missing fraud screen, model payment reinserted after approval, cost 3), the search routes the case down the *automatic* branch and reads it as "an auto-approved claim that happened to log a stray manual approval" — one model move for `Auto Approve`, one log move for `Manual Approve`, cost 2. That cheaper explanation makes `Pay Claim` synchronous and therefore **erases the ordering violation entirely**. It is also business nonsense: P1 forbids the automatic branch for a EUR 90,000 MEDIUM-risk claim, and the bank's 10:03 acceptance against the 10:20 approval is independent, external evidence that the payment genuinely preceded its authorization. The lesson generalizes beyond this dataset: cost-minimality is not truth-maximality. An aligner returns the cheapest *story* consistent with the model, and when the model contains an unguarded choice, the cheapest story can be the one that conceals the most serious finding. This is why the supplied alignments are treated as authoritative diagnostic inputs, why alternative optima must be surfaced rather than silently adopted, and why data conditions belong in the model: had the XOR carried its P1 guard, the cheap auto-approval explanation would have been infeasible and the aligner would have been forced to tell the truth.

**Synthesis.** Across the twelve supplied cases, raw cost ranges 0–4 and assessed severity ranges 1–5, and at both extremes the relationship is *inverted*: the two cost-0 cases CL-609 and CL-610 are severity 4 and among the most urgent in the portfolio, while the two cost-4 cases CL-607 and CL-612 are severity 1 and represent correct outcomes. The cheapest non-zero case, CL-602 at cost 1, carries EUR 480,000 of irrecoverable exposure and a live fraud typology. The most expensive fully-conformant-in-substance case, CL-601 at cost 3, is flawless. Alignment cost is a genuinely valuable *detector* — it localizes where log and model disagree, and its aggregate patterns (74 retries, 9 adjuster gaps, a label drift) are precise and actionable diagnostics. It is not a *severity measure*, and it must never be used as a triage ordering. The correct architecture keeps technical cost as an input, adds data-aware policy checks to cover the zero-cost blind spot, and layers explicit human judgement about exposure, reversibility, evidence quality and scope on top — reported in separate columns so that the divergence between them remains visible as a diagnostic in its own right.

  1. Technical alignment cost and business severity live in different columns

"""

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

TREE_STRING = (
    "-> ( 'Register Claim', 'Validate Coverage', 'Assess Loss', "

    "'Pay Claim', 'Notify Claimant' )"

    f"{len(net.transitions)} transitions "

# realised with silent routing transitions depends on the conversion and the

# CL-605 alignment is authoritative in stating that the converted net contains a
# hidden transition before Auto Approve. Either way the conclusion is unchanged:
# T(tau, c=0) moves are FITTING BY CONSTRUCTION and must never be reported as
# deviations. The count printed above shows what this environment produced.


# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Build a small event log from the case traces
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Sequences are the LOG PROJECTION of the supplied alignments: every S(...) and
# every L(...) contributes an observed event, in order. M(...) contributes
# nothing (no log event exists) and T(tau) is invisible.
#
# CL-604 deliberately places Pay Claim BEFORE Manual Approve: the bank accepted
# the funds at 10:03 and the approval was recorded at 10:20.

CASE_TRACES = {

    for position, activity in enumerate(activities):
        rows.append(
