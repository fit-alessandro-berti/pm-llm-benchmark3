# 1. Fairness dimensions

A procedurally fair process treats similarly situated applicants similarly, imposes burdens proportionate to documented need, and does not penalize the exercise of legal rights. Eight dimensions structure the assessment below.

**1.1 Outcome parity (raw).** Approval, cycle time, appeal, and rework rates differ sharply across groups (e.g., 78% vs. 58% approval; 8.1 vs. 15.9 median days). These are useful *flags* but weak *inferences*: the groups differ systematically in measured risk, documentation status, channel, and prior history. Raw gaps alone support no conclusion about discrimination — but they are also not dismissible as "just case mix" until a like-for-like analysis confirms it.

**1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for measured case characteristics, residual gaps remain: approval 5.5 pp (B), 2.2 pp (C), 3.1 pp (D); cycle time +2.6/+4.1/+0.9 days. These residuals are the closest thing to "similar cases treated differently," but they inherit any bias in the controls. Because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` are themselves potentially shaped by the process and by historical inequity, the adjusted figures should be read as a *bracket*, not a point truth (see §2.5, §5).

**1.3 Procedural burden.** Beyond outcomes, we should ask: what must an applicant endure to reach a decision? Indicators: number of steps, handovers between workers, extra document requests, and repetition. Here the adjusted probability of *Request Missing Documents* is +9.5 pp for Group B even relative to the same measured completeness, and Groups B/C carry handover loops (generalist  bilingual; generalist  specialist  supervisor). Burden falling asymmetrically on rights-using applicants is a core procedural-fairness failure mode, independent of final decisions.

**1.4 Timeliness.** Medians mask tail harm: 90th-percentile cycle times run 34–39 days for B/C versus 21.4 for A — consequential when benefits affect food and housing security. Timeliness must be decomposed: *waiting for the agency* (queues, understaffing) versus *waiting attributable to the applicant* (supplying documents). The evidence points to agency-side queues attached specifically to language-support and accommodation steps (§3).

**1.5 Consistency of routing.** Identical documented characteristics should trigger identical routing regardless of group. The adjusted probability of *Supervisor Review* is +8.0 pp for B and +5.5 pp for C relative to comparable A cases — higher-scrutiny routing not explained by measured complexity is a red flag, *unless* region-specific staffing (the two backlog regions) explains it. Legitimacy of routing must be tested against the written rule structure (high risk  Fraud Review; accommodation  Accommodation Review; minority language  Language Support Contact), not assumed.

**1.6 Quality/accessibility of support.** Rights-preserving steps must actually work. For Group B, did the Language Support Contact resolve comprehension problems, or did it merely add a queue stop (3.8-day median wait, 7 bilingual workers)? Proxy signals available: downstream document-request frequency, repeat contacts, appeal rates. Direct signals (was the explanation understood? were documents gathered correctly the first time?) are absent from the log — a serious limitation (§5).

**1.7 Appeal/rework burden.** Excess appeals (raw 13%/10%/16% vs. 6%; adjusted +4.4 pp B, +5.7 pp D) admit two very different readings: (a) *mechanical* — more denials produce more appeals; (b) *procedural* — poor explanations, miscalculations, or accumulated distrust drive avoidable appeals. The adjusted model apparently does not condition on decision outcome, so neither reading is settled. High rework/reopen rates additionally impose repeated burden on already-burdened applicants. Critically missing: appeal *success rates* by group, which would reveal where initial decisions err.

**1.8 Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A cases flow through 52 caseworkers; Group B concentrates on 7 bilingual workers plus 3 known-overloaded officers; Group C on 5 specialists (two of whom also carry supervisor duties). Equal-quality service requires capacity proportional to need; concentration creates queuing, fragility (one absence stalls a whole group's pipeline), and per-worker variance in practice that lands unevenly across groups. This is an institutional-design issue, not evidence of caseworker misconduct.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Goal: separate (i) *disparity* (a difference persists like-for-like), (ii) *legitimate routing* (the difference is entailed by documented, auditable rules reacting to applicant characteristics), (iii) *discrimination/unfairness* (group-linked differential treatment or rights-penalties beyond justification), and (iv) *uncertainty* (how confident we can be).

### 2.1 Matching / stratification
Define comparable units with coarsened exact matching (CEM) or propensity-score matching with calipers on:
`risk_score_band × documentation_completeness (coarse) × channel × household_size_band × prior_case_count (bands) × region × month`. Region × month matters because supervisor-review backlogs are region-specific. Compute standard balance diagnostics (SMD < 0.1 post-match) and keep unmatched cases visible rather than silently dropped. With many cells, CEM in large bins is preferable to fragile fine stratification.

Add a *within-group contrast*: inside Group B, compare members who used language support vs. those who did not (conditional on the same observables). Any persistent delay/burden gap estimates an "exercise-of-rights penalty" with group identity held constant — cleaner attribution than cross-group contrasts alone. Same trick for accommodation within Group C.

### 2.2 Path/variant comparison
Within matched sets, compare (a) full-variant distributions (`pm4py.get_variants`, ²/Fisher), (b) transition-level rates (more robust than whole-variant alignment), and (c) *rule-adherence*: reconstruct the policy's routing logic and measure, per group, the rate at which routing deviates from it. Deviation asymmetry = inconsistency regardless of outcomes. Decompose gaps using path structure as a mediator: how much of B's +2.6 days flows through (i) being routed into V4/V2 at all vs. (ii) moving slowly *within* those paths?

### 2.3 Resource-assignment comparison
Given the same variant and region, compare: which workers receive which groups; concurrent load per worker (noting the daily-granularity caveat); handovers per case; and dual-hatting overlaps. Test whether B cases handled by mixed teams fare differently from bilingual-team cases — this distinguishes a *capacity* story from a *caseworker-practice* story.

### 2.4 Waiting-time decomposition
Insert service/waiting splits (`insert_case_service_waiting_time`) and build per-group performance DFGs. Attribute the adjusted cycle-time gaps edge-by-edge: language queue (+3.8d), accommodation queue (+4.7d), doc-request loop (1.6 vs 0.4d), supervisor backlog (1.9 vs 1.0d). Report which edge dominates the *tail* (p90), since slow-path cases concentrate there.

### 2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates
- **Leave-one-out refits:** drop `risk_score_band`, then `documentation_completeness`, from the adjustment set. If gaps grow when a suspect control is removed, that control was absorbing group-linked disadvantage (over-control).
- **Bounding:** ask how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to erase the B 5.5 pp or C +4.1-day residual (E-value-style logic). Report the required strength; small requirements mean the residual is fragile.
- **Measurement audit on a sample:** blind re-assessment of `documentation_completeness` (and missing-doc requests' substantive necessity) by reviewers unaware of language/group; estimate misclassification and correct.
- **Negative-control transitions:** stages with no plausible link to group-related needs should show ~zero adjusted gaps. Decision Draft  Applicant Notification indeed shows a small spread (0.5 vs 0.8 days), which is reassuring for the late pipeline and helps localize where inequity enters.

### 2.6 Subgroup checks (region × channel)
Estimate adjusted gaps separately within each region and channel. Uniform gaps across regions suggest a systemic property (e.g., SLA absence on support steps); spikes confined to the two known-backlog regions suggest an operationally fixable cause interacting with demographic sorting. Cross channel × group to confirm in-person verification waits are a channel effect rather than a group effect (they appear conditionally similar, which supports the channel story).

### 2.7 Uncertainty discipline
Cluster-robust standard errors (by caseworker and region), bootstrap CIs for medians/tails, explicit small-n warnings for Group C (n = 820), and pre-declared thresholds for "material" disparity. Back-of-envelope sampling error (±~2.5–3 pp at these sizes) cannot explain the largest adjusted gaps by itself, but clustering and measurement noise widen real uncertainty.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **(Legitimate) Documented case-mix routing.** High measured risk triggers Fraud Review; missing documents trigger requests; more prior cases correlate with genuinely complicated histories. This explains most of D's raw pattern and much of the pathway mix overall — subject to audit of what "measured risk" actually measures.
2. **(Legitimate) Statutory steps take time.** Accommodation Review and Language Support Contact add legally required process steps; some added duration is intrinsic to honoring the rights, not per se unfairness.
3. **(Legitimate) Experienced appellants.** Applicants with long benefits-system histories (D: 49% high prior-case count) know how to appeal and re-open; some of D's +5.7 pp adjusted appeal gap may reflect system navigation knowledge, not mistreatment.
4. **(Concerning) Under-capacity support units with no SLA.** Seven bilingual workers and five specialists facing concentrated demand turn rights into multi-day queues. The absence of timeliness floors for support steps means delay is invisible in compliance reporting — a predictable design flaw, whatever the intent.
5. **(Concerning) Structural design flaws.** Dual-hatted specialists, generalistspecialist ping-pong, and 3 chronically overloaded officers handling outsized shares of Group B create queueing, context loss, and person-dependent variance that land unevenly by group.
6. **(Concerning) Biased-measurement feedback loop.** Majority-language-framed completeness standards inflate Request-Docs incidence for B; historically shaped risk scores steer B/D toward fraud/supervisor paths; today's routing becomes tomorrow's case mix, making raw *and* adjusted disparities look "explained."
7. **(Concerning) Communication-trust spiral.** Weaker explanations (possibly untested in translation) raise appeals and re-opens; prior adverse experiences amplify; appeals add months — all disproportionately to groups already receiving less timely service.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in key controls.** `documentation_completeness` conflates applicant behavior with intake-aid quality, form design, and language accessibility; the log records *that* documents were requested, not *which* or *why*. `risk_score_band` is a historical artifact that may encode enforcement patterns correlated with group membership — conditioning on it can quietly subtract real disparity (over-control), while omitting it leaves classic confounding.
- **Coarse, heterogeneous group labels.** Only 57% of B needs language support; only 62% of C requested accommodation. Aggregate gaps blend affected and unaffected members, diluting (or distorting) the signal about the subpopulations the concerns actually cover. Severity of disability, digital access, literacy, caregiving/time constraints, and transport are unrecorded but plausibly drive both routing and outcomes.
- **Unobserved workload dynamics.** Daily-granularity resource data cannot show queue positions, interruptions, simultaneous case difficulty, overtime, or triage priority. Two workers with identical daily tallies may deliver very different throughput; "utilization" inferred from the log would be largely fiction.
- **Appeal quality is invisible.** Counts of Appeal Intake tell nothing about grounds, representation, duration, or — most important — *success rates* by group, which would be the sharpest available probe of initial-decision error. Rework/reopen coding likewise lacks reason codes.
- **Outcome semantics are thin.** `case_outcome` ignores partial awards, sanction levels, and benefit duration; nothing captures comprehension, dignity, or whether notifications were understood. Process mining will faithfully characterize *how* cases move, not how it *felt* to move through them.
- **Temporal and censoring artifacts.** Staff turnover, mid-window policy changes, holidays, still-open long cases (right-censoring), and possibly abandoned applications absent from the log all bias cycle-time and outcome estimates in ways month dummies only partly absorb.
- **Small-cell fragility.** Intersections (region × channel × group × risk band) thin out fast; adjusted estimates there carry wide intervals and should inform hypotheses, not verdicts.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

1. **Expand language-support and accommodation-review capacity.** Hire/train bilingual caseworkers and accommodation staff toward demand-proportional coverage; decouple the two dual-hatted specialists from supervisor duty.
 *Side effects:* cost and hiring lag; risk of diluted expertise if staff are ramped too fast; possible pull from the general pool raising A's waits slightly (an acceptable equity trade, but monitor it); expect latent demand to surface — apparent "new" backlog is unmasked need, not deterioration.
2. **Service-level targets for rights-support steps.** E.g., support/contact step completed  2 business days; support step  Eligibility Assessment  3; accommodation decision  5; published and reported.
 *Side effects:* gaming and clock manipulation (closing steps superficially to stop timers), deflection of edge cases to dodge deadlines, pressure to rubber-stamp. Pair every SLA with quality sampling; define clocks so expedited processing can't be achieved by *skipping* the right, only by servicing it.
3. **Routing redesign to cut handovers.** Single-owner "case captain + advisory consult" model (specialist advises; generalist retains ownership), bilingual aides embedded in general teams in the two hotspot regions, and assisted-intake/doc checklists at submission for high-incompleteness-risk profiles.
 *Side effects:* somewhat thinner per-touch specialization; cross-training cost; consistency risk across advisors. Guardrails: consult rights are preserved, mandatory specialist sign-off for accommodations stays, and never remove or gate the support steps themselves — the target is their speed and integration, not their existence.
4. **Decision-explanation improvements.** Plain-language, translated, accessible decision notices with itemized reasons; translated document checklists at intake; comprehension tested with real users.
 *Side effects:* longer drafting cycles initially, translation QA cost, boilerplate drift. Expected payoff: fewer avoidable appeals/re-opens, better targeting of genuine disputes.
5. **Monitoring of adjusted disparities.** Quarterly dashboard: adjusted gaps with CIs, sliced by region/channel/variant/support-step usage; escalation thresholds agreed in advance; special attention to support-step waits and appeal-success parity.
 *Side effects:* metric fixation and gaming; small-n noise triggering false alarms (use smoothing/min-cell suppression for privacy); danger of shaming frontline staff for structural issues — pair numbers with root-cause follow-up.
6. **Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`.** Validate the band against subsequent verified fraud/error outcomes for predictive validity and differential validity by group/region/language; blind inter-rater study of completeness assessments across languages; publish methods and corrective actions.
 *Side effects:* transitional inconsistency while criteria change; short-term risk-screening errors during recalibration; legal/political exposure if bias is confirmed; and, if confirmed, obligations to review historic cases — expensive but unavoidable if the audit warrants it.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

On the current evidence, the process is **indeterminate on unlawful discrimination, but leans clearly unfavorable on procedural-burden and timeliness equity**. Most of the raw divergence — especially Group D's — is consistent with documented complexity and plausibly legitimate routing, though that exculpation depends on `risk_score_band`, which has not been validated. What survives adjustment is harder to wave off: Groups B and C face longer waits, more scrutiny, and more document churn than comparable Group A cases; the delay is specifically concentrated in queue stops attached to *rights-bearing* support steps that enjoy no timeliness floor; and support-path cases are served by structurally overloaded, tiny teams — a configuration that converts the exercise of legal rights into weeks of additional waiting. The near-zero gap in the final notification stage shows the pipeline is capable of even-handed delivery, locating the problem in capacity and design rather than uniform misconduct.

This is best characterized as **a process that is currently not delivering procedurally equal service, with unresolved questions about outcome fairness**, rather than a demonstrated case of intentional discrimination. Confidence limits: the adjusted estimates depend on covariates that may themselves be biased, appeal-success and workload data are missing, and Group C is small. Appropriate posture: implement the capacity, SLA, routing, and explanation fixes now; complete the risk-score and completeness audits; re-assess after two quarters of monitored data. If residual approval and scrutiny gaps persist in matched analyses — particularly alongside suppressed appeal-success parity — the conclusion should harden from "unfair in procedure" toward "unfair in treatment."
