# 2. Integrated explanation

Service time and error rate do not move together for a single reason; they diverge because different mechanisms dominate at different times and for different workers.

**Early shift (bins 1–5; ~0–60 min): Slowness is mainly selection and inheritance, not incompetence.** The first events carry the previous shift’s backlog: mean complexity is highest (0.72), high-priority share is 41%, and queue length is already 46. Resources—especially seniors—are deliberately selecting or being assigned complex carryover cases. The 18.8-minute mean service time reflects cognitive load from complex eligibility and risk decisions, plus circadian adjustment for 06:00 Early and 22:00 Night starts. The moderate 3.4% error rate suggests workers are being careful despite pressure, not that they are “cold.”

**Mid-shift (bins 6–30; ~60–250 min): Stabilization is partly learning, partly case-mix easing, partly queue normalization.** Service time falls to 13.6–15.2 minutes as queue length drops to 31–39 and complexity declines to 0.50–0.61. Within-resource comparisons confirm that the first ~20 events are slower for all experience levels, consistent with warm-up. However, the error rate reaches its minimum (2.5%) here precisely because queue pressure is lowest and cases are simpler, not solely because workers have “warmed up.”

**Late shift (bins 31–75): Apparent efficiency masks rising risk.** Service time continues falling to 11.4 minutes, but complexity is now lowest (0.46) and high-priority share has dropped to 22%. Some of the speed gain is genuine learning and routine development, but much of it reflects queue normalization followed by renewed pressure—queue length rebounds to 62. The error rate climbs to 4.6% and rework to 8.2%, well before the extreme bin 76+. This is the zone where queue pressure begins to dominate: workers trade accuracy for throughput.

**End of shift (bin 76+; >~400 min): Speed and quality decouple sharply.** Service time hits 10.8 minutes, yet errors spike to 7.9% and rework to 13.7%. Complexity is at its lowest (0.43) and high-priority share is only 19%, so case difficulty cannot explain the deterioration. The queue is at its highest (88). Under high WIP, low-complexity cases take only 9.4 minutes but have 5.8% errors; under the same WIP, high-complexity cases reach 10.9% errors. The mechanism is a speed–quality tradeoff driven by queue pressure: workers accelerate to prevent backlog spillover, use batching (Document Review runs of 9–18 events) to maintain flow, and defer Quality Checks until the final 45 minutes—creating an illusion of speed but accumulating hidden defects. Fatigue and circadian effects interact: Night staff show 15.2-minute service times with elevated errors, and Day/Late shifts both show late-hour degradation. Crucially, seniors resist this tradeoff (4.9% error at high WIP), while juniors collapse (12.4%), proving that the late-shift error spike is not universal fatigue but a conditional interaction between pressure, experience, and possibly batching.

**Across resources:** Seniors accelerate without major quality loss unless WIP exceeds 70; mid-level workers degrade under pressure (6.8%); juniors show both a steep learning curve (19.6 to 13.7 min) and extreme vulnerability to late-shift pressure. This heterogeneity rules out any single “human fatigue” narrative.

**Batching and automation add hidden dynamics.** Late-shift Document Review batching compresses service times artificially. Quality Check clustering at shift end delays error detection, meaning `rework_flag` within 7 days captures errors made hours earlier. Meanwhile, `Bot_Risk` performs Risk Classification instantly, but when the queue exceeds 70, humans must correct it more often—adding invisible workload that does not appear in the bot’s service time but increases the true cognitive load on staff.

In sum: service time falls because of a mixture of warm-up, simpler case mix, batching, and queue-pressure rushing; errors rise late mainly when high WIP meets lower experience, compounded by batching, deferred quality checks, and circadian stress—while seniors demonstrate that the system, not the worker, is the primary driver.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker-level conclusions are system-level confounders.** Service time and error rates are not personal traits measured in isolation. Seniors do not “work faster” in a vacuum; they are assigned complex cases early when queue is high, then accelerate as cases simplify. Juniors do not “fail under pressure” randomly; they are more likely to face high-WIP conditions (`wip_at_start` >70) with lower experience and without senior backup. Comparing raw late-shift error rates across individuals ignores that `queue_length_at_start`, `case_complexity_score`, and `shift_id` are assigned, not chosen.

**Selection bias and measurement error distort interpretation.** Early-shift complexity (0.72) makes direct comparison with late-shift (0.43) misleading. Batching by Junior_H/I in Document Review artificially lowers service time without reflecting true efficiency. Delayed Quality Checks mean `error_flag` and `rework_flag` are detected hours or days after the event occurred, making temporal attribution unreliable. `Bot_Risk` creates hidden errors that are corrected by humans—penalizing the correcting worker for automation failure is ethically wrong.

**Queue pressure is often management-created.** Backlogs inherited from Night or previous shifts (`queue_length_at_start` 61–70), understaffing during Day-shift peaks, and lack of escalation protocols drive `wip_at_start` above 70. When error rates rise in these conditions, the cause is resource scarcity and workflow design, not worker negligence. Interventions should target queue dynamics (`pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`) and staffing, not worker punishment.

**Circadian and biological context matter.** Night staff operate at 22:00–06:00 with different cognitive rhythms and receive a disproportionate share of urgent exceptions (56% high complexity first hour). Comparing Night error rates to Day rates without `shift_id` and `minutes_since_shift_start` controls is scientifically invalid and operationally unfair.

**Punitive use of error metrics is prohibited.** Intervention 8—penalizing higher late-shift error rates—must be rejected. Raw `error_flag` rates do not control for `wip_at_start`, `sequence_position_within_shift`, or `resource_experience_months`. Punishment based on unadjusted metrics drives gaming, suppresses reporting, and diverts attention from fixing queue pressure, batching, and automation quality.
