## 1. Analytical assumptions

A **case notion** is an analytical projection: a set of events ordered for one chosen lead object, here using the events directly related to that object through E2O relations. It is not assumed to be the uniquely real business case. For example, an `Order` case is useful for an order-facing question, while a `Package` case is useful for a physical-shipping question.

I use **convergence** to mean that several objects or object histories meet in one event or downstream object. Examples are P3, which merges items from O2 and O3, D2, which carries P2 and P3, and V2, which bills O2 and O3. I use **divergence** to mean that an object branches into several downstream lifecycles, such as O1 splitting into P1 and P2, or V2 being settled through M2 and M3.

Copied shared events are both:

- **Evidence** of a real synchronization or shared business action. For example, e16 is genuinely relevant to O1, O2, and O3.
- **Duplication** when a flattened DFG treats the three copied occurrences of e16 as three independent dispatch executions rather than one execution affecting three orders.

The baseline analysis uses only the supplied direct E2O relations. I would permit explicit, separately labelled propagation variants through qualified O2O paths, such as `Order  Item  Package  Delivery` or `Order  Invoice  Payment`. However, propagated relations must not be treated as observed facts. In particular, propagating e01 from O1 to I11 and I12 would make items appear related to `Create Order`, despite no direct E2O relation and no time-qualified object relation proving that the items existed at 09:00.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order flattening — cross-order package P3.**  
   e13 is one `Pack Package` event for P3, packing I21 from O2 and I31 from O3. It appears once in the O2 trace and once in the O3 trace. Thus an order DFG counts two order-level appearances of `Pack Package`, although warehouse work executed one packing event. This is harmful if interpreted as physical workload, but informative if the question is “how many orders were affected by a shared pack operation.”

2. **Order and package flattening — cross-order package P4.**  
   e14 is copied into O3 and O4 because P4 contains I32 and I41. In O3, e13 followed by e14 creates a `Pack Package  Pack Package` edge; in O4, e10 followed by e14 creates `Add Item  Pack Package`. A naïve analyst may label the repeated packing edge rework, although it reflects O3 being split over P3 and P4. Conversely, the P4 package trace e14/e17/e27 hides that P4 served two orders. This is context-dependent: it is a meaningful split-f fulfilment pattern, not evidence of poor rework.

3. **Order flattening — apparent repeated dispatch and confirmation for O1.**  
   O1 has e15 for P1 and e16 for P2/D2, followed by e23 and e24 confirmations. This yields `Dispatch  Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery  Confirm Delivery` in the O1 trace. Those edges can be mistaken for a retry or duplicate confirmation. They actually reveal divergence: O1 was split across P1 and P2. The edges are informative only when interpreted as multi-package fulfilment rather than activity repetition.

4. **Package versus Delivery flattening — consolidated delivery D2.**  
   D2 is dispatched in e16 and confirmed in e24. In the Delivery log, D2 yields one `Dispatch  Confirm Delivery` transition lasting 4 hours 55 minutes. In the Package log, that same physical movement is represented in both P2 and P3 cases, so the transition can have frequency two. Neither result is intrinsically wrong: one measures delivery movements, the other package exposures. It becomes harmful when package-level frequency is reported as carrier workload without deduplication.

5. **Invoice and Order flattening — consolidated invoice V2.**  
   V2 bills O2 and O3. e19, e22, and e25 are directly related to both orders, so each is copied into both order traces. An order DFG may therefore count `Issue Invoice  Record Payment` twice, although there is one invoice issuance e19 and one first payment posting e22. This is useful for order-level exposure to billing, but misleading for invoice-volume or accounting-workload counts.

6. **Invoice versus Payment flattening — partial payment of V2.**  
   The O2O qualifiers state that M2 `partially_settles` V2 and M3 `completes_settlement` V2. Invoice flattening creates `Issue Invoice  Record Payment  Record Payment` for V2 via e19/e22/e25. A DFG may incorrectly diagnose repeated payment posting or rework. Payment flattening makes the opposite mistake: M2 and M3 are separate singleton cases, so the partial-to-complete sequence disappears entirely. The repeated payment edge is informative only when payment-object qualifiers are retained.

7. **Payment flattening — near-singleton lifecycle.**  
   M1, M2, M3, and M4 each have exactly one directly related `Record Payment` event. The resulting payment DFG has no within-case edge, and any start-to-end duration is zero or undefined. This is harmful for lifecycle discovery but not for payment audit questions such as “which payment objects were recorded?”

8. **Item flattening — artificial physical completion boundary.**  
   I11 has e05/e11/e15/e23: `Add Item  Pack Package  Dispatch  Confirm Delivery`. It does not include O1’s e18 invoice, e21 payment, or e28 closure. Its observed physical handling time is therefore not O1’s end-to-end completion time. This is harmful if an item-confirmation timestamp is treated as revenue realization or order closure; it is useful for line-item fulfilment SLA analysis.

9. **Delivery flattening — valid transit time mistaken for fulfilment time.**  
   D3 runs from e17 at 11:15 on 2026-02-03 to e27 at 10:00 on 2026-02-04, about 22 hours 45 minutes. That is a meaningful dispatch-to-confirmation duration. It excludes O4’s creation e04, packing e14, invoice e20, payment e26, and closure e31, so it should not be compared directly with the elapsed duration of an order or invoice case.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would create several flattened logs, but label each with its unit of analysis and avoid combining their frequencies.

- **Order log, direct E2O only:** safe for order-facing questions such as time from `Create Order` to `Close Order`, incidence of split fulfilment, and the proportion of orders exposed to delayed confirmation. Shared events such as e16 must be reported as “order exposures,” not independent dispatch operations.
- **Item log:** safe for `Add Item` to `Confirm Delivery` physical line-item lead time and for detecting item-level wait between packing and dispatch.
- **Package log:** safe for pack-to-confirm lifecycle and warehouse/package exceptions. It is especially useful for comparing P1–P4 while retaining P3/P4’s cross-order status externally.
- **Delivery log:** safe for carrier transit time between dispatch and delivery confirmation.
- **Invoice log:** safe for invoice issuance-to-settlement analysis and identifying instalment settlement such as V2.
- **Payment log:** retain it mainly as an audit/transaction table. A DFG or skeleton over singleton payment cases should not be presented as a payment process model.

Questions about synchronization require object-centric views:

- An **OC-DFG** should be used for activities such as e13, e16, e19, and e22 where event, unique-object, and total-object frequencies differ materially.
- An **OTG** should expose the topology `Order  Item  Package  Delivery` and `Order  Invoice  Payment`, including the two-to-many/many-to-one structures around P3, P4, D2, and V2.
- An **ET–OT graph** should show that `Create Order` and `Close Order` concern Orders only, while packing/dispatch/confirmation involve several types.
- An **object-interaction summary** should identify the high-interaction objects D2 and V2 and distinguish them from isolated payment objects.
- **Equivalent-execution clustering** is useful only for an explicitly declared exploration of recurring network motifs, for example split O1-style fulfilment versus shared-package/shared-invoice configurations. It should not be used to silently declare those clusters as the true cases.

Contradictory results should be reconciled by preserving event IDs and stating the denominator. “D2 has one delivery” and “D2 affected two packages and three orders” are both true, but answer different questions. Raw flattened frequency, unique-event frequency, and object-exposure frequency should be shown side by side rather than averaged into one metric.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**C. Reject a single lead object for this process.**

**Decision criteria**

1. The lead should preserve the relevant business boundary without serializing meaningful branches.
2. It should not turn shared operations into independent execution counts.
3. It should support both physical fulfilment and financial settlement questions, or make its exclusions explicit.
4. Its lifecycle must have enough event diversity to support meaningful DFG and performance analysis.

**Strongest supporting evidence**

- O1 diverges into P1 and P2; O3 spans P3 and P4.
- P3 merges O2/O3, P4 merges O3/O4, and D2 consolidates P2/P3 and three orders.
- V2 consolidates O2/O3 and then diverges into M2/M3 settlement objects.
- No object type preserves all of these network structures without either copying them (`Order`, `Item`, `Package`) or collapsing them (`Delivery`, `Invoice`, `Payment`).

**Strongest counterargument**

Order is a defensible broad operational lead. Every event is directly related to at least one order, and Order traces include creation, fulfilment, invoice, payment, and closure. For customer-service reporting, O1–O4 are likely the most understandable traditional cases.

**Residual risk**

Rejecting a dominant lead makes standard process-mining dashboards less simple. Stakeholders may still need one order-based KPI view, and the absence of a single case can be mistaken for avoidance of accountability.

**Conditions that would change the decision**

I would switch to an Order-primary approach if the stated objective were explicitly contractual customer-order management; shared warehouse and finance events were intentionally allocated to every affected order; and package/delivery/invoice analyses were maintained as separate, non-comparable secondary views. A one-to-one package/order and invoice/order policy would also materially strengthen the case for Orders as a primary lead.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

# Baseline: use the observed OCEL and its direct E2O relations.
# No O2O propagation is silently applied in this script.
print(ocel)

# Standard OCEL 2.0 column names used by PM4Py OCEL dataframes.
events_df = ocel.events.copy()
objects_df = ocel.objects.copy()
relations_df = ocel.relations.copy()

activity_frequencies = events_df["ocel:activity"].value_counts(dropna=False)
object_type_frequencies = objects_df["ocel:type"].value_counts(dropna=False)
relation_object_type_frequencies = relations_df["ocel:type"].value_counts(dropna=False)

print("\nActivity frequencies:")
print(activity_frequencies)

print("\nObject-type frequencies:")
print(object_type_frequencies)

print("\nE2O relation counts by object type:")
print(relation_object_type_frequencies)

object_types = pm4py.ocel_get_object_types(ocel)
print("\nObject types reported by PM4Py:", object_types)

# Traditional projections. Each event log is a direct-E2O flattening for one type.
flattened_logs_by_type = {}
dfgs_by_type = {}
log_skeletons_by_type = {}
case_counts_by_type = {}

for object_type in object_types:
    flattened_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, object_type)

    flattened_logs_by_type[object_type] = flattened_log
    case_counts_by_type[object_type] = len(flattened_log)

    # These results are descriptive projections, not proof that the object type
    # is the semantically correct process case.
    dfgs_by_type[object_type] = pm4py.discover_dfg(flattened_log)
    log_skeletons_by_type[object_type] = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(flattened_log)

print("\nFlattened case counts:")
print(case_counts_by_type)

# Object-centric results retain multi-object participation.
ocdfg_result = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
otg_result = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot_result = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)

# Per-object interaction summaries are useful for inspecting D2, V2,
# P3, P4, and other high-interaction objects without flattening them away.
object_ids = objects_df["ocel:oid"].dropna().astype(str).tolist()
object_interaction_summaries = {
    object_id: pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel, object_id)
    for object_id in object_ids
}

focus_interaction_summaries = {
    object_id: object_interaction_summaries[object_id]
    for object_id in ["O1", "O2", "O3", "O4", "P3", "P4", "D2", "V2", "M2", "M3"]
    if object_id in object_interaction_summaries
}

# Decision C rejects a single lead object, so no pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel()
# call is made here. If clustering is later used, its anchoring object type and
# equivalence semantics must be declared explicitly rather than treated as cases.
#
# Interpretation should compare event IDs, relation multiplicities, and chosen
# frequency denominators; visual inspection of DFGs alone cannot resolve them.
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Remove or down-weight copied shared events.**  
   For each flattened type, compare raw DFG frequency with a weighted version where an event contributes \(1 / d_t(e)\), with \(d_t(e)\) the number of directly related objects of that lead type. For Order flattening, e16 and e24 would each receive weight one-third; for Package flattening they would each receive weight one-half. Also compare a unique-event view that counts e16 once. A large difference indicates synchronization-driven frequency rather than repeated execution.

2. **Compare direct E2O with explicitly propagated variants.**  
   Start with the direct baseline. Then construct bounded, qualifier-aware propagation alternatives, such as associating invoice/payment events with an Order through `bills` and `settles`, or delivery events with items through `carries` and `packs`. Report newly added relations separately. In particular, test whether propagating e01 from O1 to I11/I12 creates implausible pre-item lifecycle events; if it does, reject that propagation rule or require temporal validity.

3. **Change the lead object and compare only like-for-like measures.**  
   Recompute DFGs, skeletons, start/end activities, and durations for Order, Item, Package, Delivery, and Invoice. Compare O1’s creation-to-close time with other order times, but do not compare it directly with D2’s dispatch-to-confirm duration or V2’s invoice-to-settlement duration. This checks whether a conclusion is robust or merely a projection artifact.

4. **Compare OC-DFG frequency metrics.**  
   For e16 and e24, contrast: one unique event; three directly related Orders; two directly related Packages; three directly related Items; and nine total related objects per event. Run OC-DFG analysis using event frequency, unique-object frequency, and total-object frequency. Interpret a high total-object count as interaction complexity, not automatically high operational volume.

5. **Validate business semantics against the object graph.**  
   Inspect whether repeated activities represent retries, instalments, or split fulfilment before assigning conformance or delay labels. Specifically validate `Dispatch  Dispatch` for O1/O3 against P1–P4, and validate V2’s repeated `Record Payment` sequence against the `partially_settles` and `completes_settlement` qualifiers for M2 and M3.
