## 1. Analytical assumptions

I use “case notion” to mean the object type whose instances define traces. Flattening on Order therefore creates one trace for each O1--O4; flattening on Payment creates one trace for each M1--M4. An event is copied into every case of the selected type to which it is directly related. A projection is consequently an analytical view of the OCEL, not a replacement for it.

“Convergence” means multiple object lifecycles meet in one event or trace: e13 joins O2 and O3 through P3, e16 joins O1/O2/O3 through D2, and e19 joins O2/O3 through V2. “Divergence” means one business execution is distributed across multiple object traces or stages: O1 spans P1/P2, and V2’s settlement spans M2/M3.

Copied events are both evidence and duplication. They evidence one real shared operation, but copies are not independent executions. I would retain direct E2O relations, annotate each event’s multiplicity, and report raw counts separately from normalized counts. I permit a weighted projection in which a shared event contributes (1/k) to each of its (k) cases. I would also permit an explicitly labelled O2O-propagated variant, but never silently treat propagated reachability as an observed E2O relation.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **P3 as a cross-order package (Order and Item).** e13 is one Pack Package operation for O2/O3, I21/I31, and P3. It is copied into two order and two item cases. A DFG therefore counts two order-level pack records for one operation. That is harmful for workload or “packages per order” metrics, but informative for detecting co-packing.

2. **P4 as a second cross-order package (Order and Item).** e14 and e17 join O3/O4 and I32/I41. O3 can show a second Pack and Dispatch, which may be labelled rework or a retry by a one-package model. The repetition is genuine split/shared fulfillment, so it is informative operationally and harmful only when interpreted as O3-only work.

3. **O1’s split shipment.** O1 has P1 at e11/e15/e23 and P2 at e12/e16/e24. Its Order trace contains two Pack, Dispatch, and Confirm activities, producing edges such as Pack->Pack or Dispatch->Dispatch. That is not duplicate logging: it is evidence of a split. It is useful for shipment analysis, but a single order completion time must state whether e23 or e24 is the completion boundary.

4. **Consolidated delivery D2.** e16 and e24 concern D2, P2/P3, I12/I21/I31, and O1/O2/O3. Order flattening copies each event into three cases, so one dispatch and one confirmation become three order records. The shared 11:05 and 16:00 timestamps can make three order durations look independently measured. This is harmful for per-order carrier attribution, informative for identifying consolidation.

5. **Consolidated invoice V2.** e19, e22, and e25 directly relate V2 to O2 and O3. In an Order DFG, Issue Invoice and both payments occur in both cases, inflating invoice/payment frequencies and potentially violating a one-invoice-per-order rule. The same pattern is useful evidence of joint billing, not evidence that two invoices were issued.

6. **Partial payment.** The Invoice trace for V2 is Issue Invoice at e19, Record Payment at e22, then Record Payment at e25. The edge Record Payment->Record Payment is an installment pattern, not necessarily a rework loop. Payment flattening instead creates two singleton cases M2 and M3, losing the sequence. The invoice view is informative for settlement; the payment view is harmful for lifecycle discovery.

7. **Near-singleton Payment lifecycle.** M1, M2, M3, and M4 occur only in e21, e22, e25, and e26. A Payment log has four one-event traces, so cycle time is zero/undefined and log-skeleton constraints have little content. It remains useful for counting records and matching M2/M3 to V2, but not for process performance.

8. **Near-singleton Delivery lifecycle.** D1--D3 each occur only in Dispatch and Confirm Delivery. The resulting two-event traces validly measure a delivery leg, but omit e11--e14 packing and every invoice/payment event. Calling this order lead time truncates the lifecycle and biases performance downward; calling it carrier transit time is appropriate.

9. **Shared-item frequency inflation.** e16/e24 are copied to I12, I21, and I31, and e13 to I21/I31. “Dispatches per item” can legitimately count three item involvements, while “dispatch operations” must count one unique event. Confusing these units creates frequency inflation without any false underlying E2O relation.

10. **O3 as a coordination hub.** O3 participates in P3 with O2, P4 with O4, D2 with O1/O2, D3 with O4, and V2 with O2. Its order trace is consequently long and complex. That is informative for finding cross-object coordination, but harmful for comparing O3’s private work or attributing all waiting time to O3.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would create all six projections with declared scopes and shared-event multiplicity. Order safely supports order aging, direct creation-to-close, and milestone presence. Item supports item handling completeness. Package supports parcel throughput and pack-to-confirm duration. Delivery supports carrier-leg duration. Invoice supports issue-to-first-payment and issue-to-final-payment, including V2 installments. Payment supports reconciliation of payment records, not a lifecycle model.

The OC-DFG should be the primary end-to-end view because it retains activities with object types and shared participation. The OTG should expose P3/P4, D2, V2, and M2/M3 relationships. The ET--OT graph should validate direct E2O incidence and distinguish it from any propagated association. An object-interaction summary should quantify O2--O3 through P3/D2/V2 and O3--O4 through P4/D3. Equivalent-execution clustering is optional here and not needed to justify the lead-object decision; it should not erase the object identities.

When a flat DFG disagrees with the OC-DFG, I would align the unit first. e16 is one unique Dispatch event, three direct Order relations, and one D2 delivery event. Those are different answers, not necessarily contradictory results. I would report event counts, unique-object counts, and total event-object relations separately, trace discrepancies through ET--OT and OTG, and only then apply a business allocation rule.

## 5. Lead-object decision

I choose **C. Reject a single lead object for this process.**

My criteria are lifecycle coverage, preservation of multiplicity, meaningful performance boundaries, interpretable frequency units, and usefulness for the stated question. Order wins lifecycle coverage because O1--O4 have creation and closure, plus direct physical and financial milestones. It loses structural fidelity because O1 spans P1/P2, O3 spans P3/P4, D2 joins three orders, and V2 joins two. Delivery and Payment have only two-event or singleton lifecycles; Package and Invoice each omit major parts of the process; Item has no direct financial milestones.

The strongest evidence for rejection is that no type preserves both end-to-end milestones and the shared structure. The strongest counterargument is operational practicality: Order is the customer-facing unit and is the best navigation dimension. I therefore recommend an Order projection as the primary report, but with Item, Package, Delivery, Invoice, and Payment as explicitly secondary lifecycles. The residual risk is dashboard complexity and inconsistent counting, mitigated by publishing raw, normalized, and unique-event metrics together.

I would change to B (primary Order plus secondary lifecycles) if future data became mostly one-to-one and shared-event rates were low and stable. I would choose A only for a narrowly order-centric question with an agreed allocation rule. Persistent split shipments, consolidated deliveries/invoices, or installment payments would preserve decision C.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pm4py

print(ocel)

event_id_col = getattr(ocel, "event_id_column", "ocel:eid")
activity_col = getattr(ocel, "event_activity", "ocel:activity")
object_type_col = getattr(ocel, "object_type_column", "ocel:type")

activity_counts = ocel.events[activity_col].value_counts()
object_type_counts = ocel.objects[object_type_col].value_counts()
relation_event_counts = (
    ocel.relations.groupby(object_type_col)[event_id_col]
    .nunique().sort_values(ascending=False)
)
print("activity counts:\n", activity_counts)
print("object-type counts:\n", object_type_counts)
print("direct related-event counts:\n", relation_event_counts)

object_types = ["Order", "Item", "Package", "Delivery", "Invoice", "Payment"]
flattened_logs = {}
flattened_dfgs = {}
flattened_skeletons = {}

for object_type in object_types:
    flat_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, object_type)
    flattened_logs[object_type] = flat_log
    flattened_dfgs[object_type] = pm4py.discover_dfg(flat_log)
    flattened_skeletons[object_type] = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(flat_log)
    print(object_type, "DFG:", flattened_dfgs[object_type])

ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
object_interactions = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
print("OC-DFG:", ocdfg)
print("OTG:", otg)
print("ET-OT graph:", etot)
print("object interactions:\n", object_interactions)

# These outputs are structural evidence, not semantic judgments. Inspect
# direct incidence and event multiplicity before interpreting repeated DFG edges.
```

The results are stored separately so that a projected DFG is not mistaken for the OC-DFG. Visual or numeric output alone cannot decide whether a repeated activity is rework, a split shipment, an installment, or a copied shared event.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Copied events:** compare raw flattenings with a (1/k)-weighted version, where (k) is the number of directly related objects of the chosen type. Track e13, e16, e19, e22, e24, and e25 in DFG frequencies and lead-time summaries. Keep the original shared events in the OCEL.

2. **Direct versus propagated relations:** run a direct-E2O analysis and a separately labelled O2O-propagated analysis. Check whether edges such as Pack Package->Record Payment appear only after propagation; never count those inferred edges as observed events.

3. **Lead-object sensitivity:** repeat lifecycle coverage, shared-event rate, stage count, and performance comparisons with Order, Item, Package, Invoice, Delivery, and Payment as candidate leads. Delivery and Payment serve as negative controls for truncation and singleton behavior.

4. **Frequency-unit sensitivity:** in the OC-DFG report raw event occurrences, unique event IDs, unique focal objects, and total event-object relations. e16 is one event, one D2 involvement, and three Order relations; those counts must not be conflated.

5. **Performance boundaries:** separately measure pack-to-dispatch, dispatch-to-confirm, issue-to-first-payment, issue-to-final-payment, and create-to-close. Treat O1’s e23/e24 and V2’s e22/e25 as distinct physical and financial completion milestones.

6. **Identifier reconciliation:** verify the supplied facts directly: P3 links O2/O3 and I21/I31; P4 links O3/O4 and I32/I41; D2 links O1/O2/O3 and P2/P3; V2 links O2/O3; M2/M3 settle V2; and e28--e31 are not propagated to other types. This prevents analytical joins from becoming invented OCEL facts.
