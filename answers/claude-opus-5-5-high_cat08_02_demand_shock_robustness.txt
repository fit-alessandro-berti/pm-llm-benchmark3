# Robust Redesign of the Diagnostic-Lab Sample Process

## 1. Stress scenarios

### 1.0 What the logs say before designing scenarios

The two periods have different lengths, so the counts below are converted to cases per week. That makes the shift in demand visible.

| Indicator (per week) | Normal (8 wk) | Peak (3 wk) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| All orders | 2,300 | 3,300 | x1.43 |
| Routine orders | ~1,400 | ~1,390 | **x1.0 (flat)** |
| Urgent orders | ~414 | ~957 | **x2.3** |
| Complex-panel orders | ~299 | ~660 | **x2.2** |
| Repeat-after-failure orders | ~184 | ~297 | x1.6 |
| Recollection requests (direct + via correction) | ~104 (4.6% of received) | ~297 (9.1%) | x2.9 |
| Manual accession corrections | ~63 | ~140 | x2.2 (and correction→recollection rises from 14% to 26%) |
| Batch wait entries | ~95 | ~393 | x4.1 |
| Assay run executions | ~2,280 | ~3,420 | x1.5 |
| Repeat assays | ~143 (6.2% of runs) | ~420 (12.3% of runs) | x2.9 |
| Inflow to Medical validation | ~530 | ~1,370 | x2.6 |
| Hold for specialist review | ~36 | ~203 | **x5.6** |
| Cancellations | ~71 | ~180 | x2.5 (recollection→cancel rises from 35% to 42%) |
| Share of releases that pass Medical validation | 26% | 50% | x1.9 |

Main conclusions:

1. **The peak is not a volume shock. It is a mix shock.** Routine demand stays flat. The whole increase comes from Urgent, Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure orders. Yet Routine median cycle time still rises from 38h to 62h and Routine p90 from 68h to 120h. So Routine gets worse only because it competes for shared resources and loses on priority. It does not get worse because there are more Routine orders.
2. **Loads grow unevenly.** Assay runs grow by 1.5x, medical validation by 2.6x and specialist review by 5.6x. A station can be fine on average and still collapse when the mix shifts.
3. **Rework feeds itself.** Repeat assays rise from 6.2% to 12.3% of runs and recollection from 4.6% to 9.1%. Repeat assays now consume about 10.5% of all assay runs (1,080/10,250), roughly double the normal share. Repeat-after-failure orders are partly generated inside the process: today's failures become new orders a few days later.
4. **The loops are the tail drivers.** In peak, the median recollection loop is about 67h (7h to request plus 60h until the new sample arrives). The repeat-assay loop is about 34h (8h plus 26h). The batch-wait path is about 36h (14h plus 22h). The specialist path is about 60h (12h plus 48h). Each of these loops alone exceeds the 18h Urgent contract. The recollection loop alone uses almost the whole 72h Routine target.
5. **Urgent performance was already fragile in the normal period.** Normal Urgent p90 is 19h, so more than 10% of Urgent orders already miss the 18h contract. In peak (median 17h, p90 33h), probably 40–50% miss it. With 2.3 times as many Urgent orders, penalty exposure grows roughly eight- to ninefold. It does not grow in proportion to volume.
6. **Queueing is non-linear near saturation.** Peak utilization is 101% for technical validation, 98% for assays, 96% for pre-analytical check and 93% for medical validation. Queue length scales roughly with ρ/(1−ρ): about 13 at 93%, 49 at 98%, and unbounded at 101%. Taking even 5 percentage points of load off a station at 98% cuts its queue pressure by a factor of 3 to 4. Adding the same load to a station at 93% pushes it into the unstable range.
7. **Data caveats that affect the conclusions:**
   - The flattened DFG does not balance at Assay run. In peak, 10,250 transitions enter and 8,500 leave. In normal, 18,260 enter and 17,030 leave.
   - Technical validation has more outgoing than incoming transitions in peak (8,080 vs 7,240). This is typical of multi-tube or multi-assay orders flattened into one lab order.
   - About 13% of peak orders (1,320) have neither a release nor a cancellation. They are still open at the end of the window, so they are right-censored. The worst peak cases are therefore missing from the cycle-time statistics, and **peak p90 values are probably understated**.
   - Reported utilization does not match the growth in transitions. Medical-validation inflow grows 2.6x while utilization rises only from 58% to 93%. Specialist holds grow 5.6x while utilization rises from 43% to 87%. This means capacity was probably already stretched informally, through overtime or extra sessions. The peak figures would then hide how little buffer is left.
   - These issues should be checked with an object-centric view (order, tube, assay) and a survival-style cycle-time analysis before the triggers below are finalized.

### 1.1 Scenario definitions

| ID | Scenario | Case-mix change | Disruption | Duration / shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **S1** | Winter infection wave (mix shift) | Urgent 29–35%, Complex 20–24%, Routine absolute volume flat, Repeat-after-failure 9–10% | Total demand +40–60%. Recollection about 9%. Medical-validation demand x2.5–3. Specialist-review demand x4–6 | 3–5 weeks, ramping up over about 1 week |
| **S2** | Assay-machine degradation or outage | Normal or winter mix. Repeat-after-failure surge 2–5 days later | (a) M1 unplanned outage for 24–72h, or (b) M2 failure rate doubles for 1–2 weeks (reagent lot, calibration, maintenance backlog) | Sudden start, followed by a recovery surge |
| **S3** | Medical-validator / specialist absence | Hits Urgent and Complex-panel orders. Routine that needs no medical validation is barely affected directly | 25–35% of medical-validation capacity lost for 1–2 weeks (winter illness), overlapping the recurring every-second-Friday specialist absence | Short or medium, and correlated with S1 |
| **S4** | Courier compression or delay | Stresses Urgent orders, because their 18h clock runs before the sample arrives, and stability-sensitive samples | The 10:00 wave slips to about 13:00 and the 17:00 wave to about 19:00, or a route is cancelled and doubles the next arrival. Transport-temperature problems raise pre-analytical failures | 1–5 days, weather- or traffic-driven |
| **S5** | Compound winter storm | S1 mix | S1 plus 10–15% staff sickness across pools, M2 degradation and 1–2 courier-delay days. This is the realistic worst case because these stresses are correlated | 1–2 weeks within a wave |
| **S6** | Post-disruption recovery surge | Backlog weighted toward older and aged samples | After S2, S3 or S4, the backlog is released at once into downstream stations | 1–3 days after any disruption |

S1 to S4 cover the required dimensions. S5 exists because the stresses tend to happen together, not one at a time. S6 exists because many of the worst tails are created when a disruption *ends* and the delayed work arrives downstream all at once.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

General pattern. In peak, the stations rank as follows by median queue:

- specialist review 36h
- medical validation 22h
- assay 18h
- technical validation 16h
- accession correction 14h
- preparation 11h
- pre-analytical check 9.5h
- courier intake 7.5h
- registration 2.8h

By utilization, technical validation (101%) and assays (98%) are the throughput constraints. Pre-analytical check (96%) currently **meters** how fast work reaches them. Medical validation and specialist review are the class-specific constraints for Urgent and Complex-panel orders. Relieving an upstream station while the downstream constraint stays saturated moves the waiting work into more expensive places. There, samples age, stability windows expire and rework grows.

### S1 – Winter mix wave

Likely order of bottlenecks:

1. **Courier intake and pre-analytical check.** Arrivals bunch at 10:00 and 17:00 (courier queue 7.5h). Pre-analytical check runs at 96%, and Receive→Pre-analytical rises from 2h to 9h. Its failure rate doubles (recollection 9.1%), which starts the 67h recollection loop. Clerks then spend time explaining errors to physicians, which lowers intake capacity further.
2. **Accession correction.** Two specialists, 91% utilization, 14h queue. Correction→Test preparation rises from 5h to 16h, and 26% of corrections now end in recollection.
3. **Preparation and assay.** Urgent orders make up 29% of cases, but only 58% bypass batch wait (74% in normal). An Urgent order in the batch path waits about 36h, twice its contract. The Urgent bypasses that do happen fragment machine schedules at 98% utilization. Repeat assays double and the repeat loop takes 26h.
4. **Technical validation (101%).** This is the real throughput constraint: arrivals exceed capacity, so the queue keeps growing during the peak. Assay→Technical validation rises from 5h to 15h.
5. **Medical validation (93%, 22h queue; Technical→Medical 24h).** 42% of technical-validation output now goes to medical validation, compared with 25% in normal.
6. **Specialist review (36h queue, 48h Hold→Medical).** This gates Complex-panel orders, which are 2.4 times more likely to be held.

Migration dynamics:

- **Relieving pre-analytical check or registration** (I6, I8, more intake staff) releases more work into preparation and assay, which are already at 89% and 98%. Waiting moves from the reception bench into the lab, where samples age and repeats rise.
- **Relieving assay** (bigger batches, faster M2 routing) sends more results to technical validation at 101%, so the technical-validation queue grows without limit.
- **Relieving technical validation** sends 42% of its output to medical validation at 93%. Medical validation becomes the Urgent bottleneck and specialist review becomes the Complex-panel bottleneck.
- **Relieving medical validation alone** (I5) helps only if technical validation can feed it. Otherwise the evening shift sits partly idle while Urgent orders wait upstream in technical validation.
- **Strict Urgent-first dispatch** (I1) moves Urgent orders forward at preparation and assay. But the rest of their 18h budget is spent in the technical- and medical-validation queues, where I1 does not apply. Meanwhile Routine and Complex-panel orders build up as WIP. That raises sample age, repeats and, later, Repeat-after-failure orders.

The constraint therefore has to be relieved as a **chain**, starting where throughput is lost to rework (repeats and recollection), then technical validation, then medical validation and specialist review, with WIP control in front. Relieving one station at a time will just move the queue.

### S2 – Assay-machine degradation or outage

**S2a – M1 down for 24–72h.**

1. **During the outage:** assay capacity roughly halves. M2 takes all work at its higher failure rate. Repeat assays rise above the peak level of about 12%, and each repeat uses capacity twice and adds a 26h loop.
2. **Upstream effect:** preparation benches fill up and Batch wait→Assay (22h in peak) grows further. Samples approach their stability limits, which leads to recollection requests, extra clerk load and more cancellations (recollection→cancel is 42% in peak).
3. **Downstream effect:** technical and medical validation are *starved* during the outage. That idle validator time is wasted unless staff are redeployed.
4. **Recovery (S6):** when M1 returns, both machines drain the backlog. Technical validation, which normally runs at 76% and in peak above 100%, receives a burst it cannot absorb. The bottleneck moves to technical validation and then medical validation 12–36h after restoration.

I3 (M1 dedicated to Urgent and Complex-panel orders) is **catastrophic in this scenario**. The dedicated machine is the one that failed, and M2 with its higher failure rate becomes the only path for the orders that can least afford a repeat.

**S2b – M2 failure rate doubles.** Repeat-after-failure orders are already clustered after M2 use and after night shifts. With a doubled failure rate, repeat load could take 15–20% of assay capacity, so assays become the binding constraint. Later, repeat samples that run out of volume create recollection loops and new Repeat-after-failure orders 2–5 days later. The resulting demand looks like "more Repeat-after-failure demand", but the process created it. Under I3, Routine orders are sent to M2, so Routine repeat rates rise most, and Routine pays for a decision made to protect Urgent orders.

### S3 – Medical-validator / specialist absence

1. **Medical validation.** Losing 25–35% of capacity at 93% utilization means demand exceeds capacity. The queue (22h in peak) grows by the day. Urgent orders miss the contract *even if every upstream step is fast*, because Technical→Medical already takes 24h and Medical→Release 5h.
2. **Specialist review.** With a specialist absent every second Friday, holds from Thursday afternoon onward cannot be cleared until Monday. The 48h median Hold→Medical edge is largely this calendar effect. In a winter wave (x5.6 holds), a single Friday absence can add 72h or more to every Complex-panel order held that day.
3. **Secondary effect.** Technical validators who are unsure may escalate more cases to review ("defensive escalation"; Technical→Hold rises from 1.8% to 7.5% of technical-validation output in peak). That adds load to the resource that is absent.

Migration dynamics:

- Upstream accelerators such as I1, I8 or I2 add WIP in front of medical validation. They do not help, and they make samples older.
- I10 (outsourcing) relieves specialist review. But partner results still need local medical validation and release, and 12h of transport is added. When medical validation itself is the scarce resource, I10 moves work from one short resource to another.
- Routine orders that need no medical validation should be *unaffected*. Protecting that path is a cheap way to hold overall performance steady.

### S4 – Courier compression or delay

1. **Before the lab sees the sample.** Register→Receive is already 8h in peak. For Urgent orders, if the 18h clock starts at registration, half the budget is gone before receipt.
2. **Courier intake (88%) and pre-analytical check (96%)** receive a compressed wave, for example 60% of the day's samples within 3 hours. The pre-analytical queue can rise from 9.5h to 15h or more. Transport delays and temperature problems raise the defect rate, so recollection and accession correction grow. The recollection loop (60h to receive the new sample) is most damaging when couriers are unreliable, because the replacement sample travels by the same broken route.
3. **Downstream work is uneven over the day.** Preparation, assay and technical validation are starved in the morning and flooded in the evening and night, when staffing is thinner. Repeat-after-failure orders are clustered after night shifts, so moving work to the night increases repeats.
4. **Next morning:** the overnight backlog hits technical and medical validation together with the next day's arrivals, the same recovery surge as in S6.

Migration dynamics:

- I7 (smoothing pickup slots) helps pre-analytical check. But if slots move later into the afternoon or evening without matching lab staffing, the queue simply moves to the night shift.
- I8 does *not* relieve this scenario. Registration happens before the sample arrives, so faster registration does not change when physical samples show up.

### S5 – Compound winter storm

1. **Mix and machine.** Repeat assays rise because of both mix and M2 degradation, so assays take over as the constraint from technical validation.
2. **Validation.** Absences push medical validation and specialist review over capacity.
3. **Courier.** Delay days create arrival surges at pre-analytical check.

At this point no single-station fix works. **Not every case type can be protected at once**, so the system needs an explicit, pre-agreed order of protection:

1. Urgent orders within the contract (penalties and clinical risk).
2. Repeat-after-failure orders close to their sample-stability limit. Losing the sample means another roughly 60h recollection loop.
3. Complex-panel orders, partially outsourced.
4. Routine orders, under an aging rule that caps the maximum delay but accepts a temporarily degraded target, for example 96h, communicated to clients.

### S6 – Post-disruption recovery surge

Whichever station was blocked, its backlog then floods the next station: technical validation after an assay outage, medical validation after a technical-validation backlog, pre-analytical check after a courier delay. The main mistake is releasing the backlog in first-in-first-out order and all at once. The oldest Routine samples then block Urgent orders that arrive during the drain, and batch lumps bunch up at technical validation. Recovery needs its own policy (see Section 4, P6).

## 3. Robust redesign package

These interventions should hold up across most scenarios. Each one names the downstream station it may overload.

### R1. Slack-based dispatch with a reserved Urgent lane and Routine aging (replaces I1)

- **Design:**
  - Every order carries a due time: Urgent is registration + 18h; Routine is +72h; Complex-panel gets an internal target, for example 96h; Repeat-after-failure gets a target tied to the original order and to sample stability.
  - At preparation, assay, technical validation and medical validation, work is pulled in order of *remaining slack minus expected remaining processing and queue time*.
  - Urgent orders get **reserved insertion windows** on the assay schedule, for example a slot every 2 hours or up to about 35% of slots, instead of unconditional preemption.
  - Routine orders older than 48h are promoted automatically.
- **Why robust:** priority follows the actual risk to each order, not its label. It adapts on its own to mix shifts (S1), arrival compression (S4) and recovery surges (S6). It stops Routine starvation, which is the main risk of I1, and limits schedule fragmentation because Urgent orders enter at planned windows.
- **Assumptions:**
  - Timestamps and case-type labels in the lab information system are accurate.
  - Queue state is visible in real time.
  - The Urgent contract clock is defined clearly: from registration or from receipt.
- **Where it fails:**
  - Under severe overload (S5), slack rules spread lateness across everyone and many orders miss their targets together. It therefore needs the overload fallback of explicit class protection (P5).
  - If Urgent labeling inflates, the reserved lane fills up.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** smoother flow reaches technical validation, which is already saturated. R1 does not reduce total work, so it must be combined with R2 and R5.

### R2. Repeat-reduction and failure-cost-based machine routing (replaces static I3)

- **Design:**
  1. Root-cause analysis of M2 failures and of the post-night-shift clustering: calibration and QC checks at shift handover, maintenance windows moved out of peak hours, reagent-lot tracking, night-shift workload and fatigue.
  2. **Routing by the cost of a failure, not by case type.** Orders where a failure is expensive go to M1: Repeat-after-failure, Complex-panel, low residual sample volume, and Urgent orders with little slack. Orders where a failure is cheap go to M2: Routine with enough volume and slack. M2's faster runtime is still used.
  3. A repeat run goes to the *other* machine by default.
  4. This is a soft preference with a load threshold, not a dedication. If M1's queue exceeds M2's by more than about 3h, cheap-failure Urgent orders may use M2.
- **Why robust:** in peak, repeats use about 10.5% of assay runs compared with about 5.4% in normal. Getting halfway back to normal frees about 5 utilization points at the assay (from 98% to about 93%), which cuts assay queue pressure 3 to 4 times. It also removes technical-validation work, repeat loops of 26–34h, and future Repeat-after-failure orders. It pays off in S1, S2b, S4 (fewer night-shift repeats) and S5.
- **Assumptions:**
  - Failures are really linked to machine and shift, as the clustering suggests, rather than mainly to sample quality. R3 covers the sample-quality side.
  - M1 has headroom for its share.
- **Where it fails:**
  - An M1 outage (S2a). Then all orders go to M2 and the degraded-mode policy P2 takes over.
  - If the M2 root cause is not fixed, sending Routine work to M2 raises Routine repeats. This is the I3 conflict in smaller form.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** faster, cleaner assay output hits technical validation (101%). **R2 must not go live without R5.**

### R3. Early pre-analytical defect triage at intake (I6), plus structured digital rejection feedback

- **Design:**
  - Samples are checked for tube type, volume, labeling, hemolysis and transport temperature within 2h of receipt.
  - Defects trigger recollection immediately through a templated electronic message to the ordering physician, instead of a clerk phone call.
  - For Urgent orders, a senior technologist decides whether to reject the sample or run it with a flag when the result would still be clinically usable.
- **Why robust:** today a defect is found about 9h + 7h after receipt in peak. Moving detection to within 2h shortens the recollection loop by more than half a day. It also stops defective samples from using preparation, assay and technical-validation capacity before failing as repeats. Templated messages cut the clerk time that currently reduces intake capacity. The benefit appears in S1, S4 (transport-damaged samples) and S5.
- **Assumptions:**
  - A large share of later failures can be detected at intake.
  - Physicians accept electronic rejection notices.
- **Where it fails:**
  - False rejections, which hurt Urgent orders most, since a recollection means a missed contract.
  - Recollection volume may *rise* at first because defects are caught instead of slipping through.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:**
  - Courier intake and registration (more replacement samples, more physician contact).
  - Cancellations: 42% of recollections end in cancellation during peak.
  - Monitor the false-rejection rate through audit samples.

### R4. Registration automation for complete digital orders (I8), with freed clerk time redeployed (replaces I4 as proposed)

- **Design:**
  - Orders with complete, validated digital metadata are registered automatically.
  - Freed clerk hours go to: (a) supporting accession correction, since clerks already work with order metadata; (b) physician communication for recollections; (c) receiving support during the 10:00 and 17:00 courier waves.
  - Technical validators are **not** moved to accession correction during peaks, because technical validation is at 101%.
- **Why robust:**
  - Registration precedes sample receipt, so automating it does not flood pre-analytical check with *physical* work. Arrival timing is still set by the courier.
  - The main gain is fewer accession errors (corrections are 4.3% of received samples in peak, with a 16h correction→preparation edge) and a larger flexible staff pool.
  - This relieves the two-person accession team, which unblocks many orders, without creating a technical-validation shortage.
- **Assumptions:**
  - A meaningful share of orders arrives with complete digital metadata.
  - Clerks can be trained on accession correction quickly (weeks, not months).
- **Where it fails:** auto-registration with unvalidated metadata lets errors through silently, and they reappear later as accession corrections or mislabeled results. The gate therefore needs strict validation rules.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:**
  - Pre-analytical check. Registered orders are ready sooner, and faster order readiness can raise same-day processing expectations.
  - Accession correction, if the auto-registration rules are too lax.
  - Track the correction rate separately for auto-registered and manually registered orders.

### R5. Targeted validation flexibility at the true constraint

- **Design:**
  1. **Autoverification rules** for Routine results with no flags, within reference or delta-check limits, and a clean QC status. These are released without manual technical validation.
  2. **Pre-season cross-training** of a small flexible pool (2–3 people) from preparation and pre-analytical staff into technical validation. Training takes 4 weeks and happens in the normal period, when these stations run at about 69–74%.
  3. I5 (evening medical-validation shift) prepared in advance but *activated by trigger* (P1).
- **Why robust:** technical validation is the only station above 100% in peak, and it is the station every relief upstream feeds into. The flexible pool pays off *because different scenarios load different stations*. In S2a, preparation and technical validation are starved during the outage, so the pool can be used where needed. In S1, the pool moves to technical validation. In S4, it follows the arrival wave. This is targeted flexibility, not blanket capacity.
- **Assumptions:**
  - Regulatory and quality approval for autoverification.
  - Cross-trained staff remain competent through regular rotation, for example one shift per fortnight.
- **Where it fails:**
  - **Autoverification gives the least relief exactly when it is needed most.** In winter, sick patients produce more abnormal results, so the autoverification rate falls.
  - Pulling preparation staff into technical validation weakens preparation (89% in peak) unless preparation has slack at that moment.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** 42% of technical-validation output in peak goes to medical validation and 7.5% to specialist hold. Relieving technical validation *moves the queue to medical validation and specialist review*. That is why P1 and P3 are pre-armed.

### R6. WIP gate at test preparation (CONWIP, paced by assay and technical validation)

- **Design:**
  - Routine samples that have passed the pre-analytical check are released into preparation only while downstream WIP (assay queue plus technical-validation queue) is below a cap, for example 1.2 times the normal WIP.
  - Held samples wait in a refrigerated, stability-tracked buffer and are released in order of stability expiry.
  - Urgent and stability-critical samples bypass the gate within the reserved lane.
- **Why robust:** in peak, 54% of orders had at least one queue over 12h. Uncontrolled WIP inside the lab is where samples age, schedules fragment and priority rules stop working. A gate keeps the constraint busy without flooding it. It helps in S1, S4, S5 and especially S6, where it turns a burst into a paced drain.
- **Assumptions:**
  - Refrigerated storage capacity is available.
  - Sample stability is tracked per tube.
  - The cap is tuned by station.
- **Where it fails:** if the gate holds samples past their stability limits, it *creates* recollections. Stability-aware release is mandatory.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** waiting moves upstream into the buffer and pre-analytical area, which is the intended effect, and storage capacity becomes the constraint.

### R7. Complex-panel pathway

- **Design:**
  - Complex-panel orders are flagged at registration: they are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to be held for specialist review.
  - The assays in a panel are scheduled **in parallel and synchronized**, so the order does not wait for its slowest assay. The object-centric view should confirm which tube or assay actually holds the order.
  - Specialist-review slots are pre-booked when the flag is set.
  - The specialist calendar is built around the every-second-Friday absence: clear the backlog on Thursday, and schedule Complex-panel assays so that results needing review arrive Monday to Thursday.
- **Why robust:** Complex-panel orders are the class whose demand grows most in S1 and the class most exposed in S3. Planning their path removes calendar-driven delay (the 48h Hold→Medical edge) that no amount of capacity fixes.
- **Assumptions:** the flag predicts review needs reasonably well from order content.
- **Where it fails:** during a surge in specialist-review needs beyond what the calendar can absorb. P3 (I10) then takes over.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** synchronized panel runs take more M1 capacity at once, which competes with the Urgent lane. The number of synchronized panel runs per slot has to be capped.

### R8. Courier smoothing for Routine pickups (I7), plus a separate STAT channel for Urgent samples

- **Design:**
  - Routine pickup slots are spread so that no 2-hour window receives more than about 25% of daily samples.
  - Urgent samples use a STAT or on-demand courier channel, so smoothing never delays them.
- **Why robust:** it lowers the variability of arrivals at pre-analytical check (96%). Arrival variance drives queue length about as much as utilization does.
- **Assumptions:**
  - Clinics and courier contracts can change.
  - Stability of Routine samples tolerates later pickups.
- **Where it fails:** in weather or traffic events (S4), smoothing plans are overridden.
- **Next-bottleneck risk:** later pickups shift arrivals into the evening. Without matching evening staff for pre-analytical check, preparation and technical validation, samples queue overnight, which feeds the night-shift repeat clusters. Smoothing must be introduced together with shifted staff rosters, not on its own.

### Interventions rejected or restricted

- **I1 (strict Urgent-first):** rejected as a permanent rule and replaced by R1. It lowers Urgent waiting only at preparation and assay, while Urgent orders lose most of their time in technical and medical validation. It also fragments batches at a 98%-utilized machine, which cuts effective capacity, and it starves Routine and Complex-panel orders, which raises WIP and sample aging.
- **I2 (batch size 24):** not permanent. Routine volume is flat, so doubling the batch size roughly doubles fill time, and it releases results to technical validation in larger lumps. It is allowed only as the timer-capped policy P2/P4.
- **I3 (static machine dedication):** replaced by R2. Splitting the pool loses pooling efficiency, sends Routine work to the machine with more failures, and leaves Urgent orders without backup if M1 fails.
- **I4 (technical validators doing accession correction):** rejected during peaks, because it takes capacity from a station at 101%. It is allowed only when the technical-validation queue is below 4h, for example during an assay outage when technical validation is starved.
- **I9 (holding Routine releases for a batch report):** rejected. Release is not a constrained resource (Medical→Release is 2–5h, and no release queue is reported). I9 adds waiting to Routine orders, whose peak p90 (120h) already exceeds the 72h target, without relieving any bottleneck. Physician notifications can be batched as a report without holding results back.
- **I10 (partner lab):** kept only as the triggered policy P3.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Every policy has an ON trigger and an OFF trigger with hysteresis, so it does not switch back and forth daily. Triggers are evaluated at least twice a day (for example 07:00 and 15:00) by a named duty coordinator.

### P1 – Evening medical-validation shift (I5), used in S1, S3, S5 and S6
- **ON:** medical-validation median queue above 6h for 2 consecutive days, **or** Urgent plus Complex-panel share of 24h arrivals above 40% (normal about 31%, peak about 49%), **or** rostered medical-validation capacity for the next 48h below 85% of forecast.
- **OFF:** medical-validation queue below 3h **and** Urgent plus Complex-panel share below 35% for 3 consecutive days.
- **Why the evening:** results from the 17:00 courier wave otherwise wait overnight for validation, which is a major cause of Urgent contract misses.
- **Downstream check:** medical validation clears faster only if technical validation feeds it. Activate together with the technical-validation flexible pool (R5) if the technical-validation queue is above 8h. Watch for evening releases that need physician callbacks at night, and for validator fatigue: cap consecutive evening shifts at 3.

### P2 – Machine degraded mode, used in S2 and S5
- **ON:** any machine unavailable for more than 2h, or either machine's rolling 8h repeat rate above 1.5 times its baseline, or QC or calibration failure.
- **If M1 is down:**
  - Urgent and Repeat-after-failure orders run on M2 with an extra QC run before each batch and rerun priority.
  - Stable Routine samples are held in the WIP-gate buffer, in order of stability expiry. Complex-panel orders that can travel are sent to the partner lab.
  - Urgent insertion windows are consolidated, for example every 3h, to limit changeovers on the only working machine.
  - Idle technical validators work on the validation backlog or on accession correction (I4 allowed here).
- **If M2 is down or degraded:**
  - M1 serves orders by failure cost.
  - Routine batches may grow to 24 **with a 6h maximum fill time** to save setups. This is the only situation where I2 applies.
- **OFF:** machine restored **and** its repeat rate is back within 1.2 times baseline for 24h. Then move directly to P6.
- **Downstream check:** pre-book technical-validation surge capacity for 12–36h after restoration, before the recovery arrives.

### P3 – Complex-panel overflow to the partner lab (I10), used in S1, S3 and S5
- **ON:** forecast specialist-review queue above 24h. That is the break-even point where transport (12h) plus the partner's turnaround beats the local queue. Or a specialist absence overlaps with more than 1.5 times normal Complex-panel inflow.
- **Rules:**
  - The decision is made **at receipt, for the whole order**: all tubes and all assays go. Orders are never split mid-process, which keeps traceability intact.
  - Only transport-stable samples go.
  - The number of partner results returning per day is capped at what local medical validation and release can absorb.
  - Where possible, prefer *remote* specialist review of digital results by the partner, which avoids the 12h transport.
- **OFF:** specialist queue below 12h for 2 days, or the partner signals capacity limits. The partner is probably in the same winter wave, so its capacity has to be confirmed daily.
- **Downstream check:**
  - Returning partner results still need local medical validation and release.
  - The courier channel carries additional outbound traffic.
  - Chain-of-custody reconciliation is needed: a daily check that every sent order came back.

### P4 – Adaptive Routine batching, used in S1 and S2b
- **ON:** assay utilization above 90% **and** Routine orders have healthy slack (fewer than 10% of open Routine orders older than 36h).
- **Setting:** batch closes at 24 samples or after 4–6h, whichever comes first. Urgent orders never enter batch wait; they use the reserved lane.
- **OFF:** more than 15% of open Routine orders are older than 48h, **or** the technical-validation queue is above 8h. Large batches release results in lumps that hit technical validation.
- **Downstream check:** release batch results to technical validation in staggered portions.

### P5 – Overload triage (explicit class protection), used in S5
- **ON:** at least two stations above 95% utilization, **or** more than 15% of in-process Urgent orders projected to miss 18h, **or** in-lab WIP above 1.5 times normal.
- **Rules:**
  - The protection order is published: (1) Urgent orders within the contract; (2) Repeat-after-failure orders near their stability limit; (3) Complex-panel orders, with P3 active; (4) Routine orders with an aging cap of 96h, communicated proactively to clients.
  - R1 switches from pure slack ordering to class-first ordering, because slack rules spread lateness under overload.
- **OFF:** all stations below 92% and Urgent at-risk share below 5% for 2 days.
- **Downstream check:** Routine WIP grows in the buffer. Watch sample stability and the Routine aging tail so that Routine orders do not get stuck indefinitely.

### P6 – Recovery drain, used in S6 after S2, S3 or S4
- **ON:** automatically when P2, P7 or a medical-validation absence ends with backlog above 1.3 times normal WIP.
- **Rules:**
  - The backlog is released through the WIP gate (R6), in order of stability first and slack second.
  - It is not released first-in-first-out, and not all at once.
  - Pre-scheduled technical- and medical-validation overtime is used for 1–2 days. The 9h shift limit applies because the error rate rises afterwards.
- **OFF:** WIP below 1.1 times normal.

### P7 – Courier disruption mode, used in S4 and S5
- **ON:** more than 35% of the day's samples forecast or arriving in any 2h window, **or** a courier arrival delay of more than 2h.
- **Rules:**
  - Staggered pre-analytical shift starts: move 1–2 people to a later start.
  - Freed clerks (R4) support receiving.
  - Intake triage (R3) takes Urgent and stability-critical samples first.
  - The STAT courier is activated for Urgent samples.
  - The evening assay and technical-validation crew is extended by 2–3h, so that samples do not wait overnight for the night shift, where repeats cluster.
- **OFF:** arrival distribution normal for 2 consecutive days. Then move to P6 if a backlog remains.

### P8 – Specialist calendar protection, recurring
- **ON:** automatically on the Wednesday and Thursday before each every-second-Friday absence.
- **Rules:**
  - Clear the Thursday backlog.
  - Schedule Complex-panel runs so that results needing review do not land on Friday.
  - Arrange cover or remote review for Friday.
  - If Complex-panel inflow is above 1.5 times normal, pre-activate P3 for Friday orders.
- **OFF:** after Monday, once the specialist queue is below 12h.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Two-machine assay capacity has no real redundancy.** An M1 outage during a winter wave, while M2 is unreliable, leaves only degraded mode and outsourcing. No dispatch rule fixes the loss of half the capacity at a station that normally runs at 98% in peak.
2. **Small expert pools:**
   - Accession correction still depends on 2 specialists plus clerks with fewer skills.
   - Specialist review has a structural fortnightly gap.
   - A single long-term absence in either pool makes that pool the dominant bottleneck.
3. **Correlated shocks.** Infection waves also make staff sick. Winter weather causes courier delays. The partner lab faces the same wave. The scenarios are not independent, and the partner-lab outlet (P3) is least available exactly when it is most needed.
4. **Vulnerabilities created by the redesign itself:**
   - **Urgent-label inflation.** A dependable reserved Urgent lane rewards ordering physicians who label orders Urgent. Part of the rise from 18% to 29% Urgent may already be labeling behavior. If it grows, the lane fills, Routine and Complex-panel orders are squeezed, and R1 turns back into I1. This needs an audit of Urgent justification by ordering unit.
   - **Autoverification relief falls in winter.** More abnormal results mean fewer autoverified orders, so technical-validation relief shrinks when demand peaks. Poorly maintained rules can also let errors through, which becomes a patient-safety vulnerability.
   - **Staff counted twice in the flexible pool.** The same cross-trained people are planned for technical validation (S1), receiving (S4) and accession backup. In S5 they cannot be in three places at once. Rosters must assign each pool member a primary scenario role.
   - **The WIP gate creates stability risk.** Samples held in the buffer can expire. That produces recollections and Repeat-after-failure orders, the very loops the redesign tries to remove.
   - **Early triage can over-reject.** Aggressive intake rejection raises recollection and cancellation (already 42% of recollections in peak) and upsets physicians. This hits Urgent orders hardest.
   - **The system becomes more complex.** Several trigger-based modes plus partner routing increase coordination effort and the risk of mode confusion, traceability gaps and inconsistent rules between shifts. The duty coordinator becomes a single point of failure.
5. **Measurement fragility.**
   - The log is flattened by lab order, so tube- and assay-level waiting is hidden.
   - The DFG imbalances at Assay run and Technical validation mean some bottleneck estimates rely on inferred rather than observed transitions.
   - About 13% of peak orders are censored.
   - Reported utilization looks inconsistent with transition growth.
   - Triggers calibrated on these numbers may fire too late. Thresholds should be recalibrated after the first wave, using an object-centric event log and survival-based cycle-time estimates.
6. **The upstream-to-downstream chain has no end.** Even with every package active, a mix above about 35% Urgent plus about 24% Complex-panel pushes medical validation past its limit. At that point the only remaining levers are explicit triage (P5), outsourcing (P3), or client-facing demand management, for example agreeing Urgent criteria with major ordering units.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Review twice daily during peaks (07:00 and 15:00) by the duty coordinator. **Leading indicators** trigger policies. **Lagging outcomes** judge whether the redesign works but arrive too late to steer by.

### Leading indicators (daily, with warning bands)

| # | Indicator | Green | Amber | Red (action) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Arrival mix: Urgent + Complex-panel share of last 24h orders | < 33% | 33–40% | > 40% → P1 ON, check P3 |
| 2 | Urgent orders at risk: share of in-process Urgent orders with projected release beyond 18h (or slack < 6h) | < 5% | 5–15% | > 15% → P5 check |
| 3 | Technical-validation queue (median and oldest item) | < 4h | 4–8h | > 8h → technical-validation pool, stop P4 |
| 4 | Medical-validation queue (median) and rostered capacity for next 48h vs forecast | < 3h, ≥ 100% | 3–6h, 85–100% | > 6h or < 85% → P1 |
| 5 | Specialist-review queue forecast, and scheduled absences in next 72h | < 8h | 8–24h | > 24h → P3; Friday absence → P8 |
| 6 | Assay repeat rate per machine per shift | < 7% | 7–10% | > 10% or M2 > 1.5 times M1 → P2 |
| 7 | In-lab WIP vs normal baseline, by station | < 1.2 times | 1.2–1.5 times | > 1.5 times → gate tighter, P5 check |
| 8 | Pre-analytical rejection/recollection rate and accession backlog age | < 5%, < 4h | 5–8%, 4–8h | > 8% or > 8h → clerk redeployment, R3 review |
| 9 | Courier arrival concentration (share of samples in busiest 2h) and courier delay | < 25%, < 1h | 25–35%, 1–2h | > 35% or > 2h → P7 |
| 10 | Samples within 12h of stability expiry that are not yet run | ~0 | a few, all scheduled | any unscheduled → release from buffer immediately |
| 11 | Routine aging: share of open Routine orders older than 48h | < 10% | 10–15% | > 15% → stop P4, promote Routine orders |

### Lagging outcome metrics (reviewed weekly, not used as triggers)

- Urgent orders released within 18h, and penalty count and cost.
- Routine orders released within 72h, and the Routine p90.
- p90 cycle time by case type (Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, Repeat-after-failure), estimated with censoring-aware methods, since about 13% of peak orders were still open.
- Overall rework rate and repeat-assay rate.
- Share of cases with at least one queue over 12h (normal 22%, peak 54%). Target: stay below about 35% in a wave.
- Recollection loop time and recollection→cancel rate.
- Repeat-after-failure order inflow, which lags failures by 2–5 days.
- Partner-lab turnaround and chain-of-custody discrepancies.
- Physician complaints about rejections and delays.

The dashboard is deliberately small. It tracks the rework drivers (repeats, recollection), the true constraints (technical validation, medical validation, specialist review, assay), and the mix and arrival pattern (arrival mix, courier concentration). Those three things decided performance in the peak logs. The historical average cycle time is not the steering metric; the question is whether the next shift's constraint and the Urgent slack will hold.
