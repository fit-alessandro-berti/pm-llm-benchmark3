# 2. Integrated explanation

Service time and error rates change over the work period because **multiple process-level factors operate simultaneously and interact**, rather than any single mechanism (e.g., fatigue alone or learning alone) explaining all variation. Early in each shift (positions 1–5), the log consistently shows higher mean complexity (0.72) and higher high-priority share (41%) due to carryover from the previous shift (e.g., night backlog cleared by early shift). This selection effect—complex, often urgent cases arriving at shift start—necessitates longer initial service times (18.8 min in bin 1–5) and a moderate error rate (3.4%). The apparent "learning" in bins 6–30 (service time drops to ~13.6 min, error ~2.5%) reflects the natural resolution of the carryover backlog: as the first few cases of the shift are completed, queue length decreases (e.g., Early shift queue 6142), complexity stabilizes or modestly drops (mean complexity 0.54–0.61), and staff gain routine familiarity with the current day's typical case flow. Importantly, this speed-up is **not due to fatigue recovery** (Night staff show stable error despite fewer cases) nor purely circadian—Early shift clears backlog efficiently while Day shift peaks late, indicating the pattern depends on shift timing relative to backlog inheritance. Batching (e.g., Juniors doing consecutive Document Review events) can contribute to faster processing in early-mid bins, but it does not consistently lower error (error remains ~2.8% in bin 6–15), so it explains part of the service-time trend but not the error pattern.

Around position 31–50 (2–3.5 hours into shift), complexity continues to drop (mean 0.50–0.54), service time stabilizes or slightly decreases (~12.9–13.6 min), and error remains low (~2.5–2.7%). This aligns with continued backlog clearance and reduced selection pressure from night carryover; staff are handling the "steady state" of the shift's natural demand. Here, learning (gaining routine efficiency), reduced complexity selection, and lower queue pressure combine to produce stable or improving service time with low error—no strong indication of fatigue impact because queue pressure remains moderate (WIP ~31–37) and Night-like low-case exposure does not apply to day/late shifts.

From position 51–75 (4–6.5 hours in), queue pressure rises visibly: `queue_length_at_start` mean increases to ~62, and WIP>70 emerges consistently. Under this pressure, service time drops further (to ~11.4–12.9 min) as staff prioritize backlog clearance to meet shift throughput goals; this is a rational response to queue pressure (not fatigue slowing work), consistent with observed mean queue drop from start to end in Late/Day shifts. However, error rate rises (to ~4.6–5.0% in 51–75, then to ~7.9% in 76+). This rise occurs **only when complexity is not very low** (e.g., high complexity + WIP>70 = 10.9% error; low complexity + WIP>70 = 5.8%, which is still higher than baseline low-WIP low-complexity error 2.1%). The reason is twofold: (1) high WIP creates time pressure for quicker decisions (e.g., faster Eligibility Decision, less time for thorough Document Review), and (2) under pressure, the selection effect shifts slightly: urgent or high-priority cases (which often have higher inherent error risk or require more careful review) become more common in the queue, especially when queue length is high. Thus, queue pressure directly drives the speed-up (lower service time) but also amplifies error for cases with moderate/high complexity due to reduced review time and higher case-mix urgency—not because staff are fatigued. Night staff show less error rise under high WIP (8.2% vs 7.9%) because they typically have lower average WIP and fewer urgent exceptions, consistent with the pressure-dependent pattern.

In position 76+ (~7 hours in), service time reaches its lowest (~10.8 min), reflecting maximal backlog clearance under high WIP, while error rate peaks (~7.9%). This peak occurs because: (a) queue pressure is highest (WIP ~88), (b) complexity is lowest (0.43), but (c) the combination of high WIP + urgency (e.g., "more urgent exceptions" in Night notes) means staff are handling both routine backlog and time-sensitive cases with limited buffer; under sustained pressure, even low-complexity cases can incur errors if Quality Check is delayed (a known workaround: QC often happens at shift end), causing corrections to cluster late. Automation interaction reinforces this: when WIP>70, humans correct Bot_Risk more often, adding a rework step that may be rushed, contributing to the error rise. Fatigue could play a role here for some staff (e.g., late-shift workers), but the pattern is consistently observed across shifts (Day/Late show similar 7–8% error peaks), and Night staff (fewer cases) do not show the same peak error, so fatigue alone cannot explain the consistent pressure-related error rise; instead, the error rise is primarily driven by the queue-pressure + case-mix interaction (urgency + reduced review time) plus the late QC delay workaround. Learning (e.g., staff improving accuracy) does not offset the pressure effect in this bin because the primary driver (high WIP urgency) persists; learning effects plateau earlier (bins 16–30) as complexity drops and routine settles.

Crucially, **error does not rise steadily throughout the shift** because: (1) early bins (1–5) have high complexity + high priority but moderate WIP (~46), so staff have adequate time for careful review (error ~3.4%, not >5%); (2) bins 6–30 have lower complexity + moderate WIP (~33–37), allowing stable low error (~2.5–2.7%); (3) only in bins 51–75+ (where WIP>70 and complexity is moderate/high) does error rise noticeably. Similarly, **service time does not rise** because queue pressure consistently incentivizes faster processing of backlog; the drop in service time from ~18.8 min (early) to ~10.8 min (late) reflects effective backlog clearance under pressure, not fatigue slowing work. Thus, the observed pattern—decreasing service time with increasing error only in late-shift, high-WIP conditions—is best explained by the **interaction of case-mix selection (inherent complexity/urgency of arriving cases), queue pressure (driving speed-up to clear backlog), and batching/workarounds (e.g., delayed QC causing late errors), with circadian and fatigue adding nuance (e.g., Night shift stability due to lower average WIP, Seniors showing more stable quality under pressure). No single mechanism suffices; the pattern emerges from how selection, pressure, and workarounds co-occur across shift positions.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Worker-level conclusions are often misleading**: Raw late-shift error rates (e.g., 7.9% in 76+ bin) include effects of queue pressure (WIP>70), batching (QC delayed to end), and urgency (e.g., Night "urgent exceptions"). A junior worker showing a higher late-shift error rate may simply be handling high-complexity cases under high WIP due to workflow (e.g., batching or queue pressure), not poor skill. Penalizing or publicly labeling them as "error-prone" ignores the process context and creates stigma. For example, Juniors naturally have 12.4% error under high WIP (per resource-experience table), which matches the observed 76+ bin error (7.9%–13.7% range); this is a predictable response to pressure, not individual failure. Saying "Junior_H has high error" without conditioning on WIP and position is inaccurate and unfair.

- **Selection bias in measurement**: The log shows "Senior resources appear to take complex cases early", especially after night backlog. If error rates are compared across resources without accounting for complexity assignment (e.g., comparing Juniors' error in early positions to Seniors' error in early positions), it appears Juniors err more, but this reflects adaptive assignment (Seniors take complex early to clear backlog efficiently), not incompetence. Randomizing assignment (intervention 4) without understanding urgency could send complex cases to Juniors under pressure, increasing their error—making "reduction" from randomization counterproductive. Thus, measuring error by resource alone without complexity context reinforces bias.

- **Queue pressure created by management**: High WIP (>70) frequently occurs due to demand peaks (e.g., Day shift demand peaks late, Night inherits day backlog). If management consistently schedules cases without considering current queue, WIP pressure becomes inevitable. Penalizing workers for high-WIP late-shift errors (intervention 8) treats symptoms (worker error) as cause (management queue pressure), shifting blame unfairly. Workers cannot control incoming case volume or urgency; they respond rationally to pressure (faster service to clear backlog), which can elevate error. Calling this "fatigue" or "carelessness" misattributes cause.

- **Automation quality and human correction**: Bot_Risk classifies instantly, but humans correct it more often when WIP>70. If Bot errors are frequent under pressure (e.g., due to high complexity), correcting them adds a rework step. The observed 10.9% error for WIP>70 + high complexity may partly stem from Bot corrections under pressure (e.g., rushed correction), not solely human fatigue. Penalizing human error without analyzing Bot-related rework overlooks automation's role. Giving humans clear guidance on Bot correction thresholds (e.g., "correct only if complexity 0.7") would be more effective than penalizing error rates.

- **Punitive use of error rates**: Using raw late-shift error rate (e.g., average error in positions 76+) for performance reviews or penalties ignores: (1) natural variation due to WIP (error rises only under WIP>70), (2) batching (QC delayed causes late errors), and (3) resource experience (Juniors naturally higher under pressure). A fair approach uses **conditional metrics**: e.g., "error rate for high-complexity cases under WIP>70" – measured consistently across all staff. Staff can then receive feedback on improving the conditional metric (e.g., "when WIP>70 and complexity high, aim for <8% error"), which is actionable and context-aware. Publicly sharing raw late-shift error rates by worker without context reinforces bias and increases stress.

- **Avoiding over-interpretation of correlation**: Observing "Juniors have 19.6 min first-event service time vs 13.7 min later" might imply "Juniors learn slowly", but the table shows all groups speed up; the difference reflects early complexity load (Juniors may get complex cases early due to availability), not slower learning. Saying "Juniors learn slowly" without conditioning on complexity misrepresents data. Always compare within same complexity/WIP context (e.g., "Juniors' service time decrease from first to later events when complexity is high").

- **Involving workers in metric design**: Ethically, workers should co-design performance indicators. For example, ask: "When do you feel rushed? When do you wish you had more time for QC?" Use their input to define "urgent exceptions" and clarify when QC can be deferred. This reduces misattribution (e.g., "error" seen as worker fault vs process pressure) and improves metric validity. Avoid top-down metrics without frontline input.

> *Summary: Never infer individual ability from raw late-shift error rates. Always pair error measurement with key context (WIP bin, complexity band, position bin, shift type). Use conditional metrics (e.g., error rate for high complexity + WIP>70) for feedback; reserve raw rates for aggregate process monitoring. Involve workers in defining what "pressure" or "urgency" means. Avoid public or punitive use of error rates without context. Recognize that workflow choices (e.g., batching, QC delay) are often adaptive responses to pressure, not failures.*

**Dashboard layout & usability guidelines**:

- **Top row**: Shift selector (Early/Day/Late/Night), date range filter.
- **Main area**: Two side-by-side small line charts:
  - Left: Avg service time by position bin (color by WIP bin: low=blue, med=gray, high=orange); orange bins highlight if WIP=high and service time <12 min (pressure speed-up zone).
  - Right: Error rate by position & WIP (stacked bars); red flag icon appears if any WIP=high bin has error >6%.
- **Below charts**: Four key numeric indicators (large text, e.g., "High-complexity WIP-error: 11.2% ±1.0%", "WIP=high % at shift start: 68%", "QC in last 45 mins: 38%", "Junior WIP=high service diff: 3.2 min"); each shows 7-day trend line (simple moving average).
- **Right sidebar**: "Context view" toggle: show WIP bin color on charts, show complexity share for early positions, show batching % for Juniors Doc Review. Avoids clutter; user selects "Show WIP context" or "Show complexity context".
- **No worker names**: All indicators are team/shift-level aggregates (e.g., "Day shift", "Juniors group"). No drill-down to individual worker error rates in dashboard; for feedback, use conditional metric (e.g., "Your team's high-complexity WIP=high error rate this week: 10.5% (target <12%); last week: 9.8% – good improvement").
- **Alert logic (non-punitive)**: Dashboard shows a "Watch" badge (yellow) if:
  - WIP=high % at shift start >60% *and* QC in last 45 mins >40%, *or*
  - High-complexity WIP-error >12% (outside typical range), *or*
  - Junior WIP=high service diff >3 min (first20 - later >3 min).
  Badge says: "Pressure rising: consider checking if low-priority QC can be deferred; review batch consistency for Juniors." No "error penalty" language.
  Dashboard never says "Worker X has high error"; only "Team Day shift: WIP=high error rate 8.2% (baseline ~10.9%)".
- **Frequency & access**: Updated daily at shift end (automated script using PM4Py functions). Visible to shift leads and operations analysts; not visible to individual workers initially (use feedback meetings with context explanation). Workers receive a 2-sentence weekly summary: "This week, your shift had X% of cases start with high WIP; average service time dropped from early to late shift as expected. High-complexity WIP=high error was Y% (below target), good job on QC timing (Z% in last 45 mins). If you notice QC getting delayed, try asking the lead if low-priority checks can wait – this helps avoid late bursts."
- **Why these 8 indicators?** They directly map to the competing mechanisms:
  - Service time by position & WIP  learning vs queue pressure vs fatigue
  - Error rate by position & WIP  pressure vs selection vs batching
  - WIP pressure signal  queue pressure driver
  - QC timing  batching workaround
  - High-complexity WIP-error  case-mix + pressure interaction (key conditional metric)
  - Batching indicator  batching effect
  - Resource-experience fairness  fatigue/pressure fairness (avoids worker blame)
  - Shift-start complexity  selection/carryover
  - Queue ownership  backlog persistence (links shifts)
  All are log-based, computed simply, actionable (e.g., "check if QC can be deferred"), and avoid raw late-shift error for individuals. Thresholds are descriptive ("flag if >X", "target <Y") and contextualized (e.g., "no hard cap; watch if >6% error with WIP=high"), not punitive.

All indicators are measurable from current event log. Monitoring is practical (daily, shift-level), ethical (no individual blame), and aligned with mechanism distinction.*
