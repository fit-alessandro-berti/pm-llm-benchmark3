## 1. Decision rule

Treat `p_now` as an estimate of the no-swarm breach probability, \(p_i^0\), not as the breach probability after intervention. The swarm decision is based on estimated incremental portfolio value, not on a single probability threshold.

For an eligible case \(i\), calculate a base marginal score:

\[
N_i =
E[\min(\Delta p_i,p_i^0)] \times V_i
- €450
- B_i
\]

where \(\Delta p_i\) is a causal-effect distribution, not a known reduction, \(V_i\) is breach value, and \(B_i\) is the listed false-alarm/customer-disruption burden. I charge the full listed burden conservatively rather than relying on potentially miscalibrated probabilities to estimate whether an alert is false.

The queue-adjusted score is:

\[
S_i = N_i - H_i,
\qquad
H_i \approx d \times W_Q
\]

where \(d\) is the marginal spillover probability, approximately 0.02 in the base scenario, and \(W_Q\) is the total breach-value exposure of the six untreated diagnosis-queue cases affected by a slot. \(W_Q\) must be calculated from the live queue before dispatch; it is not available in the supplied case table.

A swarm is deployable only when all of the following hold:

1. **Process eligibility.** The case is before `Diagnosis Complete`; the swarm can begin and finish while diagnosis remains actionable; and there is enough SLA time for a 45-minute intervention plus a small execution buffer.
2. **Causal plausibility.** The alert reason suggests a diagnostic bottleneck that a senior swarm could affect. A missing or corrupt feature does not count as new evidence.
3. **Evidence status.** It is a first alert, an unresolved active alert, or a valid re-entry alert. Repeated model refreshes alone do not create new intervention opportunities.
4. **Portfolio gate.** The base queue-adjusted score is positive, the projected queue harm does not violate a protected-case or queue-delay guardrail, and the adverse scenario fits the organization’s approved downside budget.
5. **Human constraints.** Customer disruption, regulatory obligations, safety commitments, consent requirements, and fairness safeguards can override a purely monetary ranking.

Calibration is handled as uncertainty distributions rather than point corrections:

- **SME:** use \(p_i^0 \in [p_{now}+0.04,\ p_{now}+0.09]\), clipped at 1. SME cases should not be missed merely because their raw score appears moderate.
- **Strategic Enterprise:** use \(p_i^0 \in [p_{now}-0.12,\ p_{now}-0.05]\), clipped at 0. For example, I-02 is roughly 0.54–0.61 rather than 0.66, and I-04 is roughly 0.36–0.43 rather than 0.48.
- **Public Sector:** retain the current central estimate but attach an empirical calibration interval because the sample is only moderate.
- **Non-Native-Language Support:** use a broad uncertainty interval and require a language-access/data-quality review. Uncertainty is not grounds to deny service automatically.
- **I-06-style feature failure:** until the live queue feature is restored or manually verified, treat the untreated risk as spanning roughly the prior stable prediction and the imputed prediction, here 0.20–0.52.

For effect estimates, the supplied ranges are treated as prior scenario ranges. They are capped at the calibrated untreated risk and shrunk toward zero when the alert explanation is weak, the relevant segment is sparse, or the diagnostic mechanism is not credible.

Alert evidence is considered materially changed when there is either:

- a verified external event, such as a new customer log, changed diagnostic hypothesis, reassignment, or queue shock; or
- an absolute increase of at least 0.10 in the calibrated risk estimate with a stable, valid explanation; or
- a crossing of an operational action window, such as imminent loss of diagnosis-stage eligibility.

A data-quality failure is not a material change.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

An alert is a stateful case-management object, not a new intervention request every 30 minutes.

- **Active-review window:** A first qualifying alert remains active for two hours or until a human decision is recorded. Refreshes update the evidence record but do not create duplicate pages.
- **Declined-alert cool-down:** After an explicit decision not to swarm, suppress repeated model-only alerts for two hours. Continue passive monitoring.
- **Post-swarm cool-down:** No repeat swarm on the same case before `Diagnosis Complete` or within 24 hours, whichever comes first. A repeat requires senior approval and a materially new diagnostic event.
- **Valid re-entry:** Re-entry requires a verified new customer artifact, changed failure mode, re-opened diagnosis, significant verified queue shock, or calibrated risk increase of at least 0.10 with a valid explanation.
- **Escalation:** A case can be escalated for human review without receiving a swarm when it crosses an urgency window, approaches `Diagnosis Complete`, or enters a protected obligation category. Escalation is not synonymous with intervention.
- **Feature-failure rule:** A missing, imputed, stale, or malformed feature cannot trigger re-entry by itself. It triggers data-quality review.

I-05 and I-07 illustrate the distinction:

- **I-05:** The third alert is caused by ordinary 30-minute model refreshes. There is no new event, the raw increase is only 0.02, and no swarm has been justified by new causal evidence. Suppress the duplicate alert. Its SME underprediction should still be reflected in passive monitoring and later recalibration.
- **I-07:** Although its raw probability fell from 0.75 to 0.72, a new customer diagnostic log was uploaded. This is a valid re-entry because the information may change the diagnostic mechanism and swarm usefulness. It still does not receive a slot because its queue-adjusted value is low and the deadline is close. It should receive a fast ordinary diagnostic review instead.

I-02 is not treated like I-05 because it has no prior swarm and is still an unresolved active intervention candidate. The shrinking time-to-deadline creates an execution decision even though its raw probability increase is only 0.08.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

- **Value-based prioritization can starve lower-value cases.** A €100,000 case can dominate a €5,000 case even if the latter has higher risk or a larger relative treatment effect. Use a governance-approved value cap or concave value transformation in routine ranking, and add a non-monetary obligation floor for regulatory, safety, public-service, or severe customer-harm cases. Among near-tied positive-net cases, use a fairness-aware tie-breaker rather than always selecting the highest contractual value. Monitor whether low-value tiers receive systematically longer diagnostic delays or fewer swarms conditional on eligibility.

- **Non-Native-Language Support has sparse evidence.** Do not down-rank I-09 simply because it has wider uncertainty; that would embed data scarcity as service denial. Instead require language-access support, translated log review where needed, and explicit recording of whether the swarm could engage effectively. Maintain a small, safeguarded learning allocation among clinically or operationally comparable uncertain cases, only where there is genuine equipoise and no protected-case constraint.

- **Segment calibration differs.** SME underprediction can cause missed intervention opportunities; Strategic overprediction can cause excessive disruption and wasted senior time. Use segment-specific calibration distributions in the decision score, publish segment-level calibration intervals, and prohibit use of raw probability thresholds that systematically favor one segment.

- **Selective intervention changes future labels.** Swarmed cases may breach less often, so training a future breach model on observed outcomes without treatment indicators would make high-priority groups appear safer than they would have been untreated. Record the prediction time, calibrated untreated-risk estimate, intervention propensity, timing, swarm content, feature quality, and queue exposure. Train baseline risk models on appropriate untreated or counterfactually adjusted data, not naively on post-intervention outcomes.

- **Spillover harm may land on untreated groups.** Senior interruptions can systematically delay cases handled by particular teams, regions, lower-value accounts, SMEs, or language-support customers. Before dispatch, show the six affected queue cases, their segment mix, deadlines, and estimated added risk. Block a non-emergency swarm when it would push an imminent protected case into a harmful queue state unless compensating coverage is provided. Report spillover exposure and realized delay by segment, not just intervention rates.

Concrete safeguards are: a queue-protection stop rule; a monthly equitable-access review; a fairness-aware tie-breaker among near-equal cases; language-access review for sparse segments; documented customer-disruption consent or notification; and an audited exception process whenever a monetary score overrides an obligation-based concern.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

| Monitoring family | What to measure | Action if deteriorating |
|---|---|---|
| Operational | Swarm start time, stage at delivery, completion time, specialist interruptions, ordinary-diagnosis delay, number of slots used, reserve use, and queue headroom. | Pause non-emergency swarms if realized marginal queue delay exceeds the expected 8–20 minute range, if diagnosis-stage cases approach SLA danger, or if the queue-protection guardrail is breached. |
| Predictive | Calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, Brier score, reliability plots, alert rates, and missed-breach rates by stage, deadline band, segment, language-support status, and data-quality status. | Recalibrate segment-specific untreated-risk models when observed-versus-predicted error exceeds a pre-specified tolerance with sufficient confidence. The known SME and Strategic shifts should already trigger recalibration work. |
| Causal intervention effect | Time to `Diagnosis Complete`, breach, customer disruption, rework, diagnostic information uncovered, and effect heterogeneity by stage, segment, alert reason, and time-to-deadline. | Update swarm effect distributions, not merely breach probabilities. Reduce or suspend use in a subgroup if the lower confidence bound on incremental benefit becomes unfavorable. |
| Spillover and capacity | Added breach risk, diagnosis delay, queue SLA misses, and value/segment composition of untreated cases exposed to each swarm. | Lower the daily slot cap, increase reserve capacity, or require temporary backfill if observed spillover exceeds the base 0.01–0.03 estimate or becomes concentrated in a protected group. |
| Fairness | Intervention rate conditional on eligibility and score, calibration error, outcome improvement, customer burden, queue delay, and spillover exposure by segment and value tier. | Investigate disparities that remain after conditioning on stage, deadline, estimated effect, and alert quality. Use operational remedies before changing model thresholds mechanically. |
| Data quality and policy drift | Missing-feature rates, imputation frequency, workflow changes, alert-reason distribution, proportion of cases treated, and changes in queue composition caused by the policy itself. | Disable or down-weight affected features, revert to conservative priors, and require manual review for decisions like I-06 until data integrity is restored. |

Intervention effects should be estimated using a prospective target-trial design. Among marginal, non-emergency cases where there is genuine uncertainty, randomize immediate swarm versus delayed or standard handling within score bands, stratified by stage and segment. Because swarms affect other queue cases, use time-block or queue-cluster randomization as well as case-level analysis to estimate interference. Do not randomize cases subject to hard regulatory, safety, or contractual overrides.

Outside randomized bands, use logged treatment propensities and doubly robust or inverse-propensity methods for observational estimation. Every model evaluation dataset must include treatment timing and spillover exposure; otherwise post-policy labels will be biased.

The allocation rule should be reviewed weekly operationally and monthly statistically, with immediate review after workflow redesigns or feature outages. Change the minimum score, reserve policy, or queue-harm multiplier when any of the following occurs:

- the estimated lower-bound causal benefit crosses zero;
- observed queue harm exceeds the adverse scenario or becomes concentrated in a subgroup;
- segment calibration drift is material and statistically credible;
- reserve slots are repeatedly unused while preventable breaches rise, or are repeatedly exhausted before regulatory demand;
- fairness monitoring shows persistent disparity after controlling for eligibility and plausible treatment benefit.

This keeps the policy adaptive while preserving the distinction between predicting risk, estimating treatment effect, and managing a constrained portfolio.
