## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I use **policy effects** and **interventional mediation analogues** rather than classical natural direct/indirect effects. The flag is not a clean atomic cause: it activates a bundle of organizational rules, and downstream mediators such as manual-review waiting are affected by queue state, which is itself affected by earlier treated cases. Natural effects would require cross-world counterfactuals such as “the same queue state under both flag policies,” which are not credible here.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P0 current flag bundle vs P1 suppress visible flag and disable flag-triggered priority/mandatory review, while leaving `risk_score` visible | arriving case; all cases in flag-enabled period | total cycle time: arrival to close | total policy effect of the flag bundle | Yes, if P1 implementation and queue rules are specified | shared-queue interference, equilibrium capacity response, case mix |
| E2 | P0 vs P1 | arriving case; flag-eligible cases | arrival-to-triage-done | total stage effect on triage completion | Yes | priority-lane adherence, arrival load, triage resource allocation |

| E4 | P0 vs P3 retain routing but add protected manual-review capacity for flagged cases | flagged cases routed to manual review | manual queue wait and total cycle time | interventional indirect effect through `manual_queue_wait` | Yes if added capacity is specified and protected | interference, displacement of unflagged cases, time-varying load |

Interference arises because manual and escalation queues are shared: a flagged case occupying a reviewer or senior team increases waiting for concurrent flagged and unflagged cases, so one case’s treatment changes another’s outcome. Exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding occurs after the flag: priority triage changes triage completion time, which changes queue entry time for manual review; queue length and reviewer fatigue then affect both whether a case is reviewed or escalated and total cycle time. Complexity, `risk_score`, and `U_underlying_risk` confound flag assignment and downstream routing, while `arrival_load` confounds waiting times. Treatment-confounder feedback can occur if high queue pressure leads triage resources to use the priority lane differently or reviewers to defer or escalate, and those queue states are themselves produced by earlier flagged cases. Natural direct/indirect effects would require cross-world counterfactuals on queue states and mediator settings that are not credible here.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** if audits show that flagged cases’ longer processing prevents many adverse outcomes, or if unflagged low-score cases carry substantial unobserved risk, waiving mandatory review would be unsafe and the recommendation would reverse.

2. **Queue interference:** if protected capacity or review waivers cause worse global waiting times because waived cases re-enter as escalations or because shared queues shift congestion to other teams, the preferred queue policy would reverse.

3. **Decision quality:** if the P2 pilot produces a material increase in incorrect approvals, rejects, rework, or complaints, the conclusion that downstream delay is mostly waste rather than quality protection would reverse.

4. **Timestamp semantics:** if timestamps are scheduled times, queue-entry times, or affected by local clock changes rather than actual work start/completion, the stage decomposition and waiting/service interpretation would be invalid.

5. **Positivity or lack of overlap:** if there are no comparable unflagged cases near the flag threshold, or if flagged cases are almost exclusively High complexity, causal contrasts between flag groups cannot be credibly identified.

6. **Behavior change by reviewers:** if reviewers strategically slow down, speed up, or escalate flagged cases differently because they know they are being monitored or because mandatory review is waived, the inferred mechanism would change.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed: flagged cases enter triage sooner, with mean arrival-to-triage-start about 14 minutes versus 149 minutes for unflagged cases, and slightly shorter triage service. Despite this, flagged cases have much longer total cycle time, about 991 versus 490 minutes. The excess is concentrated after triage: all flagged cases enter manual review, wait roughly 6.5 hours, and are escalated more often. Unflagged cases enter review and escalation selectively.

Plausible mechanism: the flag activates a policy bundle—priority triage, mandatory manual review, and more frequent escalation. Priority triage is a direct operational effect. Manual-review occurrence and manual-review waiting are distinct mediated pathways. Shared review and senior queues create interference, so flagged cases can also affect unflagged waiting times.

Unidentified: the true underlying risk, the value added by manual review, counterfactual queue states under alternative capacity or routing rules, and decision-quality ground truth. Because `risk_score` is imperfect and case mix differs, the observed flag contrast is not a clean causal effect.

The most useful next evidence would come from a limited randomized pilot of P2 for medium-risk flagged cases, with queue guardrails and audits. It separates the cost of mandatory review occurrence from priority triage while allowing measurement of quality and spillovers.
