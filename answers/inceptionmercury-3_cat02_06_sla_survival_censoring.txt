# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Assumptions I use throughout:

- **A1.** Time origin is `Submit`, so \(t=0\) is the submission moment.
- **A2.** The SLA horizon is fixed at \(t=72\) hours.
- **A3.** The primary success event is internal `Resolve`. `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are observed terminal states that prevent internal `Resolve` from being observed.
- **A4.** In the primary analysis, `Transfer` is treated as a competing terminal event. I relax this in sensitivity analyses because the eventual external outcome is unknown.

The phrase “probability of breaching the SLA” is ambiguous. I define several usable estimands:

- **E1 — Cumulative incidence of internal Resolve by 72 hours:**
  \[
  F_R(72)=P(\text{first terminal state is Resolve and occurs by }72).
  \]
  This is the SLA-compliance estimand. The complement, \(1-F_R(72)\), is the probability that the case has not achieved internal Resolve by 72 hours, including cases that are still open or have already reached another terminal state.

- **E2 — Probability of still being active, without any terminal disposition, at 72 hours:**
  \[
  S_A(72)=P(\text{no terminal event by }72),
  \]
  where terminal events are `Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and, under the primary transfer treatment, `Transfer`. This estimand is most useful for backlog and workload planning.

- **E3 — Conditional probability of late resolution after 72 hours:**
  \[
  L_{72}(h)=P(T\le 72+h,\ K=\text{Resolve}\mid T>72),
  \]
  where \(T\) is the time to first terminal event and \(K\) is the terminal cause. This is a landmark estimand for cases that are still active at 72 hours, and it supports post-breach escalation decisions.

- **E4 — Cause-specific cumulative incidences at 72 hours:**
  \[
  F_W(72),\ F_J(72),\ F_T(72)
  \]
  for `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`. These help explain why cases are not resolved on time.

Concrete contributions:

- **Case resolved at 76 hours, e.g. S03:** Under E1, it is not resolved by 72; it contributes to risk sets before 72 and then contributes a Resolve event at 76. Under E2, it is still active at 72. Under E3, it is in the landmark risk set at 72 and resolves 4 hours later.
- **Case open at 92 hours, e.g. S09:** Under E1, it is known to be unresolved at 72 and is right-censored at 92. Under E2, it is active at 72. Under E3, it is in the landmark risk set and is censored at 92.
- **Case withdrawn at 27 hours, e.g. S06:** Under E1, withdrawal is a competing terminal event; the case is not resolved by 72 and is not treated as ordinary censoring. Under E2, it is not active at 72. Under E3, it is not eligible because it had a terminal event before 72.
- **Case transferred at 68 hours, e.g. S08:** Under the primary transfer-as-competing treatment, it contributes a Transfer event at 68 and is not resolved by 72. Under a transfer-as-censoring sensitivity, it is right-censored at 68 and its status at 72 is unknown. Under a transfer-exclusion sensitivity, it is removed from the analysis.

Right censoring, competing events, and already-observed breaches are different:

- **Open cases are right-censored.** For example, S15 is open at 35 hours; it is censored before the horizon and cannot be classified at 72. S09 is open at 92 hours; it is censored after the horizon and is known to be unresolved at 72.
- **Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer are competing terminal events** under the primary estimand, not censoring, because they end the internal process before `Resolve`.
- **Already-observed breaches** include cases that resolve after 72, such as S03 at 76, S13 at 110, S20 at 74, and S28 at 88. These are not censored at 72; they are observed events after the horizon.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The table below gives the primary encoding for E1 and E2, plus sensitivity alternatives.

| observed_status | E1 cause code | E2 terminal_event | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| `resolved` | 1 = Resolve | 1 | no censoring | If duration > 72, it is an observed breach but still a Resolve event at its observed time. |
| `rejected` | 2 = Reject | 1 | no censoring | Could be treated as censoring if one only wants time-to-Resolve, but this is not recommended because Reject is an observed absorbing state. |
| `withdrawn` | 3 = Withdraw | 1 | no censoring | Same sensitivity option as Reject. |
| `transferred` | 4 = Transfer under T1; 0 under T2; excluded under T3 | 1 under T1; not well-defined under T2; excluded under T3 | Under T1, no censoring; under T2, censor at transfer time; under T3, remove case. | T1: competing terminal event. T2: independent right-censoring. T3: exclude transferred cases. T4: worst-case/best-case bounds using external-success assumptions. |
| `open` | 0 | 0 | Right-censor at `duration_or_age_h`. | If open age < 72, it is censored before the horizon. If open age > 72, it is known to be unresolved at 72 but is still right-censored at its cutoff age. |

Three explicit ways to treat `Transfer`:

- **T1 — Transfer as a competing terminal event.** This is the primary treatment. Transfer is absorbing in the internal process, and the eventual external outcome is unknown. It best matches the question “did this case reach internal Resolve before 72 hours?”
- **T2 — Transfer as independent right-censoring.** This is useful if the eventual external resolution may count as success and if one is willing to assume transfer is unrelated to the unobserved internal resolution process. That assumption is not credible from the supplied data, so I use it only as a sensitivity.
- **T3 — Exclude transferred cases.** This avoids making an assumption about transfer but reduces the sample and is biased if transfers are not missing completely at random.
- **T4 — Extreme bounds.** For a binary “resolved by 72” endpoint, assign every transfer either as a success or as a failure by 72. The two resulting estimates bracket the possible answer. The data cannot identify a point estimate for the external-success interpretation.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A completed-case analysis that uses only `resolved` rows and excludes open patients is biased for several reasons.

- **Survivor bias.** Open cases are dropped. Among them, S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open beyond 72 hours and are known to be unresolved at 72. Dropping them makes the sample look more successful than the actual process. S15, S24, S29, and S31 are open before 72 hours; they are legitimate censored observations, not missing data.
- **Informative censoring.** Several open cases have long inactivity gaps at cutoff: S09 gap = 31 h, S11 gap = 55 h, S25 gap = 48 h, S26 gap = 72 h. These cases are not exchangeable with cases that resolved with zero gap. Standard non-informative censoring would understate the tail risk if these stalled cases are more likely to fail or to remain unresolved.
- **Competing risks.** Cases like S06 withdrawn at 27, S12 withdrawn at 15, S14 rejected at 63, S22 withdrawn at 44, and S30 withdrawn at 70 are no longer at risk of internal Resolve after their terminal event. Treating them as ordinary censoring would encode them as though they could still resolve later, which is false for the internal process.
- **Transfer as a special competing risk.** S08, S21, and S32 are transferred. If the analysis simply censors them, it assumes that transfer is independent of outcome and that external resolution is known or irrelevant. The data do not support that assumption.
- **Cohort imbalance.** Cohort B contains more P1 cases than cohort A: 5 versus 3. Cohort B also has more paths beginning with `AutoCheck`. Because the policy rollout was not randomized and compliance was incomplete, any crude cohort difference is confounded by priority, path, and unmeasured rollout compliance.
- **Open cases below versus above 72 hours.** S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are open before 72 hours; they are censored before the horizon and cannot be classified at 72. S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open after 72 hours; they are known to be unresolved at 72. A binary completed-case analysis would incorrectly treat all open cases in the same way, losing the distinction between “not yet followed to 72” and “known to have passed 72 without resolution.”

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## Primary analysis

I recommend the **Aalen-Johansen estimator of cumulative incidence functions** as the primary method.

For cause \(k\), the estimator is

\[
\hat F_k(t)=\sum_{t_j\le t}\frac{d_{kj}}{n_j}\hat S(t_{j-1}),
\]

where:

- \(t_j\) are observed terminal event times;
- \(n_j\) is the number of cases still event-free just before \(t_j\);
- \(d_{kj}\) is the number of terminal events of cause \(k\) at \(t_j\);
- \(\hat S(t)\) is the Kaplan-Meier estimate of survival free of any terminal event.

This estimator is appropriate because:

- It handles right-censored open cases without dropping them.
- It handles competing terminal events without assuming they are independent censoring.
- It produces the cumulative incidence estimand E1 directly.
- It can be stratified by priority and cohort.
- It can be bootstrapped to obtain confidence intervals, which is important with only 32 cases.

Primary outputs:

- \(\hat F_R(72)\), the estimated probability of internal Resolve by 72 hours.
- \(1-\hat F_R(72)\), the estimated probability of not being resolved by 72 hours.
- \(\hat S_A(72)\), the estimated probability of still being active at 72 hours.
- Cause-specific cumulative incidences for `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and `Transfer`.
- Stratified estimates by cohort and priority.

## Secondary analyses

- **Landmarking at 72 hours.** For operational decisions after the deadline, restrict to cases still active at 72 and estimate the probability of subsequent Resolve within a window \(h\). This directly estimates E3. The risk set at 72 is small in the supplied data, so this should be treated as exploratory.
- **Multi-state route analysis.** Describe the process as a multi-state model with transient stages and absorbing terminal states. This is conceptually the best process-aware representation, but exact inter-event timestamps are missing, so a full multi-state model cannot be fitted from the supplied extract. I therefore use it as a descriptive secondary analysis, not as the primary estimator.

## Method comparisons

- **Kaplan-Meier** is not recommended for the primary `Resolve` endpoint because it treats non-Resolve terminal events as censoring. That requires the strong and implausible assumption that `Reject`, `Withdraw`, and `Transfer` are independent of the future `Resolve` process.
- **Cause-specific hazards** are useful for understanding the rate of each terminal event among cases currently at risk, but they do not directly give the cumulative incidence of an event. I would use them as a diagnostic, not as the primary SLA metric.
- **Subdistribution hazards** are useful if one wants a regression model for the cumulative incidence of `Resolve` directly. With 32 cases and confounding by priority and cohort, I would not use it as the primary approach. It could be a sensitivity if more data are collected.
- **Restricted mean time** can supplement the analysis by estimating the mean active time within the first 72 hours, for example \(\int_0^{72} S_A(u)\,du\). It is useful for workload but less directly tied to the SLA threshold.
- **Multi-state models** are the most process-aware, but they are data-hungry and require timestamps for transitions. I recommend them as the next-step analysis after richer event data become available.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The `prefix` field is an ordered sequence of activities, not a set of timestamps. That constrains how much process information can be used safely.

| Feature | Derivation | Type | Leakage / timing risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| `cohort` | supplied | baseline | Safe, but non-randomized policy exposure. |
| `priority` | supplied | baseline | Safe as a baseline characteristic. |
| First activity after Submit | first element after `Submit` in prefix | early time-varying | Known only after the first event; not safe as a t=0 baseline. |
| Current stage | last activity seen so far | time-varying | Safe only if evaluated as of the prediction time. |
| Review count | number of `Review` occurrences | time-varying cumulative | Not baseline; must be updated over time. |
| `rework_count` | occurrences of `Rework` | time-varying cumulative | Not baseline; occurs after case start. |
| `escalated` | any `Escalate` activity | time-varying post-treatment | Not baseline; using it as a baseline covariate creates immortal-time bias. |
| `last_event_gap_h` | supplied at cutoff or terminal event | snapshot, ideally time-varying | Cannot be reconstructed for every past time from the supplied data. |
| Full variant / path pattern | complete prefix | post-hoc | Leakage if used to predict an outcome earlier than the end of the prefix. |

**Immortal-time and leakage risks.**

- `Escalate` occurs after `Submit`. If `escalated` is used as a baseline covariate, then cases that escalate later are coded as exposed for the entire follow-up, including the period before escalation. This is immortal-time bias.
- `rework_count` has the same problem: a case cannot be in “rework” before the `Rework` activity occurs.
- A full prefix variant, such as `Submit>Screen>Review>Rework>Review>Escalate>Resolve`, is only known at the end of the case or at cutoff. It cannot be used as a predictor at time 0 or at an earlier landmark without leaking future information.

**Proposed state-transition diagram.**

A text version of the multi-state process is:

- `Submitted`  `Screened` via `Screen`
- `Submitted`  `AutoChecked` via `AutoCheck`
- `AutoChecked`  `Screened` via `Screen`
- `Screened`  `AutoChecked` via `AutoCheck`
- `Screened`  `Reviewed` via `Review`
- `Reviewed`  `Rework` via `Rework`
- `Rework`  `Reviewed` via `Review`
- `Reviewed`  `Escalated` via `Escalate`
- `Escalated`  `Reviewed` via `Review`
- `Reviewed`  `Resolved` via `Resolve`
- `Reviewed`  `Rejected` via `Reject`
- `Reviewed`  `Withdrawn` via `Withdraw`
- `Escalated`  `Resolved` via `Resolve`
- `Escalated`  `Transferred` via `Transfer`

This diagram is not fully connected for every observed prefix; for example, S29 is `Submit>AutoCheck>Escalate>Review`, so `AutoChecked` can transition directly to `Escalated`, and S18 is `Submit>AutoCheck>Escalate>Resolve`.

Stage hazards, such as the rate of `Review  Escalate` or `Escalate  Transfer`, cannot be estimated reliably from the current extract because event timestamps are not supplied. The prefix only gives the order, not the waiting time between activities.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least three sensitivity analyses are needed because the primary estimand depends on assumptions about transfer, competing events, and informative censoring.

| Analysis | Treatment of transfer | Treatment of withdraw/reject | Quantity of interest | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | Competing terminal event (T1) | Competing terminal events | \(F_R(72)\), \(1-F_R(72)\), \(S_A(72)\) | Internal SLA compliance. |
| Sensitivity 1 | Independent censoring at transfer (T2) | Competing terminal events | \(F_R(72)\) | Assumes external resolution may count and is uninformatively censored. |
| Sensitivity 2 | Exclude transferred cases (T3) | Competing terminal events | \(F_R(72)\) among non-transferred | Checks sensitivity to dropping transfer cases. |
| Sensitivity 3 | Competing terminal event | Treat withdraw/reject as ordinary censoring | \(F_R(72)\) | Approximates a naive survival analysis; should show the impact of ignoring competing risks. |
| Sensitivity 4 | Worst-case/best-case bounds | Competing terminal events | lower and upper bounds for “resolved by 72” | The truth is between treating all transfers as failures and all transfers as external successes. |

**Qualitative conclusions that could reverse.**

- **P1 performance is fragile.** P1 cases include 2 resolved, 3 transferred, and 3 open in the supplied data. If transfers are actually external successes, P1’s apparent SLA performance could improve substantially. If transfers are internal failures, P1 looks worse.
- **Cohort effects are fragile.** Cohort B has more P1 cases and more `AutoCheck`-first prefixes. Because assignment was non-randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, any observed cohort advantage or disadvantage could be caused by priority mix, process changes, or unmeasured compliance, not by the policy itself.
- **Tail-risk conclusions are fragile.** Open cases with long gaps may later resolve, be withdrawn, or be rejected. If long-gap open cases are eventually withdrawn, censoring them as open understates the probability of no terminal disposition. If they eventually resolve, censoring them understates late resolution.
- **Competing-risk conclusions are fragile.** If withdraw/reject are considered valid administrative closures rather than failures, then the “not resolved by 72” estimand may be too pessimistic for cases that close early through those routes.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival model outputs should be used as risk estimates, not as causal effects.

- **Alerts.** For an active case at age \(t\), estimate the probability that it will not be resolved by 72. If that probability exceeds an operational threshold, raise an alert. For example, a case that is already open at 60 hours with a long inactivity gap should be flagged more strongly than one with recent activity.
- **Workload actions.** Use E2 to estimate the expected number of cases still active at 72 hours. If \(\hat S_A(72)\) is the probability that a case has no terminal event by 72, then the expected open backlog is the sum of these probabilities over all cases. This supports staffing and capacity planning.
- **SLA governance.** Compare the realized cumulative incidence of `Resolve` with the predicted \(\hat F_R(72)\) in rolling time windows. Calibration plots can show whether the model is systematically over- or under-predicting breaches. Because the extract lacks calendar dates, this should be implemented after adding cutoff/start dates.
- **Calibration and fairness checks.** Check calibration separately by priority and cohort. For example, compare predicted and observed \(F_R(72)\) within P1, P2, and P3. A model that is well calibrated overall but poorly calibrated for P1 would be unsafe for triage. Priority is a business rule, not a protected demographic, but the alert system should not create systematic false-confidence for any priority class.
- **Do not present risk scores as causal effects.** The fact that a case is escalated is itself a process decision. Escalated cases may look riskier because escalation is triggered by risk. Changing the escalation policy will not necessarily change the predicted risk in the same way as the model suggests.

The code below is self-contained. It constructs the supplied table, encodes estimands and transfer scenarios, computes an Aalen-Johansen-style cumulative incidence table without dropping open cases, performs a stratified comparison, and builds a PM4Py-compatible event log from the prefixes. # 2. Encode estimands and transfer-treatment scenarios
```python
# ------------------------------------------------------------
def encode_scenario(frame, transfer_handling):
    d = frame.copy()
    d["is_open"] = d["status"] == "open"
    d["is_resolved"] = d["status"] == "resolved"
    d["is_rejected"] = d["status"] == "rejected"
    d["is_withdrawn"] = d["status"] == "withdrawn"
    d["is_transferred"] = d["status"] == "transferred"

    if transfer_handling == "competing":
        d["terminal_event"] = ~d["is_open"]
        d["transfer_event"] = d["is_transferred"].astype(int)
        d["censor"] = d["is_open"].astype(int)
        d["cause"] = np.select(
            [d["is_resolved"], d["is_rejected"], d["is_withdrawn"], d["is_transferred"]],
            [1, 2, 3, 4],
            default=0
        )
    elif transfer_handling == "censor":
        d["terminal_event"] = d["is_resolved"] | d["is_rejected"] | d["is_withdrawn"]
        d["transfer_event"] = 0
        d["censor"] = (d["is_open"] | d["is_transferred"]).astype(int)
        d["cause"] = np.select(
            [d["is_resolved"], d["is_rejected"], d["is_withdrawn"]],
            [1, 2, 3],
            default=0
        )
    elif transfer_handling == "exclude":
        d = d[~d["is_transferred"]].copy()
        d["terminal_event"] = d["is_resolved"] | d["is_rejected"] | d["is_withdrawn"]
        d["transfer_event"] = 0
        d["censor"] = d["is_open"].astype(int)
        d["cause"] = np.select(
            [d["is_resolved"], d["is_rejected"], d["is_withdrawn"]],
            [1, 2, 3],
            default=0
        )
    else:
        raise ValueError("Unknown transfer handling")

    d["event_resolve"] = d["is_resolved"].astype(int)
    return d

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence / survival table
# ------------------------------------------------------------
def aj_cif(data, cause_of_interest=1):
    data = data.sort_values("dur").reset_index(drop=True)
    prev_s = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    out = []

    for t in sorted(data["dur"].unique()):
        at_risk = int((data["dur"] >= t).sum())
        if at_risk == 0:
            continue

        d_all = int(((data["dur"] == t) & data["terminal_event"]).sum())
        d_k = int(((data["dur"] == t) & (data["cause"] == cause_of_interest)).sum())

        s = prev_s * (1.0 - d_all / at_risk) if d_all > 0 else prev_s

        if d_k > 0:
            cif += (d_k / at_risk) * prev_s

        out.append({
            "time": t,
            "n_risk": at_risk,
            "d_all_terminal": d_all,
            "d_cause": d_k,
            "surv_no_terminal": s,
            "cif_cause": cif,
        })

        prev_s = s

    return pd.DataFrame(out)

def cif_at_time(data, cause=1, horizon=72.0):
    tab = aj_cif(data, cause)
    past = tab[tab["time"] <= horizon]
    if past.empty:
        return 0.0, tab
    return float(past.iloc[-1]["cif_cause"]), tab

def surv_no_terminal_at_time(data, horizon=72.0):
    tab = aj_cif(data, cause=1)
    past = tab[tab["time"] <= horizon]
    if past.empty:
        return 1.0, tab
    return float(past.iloc[-1]["surv_no_terminal"]), tab

print("Primary transfer treatment: transfer as competing terminal event")
print(f"E1: P(Resolve by 72) = {e1_resolve_by_72:.3f}")
print(f"E1 complement: P(not resolved by 72) = {1 - e1_resolve_by_72:.3f}")
print(f"E2: P(active / no terminal event at 72) = {e2_active_at_72:.3f}")
print(f"Cases observed beyond 72 h (known unresolved at 72): {int((primary['dur'] > 72).sum())}")

    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    print(f"\nPM4Py variant count: {len(variants)}")

    top_variants = sorted(variants.items(), key=lambda kv: len(kv[1]), reverse=True)[:5]
    for variant, traces in top_variants:
        variant_str = ">".join(variant) if isinstance(variant, tuple) else str(variant)
        print(f"  {variant_str}: {len(traces)} traces")

    auto_first_count = 0
    for trace in log:
        activities = [event["concept:name"] for event in trace]
        if "AutoCheck" in activities and "Screen" in activities:
            if activities.index("AutoCheck") < activities.index("Screen"):
                auto_first_count += 1

    print(f"Control-flow feature: AutoCheck before Screen count = {auto_first_count}")

    try:
        prefixes = pm4py.get_prefixes_from_log(log)
        print(f"PM4Py prefix extraction count: {len(prefixes)}")
    except Exception:
        print("PM4Py prefix extraction not available; variant count used instead.")

except Exception as exc:
    print(f"\nPM4Py unavailable; using manual variant fallback. Reason: {exc}")
    manual_variants = Counter(tuple(r["prefix"].split(">")) for _, r in df.iterrows())
    print(f"Manual variant count: {len(manual_variants)}")
```

The code does not require a survival library. If `lifelines` is available, it could be used to cross-check the manual Aalen-Johansen estimates; the manual implementation above is the fallback.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The most important missing data, in priority order:

1. **Exact event timestamps.** The prefixes give activity order, but not the time of each activity. Without timestamps, I cannot estimate stage hazards, time-varying covariates, current stage at a landmark, or a full multi-state model.
2. **External outcomes after transfer.** Transfer cases stop being observed internally. The eventual external resolution is unknown, so the transfer treatment is fundamentally unidentified without external follow-up.
3. **Start dates and cutoff dates.** The extract is frozen at one cutoff, but calendar dates are not supplied. Calendar-time effects, seasonality, policy rollout timing, and cohort overlap cannot be assessed.
4. **Per-case policy exposure and rollout compliance.** Cohort B began after the policy change, but assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete. A binary cohort indicator is a poor substitute for actual exposure to the automated-screening and escalation-policy changes.
5. **Time-varying workload and resource availability.** Delays may be caused by staffing or queue load, not only by process path.
6. **More case-level covariates.** Application type, customer/market segment, intake channel, and other baseline attributes would help reduce confounding in priority and cohort comparisons.
7. **Longitudinal activity-gap data.** `last_event_gap_h` is only a snapshot at cutoff or terminal event. A time-updated gap variable would substantially improve informative-censoring sensitivity analyses.
8. **More observations overall.** With 32 cases, confidence intervals will be wide, subgroup estimates by priority and cohort will be unstable, and complex models will overfit. The analyses in this response should be treated as exploratory decision support, not definitive inference.
