# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let time zero be each case’s `Submit`, and let the SLA horizon be τ = 72 elapsed hours. For a resolved case, the recorded duration is its time to `Resolve`; for an open case it is its age at the common extract cutoff. A terminal non-resolution (`Withdraw`, `Reject`, or, for the internal system, `Transfer`) ends the observed internal process. Open cases are administratively right-censored at their recorded ages for outcomes after that age. These are different facts: a resolution after 72 hours is an observed SLA breach, while an open case censored after 72 is known to have passed the deadline without resolution but has an unknown later outcome.

Two useful estimands answer different operational questions:

1. **Resolution success by the deadline:** (F_R(72)=P(T_R\le72,\ T_R\text{ occurs before a competing terminal outcome})), the cumulative incidence of internal `Resolve` by 72 hours from all submitted cases. Withdrawals, rejections, and (for an internal-resolution target) transfers are competing outcomes, not ordinary censoring. Its complement is the probability of *no internal Resolve by 72*, which includes both cases still active at 72 and cases that reached a different terminal outcome first. This is appropriate for a submitted-case service outcome, provided the denominator and treatment of exits are explicit.
2. **Active, unresolved occupancy at 72:** (P(X(72)\text{ is an active, non-resolved process state})). This excludes cases already withdrawn, rejected, or transferred before 72, and includes cases that later resolve or transfer if they were still active at hour 72. It measures deadline backlog and is useful for staffing and intervention decisions. It is not the complement of (F_R(72)).

A third useful quantity is the **late-resolution probability among cases still active and unresolved at 72**, over a stated follow-up window (for example, resolve by 120 hours, before another terminal outcome). This is a 72-hour landmark estimand; it measures recoverability after a breach, not on-time performance. This extract does not supply a common follow-up horizon beyond 72, so it should be reported only for a specified follow-up window and with censoring handled.

Examples under the first two estimands: S03 resolves at 76, so it is a known failure to resolve by 72 and contributes a `Resolve` event at 76 to a time-to-resolution analysis; it was active and unresolved at hour 72. S09 is open at age 92, so it is known to have been active and unresolved at 72, contributes to the risk set through 72, and is right-censored at 92 for later outcomes. S06 withdraws at 27, so it has no internal resolution by 72 and contributes a competing `Withdraw` event at 27 to (F_R); it is not active backlog at 72. S08 transfers at 68: for the internal-process occupancy target it leaves the active internal state at 68, but whether its external handling resolves by 72 is unknown. A different external-service estimand must treat that uncertainty explicitly.

For the active-occupancy interpretation, observed cases whose terminal event is after 72 (including S13, S20, S28, and S32) were still unresolved and active at 72. Open cases with age at least 72 (S09, S11, S25, S26) are also known to be active then. Open cases censored before 72 (for example, S10 at 47 and S24 at 67) have unknown status at hour 72 and cannot simply be counted as either successes or breaches.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use elapsed hours from `Submit`; event time is the supplied duration for terminal rows and censoring time is the supplied age for open rows. For an internal-resolution cumulative-incidence analysis, use `Resolve` as the event of interest and retain distinct competing causes. “Censoring rule” below refers to follow-up after the observed time, not to deleting the row.

| Observed status | Resolve event indicator | Competing-risk code for internal resolution | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | 1 at recorded duration | `Resolve` | No censoring at that event; if time >72, it is an observed late resolution | Keep as a known SLA breach when >72; never recode it as censored merely because it missed the deadline |
| `withdrawn` | 0 | `Withdraw` at recorded duration | No censoring at withdrawal for a process that treats withdrawal as absorbing | Treat as a competing outcome (preferred for submitted-case outcomes); separately report as SLA non-success; censoring here would require a hypothetical post-withdrawal resolution estimand and strong independent-censoring assumptions |
| `rejected` | 0 | `Reject` at recorded duration | No censoring at rejection for an absorbing rejection | Same alternatives as withdrawal; report rejection separately because it may represent screening rather than service delay |
| `transferred` | 0 for *internal* `Resolve` | `Transfer` at transfer time if transfer ends the internal service; keep it separate from `Withdraw`/`Reject` | No censoring under the internal absorbing-state definition | (a) Competing transfer event for internal resolution; (b) right-censor at transfer for a hypothetical resolution outcome that could continue externally, assuming conditional independent transfer censoring; (c) count transfer as operational non-success / an SLA-exit cause, or exclude it from an explicitly transfer-ineligible denominator. For external end-to-end success, show bounds or obtain the external outcome; do not assume transfer itself is resolution. |
| `open` | 0 through observed age | None | Administrative right-censor at age at cutoff; retain in every risk set up to that age | If age is at least 72, its no-resolution status at 72 is known even though later outcome is censored. If age <72, its deadline status is unknown. Examine informative cutoff/censoring sensitivity. |

For the all-submitted “no internal Resolve by 72” outcome, (1-F_R(72)) counts competing exits before 72 as no resolution by the deadline. That outcome should not be described as “active SLA breach”: early rejection or withdrawal is not active backlog, and whether such cases are SLA-eligible is a governance definition. Transfer is especially sensitive: S08 and S21 transfer before 72, whereas S32 transfers at 96 and is known to have remained unresolved in the internal process through 72.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Resolved-only analysis conditions on eventual observed resolution. It drops open cases and all competing exits, even though those outcomes are associated with process duration and policy. Among observed resolved cases only, cohort A has 4 on-time resolves out of 6 (S01, S02, S04, S07 on time; S03 and S13 late); cohort B has 5 out of 7 (S16, S17, S18, S19, S27 on time; S20 and S28 late). The resulting 4/6 versus 5/7 comparison (about 67% versus 71%) describes only completed resolves. It is not an estimate of either submitted-case on-time probability or active backlog, and the small denominators make it unstable.

Open cases are not all equivalent. S09 (92 hours) and S11 (81) are known unresolved at the deadline; they crossed 72 without a terminal event and remain censored only for what happens later. S10 (47) and S15 (35) were censored before the deadline, so their SLA outcome is unknown. Similarly, in B, S25 (79) and S26 (104) are known unresolved at 72, but S24 (67), S29 (51), and S31 (23) have not yet been observed to the horizon. Dropping every open case loses known breaches; counting every open case as a breach incorrectly labels those censored early.

Transfers create potentially informative loss of internal observation. S08 leaves at 68, S21 at 26, and S32 at 96. Treating these as independent censoring assumes that, conditional on modeled information, transferring a case says nothing about its counterfactual resolution prospects. That is doubtful if difficult cases, capacity constraints, or escalation policy drive transfer. Conversely, declaring transfer a failure for an end-to-end service assumes no timely external resolution. These assumptions can change the cohort comparison.

Withdrawal and rejection are competing outcomes, not routine independent censoring. S06 withdraws at 27 and S05 is rejected at 9; neither can later have an internal `Resolve` in the observed process. A Kaplan–Meier calculation that censors these exits estimates a hypothetical net resolution risk under removal of competing outcomes, not the real-world probability of resolution before another disposition. It will generally overstate the observed-world resolution probability when it treats competing exits as if they could later resolve.

Cohort B is not randomized and differs in composition: it has 5 P1 cases among 17 versus 3 among 15 in A. P1 cases also have visibly different paths and outcomes, including transfers and long-open cases. Cohort membership is a proxy for calendar period and a policy rollout with incomplete compliance, not a clean treatment indicator. AutoCheck appears in some A paths (S01, S15) and is absent from some B paths (for example S20, S23, S30); `cohort` cannot be equated to receipt of the changed workflow. A crude cohort contrast is confounded by priority, case mix, time, and policy uptake.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Primary:** report the Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence of internal `Resolve` by 72 hours, with `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` as separate competing causes. This estimates the all-submitted internal-resolution success probability while retaining exits as observed outcomes. Pair it with the multi-state probability of occupying any active, unresolved state at hour 72 for workload planning. With only 32 cases, show raw event counts, risk-set counts, and uncertainty intervals; do not imply precise subgroup estimates. Stratify descriptively by cohort and priority, and avoid a causal reading of cohort differences.

**Secondary:** estimate cause-specific hazards for `Resolve` and each exit, with active cases at risk until their first terminal event. They can show where in elapsed time resolution or exit occurs and support a process-aware model. A cause-specific hazard is conditional on remaining event-free and is not itself the probability of resolution by 72. If an absolute probability is needed, combine cause-specific hazards into cumulative incidence or use Aalen–Johansen. A Fine–Gray subdistribution-hazard model can regress the resolution CIF while retaining competing-event cases in a modified risk set, but its hazard ratio is less operationally direct and the small sample, limited events, and nonrandom cohort make it a poor primary model here.

A standard Kaplan–Meier curve for time to `Resolve` is appropriate only if the target is a hypothetical net survival distribution and competing outcomes are censored under a defensible conditional-independence assumption. It is not the primary estimator for actual-world resolution probability in the presence of withdrawals, rejections, and transfers. Restricted mean time can summarize expected time without resolution up to a fixed horizon (for example 72), but must define whether competing exits count as no resolution, remain as separate states, or are censored. It can complement the deadline probability and be easier to interpret than a hazard ratio.

A 72-hour landmark analysis is suitable for the conditional question “among cases active and unresolved at 72, what happens next?” Use only information observed by the landmark and define a later horizon such as 120 hours. Cases with an observed terminal outcome before 72 are outside that landmark risk set; cases censored before 72 have unknown eligibility and require censoring assumptions or bounds. This extract is too small for reliable rich landmark regression.

A multi-state model best represents the process: active stages can progress, revisit review, rework, escalate, resolve, or exit. It estimates transition probabilities and state occupancy, distinguishes a late resolve from an early exit, and supports queue questions. The supplied prefixes do not include timestamps for intermediate activities, so only the terminal-time competing-risk analysis can be timed now. A real stage-hazard model needs event timestamps. For a small-sample report, use nonparametric Aalen–Johansen summaries and process-path counts first; treat fitted regression models as exploratory.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Prefixes support control-flow features but not stage durations. Candidate features include initial path (`AutoCheck` before or after `Screen`), current last observed activity for open cases, whether `Review` has been repeated, count of `Rework`, whether `Escalate` has occurred, path sequence/variant, and `last_event_gap_h`. `rework_count` and the observed prefix can validate one another. For open cases, a long gap may indicate a stalled case; it may also reflect delayed logging or a scheduled wait. For terminal rows, `last_event_gap_h` is zero by construction, so it cannot be used as a comparable predictor of time to that terminal event.

Separate feature timing carefully:

- Baseline candidates available at `Submit`: priority, cohort/calendar period, and any genuinely pre-existing case attributes. Cohort is a policy-period proxy, not randomized assignment.
- Time-varying process history: current stage, prior review count, rework, escalation, and time since last event. These change after start and should enter a start–stop or multi-state model only from the time they become observed. At a prediction landmark, they can be used if known by that landmark.
- Post-treatment variables: AutoCheck use, escalation, and rework may be consequences or mediators of the policy. Adjusting for them in a cohort-effect analysis can remove part of the policy pathway or induce selection; decide whether the goal is prediction or a causal total effect.
- Leakage/immortal-time risks: `escalated=yes` is an “ever escalated” summary. A case must remain in process until escalation to acquire that label. Treating it as a baseline covariate gives escalated cases guaranteed event-free time before escalation and uses future information. The same issue applies to ever-reworked and final path labels. Use time-varying indicators with actual activity times, or define a landmark after the relevant activity. No event-time ordering beyond the sequence and no activity timestamps should be invented.

An appropriate conceptual state graph is:

Proposed transitions in text: `Submit -> Screen` or `AutoCheck`; `AutoCheck -> Screen` (and the observed ordering variant `Screen -> AutoCheck`); `Screen/AutoCheck -> Review`; `Review -> Review` (repeat), `Rework -> Review`, or `Escalate`; `Escalate -> Review`; active `Screen`, `Review`, or `Escalate -> Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer` where supported by the observed paths. `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are terminal states for the internal log.

This is a proposed abstraction of the observed paths, not a claim that every arrow is present or that any transition duration is known. The graph allows AutoCheck and Screen ordering variants; stage hazards require timestamps for transitions and time-varying covariates.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

1. **Transfer assumptions.** Primary internal-service analysis: Transfer is a distinct competing terminal cause. Refit with transfer right-censored at its observed time to estimate a hypothetical resolution process continuing beyond handoff; state the strong conditional independent-censoring assumption. Also report an operational non-success version in which transfer counts as failure to achieve internal resolution, and, for an end-to-end SLA, give optimistic/pessimistic bounds for transferred cases rather than impute an external event time. If difficult cases are preferentially transferred, the censoring version may look too favorable; if transfers rapidly obtain external resolution, counting all as failure may look too unfavorable. The direction is not identified here.
2. **Withdrawal/rejection definition.** Keep them as separate competing terminal outcomes for the submitted-case resolution CIF. Compare with a governance KPI that counts them as non-success, and with an explicitly labeled eligible-case denominator that excludes pre-deadline exits. Do not silently censor them. The eligible-only result can reverse if one cohort has more early exits, because removing those cases changes both denominator and case mix. S05/S06/S12/S14 in A and S22/S23/S30 in B show these exits are present in both cohorts.
3. **Administrative and potentially informative censoring.** Compare standard administrative censoring at open-case age with horizon bounds: open cases censored before 72 can be unresolved at 72 or resolve by 72 after the cutoff; they are unknown. Use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights only if cutoff/start dates and predictors of follow-up are available and the positivity/model assumptions are credible. Report sensitivity to censoring related to long inactivity gaps, with care because gaps may mean either stalled work or missing events. At 72, S10/S15/S24/S29/S31 are examples censored before the horizon; S09/S11/S25/S26 are observed through it.
4. **Policy and case mix.** Show cohort-specific and priority-stratified CIFs/state occupancy, then standardize to a common priority distribution if cell sizes permit. Compare models with and without process variables only for prediction; process variables may mediate policy effects. A causal policy claim would need assignment and compliance data, calendar-time controls, and a credible comparison design. Because B has more P1 cases and incomplete, nonrandom rollout, even the direction of an adjusted cohort association could differ from the resolved-only 67% versus 71% comparison.

Small-cell variation, transfer assumptions, and how early exits are counted could reverse a claim that cohort B improved on-time service. They could also reverse a claim that B has more active backlog: S32 is observed to transfer only at 96 and was active at 72, while early transfers S08 and S21 were not active internal cases at the horizon. Report each endpoint separately rather than compressing them into one “breach probability.”

# 7. Operational interpretation

For daily alerts, estimate the conditional risk that an active case will remain unresolved at 72 using only its history available at alert time. Recompute as the case changes stage; flag a case based on calibrated remaining-time risk, current age, stage, and inactivity gap. An alert threshold should reflect available capacity and the cost of missing an SLA, and should trigger review, queue balancing, or escalation while there is still time. A 72-hour landmark view can identify the active backlog already past deadline and estimate whether late resolution is likely, but it must be labeled as post-breach recovery.

For workload planning, report the number and estimated probability of cases still active at the deadline alongside cumulative incidence of resolution and each terminal exit. Keep internal transfers visible as a separate destination and audit external outcomes. For governance, publish the SLA denominator, eligibility/exemption rules, clock-stop rules, treatment of external transfers, and extract cutoff. Show uncertainty intervals and raw counts, especially for P1 and cohort subgroups.

These are predictive/monitoring quantities, not causal effects. A high-risk score does not show that escalation, AutoCheck, or a policy change caused delay; those actions may target harder cases. Check calibration (observed versus predicted probabilities) by cohort and priority, calibration drift over time, and subgroup error rates and alert rates. Review whether P1 cases receive timely attention and whether missing or stale activity data differ by cohort or priority. With this sample, subgroup checks are descriptive and should not be interpreted as precise fairness guarantees.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

The following self-contained Python uses pandas and a direct Aalen–Johansen calculation, so no survival package is required. The risk-set rows retain open cases. PM4Py is used when installed to construct an event log from activity names only; the fallback counts path variants. No timestamps are fabricated. The third transfer scenario is an operational failure coding; a separate empirical bound illustrates the unknown external outcome without assigning it a made-up event time.

```python
from io import StringIO
from collections import Counter
import pandas as pd

CSV = """case,cohort,priority,duration_or_age_h,observed_status,prefix,escalated,rework_count,last_event_gap_h

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(CSV))
df["duration_or_age_h"] = pd.to_numeric(df["duration_or_age_h"])
df["last_event_gap_h"] = pd.to_numeric(df["last_event_gap_h"])
df["rework_count"] = pd.to_numeric(df["rework_count"])
HORIZON = 72.0

# Three transfer codings: competing internal exit, independent censoring
# (hypothetical external continuation), and operational non-success cause.
def survival_ready(frame, transfer_mode):
    out = frame[["case", "cohort", "priority", "duration_or_age_h",
                 "observed_status"]].copy()
    code = []
    for status in out["observed_status"]:
        if status == "resolved":
            code.append("resolve")
        elif status in ("withdrawn", "rejected"):
            code.append(status)
        elif status == "transferred":
            if transfer_mode == "competing":
                code.append("transfer")
            elif transfer_mode == "censor":
                code.append(None)
            elif transfer_mode == "failure":
                code.append("transfer_sla_failure")
            else:
                raise ValueError(transfer_mode)
        else:  # open: right-censored at observed age, never dropped
            code.append(None)
    out["event_code"] = code
    out["censored"] = out["event_code"].isna()
    out = out.rename(columns={"duration_or_age_h": "time_h"})
    return out

def aj_cif_at(risk_data, cause="resolve", horizon=HORIZON):
    """Aalen-Johansen CIF for one cause; all non-null event codes compete."""
    s = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    event_times = sorted(risk_data.loc[
        risk_data["event_code"].notna() & (risk_data["time_h"] <= horizon),
        "time_h"].unique())
    for t in event_times:
        at_risk = int((risk_data["time_h"] >= t).sum())
        at_t = risk_data[(risk_data["time_h"] == t) &
                         risk_data["event_code"].notna()]
        d_all = len(at_t)
        d_cause = int((at_t["event_code"] == cause).sum())
        if at_risk:
            cif += s * d_cause / at_risk
            s *= 1.0 - d_all / at_risk
    return cif

def event_risk_table(risk_data, horizon=HORIZON):
    rows = []
    times = sorted(risk_data.loc[
        risk_data["event_code"].notna() & (risk_data["time_h"] <= horizon),
        "time_h"].unique())
    for t in times:
        at_t = risk_data[(risk_data["time_h"] == t) &
                         risk_data["event_code"].notna()]
        rows.append({
            "time_h": t,
            "at_risk_just_before": int((risk_data["time_h"] >= t).sum()),
            "events_by_cause": at_t["event_code"].value_counts().to_dict(),
            "censored_at_time": int(((risk_data["time_h"] == t) &
                                      risk_data["censored"]).sum()),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

# Two estimands from the same retained risk rows: CIF(Resolve by 72),
# and its complement, no internal Resolve by 72 (including competing exits).
for mode in ("competing", "censor", "failure"):
    ready = survival_ready(df, mode)
    f72 = aj_cif_at(ready, cause="resolve")
    print(f"transfer={mode}: CIF internal Resolve by 72={f72:.3f}; "
          f"complement/no internal Resolve by 72={1-f72:.3f}")
    print("risk-set/event rows through 72:")
    print(event_risk_table(ready).to_string(index=False))

# Cohort-by-priority stratification; small cells are descriptive only.
primary = survival_ready(df, "competing")
print("\nCohort x priority summaries (all cases retained):")
for (cohort, priority), g in df.groupby(["cohort", "priority"], sort=True):
    rg = survival_ready(g, "competing")
    f72 = aj_cif_at(rg, cause="resolve")
    print({
        "cohort": cohort, "priority": priority, "n": len(g),
        "resolve_CIF_72": round(f72, 3),
        "no_resolve_by_72": round(1 - f72, 3),
        "open_n": int((g["observed_status"] == "open").sum()),
        "transfer_n": int((g["observed_status"] == "transferred").sum()),
    })

# Horizon labels distinguish known event-free-through-72 from early censoring.
def horizon_label(row):
    status, t = row["observed_status"], row["duration_or_age_h"]
    if status == "resolved":
        return "on_time_resolve" if t <= HORIZON else "known_late_resolve"
    if status == "open":
        return "active_at_72" if t >= HORIZON else "censored_before_72_unknown"
    if t < HORIZON:
        return f"{status}_before_72"
    return f"active_at_72_then_{status}"

df["horizon_label"] = df.apply(horizon_label, axis=1)
print("\nObserved horizon labels:")
print(df["horizon_label"].value_counts().to_string())

# Empirical end-to-end external-resolution bound, not a survival estimate:
# early transfers and early-censored opens have unknown by-72 outcomes.
resolved_by72 = int(((df["observed_status"] == "resolved") &
                     (df["duration_or_age_h"] <= HORIZON)).sum())
transfer_before72 = int(((df["observed_status"] == "transferred") &
                         (df["duration_or_age_h"] < HORIZON)).sum())
open_censored_before72 = int(((df["observed_status"] == "open") &
                              (df["duration_or_age_h"] < HORIZON)).sum())
n = len(df)
print("external outcome worst/best crude bounds for on-time resolution:",
      (resolved_by72 / n,
       (resolved_by72 + transfer_before72 + open_censored_before72) / n))

# Process feature: repeated Review and path variants. Construct a PM4Py log
# from activities only. No event timestamp is supplied or invented.
try:
    import pm4py
    from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event
    log = EventLog()
    for row in df.to_dict(orient="records"):
        trace = Trace(attributes={"concept:name": row["case"]})
        for activity in row["prefix"].split(">"):
            trace.append(Event({"concept:name": activity}))
        log.append(trace)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    print("PM4Py trace count:", len(log), "distinct control-flow variants:",
          len(variants), "largest variant frequencies:",
          sorted((len(traces) for traces in variants.values()), reverse=True)[:5])
except ImportError:
    paths = [tuple(p.split(">")) for p in df["prefix"]]
    variants = Counter(paths)
    print("PM4Py unavailable; equivalent path-variant fallback:",
          len(paths), "traces,", len(variants), "distinct variants; top counts:",
          variants.most_common(5))

df["review_visits"] = df["prefix"].str.split(">").map(
    lambda activities: activities.count("Review"))
print("\nControl-flow feature by cohort/status (mean observed Review visits):")
print(df.groupby(["cohort", "observed_status"])["review_visits"].mean().round(2))
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The most important additions are: (1) exact timestamps for `Submit`, every activity, each terminal event, and the observation cutoff, to estimate stage-specific hazards and time-varying escalation/rework effects; (2) external post-transfer outcome, resolution time, clock rules, and responsibility/eligibility definitions; (3) case start dates and the common cutoff date, needed to characterize administrative follow-up and calendar-time changes; (4) time-varying queue size, staffing, workload, and service interruptions; (5) the policy assignment mechanism, rollout dates, actual screening/escalation exposure, and compliance; and (6) relevant baseline case complexity and outcome eligibility. Also needed are logging completeness and the reason a case is open or transferred. Without these, transfer censoring, long inactivity gaps, cohort effects, and policy causality remain uncertain; the small sample further limits adjusted modeling and subgroup precision.
