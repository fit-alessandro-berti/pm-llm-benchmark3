# 2. Integrated Explanation

## The Observed Pattern

Service time falls continuously across the shift, from 18.8 minutes early to 10.8 minutes late. Error rates first decline slightly (3.4%  2.5%) then reverse and climb sharply (2.5%  7.9%). This combination—accelerating throughput with late-emerging quality degradation—is the central puzzle, and it cannot be explained by any single mechanism.

## Why Service Time Falls: A Composite Decline

The service time decline has at least three overlapping drivers:

**Case-mix dilution.** Early-shift events are disproportionately complex (mean complexity 0.72 vs. 0.43 late) and high-priority (41% vs. 19%). Early shifts inherit overnight backlog that includes difficult cases deferred by the night team. Complex cases intrinsically take longer. As the backlog of hard cases is cleared, the remaining queue contains simpler work. Service time falls partly because the *work itself* gets easier, not because workers get faster. If you hold complexity constant, the service time decline is substantially attenuated—though not eliminated.

**Genuine skill execution effects.** Within the same complexity band, there is still a modest service time decline. This is consistent with warm-up: workers become more fluent with the specific patterns of the current shift's workload, internalize queue dynamics, and reduce task-switching overhead. The effect is real but smaller than it appears in unadjusted data. Juniors show a larger raw decline (5.9 min) than seniors (4.1 min), consistent with steeper initial learning curves, but juniors also start with harder average cases and face more selection effects.

**Queue-pressure acceleration.** As WIP builds through the shift (mean queue length rises from 33 to 88), workers adopt time-saving strategies: they batch similar cases, skip discretionary quality checks, spend less time on individual document review, and defer complex judgments. This is not learning—it is effort rationing under load. The high-WIP cells show service times 3–5 minutes shorter than low-WIP cells for the same complexity band, confirming that pressure itself drives speed.

## Why Errors Rise Only Under Certain Conditions

The error rate trajectory is not monotonic because it reflects competing forces:

**Early errors (positions 1–5): moderate (3.4%).** Workers are fresh but facing hard cases. The errors are complexity-driven. Workers are careful but working on unfamiliar ground (new backlog composition).

**Mid-shift errors (positions 16–30): lowest (2.5%).** Workers have warmed up, complexity has declined, and WIP has not yet peaked. This is the sweet spot: skill is up, difficulty is down, and pressure is manageable.

**Late-shift errors (positions 51–75 and 76+): rising (4.6%  7.9%).** Here the mechanism shifts fundamentally. Complexity continues to fall (0.46  0.43), so the case-mix should make errors *easier* to avoid. But WIP has surged (queue 62  88), and workers are making errors on simple cases they would have handled correctly earlier. This is the signature of queue-pressure–driven speed–accuracy tradeoff, not classical fatigue.

**Why not pure fatigue?** If fatigue were dominant, we would expect service time to rise (workers slowing down as they tire) alongside rising error rates. Instead, service time continues to *fall*. Fatigue predicts a U-shaped service time curve; we observe a monotonic decline. Moreover, night staff—who work at the worst circadian time—show lower late-shift error rates (5.1%) than day shift (7.5%), which is the opposite of what a pure circadian-fatigue model predicts. Night shift has lower WIP at shift start (29 vs. 38 for day), suggesting that queue volume, not time of day, is the binding constraint.

**The interaction effect.** The worst performance (10.9% error rate) occurs at the intersection of high WIP, high complexity, and presumably late shift position. This is a multiplicative interaction: pressure degrades quality, complexity amplifies the cost of attentional shortcuts, and the two together produce catastrophic error rates. Neither factor alone explains the magnitude. The resource-experience data shows that juniors under high WIP have 12.4% error rates—six times the low-WIP baseline—while seniors under the same conditions have 4.9%—only twice their baseline. Experience moderates the pressure effect, possibly because experienced workers have more robust heuristics for triaging attention.

## The Batching Complication

Late-shift batching (document review by juniors, deferred quality checks) creates a structural pattern where many short, potentially lower-quality events cluster near end of shift. This inflates the apparent speed improvement and may concentrate errors in batched segments. The error rate increase in late shift positions is partly a *composition* effect: late-shift events are disproportionately batched, and batched events may have higher per-event error rates due to reduced individual attention.

## Summary of the Integrated Mechanism

| Phase | Dominant Mechanism | Service Time | Error Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positions 1–10 | Case-mix (complex backlog) + warm-up | High (18.8 min) | Moderate (3.4%) |
| Positions 10–30 | Warm-up effect + declining complexity | Falling (13.6 min) | Lowest (2.5%) |
| Positions 30–50 | Stable performance, moderate WIP | Low (12.9 min) | Stable (2.7%) |
| Positions 50–75 | Queue pressure rising, batching starting | Very low (11.4 min) | Rising (4.6%) |
| Positions 76+ | Queue pressure dominant, batching widespread | Lowest (10.8 min) | Highest (7.9%) |

The pattern is not a simple fatigue curve. It is a phase transition from a complexity-dominated regime (early shift) to a pressure-dominated regime (late shift), mediated by case-mix selection, worker experience, and strategic workarounds like batching.

## 3.1 Within-Resource Comparisons

**Goal:** Isolate individual-level effects from compositional effects by tracking the same resource across shifts.

**Approach:** For each resource, compute mean service time and error rate by sequence position bin across multiple shifts. Compare a resource's performance when they work early shift vs. late shift. If a resource's error rate rises in position 76+ regardless of which shift they work, the effect is position-driven (likely fatigue or pressure). If it only rises in day shift but not early shift at the same position, the effect is clock-time or WIP-driven.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

# Format the dataframe
log = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id='case:concept:name', activity_key='concept:name',
                              timestamp_key='time:timestamp',
                              start_timestamp_key='start_timestamp')

# For each resource, compute performance by sequence position across shifts
resource_position_perf = log.groupby(['org:resource', 'shift_id', 'sequence_position_within_shift_bin']).agg(
    mean_service_time=('duration', 'mean'),
    error_rate=('error_flag', 'mean'),
    rework_rate=('rework_flag', 'mean'),
    mean_complexity=('case_complexity_score', 'mean'),
    n_events=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()

# Within-resource, cross-shift comparison
# Pivot: for the same resource, same position bin, compare across shifts
within_resource = resource_position_perf.pivot_table(
    index=['org:resource', 'sequence_position_within_shift_bin'],
    columns='shift_id',
    values=['mean_service_time', 'error_rate'],
    aggfunc='mean'
)
```

**Key test:** Resource × shift interaction in a mixed-effects model:

## 3.4 Complexity Adjustment

**Goal:** Decompose the observed service time decline into case-mix and worker-effort components.

```python
# Oaxaca-Blinder-style decomposition
# Compare early-shift events vs. late-shift events
early = log[log['pos_bin'].isin(['1-5', '6-15'])]
late = log[log['pos_bin'].isin(['76+'])]

# Regress service time on complexity separately for early and late
early_model = smf.ols("duration ~ case_complexity_score + priority + activity_family", data=early).fit()
late_model = smf.ols("duration ~ case_complexity_score + priority + activity_family", data=late).fit()

# Predicted service time for late-shift cases using early-shift coefficients
# This gives the "expected" service time if late workers had early-shift behavior
late['predicted_early_behavior'] = early_model.predict(late)

# Gap = composition effect + behavior effect
composition_effect = late['predicted_early_behavior'].mean() - early['duration'].mean()
behavior_effect = late['duration'].mean() - late['predicted_early_behavior'].mean()
print(f"Composition effect: {composition_effect:.2f} min")
print(f"Behavior/pressure effect: {behavior_effect:.2f} min")
```

# 4. Intervention Options

## 4.1 Strictly Cap Shift Length for All Workers

**Expected Benefit:** Reduces exposure to the high-WIP, high-error-rate late-shift period. If positions 76+ are where errors concentrate, a shorter shift eliminates the worst phase.

**Risk:** Reduces total capacity, which may increase queue buildup across shifts, creating *more* high-WIP periods in subsequent shifts. Workers lose earnings if paid hourly. Senior resources who handle complex early cases are disproportionately removed from the system. The underlying demand does not decrease—work simply accumulates.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track mean WIP at shift end across all shifts before and after the change. Monitor queue_length_at_start for the *next* shift. Compare error rates in the remaining positions (should improve if fatigue/pressure is the driver). Watch for rework_flag increases if compressed staffing leads to rushed handoffs.

## 4.2 Move Complex Cases Away from the First Hour of Each Shift

**Expected Benefit:** Reduces the complexity burden during the warm-up period when workers are least fluent. Could reduce early-shift error rates (currently 3.4%) and allow a smoother ramp-up.

**Risk:** Complex cases must go somewhere. Deferring them pushes high-priority work later, when capacity may already be strained. The backlog of complex cases grows, increasing their waiting time and potentially violating SLAs. Complex cases often need senior attention, and seniors may be most available early in shift. This intervention also assumes that complexity is the *cause* of early errors, but early errors may also reflect genuine warm-up—young workers making mistakes on routine tasks.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track waiting time (pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time) for high-complexity cases before and after. Monitor the high-complexity share in each hour of the shift. Compare error rates in the first hour (should decrease) and the second hour (may increase if complexity is deferred there). Use pm4py.filter_case_performance to check if total case cycle time increases.

## 4.3 Add a Queue-Pressure Alert When WIP > 70

**Expected Benefit:** Creates awareness and triggers tactical responses (pulling additional resources, deferring discretionary work, pausing Bot_Risk classifications that need correction). May interrupt the pressurespeederror cascade.

**Risk:** Alerts without actionable responses create alert fatigue. If the alert triggers only when WIP is already >70, the damage has begun. Defining the threshold at 70 is arbitrary—some resources may degrade at 50, others handle 80 fine. If the response to the alert is to pause non-essential work, this pushes work into future periods, increasing inter-shift queue pressure.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track the proportion of events occurring when WIP >70 before and after. Monitor whether error_rate under high WIP changes. Track whether the alert triggers resource reassignment (new org:resource values on cases during high-WIP periods). Use pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph to visualize event density by WIP level.

## 4.4 Force Random Assignment of Cases to Reduce Selection Bias

**Expected Benefit:** Eliminates preferential routing of complex cases to seniors and simple cases to certain positions. Makes performance comparisons fairer. Removes the confound between case-mix and worker ability that currently distorts all performance metrics.

**Risk:** Random assignment ignores expertise matching. Seniors lose their advantage on complex cases; juniors are overwhelmed by cases they cannot handle. Error rates may *increase* overall if the current selective routing, while introducing bias, is also *efficient*. Complex cases assigned to junior staff near end of shift could produce the worst possible outcome (inexperienced worker + hard case + high WIP). Fairness of measurement comes at the cost of operational effectiveness.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track case_complexity_score distribution across resources before and after. Monitor error rates by resource group—juniors' error rates should spike if they receive proportionally more complex cases. Track rework_flag by resource group. Use pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values to compare case mix across resources. Measure overall system error rate (should increase if current routing is efficient).

## 4.5 Introduce Mandatory Micro-Breaks in the Final Two Hours

**Expected Benefit:** If fatigue contributes to late-shift errors, brief rest periods should reduce cognitive depletion. Even 5-minute breaks may restore attention. This directly targets the fatigue mechanism without reducing shift length.

**Risk:** Micro-breaks during high-WIP periods mean the queue continues building while the worker rests. After the break, the worker returns to an even larger queue, potentially increasing pressure. If the real driver is queue pressure rather than fatigue, breaks do not address the root cause. Mandatory breaks may interrupt flow states and batching strategies that workers use to manage complexity. Night staff with fewer total cases may not need breaks.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track service time and error rate in the 10 events before and after each break. Monitor queue_length_at_start for post-break events (should be higher). Compare error rates in the final two hours before and after implementation. Track whether workers voluntarily extend breaks (indicating perceived need) or resist them (indicating they interrupt productive work).

## 4.6 Defer Low-Priority Quality Checks to the Next Shift

**Expected Benefit:** Reduces end-of-shift workload, eliminating the Quality Check burst in the last 45 minutes. Frees capacity for final case closures. May reduce the "rushed quality check" error pattern.

**Risk:** Deferred quality checks mean errors are not caught until the next shift—potentially hours later. If the next shift also has high WIP, the deferred checks may be deferred again, creating a growing backlog. Cases that should be reworked immediately sit in limbo. The quality check function exists for a reason: deferring it means accepting that some errors persist for longer. If the next shift inherits these deferred checks, their first-hour complexity effectively increases, recreating the problem for another team.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track the number of Quality Check events occurring in the last 45 minutes of each shift before and after. Monitor mean waiting time between activity completion and Quality Check. Track rework_flag rates—do they increase if quality checks are delayed? Use pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate to measure whether total case cycle time increases.

## 4.7 Add an Extra Senior Reviewer During Day-Shift Demand Peaks

**Expected Benefit:** Day shift has the highest error rate in the last two hours (7.5%) and the largest queue growth (3875). An additional senior could absorb complex cases, mentor juniors, and reduce WIP before it reaches critical levels. Seniors show the most stable error rates under pressure (4.9% vs. 12.4% for juniors).

**Risk:** Seniors are a scarce resource. Adding one to day shift means removing one from another shift (likely early, where they clear night backlog). If the senior is pulled from early shift, the early-shift backlog clearance suffers, and the day-shift team inherits a larger queue—exactly the opposite of the intended effect. Also, one additional senior may be insufficient to meaningfully reduce WIP from 75 to below the pressure threshold.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** Track queue_length_at_start for day-shift events before and after. Monitor whether the extra senior actually handles high-complexity cases (check case_complexity_score for their events). Compare day-shift error rates in the last two hours. Track the early-shift queue at shift end to ensure it hasn't deteriorated. Use pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values on org:resource to track the senior's caseload and quality.

## 4.8 Penalize Workers with Higher Late-Shift Error Rates

**Expected Benefit:** Creates a financial incentive for workers to maintain quality even under pressure. Theory: if workers know they will be penalized, they will slow down and be more careful, breaking the speed–accuracy tradeoff in favor of accuracy.

**Risk:** This is the most problematic intervention. The data shows that late-shift errors are driven by *system conditions* (high WIP, complex case mix, Bot_Risk corrections, batching strategies)—not by worker negligence. Penalizing workers for errors they cannot control is unfair, demoralizing, and likely to cause attrition. Workers may respond by gaming metrics (underreporting errors, deferring error-prone work, avoiding complex cases). Juniors, who already have the highest error rates, would be disproportionately penalized—they are also the lowest-paid and least empowered to change their work conditions. The intervention assumes workers *choose* to make errors, when the data suggests errors are an emergent property of system pressure. Punishing workers for system failures is both ethically wrong and operationally counterproductive.

**Monitoring with Event-Log Indicators:** If implemented (against recommendation), track error_flag rates—but also track *changes in error reporting behavior* (are errors being logged differently?). Monitor rework_flag rates (do they increase as workers under-report direct errors?). Track voluntary turnover by resource group. Watch for changes in case acceptance patterns (do workers avoid complex or late-shift cases?). Use pm4py.filter_paths_performance to detect whether workers are routing error-prone work to colleagues.

# 5. Ethical and Operational Cautions

## 5.1 Selection Bias Makes Worker-Level Conclusions Unfair

The observed data is not a controlled experiment. Workers are assigned cases through a process that involves implicit and explicit selection: seniors get complex early cases, juniors get whatever is left, night staff get urgent exceptions. When we compute "Junior_H has a 12.4% error rate under high WIP," we are not measuring Junior_H's ability in isolation—we are measuring Junior_H's ability *given the hardest cases, the least support, the worst timing, and the most pressure*. Comparing this to a senior's 4.9% without adjusting for case mix is like comparing emergency room error rates to elective surgery error rates and concluding that ER doctors are worse.

The complexity scores, priority levels, and activity families all differ systematically by resource and shift position. Any performance metric that does not adjust for these confounders will attribute system effects to individual workers. Propensity-score matching, regression adjustment, or stratified analysis are necessary before drawing any individual-level conclusions.

## 5.2 Measurement Error in Error Flags

The `error_flag` is a binary indicator, but errors have varying severity. A minor documentation omission and a misclassified risk level are both flagged, but their consequences differ enormously. If the severity distribution of errors shifts across the shift (e.g., late-shift errors are more likely to be minor omissions while early-shift errors are judgment errors), raw error rates are misleading. Similarly, some errors are caught immediately (within-event quality check) while others are caught days later (downstream rework). The detection rate itself varies by when and how quality is checked—the end-of-shift Quality Check burst may catch errors that would have been missed earlier in the shift, inflating the apparent late-shift error rate.

## 5.3 Queue Pressure Is Created by Management, Not Workers

The queue builds during shifts because demand exceeds processing capacity. Workers do not choose to have WIP >70; this is a staffing and scheduling decision. When management sees high error rates under high WIP, the correct response is to question whether the staffing model is adequate—not whether workers are performing adequately. The "high WIP  high error" relationship is a *system design* problem. Punishing workers for adapting to conditions they did not create is not only unfair but counterproductive: it creates a culture of fear where workers slow down (hurting throughput) or hide errors (hurting quality).

## 5.4 Automation Quality Creates Unfair Burden

Bot_Risk classifications are corrected more often under high WIP, meaning human workers spend their most pressured moments fixing automated mistakes. This is an invisible workload that does not appear in their throughput metrics but degrades their performance on other tasks. Holding humans accountable for errors while the bot is not accountable creates a double standard. Before measuring human error rates, the organization should measure and improve bot accuracy—particularly under the high-complexity, high-WIP conditions where corrections are most costly.

## 5.5 Punitive Use of Error Rates Discourages Honest Reporting

If error rates are used punitively, workers have an incentive to under-report errors, delay reporting, or reclassify errors as "information requests" or "customer-initiated changes." This makes the data unreliable for process improvement. The event log should be a diagnostic tool, not a surveillance instrument. The most productive use of error data is identifying systemic patterns (e.g., "errors spike when WIP > 70 and complexity > 0.6") that point to structural interventions—not individual performance problems.

## 5.6 Batching Is a Rational Adaptation, Not a Failure

Workers batch similar cases to manage cognitive load. This is an intelligent response to high volume and switching costs. If management penalizes batching (or the errors it may produce), workers lose a coping mechanism without gaining an alternative. The organization should ask *why* workers batch—what would they need to avoid it?—rather than simply prohibiting the practice.

## 5.7 Night Shift Workers Face Unique Vulnerabilities

Night_L and Night_M work at the worst circadian time with fewer colleagues and more urgent exceptions. Their error rates under high WIP (8.2%) may reflect inadequate staffing, poor lighting, reduced support infrastructure, or genuine circadian impairment—but they are held to the same quality standards as day-shift seniors with full support. Any performance evaluation must account for the fundamentally different working conditions.

# 6. Monitoring Dashboard Specification

## Dashboard Architecture

The dashboard should provide real-time and historical views at three levels: **system level** (overall process health), **shift level** (operational management), and **resource level** (individual support, not surveillance). Resource-level views should be accessible only to the worker and their direct coach—not to performance management—unless aggregated to a minimum group size of 5.

## Indicator Specifications

### Indicator 1: Mean Service Time by Shift Phase

**Definition:** Mean event duration (minutes) by sequence position bin within shift, stratified by activity family.

**Purpose:** Tracks whether the service time decline is accelerating (suggesting increasing pressure) or stabilizing (suggesting normal warm-up). Distinguishes activity families to detect whether the decline is concentrated in specific activities (e.g., Document Review batching).

**Calculation:**
```python
service_time_by_phase = log.groupby(['pos_bin', 'activity_family']).agg(
    mean_service_time=('duration', 'mean'),
    median_service_time=('duration', 'median'),
    p90_service_time=('duration', lambda x: x.quantile(0.9)),
    n=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If mean service time in positions 76+ falls below 8 minutes for any activity (well below the complexity-adjusted baseline), flag as potential corner-cutting.

### Indicator 2: Mean Waiting Time (Case-Level)

**Definition:** Mean time between case arrival and first event start, by shift and priority level.

**Purpose:** Tracks whether operational changes improve or worsen customer-facing delays. Complements service-time metrics by showing the downstream impact.

**Calculation:**
```python
log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
waiting_by_shift = log.groupby(['shift_id', 'priority']).agg(
    mean_waiting_time=('case_waiting_time', 'mean'),
    p95_waiting_time=('case_waiting_time', lambda x: x.quantile(0.95)),
    n_cases=('case:concept:name', 'nunique')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If mean waiting time increases by >20% week-over-week for any priority level.

### Indicator 3: WIP Distribution Over Time

**Definition:** Time series of WIP at event start, with overlays for shift boundaries, day-of-week, and intervention dates.

**Purpose:** Visualizes the queue dynamics that drive pressure. Shows whether interventions are reducing peak WIP or merely shifting it.

**Calculation:**
```python
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

wip_ts = log.set_index('time:timestamp')['wip_at_start'].resample('1h').mean()
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(14, 5))
wip_ts.plot(ax=ax)
ax.axhline(y=70, color='red', linestyle='--', label='High-WIP threshold')
ax.set_ylabel('Mean WIP at event start')
ax.legend()
```

**Alert:** Persistent WIP >70 for more than 2 consecutive hours triggers a queue-pressure notification.

### Indicator 4: Rework Rate by Activity and Shift Phase

**Definition:** Proportion of events with `rework_flag=True`, by activity, sequence position bin, and resource group.

**Purpose:** Captures the true quality cost, which is more reliable than `error_flag` because rework represents errors that were severe enough to require correction. Tracks whether quality degradation in late shift translates to actual rework.

**Calculation:**
```python
rework_matrix = log.groupby(['activity_family', 'pos_bin']).agg(
    rework_rate=('rework_flag', 'mean'),
    error_rate=('error_flag', 'mean'),
    rework_events=('rework_flag', 'sum'),
    total_events=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()

# Downstream rework analysis
rework_cascade = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
```

**Alert:** If rework rate in any activity-position cell exceeds 15%, or if rework rate doubles relative to the prior week.

### Indicator 5: Error Rate by WIP × Complexity × Position

**Definition:** Three-dimensional heatmap of error rates across WIP bands, complexity bands, and shift position bins.

**Purpose:** The core diagnostic tool. Identifies the specific conditions under which errors concentrate. Enables targeted intervention (e.g., "errors spike only when WIP >70 AND complexity >0.6 AND position >50").

**Calculation:**
```python
error_3d = log.groupby(['wip_bin', 'case_complexity_bin', 'pos_bin']).agg(
    error_rate=('error_flag', 'mean'),
    n=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()

# Pivot for heatmap visualization
error_heatmap = error_3d.pivot_table(
    index=['wip_bin', 'case_complexity_bin'],
    columns='pos_bin',
    values='error_rate'
)
```

**Alert:** If any cell in the "High WIP × High Complexity × Position 76+" category exceeds 10% error rate with n > 50.

### Indicator 6: Complexity Mix by Shift Phase

**Definition:** Distribution of case_complexity_score by sequence position bin and shift.

**Purpose:** Tracks the selection/case-mix confound. If complexity in early positions increases over time (suggesting more backlog accumulation), the system is deteriorating. If complexity in late positions increases (suggesting better backlog management), the system is improving.

**Calculation:**
```python
complexity_by_phase = log.groupby(['pos_bin', 'shift_id']).agg(
    mean_complexity=('case_complexity_score', 'mean'),
    high_complexity_share=('case_complexity_score', lambda x: (x > 0.7).mean()),
    high_priority_share=('priority', lambda x: (x == 'high').mean()),
    n=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If high-complexity share in the first hour of any shift exceeds 55% (indicating severe backlog transfer).

### Indicator 7: Batching Indicator

**Definition:** Rolling count of consecutive events by the same resource on the same activity with service time below the activity median. Also: proportion of events occurring in detected batches.

**Purpose:** Monitors batching intensity, which is both a coping mechanism and a quality risk. Tracks whether batching is increasing under rising WIP.

**Calculation:**
```python
# Detect consecutive same-activity events by same resource
log = log.sort_values(['org:resource', 'time:timestamp'])
log['same_activity_as_prev'] = (
    (log['org:resource'] == log['org:resource'].shift(1)) &
    (log['concept:name'] == log['concept:name'].shift(1))
)
log['batch_run'] = (~log['same_activity_as_prev']).cumsum()
batch_sizes = log.groupby('batch_run').agg(
    batch_length=('concept:name', 'count'),
    resource=('org:resource', 'first'),
    activity=('concept:name', 'first'),
    mean_service_time=('duration', 'mean'),
    start_position=('sequence_position_within_shift', 'first'),
    error_rate=('error_flag', 'mean')
).reset_index()

# Batching intensity by shift phase
batching_by_phase = batch_sizes.groupby('start_position_bin').agg(
    mean_batch_length=('batch_length', 'mean'),
    share_in_batches=('batch_length', lambda x: (x >= 3).sum() / len(x)),
    batch_error_rate=('error_rate', 'mean')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If mean batch length exceeds 10 for any activity, or if batched event error rate exceeds 1.5× non-batched error rate.

### Indicator 8: Handover and Queue Ownership Changes

**Definition:** Number of cases that change assigned resource (handover) within a shift, and the time gap between handover and next event. Also: inter-shift handover queue (queue_length at shift boundary).

**Purpose:** Handovers are a source of errors (information loss, context switching) and are a consequence of shift design. Tracks whether cases are being "tossed over the wall" between shifts or within shifts.

**Calculation:**
```python
# Detect within-shift handovers
log['prev_resource'] = log.groupby('case:concept:name')['org:resource'].shift(1)
log['handover'] = (log['org:resource'] != log['prev_resource']) & log['prev_resource'].notna()

handover_stats = log.groupby(['shift_id', 'pos_bin']).agg(
    handover_rate=('handover', 'mean'),
    n_handovers=('handover', 'sum'),
    n_events=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()

# Inter-shift handover: queue at shift boundaries
shift_boundaries = log.groupby('shift_id').agg(
    queue_at_start=('queue_length_at_start', 'first'),
    queue_at_end=('queue_length_at_start', 'last')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If within-shift handover rate exceeds 15% of events, or if inter-shift queue transfer exceeds 40 cases.

### Indicator 9: Worker-Load Fairness Index

**Definition:** Gini coefficient of event counts across resources within each shift, and coefficient of variation of WIP_at_start across resources within each shift.

**Purpose:** Detects whether work is distributed equitably or whether some resources are systematically overloaded. Unfair distribution leads to burnout and higher error rates for overloaded workers, but also makes performance comparisons meaningless.

**Calculation:**
```python
def gini(array):
    array = np.sort(array)
    n = len(array)
    index = np.arange(1, n + 1)
    return (2 * np.sum(index * array) / (n * np.sum(array))) - (n + 1) / n

fairness = log.groupby(['shift_id', 'org:resource']).agg(
    events_count=('concept:name', 'count'),
    mean_wip=('wip_at_start', 'mean'),
    max_wip=('wip_at_start', 'max')
).reset_index()

fairness_summary = fairness.groupby('shift_id').apply(
    lambda g: pd.Series({
        'gini_events': gini(g['events_count'].values),
        'cv_wip': g['mean_wip'].std() / g['mean_wip'].mean(),
        'max_load_ratio': g['events_count'].max() / g['events_count'].min()
    })
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If Gini coefficient exceeds 0.35 for any shift, or if any resource's WIP is consistently >2 standard deviations above the shift mean.

### Indicator 10: Automation Correction Rate

**Definition:** Proportion of Bot_Risk classifications that are followed by a Risk Classification correction activity, stratified by WIP level, complexity, and shift.

**Purpose:** Monitors bot accuracy and its interaction with human workload. Increasing correction rates under high WIP mean the bot is adding to human burden when humans can least afford it.

**Calculation:**
```python
# Filter for Risk Classification events
risk_events = log[log['concept:name'] == 'Risk Classification'].copy()
risk_events['is_bot'] = risk_events['org:resource'] == 'Bot_Risk'
risk_events['is_correction'] = risk_events['rework_flag'] == True

bot_correction_rate = risk_events[risk_events['is_bot']].groupby(
    ['wip_bin', 'case_complexity_bin', 'shift_id']
).agg(
    correction_rate=('is_correction', 'mean'),
    n_classifications=('concept:name', 'count')
).reset_index()
```

**Alert:** If bot correction rate exceeds 8% under any condition, or if it increases week-over-week for three consecutive weeks.
