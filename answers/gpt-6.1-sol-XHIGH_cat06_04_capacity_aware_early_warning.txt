## 1. Decision rule

Use a rolling, capacity-constrained policy that values the incremental effect of a swarm and the harm it creates elsewhere. A high breach probability alone does not justify a swarm.

**Eligibility and evidence gates.** A case must still be before `Diagnosis Complete`, have a diagnostic problem the senior team can plausibly address, and have enough time for the 45-minute swarm and subsequent work to improve the outcome. Check these conditions again immediately before starting. Exclude cases already past the effective stage, including I-03. Verify previous treatment in an intervention ledger: another alert is not another swarm, and a second swarm requires a materially new event and a fresh estimate of incremental benefit.

Normally require an actionable signal at two consecutive 30-minute refreshes, or one verified material event. A material event includes a new diagnostic log, confirmed missing information, a newly identified blocker, or a real queue change. A rise of at least 0.10 in adjusted risk triggers immediate review, but counts as sufficient evidence only after confirming that real input changes caused it. Imputation, duplicate updates, and passage through another refresh are not material events. For a case approaching its last feasible intervention time, conduct an immediate human review instead of waiting for persistence. Review can confirm actionability without waiting for another model score.

**Calibration and uncertainty.** For planning, use the supplied biases as sensitivity bands, clipped to [0,1]:

- SME: untreated risk approximately `p_now + 0.04` to `p_now + 0.09`. This gives I-05 0.61–0.66, I-07 0.76–0.81, and I-10 0.50–0.55.
- Strategic Enterprise: approximately `p_now − 0.12` to `p_now − 0.05`. This gives I-02 0.54–0.61, I-03 0.70–0.77, I-04 0.36–0.43, and I-08 0.31–0.38.
- Public Sector: retain the current estimate provisionally, with sampling uncertainty. I-06 additionally needs a data-quality investigation before its jump is trusted.
- Non-Native-Language Support: retain I-09's 0.69 as an uncertain observation; there is insufficient evidence to invent a numerical correction. Obtain an accessible diagnostic review and use wider benefit stress tests, including zero benefit.

These are planning bands, not statistical confidence intervals or fitted recalibration functions. Compare prefixes under the same calibration version. Neither a probability increase nor a calibrated probability is the treatment effect. The supplied effect ranges are provisional hypotheses; stage, timing, diagnostic mechanism, and congestion may invalidate them. Any proposed absolute reduction must be compatible with the untreated risk and cannot reduce risk below zero.

**Prioritization and slot release.** Let `tau_i` denote the incremental absolute reduction attributable to a swarm, relative to no swarm at the same decision time. Calculate a midpoint direct margin and a lower-range stress margin:

`D_i = V_i × tau_i − €450 − F_i`.

Here I charge the entire listed false-alarm burden `F_i` as a conservative planning allowance. An expected false-alarm charge would usually be smaller, but requires a defensible definition and estimate of a false alarm. Successful prevention of a breach must not be labelled a false alarm simply because the treated case closes on time.

First honor verified safety, statutory, or other non-monetary obligations; the table does not establish a mandatory override for any particular case. Among other cases, favor a positive midpoint margin, a non-negative lower-range direct margin, credible diagnostic actionability, and an expiring intervention window. Use midpoint margins to order economically promising candidates; use lower-range margins and the adverse scenario to identify fragility. This is a cautious expected-value policy, not a claim that the range endpoints are guaranteed causal bounds. I-08 fails the lower-range direct-margin screen; I-06 fails the present evidence gate.

For a proposed portfolio S, assess:

`Net(S) = sum of direct margins − H(S)`,

where `H(S) = sum over affected untreated cases of [breach value × incremental breach probability caused by S]`.

Release an ordinary slot only if its incremental midpoint benefit exceeds its estimated marginal queue harm and the opportunity cost of displacing a better candidate or the reserve. Do not merely check that the whole portfolio remains profitable: a profitable I-04 must not subsidize a harmful additional swarm. Check likely delay to each untreated deadline as well as monetary totals. If the queue's monetary values or congestion cannot be bounded adequately, hold the marginal slot pending a rapid queue review.

Keep one slot provisionally for the possible regulatory arrival until 15:00. Its value depends on arrival likelihood, eligibility, and obligations, which are not supplied. Release it earlier for a verified mandatory need or a compelling case reaching its last feasible intervention time; otherwise reassess at 15:00. Reserve is a preference, not an absolute restriction.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

Maintain one case-level alert record containing the latest stage, calibrated risk band, diagnostic evidence, alert reason, last notification, owner, treatment history, and last feasible intervention time. Every refresh updates the record and waiting-list position; it does not automatically notify staff or consume another slot.

Use a 60-minute notification cool-down for unchanged evidence. Suppress duplicates based on the underlying event and feature changes, not merely on a new model timestamp. Reassess open, untreated cases at least hourly and before their last feasible intervention time; urgent cases receive more frequent owner checks. Determine that time from the 45-minute swarm plus a conservative estimate of remaining process work, rather than a universal deadline offset. Avoid closing an unresolved alert solely because its score fluctuates slightly downward; normally require two reassuring, trustworthy updates and owner confirmation.

Bypass the cool-down for a verified new diagnostic event, a confirmed substantive risk increase, an approaching last feasible start, or a safety/statutory escalation. This initiates review and reprioritization, not automatic treatment. An alert without a response or owner acknowledgement must escalate through the operational chain; suppression must not hide neglect.

I-05's third alert reflects unchanged evidence and a 0.02 score movement. Suppress the repeated notification, preserve the original open need, and check whether it has ever received a swarm. If untreated, it may still enter when relative benefits or queue conditions improve; new evidence is not required for its first treatment simply because notifications repeated. If already treated, a new swarm is normally forbidden without a material event.

I-07's new customer log is a material event even though its probability fell from 0.75 to 0.72. Reopen the diagnostic assessment immediately, account for SME underprediction, and verify whether useful work can finish within its remaining window. It is not automatically entitled to a swarm, but an unchanged-alert suppression rule must not block it.

After any swarm, record findings, actions, and remaining risk. Do not reuse the original effect estimate for another swarm. Re-entry requires a new tractable issue, a fresh incremental-benefit assessment conditional on the first treatment, current stage eligibility, and explicit operational approval. Check for cross-case duplicates and stop when diagnosis completes. Threshold crossings alone never authorize repeat treatment.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**Value-based starvation.** Large contractual values can repeatedly outrank SME cases even when smaller cases have greater preventable risk. Provide all cases an owner, a timely evidence review, and a deadline escalation path. Audit waiting time, missed intervention windows, and preventable breaches by segment, conditional on stage and urgency. Establish a rolling service floor, such as human review within 30 minutes of a credible alert and before the last feasible start. Apply aging priority to unresolved cases and a rotating share of discretionary capacity to clinically/operationally comparable opportunities in under-served groups. Do not force economically or operationally harmful swarms merely to meet a quota; use documented service obligations and measured unmet need to revise allocation weights. In this portfolio, I-07 receives immediate review and I-05 remains visible despite duplicate suppression.

**Language-context uncertainty.** Sparse validation can create permanent exclusion if “uncertain” is treated as “low benefit.” Give I-09 equal access to interpreters or appropriate language expertise, review whether logs and features have equal diagnostic meaning, and avoid treating language as a proxy for customer value or cooperation. Pool estimates across genuinely comparable workflows while retaining subgroup uncertainty. Include this context in protected, ethically eligible effect-estimation opportunities. Audit exclusions for “insufficient evidence” and assess whether the evidence requirement itself is harder to satisfy for this group.

**Differential calibration.** A common raw-probability threshold would under-serve SMEs and over-alert Strategic Enterprise cases. Maintain segment-sensitive calibration and uncertainty reporting, but base service eligibility on actual needs and obligations. Report performance both with and without corrections, test intersectional slices where feasible, and escalate insufficient samples for review rather than interpreting absence of evidence as good calibration. Segment corrections require periodic validation after workflow changes.

**Selective labels and policy feedback.** Successful treatment lowers observed breach rates precisely among selected cases. Training directly on those labels can teach the model that originally high-risk cases are safe, depress future alerts, and distort apparent segment differences. Log every decision opportunity, eligibility, scores, features, treatment timing, selection reason, propensity where defined, and queue exposure. Distinguish prediction under the deployed policy from estimated untreated risk. Estimate effects with an explicit causal design; do not relabel prevented breaches as ordinary negative examples or as false alarms.

**Unequal spillover exposure.** Swarms may protect high-value customers while concentrating delay on untreated SMEs or language-support queues. Track affected untreated cases and marginal deadline loss by segment, not just recipients. Before release, block or reroute a swarm if interruption threatens a verified hard obligation in an untreated case. Monitor each group's additional waiting time and breach risk per unit of swarm exposure. When harms are concentrated, change staffing, timing, or queue protection and reduce swarm intensity. Include all spillover harms in portfolio evaluation even if they fall outside the displayed ten cases.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

**Operational monitoring, each refresh and before each start:** verify stage, SLA slack, live-feature freshness, alert owner acknowledgement, treatment history, and diagnostic actionability. Measure notification volume, duplicate suppression, time to decision, missed feasible starts, diagnostic findings, time to diagnosis completion, downstream resolution time, customer disruption, and reasons a swarm produced little benefit. Alert immediately on input failures such as I-06's missing queue field; route affected decisions to manual review until repaired.

**Predictive monitoring, as outcomes mature:** assess calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, reliability plots, Brier score, and discrimination by segment, stage, time to deadline, and language context. Report uncertainty and sample sizes. Outcomes may be delayed, so account for unresolved cases rather than silently excluding them. Track untreated-risk calibration on suitably controlled or adjusted observations and deployed-policy calibration separately. Monitor Enterprise redesign effects and SME underprediction; do not apply fixed offsets indefinitely.

**Causal-effect estimation:** conduct a prospective pilot among eligible, non-mandatory cases for which clinicians/operations have genuine equipoise. Randomize swarm access versus usual care, or randomized tie-breaking where capacity is scarce, with known assignment probabilities. Never withhold a legally required response to create a control. Because cases share a queue, an individual comparison alone is insufficient: where operationally feasible, randomize queue/time-block swarm availability or intensity, respecting reserve and staffing constraints, and measure outcomes for both selected and untreated cases. Allow for carryover across blocks. This estimates total portfolio effects and spillovers as well as direct effects.

Analyze assignment effects for the capacity policy, and distinguish them from treatment-received effects when stage changes or cancellations cause non-delivery. Stratify or partially pool by stage, workflow, urgency, and language context; report wide intervals when samples are small. Predefine breach, diagnostic duration, disruption, untreated-case delay, and subgroup harm outcomes. If randomization is unavailable, use a target-trial-style longitudinal design with measured treatment propensities, time-varying confounders, and queue exposure; apply overlap checks and sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding. Observational adjustment does not establish causality by itself.

**Capacity and queue monitoring:** record specialist minutes removed from ordinary work, slot occupancy, congestion at each start, six-case exposure when applicable, and actual additional delay and breaches. Three swarms occupy 135 minutes of the two-person team, or 270 specialist-minutes. This workload measurement is separate from the stated aggregate delay to other cases; do not add it to the monetary spillover estimate again. Record reserve utilization, whether the regulatory arrival occurred, and lost opportunities from holding the slot. Use queue replay or simulation, validated against observed delays, to replace the flat spillover increment with state-dependent estimates.

**Fairness monitoring:** compare access, review delays, treatment rates, effect estimates, last-window misses, customer burden, and spillover exposure across segments and language contexts. Adjust comparisons for legitimate differences in eligibility and urgency, while also reporting absolute service gaps. Use rolling windows and uncertainty intervals; investigate severe individual failures without waiting for statistical significance. Assign an accountable owner for corrective action.

**Policy-induced drift and revision:** record model, calibration, workflow, and policy versions. Compare eligible-case distributions, selection rates, queue exposure, and outcome patterns before and after changes. Changes in observed breach rates may reflect treatment or congestion rather than better prediction. Maintain a contemporaneous evaluation design where feasible; a shadow policy alone cannot reveal counterfactual outcomes.

Reevaluate allocation immediately when a feature failure, stage change, new obligation, or high congestion invalidates the assumptions. Hold an individual start when marginal harm exceeds its credible benefit or an untreated hard deadline is endangered. Review calibration and outcome monitoring weekly, with formal policy/effect updates monthly or sooner after a material workflow change, using sufficient matured outcomes.

Revise the allocation rule when evidence shows persistent calibration bias, materially smaller effects, systematic subgroup exclusion, repeated negative marginal portfolio impact, or a changed value of reserve capacity. Changes may affect calibration maps, evidence gates, value/urgency weights, the maximum number of swarms, queue-harm limits, or reserve duration. Backtest against logged decision opportunities, evaluate with the causal and queue model, introduce in shadow mode for operational checking, then deploy with monitored rollback criteria. Do not lower one global probability threshold merely to fill unused slots: fewer swarms are appropriate when additional treatment would harm the portfolio.
