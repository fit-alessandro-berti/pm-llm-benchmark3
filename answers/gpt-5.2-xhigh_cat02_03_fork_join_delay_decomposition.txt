# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let cases be indexed by \(c\). Branches are indexed by \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\). Observed timestamps:

- \(t^{arr}_c\): `arrival`
- \(t^{fork}_c\): `fork_release`
- \(t^{s}_{ci}\), \(t^{e}_{ci}\): branch \(i\) start/end (`i_start`, `i_end`)
- \(t^{sF}_c\), \(t^{eF}_c\): final activity start/end (`final_start`, `final_end`)

Define elapsed-time components (all are case-level wall-clock durations):

- **Upstream elapsed**: \(U_c = t^{fork}_c - t^{arr}_c\)
- **Join-ready time (inferred)**: \(J_c = \max_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} t^{e}_{ci}\)
- **Fork–join elapsed stage time**: \(FJ_c = J_c - t^{fork}_c = \max_i (t^{e}_{ci} - t^{fork}_c)\)
- **Downstream (post-join) delay before final**: \(DQ_c = t^{sF}_c - J_c\) (interpretable as queueing only if `final_start` is driven by capacity/queue rather than a release policy)
- **Final service**: \(FS_c = t^{eF}_c - t^{sF}_c\)
- **Total case flow time**: \(T_c = t^{eF}_c - t^{arr}_c = U_c + FJ_c + DQ_c + FS_c\)

Define branch-local (non-additive) diagnostics inside the fork–join stage:

- **Branch-specific queue delay (from fork release)**: \(Q_{ci} = t^{s}_{ci} - t^{fork}_c\)
- **Branch active service**: \(S_{ci} = t^{e}_{ci} - t^{s}_{ci}\)
- **Branch elapsed to completion (from fork release)**: \(E_{ci} = t^{e}_{ci} - t^{fork}_c = Q_{ci} + S_{ci}\)
- **Branch synchronization wait after completion**: \(W^{sync}_{ci} = J_c - t^{e}_{ci}\) (slack until all branches complete)

Define the critical set of branches:

- \(I^*_c = \{i: t^{e}_{ci} = J_c\}\)
- For deterministic reporting, define `critical_branch(c)` as the alphabetically-first element of \(I^*_c\) (A before B before C) when ties occur.

**Why summing synchronization waits would double count elapsed time**

For any branch \(i\),
\[
Q_{ci} + S_{ci} + W^{sync}_{ci}
= (t^{s}_{ci}-t^{fork}_c) + (t^{e}_{ci}-t^{s}_{ci}) + (J_c-t^{e}_{ci})
= J_c - t^{fork}_c
= FJ_c.
\]
So \(\sum_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} (Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W^{sync}_{ci}) = 3FJ_c\): each branch’s “queue + service + sync” spans the same wall-clock interval \([t^{fork}_c, J_c]\). Therefore, synchronization waits are **parallel slack** and are not additively composable. A non-double-counting elapsed-time decomposition uses \(FJ_c\) once, while \(Q_{ci}, S_{ci}, W^{sync}_{ci}\) are used for critical-path attribution and slack analysis.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

## 3.1 Branch comparison (queue, service, slack, criticality)

All values are minutes. “Elapsed” means \(Q+S\) from `fork_release` to the branch end.

| branch | mean queue | p90 queue | mean service | max service | mean elapsed (Q+S) | max elapsed (Q+S) | critical freq | mean sync wait |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 0.00 | 0 | 50.00 | 50 | 50.00 | 50 | 2/12 | 11.00 |
| B | 19.17 | 30 | 37.50 | 45 | 56.67 | 75 | 8/12 | 4.33 |
| C | 10.42 | 24 | 20.17 | 30 | 30.58 | 57 | 2/12 | 30.42 |

Key observations:

- **A has the largest mean service**, but it is *deterministic* here (50 minutes, always starts at fork release). It sets a **hard floor** of 50 minutes on \(FJ_c\) unless A is improved.
- **B most often controls `join_ready`**: it is the last-completing branch in 8/12 cases and produces the longest fork–join times (up to 75 minutes).
- **C is usually slack**: the very large mean synchronization wait (30.4 minutes) indicates C typically finishes well before the join; it matters mainly in rare spike cases (F04, F08).

## 3.2 “Who controls completion time?” in fork–join terms

Let A’s deterministic elapsed be \(E_A=50\). Define “extension beyond A” per case as \(FJ_c - 50\). Summed over the 12 cases, the total extension is 132 minutes, attributable by the critical branch as:

- **B**: 120 minutes (about 91% of the extension)
- **C**: 12 minutes
- **A**: 0 minutes (A is the floor, not the source of extension)

This is why the most influential branch is **B (Machine Stress Test)** even though its mean service time is *smaller* than A’s: B combines **large, variable queueing** with substantial service, and it most frequently sets the maximum completion time.

## 3.3 Influence ranking (with uncertainty acknowledged)

1. **B (Machine Stress Test)**: dominant driver of `join_ready` frequency and tail fork–join times; improvements to B queueing or throughput most reliably reduce completion.
2. **A (Laboratory Assay)** as a **floor**: not a variability driver, but it caps the benefit of speeding other branches (many improvements saturate once B and C fall below A’s 50-minute completion).
3. **C (Documentation Review)**: generally noncritical with high slack, but can control completion in rare high-queue episodes; managing spikes helps tail risk more than the mean.

Downstream note: post-join delay is nontrivial (mean 14.7 minutes) and can **mask** branch improvements when the final station is busy or gated by policy.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

I evaluate counterfactuals in terms of impact on total flow time. Because `final_start - join_ready` may include both capacity waiting and release/dispatch policy, I distinguish:

- **Frozen-downstream scenario**: keep each case’s observed `downstream_queue` unchanged when `join_ready` changes (isolates the direct fork–join benefit).
- **Final-station replay scenario**: keep the observed `final_resource` assignment and within-resource order, and keep each case’s inferred dispatch lag constant; recompute final starts as \(t^{sF}=\max(J,\text{prev\_end})+\text{dispatch\_lag}\). This captures masking/cascading under one plausible downstream model (but is not uniquely identifiable from the data).

## 4.1 Counterfactual 1 — reduce A service time by 20%

Change: \(S_{cA}\) goes from 50 to 40 minutes (A starts unchanged).

- **Direct benefit**: only cases where A is critical (or near-critical) benefit.
  - F01: `join_ready` reduces by 5 minutes (A no longer the max; B becomes max)
  - F11: `join_ready` reduces by 10 minutes (A ties B at 11:20)
- **Zero direct benefit**: 10/12 cases, because B or C still dominates `join_ready`.
- **Downstream masking example**: in the replay scenario, F11 gets **0** total-flow reduction because final work on F1 is still not available until 11:50 (earlier join readiness is absorbed by downstream congestion).

Aggregate effect:

- Frozen-downstream mean saving: **1.25 minutes per case**
- Replay mean saving: **0.42 minutes per case**

Secondary effects to watch:

- A speedups can shift criticality to B, increasing the importance of B queue control.
- If downstream is gated (not purely capacity), earlier joins may simply increase synchronization slack rather than improve completion.

## 4.2 Counterfactual 2 — reduce B queue delay by 50%

Change: \(Q_{cB}\) is halved; B service is unchanged.

**Direct fork–join effects (frozen downstream):**

- `join_ready` reduces in 8 cases (the B-driven ones): F02 (10), F03 (10), F05 (10), F06 (15), F07 (15), F09 (12.5), F10 (5), F12 (15) minutes.
- Cases with **0 direct benefit** because another branch remains critical: F01 (A), F04 (C), F08 (C), F11 (A).
- Diminishing returns against the A floor: several B-critical cases collapse to an A-limited fork–join time of 50 minutes after the reduction (e.g., F02, F05, F06, F10).

Aggregate effect:

- Frozen-downstream mean saving: **7.71 minutes per case**

**System interaction (final-station replay):**

- Replay mean saving rises to **10.17 minutes per case** because earlier join readiness for B-driven cases can also reduce downstream busy time on the final resources, pulling later cases earlier even when their own `join_ready` does not change (e.g., F11 gains 15 minutes under this particular replay model).

Secondary effects to watch:

- Reducing B queueing increases the rate at which cases hit the join, potentially increasing utilization and congestion at the final station unless dispatch/capacity is adjusted.
- If B queue reduction is achieved via prioritization, check fairness and starvation risk for low-priority cases.

## 4.3 Counterfactual 3 — add final-station capacity / reduce downstream queueing

The observed post-join delay has mean **14.67 minutes**. Under the replay interpretation of the two final resources, it decomposes (on average) into:

- **Capacity waiting (resource busy)**: 6.08 minutes
- **Dispatch/release lag (policy/coordination)**: 8.58 minutes

If we “add enough final-station capacity” to remove only the capacity-wait component (keeping the policy lag), the mean flow-time saving is **6.08 minutes per case**, concentrated in:

- F04 (8), F05 (10), F08 (15), F09 (15), F10 (5), F11 (20) minutes (others 0 under this model)

Implications:

- **Capacity alone may have diminishing returns** if dispatch/release lag dominates; improving dispatch frequency/pull behavior can yield larger benefits.
- Reducing downstream delay also **unmasks upstream improvements**: if final work starts closer to `join_ready`, speeding B (or A) more reliably reduces completion time rather than being absorbed by downstream waiting.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## 5.1 Redesign 1 — attack B (Machine Stress Test) queueing variability

Why: B is critical in 8/12 cases and accounts for ~91% of the fork–join extension beyond A’s 50-minute floor.

Candidate interventions:

- Add/shift capacity for B during the observed peak window (reduce \(Q_B\) p90 from 30 minutes).
- Appointment/slotting for B to smooth arrivals after fork release.
- Pre-stage setups (where feasible) so the “true queue” is not hidden inside B’s start delay.

Expected impact:

- **Mean flow time**: meaningful reduction (7.7 minutes per case under the frozen-downstream scenario; potentially larger if downstream congestion also eases).
- **Tail flow time**: strong reduction in the worst fork–join cases (e.g., 15 minutes in F07 and F12 under the 50% queue cut).
- **Synchronization**: less slack for A and C (less waiting at the join), lowering WIP trapped behind the join rule.

Risks / new bottlenecks:

- Benefits will saturate against A’s 50-minute floor unless A is also improved.
- More cases will reach the final station sooner; without downstream improvements, waiting may shift from B to the final stage.

What to pilot/measure first:

- Validate that \(Q_B = B\_start - fork\_release\) is truly queueing (vs setup/transport/release policy).
- Measure B resource calendars, queue length, and whether the 30-minute waits are systematic or special-cause.

## 5.2 Redesign 2 — reduce post-join delay at the final station (dispatch + capacity)

Why: downstream delay is large (mean 14.7 minutes) and can mask upstream improvements.

Candidate interventions:

- Increase decision cadence (reduce dispatch/release lag) via smaller, more frequent “decision slots” or a pull-based rule: when a resource becomes free, take the longest-waiting join-ready case.
- Add temporary capacity for peak periods, but only if dispatch lag is also addressed (capacity alone targets ~6 minutes of the 14.7-minute mean under the replay interpretation).

Expected impact:

- **Mean flow time**: up to ~6 minutes/case from capacity-wait removal alone; larger if policy lag is reduced.
- **Tail**: large wins in cases with big capacity waits (e.g., F11 shows 20 minutes of capacity waiting under the replay interpretation).
- **System effect**: makes fork–join improvements (especially B queue reduction) translate more directly into completion-time reductions.

Risks / new bottlenecks:

- If dispatch lag is policy-driven (e.g., required review windows), adding capacity may not help.
- Pulling decisions earlier can affect decision quality/compliance; monitor rework/defect rates.

What to pilot/measure first:

- Instrument “join-ready arrival to final queue” explicitly and measure queue discipline, idle gaps, and policy constraints.
- Separate downstream delay into capacity wait vs policy lag with additional logs (resource availability, handoffs).

                prev_end = jr
            feasible = max(jr, prev_end)
            st = feasible + lag
            en = st + srv
            final_end_map[c] = en
            prev_end = en
    final_end = df["case"].map(final_end_map)
    return (final_end - df["arrival"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

def compute_join_ready(mod_ends_df):
    jr = mod_ends_df[[f"{b}_end" for b in branches]].max(axis=1)
    return pd.Series(jr.values, index=mod_ends_df["case"])

base_jr = pd.Series(out["join_ready"].values, index=df["case"])

base_freeze = total_flow_freeze(out["join_ready"])

# 7. Limits of inference

From the provided minute-granularity start/end timestamps alone, the following cannot be identified (or only weakly bounded):

- **Causality of delays**: we can attribute elapsed time to components, but not claim why a queue formed (e.g., staffing, machine downtime, prioritization) without additional context.
- **True resource capacities and contention on A/B/C**: branch queue \(Q_{ci}\) is inferred as `start - fork_release`, but that interval could include travel/setup/release policies and not purely “waiting for a server.”
- **Preemption and within-service structure**: service intervals may include pauses or multi-phase work; we only observe a coarse interval.
- **Unobserved join logic**: we infer `join_ready` as the latest branch end. If the real rule includes buffers, quality checks, or handoffs after branch completion, the inferred `join_ready` would be early.
- **Whether downstream delay is a queue vs a release policy**: `final_start - join_ready` may reflect capacity waiting, batching, meeting schedules, or compliance delays; the data do not separate these mechanisms.
- **Queue discipline and dispatch rules at the final station**: even with two named resources, we cannot uniquely infer FIFO vs priority, reassignment rules, or whether resources are interchangeable; counterfactuals therefore depend on explicit modeling assumptions.
- **Generalization beyond this day/sample**: all observations are for a single date and 12 cases; variability across days, shifts, and demand levels is not observed.
