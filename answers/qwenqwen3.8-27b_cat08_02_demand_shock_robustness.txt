# 1. Stress scenarios

**S1 — Demand mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases**  
A winter infection wave or outbreak drives volume up and changes case mix, not just total volume. Peak data show volume roughly +43% versus the normal weekly rate, with Urgent rising from 18% to 29% and Complex-panel from 13% to 20%, while Routine falls from 61% to 42%. This scenario stresses medical validation, specialist review, assay capacity, and urgent SLA compliance. Peak p90 cycle times are already severe: Urgent 33h, Complex-panel 210h, Repeat-after-failure 240h.

**S2 — Assay-machine degradation or outage**  
M2 is faster but has a higher failure rate; M1 is the more reliable asset for critical work. This scenario has two variants:  
- **M2 degradation:** routine cases routed to M2 generate more repeat assays, especially after night shifts. Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and some go directly to medical validation.  
- **M1 outage or degradation:** Urgent and Complex-panel cases lose their most reliable assay capacity, increasing urgent SLA risk and complex-panel multi-assay delays.  
Peak assay utilization is 98% with an 18h median queue, and assay-to-repeat-assay flow rises from 6.7% in the normal period to 14.8% in the peak period.

**S3 — Medical validator or specialist-review absence**  
Medical validators are required for Urgent and Complex-panel cases, and specialist review is a single-person bottleneck with one specialist unavailable every second Friday. In the peak period, medical validation queue is 22h and specialist-review queue is 36h. The DFG shows that technical validation routes more cases to medical validation in peak: 34.5% of all cases versus 21.2% in the normal period, and hold-for-specialist-review rises from 290 to 610 cases. This scenario primarily threatens the validation tail, not just assay throughput.

**S4 — Courier arrival compression or delay**  
Courier routes arrive in spikes at 10:00 and 17:00. Compression creates an intake burst, while delay increases pre-analytical defect risk. Peak courier intake utilization is 88% with a 7.5h median queue. Pre-analytical check utilization is 96% with a 9.5h median queue. Peak DFG shows pre-analytical recollection requests rising to 8.0% of received cases and manual accession correction to 4.3%. Recollection loops are expensive: peak recollection-to-receive median is 60h and consumes registration/receive capacity.

**S5 — Compound winter wave**  
A plausible worst-case combines S1, S2, S3, and S4: Urgent/Complex mix shift, courier compression, M2 degradation, and specialist absence. This is the scenario the redesign must survive without simply adding capacity everywhere. In S5, bottlenecks migrate quickly from intake to pre-analytical check to preparation/assay to technical/medical validation, and relieving one station can immediately overload the next.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

**S1 — Demand mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases**  
Likely sequence: **pre-analytical/accession  preparation/batch  assay/repeat  technical validation  medical/specialist review**.

- The peak DFG shows more pre-analytical failure: 780 pre-analytical recollection requests and 420 manual accession corrections. Manual accession correction itself creates recollection in 26% of cases in peak. Recollection-to-receive takes 60h, so upstream defects re-enter the front of the process and consume registration/receive capacity.
- Accession correction has only two specialists and a 14h peak queue. It is a small resource with large downstream unblocking effect.
- If pre-analytical and accession correction are relieved, cases move faster into preparation. Peak preparation already has 12.6% of cases entering batch wait, with batch-wait-to-assay median of 22h. Urgent cases bypass batch wait in only 58% of peak cases, so urgent cases are increasingly exposed to batch fragmentation.
- If preparation is relieved, assay becomes the next constraint: 98% utilization, 18h queue, and 14.8% assay-to-repeat-assay flow. Repeat-assay-to-assay median is 26h, and some repeats go directly to medical validation.
- If assay is relieved, technical validation becomes the next constraint: 101% utilization and 16h queue. Technical validation then routes more cases to medical validation and specialist hold in peak.
- Relieving medical validation without relieving specialist review can leave Complex-panel cases stuck in hold-for-specialist-review, whose peak median to medical validation is 48h.
- A strict Urgent-first rule would reduce urgent waiting but can starve Routine and Complex-panel cases, increase total WIP, and fragment machine schedules. It optimizes one SLA at the expense of the system.

**S2 — Assay-machine degradation or outage**  
Likely sequence: **assay run / repeat-assay loop  technical validation  medical validation**, with preparation and batching as secondary constraints.

- If M2 degrades, routine cases routed to M2 are likely to generate more repeat assays. Repeat-after-failure cases already consume assay capacity twice and are clustered after night shifts and M2 usage. More repeats mean more assay capacity consumed and more technical/medical validation work.
- The DFG shows repeat-assay-to-medical-validation flow increasing from 80 normal cases to 120 peak cases. Repeats therefore do not only consume assay time; they can also bypass or compress technical validation and land directly on medical validation.
- If M1 is degraded or unavailable, Urgent and Complex-panel cases lose the most reliable assay path. Complex-panel cases require multiple assays, so an M1 outage has a multiplicative effect on complex-panel cycle time.
- If the lab responds by increasing routine batch sizes to improve M2 setup efficiency, Urgent cases may be delayed unless priority exceptions are tightly controlled.
- If the lab responds by adding assay capacity or outsourcing assay work, completed assays arrive faster at technical validation. Technical validation is already at 101% utilization in peak, so the bottleneck can migrate to technical validation and then medical validation.
- If the lab responds by pulling technical validators to accession correction, technical validation capacity falls, potentially worsening the validation bottleneck.

**S3 — Medical validator or specialist-review absence**  
Likely sequence: **technical validation  medical validation / specialist review  release**, with technical validation queue growing because downstream is blocked.

- Medical validation peak queue is 22h; specialist-review peak queue is 36h. These are the longest validation queues in the peak data.
- Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter hold-for-specialist-review. With Complex-panel share rising to 20% in peak, specialist review and medical validation become disproportionately stressed.
- If medical validators are absent, technical validation cannot release cases that require medical validation. Technical validation utilization is already 101% in peak, so the queue will grow even if assay throughput is unchanged.
- If specialist review is absent, Complex-panel cases may accumulate in hold-for-specialist-review. The peak hold-to-medical-validation median is 48h, making this a major p90 driver.
- Adding an evening medical-validation shift can help, but only if technical validation is also available or cases are already technically validated. Otherwise the medical shift may have limited work or create a new handoff bottleneck.
- Outsourcing Complex-panel overflow can reduce specialist-review pressure, but the 12h transport delay can breach Urgent Complex-panel SLAs and adds traceability complexity.

**S4 — Courier arrival compression or delay**  
Likely sequence: **courier intake  registration/receive  pre-analytical check  recollection/manual correction loop  preparation/assay**.

- Courier intake peaks at 88% utilization with a 7.5h median queue. Arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00 create batch-like intake pressure even though the process is not formally batched at intake.
- Registration utilization rises to 85%, and error rate rises after 9h shifts. Compressed arrivals can force overtime, increasing registration errors and downstream manual accession correction.
- Pre-analytical check is at 96% utilization with a 9.5h queue. Delayed or compressed samples are more likely to be defective, increasing recollection requests. Peak pre-analytical recollection is 8.0% of received cases.
- Recollection loops are especially damaging: recollection-to-receive median is 60h in peak, and 520 cases return to receive. These cases consume registration, receive, and pre-analytical capacity again.
- If courier arrivals are smoothed but pre-analytical check is not strengthened, the bottleneck simply moves from courier intake to pre-analytical check.
- If early defect triage is introduced but relies on registration clerks to explain errors to physicians, registration can become overloaded. The triage must be automated or physician-portal-driven to avoid shifting the bottleneck upstream.

**S5 — Compound winter wave**  
Likely sequence: **intake burst  pre-analytical/accession  preparation/batch  assay/repeat  technical validation  medical/specialist review**, with rapid migration.

- In a compound scenario, no single station is the only bottleneck. The system has several near-capacity resources: pre-analytical 96%, accession correction 91%, preparation 89%, assay 98%, technical validation 101%, medical validation 93%, specialist review 87%, courier intake 88%.
- Relieving one station without WIP caps and synchronized surge can overload the next. For example, smoothing courier intake can push more samples into pre-analytical check; fixing pre-analytical check can push more cases into preparation and assay; fixing assay can push more completed work into technical and medical validation.
- The robust response is not to “fix the current bottleneck” in isolation. It is to slow upstream intake when downstream is saturated, protect Urgent/Complex capacity, contain defects early, and activate validation surge only when technical validation can feed it.

# 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign is organized around case-type stress, not historical average cycle time. Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure cases stress different resources. The goal is to keep Urgent within 18h, protect Routine p90, prevent Complex-panel validation collapse, and reduce rework loops without adding capacity everywhere.

**3.1 Smooth intake and contain pre-analytical defects early — I7, I8, I6**  
- Shift some courier pickup slots away from the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes to create a flatter intake profile.  
- Automate registration for orders with complete digital metadata. Keep manual registration for exceptions only.  
- Introduce a 2-hour pre-analytical defect triage. Poor samples are rejected or sent to recollection before test preparation, batch wait, assay, or validation. Use automated physician notifications rather than registration clerks explaining every error.

Why this is robust:  
- It attacks the upstream loops that become much worse in peak: pre-analytical recollection rises to 8.0%, manual accession correction rises to 4.3%, and recollection-to-receive takes 60h.  
- It reduces the number of defective or invalid cases entering capacity-intensive preparation, assay, and validation steps.  
- It reduces registration overtime pressure, which is important because registration error rate rises after 9h shifts.

Assumptions and failure modes:  
- Assumes courier partners can shift pickup slots and that sample stability is acceptable.  
- Assumes digital metadata is complete enough for automation. If metadata is poor, registration errors may simply move to pre-analytical check.  
- If early triage is too aggressive, it can increase recollection/cancel volume and physician burden.  
- Downstream overload risk: pre-analytical check and physician communication. If triage is manual and clerk-driven, registration can become the next bottleneck.

**3.2 Create an accession-correction surge pool — I4**  
- Cross-train two technical validators to perform accession correction as a surge pool, not as a permanent transfer.  
- Activate the surge pool only when accession correction is the bottleneck and technical validation is not.  
- Use short, scheduled blocks, for example morning or midday blocks when technical validation queue is low.

Why this is robust:  
- Accession correction has only two specialists and a 14h peak queue. It is a small resource that unblocks many cases.  
- Manual accession correction in peak can itself create recollection: 26% of manual corrections go to recollection. Faster correction reduces loop risk.

Assumptions and failure modes:  
- Assumes cross-training can be completed within the 4-week window and that the work is transferable.  
- The main failure mode is creating a technical-validation shortage. Technical validation is already at 101% utilization in peak.  
- Downstream overload risk: technical validation. The policy must be trigger-based, not standing.

**3.3 Use protected lanes and WIP caps instead of strict Urgent-first — limited I1**  
- Do not use a strict Urgent-first dispatch rule. Instead, create protected capacity lanes:  
  - **Urgent lane:** protected preparation, assay, technical validation, and medical validation slots; maximum wait thresholds; contractual 18h SLA protection.  
  - **Complex-panel lane:** protected assay and medical/specialist validation capacity; capacity should be measured in assay steps, not just cases, because Complex-panel cases require multiple assays.  
  - **Routine lane:** flexible, batchable, and able to absorb slack, but with WIP caps to prevent starvation.  
- Enforce WIP caps at each major station. If a downstream station exceeds its cap, upstream intake or registration is slowed rather than pushing more work into the bottleneck.  
- Treat Repeat-after-failure cases as high-assay-load cases: schedule them on reliable machines, avoid M2 night usage where possible, and count them as double assay capacity where practical.

Why this is robust:  
- It protects the cases with contractual or validation-heavy risk without assuming all case types can be prioritized simultaneously.  
- It prevents the common failure of “helping Urgent” by starving Routine and Complex-panel, which would increase total WIP and p90 tail.  
- It directly addresses the peak problem where Urgent and Complex-panel share rises while Routine still needs to meet a 72h target.

Assumptions and failure modes:  
- Assumes case classification is accurate and that capacity reservations can be enforced in scheduling.  
- If Urgent/Complex reservations are too high, Routine cases will starve and routine p90 will worsen.  
- If reservations are too low, Urgent SLA risk remains.  
- Downstream overload risk: Routine preparation/assay if Urgent/Complex are overprotected; medical/specialist review if Complex-panel cases are protected too strongly without validation surge.

**3.4 Specialize assay machines with health-based reallocation and controlled batching — I3, I2**  
- Default routing:  
  - **M1:** Urgent, Complex-panel, Repeat-after-failure, and other high-risk or high-value cases.  
  - **M2:** Routine cases, using its faster runtime.  
- Do not route all Routine cases to M2 unconditionally. M2 has a higher failure rate, and peak repeat-after-failure cases are clustered after M2 usage and night shifts.  
- Use routine batching with fixed cutoffs. Default batch size can remain 12. Increase to 24 only when M2 is healthy, routine queue is high, and Urgent/Complex WIP is below cap.  
- Replace ad-hoc Urgent bypass of batch wait with scheduled Urgent slots. The current bypass helps Urgent cases but fragments machine schedules; in peak, Urgent bypass drops to 58%, showing that the system is not cleanly separating urgent and batched work.

Why this is robust:  
- It protects critical cases on the more reliable machine while using M2 speed for Routine volume.  
- It reduces machine schedule fragmentation by making Urgent work scheduled rather than random bypass.  
- It makes batching explicit and time-boxed, so Routine efficiency does not silently delay Urgent cases.

Assumptions and failure modes:  
- Assumes M1 and M2 can be partitioned and monitored by machine.  
- If M2 degrades, routine repeats can rise and consume assay capacity twice.  
- If M1 is overreserved or fails, Urgent and Complex-panel cases lose capacity.  
- If batch size is increased too aggressively, Urgent cases may wait longer and machine fragmentation may return.  
- Downstream overload risk: M2 if routine volume is too high; technical validation and medical validation if repeats increase; M1 if routine is shifted onto it during M2 degradation.

**3.5 Add validation surge and specialist resilience — I5, with I10 as scenario-specific overflow**  
- Add a temporary evening medical-validation shift during peaks.  
- Stagger it with technical validation so medical validators receive technically validated work. If technical validation is not available in the evening, the medical shift should be limited to already-validated cases or paired with a technical-validation handoff.  
- Use asynchronous specialist review for non-urgent Complex-panel cases where possible. Pre-schedule work around the known second-Friday specialist absence.  
- Keep Complex-panel outsourcing as a scenario-specific overflow, not a default. Use it when specialist review or medical validation is saturated, but only for non-urgent Complex-panel cases unless the 12h transport delay still allows the Urgent SLA.

Why this is robust:  
- Medical validation and specialist review are the longest validation queues in peak: 22h and 36h respectively.  
- Complex-panel cases are disproportionately likely to need medical validation and specialist review, so validation surge is essential when mix shifts.  
- Asynchronous specialist review and planned outsourcing reduce dependence on a single specialist.

Assumptions and failure modes:  
- Assumes medical validators can work evening shifts and that technical validation can feed them.  
- If technical validation is the true bottleneck, adding medical validation alone may not improve cycle time and may create idle medical capacity.  
- If specialist review is not addressed, Complex-panel cases may still be stuck in hold-for-specialist-review.  
- Outsourcing adds transport delay and traceability complexity.  
- Downstream overload risk: technical validation if medical surge is not synchronized; transport/partner lab if outsourcing is used too broadly; local Urgent Complex-panel cases if partner transport delay is incompatible with the 18h SLA.

**3.6 Interventions to avoid as standalone fixes**  
- **I1 strict Urgent-first:** not robust. It can starve Routine and Complex-panel cases, increase WIP, and fragment machine schedules. Use protected Urgent slots instead.  
- **I9 hold Routine releases until batch report is ready:** not robust. Release is not the bottleneck in the data; medical validation to release is only 2h normal and 5h peak. Holding Routine releases adds waiting and worsens routine p90.  
- **Blanket capacity addition:** not robust. Adding capacity at one station without WIP caps and case-type routing usually moves the bottleneck downstream.

Strict I1 and I9 are deliberately not adopted as standalone bundles. Strict Urgent-first is replaced by protected lanes and WIP caps. Holding Routine releases is avoided because release is not the bottleneck and would add waiting without reducing upstream rework.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Redesign-caused priority starvation:** Protected Urgent/Complex lanes and M1 dedication can starve Routine cases, especially when Urgent/Complex demand is lower than expected. This can increase Routine WIP, worsen routine p90, and create idle reserved capacity. The system must monitor Routine aging, not only Urgent SLA.
- **Redesign-caused M2 dependence:** Routing Routine cases to M2 makes the system sensitive to M2 failure, night-shift repeat clustering, and batch-size errors. If M2 degrades, routine repeats can consume assay capacity twice and push technical/medical validation into overload.
- **Validation scarcity remains:** Medical validators and specialist reviewers are still scarce. An evening medical-validation shift helps, but it may not cover simultaneous technical-validation backlog, specialist absence, and Urgent/Complex surge. Asynchronous specialist review may reduce but not eliminate the single-specialist risk.
- **Upstream triage can create new loops:** Early pre-analytical defect triage reduces downstream waste, but it can increase recollection and cancellation volume. If physician communication is manual, registration clerks can become overloaded. If triage rules are too aggressive, false rejects can increase patient burden and resampling time.
- **Courier dependency:** Smoothing courier pickups depends on courier cooperation, route capacity, and sample stability. Winter disruptions can still compress arrivals, and shifting slots may move the peak rather than eliminate it.
- **Cross-training conflict:** Accession-correction surge uses technical validators. If accession correction and technical validation are both backlogged, the lab cannot fully use the surge pool without worsening technical validation.
- **Outsourcing complexity:** Complex-panel outsourcing reduces specialist-review pressure but adds 12h transport delay, traceability complexity, and partner-lab variability. It is not a universal fix, especially for Urgent Complex-panel cases.
- **Trigger lag:** If policies are switched only on daily data, bottlenecks may migrate before the response. Peak periods require hourly review of leading indicators, especially intake, assay, and validation queues.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

During peak periods, monitor the following leading indicators daily, and review them hourly when any critical threshold is active. These are leading indicators because they predict downstream queue growth, SLA breach, and rework before final cycle-time metrics deteriorate.

| Leading indicator | Why it leads | Target / warning / critical |
|---|---|---|
| **Courier intake arrival rate and intake queue** | Predicts registration and pre-analytical pressure from arrival spikes | Target: intake queue <3h and arrivals near plan. Warning: 3–5h or arrivals > plan +20%. Critical: >7.5h or arrivals > plan +40%. |
| **Pre-analytical queue and defect/recollection rate** | Predicts test preparation, assay, and validation load from upstream defects | Queue target <4h; warning 4–8h; critical >12h. Recollection rate target <5%; warning 5–8%; critical >10%. Manual correction queue target <6h; warning 6–10h; critical >14h. |
| **Accession correction queue and technical validation queue** | Accession correction unblocks many cases; technical validation is the peak over-capacity point | Accession queue target <6h; warning 6–10h; critical >14h. Technical validation queue target <8h; warning 8–12h; critical >16h. |
| **Preparation/batch wait and Urgent preparation age** | Predicts assay queue and Urgent SLA risk from batching and priority fragmentation | Routine batch wait target <8h; warning 8–14h; critical >22h. Urgent prep wait target <4h; warning 4–6h; critical >8h. |
| **Assay machine queue and repeat rate by machine** | Predicts technical/medical validation load and rework amplification | M1/M2 queue target <10h; warning 10–16h; critical >24h. M2 repeat rate target <10%; warning 10–15%; critical >18%. Monitor repeat-after-failure WIP separately. |
| **Medical/specialist queue and case aging** | Predicts release delay, Urgent SLA breach, and Complex-panel p90 deterioration | Medical queue target <8h; warning 8–12h; critical >22h. Specialist queue target <12h; warning 12–24h; critical >36h. Urgent age target <8h; warning >12h. Complex age target <18h; warning >24h. |

**Lagging outcome metrics** should be reviewed weekly or after peak episodes, not used as the primary daily switching signal:  
- p50 and p90 cycle time by case type, especially Urgent 18h, Routine 72h, Complex-panel p90, and Repeat-after-failure p90.  
- Urgent SLA breach rate.  
- Routine SLA breach rate.  
- Overall rework rate, with normal baseline 7.5% and peak baseline 11.8%.  
- Percentage of cases with at least one queue over 12h, with normal baseline 22% and peak baseline 54%.  
- Cancellation and recollection rates, especially recollection-to-receive loops.
