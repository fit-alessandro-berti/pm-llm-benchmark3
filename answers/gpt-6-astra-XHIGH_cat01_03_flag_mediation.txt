## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

The observed contrast is an association between cases under one policy, not the effect of introducing that policy. All 20 cases are observed after introduction; there is no untreated policy period. The flag is a decision signal generated from risk score and possibly complexity. Changing its display, its stored value, its routing consequences, and the underlying risk are different interventions.

Use the following operational versions throughout:

- **P0:** the current display, priority, review-routing, escalation, staffing, and scheduling rules.
- **P1:** hide the flag badge from staff at every stage, while retaining the score and other human information. Keep the stored flag, automated priority assignment, and automatic review routing as in P0. Human decisions may respond to the missing badge. Staff may infer flag status from score or lane, so this is an information-availability intervention, not guaranteed blinding. Disabling backend rules as well would be a different P1.
- **P2:** keep display and priority triage; replace flag-based mandatory review with a prespecified discretionary routing protocol using the existing score, complexity, and human findings. Preserve any separately established mandatory-review requirements and permit documented human overrides. This does not mean all flagged cases skip review.
- **P3:** keep routing rules and add one qualified reviewer per staffed shift to each treated review pool, reserved for flagged cases. Keep existing staffing, reviewer qualifications, review standards, and senior capacity unchanged. Use FIFO within the protected lane, subject to the same documented urgent exceptions. Capacity is additional, not borrowed from unflagged cases.
- **P4:** distinguish **P4a**, additional qualified senior capacity with unchanged escalation criteria, from **P4b**, a prespecified change to escalation criteria with unchanged senior capacity. Retain manual review. These are different treatments and should not be pooled.

Policy versions must also specify operating calendars, urgent exceptions, and implementation fidelity. Let a cluster contain all cases sharing review or senior capacity. For the same target arrival population and initial queue-state distribution, let (T_i^p) be arrival-to-close time under system policy (p), including other cases' responses. Define baseline flag eligibility (F_i^*) using the original rule, regardless of whether a policy displays it. Follow all enrolled cases to closure; report censoring if that is impossible. Differences below are intervention minus reference, so a negative time effect is beneficial.

I use **system-level policy-component effects and dynamic interventional mediation analogues**. E3 and E4 replace feasible routing or scheduling mechanisms rather than setting an arbitrary numerical wait or assigning a waiting time to a case that never enters review. Their effects are not additive shares of E1 or E7: routing, waiting, escalation, and capacity interact.

For comparison, a conventional natural indirect effect would involve a nested counterfactual such as (E[T(1,M(1))-T(1,M(0))]). Here “1” does not identify a unique flag intervention, manual waiting is undefined when review is absent, and an individual's mediator trajectory depends on other cases' treatment. Such cross-world combinations also require strong mediator–outcome assumptions that post-triage findings can violate. I do not identify or estimate natural effects. A controlled direct effect would require a specified feasible joint mediator regime; simply including review, escalation, and resources as regression controls does not produce one. Nor do independently manipulable policy components by themselves establish the assumptions needed for formal separable effects.

Priority changes when a case reaches downstream queues, not just its initial wait. Review findings and urgency assessments can be affected by the displayed flag, triage, and review delay; they then influence escalation and completion. These are exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounders, so ordinary adjustment can block part of the effect or induce selection bias. Conditioning on reviewed or escalated cases can also open associations between flag handling and hidden risk.

Shared queues create interference: adding a review changes other cases' waits, and faster reviews can increase senior arrivals. Staff may respond to queue length by changing routing thresholds; previous routing then changes subsequent congestion, producing treatment–confounder feedback. The time-indexed system, rather than independent case-level potential outcomes, is therefore the appropriate unit of analysis. `arrival_load` is only a coarse proxy for an initial state and does not control these evolving conditions. Several displayed arrows, especially priority-to-review scheduling, are hypotheses requiring additional logs.

There are no flagged low-complexity cases. Conditional means in a row generally **do not sum** to its total. The correct accounting identity weights optional stages by their occurrence probabilities:

\[
E[T]=E[W_T]+E[S_T]+P(R=1)E[W_R+S_R\mid R=1]
+P(E=1)E[W_E+S_E\mid E=1]+E[Z].
\]

Thus the flag-1 minus flag-0 difference of 8.34 hours is descriptively

\[
-2.24\;(\text{triage wait})-0.35\;(\text{triage service})
+5.75\;(\text{review wait contribution})+1.60\;(\text{review service contribution})
+2.90\;(\text{escalation wait contribution})+0.75\;(\text{senior service contribution})
-0.07\;(\text{residual}).
\]

These are arithmetic contributions from different observed route mixtures, not causal mediation estimates.

Flagged cases finish triage after 0.84 hours on average, versus 3.43 hours for unflagged cases, yet close after 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All ten flagged cases undergo review, compared with four unflagged cases; escalation occurs in six versus two. Among reviewed cases, the post-triage gap averages 6.50 versus 1.88 hours, while review service averages 2.70 versus 2.75 hours. This supports a downstream waiting and routing explanation more directly than uniformly slower review execution. Flagged high-load cases have longer review gaps than flagged normal-load cases. However, risk scores do not overlap between flag groups, complexity composition differs, and escalation subgroups are selected after treatment. Calendar effects, readiness requirements, and resource allocation may explain apparent congestion. Stage gaps measure elapsed time, including possible off-hours or handoffs, rather than verified time ready and waiting. These contrasts locate delays; they do not identify the effect of changing flag handling.

Resource assignment is potentially a mediator and an exposure-induced confounder; controlling for it indiscriminately is not a solution.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does flag-triggered handling change outcomes for similar measured risk? | Recover the actual flag rule. With many cases near a verified cutoff, estimate discontinuities in triage time, review occurrence, and cycle time; use fuzzy RD if adherence is imperfect. This identifies a local bundled assignment effect, not separate mediators. If the rule also uses complexity, analyze valid within-rule boundaries | Running score, prespecified local bandwidth, complexity where part of assignment, arrival calendar; only baseline variables for the total effect | Smooth potential outcomes and baseline composition at the cutoff; no score manipulation or coincident rule change; sufficiently stable background congestion | Score density and heaping, covariate continuity, placebo cutoffs, bandwidth checks, routing first stage. Here score groups are separated by 0.61–0.64; the cutoff itself is unknown | A precise triage jump without a review or cycle jump would weaken the downstream policy explanation; no usable local support makes RD unavailable |
| Are long stage gaps actually queueing? | Log ready-to-review and ready-to-escalate times, then reconstruct everyone waiting and in service immediately before each entry. For a nonrandom policy rollout, a controlled interrupted time series or difference-in-differences analysis could estimate a bundled policy effect if comparison systems have credible parallel untreated trends, no simultaneous staffing changes, and no queue spillovers. Pretrends and placebo intervention dates would be essential. These 20 post-policy cases alone cannot support that design.

Do not adjust for `decision_outcome` when estimating total cycle-time effects: it is potentially affected by handling. Likewise, conditioning on actual escalation estimates a selected association, not the effect of assigning escalation. For a total policy effect, evolving queues belong to the mechanism; for a dynamic scheduling effect, queue history can also confound subsequent actions and must be modeled in its correct temporal order.

Pilot **P3**, because it targets the largest observed conditional waiting disparity while preserving review requirements.

- **Eligibility:** all arrivals to participating connected review-and-senior queue systems; protected service applies to cases meeting the original flag rule and routed to review. Evaluate all arrivals to capture spillovers.
- **Assignment:** randomize whole connected systems to P3 or P0, stratified by baseline workload. If only one system exists, use randomized week-long blocks, prespecified transition periods, and backlog carryover analysis. Keep routing standards fixed and analyze assignment regardless of uptake.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** add one qualified reviewer per staffed shift; do not reduce baseline unflagged capacity. Pause the pilot for assessment if senior backlog or unflagged 90th-percentile waiting exceeds its matched baseline by 20% for two consecutive shifts.
- **Primary outcome:** mean arrival-to-close time across all enrolled arrivals. **Secondary outcomes:** flagged mean and 90th-percentile cycle time, stage readiness waits, unflagged delays, senior backlog, review/escalation rates, audited decision errors, rework, and staffing cost. Follow cases beyond block boundaries.
- **Minimum data:** all arrivals and stage timestamps, queue-ready/entry times, resources and staffing calendars, priority changes, routing reasons and findings, score and complexity, queue states, assignment/adherence, and independently adjudicated outcomes with common follow-up.
- **Stopping:** any serious missed-risk event, deterioration beyond a prespecified quality noninferiority margin, persistent guardrail breach, or unusable timestamp/adherence data. Set sample size and futility rules from larger baseline data; twenty cases are insufficient.

## 7. Reproducible PM4Py-oriented Python

The code requires Pandas, NumPy, and PM4Py. It uses the supplied local timestamps without inferring a timezone; duration calculations assume a common clock and no offset change during the observation window. `Register` and `Close` are point events at the recorded arrival and close milestones; their unknown work durations are not estimated. Triage, Manual Review, and Escalation are interval events, emitted only when present. `org:resource` is populated only where a stage-specific resource is recorded.

The explicit timestamp calculations are authoritative for the stage table. PM4Py performance DFGs summarize timing along observed adjacent activities; their exact interval convention can depend on the installed implementation. They do not by themselves isolate readiness waiting or causal effects. The code prints empirical edge gaps separately to make that distinction inspectable. No case-service/waiting helper is needed.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** independent pre-routing assessment reveals that flagged cases require substantially more specialist preparation or necessary investigation, and this explains the downstream gaps. I would shift from flag-driven excess routing toward justified case-mix differences and targeted specialist support.
2. **Queue interference:** added protected review capacity speeds flagged reviews but creates larger senior backlogs or unflagged delays, leaving system cycle time worse. I would stop P3 expansion and evaluate coordinated review/senior capacity or a different scheduling policy.
3. **Decision quality:** blinded adjudication shows that faster handling materially increases serious errors, or that proposed review reductions lose valuable findings. I would favor quality-preserving throughput changes and reject a time-only justification for P2 or the tested P3 implementation.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** review gaps mainly reflect incomplete documentation, closed operating hours, or inconsistent clocks, and service intervals include extensive pauses or pooled-resource overlap. I would replace the queue interpretation with readiness/calendar mechanisms and redesign the capacity pilot.
5. **Positivity or overlap:** richer data still contain no comparable discretionary routes at relevant risk levels, or valid local-overlap/randomized evidence shows no downstream effect of flag-triggered handling. I would withdraw observational mediation claims, restrict the target population, and reconsider whether this policy component is the right intervention.
6. **Reviewer behavior:** removing mandates induces compensatory escalation, or added capacity lowers reviewers' thresholds, lengthens reviews, or changes expertise enough to offset waiting benefits. I would revise the fixed-routing/skill assumption and test the resulting behavioral policy rather than recommend the original P3 version.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed, flagged cases complete triage in 0.84 hours on average versus 3.43 hours for unflagged cases, but their total cycles average 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. Review occurs in all ten flagged cases and four unflagged cases; escalation occurs in six and two. Reviewed flagged cases have substantially longer post-triage gaps, while mean review service duration is similar. Both groups contain two rejections, which says nothing by itself about decision accuracy.

A plausible mechanism is that priority advances flagged cases through triage, after which frequent review and escalation expose them to scarce downstream resources. Congestion can transmit delays between cases, and faster upstream handling can move or intensify a downstream bottleneck. Additional badge-induced handling and resource selection remain possible pathways, although the residual-to-close comparison does not show a positive flagged delay.

What remains unidentified is the causal contribution of flag handling, review occurrence, waiting, escalation, and hidden case risk. Score ranges do not overlap, all flagged cases receive review, queue readiness is unrecorded, and no alternative policy is observed. Cross-world mediation and independent-case assumptions are particularly implausible here.

The most useful next evidence is a limited, randomized P3 pilot adding protected review capacity while retaining routing standards. Assignment must respect shared queues; measurement must cover all arrivals, senior congestion, readiness times, and independently assessed quality. A reduction in verified review waiting and system cycle time without quality loss would support expansion; displacement downstream would motivate a coordinated capacity intervention.
