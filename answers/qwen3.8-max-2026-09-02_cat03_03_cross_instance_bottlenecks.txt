## 1. Interaction-graph description

The useful representation is not a single case-centric DFG, but a **typed, temporal, object-centric interaction graph**. Jobs look independent in a job-flattened log because each job has its own component and its own activity sequence. The coupling appears when jobs share **batches, tools, component lots, calibration resources, and shipment consolidations**.

Important node types:

- **Job**: J1–J6, the production-order carriers.
- **Component**: C1–C6, directly required by jobs.
- **ComponentLot**: L7, L8, quality/release containers for components.
- **Machine**: CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1.
- **Tool**: FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3.
- **Batch**: B1, B2, synchronization containers for oven processing.
- **Shipment**: S1, S2, S3, dispatch consolidation containers.

Important edge types and qualifiers:

- `requires`: Job  Component. Direct static relation.
- `contains`: ComponentLot  Component. Direct static relation.
- `batches`: Batch  Job. Direct static relation; creates an AND-join before oven processing.
- `consolidates`: Shipment  Job. Direct static relation; creates a dispatch AND-join.
- `uses` / `related_to`: event-derived relation between jobs, machines, tools, batches, components. Directly observed when an event references multiple objects.
- `acquires` / `releases`: Job  Tool. Directly observed for FIX-A; partly inferred for other tools.
- `quarantines` / `releases`: quality event  ComponentLot  Components  Jobs. Direct for lot/component, inconsistently direct for jobs.
- `waits_for`: inferred edge used only as an explanatory hypothesis, not as a logged activity.

Higher-order motifs:

1. **Batch synchronization motif**: B1 `batches` J1, J2, J3; the 12:05 `Batch Cure` event relates B1, OVEN-1, RACK-7, J1, J2, J3, C1, C2, C3. This is not a simple pairwise edge; it is a hyperedge that forces early-ready jobs to wait for the latest-ready job.
2. **Tool-mediated machine coupling motif**: J3 holds FIX-A from 09:00 to 11:40; J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50; J4’s `Machine Process` at 11:45 relates J4, C4, CNC-2, FIX-A. The apparent CNC idle gap is coupled to a tool lifecycle outside the machine view.
3. **Component-lot quality gate motif**: L7 contains C2 and C5; J2 requires C2 and J5 requires C5. The 10:20 `Quarantine Lot` and 13:50 `Release Lot` events relate L7, C2, C5, J2, J5, but job flattening may omit them because extraction only used direct job relations.
4. **Shipment consolidation motif**: S1 consolidates J1 and J4. J1 is packed at 13:30, J4 at 19:10, S1 dispatches at 19:25. The dispatch gate is a shipment-level fan-in, not a job-level activity.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

The following calculations are **counterfactual decompositions**, not causal proof. Each states what must be assumed for the waiting to be treated as avoidable.

1. **J1 synchronization waiting in B1**

   - Ready for batch: 08:10.
   - B1 starts: 12:05.
   - Waiting: `12:05 - 08:10 = 3 h 55 min`.

   Counterfactual: if J1 could have been processed individually, or in an earlier batch, when it was ready, and if readiness is trusted, then 3 h 55 min is synchronization-induced waiting. This assumes OVEN-1/RACK-7 capacity would have been available and that B1’s batch rule is not externally mandatory.

2. **J2 synchronization waiting in B1**

   - Ready for batch: 08:25.
   - B1 starts: 12:05.
   - Waiting: `12:05 - 08:25 = 3 h 40 min`.

   Counterfactual: if J2 could have been processed before J3 became ready, 3 h 40 min would be avoidable. This assumes J2’s component C2 was truly usable despite the later L7 release issue and that the supervisor override was not needed for process integrity.

3. **J4 delay plausibly associated with FIX-A**

   - J4 reaches CNC-2: 09:50.
   - J4 `Machine Process` starts: 11:45.
   - Observed pre-process interval: `11:45 - 09:50 = 1 h 55 min`.
   - FIX-A held by J3 until: 11:40.
   - Interval during which FIX-A was unavailable after J4 reached CNC-2: `11:40 - 09:50 = 1 h 50 min`.

   Counterfactual: if FIX-A had been available at 09:50, or if a second fixture existed, J4 could plausibly have used CNC-2 during the recorded CNC idle window 10:05–10:47. A conservative avoidable-delay estimate is approximately `11:45 - 10:05 = 1 h 40 min`; a stronger estimate is up to 1 h 55 min. This does not prove causality because CNC idle may reflect setup, operator, or material constraints not recorded.

4. **J1 delay imposed by S1 consolidation**

   - J1 packed: 13:30.
   - J4 packed: 19:10.
   - S1 dispatches: 19:25.
   - J1 pack-to-dispatch waiting: `19:25 - 13:30 = 5 h 55 min`.
   - Waiting for shipment partner: `19:10 - 13:30 = 5 h 40 min`.

   Counterfactual: if S1 could have been split, or if J1 could have been dispatched independently, J1 could have left much earlier. Using the shipment-flattened median handling time of 1.1 h as a rough post-pack handling allowance, an independent dispatch around 14:40 is plausible, giving a consolidation-associated excess of roughly 4 h 45 min. This assumes customer, carrier, and documentation rules allow early dispatch.

5. **J6 delay plausibly associated with CAL-3**

   - J6 ready for final test: 16:05.
   - J5 tests: 16:10–16:35.
   - CAL-3 recalibration: 16:35–17:20.
   - J6 tests: 17:25–17:50.
   - J6 ready-to-test waiting: `17:25 - 16:05 = 1 h 20 min`.
   - If J6 could have started immediately after J5, the earliest plausible start without recalibration is 16:35.
   - Excess waiting after J5 completion: `17:25 - 16:35 = 50 min`.

   Counterfactual: if CAL-3 could have been recalibrated offline, or if calibration were not required between J5 and J6, J6 could have tested around 16:35. This assumes TEST-1 availability and that J5 legitimately had priority because it was ready at 16:00, five minutes before J6.

6. **L7 lot-release coupling**

   - L7 quarantine: 10:20.
   - L7 release: 13:50.
   - Lot gate duration: `13:50 - 10:20 = 3 h 30 min`.
   - J5 ready for B2: 14:40, i.e. 50 min after release.

   Counterfactual: if L7 had been released earlier, J5 could have been ready earlier. However, B2 still could not necessarily start earlier because J4 was ready at 13:10, RACK-7 was cleaning/quarantined until 14:35, and B2 requires both J4 and J5. Thus the lot gate is a strong coupling mechanism, but its marginal delay on B2 is not separately provable from the supplied times.

7. **J4 waiting in B2**

   - J4 ready: 13:10.
   - B2 starts: 14:50.
   - Waiting: `14:50 - 13:10 = 1 h 40 min`.

   Counterfactual: if B2 could have been split, or if J5 had been ready earlier and RACK-7 available, J4 could have started sooner. This calculation shows synchronization again, but the responsible constraint may be J5 readiness, RACK-7 cleaning, or batch policy.

## 4. What flattening misses

**Job flattening**

A job flattening makes visible the sequence of activities attached directly to each job: release, stage, machine process, inspect, pack, dispatch. It can show job-level cycle times and activity frequencies.

It hides or duplicates:

- Batch synchronization waiting before `Batch Cure`, because the batch event may be represented as a single point rather than a waiting interval.
- Tool waiting, because there is no explicit `Wait for Tool` activity.
- Lot quarantine/release events when the extraction only keeps events directly related to Job. This is exactly why L7’s 10:20 and 13:50 events may not appear for J2 and J5.
- Shipment consolidation waiting, because dispatch may be attached to the shipment object rather than the packed job.

Misleading conclusion: because no job exceeds 1.8 standard deviations above the six-job mean, the process appears stable. This can hide large waits that are distributed across objects and not represented as job activities.

**Machine flattening**

A machine flattening makes visible machine utilization, activity sequences, and gaps between machine events.

It hides or duplicates:

- Why a machine is idle. CNC-2’s 10:05–10:47 gap may be due to FIX-A contention, but the machine view does not show the tool.
- Batch-related waiting. OVEN-1 idle 12:55–14:50 may look like underutilization, while the real constraints are RACK-7 cleaning and J5 readiness.
- Shared tool and lot constraints that prevent jobs from arriving at the machine.

Misleading conclusion: low utilization suggests no machine bottleneck. In fact, low utilization can coexist with severe job waiting when jobs are blocked by batch, tool, lot, or shipment gates.

**Batch flattening**

A batch flattening makes visible batch formation, batch start, cure duration, and batch completion.

It hides or duplicates:

- Individual job readiness and waiting before batch start. B1’s 12:05 start looks normal, but J1 and J2 have waited almost four hours.
- The fact that one batch event relates to multiple jobs and components; flattening may duplicate the event or average it away.
- Interaction with RACK-7 cleaning/quarantine.

Misleading conclusion: batch processing appears efficient because cure time itself is not excessive, while the synchronization penalty before the batch is invisible.

**Tool flattening**

A tool flattening makes visible tool acquire/release, tool busy time, and calibration intervals.

It hides or duplicates:

- Jobs waiting while a tool is held. FIX-A may appear busy from 09:00 to 11:40, but the view does not show J4 blocked at CNC-2.
- The operational meaning of CAL-3 recalibration. It appears as tool activity, not as J6 waiting.
- RACK-7 cleaning as a batch gate unless batch and tool views are joined.

Misleading conclusion: tool utilization may look acceptable, while tool scarcity creates cascading job delays.

**ComponentLot flattening**

A lot flattening makes visible lot quarantine, release, and containment relations.

It hides or duplicates:

- Job impact when lot events are not linked to jobs. The prompt explicitly states that job flattening misses L7 events because extraction used only direct job relations.
- Supervisor overrides. J2 entered B1 before L7 release, but the override is in a separate system.
- Whether the lot gate is binding for downstream batches.

Misleading conclusion: lot cycle time may be treated as a quality-process metric only, not as a coupling mechanism between J2 and J5.

**Shipment flattening**

A shipment flattening makes visible `Create Shipment -> Load -> Dispatch`.

It hides or duplicates:

- Packed-job waiting for shipment partners. J1 waits from 13:30 to 19:25, but the shipment DFG only sees CreateLoadDispatch.
- The difference between shipment processing time and shipment consolidation delay.
- Customer or carrier constraints that force consolidation.

Misleading conclusion: shipment processing looks fast because CreateLoadDispatch totals about 1.1 h median, while the real delay occurs before Load.

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because machines are not the gating objects. Jobs wait for batch partners, fixtures, calibration, lot release, or shipment partners. The machine is idle because the required job-machine-tool-lot combination is not yet feasible.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

### Narrative 1: J1 from component lot to shipment delay

Objects: Job J1, Component C1, ComponentLot L8, Batch B1, Machine OVEN-1, Tool RACK-7, Shipment S1.

- **Temporal precedence**: J1 requires C1 from L8. J1 is ready for B1 at 08:10. B1 does not start until 12:05 because J3 is ready only at 11:55. J1 is packed at 13:30. S1 dispatches at 19:25 after J4 is packed at 19:10.
- **Resource feasibility**: B1 requires J1, J2, J3, OVEN-1, and RACK-7. S1 requires both J1 and J4.
- **Policy constraints**: Batch policy appears to require all B1 jobs before cure. Shipment policy appears to require both S1 jobs before dispatch.
- **Causal speculation**: J1’s long lead time is plausibly produced by two AND-join gates: B1 synchronization and S1 consolidation. This is not proof because J3’s late readiness and J4’s late pack may have independent causes.

### Narrative 2: J4 through fixture contention and batch synchronization

Objects: Job J4, Component C4, Tool FIX-A, Machine CNC-2, Batch B2, Tool RACK-7, Shipment S1.

- **Temporal precedence**: J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00. J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50. CNC-2 is idle 10:05–10:47. FIX-A is released at 11:40. J4’s `Machine Process` starts at 11:45. J4 is ready for B2 at 13:10. B2 starts at 14:50. J4 is packed at 19:10. S1 dispatches at 19:25.
- **Resource feasibility**: J4’s CNC process requires FIX-A. B2 requires J4 and J5, plus OVEN-1 and RACK-7. RACK-7 is cleaning/quarantine until 14:35.
- **Policy constraints**: There is no explicit `Wait for Tool` activity, so the wait is invisible in the job log. Batch synchronization prevents B2 from starting with J4 alone.
- **Causal speculation**: FIX-A contention plausibly delays J4’s CNC operation, which may contribute to later batch and shipment delays. However, the exact contribution to J4’s 19:10 pack time is not provable from the given data.

### Narrative 3: J5 through lot release, batch, and calibration coupling

Objects: Job J5, Component C5, ComponentLot L7, Batch B2, Machine OVEN-1, Tool RACK-7, Machine TEST-1, Tool CAL-3, Shipment S3.

- **Temporal precedence**: L7, containing C5, is quarantined at 10:20. L7 is released at 13:50. J5 becomes ready for B2 at 14:40. B2 starts at 14:50. J5 is ready for final test at 16:00 and tests 16:10–16:35. CAL-3 is recalibrated 16:35–17:20. J6 tests 17:25–17:50. S3 consolidates J5 and J6.
- **Resource feasibility**: J5 cannot proceed before L7 release under normal policy. B2 requires J5 and J4. Final testing requires TEST-1 and CAL-3.
- **Policy constraints**: Lot release is a quality gate. Calibration appears to be required between tests. S3 dispatch waits for its last job.
- **Causal speculation**: L7 release couples J2 and J5, and CAL-3 recalibration couples J5 to J6. The actual B2 start may be constrained jointly by J5 readiness, RACK-7 cleaning, and batch policy.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

1. **Dynamic batching or batch splitting**

   - **Target mechanism**: Batch synchronization in B1/B2.
   - **Expected benefit**: Reduce J1’s 3 h 55 min and J2’s 3 h 40 min B1 waits; reduce J4’s 1 h 40 min B2 wait if J4 can be processed separately.
   - **Helped objects**: J1, J2, J4; possibly OVEN-1 schedule stability.
   - **Potentially harmed**: J3 or J5 if they lose batch priority; energy efficiency if smaller batches are cured.
   - **New cost or risk**: Higher energy per unit, more oven setups, possible violation of batch quality rules.
   - **Perverse metric**: Batch utilization or oven load factor could incentivize delaying jobs to fill batches.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Compare job-level ready-to-Batch Cure waiting before and after policy change, stratified by batch, rack, and lot. Use OCEL to keep B1/B2, RACK-7, and job readiness visible.

2. **Duplicate or reserve critical tools, especially FIX-A and CAL-3**

   - **Target mechanism**: Shared-tool contention and calibration coupling.
   - **Expected benefit**: Reduce J4’s ~1 h 40–1 h 55 min CNC waiting and J6’s ~50 min calibration-associated waiting.
   - **Helped objects**: J4, J6, CNC-2, TEST-1.
   - **Potentially harmed**: J3 if FIX-A is forcibly released earlier; maintenance or calibration staff if tool usage increases.
   - **New cost or risk**: Capital cost for extra fixtures/calibration units; risk of using uncalibrated tools if calibration is compressed.
   - **Perverse metric**: Tool utilization could incentivize hoarding tools or unnecessary calibration.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Track acquire/release intervals for FIX-A and CAL-3, and join them with machine idle intervals where a job is ready. Measure “job waiting while required tool is held” rather than tool busy time alone.

3. **Shipment deconsolidation or exception dispatch policy**

   - **Target mechanism**: Shipment consolidation, especially S1.
   - **Expected benefit**: Reduce J1’s 5 h 55 min pack-to-dispatch wait; improve delivery performance for early-ready jobs.
   - **Helped objects**: J1, and potentially any early-packed job in S2/S3.
   - **Potentially harmed**: J4 if S1 is split and later shipment handling becomes more complex; logistics cost may rise.
   - **New cost or risk**: Higher freight cost, incomplete customer deliveries, more shipment paperwork.
   - **Perverse metric**: On-time dispatch rate could incentivize shipping incomplete orders or creating artificial shipments.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Measure packed-job waiting per job, not only shipment CreateDispatch time. Compare S1/S2/S3 job-level pack-to-dispatch distributions and freight cost per delivered job.

4. **Improve logging and integration, not operations**

   - **Target mechanism**: Data-integration artifacts and missing waits.
   - **Expected benefit**: Make batch, tool, lot, and shipment waits explicit; reduce false normality in flattened DFGs.
   - **Helped objects**: All jobs and resources, because diagnosis improves.
   - **Potentially harmed**: Operators if data entry becomes burdensome; analysts if incomplete historical data are overinterpreted.
   - **New cost or risk**: Logging overhead, inconsistent reason codes, gaming of wait categories.
   - **Perverse metric**: Event completeness rate could incentivize checkbox logging without accurate reasons.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Audit whether each long interval between job activities has an explicit wait object and reason. Verify that L7 quarantine/release events are linked to J2 and J5, and that supervisor overrides are connected to the affected job.

5. **Lot-release risk segmentation and synchronized quality gates**

   - **Target mechanism**: Component-lot release coupling, especially L7.
   - **Expected benefit**: Reduce the 3 h 30 min L7 gate’s impact on J5 and prevent hidden coupling between J2 and J5.
   - **Helped objects**: J2, J5, components C2/C5, downstream batches B1/B2.
   - **Potentially harmed**: Quality department if release risk increases; jobs using lots that are released too early.
   - **New cost or risk**: Nonconformance, rework, regulatory exposure.
   - **Perverse metric**: Lot cycle time could incentivize premature release.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Track quarantine-to-release time by lot, then link release time to job readiness and batch start. Monitor overrides separately to see whether bypasses are exceptional or systemic.

6. **RACK-7 cleaning parallelization or additional rack**

   - **Target mechanism**: RACK-7 cleaning/quarantine as batch gate.
   - **Expected benefit**: Reduce B2 delay risk; OVEN-1 idle 12:55–14:50 becomes usable if rack availability improves.
   - **Helped objects**: J4, J5, B2, OVEN-1.
   - **Potentially harmed**: Cleaning staff schedule; contamination control if cleaning is shortened.
   - **New cost or risk**: Extra rack cost, validation burden, cleaning quality risk.
   - **Perverse metric**: Rack utilization could incentivize delaying cleaning.
   - **Object-centric evaluation**: Measure rack cleaning end time versus batch ready time and batch start time. Check whether OVEN-1 idle decreases without increasing cleaning exceptions.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization explanation:

1. J1 and J2 “ready for batch” timestamps are unreliable because C1/C2 were not actually staged, inspected, or released.
2. OVEN-1 or RACK-7 had unrecorded mandatory downtime, cleaning, or operator unavailability between 08:25 and 12:05.
3. B1 could not legally or technically start before 12:05 due to a batch recipe, minimum batch size, or quality hold independent of J3 readiness.
4. J3’s late readiness at 11:55 was caused by a systemic upstream constraint that would also have delayed J1 and J2 under an alternative batch policy.
5. Historical experiments show that splitting B1 or B2 does not reduce total job lead time because shipment consolidation or CAL-3 calibration still delays dispatch.
6. RACK-7 was not actually required for B1, or an alternative rack was available but not recorded, meaning the observed rack constraint is a data artifact.

Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:

1. Explicit `Wait for Batch`, `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Lot`, and `Wait for Shipment` events with reason codes.
2. Job/component readiness flags and timestamps, not only activity start times.
3. Complete tool acquire/release events for FIX-A, RACK-7, and CAL-3, including calibration start/end and cleaning start/end.
4. ComponentLot quality history linking quarantine/release events to jobs, including supervisor override records in the same object graph.
5. Shipment constraints: carrier cutoff times, customer promise dates, minimum consolidation rules, and split-shipment permissions.
6. Machine downtime reason codes, operator assignments, and setup requirements, so machine idle gaps can be classified rather than inferred.
