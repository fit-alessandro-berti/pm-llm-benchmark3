# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is not best explained by one mechanism. It is a combination of case mix, warm-up, queue pressure, fatigue/circadian effects, batching, delayed quality checks, and automation interaction.

Early in the shift, service time is high because the first events include night or weekend backlog, higher complexity, and higher priority. The first bin has mean complexity 0.72, high-priority share 41%, and queue length 46. Some of the early slowness is therefore not simply “cold start” or poor performance; it is the cost of processing difficult carryover cases. Early error is moderate, not maximal, because complex cases may receive more scrutiny, and senior resources may be taking the harder cases.

As the shift progresses, complexity and priority fall. The apparent learning/warm-up effect is real in the sense that resources become faster and, under low WIP, quality remains stable or improves. Seniors improve from 15.9 to 11.8 min with stable low-WIP error. Mid-levels improve from 16.8 to 12.1 min with stable low-WIP error. Juniors improve from 19.6 to 13.7 min, and their low-WIP error falls from 5.1% to 4.4%. This pattern is consistent with warm-up or within-shift learning, but it is entangled with the fact that later cases are less complex.

The late-shift speed-up should not be interpreted as learning. By sequence position 51–75 and 76+, WIP rises sharply, queue length rises to 62 and 88, and complexity is lower. The faster service time in these bins is more plausibly pressure-driven: workers are moving through a larger queue, batching similar work, relying more on automation, or reducing verification. This is a speed-quality tradeoff, not evidence that workers have become better at the task.

Error rates are therefore not monotonic. Early error reflects complex carryover and junior inexperience. Mid-shift error falls because complexity is lower and WIP is manageable. Late error rises when WIP becomes high, especially for complex cases. The WIP/complexity table shows the interaction clearly: high WIP with high complexity has 10.9% error, while low WIP with low complexity has 2.1% error. Under low WIP, learning and case mix dominate, so errors remain stable or fall. Under high WIP, pressure dominates, so errors rise for all groups, most strongly for juniors and night staff.

Shift patterns support this integrated view. The Day shift starts with lower queue but ends with high queue, and its last two hours have the highest error rate and fastest service time. That is consistent with late demand pressure. The Early shift starts with a large backlog but clears it, ending with lower queue and lower late error. The Late shift inherits day backlog and shows high late error. The Night shift has fewer cases but a high share of urgent, complex exceptions, so service time is slower and error is moderate rather than minimal.

Batching and delayed quality checks add measurement artifacts. The Document Review batching by Junior_H and Junior_I creates very short service times that may look like high performance but may also reflect reduced verification. Quality Check being delayed until the end of shift means that corrections can appear as late-shift error bursts even if the underlying mistakes occurred earlier. Therefore, late error rates may partly reflect detection lag, not only late performance.

Automation also matters. Bot_Risk makes Risk Classification instant, which lowers service time. But if human reviewers correct Bot_Risk more often under high queue pressure, then automation can create hidden rework. The error may not appear until later, and the apparent speed gain may be offset by downstream correction.

In short: service time falls because cases become less complex, workers warm up, and queue pressure induces faster processing. Errors rise only in some conditions because pressure, fatigue, batching, delayed QC, and automation corrections become dominant when WIP is high. Under low WIP, learning and case mix dominate, so quality can improve or remain stable.

    log,

log_sorted = log.sort_values(["org:resource", "time:timestamp"]).reset_index(drop=True)

log_sorted["gap_min"] = (
    log_sorted.groupby("org:resource")["time:timestamp"]
    .diff().dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
)

log_sorted["same_resource"] = (
    log_sorted["org:resource"] == log_sorted["org:resource"].shift()
)

log_sorted["same_activity"] = (
    log_sorted["concept:name"] == log_sorted["concept:name"].shift()
)

    (log_sorted["gap_min"] < 3)
    & log_sorted["same_resource"]
    & log_sorted["same_activity"]
)

log_sorted["batch_id"] = (~log_sorted["batch_candidate"]).cumsum()

# 4. Intervention options

**1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers**

- Expected benefit: may reduce fatigue-related late errors, especially in long shifts or when workers are exposed to sustained high WIP.
- Risk: a blanket cap may ignore demand peaks, circadian differences, and handover effects. It may increase next-shift WIP, create incomplete cases, or push work into already pressured shifts.
- Monitor: error and rework by `minutes_since_shift_start`; WIP/queue at shift end and next shift start; handover cases; waiting time; service time; SLA; overtime; case completion rate.

**2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift**

- Expected benefit: reduces early complex backlog, allows warm-up, and may lower early service time and early error.
- Risk: complex cases may wait longer and breach SLAs. If they accumulate into late shift, they may enter the worst zone: high complexity plus high WIP.
- Monitor: complexity mix by sequence position; wait time for complex cases; error/rework by complexity and position; queue length; high-complexity first-hour share; senior case mix.

**3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70**

- Expected benefit: gives managers an early signal to add staffing, pause low-priority work, reroute cases, or trigger breaks. It can directly target the strongest observed risk zone.
- Risk: alert fatigue if threshold is too low or too noisy. WIP at start may not capture real-time pressure. The alert may slow throughput if not paired with a clear response protocol.
- Monitor: WIP distribution; share of events with WIP >70; error/rework above threshold; service time; alert response time; staffing; queue length; Bot correction rate; case age.

**4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias**

- Expected benefit: reduces selection bias, improves fairness, and creates a cleaner experiment to identify true resource effects.
- Risk: random assignment may give complex cases to juniors without adequate support, increasing error. It may ignore skill matching and operational constraints.
- Monitor: case mix by resource; error/rework by resource and complexity; SLA; waiting time; workload fairness; rework root cause; resource satisfaction.

**5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours**

- Expected benefit: may reduce fatigue and late-shift error, especially under high WIP and complex tasks.
- Risk: reduces available capacity. If breaks are not covered, WIP may rise further. Breaks may not fix queue pressure if staffing is inadequate.
- Monitor: error/service time before and after breaks; WIP/queue; rework; break compliance; next-hour performance; case completion; staff fatigue indicators.

**6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift**

- Expected benefit: reduces late-shift QC bursts and may lower immediate WIP, allowing more processing before shift end.
- Risk: delayed error detection can increase customer impact, next-shift backlog, and downstream rework. It may hide late-shift quality problems rather than solve them. This should not be framed as removing quality checks.
- Monitor: QC lag; share of QC in last 45 minutes; next-shift rework; error escapes; customer complaints; SLA; rework cost; error rate by priority; case age.

**7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks**

- Expected benefit: increases capacity during the Day shift’s late demand peak, reduces WIP, handles complex/urgent cases, and may provide mentoring.
- Risk: cost. Senior reviewers may become a bottleneck. If they selectively take easier cases, selection bias may worsen. It does not fix underlying process or automation issues.
- Monitor: WIP/queue; error by activity and resource; senior workload; complex case wait time; rework; queue length; cost per case; arrival/finish balance.

**8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates**

- Expected benefit: limited. Accountability may increase attention, but raw late-shift error rates are heavily confounded by WIP, case mix, automation, and delayed QC.
- Risk: high. Punishment based on raw late error is likely unfair and can encourage error hiding, unsafe shortcuts, attrition, and reduced reporting. It may punish workers for management-created queue pressure or bot quality issues.
- Monitor: do not monitor for punishment based on raw late error. If any performance review is used, monitor system-adjusted error rates, root cause, WIP exposure, complexity mix, automation corrections, worker appeals, and rework attribution. A better approach is coaching and system fixes, not individual penalties.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair or misleading because the event log contains strong selection and system effects.

- Selection bias: seniors may take complex cases early, juniors may receive later or more routine cases, and Bot_Risk may handle specific risk classifications. Raw resource comparisons can therefore measure case assignment, not worker quality.
- Case-mix confounding: early cases are more complex and higher priority. Late cases are less complex but may arrive under higher queue pressure. Without complexity and priority adjustment, apparent learning or fatigue may be case mix.
- Queue pressure created by management: WIP is not only a worker trait. It is shaped by staffing, arrival rates, shift handovers, demand peaks, and process design. Workers may be pressured to speed up when WIP is high.
- Measurement error: `error_flag` may reflect detected error, not true error. Rework may be delayed. Complexity score may be coarse. `wip_at_start` may not equal current WIP. Event duration may exclude waiting time. Timestamps may be imprecise.
- Delayed quality checks: if Quality Check is concentrated at shift end, late error rates may reflect detection lag rather than late performance. This can unfairly penalize workers whose earlier work is corrected later.
- Automation quality: Bot_Risk may be fast but imperfect. Human correction rates under high WIP may reflect bot error, rubber-stamping, or pressure to accept bot output. Error attribution must distinguish bot error from human error.
- Punitive use of error rates: using raw late-shift error rates for punishment can create fear, underreporting, gaming, and unsafe shortcuts. Performance metrics should be used for system improvement, training, staffing, and process redesign, not simple blame.
- Fairness: workload should be assessed using complexity-weighted load, WIP exposure, shift type, case type, and support available. Individual metrics should be transparent, auditable, and subject to appeal.
