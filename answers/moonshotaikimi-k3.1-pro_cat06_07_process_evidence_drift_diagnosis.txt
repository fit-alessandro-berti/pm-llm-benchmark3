## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

- **January:** A stable reference month: fully mature labels, AUROC 0.81, Brier 0.132, ECE 0.025, low data-quality errors, and variant rates consistent with V3 being intrinsically higher risk.
- **February:** Essentially unchanged predictive performance and outcomes. The slight increase in part-wait time has no visible outcome consequence.
- **March:** Still a suitable baseline. Part-wait time reaches 35 h, but mature V3 breaches remain around their historical level and predictive metrics are stable.
- **April:** Primarily a **schema/pipeline incident**, not a sudden business deterioration. Label maturity is 100%, the part edge is stable at 34 h, and mature variant rates move only modestly. In contrast, activity recognition, prefix construction, missingness, duplicates, and variant mapping all deteriorate sharply. The unmapped share approximately accounts for the missing variant percentages. April therefore should not be interpreted as a genuine 18% process-variant shift or used to calibrate the model.
- **May:** A **partial recovery month**. Data quality and predictive metrics improve after the patch, while mature outcomes and process evidence remain close to baseline. The incomplete metric recovery is consistent with residual migration/cache problems, but could also include ordinary variation; replay is needed before assigning a drift label.
- **June:** A genuine **routing/process-mix intervention**, with 100% mature labels and much better data quality. The increase in low-breach V2 cases and reduction in V1/V3 plausibly explains most of the aggregate breach-rate decline. The stable V1/V2 rates and roughly matching mix-weighted benchmark argue against treating June as strong concept-drift evidence. The higher mean predicted risk and ECE instead justify within-variant calibration checks and a review of whether deployment thresholds/resource planning reflect the new mix. Part waits and V3 breaches show only an early warning.
- **July:** The bridge to a different failure mode. Supplier problems begin, the part edge reaches 65 h, and mature V3 breaches rise to 0.46 while V1/V2 remain stable. Aggregate observed versus predicted risk still looks reassuring, but this can conceal offsetting errors across variants. Near-complete maturity makes censoring a secondary, rather than primary, explanation.
- **August:** Strong **localized process deterioration plus V3 mix change**, with probable model-regime/concept drift. V3 reaches 35% of cases, its matured breach rate reaches 0.64, and mean predicted risk remains 0.19. A rough variant-weighted benchmark is \(0.25(0.18)+0.40(0.11)+0.35(0.64)=0.313\), above the currently observed 0.28; the 93% maturity may affect that comparison but cannot erase the process and matured-V3 evidence. Partner Z cannot explain August because it was not yet present.
- **September:** August’s supplier/process problem persists, but **population shift and greater censoring are overlaid**. Partner Z explains much of the additional V3 concentration, while V3’s matured breach rate remains very high at 0.61. The rough variant-weighted benchmark is about 0.341, versus the provisional observed 0.31. Neither “Partner Z explains everything” nor “the 82% maturity makes the deterioration unreal” is supported.
- **October:** Predominantly an **outcome-maturity and selection problem for predictive evaluation**, not evidence of recovery. Process evidence remains adverse, but only 55% of labels are mature and long paths are disproportionately delayed. AUROC, Brier, ECE, and the 0.16 observed breach rate are not comparable with mature historical months. October is also not another April: event-data quality remains comparatively good.

Thus April, June, August–September, and October have different primary mechanisms and require different responses: replay, mix-standardization, localized process/model investigation, and label maturation respectively.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

1. **Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting**

   - Re-evaluate identical stored predictions at a common, effectively complete vintage. Report both complete-cohort results and, as sensitivity analyses only, censoring-weighted estimates. Do not simply train on the cases whose labels happen to be available.
   - **Under label delay/censoring:** September–October breach rates and calibration/discrimination estimates move materially as labels arrive; October’s apparent improvement weakens or disappears.
   - **Under genuine August–September deterioration:** the high V3 breach rates and underprediction persist at fixed vintage.
   - **Under a real October improvement:** its lower breach rate remains after complete maturity and comparable case-mix adjustment, falsifying the suspected censoring explanation for that improvement.

2. **Deterministic replay after activity normalization and duplicate cleaning**

   - **Under a real process/concept explanation:** cleaned replay leaves a substantial within-variant performance gap. In particular, cleaning April should not make August’s supplier delays or mature V3 outcomes disappear.

3. **Calibration and discrimination stratified by month, variant, and source**

   - **Under supplier-related concept/regime drift:** V3 underprediction persists within source and comparable risk bands after July; V1/V2 may remain stable.
   - **Under Partner Z transport failure:** materially worse residuals are concentrated in Z after maturity adjustment, rather than appearing equally in Z and non-Z V3 cases.
   - **Under label censoring:** stratum estimates remain unstable and converge as the label vintage changes.

4. **Variant, DFG, and performance-DFG comparisons**
   - Compare cleaned variant frequencies and directly-follows relations by both event-calendar month and case-start cohort. Examine median, p90, and unfinished-order duration for `Order Part -> Part Received`, broken down by supplier and source; compare them with the diagnosis-to-`Remote Fix` edge.
   - **Under pipeline failure:** April’s artificial variants, `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` edges, and duplicate transitions shrink after cleaning.
   - **Under process drift:** June’s V2 routing change and July–October’s part-wait deterioration remain after cleaning, while remote execution stays stable.
   - **Under pure case-mix change:** edge times within variant/supplier remain stable and aggregate outcomes move mainly with variant shares.
   - If cleaned process timings remain normal while comparable mature outcomes change, the supplier/process-performance explanation is weakened and other concept or outcome-definition causes should be investigated.

5. **Test the outcome relationship conditional on the same path**
   - Within the same diagnostic variant or activity sequence, compare mature breach rates across months within prediction-risk bands and comparable pre-diagnosis severity, promised-date, queue, and case-mix strata. Use matching, standardization, or a model with month/supplier-period interactions.
   - Full eventual path is suitable for diagnosis, but must not become a prediction feature unless it was known at prediction time. Also verify that the outcome definition and promised-date logic did not change.
   - **Under concept drift with respect to the deployed features:** comparable V3 cases at similar old-model risk have higher breach probability after July, and the interaction persists after population reweighting.
   - **Under covariate/population shift:** these conditional curves largely realign after case-mix standardization.
   - **Under omitted supplier-lead-time effects:** adding a prediction-time supplier-state feature explains the interaction; the old model then suffered a process-regime/omitted-variable failure rather than an unexplained universal change.
   - **Under pipeline or censoring artifacts:** the interaction disappears after feature replay or fixed-vintage labeling respectively.

6. **Partner Z reweighting and transport checks**
   - First standardize on prediction-time covariates; separately perform a diagnostic standardization by variant. Transport non-Z or historical reference results to Z’s rural-distance, device-age, and routing mix using inverse-odds or similar weights. Inspect overlap and do not extrapolate silently where Z has no historical support.
   - **Under composition-only effects:** reweighting explains most aggregate differences and within-stratum model residuals become similar.
   - **Under genuine transport failure:** residual calibration/discrimination gaps remain in regions of common support, or lack of overlap shows that the reference population cannot answer the question without new data/features.
   - **Under a common supplier mechanism:** elevated mature V3 breaches and long part waits occur in both Z and non-Z cases.
   - **Under differential label delay:** apparent source differences change systematically as Z’s maturity catches up.

7. **Feature missingness and lineage audit**
   - Join every score to its raw-event version, activity-mapping version, prefix identifier, feature-cache version, queue-data timestamp, deduplication version, and model version. Audit missingness and feature distributions only after excluding structurally invalid prefixes.
   - **Under pipeline failure:** anomalies cluster by migration source, deployment window, or cache version, and repaired lineage explains the score changes.
   - **Under genuine covariate/population drift:** valid, freshly computed features change in June or September despite healthy lineage.
   - **Under concept drift:** cleaned input distributions remain comparatively stable while mature conditional outcomes change.
   - **Under outcome-pipeline delay:** feature lineage is healthy but outcome-arrival lineage shows the October backlog.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment**

- Freeze automatic retraining and model promotion based on September–October outcomes. Do not calibrate downward toward October’s observed 0.16 breach rate.
- Keep a frozen champion model for comparison, but flag recent evaluations as provisional. For cases known at decision time to require parts or use the affected supplier, use a conservative, human-reviewed operational overlay based on current supplier/queue information. This should prioritize review and mitigation, not silently become an unvalidated adverse-decision rule.
- Escalate supplier performance and open-part queues now; model work does not remediate the underlying process problem.
- Repair the outcome API backlog while preserving original outcome timestamps so that maturity can be reconstructed.

**Feature repair before any recalibration**

- Replay April–May from raw events, normalize labels, deduplicate conservatively, rebuild every derived feature, and validate serving-versus-replayed scores.
- Quarantine original April scores and any unrepaired cached features. Re-admit those records only after record-level lineage and prefix/feature checks pass.
- Keep the event-feature repair and the October outcome-backlog repair as separate workstreams.

**Short-term recalibration**

Recalibration is appropriate only on repaired features and sufficiently mature, representative labels:

- If June-style aggregate miscalibration disappears within variant/risk strata after mix standardization, do **not** globally recalibrate. Update operating thresholds, staffing, and expected case mix instead.
- If V3 shows a stable ranking relationship but a persistent intercept/slope shift, a simple recent-window calibration—potentially with a V3-specific intercept—is a reasonable, reversible shadow deployment. Validate it forward and by Partner Z status.
- Do not use recalibration to conceal invalid prefixes, add information the model never had, or correct materially degraded within-stratum discrimination.
- If supplier performance subsequently returns to normal, reassess and roll back any temporary calibration rather than assuming the severe regime is permanent.

**Full retraining**

Retrain when fixed-vintage, cleaned evaluation shows persistent within-stratum discrimination loss or a conditional outcome change that calibration cannot address, or when new prediction-time information is clearly needed. Candidate features include current supplier lead-time performance, expected parts availability, routing/policy information known at diagnosis, queue state, rural travel distance, and device age. Actual future part-wait duration or eventual variant must not be leaked into the predictor.

Use time-based forward validation and include both normal and disrupted regimes so the model does not merely memorize a temporary supplier outage.

**Training-month eligibility**

| Months | Current training status |
|---|---|
| January–March | Technically safe as clean, mature historical baseline data, but insufficient alone to represent later routing and supplier regimes. |
| April–May | Unsafe until replay, cache reconstruction, deduplication, and lineage validation are complete. Labels being mature does not make the features safe. |
| June | Labels and pipeline are broadly usable; valuable for the new routing mix, but not by itself evidence that retraining is required. |
| July | Near-eligible once remaining labels and the fixed-vintage extract are reconciled; important as supplier-onset evidence. |
| August–September | Unsafe for final supervised training on the currently available-label subset. After maturity and cleaning, both are essential for representing the supplier regime and, for September, Partner Z. Do not discard them as mere noise. |
| October | Unsafe now. Wait for outcome-backlog repair and mature, representative labels. |

A **V3-specific model or a global model with V3/supplier interactions** is justified only if parts routing is known at prediction time, the residual difference persists after repair and standardization, enough mature data exist, and forward validation shows a material benefit. A global interaction model is the lower-complexity first comparison. A Partner-Z-specific model is **not currently justified**: there are only two recent months, immature labels, and possible support problems. First add legitimate underlying features and assess transport; avoid turning source identity into an unnecessary segmentation or fairness proxy.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

| Monitor layer | Required measurements and use |
|---|---|
| **Predictive** | Continuously monitor score mean/quantiles and score-distribution shifts by source and known routing. Compute AUROC, Brier, and observed/expected risk only on eligible fixed-vintage cohorts, with sample sizes and uncertainty. |
| **Calibration** | Reliability curves, calibration intercept/slope, observed/expected ratios, and ECE by month, variant, source, and risk band. Aggregate ECE must not be the sole calibration alert. |
| **Label maturity** | Maturity curves, median/p90 label delay, backlog age/count, and maturity by variant, source, and path length. Publish data vintage alongside every metric. |
| **Data quality and lineage** | Schema-contract failures, unrecognized activities, duplicate rate, valid-diagnosis-prefix coverage, queue-feature missingness/freshness, cache lineage, training/serving skew, and raw-to-serving case reconciliation. |
| **Process variants** | Cleaned DFG/variant frequencies, new or unmapped paths, V1/V2/V3 shares, and routing-policy conformance. Distinguish event-calendar operational views from case-start cohorts. |
| **Performance DFG** | Median/p90 edge times and open-item aging, especially part waits by supplier and source, alongside remote-fix and other control edges. Include unfinished work so completed-edge medians do not hide long unresolved waits. |
| **Population** | Source shares, case volumes, rural-distance/device-age distributions, missing-feature patterns, V3 share by source, covariate overlap, and reweighted performance. |
| **Intervention/deployment** | Versioned annotations for the migration, 6 May patch, 1 June routing policy, 10 July supplier onset, 1 September Z onboarding, October backlog, model/calibration deployments, manual overlays, and supplier remediation. Monitor shadow/champion agreement and post-intervention performance. |

**Alert dependencies**

1. A critical schema, prefix, or feature-lineage alarm invalidates affected predictions and outcome metrics. It triggers quarantine/replay and suppresses concept-drift, recalibration, and retraining recommendations.
2. A label-delay alarm makes outcome metrics provisional and suppresses performance-triggered training. It does **not** suppress score-distribution, data-quality, or process-performance monitoring.
3. Only when data are valid and labels meet cohort- and stratum-specific maturity requirements should predictive degradation initiate a model-review workflow.
4. A mix alarm with stable within-stratum outcomes leads to threshold/resource review, not automatic retraining.
5. Stable discrimination plus persistent within-stratum calibration shift leads to recalibration review; persistent conditional/discrimination change leads to retraining review.
6. A supplier performance-DFG alarm can trigger immediate operational escalation without any mature labels, but it does not by itself prove concept drift.

The following sketch produces process evidence only; it does not fit a model or assume a PM4Py drift detector.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

def monthly_process_audit(path="field_service_events.csv"):
    raw = pd.read_csv(path)
    raw["activity"] = raw["activity"].replace({
        "Initial Diagnosis Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed",
        "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed",
    })
    raw["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(raw["timestamp"], utc=True)

    # Conservative strategy: exact duplicate rows only. In production, prefer a
    # verified immutable source-event ID + payload hash and quarantine conflicts;
    # never deduplicate merely on case/activity, since activities can legitimately repeat.
    duplicate = raw.duplicated(keep="first")
    clean = raw.loc[~duplicate].copy()

    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        clean, case_id="case_id", activity_key="activity",
        timestamp_key="timestamp"
    )
    C, A, T = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"
    df = df.sort_values([C, T])
    case_start = df.groupby(C)[T].min()  # Prefer actual Case Created time if available.
    rows = []

    def summarize(month, cohort_type, frame):
        ans = {"month": month, "cohort_type": cohort_type,
               "cases": 0, "events": 0}
        if frame.empty:
            return ans

        log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(frame)
        dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
        perf, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)

        # Extraction occurs only after label normalization and duplicate handling.
        # It is audited here, not used to train a model.
        temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)

        part = perf.get(("Order Part", "Part Received"))
        median_h = None
        if isinstance(part, dict) and part.get("median") is not None:
            median_h = part["median"] / 3600.0  # PM4Py durations are seconds.

        variant_counts = {}
        for variant, members in variants.items():
            name = " > ".join(variant) if isinstance(variant, tuple) else str(variant)
            variant_counts[name] = members if isinstance(members, int) else len(members)

        ans.update({
            "cases": frame[C].nunique(),
            "events": len(frame),
            "unknown_activity_share": float(frame[A].eq("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY").mean()),
            "dfg_edge_count": len(dfg),
            "part_wait_median_hours": median_h,
            "part_edge_performance_raw": part,  # Preserve version-specific statistics.
            "top_variants": dict(sorted(
                variant_counts.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True
            )[:10]),
            "temporal_feature_rows": len(temporal),
        })
        return ans

    for month in sorted(df[T].dt.strftime("%Y-%m").unique()):
        lo = pd.Timestamp(month + "-01", tz="UTC")
        hi = lo + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)

        # Events executed in this calendar window; traces can be truncated at boundaries.
        calendar = pm4py.filter_time_range(
            df, lo.isoformat(), hi.isoformat(), mode="events"
        )
        rows.append(summarize(month, "event_calendar", calendar))

        # All currently available events for cases started in the month, including
        # events after month-end. Recent cohorts can therefore be right-truncated.
        ids = case_start.index[(case_start >= lo) & (case_start < hi)]
        started = df[df[C].isin(ids)]
        rows.append(summarize(month, "case_start_full_trace_to_cutoff", started))

    out = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    out.attrs["raw_rows"] = len(raw)
    out.attrs["exact_duplicate_rows_removed"] = int(duplicate.sum())
    out.attrs["activity_mapping"] = "both diagnosis labels -> Initial Diagnosis Completed"
    return out
```

`event_calendar` describes work executed during a month and is useful for current operational signals. `case_start_full_trace_to_cutoff` describes the eventual paths of cases starting in that month, but recent cohorts require explicit truncation/maturity interpretation. Neither view is, by itself, a mature predictive-performance evaluation.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

| Failure mode | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Retraining on immature September–October labels** | The model could learn that long V3 and Partner Z cases are unusually safe because their adverse outcomes are still missing. October’s 0.16 could be mistaken for recovery, worsening underprediction exactly where delays are greatest. | Gate training on fixed-vintage, stratum-specific maturity; never train on the convenient available-label subset. Preserve and repair outcome timestamps, and use censoring methods only as validated sensitivity analyses. |
| **Calibrating around April’s pipeline bug** | A calibration layer could learn to correct invalid April scores, then become harmful once prefixes and features are repaired. It would also conceal recurring schema failures. | Quarantine and replay first. Require data-quality and lineage gates before calibration, compare old/repaired scores, and retain rollback. |
| **Treating June’s routing mix shift as concept drift** | Unnecessary global retraining or recalibration could overfit a remote-heavy month and degrade V1/V3 performance without addressing any real conditional change. | Standardize by variant, inspect within-stratum calibration, and adjust operational thresholds or resource planning when within-stratum relationships remain stable. |
| **Dismissing August–September as label delay or Partner Z composition** | This would ignore the matured V3 deterioration and the 130–148 h part waits. The model would continue underpredicting supplier-exposed cases, while the operational root cause remained unresolved. | Combine performance-DFG monitoring with mature V3 outcomes. Escalate supplier remediation immediately and test localized recalibration/retraining after label and feature validation. |
| **Attributing Partner Z’s higher observed breach rate to unfair model behavior without stratified evidence—or dismissing a real Z transport problem as “just mix”** | The first error confuses base rates and process exposure with model error; the second can leave rural customers systematically underserved. Source-specific thresholds could also introduce new fairness problems. | Compare mature calibration, false-negative/false-positive rates, and intervention outcomes within source-by-variant strata. Reweight on rural distance, device age, and routing; inspect overlap and access to mitigation before making fairness or segmentation decisions. |
| **Permanently retraining on a temporary supplier outage, or using future process information** | The model may become miscalibrated after supplier recovery, while features such as actual part-wait duration or eventual variant would produce leakage and misleading backtests. | Include normal and disrupted regimes, use only prediction-time features, validate forward in time, deploy temporarily in shadow mode, and reassess after supplier/process interventions. |
