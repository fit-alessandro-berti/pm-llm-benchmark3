## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation

The raw-vs-mapped fitness gap (0.55 vs 0.90 in March) is essentially a **semantic** recovery: when `Eligibility Screen` is aliased to `Validate Eligibility` and duplicate `Open Case` events are removed, conformance with the old normative model rises almost back to baseline. This proves the event log is the proximate source of the March drop, not a true control-flow redesign. However, a label mapping can only demonstrate that the *recorded sequence of activities* still matches the reference model on the edges it knows about; it cannot prove the *business process* is unchanged. Conformance checkers score observed edges against the model — they do not evaluate whether `Request Info` is being invoked for substantively different reasons, whether the rework loop is genuinely new, or whether queue dynamics shifted.

Duplicate `Open Case` events and missing resource identifiers distort different artifacts in different ways. Duplicated technical events inflate activity counts and case duration, biasing cycle-time statistics upward; they also produce spurious variants and inflate the directly-follows graph (DFG) edge count. Missing resource identifiers do not affect DFG structure or conformance much, but they cripple resource-based performance analysis (queue waiting, social network, handover-of-work) and can make handover patterns look anomalous.

The post-May patterns cannot be dismissed as logging drift. The mapped fitness keeps declining (0.91  0.87  0.86) at the same time that `Assess  Request Info` rises from 12/100 to 29/100 cases and rework-variant share doubles. Median cycle time grows by ~13 hours while the recorded `Assess` service time changes only ~0.6 hours. Those are behavioral and performance signals that the label mapping alone does not explain.

## 9. Provisional attribution

**Strongest conclusions (high confidence).**
- **The March raw-fitness collapse is almost entirely a logging/semantic artifact.** A label rename (`Validate Eligibility`  `Eligibility Screen`) and duplicated `Open Case` events together account for the entire 0.92  0.55 fitness drop; once they are mapped or removed, fitness returns to  0.90. The distinct-label count rising from 9 to 11 and the simultaneous 4% duplicate share make this attribution close to certain.
- **The May 2026 rework surge is supplier-driven.** The Supplier Z share doubles (18%  46%) at almost exactly the supplier disruption date (May 5), and the rework variant share rises in lock-step from 13% to 24–27%. The partial recovery by September (Supplier Z share 24%, rework 20%) coincides with Supplier Z's reported operational recovery on September 10.
- **The cycle-time deterioration from May onward is dominated by queueing, not by service-time growth.** Per-activity `Assess` service moved only from ~4.8h to ~5.4h, while median and p90 cycle grew by 13h and 35h respectively. That signature is consistent with capacity loss (the four-assessor departure on June 15), not with per-task complexity.

**Plausible but not strongly attributable.**
- **Priority rule (July 1) redistributed rather than eliminated waiting.** The aggregate monthly statistics do not let us observe this directly, but the prediction is testable: standard-class waiting should rise, high-complexity waiting should stay flat or fall, after July 1. The aggregate hides the split.
- **Renewal season (October 1) drove both volume and complexity share upward.** High-complexity rose from 26% to 45% and the senior-assessment rate jumped from 7/100 to 22/100 cases; the calendar timing aligns. Without a prior-year October baseline, however, this could also reflect a permanent policy shift in complexity classification.
- **The staffing cut (June 15) compounded the supplier-driven rework.** June and July show the steepest cycle-time acceleration, even before renewal season. Staffing and supplier rework are plausible joint drivers of this acceleration, but they cannot be cleanly separated with monthly aggregates.

**Where interaction prevents clean attribution.** Supplier Z (May–June), staffing cut (June 15), and the priority rule (July 1) all act within a six-week window, and the renewal season arrives three months later. Any one of them could be the dominant driver of cycle-time growth in mid-year; the December improvement cannot be cleanly attributed between overtime/campaign interventions and natural seasonal decay either.

**What would revise the account.** A year-over-year October/November baseline would let us separate seasonal population drift from permanent behavioral change. Event-level data stratified by complexity class and supplier would let us decompose the priority-rule effect. Resource-complete cases would let us redo the team-level queue analysis without missingness bias. If the December improvements persist into January of the next year at the same workload, the intervention hypothesis becomes stronger than the seasonality hypothesis; if they vanish with the season, seasonality is doing most of the work.

## 10. Monitoring decision rules

1. **IF** raw_model_fitness drops by > 0.30 in a month **AND** mapped_model_fitness stays within 0.02 of its prior value, **THEN** trigger a label/schema audit (check for activity renames, new technical events, value-frequency drift), **BECAUSE** this is the signature of a logging/semantic migration, not a behavioral change.

2. **IF** `Assess  Request Info` rate per 100 cases rises by > 5 pp absolute month-over-month **AND** supplier_z_pct rose by > 5 pp in the same window, **THEN** prioritize supplier-bundle completeness review over internal rework remediation, **BECAUSE** Supplier Z has historically been the dominant driver of rework loops and supplier-side fixes are cheaper than absorber-side ones.

3. **IF** median cycle time rises by > 20% relative **AND** per-activity service time rises by < 10% relative in the same window, **THEN** diagnose as queue build-up (arrivals exceed capacity) rather than task-complexity growth, **BECAUSE** this is the canonical signature of capacity loss such as the June 15 staffing cut.

4. **IF** `Assess  Senior Assessment` rate per 100 cases more than doubles vs the same calendar month in the prior year, **THEN** treat as candidate seasonal population drift (renewal) rather than a permanent process change, **BECAUSE** the renewal season has historically driven both complexity share and senior-assessment demand upward.

5. **IF** rework variant share is rising **AND** high_complexity_pct is also rising month-over-month, **THEN** decompose each component separately (supplier vs complexity vs queue) before assigning remediation, **BECAUSE** the three mechanisms share observable signatures and conflating them leads to ineffective interventions.

6. **IF** missing_resource_pct exceeds 3% in any rolling 30-day window **AND** is concentrated in specific teams, **THEN** trigger a source-system audit on those teams rather than imputing resources globally, **BECAUSE** missing resource identifiers bias queue, handover, and workload analyses non-randomly.

7. **IF** any single monitor fires in a month that is also a known seasonality boundary (October 1, January 1) **AND** no off-season comparator is available, **THEN** require a calendar-matched YoY baseline before escalation, **BECAUSE** seasonal effects can mimic or mask structural drift, and false positives on renewal months erode trust in the monitoring system.
