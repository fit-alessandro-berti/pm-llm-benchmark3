## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this case, **resilience** means preserving the wholesaler’s critical service function while maintaining a defensible minimum control set. It does not mean reproducing the baseline DFG during a cyber outage. For example, `Emergency triage`, `Hospital-critical fast lane`, `Manual authorization check`, `Temporary release control`, `Defer noncritical order`, `Backlog prioritization`, and `Reconcile manual reservation` can all be resilient when they are explicit, risk-based, documented, time-bounded, and followed by reconciliation.

**Concealed noncompliance** occurs when the process appears operationally successful because orders move, but required control evidence is missing, delayed, or reconstructed too late to prove that the shipment was authorized, traceable, secure, temperature-compliant, or properly released. In this scenario, `Skip system authorization check`, `Dispatch without scan`, weak `Phone proof of delivery`, missing quality/release checks, missing temperature-control records, and delayed `Invoice close -> Audit remediation` are the main warning signs. The most dangerous pattern is not a visible delay; it is a shipment that looks complete while its authorization, lot traceability, proof of delivery, or temperature/security evidence is incomplete.

The strongest examples of **true resilience** are:

* **B. Manual authorization check**, because it substitutes human compliance review for unavailable system validation.
* **A. Hospital-critical fast lane**, but only where minimum authorization and product-risk controls are preserved.
* **F. Temporary release control**, but only if high-risk product classes receive full checks rather than sampling.
* **I/J. Defer noncritical order and backlog prioritization**, when governed by explicit rules rather than informal customer pressure.
* **K. Reconcile manual reservation**, because it directly addresses inventory and record mismatch created by manual operations.

The clearest **risky shortcuts** are:

* **C. Skip system authorization check**, especially outside prequalified low-risk ambient orders.
* **G. Dispatch without scan**, because it creates the largest traceability gap unless replaced by a robust manual chain-of-custody record.
* **H. Phone proof of delivery**, if used as primary proof for controlled, cold-chain, hospital-critical, or disputed deliveries.
* **L. Audit remediation**, if management treats it as proof that compliance was maintained in real time.

Several adaptations are **ambiguous depending on risk appetite and execution quality**. Manual inventory snapshots and manual pick lists are reasonable early in T2, but become risky as inventory data ages. Deferring wholesale-bulk orders is defensible to protect hospitals, but deferring pharmacy-standard orders to the point of a **61%** fill rate creates fairness, continuity-of-care, and customer-retention concerns. Temporary controls are resilient only when they are designed controls, not improvised justifications for missing evidence.

## 4. Recovery process model

A redesigned disruption process should make the emergency variant explicit rather than allowing teams to invent inconsistent workarounds.

**Proposed recovery model: controlled resilience mode**

1. **Trigger conditions**

   Activate controlled resilience mode when any of the following occurs:

   * WMS unavailable or unreliable.
   * Inventory synchronization unavailable or materially delayed.
   * Scanning unavailable or unreliable.
   * Digital proof of delivery unavailable.
   * Cyber incident creates uncertainty about data integrity.
   * Traceability, temperature, authorization, or dual-control evidence cannot be captured through the normal workflow.

   Activation should create an incident ID, a start time, named incident owner, named QA/compliance owner, and a declared scope: sites, systems, product classes, customer groups, and permitted manual procedures.

2. **Order intake and risk classification**

   Every order receives a manual or system-generated recovery ID and is classified on two axes:

   * **Customer criticality:** Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, Wholesale-bulk.
   * **Product risk:** Controlled-substance, Cold-chain, Recall-sensitive, Ambient.

   The product risk classification should override pure customer priority. A wholesale-bulk controlled-substance order cannot be treated as low risk simply because the customer is non-hospital.

3. **Triage rules**

   Use a documented priority sequence:

   1. Hospital-critical orders required for continuity of care, subject to minimum product controls.
   2. Cold-chain orders with temperature stability constraints, because delay can destroy product usability.
   3. Controlled-substance orders with mandatory security and dual-control checks.
   4. Recall-sensitive products requiring lot/expiry certainty.
   5. Pharmacy-standard urgent replenishment.
   6. Pharmacy-standard routine replenishment.
   7. Wholesale-bulk orders.
   8. Non-urgent orders eligible for deferral or partial fulfillment.

   Overrides are allowed only with reason code, approver, timestamp, and affected customer/product risk.

4. **Minimum controls by product/customer risk**

   **All orders, including low-risk ambient orders:**

   * Customer ID and order ID.
   * Product, quantity, lot if applicable, expiry if applicable.
   * Manual recovery ID.
   * Picker, packer, release approver where applicable.
   * Timestamped authorization basis.
   * Dispatch evidence.
   * Proof-of-delivery evidence or documented exception.
   * Reconciliation status.

   **Ambient, known customer, low-risk orders:**

   * Offline authorization list or standing authorization fallback.
   * Manual inventory check with snapshot timestamp.
   * Manual dispatch ledger if scanning fails.
   * Post-event reconciliation within defined SLA.

   **Recall-sensitive orders:**

   * Mandatory lot and expiry capture before dispatch.
   * Recall block check using latest available offline recall list.
   * No dispatch if lot identity is uncertain.
   * Reconciliation before invoice close.

   **Cold-chain orders:**

   * Temperature pack-out record.
   * Pack validation record.
   * Logger or temperature-control identifier where available.
   * Courier handoff time and condition.
   * Temperature exception process before release or customer use.
   * Phone POD alone is insufficient.

   **Controlled-substance orders:**

   * Customer/product authorization must be confirmed before dispatch.
   * Dual-control check before release.
   * Secure storage and handoff record.
   * Recipient identity and delivery proof stronger than phone confirmation.
   * No “skip authorization” path.

   **Hospital-critical orders:**

   * Fast lane allowed.
   * Minimum controls cannot be skipped; they must be compressed, parallelized, or manually substituted.
   * If full evidence cannot be captured immediately, the order must carry a named compliance exception with same-day review.

5. **Manual evidence capture**

   Replace informal workarounds with a manual evidence package:

   * Pre-numbered pick list.
   * Manual authorization form.
   * Manual inventory reservation ledger.
   * Lot/expiry/quantity confirmation.
   * Manual release checklist.
   * Cold-chain temperature form.
   * Controlled-substance dual-control form.
   * Courier manifest replacing scan when scanning is down.
   * POD form with recipient, timestamp, order/package ID, and signature or equivalent auditable evidence.
   * Exception form for any missing evidence.

   The key design principle is: **manual evidence may replace electronic evidence, but missing evidence should not be normalized.**

6. **Backlog prioritization**

   Backlog should be scored using:

   * Customer criticality.
   * Product risk.
   * Clinical or service urgency.
   * Order age.
   * Temperature stability or expiry exposure.
   * Previous deferral count.
   * Fairness across customer groups.
   * Cancellation risk.
   * Availability of compliance capacity.

   The backlog queue should have maximum age thresholds. If pharmacy-standard fill rate drops sharply while hospital-critical supply is preserved, management should see that trade-off explicitly rather than discovering it later through cancellations and complaints.

7. **Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow**

   Reconciliation should begin during the incident, not only after systems return.

   Suggested flow:

   ```text
   Manual shipment completed
   -> Evidence package assembled
   -> Risk-based reconciliation
      -> If complete: regulatory/compliance close
      -> If incomplete but low-risk: remediation queue with SLA
      -> If incomplete and high-risk: compliance hold / escalation / customer notification if needed
   -> Inventory correction
   -> Authorization confirmation
   -> POD confirmation
   -> Temperature/security confirmation where applicable
   -> Invoice close only after required evidence threshold
   -> Audit remediation only for residual exceptions
   -> CAPA / lessons learned
   ```

   Operational close and compliance close should be separate states. A shipped order may be operationally complete but not compliance-complete. This would prevent the T4 problem where cycle time looks mostly recovered while `Invoice close -> Audit remediation` continues as a long hidden tail.

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept **controlled, documented, time-limited deviations** from the baseline process when they protect hospital-critical supply and preserve minimum product/customer controls. It should not accept shipment of controlled-substance, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, or authorization-restricted products without real-time substitute evidence for authorization, release, traceability, temperature/security, dispatch, and delivery. Throughput preservation is valuable only when it does not convert mandatory controls into after-the-fact guesses. The recommendation would become less restrictive only if future drills or incidents show that manual authorization, manual dispatch manifests, temperature records, dual-control forms, and POD evidence can be captured with exception rates close to baseline. It should become more restrictive if audit remediation finds unprovable deliveries, authorization gaps, temperature uncertainty, controlled-substance chain-of-custody failures, recall-tracing failures, or persistent unfair harm to pharmacy-standard customers.
