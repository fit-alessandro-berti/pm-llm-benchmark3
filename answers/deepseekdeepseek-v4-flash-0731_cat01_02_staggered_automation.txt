## 1. What is observed

`automation=1` records system availability, not actual use. In adopting branches, `automated_fast_path_pct` rose gradually from roughly 4–6% before adoption to 25–50% later, so availability is a fuzzy treatment. Adoption was not randomized: Alpha and Beta were selected for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma received a pilot; Delta and Epsilon remained controls. During the study, a centralized fraud desk began serving all branches in March, a routing campaign sent more arrivals to Alpha and Beta from April, Alpha lost two FTEs in May, Gamma lost one in June, and a May product change increased recorded high-complexity shares. Outcomes are branch-month aggregates: median and p90 cycle times mix different process paths; rework and escalation are percentages; CSAT is a monthly survey mean with response rates between roughly 20% and 45% but without response counts. No case-level timestamps, queue-state data, fraud-desk waits, or risk-specific cycle times are available. These data describe associations and process changes; they do not by themselves identify a causal effect of automation.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: cohort-specific event-study / CITS with not-yet-treated controls | Separate relative-time indicators for early cohort (Alpha/Beta, t0=Apr) and late cohort (Gamma, t0=Jul); no single pooled TWFE coefficient | Delta/Epsilon as never/not-yet-treated controls at the same calendar months; within-branch pre-period | Log arrivals, FTE, `high_complexity_pct`, March fraud-desk indicator, pre-treatment outcome levels | E3/E4/E7/E8 cohort-specific ATT by relative month | No unobserved confounders after controls; no anticipation; no fraud-desk spillovers; no differential co-interventions | Pre-trend coefficients; placebo adoption months; compare Gamma to Delta/Epsilon in May–Jun before adoption | Co-interventions and only 3 treated clusters; controls may be contaminated by fraud-desk spillovers |
| Alternative 1: interrupted time series | Level and slope break at adoption month; use adoption-month outcome | Own pre-adoption trend plus Delta/Epsilon calendar-month changes | Time trend, FTE, arrivals, complexity | Immediate ATT around month 0/1 | No other shock exactly at adoption month; linear pre-trend | Check April non-adopter changes; placebo March break | Routing campaign starts in April; fraud desk already began in March; anticipation |
| Alternative 2: synthetic control / nearest-neighbor matching | Binary availability at adoption; donor outcomes pre-weighted | Delta/Epsilon donor pool, weighted control path | Pre-treatment median/p90/rework/escalation/CSAT levels, complexity, arrivals/FTE | Adopter-specific ATT | Donor combination is valid; no spillovers; no structural breaks | Pre-period RMSE; placebo donor weights; sensitivity to donor set | Only two donors and too few pre-periods to fit reliable synthetic weights |
| Alternative 3: Bayesian hierarchical model | Availability plus intensity with monthly effects | All branches, borrowing strength across units | Priors for complexity, FTE, routing, fraud-desk period effects | E1/E7 posterior effects | Priors correctly encode confounding; no unmeasured common shocks; model extrapolation | Posterior predictive checks; prior sensitivity | Subjective priors; small N can produce overconfident posteriors |

The primary strategy is preferable because it directly respects the staggered timing and the small number of clusters. Pooling Alpha, Beta, and Gamma into one TWFE coefficient can create negative weights when treatment effects are dynamic or heterogeneous. Estimating early and late cohorts separately, and using Delta/Epsilon as not-yet-treated controls, allows common shocks such as the fraud desk and product change to affect both treated and control months, at least in principle. Pre-treatment relative-time indicators provide a transparent placebo check. However, the primary strategy should not be oversold: with five branches, only three treated branches, and co-interventions, the resulting estimates are better viewed as descriptive causal-identification exercises than as policy-grade effects.

| Beta | May–Aug minus Jan–Mar | +2.0 | +11.6 | -0.1 | +2.6 | -0.02 |
| Gamma | Jul–Aug minus Jan–Mar | -0.3 | +7.5 | +0.3 | +2.8 | +0.03 |
| Gamma | Jul–Aug minus Apr–Jun | -6.5 | -9.3 | -1.2 | +1.2 | +0.14 |

The adoption-month contrast is less confounded by the May product change but still overlaps with the routing campaign and fraud desk. The later contrast is heavily confounded.

**Comparison against not-yet-treated or never-treated branches** from March to August:

| Branch | adoption |  median_cycle_h |  p90_cycle_h |  rework_pct |  escalation_pct |  csat_1to5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | Apr | -6.1 | +1.8 | +1.3 | +1.8 | +0.12 |
| Beta | Apr | -1.4 | +5.4 | -0.1 | +1.3 | +0.04 |
| Gamma | Jul | -3.6 | +1.4 | +0.3 | +1.5 | +0.08 |
| Delta | never by Aug | +2.8 | +13.2 | +2.2 | +0.5 | -0.04 |
| Epsilon | never by Aug | +0.6 | +9.0 | +2.7 | +0.8 | -0.01 |

Thus, adopters did not share a consistent p90 improvement; controls also worsened in p90 and rework. Escalation increased in all branches, with adopters showing larger increases.

**Treatment-intensity analysis:** The code in Section 9 estimates branch- and month-fixed-effects models using `automated_fast_path_pct` rather than the binary `automation` flag. Descriptively, higher automated-fast-path use is not consistently associated with lower median or p90 cycle times. For example, Alpha in August had 48% automated-fast-path use and a low median, but Alpha in June had 39% use and the worst median; controls with 4% use also varied widely. The intensity model is descriptive only because use share is endogenous.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean:** Alpha and Beta were selected for poor historical cycle times, so some later improvement could be regression to the mean. Delta and Epsilon, which were not treated, also worsened in p90 and rework over the same period, suggesting common shocks. Gamma’s May–June deterioration mirrors controls, and its July–August improvement follows a July–August recovery in Delta/Epsilon as well. With only three pre-period months for early adopters and five total clusters, no reliable pre-trend test can separate differential trends from anticipation or selection.

**Sensitivity / bounded conclusion:**

- `median_cycle_h`: If one assumes adopters would have followed the average Delta/Epsilon March-to-August change (+1.7 h), then the implied “effect” is roughly 7.8 h for Alpha, 3.1 h for Beta, and 5.3 h for Gamma. If one instead assumes adopters would have followed their own Jan–Mar flat trend, the implied effect is near 0 to +2 h. A defensible interval is roughly **8 h to +3 h**, centered near zero.
- `p90_cycle_h`: Absolute p90 rose in all adopters, but controls rose even more. Depending on which control trend is used, the implied effect ranges from **about 10 h to +15 h**. The data cannot support a claim that automation improved tail performance.
- `csat_1to5`: Observed changes are within about ±0.15. Given response rates of 20–45% and no response counts, a plausible nonresponse bias interval is **0.2 to +0.2**, so no satisfaction effect is identified.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta chosen for high volume and poor cycle; Gamma after pilot | Likely overstates improvement for early adopters via regression to the mean | Compare pre-period trends; placebo adoption months; match to controls | Apparent ITT effect is an artifact of selection |
| Nonparallel trends | No randomization; Delta/Epsilon differ in levels and trends | Unknown; can bias in either direction | Event-study pre-trends; synthetic control | DiD estimates are invalid |
| Time-varying confounding | Product change increased complexity; routing campaign changed arrivals | Likely worsens observed cycle/rework/csat; may mask automation benefit | Adjust for `high_complexity_pct`, arrivals, FTE; stratify if possible | Effects are misattributed to automation |
| Treatment intensity | `automation=1` does not mean full use; share ranged 25–50% | Attenuates availability effect toward null | Use `automated_fast_path_pct` models; track actual use | Availability effect is not a use effect |
| Spillovers through fraud desk | Automated triage sends more cases to shared fraud desk; waits unrecorded | Worsens p90/escalation in adopters and may contaminate controls | Collect fraud-desk queue length and waiting time by source branch | SUTVA violation; controls are not valid counterfactuals |
| Staffing changes | Alpha lost FTE, Gamma lost FTE, Beta gained FTE | Worsens p90/rework; confounds later months | FTE-adjusted models; per-FTE workload | Automation blamed for staffing harms |
| Case-mix shift | `high_complexity_pct` rose in May in several branches | Worsens median/p90/rework in treated and controls | Segment outcomes by complexity; compare complexity-adjusted paths | Late-post effects are not interpretable |
| Anticipation | Managers knew rollout dates; Gamma had readiness pilot | Can shift behavior before adoption, biasing pre-period | Examine Feb–Mar and Apr–Jun discontinuities | Time-zero is mis-specified |
| Survey nonresponse | CSAT response rates vary 20–45%; no counts | Unknown; likely masks dissatisfaction if unhappy customers do not respond | Collect response counts/denominators; compare administrative complaints | No valid CSAT conclusion |
| Measurement aggregation | Branch-month medians/p90s aggregate heterogeneous paths | Can hide compositional changes; Simpson’s paradox possible | Use event logs and path/risk-stratified summaries | Cycle-time estimates are not process-level |
| Small number of branches | Five branches, three treated; cluster inference is unreliable | Overconfident standard errors and intervals | Wild cluster bootstrap; permutation tests; Bayesian shrinkage | False certainty about effects |
| Dynamic/heterogeneous treatment effects | Effects may change with learning; early and late cohorts differ | Pooled TWFE estimates can have negative weights | Cohort-specific event-study estimates | A single “effect” is meaningless |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**Expand with an embedded evaluation.**

Do not expand immediately to both Delta and Epsilon. The most informative next step is a staggered phase-in with Delta first and Epsilon held as a control for at least three to six months after Delta goes live. Delta is more structurally similar to Gamma and has lower recorded complexity; Epsilon has high complexity and would be harder to evaluate first. If leadership can accept a pre-committed random start date, randomize the order of Delta and Epsilon. If randomization is operationally unacceptable, use a pre-registered readiness rule that is not based on current outcome trends.

Minimum design:

- **Pre-period:** at least 6 monthly observations before Delta adoption, with weekly process metrics when possible.
- **Post-period:** at least 6 monthly observations after Delta adoption before deciding whether to offer automation to Epsilon.
- **Treatment assignment:** Delta receives automation on a pre-announced date; Epsilon remains control during that window. Later Epsilon can receive automation if pre-registered harm thresholds are not breached.
- **Case-level data:** collect event timestamps for registration, automated/manual triage, specialist review, rework loops, resolve, and close, plus an indicator of whether the automated fast path was actually used for each case.
- **Queue-state data:** collect specialist-review queue lengths and waiting times, and especially central fraud-desk queue lengths and waiting times by source branch.
- **Spillover monitoring:** if Delta adoption increases fraud-desk waits for Epsilon, the control is contaminated. Track fraud-desk referrals and waits for all branches weekly.
- **Customer-harm guardrails:** stop or pause expansion if Delta’s p90 cycle time worsens more than 10% relative to Epsilon, if rework/escalation increases materially, or if CSAT response-rate-adjusted satisfaction falls by more than 0.2.
- **Decision rule for scale-up:** pre-register success criteria before Delta launches. A reasonable rule is: scale to Epsilon only if, after six post-period months, the upper confidence bound for p90 harm is below 10%, median cycle is no worse than a pre-defined margin, and CSAT is not lower. If the evidence is ambiguous, do not scale.

This design trades a small delay in expansion for much clearer causal learning and prevents a second uncontrolled rollout to Epsilon.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automated-fast-path share rose from roughly 4–6% to 25–50% in adopting branches while median cycle times were roughly flat, p90 times and rework/escalation rose in several windows, and never/not-yet-treated controls also showed p90 and rework deterioration.
- Suggestive: The absence of a large median-cycle increase in Alpha and Beta despite 10% extra arrivals, FTE losses, and higher case complexity is suggestive that automation may have absorbed some workload, but this is not a causal estimate and could reflect regression to the mean or other changes.
- Conditionally causal: Relative to a control trend taken from Delta/Epsilon from March to August, automation appears to reduce median cycle time by roughly 3–8 hours in adopting branches, but this conditional estimate is not credible as a policy effect because of endogenous rollout, fraud-desk spillovers, and only two control branches.
- Not supported: Any claim that automation reduces p90 cycle time, rework, escalation, or improves customer satisfaction, or that leadership can state “automation reduces cycle time by X%” for expansion to Delta and Epsilon.
