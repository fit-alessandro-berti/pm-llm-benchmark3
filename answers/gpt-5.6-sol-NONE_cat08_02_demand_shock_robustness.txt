# 1. Stress scenarios

### S1 — Winter mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases

Assume demand resembles or exceeds the observed peak: Urgent rises from 18% to about 29%, Complex-panel from 13% to 20%, while overall weekly volume also rises. This is not a uniform load increase:

- Urgent cases require rapid preparation, assay access, and medical validation.
- Complex panels use multiple assays, are 3.1 times more likely to require medical validation, and 2.4 times more likely to require specialist review.
- Urgent bypass of batch waiting falls from 74% to 58% during peaks, indicating that priority loses effectiveness once preparation and assay capacity saturate.
- Routine work accumulates behind priority traffic, creating a later recovery backlog.

Acceptance objective: protect the 18-hour Urgent contract without allowing Routine or Complex-panel queues to grow without bound.

### S2 — Assay degradation or outage

Assume M1 is unavailable for part of a shift or M2 develops a higher failure rate. The assay pool is already at 98% peak utilization with an 18-hour median queue. Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after night shifts and M2 usage and consume assay capacity twice.

A partial outage therefore combines:

- lower effective machine capacity,
- longer assay queues,
- more repeats,
- extra technical-validation work,
- delayed medical validation for Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

Acceptance objective: preserve critical testing while preventing a repeat-feedback loop from consuming the remaining capacity.

### S3 — Medical validator or specialist absence

Assume a medical validator is absent during a winter peak, or the known every-second-Friday specialist absence coincides with high Complex-panel demand.

Peak medical-validation utilization is 93% with a 22-hour median queue. Specialist review reaches 87% utilization and a 36-hour median queue. The performance path is particularly severe:

- Technical validation  Medical validation: 24 hours at peak.
- Hold for specialist review  Medical validation: 48 hours at peak.

Acceptance objective: maintain coverage for Urgent and time-sensitive Complex-panel cases while using controlled deferral or outsourcing for less time-sensitive specialist work.

### S4 — Courier compression or delay

Two variants should be tested:

1. Several routes arrive together around 10:00 or 17:00.
2. A weather delay produces a late, compressed wave of samples.

Courier intake rises to 88% utilization and a 7.5-hour median queue at peak. A compressed wave then reaches pre-analytical check, already at 96% utilization, followed by accession correction, preparation, and assay.

Acceptance objective: flatten release of work into the lab without compromising sample stability or delaying Urgent material inside an undifferentiated intake queue.

### S5 — Pre-analytical quality deterioration

Assume winter collection pressure produces more incomplete metadata, poor tubes, or unsuitable specimens. The peak DFG shows:

- 780 direct recollection requests versus 760 in a much larger eight-week normal period.
- 420 manual corrections and 110 correction-to-recollection transitions.
- Recollection takes a median 60 hours at peak.
- Overall rework rises from 7.5% to 11.8%.

These loops also consume clerk and intake time through physician communication.

Acceptance objective: stop defective material early and reduce avoidable recirculation rather than merely processing defects faster.

### S6 — Recovery period after priority operation

After an Urgent-heavy wave or outage, Routine work has been repeatedly displaced. Routine p90 already reaches 120 hours at peak against a 72-hour target. Strict priority can make the apparent Urgent recovery successful while leaving excessive Routine work-in-process (WIP), delayed Complex panels, and unstable future shifts.

Acceptance objective: drain accumulated WIP safely and prevent permanent starvation.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1 — Mix shift

The initial bottleneck is likely to form at **pre-analytical check**: utilization rises from 74% to 96%, and the median queue from 1.8 to 9.5 hours. More work then reaches:

1. **Preparation benches**, where the peak median queue is 11 hours.
2. **Assay machines**, at 98% utilization and an 18-hour queue.
3. **Technical validation**, at 101% utilization and a 16-hour queue.
4. **Medical validation/specialist review**, disproportionately loaded by Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

A strict Urgent-first rule at preparation and assay would improve some Urgent cases, but it would fragment machine schedules and starve Routine and possibly long Complex-panel work. Relieving assay capacity alone would discharge work faster into technical validation, already over nominal capacity. Relieving technical validation would then increase arrivals to medical validation, whose peak edge from technical validation is 24 hours.

Thus the likely bottleneck migration is:

**Pre-analysis  preparation/assay  technical validation  medical validation/specialist review.**

### S2 — Assay degradation or outage

The first bottleneck is the **remaining assay capacity**. M2’s higher failure propensity is important because assigning all Routine work to it, as in I3, could increase repeat demand precisely when capacity is scarce.

The repeat loop expands:

**Assay run  Repeat assay  Assay run**, whose second edge rises from 10 to 26 median hours at peak.

Every repeat both occupies another machine slot and later generates another validation event. If critical cases are protected by dedicating M1, Routine cases on M2 may develop more repeats and a large recovery backlog. Once assay service resumes, a burst reaches:

- technical validation at 101% peak utilization,
- then medical validation for Urgent and Complex-panel work.

Therefore restoring the machine is not the end of the disruption; release from the assay backlog can move the bottleneck to validation. Recovery dispatch must be paced against downstream validation headroom.

### S3 — Validator or specialist absence

The bottleneck begins at **medical validation or specialist review**, rather than assay. Complex-panel demand amplifies it because these cases are far more likely to use both resources.

If preparation and assay continue feeding this path at full speed, WIP accumulates after technical validation. The observed peak flows are substantial:

- Technical validation  Medical validation: 3,420.
- Technical validation  Hold for specialist review: 610.
- Hold  Medical validation: 560.

Accelerating technical validation during this scenario would worsen the downstream queue unless cases eligible for direct release are separated from cases requiring medical review. Conversely, indiscriminately slowing all technical validation would unnecessarily delay the 4,050 peak cases that can go directly to release.

The migration sequence is:

**Specialist review/medical validation  post-technical-validation holding WIP  eventually release work**, if a large backlog is cleared simultaneously.

### S4 — Courier compression or delay

The bottleneck first appears at **courier intake**, then migrates rapidly:

1. Courier intake receives a route wave.
2. Pre-analytical check receives a compressed burst; its peak median queue is 9.5 hours.
3. Defects are discovered late, creating recollection and correction loops.
4. Acceptable samples are released as another wave to preparation and assay.
5. The assay wave then reaches technical validation together.

I7 can smooth the initial arrival pattern, but if courier changes merely cause more samples to arrive earlier without paced intake, the bottleneck moves to pre-analysis. I8 can accelerate registration, but registration is not the dominant peak constraint; faster registration could expose pre-analysis and assay saturation sooner.

### S5 — Pre-analytical quality deterioration

The first bottleneck is **pre-analytical check**, followed by **accession correction specialists**, who have a 14-hour peak queue and only two specialists. If correction is accelerated using I4, corrected cases move to preparation in 310 peak transitions, while another 110 still require recollection.

Cross-training technical validators into correction can shorten that queue, but it may deepen the technical-validation bottleneck, already at 101% peak utilization. The migration is therefore:

**Pre-analysis  accession correction  preparation/assay**, while the labor transfer may separately cause **technical-validation congestion**.

I6 is preferable when it prevents bad samples from entering downstream work, but it can produce a short-term increase in recollection communication and courier workload. Its benefit depends on rapid notification and better recollection quality; otherwise it simply starts the 60-hour loop earlier.

### S6 — Recovery after priority operation

The initial bottleneck is accumulated **Routine WIP** at preparation and assay. Removing priority suddenly can then send a large Routine wave to technical validation and release.

I2, larger Routine batches, may improve machine setup efficiency during recovery, but batch wait already rises to 22 hours on the peak Batch wait  Assay run edge. Oversized batches may improve utilization while worsening individual Routine target compliance.

I9 would suppress release workload by delaying Routine release until batch reporting, but it creates deliberate non-value-added waiting and obscures the true completion state. It should not be a default redesign.

# 3. Robust redesign package

## A. Case-class lanes with bounded, not absolute, priority

Replace I1’s strict Urgent-first rule with three dispatch classes:

1. **Expedite lane:** genuine Urgent cases approaching contractual risk.
2. **Protected specialist lane:** Complex panels whose assay sequence or validator availability makes delay costly.
3. **Routine/recovery lane:** Routine and repeat work, with an aging rule.

Use weighted quotas rather than universal priority—for example, reserve a configurable share of preparation and assay starts for expedite work, while guaranteeing service to aged Routine and Complex-panel cases. A case should gain priority as its remaining SLA slack falls, not merely because of its label.

Why robust:

- Protects the 18-hour Urgent target.
- Avoids indefinite Routine starvation.
- Recognizes that Complex panels cannot always be delayed behind shorter Urgent jobs.
- Can be retuned during outages or validator absences.

Assumptions and failure modes:

- Requires reliable case classification and timestamps.
- Excessive Urgent labeling will defeat the design.
- Too much expedite reservation fragments assay schedules and overloads medical validation.
- Dispatch must consider downstream validator capacity, not only machine availability.

## B. Early defect triage plus source-quality feedback

Implement I6 with a two-hour target from physical receipt:

- verify tube suitability, identity, metadata, and routing requirements;
- immediately reject unrecoverable defects;
- send correctable metadata defects to a fast correction lane;
- use standardized digital recollection messages;
- report defect rates by collector, route, facility, shift, and tube type.

This addresses the rise in rework and the 60-hour recollection loop. It is more robust than simply adding correction labor because it reduces work entering preparation and assay.

Downstream effect:

- In the short term, faster rejection may increase recollection contacts and later courier returns.
- Successful recollections will return as another intake wave, so they need tagged priority and scheduled reinsertion.
- If triage standards are too aggressive, unnecessary rejection will increase total delay and sample consumption.

## C. Smooth intake and pace release into pre-analysis

Adopt I7 where route contracts permit, supplemented by appointment windows, route staggering, and a small controlled intake buffer. Within a compressed arrival, sort first by:

- sample stability deadline,
- Urgent contractual slack,
- defect risk,
- then arrival time.

The purpose is not to hide samples at courier intake; it is to avoid releasing a 10:00 or 17:00 wave all at once to pre-analysis.

This is robust against both normal peaks and weather recovery. However, smoothing intake without considering stability limits can damage specimens, and moving courier slots may shift workload to collection sites.

## D. Selective registration automation with a release gate

Use I8 only for orders with complete, validated digital metadata. Automatically registered orders should enter a visible pre-analysis admission queue rather than flooding the bench.

Controls should include:

- metadata completeness and identifier validation,
- duplicate-order detection,
- exception routing,
- rate-limited release to pre-analysis during saturation.

This reduces clerical work without treating registration as the principal bottleneck. Saved clerk capacity can support rapid recollection communication, but automation must not simply move waiting downstream.

## E. Flexible correction coverage without routine validator depletion

Use I4 only as contingency coverage, not as a permanent transfer. Cross-train two technical validators over four weeks, but deploy them to accession correction only when:

- the correction queue exceeds a threshold,
- technical-validation utilization and age remain below their own safety threshold,
- and the expected number of corrected cases justifies the transfer.

One person at a time should generally rotate for a short block. Because corrections unblock many cases, this can be high leverage, but technical validation must remain protected.

## F. Peak medical-validation coverage and pooled scheduling

Implement I5 during forecast peaks and known specialist-risk days. The evening shift should cover both:

- new Urgent/Complex-panel arrivals,
- aging cases already technically validated.

Pre-assign validator windows to expected assay completions and use remote validation where legally and clinically permissible. Separate cases requiring genuine specialist judgment from those eligible for protocolized medical validation.

This is robust against the observed case-mix shift, but only if upstream dispatch is coordinated. Otherwise more medical capacity may encourage excessive feeding of complex work and move congestion back to assay or specialist review.

## G. Reliability-centered machine routing

Do not adopt I3 as a permanent rule. Instead:

- route by assay compatibility, urgency, remaining SLA slack, and observed machine failure risk;
- cap M2 loading when repeat rate rises;
- avoid concentrating all Routine work on the less reliable machine;
- reserve some M1 access for aged Routine/repeat cases;
- perform enhanced quality checks after night shifts and after M2 fault signals.

This reduces the risk of a positive repeat loop. The machine schedule should optimize **good results per machine hour**, not nominal runtime.

## H. Controlled Routine batching

Use I2 selectively rather than fixing batch size at 24. Batch size should vary with:

- oldest Routine case age,
- machine availability,
- current Urgent reservation,
- setup savings,
- technical-validation headroom.

Large batches can be used during stable recovery periods, but split or launch partial batches when the oldest Routine case approaches its target. I9—holding completed Routine results for batch release—should not be standard because it adds waiting after clinical work is complete.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

### Policy P1 — Mix-surge mode

**Switch on when any two occur:**

- Urgent share exceeds 25% over a rolling day.
- Complex-panel share exceeds 17%.
- Assay utilization exceeds 90% or assay median queue exceeds 10 hours.
- Medical-validation queue exceeds 12 hours.
- Forecast Urgent breach rate exceeds 10%.

**Actions:**

- Activate bounded expedite quotas.
- Start the evening medical-validation shift.
- Reduce Routine batch launch frequency only within an aging cap.
- Reserve machine slots for Complex panels whose downstream validator slot is confirmed.
- Freeze nonessential schedule changes that fragment assay runs.

**Switch off when:** all above signals remain below thresholds for two consecutive shifts and aged Routine WIP is declining.

### Policy P2 — Machine-degradation mode

**Switch on when:**

- either machine is unavailable for more than 30 minutes during a high-load shift;
- first-pass assay success falls below a locally established control limit;
- repeat rate exceeds 10% overall or exceeds its M2/night-shift control limit;
- assay queue exceeds 14 hours and is increasing.

**Actions:**

- Protect critical Urgent assays, but retain a small aged-case quota.
- Route by expected good-result yield rather than permanent M1 dedication.
- Stop launching low-priority work whose stability allows deferral.
- Add enhanced QC and engineering review.
- Pace assay recovery against technical-validation capacity.
- Consider partner-lab routing only for compatible Complex-panel work.

**Switch off when:** machine availability and first-pass yield remain in control for a full shift and assay WIP falls below the recovery threshold.

### Policy P3 — Validator/specialist-loss mode

**Switch on when:**

- an absence is known for the shift;
- medical-validation median queue exceeds 12 hours or oldest Urgent exceeds 4 hours there;
- specialist-review queue exceeds 24 hours;
- projected specialist demand exceeds available slots by 20%.

**Actions:**

- Activate I5 or remote coverage.
- Pre-book scarce specialist slots for Urgent and high-consequence Complex panels.
- Continue direct-release technical-validation cases normally.
- Hold or pace nonurgent assays that necessarily require unavailable review.
- Activate I10 only for eligible Complex panels when expected local completion exceeds partner completion, including the 12-hour transport delay.

**Switch off when:** coverage returns and specialist WIP is below one shift of capacity.

### Policy P4 — Courier-wave mode

**Switch on when:**

- projected arrivals in any two-hour window exceed 125% of normal intake capacity;
- a route is delayed more than two hours;
- courier-intake queue exceeds four hours;
- weather cancellation creates a known catch-up wave.

**Actions:**

- Stagger rerouted pickups where feasible.
- Pre-register complete digital orders, but meter their release.
- Triage by stability and SLA slack at arrival.
- Extend a short intake/pre-analysis overlap shift rather than indiscriminately adding overtime everywhere.
- Notify preparation and assay of the expected downstream wave.

**Switch off when:** intake and pre-analysis queues are below four hours and no compressed route remains.

### Policy P5 — Defect-surge mode

**Switch on when:**

- pre-analytical defect/recollection rate exceeds 9%;
- accession-correction median queue exceeds eight hours;
- a collector, route, or shift crosses a defect control limit.

**Actions:**

- Activate enhanced two-hour triage.
- Deploy one cross-trained technical validator to correction only if technical-validation queue is below eight hours and utilization is below 90%.
- Contact high-defect collection sites immediately.
- Reserve intake slots for returning recollections.

**Switch off when:** defect rate and correction queue are within control for two shifts.

### Policy P6 — Backlog-recovery mode

**Switch on when:**

- Routine p90 projection exceeds 72 hours;
- more than 15% of Routine WIP is older than 48 hours;
- critical disruption has ended but total WIP remains above 1.5 shifts.

**Actions:**

- Reduce the expedite reservation gradually rather than abruptly.
- Use controlled larger Routine batches where setup savings are real.
- Apply age-based dispatch.
- Pace output to technical-validation capacity.
- Do not hold clinically completed results merely to create batch reports.

**Switch off when:** aged Routine WIP is below 5% and all downstream queues are stable or falling.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Simultaneous correlated failures remain dangerous.** A courier catch-up wave combined with M1 outage and specialist absence can exceed what dispatch rules can absorb without temporary external capacity or explicit rationing.
- **Medical-validation skills remain scarce.** I5 helps planned peaks but does not fully protect against prolonged illness or simultaneous absences.
- **Complex panels are structurally fragile.** Multiple assays, medical validation, and elevated specialist-review probability create several serial failure points.
- **Recollection remains slow.** Early triage starts the loop sooner, but the observed 60-hour peak return time depends substantially on external clinicians and patients.
- **Flattened order-level logs obscure tube-level workload.** A Complex-panel order with several tubes and assays may appear as one case, so order counts can underestimate preparation and machine demand.
- **Partner-lab dependence creates new fragility.** I10 introduces transport, chain-of-custody, interface, and result-reconciliation risks, especially during regional winter disruption.
- **The redesigned priority system may be gamed.** Users may relabel work as Urgent, eroding reserved capacity and starving genuinely constrained cases.
- **Paced admission can hide waiting upstream.** If the dashboard measures only internal station queues, intake gating may make downstream performance look better while total order cycle time does not improve.
- **Dynamic batching may create output waves.** Larger Routine batches during recovery can overload technical validation even when machine utilization improves.
- **Cross-training creates a labor-coupling vulnerability.** The same people become contingency capacity for accession correction and core capacity for technical validation; simultaneous queues cannot both be relieved.
- **Automation may accelerate bad work.** Incomplete validation rules could allow registration defects to reach pre-analysis faster.
- **Staff fatigue remains a constraint.** Registration overtime beyond nine-hour shifts increases errors, potentially generating the very correction and recollection workload the redesign seeks to reduce.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

## Leading indicators

These should be segmented by case type, machine, shift, and where relevant collection source.

| Arrival concentration in busiest 2h | <100% of planned intake capacity | 100–125% | >125%: courier-wave mode |

Queue medians should be supplemented by the **oldest case** and upper-tail age; medians alone can conceal starvation.

## Lagging outcome metrics

Review daily, but do not wait for them before switching policies:

- Urgent percentage released within 18 hours, plus p90 cycle time.
- Routine percentage released within 72 hours, plus p90 cycle time.
- Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure median and p90 cycle times.
- Percentage of cases with any queue over 12 hours.
- Rework, recollection, and repeat-assay rates.
- Cancellation rate following recollection.
- First-pass yield by M1/M2 and shift.
- Total cycle time measured from registration—including time in intake gates or outsourcing transport.
- Cases completed technically but not yet released.
- Partner-lab transport, traceability, exception, and turnaround performance.

The operating objective should be a balanced envelope: protect Urgent contractual performance, cap aged Routine and Complex-panel WIP, reduce repeat-generating work, and prevent any local improvement from silently transferring congestion to the next station.
