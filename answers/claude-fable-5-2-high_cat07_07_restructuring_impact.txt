# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring has one plausible gain (pooling relieves the overloaded East specialist) and several plausible costs (central queueing, loss of regional knowledge, unclear ownership). Which dominates depends mainly on how loaded the pooled specialists end up, and that can be read from the event log within weeks.

"Technical pool" below means the proposed Technical Shared Service; "shared queue" means the common queue for Specialist Review, Legal Review, Risk Review and difficult Quality Check.

## What management's own estimate already implies

Total volume is 2,100 cases per month (820 + 760 + 520).

| Check on the two handover tables | Current | Management estimate | Implication |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Listed handovers per 100 cases | 343 | 359 | Nominal handovers rise by 4.7%. Only intake → caseworker (88 → 83) and cross-team (6 → 3) fall. |
| Specialist loop, out and back | 67 | 77 | The "local-specialist loop" is replaced by a larger central loop. |
| Approval loop, in and out | 81 | 92 | More approvals, through fewer approvers. |
| Frequency × median wait, hours per 100 cases | 588 | 722 | Handover waiting rises about 23% (rough proxy: medians do not add). |
| Technical referrals per 100 cases | 36 | 41 | +14% demand. Applied to today's mean technical utilization of about 74%, that gives about 84%. The "15% higher utilization" is mostly extra demand, not extra efficiency. |
| Legal/Risk referrals per 100 cases | 9 | 12 | +33% demand on specialists at 93% and 91%. Without added capacity the implied load is about 124% and 121%, which no queue can absorb. The work will spill onto technical specialists, as staff fear. |
| Approval handovers per approver per month | 294 | about 515 | +75%, assuming the two remaining supervisors staff the shared approval queue (882 / 3 versus 1,029 / 2). |
| Approvals not passing straight to implementation, per 100 cases | 3 | 6 | Doubles (42 − 39 versus 49 − 43). Either more rejections or more loops; the estimate does not say which. |

## Why the pool's load decides the outcome

An idealized queue model (random arrivals, interchangeable specialists, first-come-first-served) gives the mean queue wait in multiples of the mean service time S:

| Configuration | Utilization | Mean queue wait |
|---|---:|---:|
| S_Tech alone | 68% | 2.1 S |
| N_Tech alone | 71% | 2.4 S |
| E_Tech alone | 82% | 4.6 S |
| Pool of three | 74% (today's load) | 0.7 S |
| Pool of three | 85% | 1.6 S |
| Pool of three | 88% | 2.2 S |
| Pool of three | 90% | 2.7 S |
| Pool of three | 93–94% | 4.2–4.9 S |
| Pool of three | 95% | 6.0 S |

- **Pooling is a real benefit at today's load.** Three pooled specialists at 74% would wait far less than any of the three alone.
- **North and South break even at about 88% pool utilization; East still gains up to about 93%.** This is the structural reason East and North/South will experience the change differently.
- **The plan aims at the break-even zone.** "15% higher utilization" means about 85% if relative and about 89% if percentage points, before legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Check are added to the same people.
- **The model is optimistic.** Specialists are not interchangeable on regional conventions, and management itself expects the wait to rise from 3.2h to 4.0h.

Two reinforcing loops matter. Less informal advice produces more formal referrals, which raise pool utilization, which lengthens waits, which pushes caseworkers to refer earlier. Routing hard cases away reduces local skill, which increases referrals and rework on returned advice.

## Optimistic scenario

Assumes Legal/Risk capacity is added, case notes are standardized first, each case keeps a named owner, and a channel for quick informal advice survives.

- **Handover pattern:** Cross-team cover halves. Specialist referrals stay near today's level because quick questions are answered without a formal referral. Advice returns to the caseworker who asked.
- **Queue and waiting-time pattern:** The pool runs at 80–85%, so waits into the technical pool are at or below today's 3.2h. East's specialist wait falls most.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East's median and P90 both improve. North and South are flat. The whole distribution shifts or holds; the tail does not fatten.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** East's rework falls toward 10%. North/South rework stays at 8–9%. Rework after a shared-service handover stays near the local 9%, not the cross-team 18%.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Discovered roles stay distinct. Approval decisions become more consistent across approvers, and each has a named decider.
- **Fairness implications:** The gap between East and the other teams narrows. Waits at equal complexity and priority are similar across originating teams and customer segments.
- **Early warning signs of leaving this scenario:** Pool utilization above 85% for three weeks; referrals above 41 per 100; rework after shared-service handovers above 12%.

## Central scenario

Assumes the plan is implemented roughly as proposed, with management's routing frequencies but realistic queueing.

- **Handover pattern:** Total handovers rise 5–10%. Some advice bounces: a second referral on the same case, or advice picked up by a different caseworker. Legal/risk/security triage lands on technical specialists.
- **Queue and waiting-time pattern:** The pool runs at 85–90%. Technical waits are 4–6h; Legal/Risk waits are 12–18h, with backlog during peaks. A new, unmanaged wait appears between "advice completed" and "advice pulled".
- **Cycle-time distribution:** The overall median is roughly flat, because East improves by about half a day while North/South worsen slightly. The P90 for high-complexity and specialty cases grows 10–25%.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Overall rework drifts up about one point. It falls in East and rises on complex cases in North/South, where regional conventions used to be applied locally.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Reassignments per case rise. Approvals sit unclaimed longer. The specialist role absorbs Quality Check and triage, so the role discovered from the log blurs.
- **Fairness implications:** Simple cases are served as before or better; high-complexity, specialty and accommodation cases wait longer. North/South subsidize East's improvement.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue-wait P90 growing faster than its median; pick-up delay above two hours; a rising share of cases with two or more reassignments; North/South P90 rising while the overall median is stable.

## Adverse scenario

Assumes informal advice disappears, defensive referral takes hold, and Legal/Risk demand exceeds capacity.

- **Handover pattern:** Referrals reach 48–55 per 100 cases. Repeated local → shared → local loops and referral chains through several specialists become common variants. Cross-team-style handover problems apply to most complex cases.
- **Queue and waiting-time pattern:** The pool exceeds 92%. Technical waits are 8–12h. The Legal/Risk backlog grows week over week because arrivals exceed completions. Difficult Quality Check competes with Specialist Review for the same people.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Medians worsen by a day or more and the P90 by four to eight days in every team. East's initial gain is eroded within months.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework reaches 11.5–13%. Quality Check is done late or by the specialist who gave the advice, weakening its independence.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases stall with no identifiable owner. Approval outcomes vary by whoever picks the item up. Supervisors cannot explain delays in their own team's cases.
- **Fairness implications:** Outcomes depend on originating team and customer segment. Customers needing language or regional accommodation face the most handovers and the longest tails. Urgent cases displace others without a transparent rule.
- **Early warning signs:** Weekly arrivals above completions at the shared queue for three weeks; pool utilization above 90%; referrals per caseworker rising; staff building workarounds such as direct messages to former local specialists.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency.** A queue near capacity lengthens the slowest cases far more than the typical case. If 85% of cases become 0.3 days faster and 15% become 1.5 days slower, the mean moves by only −0.03 days. Track P90 and P95 and the share of cases over the SLA, per cohort.
2. **High-complexity cases.** They are the cases the routing rule sends to the pool, so they carry nearly all the new queue time. They are a minority of volume and barely move the average. Report every measure separately for high complexity and for legal, risk and security specialties.
3. **Team mix masking (East versus North/South).** East is 25% of volume. An improvement of 0.8 days there offsets a worsening of about 0.25 days in North and South, leaving the overall figure flat while two of three teams decline. Always report by originating team.
4. **Local knowledge loss.** In the first months, pooled specialists still remember their regions, so nothing appears in cycle time. The loss shows later as rework and repeated customer contact on regional cases, and after staff turnover. Watch `local_expertise_flag` coverage and rework by flag.
5. **Fairness.** Waits can be redistributed between teams, priorities and customer segments with no change in the mean. Customers needing language or regional accommodation can face more handovers while simple cases speed up. Compare waits and SLA misses at equal complexity and priority.
6. **Learning.** Routing hard cases away can shorten cycle time now, because experts handle them, while local staff stop developing. The cost appears as rising referral rates and falling unassisted resolution over quarters.
7. **Accountability.** A shared approval queue can be fast and still leave no one answerable for a case. Cycle time does not show who decided, how often cases were reassigned, or whether similar cases get similar outcomes. Watch reassignments, distinct approvers per case, and outcome variation by approver.
8. **Exception handling.** Standard routing rules fit standard cases. Misrouted cases, cases with two specialties, and urgent regional exceptions create rare, long variants that the mean ignores. Watch new and low-frequency variants and their durations.
9. **Resilience.** Average performance in normal weeks says nothing about peaks or simultaneous absences, when a single shared backlog delays all teams at once. Compare absence weeks with normal weeks.
10. **Unfinished cases.** Shortly after a change, only fast cases have closed. An average over closed cases is biased downward until the slow ones finish. Compare cohorts by start date with equal follow-up, and report the share still open.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope

- **Step 0, preparation (4 weeks, no structural change):** Introduce one case-note template across locations, since inconsistent notes are the known cause of poor cross-team cover. Log queue events with individual resource identifiers and start timestamps. Record a named owner per case. Agree SLA thresholds and freeze the baseline tables.
- **Step 1, technical pooling (3 weeks' transition plus 12 observed weeks):** Pool Specialist Review for South and East only, with S_Tech and E_Tech forming the pool. South has the most spare specialist capacity (68%) and East the least (82%). Legal/Risk routing, difficult Quality Check and supervisors stay as they are.
- **Step 2, approval queue (12 further weeks, only if Step 1 guardrails hold):** Add a shared approval queue for high-value cases in the pilot teams, with a named approver per case. Keep the supervisor position until the evidence is in. Bring North into the pool.
- **Step 3, specialty routing:** Broaden legal/risk/security routing only with added capacity or a triage filter. Centralize difficult Quality Check only with a rule that the checker did not advise on the case.

Piloting East alone would show the benefit and hide the cost. Pairing East with South tests whether a well-performing team loses when its specialist is shared. Changing one element per step keeps effects attributable.

## Comparison group

- **North** stays unchanged and is the comparison team. It resembles South at baseline (6.8d versus 7.4d median, 8.2% versus 9.0% rework).
- **South** is judged by difference-in-differences against North.
- **East** has no comparable team, so it is judged against its own pre-change trend and against North's trend over the same weeks.
- Three teams cannot support a team-level significance test. The pilot gives direction and size, with case-level uncertainty intervals inside each segment.

## Pre/post observation window

- **Pre:** 6 to 12 months of history, using the same calendar months where possible.
- **Post:** 12 weeks after a 3-week transition period that is reported separately.
- **Cohorts:** Group cases by start date and follow each cohort for 45 days (about twice East's P90 of 22.8 days) before judging it.
- **Volumes:** Twelve weeks give roughly 2,270 cases in North, 2,100 in South and 1,440 in East. East will have only about 130 Legal/Risk referrals, so tail estimates in small segments are noisy.

## Segmentation by team, complexity, specialty, and customer segment

Report every guardrail by originating team, `case_complexity`, `required_specialty` (none, technical, legal, risk, security) and `customer_segment`, plus `priority`. Require a minimum cell size, for example 50 closed cases, before reading a P90. Pool weeks rather than drop a segment.

## Guardrail metrics

Thresholds are proposals, to be calibrated against the baseline's week-to-week variation.

| Guardrail | Adjust | Roll back |
|---|---|---|
| Median wait into technical pool | Above 6h for two weeks | Above 10h, or backlog growing three weeks running |
| Pool utilization | Above 85% for three weeks | Above 90% for three weeks |
| P90 cycle time, high-complexity cases, any pilot team | +15% against control-adjusted baseline | +25% |
| Rework after shared-service handover | Above 12% | Above 15% |
| SLA miss rate, any pilot team | +2 points | +4 points |
| South median or P90 while East improves | Worse by 10% | Worse by 20% |
| Queue-wait gap between teams at equal complexity and priority | Above 25% | Above 50% |
| SLA-miss gap between customer segments | Widens 2 points | Widens 4 points |
| Technical referrals per 100 cases | Above 45 | Above 50 |
| Cases with two or more reassignments | +30% relative | +60% relative |
| Customer satisfaction, any team | −0.2 | −0.4 |

## Staff feedback collection

- A five-question pulse survey every two weeks on ownership clarity, access to advice, note quality and workload.
- A tally of "would have asked the local specialist" moments, to size the informal advice that the log does not record.
- A misrouting flag tied to the case identifier, so comments can be joined to the event log.
- Structured interviews with caseworkers, specialists and supervisors at weeks 4 and 12, covering workarounds and skill development.

## Rollback or adjustment triggers

- **Adjustments, in order:** add specialist capacity; name a liaison specialist per region; replace "all high-complexity cases" with caseworker discretion plus triage; reserve pool capacity per team; split the queue by work type; switch to overflow-only pooling, where specialists stay embedded and take other teams' cases only when a local queue exceeds a threshold.
- **Rollback:** any rollback-level breach sustained for the stated period returns the specialist to the affected team. Positions, seating and system permissions stay in place during the pilot so that this takes days.
- **Stop expansion:** if East does not improve within the 12 observed weeks, the main benefit is absent and Step 2 does not start.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with modifications. Do not roll out fully, and do not remove the supervisor position yet.**

Reasons:

- **The plan's stated benefit is not in its own numbers.** Listed handovers rise from 343 to 359 per 100 cases and handover waiting rises about 23%.
- **Pooling can work, but the plan aims at its limit.** North/South break even near 88% pool utilization; the target is 85–89% before triage and Quality Check are added.
- **Legal/Risk cannot absorb the plan.** Specialists at 93% and 91% cannot take 33% more referrals.
- **The one piece of evidence on cross-location handovers is unfavorable.** They carry 18% rework because of inconsistent notes, and the plan makes most specialist handovers cross-location without fixing the notes.
- **East has a real problem worth solving.** E_Tech at 82% with the worst cycle time, rework and SLA results is a legitimate case for sharing capacity, so rejection is not warranted either.

Modifications to make before the pilot: standardize case notes, keep a named owner per case, keep a quick-advice channel to a regional specialist, give Legal/Risk capacity or a triage filter before broadening routing, and separate the checker from the adviser on difficult Quality Check.

The decision after the pilot depends on the indicators:

- **Expand** if East's P90 and SLA misses improve, South is no worse than North beyond the guardrails, rework after shared-service handovers stays at or below 12%, pool utilization stays at or below 85%, and no fairness gap widens.
- **Modify** if East improves and South deteriorates. Overflow-only pooling or added capacity in East would then deliver East's gain without North/South's loss.
- **Stop** if any rollback trigger holds, or if East does not improve.

Management should not infer the following from early averages:

- **A lower early average does not show the change works.** The first closed cases are the fast ones; the slow ones are still open.
- **Short early waits do not show the queue is stable.** The queue starts empty, and near capacity a backlog takes weeks to build.
- **A flat overall average does not show nobody lost.** It can combine East's gain with North/South's loss, or simple cases' gain with complex cases' loss.
- **Higher utilization is not a benefit in itself.** It is the load that produces the queue; the benefit is only what happens to waits, rework and SLA misses.
- **Fewer recorded handovers may be a logging effect.** A queue logged as one resource hides handovers inside it.
- **No early rise in rework does not show local knowledge is safe.** Specialists still remember their regions; knowledge and skill erode over quarters.
- **Pilot results flatter the change.** A watched pilot gets more attention and goodwill than steady operation.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The script below compares the periods before and after the change. I ran it under PM4Py 2.7 on a small synthetic log with the stated columns to confirm it executes; it has not been run on the real log. The cutover date, SLA thresholds, team labels, activity names and the `start_timestamp` column are placeholders to adapt.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE, ACT, TS, RES = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "org:resource"
CUTOVER = pd.Timestamp("2027-01-04", tz="UTC")      # placeholder go-live date
WASHOUT = pd.Timedelta(days=21)                     # transition weeks reported separately
FOLLOW_UP = pd.Timedelta(days=45)                   # equal follow-up for every cohort
SLA_DAYS = {"high": 7, "medium": 14, "low": 21}     # placeholder SLA per priority
LOCAL_TEAMS = ["North", "South", "East"]
PILOT_TEAMS, CONTROL_TEAMS = ["South", "East"], ["North"]

#--- 0. Load and prepare ---
raw = pd.read_csv("event_log.csv")
log = pm4py.format_dataframe(raw, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS)
if "start_timestamp" in log.columns:                # needed to separate waiting from service
    log["start_timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(log["start_timestamp"], utc=True)
    START = "start_timestamp"
else:
    START = TS                                      # only inter-event gaps are measurable

def role_of(resource: str) -> str:
    """Stable role-level node so the before/after networks are comparable."""
    if resource.startswith("Bot_"):
        return "bot"
    if resource.startswith("Specialist_"):
        return "legal_risk_specialist"
    for key, role in [("Intake", "intake"), ("Case", "caseworker"),
                      ("Tech", "tech_specialist"), ("Supervisor", "supervisor")]:
        if key in resource:
            return role
    return "shared_queue"

log["role"] = log[RES].map(role_of)
log = log.sort_values([CASE, START, TS]).reset_index(drop=True)

#--- 1. Case table: cohorts, segments, outcomes, SLA misses ---
home_team = log[log["team"].isin(LOCAL_TEAMS)].groupby(CASE)["team"].first().rename("home_team")
cases = log.groupby(CASE).agg(
    start=(START, "min"), end=(TS, "max"),
    complexity=("case_complexity", "first"), specialty=("required_specialty", "first"),
    segment=("customer_segment", "first"), priority=("priority", "first"),
    rework=("rework_flag", "max"), reassignments=("reassignment_count", "max"),
    local_expertise=("local_expertise_flag", "max"),
    closed=(ACT, lambda s: "Close" in set(s)),
).join(home_team)
cases["rework"] = cases["rework"].astype(bool)
cases["cycle_days"] = (cases["end"] - cases["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
cases["sla_miss"] = cases["cycle_days"] > cases["priority"].map(SLA_DAYS)
cases["arm"] = cases["home_team"].map(lambda t: "pilot" if t in PILOT_TEAMS else "control")

          & (cases["start"] <= extract_time - FOLLOW_UP), "period"] = "after"

                left_on=CASE, right_index=True)

def kpis(g: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.Series:
    return pd.Series({
        "n": len(g),
        "median_d": g["cycle_days"].median(),
        "p90_d": g["cycle_days"].quantile(0.90),
        "p95_d": g["cycle_days"].quantile(0.95),
        "rework_rate": g["rework"].mean(),
        "sla_miss_rate": g["sla_miss"].mean(),
        "multi_reassign": (g["reassignments"] >= 2).mean(),
    })

def segment_table(by: list[str]) -> pd.DataFrame:
    sel = cases[cases["period"].isin(["before", "after"])]
    rows = {key: kpis(g) for key, g in sel.groupby(["period"] + by)}
    out = pd.DataFrame(rows).T
    out.index.names = ["period"] + by
    return out.unstack("period")

by_specialty = segment_table(["specialty"])

def diff_in_diff(metric: str, by: list[str]) -> pd.Series:
    """(pilot after - pilot before) - (control after - control before), per segment."""
    t = segment_table(["arm"] + by)[metric]
    change = t["after"] - t["before"]
    return change.xs("pilot", level="arm") - change.xs("control", level="arm")

#--- 3. Handover-of-work, working-together and subcontracting networks ---
def edges(sna) -> pd.Series:
    connections = getattr(sna, "connections", sna)   # SNA object in recent PM4Py versions
    return pd.Series({pair: float(value) for pair, value in dict(connections).items()}, dtype=float)

def compare_network(discover, log_before, log_after, **kwargs) -> pd.DataFrame:
    out = pd.concat([edges(discover(log_before, **kwargs)), edges(discover(log_after, **kwargs))],
                    axis=1, keys=["before", "after"]).fillna(0.0)
    out["delta"] = out["after"] - out["before"]
    return out.sort_values("delta")

## Edge values are normalized weights; section 4 gives counts per 100 cases.
handover_roles = compare_network(pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network, before, after, resource_key="role")

together = compare_network(pm4py.discover_working_together_network, before, after)
subcontracting = compare_network(pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network, before, after, n=2)

def concentration(network: pd.DataFrame, column: str) -> pd.Series:
    """Share of all incoming handover weight per resource: bottleneck / single point of failure."""
    incoming = network[column].groupby(level=1).sum()
    return (incoming / incoming.sum()).sort_values(ascending=False)

bottleneck_before, bottleneck_after = concentration(handover_people, "before"), concentration(handover_people, "after")

#--- 4. Handover table with waits and rework (mirrors the scenario table) ---

def handover_table(by: list[str]) -> pd.DataFrame:
    t = ho.groupby(["period", "type"] + by).agg(
        n=("wait_h", "size"), median_wait_h=("wait_h", "median"),
        p90_wait_h=("wait_h", lambda s: s.quantile(0.90)), rework_after=("case_rework", "mean"))
    cases_in_segment = ev.groupby(["period"] + by)[CASE].nunique()
    t["per_100_cases"] = 100 * t["n"].to_numpy() / cases_in_segment.reindex(t.index.droplevel("type")).to_numpy()
```
