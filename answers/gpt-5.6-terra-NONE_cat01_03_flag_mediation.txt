## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I use **policy/interventional effects** as the primary framework, rather than natural direct/indirect effects. Natural effects require cross-world quantities such as the outcome under flag display while manual review is set to whatever it would have been without display; these are especially implausible with sequential routing, unmeasured risk, and shared queues. Controlled and stochastic/interventional mediator policies are more interpretable here.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P1 suppress flag display versus P0 current policy | Arrival cohort during specified operating hours | Total cycle time, close minus arrival | Total policy effect of display/routing bundle | Conditionally; must specify whether priority and automatic review rules are disabled when display is suppressed | Hidden underlying risk; changing display changes human behavior and queues |
| E2 | P0 versus P1 | Cases arriving within a risk-score threshold band and complexity/load strata | Arrival-to-triage completion | Total effect on triage completion | Mostly; the operational rule after suppression must be fixed | Score/risk discontinuity, time-varying congestion, priority spillover |
| E3 | P2 retain priority but remove mandatory review versus P0 | Flag-eligible cases | Probability of manual-review occurrence | Controlled policy effect on occurrence mediation | Yes if “mandatory” and exceptions are specified | Human discretionary review may replace automatic review; quality consequences |
| E4 | P2 versus P0, among cases reviewed under each policy | Flag-eligible arrival cohort | Total cycle time | Interventional analogue: effect through changing the distribution of manual-review occurrence | Yes as a stochastic routing-policy contrast, not a natural indirect effect | Selection into review; review occurrence is post-treatment and affected by risk signals |
| E5 | P3 protected review capacity versus P0 | All arrivals, separately flagged and unflagged | Manual-review queue wait, manual start minus triage done | Queue-mediated policy effect / controlled capacity effect | Yes only with a precise reservation rule and protected-capacity amount | Interference: reserving capacity may delay unflagged cases; workload displacement |
| E6 | P4 changed escalation criteria/capacity versus P0 | Reviewed cases and full arrival cohort | Escalation occurrence, escalation wait, cycle time | Interventional escalation-mediated effect | Yes if criteria, staffing, and senior capacity are specified | Escalation is selected by evolving case evidence; senior-team queue spillovers |
| E7 | P0 versus P2 | Flag-eligible cases, evaluated as an arrival cohort | Total cycle time | Total cycle-time policy effect | Yes | Unobserved severity, outcome-dependent routing, carryover queues |
| E8 | P0 versus P2/P4 | Flag-eligible cases and comparable unflagged cases | Decision outcome, rework, override, appeal, adverse-error proxy | Total policy effect on decision quality/compliance | Partly; “quality” needs a gold-standard or delayed outcome | Approve/reject is not itself quality; selective observation of errors |
| E9 | P0 versus a policy setting `priority_lane=0` while preserving score visibility and review rule | Flag-eligible cases | Triage completion and total cycle time | Controlled direct component of priority routing, conditional on specified downstream routing rule | More well-defined than “set flag=0” | Priority affects shared triage queue and may alter reviewer perception |
| E10 | P0 versus stochastic review assignment matching the P2 review rate within score/complexity/load strata | Flag-eligible arrival cohort | Cycle time and decision quality | Randomized/interventional indirect-effect analogue through review occurrence | Potentially, if randomization/stochastic assignment is feasible | Requires no unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding after intervention; interference remains |

“Set flag to zero” is not inherently a coherent causal intervention because the flag is derived from the score and embedded in a policy. P1 changes **visibility and downstream use**, while P2-P4 alter distinct organizational actions.

Interference arises because priority triage, manual review, and escalation use shared resources: routing one flagged case can alter waits of other cases. Exposure-induced confounding occurs when triage findings or reviewer judgment are affected by flag/priority and then influence both escalation and final time or outcome. Treatment-confounder feedback appears if earlier treatment changes later workload state, reviewer assignment, evidence quality, or escalation eligibility. Conditioning on “reviewed” or “escalated” can therefore create selection bias. `arrival_load` is only a coarse baseline proxy; it does not replace event-time queue state, active work-in-process, staffing, or the priority composition of the queue.

Observed timestamps strongly support a descriptive pattern: flagged cases begin triage much sooner (0.28 versus 2.37 hours) and have shorter triage service (0.61 versus 1.01 hours), yet have longer total cycles (14.25 versus 5.76 hours). The largest flagged delay is downstream: mean triage-done-to-manual-start is 5.55 hours, and escalated flagged cases average nearly 19 hours total. This is consistent with priority triage followed by review and senior-team queueing. However, the contrast is not automatically causal. Flagged cases have more high complexity, higher scores, much more review and escalation, potentially different reviewer assignment, and may contain unrecorded risk or documentation needs. Shared queues also mean the relevant counterfactual is a system-level policy state, not merely another individual case with `flag=0`.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does flag display accelerate triage near the rule threshold? | Local-threshold/regression-discontinuity analysis around the score cutoff generating the flag; estimate discontinuity in triage wait and triage completion | Risk score running variable, narrow bandwidth, intake time, complexity, arrival-load, calendar/shift indicators | No sorting/manipulation at threshold; other policies change smoothly; score accurately recorded | Test covariate continuity, density bunching, and no discontinuity in pre-flag variables | No triage-time discontinuity despite a large flag discontinuity weakens the priority-lane mechanism |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Fast for flagged; slow for unflagged | High flagged flow into review; escalation bottleneck remains | Observed flagged mean is long despite fast triage | Current safeguards retained, but quality unmeasured | Priority may disadvantage unflagged cases; shared review load | Descriptive only | Maintain only as baseline while measuring queues and quality |
| P1: suppress flag display | Likely removes or weakens priority, unless explicit priority rule remains | May reduce automatic review/escalation if humans cannot infer flag; could also create inconsistent discretionary routing | Ambiguous: flagged cases may lose triage gains but avoid downstream routing | Potential loss of vigilance/compliance; human use of score may partially substitute | Could relieve review queues but increase triage congestion | Weak without a randomized display study | Use only as a carefully monitored display experiment, not as immediate rollout |
| P2: retain priority, remove automatic mandatory manual review | Preserves fast triage for eligible cases | Reduces review demand; may lower escalation demand and waits | Likely shorter for low-evidence flagged cases; uncertain for truly high-risk cases | Main risk: bypassed review may increase errors, rework, or policy violations | Frees review capacity for all cases; may improve unflagged waits | Moderately testable in randomized pilot | Preferred limited pilot with safeguards and quality adjudication |
| P3: retain routing, add protected manual-review capacity | No material triage change | Reduces flagged review waiting if capacity is genuinely additive; may displace unflagged work if reserved | Likely reduces flagged cycle time, especially review wait; total system effect uncertain | Low direct quality risk; review intensity retained | Potentially harmful if capacity is reserved rather than added | Plausible but requires queue-level data | Consider after verifying queue bottleneck and protecting unflagged SLAs |
| P4: retain review, change escalation criteria or capacity | No material triage change | May reduce senior queue wait or escalation rate; can shift burden back to review | Potentially large benefit for escalated cases; average benefit depends on escalation prevalence | Under-escalation can create serious safety/compliance loss | Added senior capacity may improve all escalated cases; stricter criteria may shift work downstream | Moderate if quality endpoint exists | Pilot only with independent review/audit and explicit safety stopping rules |

**Selected limited pilot: P2, priority retained with randomized mandatory-review override.**

- **Eligibility:** newly flagged cases in a prespecified medium-score band, medium complexity, and no intake hard-stop/compliance criterion. Exclude legally mandated review, clearly high-risk cases, and cases requiring specialist review by rule.
- **Assignment rule:** individual randomization within 4-hour operational blocks and queue-state strata to current mandatory review versus discretionary risk-based review; triage priority remains unchanged. Log all reviewer overrides.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** pause enrollment if manual-review work-in-process exceeds a prespecified threshold, if unflagged review 90th-percentile wait rises by more than 15%, or if senior-team backlog exceeds threshold.
- **Primary outcome:** quality-adjusted total cycle time, with noninferiority on blinded audit correctness/compliance.
- **Secondary outcomes:** review occurrence, manual wait/service, escalation, rework, appeals, triage time, unflagged waits, and reviewer overrides.
- **Minimum process data:** all stage starts/completions, queue state/WIP, staffing, resource, score, complexity, intake facts, review rationale, escalation rationale, and delayed quality outcomes.
- **Stopping conditions:** predefined excess in audited error, compliance exceptions, adverse outcomes, rework/appeals, or material unflagged queue harm.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** A blinded audit finds that, within score and recorded complexity strata, flagged cases contain substantially greater unrecorded legal, fraud, safety, or documentation risk, and that risk explains both review/escalation and long processing.

2. **Queue interference:** Protected review capacity reduces flagged waits but materially increases unflagged review waits, missed service-level targets, or aggregate completion time because capacity was reallocated rather than added.

3. **Decision quality:** Removing mandatory review produces a meaningful increase in audited incorrect approvals/rejections, compliance violations, later losses, appeals, or rework that outweighs cycle-time savings.

4. **Timestamp semantics:** Manual-start or escalation-start timestamps reflect batch release, overnight holding, document readiness, or administrative posting rather than actual queue entry/service start, invalidating the interpretation of the intervals as queue waits.

5. **Positivity/lack of overlap:** No comparable unflagged cases exist for high-score/high-complexity flagged cases, or all eligible flagged cases are reviewed, making adjusted observational comparisons reliant on extrapolation.

6. **Reviewer behavior change:** Under P2, reviewers compensate by escalating far more discretionary cases, delaying documentation, or selectively reviewing only difficult cases, eliminating expected queue relief and changing the meaning of observed review groups.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed, flagged cases reach triage rapidly: their mean arrival-to-triage-start time is about 0.28 hours versus 2.37 hours for unflagged cases. They also have shorter observed triage service. Yet flagged cases have much longer total completion times, about 14.25 versus 5.76 hours. The descriptive delay is downstream: virtually all flagged cases undergo manual review, their mean wait from triage completion to review start exceeds five hours, and escalated flagged cases average nearly 19 hours total.

A plausible mechanism is therefore a policy bundle: flag display triggers priority triage, automatic review, and frequently escalation into scarce shared queues. Priority accelerates the first stage while review and senior-team routing add waiting and service. This can coexist with longer total time without implying that priority itself is harmful.

What remains unidentified is whether the flag-driven policy, rather than underlying unmeasured risk, complexity, reviewer assignment, or time-varying congestion, causes the downstream delay. Natural direct/indirect decompositions are particularly fragile because review and escalation are post-treatment, selectively assigned, and occur in shared queues.

The most useful next evidence comes from a guarded P2 pilot: preserve priority triage but randomize mandatory versus discretionary review for a safe, medium-risk eligible band. Measure full queue state, routing rationales, resource assignment, later quality outcomes, and spillovers to unflagged cases. That pilot can determine whether reduced review demand shortens cycle time without sacrificing decision quality or merely shifts delay elsewhere.
