# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The apparent contradiction dissolves once we recognize that each of these networks is a *different projection* of the same event sequence, and that they measure different things: adjacency in time, co-membership in a case, and temporary delegation. A resource can be maximal on one and minimal on another without any inconsistency. Mira_Coordinator is a textbook example of a **short-contact, high-fan-out router**: she is present at many transition points but resident in few cases.

## Handover-of-work network

**What it measures.** `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` counts directed pairs (A, B) where B executes an event *after* A within the same case (directly-follows by default; `beta`/`n` parameters allow indirect variants). It is a *sequence-adjacency* statistic, not an authority statistic. Frequency is normalized per case by default, so a resource who appears once in many cases can accumulate large out-degree cheaply.

**What it implies about Mira.** Out-degree centrality 0.82 and betweenness 0.77 are the highest in the organization by a wide margin. Structurally, she is an *articulation point*: work reaching her is redistributed to many distinct downstream resources (Omar, Priya, Carla, Diego, Elise, Farid, and back into Bot_Rules). In-degree 0.51 shows the flow is genuinely bidirectional — specialists and temporaries return work to her (Omar→Mira 160, Priya→Mira 140, Temp_1→Mira 190, Temp_2→Mira 170). She is therefore a *hub-and-spoke convergence and divergence point* on the exception path.

**What it does not imply.** (a) It does not imply hierarchical authority. Handover edges are symmetric in kind whether the "handover" is a delegation, an escalation, a reassignment, or a pure clerical pass-through. (b) It does not imply effort or workload: her median service time is 4.2 minutes, among the lowest of the human resources. (c) It does not imply *causal* control of routing — the edge Bot_Rules→Mira (920) shows that the rules engine, not Mira, selects who enters her queue. She controls the *outbound* choice, not the inbound one. (d) High betweenness in a handover graph is partly an artifact of *variety of successors*, and Mira's successor set is large precisely because exception cases are heterogeneous. (e) The metric is unweighted by business value; her edges may be short administrative steps.

**Caveat.** Handover on a directly-follows basis is sensitive to timestamp granularity and to lifecycle transitions. If "Assign Specialist" or "Reopen Claim" events are logged with coarse timestamps or as instantaneous `complete`-only events, spurious adjacency can inflate her degree. This must be checked before treating 0.82 as a real number.

## Working-together network

**What it measures.** `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` counts *co-occurrence in the same case*, irrespective of order or adjacency, normalized by case counts. It is a *co-membership* statistic — a measure of shared case ownership over time, not of interaction.

**What it implies about Mira.** Weighted degree 0.16 versus 0.46–0.49 for the team leads and senior adjusters. Her pairwise co-case rates (5–8%) are low. Read literally, she shares few cases with anyone.

**Why this is not a contradiction.** The metric is dominated by two factors she structurally lacks: (i) *case residency* — Carla and Alice stay attached to a case across assignment, assessment, negotiation, approval, and payment release, so every pair among the residents scores highly; and (ii) *case volume base* — she touches 22% of claims (905 distinct cases against ~4100), so her denominator-normalized share is inherently capped near 0.22 for any single partner. Given that ceiling, a 8% co-case rate with Carla means roughly a third of Mira's own cases involve Carla — which is actually *high* conditional on her case set. **The peripherality is an artifact of unconditional normalization.** The correct comparison is `P(partner | Mira touched the case)`, not `P(both | all cases)`.

The second factor is duration of involvement. Working-together weights every co-present resource equally regardless of how many events they contribute. Mira contributes 960 events across 905 cases — approximately **1.06 events per case**. She enters, routes, and leaves. This matches the stated observation that she "disappears from the same case for several events." A one-touch router is by construction peripheral in a co-membership graph and central in an adjacency graph. There is no paradox: *centrality in flow ≠ centrality in ownership.*

**What it does not imply.** It does not imply she is unimportant, socially isolated, or replaceable. It also does not imply she is *not* a team member — working-together says nothing about advisory, verbal, or off-system coordination, which for a "person who knows where hard cases should go" is likely a large part of the real work and is invisible to the log.

## Subcontracting network

**What it measures.** `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with n=2 counts patterns where A performs an event, B performs events in between, and **A returns** within n events. It is designed to capture delegation-with-return-of-control: A retains ownership, B does a bounded piece of work.

**What it implies about Mira.** Subcontracting betweenness 0.69, far above everyone else (next: Omar 0.24). The two dominant patterns are Mira→Omar→Mira (126) and Mira→Priya→Mira (98). Semantically this is a **request–response delegation to specialists with control returning to her**. Combined with the fragments (`Request Missing Information → Medical Review → Assign Specialist/Reopen`, `Escalate Complaint → Legal Review → Reopen/Assign Specialist`), the strong reading is that she *convenes specialist input and then decides the next disposition*. That is a genuine coordination function, and it is the single most substantive evidence that she does more than clerical queue-clearing.

**What it does not imply.** (a) Subcontracting patterns are *positional*, not intentional: the same footprint arises if a workflow system automatically returns the case to the originating queue after specialist completion. An A→B→A pattern proves return-of-token, not exercise of judgment. (b) With n=2 the pattern is fragile — it will miss delegations where the specialist logs two events, and it will over-count if the same person happens to appear twice for unrelated reasons. Sensitivity analysis across n∈{1,2,3,5} is mandatory. (c) The Bot_Rules→Mira→Bot_Rules pattern (62, `Fraud Screen → manual exception → Fraud Screen update`) is a case where the "subcontractor" is a machine, showing that the metric happily conflates human delegation with human-in-the-loop override of automation. (d) It does not tell us whether her choice of specialist was correct or whether an alternative routing would have been better.

## Performance / time statistics

**What they imply.** Three distinct signals must be separated.

1. **Her own service time is small.** 4.2 min median, ~960 events ≈ 67 hours of touch time over 12 weeks. She is nowhere near capacity-saturated on logged work. She is therefore *not* a queueing bottleneck in the classical utilization sense.
2. **The cycle-time load sits elsewhere.** Priya (51 min/event, "very high" contribution), Omar (37 min), the adjusters (17–24 min), and above all the vendors: Vendor_Verify at 2.1 h median service and Vendor_Pay at 1.3 h, both flagged "high waiting-time component" and both with very high case percentiles (0.85, 0.93). **Any discussion of cycle time that focuses on Mira and ignores the vendors is misdirected.** Vendor_Verify touches ~1500 cases at 2.1 hours; that is a far larger aggregate time sink than Mira's entire footprint. Similarly Bot_Rules touches 4090 cases — the automation, not Mira, is the true structural gatekeeper of routing.
3. **The absence experiment is the most informative datum.** During her 6-day absence, median cycle time for ordinary low-value claims was unchanged, but exception cases queued and P90 rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days (+65%). This is a **natural experiment with a clean differential**: it identifies her as load-bearing *specifically on the exception path* and non-load-bearing on the mainstream path. It is close to a segmented interrupted-time-series result.

**What they do not imply.** The absence is n=1, 6 days, uncontrolled. Confounders are unaddressed: was that period a volume peak, a holiday, coincident with specialist absence, or a month-end payment cycle? Was the P90 rise driven by a handful of extreme cases? Was work merely *deferred* (a queue that drained afterwards) rather than lost? Was there a substitute who absorbed part of the load? Before treating +65% P90 as her causal effect, one needs a matched control period, a per-case-type decomposition, and an arrival-rate check. My calibrated reading: the direction of the effect is credible and consistent with everything else in the data; the magnitude is not yet trustworthy.

Also note: "high variance" cycle-time contribution for Mira is exactly what a router with heterogeneous downstream paths produces, and is not by itself evidence of erratic behavior.

## Role-discovery / activity-profile similarity

**What they measure.** `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` builds a resource×activity profile and computes pairwise similarity; `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` clusters resources into roles by shared activity execution. These are *behavioral* definitions of role — what you do, not who you report to.

**What they imply about Mira.** Her activity set is {Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, Assign Specialist}. This set is essentially **disjoint from the value-adding chain**: no Assess Damage, no Negotiate Settlement, no Approve Settlement, no Validate Coverage, no Close Claim. Role discovery will therefore almost certainly place her in a **singleton or near-singleton cluster**. Overlap with Temp_1/Temp_2 and Elise/Farid exists only via Request Missing Information; overlap with Alice/Bruno is nil (she never approves).

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions:

- She is a **structurally unique role**, not a redundant one. There is no natural understudy — this is the formal signature of key-person risk.
- She has **no decision authority in the activity sense**. She never approves a settlement, never issues payment, never closes a claim. Whatever discretion she exercises is *routing* discretion, not *outcome* discretion. This materially bounds the compliance concern.

Note the contrast with Alice and Bruno, who share {Triage, Approve Settlement} and are mutually substitutable — the org has redundancy at team-lead level and none at coordinator level.

**What they do not imply.** Activity-label similarity is only as good as the labels. "Assign Specialist" appears in her activity list in the resource table but is absent from the 14-activity list given in the scenario. That inconsistency is itself a finding (see §3, data-quality risk): either the activity list is incomplete, or "Assign Specialist" is a sub-label, or these events are synthesized/derived. Role discovery on inconsistent labels will produce artifacts.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

I regard H1, H3 and H5 as jointly likely and largely complementary — the honest reading is that they are *facets of the same position*, not rivals. H2 is a real risk that is partially realized. H4 and H6 are lower-probability but cheap to test and would change the interpretation substantially if true.

---

## H1 — Formal coordinator / exception desk (working as designed)

**Mechanism.** The process design routes exception-flagged cases from Bot_Rules to a dedicated desk. The desk's job is to diagnose the exception, obtain the missing input (information request, specialist opinion), and dispatch the case to the correct downstream owner. Ownership never transfers to the desk; the desk is a *switch*, and the switch is deliberately staffed by one person to keep exception handling consistent.

**Supporting evidence.** Bot_Rules→Mira is the single largest human-facing routing edge (920) and is *machine-initiated* — she does not self-select her inbound work, which is the hallmark of a designed queue rather than an emergent one. Exception rate 0.48, and 64%/71% of reopens/complaints. Activity set is purely dispatch-and-clarify. ~1.06 events per case — designed for short touches. Return edges from Omar/Priya/Temp_1/Temp_2 close the loop as an exception desk should. Alice and Bruno retain approval authority, so the control structure is intact.

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** A pure formal desk would not usually be described by adjusters as "the person who knows where hard cases should go" — that phrasing describes tacit expertise, not a documented rule. If routing were fully specified, Bot_Rules could do it. The process owner's dismissive "queue cleaner" framing suggests the role is *not* well documented or well recognized, which is unusual for a formally designed control point. And the 6-day absence would matter less if written routing rules existed for others to follow.

**Predictions.** (i) Her routing decisions are highly predictable from `case_type`, `exception_flag`, `amount_band` and the preceding activity — a decision-tree or logistic model on (case attributes → chosen specialist) should reach high accuracy with low residual entropy. (ii) Time-from-arrival-to-her-event is tightly distributed (queue discipline, low variance). (iii) Her routing distribution is stable across weeks and across region/channel. (iv) Documentation/SOP exists naming the desk. (v) Conditional co-case rate `P(Carla | Mira)` is high while unconditional stays low.

---

## H2 — Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

**Mechanism.** All exception traffic is serialized through a single unbacked node. Capacity is fine in steady state (67 h of touch time), so the dependency is invisible in utilization metrics — but the node is *non-substitutable*, so any unavailability converts directly into queue accumulation on the exception path. The bottleneck is in **knowledge and authority-to-dispatch**, not in throughput.

**Supporting evidence.** The absence experiment is direct and differential: exception P90 8.4 → 13.9 days while median low-value cycle time was flat. Handover betweenness 0.77 and subcontracting betweenness 0.69 mean she lies on most exception paths — removing the node disconnects them. Role discovery puts her in a singleton cluster: no behavioral backup exists. Nobody else executes Escalate Complaint or Assign Specialist. Both team leads are mutually redundant; she is not.

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** Low service time and modest event count argue against a *capacity* bottleneck; the queues formed because nothing was pulling, not because she was slow. Ordinary claims were entirely unaffected — the dependency is scoped to ~22% of volume. Some of the P90 rise may be deferral that drained on her return rather than permanent loss. n=1 absence, uncontrolled for arrival rate and specialist availability. Also, "high variance" contribution may reflect case heterogeneity rather than her.

**Predictions.** (i) Exception-path cycle time correlates with her presence/shift pattern (test with a per-day availability regressor). (ii) Waiting time *before* her events grows super-linearly with daily exception arrivals — a queueing signature. (iii) Her queue exhibits burstiness and end-of-day batching. (iv) If a second coordinator is trained, exception P90 falls and her betweenness drops without throughput loss. (v) Post-absence, a catch-up spike in her event rate.

---

## H3 — Boundary spanner / triage broker

**Mechanism.** In network terms she is a classic **structural hole broker** (Burt): she connects otherwise weakly-connected clusters — Team North, Team South, medical, legal, temporaries, vendors, and the automation. Value is created by *knowing the map*, not by doing the work. Brokerage explains the exact centrality pattern: high betweenness, low cohesion, short ties, many distinct partners.

**Supporting evidence.** This hypothesis alone cleanly explains the paradox. Out-degree 0.82 with weighted working-together degree 0.16 is the canonical broker signature. She spans clusters that otherwise barely touch: Alice—Bruno teams have no direct edge in the tables; Omar—Priya co-occur on only 4% of cases; the temporaries escalate to her rather than to team leads (Temp_1→Mira 190, Temp_2→Mira 170 — note there are *no* Temp→TeamLead edges in the top list). She routes into both teams roughly symmetrically (Carla 350 / Diego 330 / Elise 290 / Farid 280) whereas Alice→Carla (320) and Bruno→Diego (310) are strictly within-team. Adjusters' testimony ("knows where hard cases should go") is exactly how brokers are described. Operations manager's prediction — removal raises formal escalations and specialist overload — is the standard consequence of losing a broker: traffic reroutes into slower, more formal channels.

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** Brokerage is inferred from topology and could equally be produced by a mandated routing rule (then it is H1, not tacit expertise). Bot_Rules also spans clusters (betweenness 0.41) and is plainly mechanical — proving that this topology does not require human judgment. Brokerage that is *not* documented is fragile and is precisely what the compliance reviewer objects to. Also, brokers sometimes preserve structural holes for their own indispensability; there is no evidence of that here, but it is not excluded.

**Predictions.** (i) Her successor set has high entropy and is only weakly predictable from case attributes — the opposite of H1's prediction. This is the **cleanest discriminating test between H1 and H3**: run the same routing model and read the residual entropy. Low entropy → rule-following desk; high entropy with good outcomes → expert brokerage. (ii) Cases she routes reach the *correct* specialist first time more often than cases routed by others (fewer specialist-to-specialist re-routes). (iii) Removing her raises cross-cluster path length and raises the count of Escalate Complaint events. (iv) Her ties are short-lived and non-repeating on the same case.

---

## H4 — Data artifact / logging convention

**Mechanism.** No behavioral claim at all. A workflow system writes a queue-transition event stamped with the exception-desk account whenever a case changes queue. Every queue change then manufactures two handover edges (in and out) and an A→B→A subcontracting pattern, without any human act.

**Supporting evidence.** Her events are described as "short routing, assignment, reopen, clarification events" — the vocabulary of system state transitions. 4.2-minute median with ~1.06 events per case is consistent with automated or semi-automated stamping. "Assign Specialist" does not appear in the process's own 14-activity list, which is a genuine red flag about how these events originate. Bot_Rules→Mira→Bot_Rules (62) shows her account interleaved with machine events. Betweenness of this kind is a well-known artifact of queue-management logging.

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** A pure logging artifact cannot explain the absence experiment — a system account does not take 6 days off, and exception queues would not have accumulated. Her exception concentration (48%; 64% of reopens; 71% of complaints) is behaviorally selective in a way a uniform stamping convention would not be. Human testimony from multiple adjusters describes deliberate judgment. Verdict: **likely a partial contaminant, not the explanation** — some fraction of her 960 events are probably system-generated, which would *deflate* her true per-decision importance while her real coordination role remains.

**Predictions.** (i) Timestamps cluster on exact-second or exact-minute boundaries, or show implausibly short inter-event gaps. (ii) Lifecycle transitions are `complete`-only with no `start`. (iii) Her event rate is flat across nights, weekends, and her documented absence — a decisive test. (iv) Events correlate 1:1 with queue-change records in the source system.

---

## H5 — Quality gatekeeper / rework absorber

**Mechanism.** She is where the process sends work it has already got wrong. Her exception share (0.48) is the highest of any human except the specialists, and she owns Reopen Claim and Escalate Complaint — both *failure-recovery* activities. On this reading she is not on the value chain at all; she is the repair channel, and her centrality is a **symptom of upstream defect rate**, not of organizational design.

**Supporting evidence.** 64% of reopens, 71% of complaints. Bot_Rules carries a 0.31 exception rate on 4090 cases — the automated triage is generating a large volume of exceptions that must land somewhere. Temp_1/Temp_2 escalate to her (360 combined) rather than resolving in place; both have very low exception rates (0.14/0.13) *and* low case percentiles, consistent with them pushing anything non-routine upward. Temp_1→Vendor_Verify→Request Missing Information (68) is a visible rework loop. Mira→Elise/Farid (290/280) is explicitly labelled "rework/missing info".

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** She also handles first-time specialist convening (medical/legal), which is legitimate complexity, not rework. Some exception traffic is inherent to regulated claims (fraud, medical, legal) and would exist under any design. Attributing her volume to upstream defects requires showing the exceptions were *avoidable*.

**Predictions.** (i) Improving Bot_Rules precision or Temp training reduces her inbound volume proportionally. (ii) Cases she touches show elevated prior-activity repetition. (iii) Her inbound volume correlates week-over-week with Bot_Rules' exception rate and with temp staffing levels. (iv) Root-cause clustering of her inbound cases yields a small number of recurring upstream defects.

---

## H6 — Specialist scheduler / capacity manager

**Mechanism.** Omar and Priya are single points of specialist capacity (330 and 260 events; 37 and 51 min each). Someone must ration them. Mira's real function may be *demand management for scarce specialists* — deciding which cases deserve 51 minutes of legal time.

**Supporting evidence.** Mira→Omar (210) and Mira→Priya (180) are essentially the *only* routes into the specialists in the data. Subcontracting with return (126, 98) is a request-response scheduling pattern. Mean elapsed times Mira→Omar 42 min and Mira→Priya 51 min are far longer than her other outbound edges (16–19 min) — consistent with queueing for a scarce resource. The ops manager's specific worry about "specialist overload" on her removal is exactly this. Omar/Priya exception rates (0.62/0.71) show she is filtering hard cases toward them rather than dumping volume.

**Contradicting / limiting evidence.** Only ~390 of her 960 events go to specialists; most of her work is adjuster reassignment and info requests. Scheduling could equally be pull-based (specialists picking from a queue) with her events merely marking the request. Elapsed times may reflect specialist queue depth rather than her deliberation.

**Predictions.** (i) Her specialist referral rate is anti-correlated with specialist backlog — she throttles when they are loaded. (ii) Referred cases have systematically higher amount_band / higher downstream settlement values than non-referred exception cases. (iii) During her absence, specialist idle time rises *and* specialist queue variance rises simultaneously. (iv) Referral acceptance rate is high (few specialist→bounce-back events).

# 3. Risk assessment

I state these as risks with an evidential grade, not as findings. Nothing in this data establishes misconduct by anyone.

**Bottleneck risk — moderate, and mis-specified in the usual sense.** She is *not* a capacity bottleneck: ~67 hours of touch time over 12 weeks, 4.2-min median service, low relative to the adjusters and far below the vendors. She is a **structural/availability bottleneck on the exception path only**: betweenness 0.77 means exception flow is topologically serialized through her, and the absence data show what happens when the node is down (exception P90 +65%, ordinary claims unaffected). Two important qualifications. First, the scoped nature of this (22% of claims) bounds the exposure. Second, and more important for management priorities: **the largest cycle-time components in this process are Vendor_Verify (2.1 h service, ~1500 cases) and Vendor_Pay (1.3 h, ~2840 cases)**, both explicitly flagged as high waiting-time contributors, plus the specialists' long service times. An improvement program that starts with Mira and not with vendor turnaround is optimizing the wrong node. Bot_Rules' 0.31 exception rate on 4090 cases is likewise a bigger lever on her workload than anything she does herself.

**Coordination risk — moderate to high, and it is the risk most likely to be realized.** The coordination logic appears to be *undocumented and person-resident*. The signals: singleton role cluster in activity space; no second executor of Escalate Complaint or Assign Specialist; conflicting stakeholder accounts of what she even does; temporaries escalating to her rather than through their team leads. The organization has redundancy where it matters least (Alice/Bruno are interchangeable) and none where it matters most. Note also the operations manager's point, which I think is correct in direction: informal coordination that is removed does not vanish, it *reappears as formal escalations* — usually slower, more expensive, and more visible to regulators.

**Key-person risk — high, and this is the best-evidenced risk.** Non-substitutability is established by two independent lines of evidence: behavioral (unique activity profile, no overlap with any other resource on her core activities) and empirical (the absence experiment produced a large, direction-consistent, path-specific degradation). It is the *combination* that makes it credible; either alone would be weak. Calibration: the +65% P90 figure itself should be treated as indicative only (n=1, unmatched period, no arrival-rate control), but the existence of the dependency is well supported.

**Compliance / control risk — real as a control-design gap; unsupported as a conduct concern.** The reviewer's concern is legitimate *in form*: the same person detects the exception and selects the resolver, which is a segregation-of-duties weakness and would be flagged by most control frameworks. In a regulated claims process, undocumented routing discretion over which cases get medical or legal review is a defensible audit finding on its own.

Three counterweights must be stated for calibration. (1) **Her discretion is bounded.** She executes no Approve Settlement, no Issue Payment, no Close Claim — the money decisions remain with Alice/Bruno and Vendor_Pay. She cannot approve what she routes. (2) **She does not select her own inbound work.** Bot_Rules routes to her (920 edges); she cannot cherry-pick cases into her queue, which removes the most dangerous form of selection discretion. (3) **There is no evidence of adverse outcomes.** Nothing in the data links her routing to biased, incorrect, or self-serving results. Before any conduct-flavored conclusion, one would need outcome analysis: do her routings correlate with region, channel, amount_band or claimant characteristics in ways unexplained by case merits, and are outcomes worse or systematically different? **Absence of documented rules is a control finding; it is not evidence of misuse of discretion, and the two must not be conflated.** The presence of a compliance concern in a well-run regulated business is normal and is an argument for documentation, not for suspicion of a person.

**Workload fairness risk — low-moderate, and partly the inverse of what is assumed.** By logged effort she is not overloaded (4.2 min × 960 ≈ 67 h). But logged effort is a poor proxy for a router's real load: decision density, interruption rate, and the cognitive cost of context-switching across 6+ downstream partners and 4 activity types are invisible here. Her role is also *structurally low-credit*: she does no work that appears in throughput or settlement statistics, and she absorbs the 48%-exception, 71%-complaint traffic. That combination — high interruption, low measured output, negative-valence caseload — is a well-known burnout and attrition profile, and it compounds the key-person risk. There is a second fairness dimension: Elise/Farid receive her rework routing (290/280) on top of their own assessment work while carrying lower case percentiles and lower seniority; and the temporaries (case percentile ~0.5, exception rate ~0.13) appear to be shielded from difficulty in a way that may be appropriate or may indicate under-utilization. Also worth noting: the specialists Omar and Priya carry the highest exception exposure (0.62, 0.71) and the longest service times — they, not Mira, are the most load-stressed humans in the log.

**Data-quality risk — moderate, and it conditions every number above.** Concrete concerns, in descending order of importance:
- **"Assign Specialist" appears in her activity profile but not in the scenario's 14-activity list.** Either the activity taxonomy is incomplete or these events are derived/system-generated. This directly affects her degree and betweenness.
- **Short-event contamination.** If routing/queue-transition events are system-stamped under her account, her centrality is inflated relative to her actual decisions (H4).
- **Handover semantics.** Directly-follows handover conflates delegation, escalation, reassignment, and mere temporal adjacency. It also mis-handles parallelism: if Fraud Screen runs concurrently with Assess Damage, adjacency edges are spurious. `beta`/`n` sensitivity is untested.
- **Lifecycle and timestamps.** If only `complete` is recorded, `get_service_time` is unreliable and the 4.2-min median may be a tie-broken artifact; sub-second bot events (4 s, 7 s) suggest mixed timestamp granularity across systems.
- **Vendor and bot account aggregation.** Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay are almost certainly *organizations*, not individuals; treating them as single nodes understates their internal parallelism and distorts every centrality measure in the graph. The same applies to Bot_Rules.
- **Resource identity.** Shared logins, delegated accounts, or a substitute covering her absence would corrupt the natural experiment.
- **Normalization.** Working-together peripherality is partly a denominator effect (§1); conclusions drawn from the raw 0.16 without conditioning are unsound.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

Preparation for all of the below: `log = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id='case:concept:name', activity_key='concept:name', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')`, and resolve the "Assign Specialist" taxonomy question before anything else.

**A. Handover-of-work analyses.**
1. *Conditional and segmented handover.* Compute `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` separately on `exception_flag=True` and `False` sublogs (via `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values`), and on `case_type` / `amount_band` / `region` strata. Expected: her centrality collapses on the False sublog and rises on True — confirming a scoped rather than global role.
2. *Parameter sensitivity.* Recompute with `beta` > 0 (indirect succession with decay) and with/without `resource_key` normalization. A role that survives only at `beta=0` is an adjacency artifact.
3. *Routing entropy.* From her outbound edge distribution compute Shannon entropy of successor choice, overall and conditional on case attributes. Then fit a classifier (`sklearn` decision tree / logistic regression) predicting her chosen successor from `case_type, priority, amount_band, region, channel, exception_flag`, and preceding activity. **This is the H1-vs-H3 discriminator**: high accuracy ⇒ codifiable rules ⇒ automate into Bot_Rules; low accuracy with good outcomes ⇒ genuine tacit expertise ⇒ document and train, do not automate.
4. *Node-removal simulation.* Build the handover graph in `networkx`, delete her node, and measure the change in average shortest-path length, number of connected components, and cross-team reachability. Quantifies the structural-hole claim.
5. *Do not stop at Mira.* Run the same centrality profile for Bot_Rules (betweenness 0.41) and the vendors — the automation is a comparably central router and deserves equal scrutiny.

**B. Working-together analyses.**
6. *Conditional co-occurrence.* Recompute `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` restricted to her 905 cases and report `P(partner | Mira present)`. Prediction: 30–40% for Carla, i.e. she is *core* within her own case population. This is the single analysis that most directly resolves the stated paradox.
7. *Community detection.* Apply Louvain/label-propagation to the working-together graph. Expected: clean Team North / Team South communities with Mira as a low-weight bridge; compare her participation coefficient and Burt's constraint against everyone else. Low constraint = broker.
8. *Duration-weighted variant.* Reweight co-occurrence by number of events (or time) each resource contributes to the case, not binary presence. This corrects the one-touch penalty and shows how much of her peripherality is metric artifact.
9. *Temporal stability.* Slice the 12 weeks into weekly windows and track edge stability — a designed desk should be stable; an emergent broker may drift.

**C. Subcontracting analyses.**
10. *n-sensitivity.* Run `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` for n ∈ {1,2,3,5} and check whether Mira→Omar→Mira and Mira→Priya→Mira persist and grow monotonically. Robustness across n distinguishes real delegation from a coincidental n=2 window.
11. *Fragment-level inspection.* Extract and manually review the 126 medical and 98 legal fragments: what is the actual activity triple, does she *change* the disposition after the specialist answers (evidence of judgment), or merely acknowledge and pass through (evidence of clerical return-of-token)? This is the most direct test of "coordinator vs. queue cleaner."
12. *Bot subcontracting.* Analyze Bot_Rules→Mira→Bot_Rules (62) separately: human-in-the-loop override of automated fraud screening is a distinct, compliance-relevant function that should be measured on its own — including how often her override changes the Fraud Screen outcome.

**D. Role discovery / activity similarity.**
13. `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` on the full log and on the exception sublog. Confirm the singleton-cluster prediction and, critically, identify the *nearest* resource in role space — that person is the cheapest cross-training candidate.
14. `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` to produce a similarity matrix; cluster hierarchically and inspect where Mira attaches. Compare her similarity to Alice/Bruno (expected ≈ 0) versus to Temp_1 (shared Request Missing Information).
15. *Substitutability index.* For each activity, count distinct executors and compute a Herfindahl concentration. Expected: Escalate Complaint and Assign Specialist show concentration ≈ 1 (single executor) — a quantified, auditable key-person metric that generalizes beyond this one person. Run it for *every* activity; Medical Review and Legal Review will likely score just as badly (Omar and Priya are also single points of failure, and this deserves equal management attention).

**E. Filtering / variant analyses.**
16. *Variant comparison.* Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to split variants containing Mira from matched variants that do not (matched on case_type, amount_band, priority), then compare cycle time, rework count, reopen rate, and settlement outcome. This estimates her *value added*, not just her centrality.
17. *Absence natural experiment, done properly.* Filter to her 6-day absence window plus matched control windows (same weekday composition, comparable arrival rate). Run a segmented/difference-in-differences comparison with ordinary claims as the control group, and check whether the post-absence period shows a catch-up spike (deferral) or a permanent loss. Decompose the P90 rise by case_type and check whether it is driven by a few extreme cases. Also verify no substitute account absorbed her work.
18. *Concept-drift checks.* `pm4py` drift utilities or simple weekly time-series on her inbound volume, Bot_Rules exception rate, and temp staffing — tests H5 (is her load a function of upstream defect rate?).
19. *Fairness/consistency audit.* Condition her specialist-referral rate on `region`, `channel`, `priority` and `amount_band` while controlling for case merits. Framed as a control-effectiveness check, not an accusation; a null result is a useful and publishable finding that closes the compliance concern.

**F. Service and waiting-time analyses.**
20. `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to decompose exception-case cycle time into service vs. waiting. Expected finding: her *service* share is negligible while *waiting before her events* is material — the correct diagnosis is queue-and-availability, not slowness, and it points to shift coverage rather than to headcount or to her performance.
21. *Full waiting-time attribution.* Do the same for Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay. Given 2.1 h and 1.3 h median service across ~1500 and ~2840 cases, I expect the vendors to dominate total waiting time. **Publish this alongside the Mira analysis so management sees the true ranking of levers.**
22. *Queueing analysis.* Model her inbound as an arrival process: measure inter-arrival distribution, queue length by hour/day, and test for super-linear waiting growth with load. Distinguishes "structurally serialized but idle" from "genuinely saturated at peaks."
23. *Specialist utilization.* Track Omar/Priya utilization and backlog against her referral timing to test H6's throttling prediction.

# 5. Recommendation

## Change immediately (low-regret, no dependence on unresolved questions)

1. **Document the routing logic.** Have her write down, over 2–3 weeks, the decision rules she actually applies for exception dispatch — which case signatures go to medical, legal, senior adjuster, or back to a junior. This is the single highest-value action: it converts person-resident knowledge into an organizational asset, satisfies the auditability concern, and is a prerequisite for everything else. Cost is low; it is valuable under *every* hypothesis, including H4.
2. **Make routing decisions traceable.** Require a short structured reason code on Assign Specialist / Escalate Complaint / Reopen Claim events. This directly addresses the compliance reviewer's segregation-of-duties concern *without changing anyone's role* — discretion exercised transparently is a controlled process, not an uncontrolled one — and it makes analysis A3 and E19 possible.
3. **Fix the data.** Reconcile "Assign Specialist" with the activity taxonomy; separate system-generated from human events under her account; verify lifecycle completeness. Several conclusions above are conditional on this and it should not wait.
4. **Name a designated backup and start shadowing.** Do not wait for further evidence: key-person risk is the best-evidenced risk, and the mitigation is cheap and reversible. Use analysis D13 to pick the nearest role-space candidate; the exception desk should never again be uncovered. Extend the same reasoning to Omar and Priya, who are equally single-threaded (D15) — arguably a larger exposure than Mira, since their service times are 8–12× hers.
5. **Publish the honest cycle-time ranking.** Circulate the waiting-time decomposition (F20/F21) so that the vendor turnaround times — which are almost certainly the dominant lever — get attention proportional to their impact rather than being crowded out by the debate about one coordinator.

## Monitor (instrument now, decide later)

- Exception-path P90 and P99 cycle time, segmented from ordinary claims, with her availability as an explicit covariate — turn the accidental natural experiment into a standing metric.
- Her handover/subcontracting betweenness and the substitutability index (D15) tracked weekly; treat rising concentration as a leading indicator of key-person risk across the whole org, not just for her.
- Routing entropy and the predictive accuracy of the routing model (A3). Rising predictability is the trigger for partial automation into Bot_Rules.
- Bot_Rules exception rate (0.31) and Temp escalation rates — if H5 holds, these are upstream levers that reduce her load without touching her role.
- Specialist queue depth, utilization and idle-time variance (H6).
- Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay turnaround against SLA.
- Her own workload health: interruption rate, event dispersion across the day, and after-hours activity — the burnout profile is real even though logged effort looks light.
- Routing-outcome consistency across region/channel/amount_band (E19), reported as control assurance.

## Do not change until more evidence is available

- **Do not remove, reassign, or "flatten" the exception desk.** The absence experiment is the only causal evidence available and it points the other way: exception queues accumulate without her. The operations manager's prediction — more formal escalations, specialist overload — is the standard consequence of eliminating a broker, and I find it credible. *If* management nonetheless decides to remove the role, the coordination function must be explicitly replaced first, and I would want all four of: (i) the documented routing rules from item 1 encoded into Bot_Rules for the high-confidence subset only; (ii) two named and trained coordinators covering the residual judgment cases, with rostered coverage; (iii) an explicit owner for specialist demand management, since rationing Omar's and Priya's scarce hours would otherwise fall to whoever shouts loudest; and (iv) a monitored transition period with exception P90 as the rollback trigger. Removal without all four converts a measured, contained dependency into an unmeasured, distributed one — which is worse, not better.
- **Do not automate the routing yet.** Automate only after A3 shows which fraction of decisions is genuinely rule-based, and then only that fraction. Bot_Rules already produces a 0.31 exception rate; extending it blindly risks amplifying the very problem she absorbs.
- **Do not act on the compliance concern as if it were a conduct finding.** Items 1–2 address the control gap. An outcome-consistency analysis (E19) should be run before any conclusion about discretion is drawn, and a null result should be reported as clearing the concern.
- **Do not treat her low working-together score as evidence of low contribution.** It is largely a normalization artifact (B6/B8); re-run those before anyone cites the 0.16 in a performance context.
- **Do not restructure the teams.** Nothing in the evidence supports changes to Team North/South or to the temp staffing model; that would be acting far ahead of the data.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator looks contradictory in the data only because our three network views measure different things. Handover and subcontracting measure *flow* — who work passes between — and on those she is the most central person in the process. Working-together measures *shared ownership*, and she scores low simply because she touches roughly one event per case and then moves on. A router is central in flow and peripheral in ownership; both readings are correct.

Practically, she is the exception desk: about a fifth of claims, but most reopened claims and complaints. She is not overloaded, and she is not the biggest delay in the process — the external verification and payment vendors are, and they deserve more management attention than she does. Her real exposure is that nobody else does her job. When she was away six days, ordinary claims were unaffected while difficult ones backed up sharply.

We recommend documenting her routing rules, recording a reason for each routing decision, training a named backup, and fixing some logging gaps. We do not recommend removing or automating the role on current evidence.
