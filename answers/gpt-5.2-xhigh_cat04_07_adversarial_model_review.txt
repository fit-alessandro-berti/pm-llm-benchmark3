# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

This review distinguishes four use cases because they imply different acceptable trade-offs and validation burdens:

- **Exploration and process understanding**: prioritize high replay fitness on imperfect logs, simplicity, and stakeholder comprehension; tolerate lower precision and known policy gaps as long as they are clearly labeled as “descriptive, not normative.”
- **Soft conformance monitoring (signal generation)**: allow some uncertainty and missing-event tolerance, but require that clear policy violations are not normalized as “allowed”; emphasize calibrated false-positive/false-negative behavior and transparent “unknown/uncertain” outcomes.
- **Compliance enforcement (hard gating / audit)**: requires an explicitly normative model plus a curated negative-behavior test suite; high precision must be demonstrated against negatives, not inferred from positive logs; mandatory requirements must never be optionalized “for robustness.”
- **Simulation / what-if and impact analysis**: requires soundness/reachability, stable semantics under model updates, and minimal duplicate-label ambiguity; runtime and maintainability matter.

Assumptions used to interpret the evidence:

- **Language acceptance vs alignment**: “Model accepts?” below means the trace is in the model language (i.e., perfectly fitting replay without deviations), not merely alignable with log/model moves.
- **Known missing information**: the external/internal sourcing attribute and “affected check” after corrections are missing; therefore any strict enforcement of P3 and the fine-grained aspect of P6 is intrinsically limited.
- **Operator semantics**: in the given tree syntax, `->` is sequence, `+` is parallel/AND, `X` is XOR, `tau` is silent, `*` is loop, `O` is inclusive OR (one or more children).

# 2. Metric Interpretation

| Measure | What it suggests | What it does not establish | Concern about comparability | Decision relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Training alignment fitness | How easily the model can reproduce the training log traces under alignment | Whether the model represents policy correctly; whether it over-permits unobserved behavior | High: preprocessing, event filtering, and alignment costs are unspecified; training cases may differ | Medium for exploration; low for enforcement without negatives |
| Holdout alignment fitness | Out-of-sample replayability; sensitivity to unseen variants and missing events | Whether “unseen” is noncompliant vs simply unrecorded/rare; whether low fitness is desirable strictness | Very high: no seeds, no case IDs, unknown split method; potential distribution shift/leakage | High for monitoring; ambiguous for enforcement (depends on governance stance) |
| Alignment precision on training log | How much extra behavior the model allows beyond the observed training traces | False positive rate on forbidden behaviors; robustness to legitimate novelty | High: computed “only against observed positives” (F5); duplicates and taus affect alignments | High for enforcement only if paired with negative suite |
| Token-based generalization | Tendency to allow plausible variations rather than memorizing exact sequences | Whether allowed variation is policy-allowed; whether generalization is achieved by permissiveness | High: sensitive to model structure and alignment parameters; not a policy score | Medium (monitoring), low (enforcement) unless tied to policy probes |
| Petri-net simplicity (arc-degree) | Structural complexity of the induced net; proxy for maintainability and explainability | Correctness, completeness, soundness, or decision transparency | Medium: conversion choices and duplicate labels can inflate complexity | Medium-high for long-term maintainability |
| Median alignment runtime per case | Operational cost of alignment-based conformance checking | End-to-end runtime in production; explainability of deviations | Medium: hardware, caching, parameter settings, and log size are unspecified | Medium (operational feasibility), not a correctness signal |
| Duplicate-label count (undocumented) | Potential ambiguity in mapping events to multiple same-labeled transitions; potential maintenance/ownership pain | The severity of ambiguity (depends on definition and how alignments break ties) | Very high: definition undocumented; different pipelines can count differently | High (auditability, explainability, stable metrics) |
| Stakeholder comprehension (informal 1–5) | Usability for communication and adoption | Whether stakeholders understood edge cases; whether the model is policy-correct | Very high: informal, likely small sample, framing effects, and anchoring | High for adoption; must not override compliance requirements |

Key takeaway: these metrics are **evidence about behavior under a specific (undocumented) pipeline**, not proof of policy correctness.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

## Strongest case for Model S

- **Operationally robust on messy logs**: optional activities and parallelism reduce penalties from missing events (notably Supplier Review per F4) and noisy ordering.
- **Excellent for exploration and stakeholder dialogue**: very simple structure, fast alignments, high comprehensibility score.
- **Good “upper envelope” baseline**: if even this permissive model has low fitness on some traces, data quality or label mapping is likely broken.

## Strongest case against Model S

Model S is not merely “noise tolerant”; it is **normatively unsafe** relative to agreed policy:

- It **optionalizes mandatory checks** (violates P2) and can therefore treat clearly forbidden behavior as compliant (F1, B3).
- It allows **Expedite Review before the mandatory checks** (violates P4 and probe B4).
- It has **no representation of corrections** (P6) and therefore cannot distinguish legitimate rechecks from suspicious repetition (P7).

## Concrete undesirable or policy-ambiguous behaviors admitted by Model S

All of the following are accepted by Model S (examples; not exhaustive):

- `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` (skips both mandatory checks; matches B3; forbidden)
- `Register Dossier, Auto Approval, Release Order` (skips both mandatory checks; forbidden)
- `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` (expedite without checks; forbidden)
- `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` (expedite before checks; matches B4; forbidden)
- `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order` (missing Technical Review; policy-violating)
- `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Auto Approval, Release Order` (missing Credit Check; policy-violating)
- `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order` (supplier review without checks; policy-ambiguous and likely invalid)

## Noise tolerance vs semantic flaws

- **Intentional tolerance**: `X(activity, tau)` can be justified for exploration if the log is known to miss events (F4).
- **Accidental/semantic flaw**: placing `Expedite Review` in the same parallel block as the checks makes “expedite-before-checks” a first-class allowed behavior, directly contradicting P4. Optionalizing P2 is not a “missing-event fix” when stakeholders assert the checks are mandatory.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

## Strongest case for Model C

- **Explicit enforcement posture**: it largely encodes “checks before approval,” includes a correction variant, and can be defended as a conservative compliance gate if the organization adopts a strict “closed world” stance (F8).
- **High training precision**: consistent with a model that allows little beyond observed training variants.

## Strongest case against Model C

- **Branch enumeration is a maintenance trap**: every newly accepted combination becomes a new branch; this scales combinatorially and is difficult to update safely (F2, F3).
- **Duplicate labels are not just cosmetic**: many same-labeled transitions can create ambiguous alignments, unstable diagnostics, and inflated precision, especially when precision is measured only on positives (F5).
- **Poor holdout fitness and low generalization**: suggests either overfitting to frequent training variants or sensitivity to missing-event bias (F4). Treating all unseen behavior as noncompliant (F8) is a governance choice, not a metric conclusion.
- **Policy incompleteness**: it rejects policy-valid behavior such as alternate check order with Auto Approval (B2) and plausible external/urgent mixes (B5). It also hard-codes Supplier vs Expedite ordering in one direction only (supplier then expedite), despite P4 allowing either.
- **Correction handling is too rigid**: only “repeat both checks” is modeled; “repeat only affected check” (F6, B6) is not transparent and becomes a forced nonfit.

## Is high precision likely memorization?

Given F5 (precision computed against observed positives only) and the enumerative structure, Model C’s high training precision is consistent with **memorizing frequent variants** rather than representing policy. Without a negative suite and split reproducibility, “0.941 precision” is not audit-ready evidence.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by likely decision impact (highest first):

1. **Positive-log-only precision (F5)**: precision computed without a curated negative suite cannot justify enforcement; enumerative models can appear “precise” by construction.
2. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior**: Model S can achieve near-perfect fitness by allowing omission of checks; this is a classic fitness pathology for governance.
3. **Missing-event bias (F4)**: absent Supplier Review (and possibly other events) can severely penalize stricter models and artificially favor permissive ones; also confounds holdout fitness.
4. **Train/holdout leakage or split sensitivity**: no seeds, no case IDs, and no published split logic means the holdout result could reflect accidental overlap or distribution shift.
5. **Alignment-cost sensitivity**: different costs (log move vs model move vs sync) can change fitness/precision materially, especially with duplicates and silent transitions.
6. **Duplicated-label effects**: ambiguous mapping to same-labeled transitions can destabilize diagnostics and inflate metrics depending on alignment tie-breaking.
7. **Silent-transition effects**: `tau` can hide missing behavior and increase fitness/generalization while reducing interpretability; it can also mask where policy was weakened.
8. **Runtime confounding by model size**: Model C’s runtime reflects structural size and duplication; not directly a quality signal, but it impacts feasibility of per-case alignment in operations.
9. **Stakeholder-score methodology**: informal comprehension scores are valuable for adoption but are highly sensitive to framing and do not validate compliance semantics.
10. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness**: token-based generalization can rate “anything goes” models highly; must be interpreted with policy probes.

# 7. Third Alternative

## Strategy

Use a **compact procedural backbone (process tree) that encodes the agreed control-flow policy** plus **post-hoc policy checks** for attribute-conditioned requirements (P3) and for the unobservable “affected check” decision (P6). This is safer than Model S (mandatory checks and expedite ordering enforced) and more generalizable/maintainable than Model C (no variant enumeration; limited duplication).

## 1) Compact formal description

Process-tree style (one readable option):

```text
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  +('Credit Check', 'Technical Review'),
  X(
    tau,
    *( ->('Correct Dossier', O('Credit Check', 'Technical Review')), tau)
  ),
  +(
    X('Supplier Review', tau),
    X('Expedite Review', tau)
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

## 3) Declarative checks / external policy dictionaries

Because P3 and the “affected check” detail in P6 are not observable in the discovery log, a governance-grade deployment should add a case-attribute layer and evaluate rules explicitly:

```python
POLICY = {
  "first": "Register Dossier",
  "last": "Release Order",
  "mandatory_checks": {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"},
  "approval_set": {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"},
  "supplier": "Supplier Review",
  "expedite": "Expedite Review",
  "correction": "Correct Dossier",
}

def assess_case(activity_sequence, case_attrs):
  labels = list(activity_sequence)
  out = {"status": "unknown", "reasons": []}

  if not labels or labels[0] != POLICY["first"] or labels[-1] != POLICY["last"]:
    out["status"] = "noncompliant"
    out["reasons"].append("P1 violated")
    return out

  approvals = [i for i,a in enumerate(labels) if a in POLICY["approval_set"]]
  if len(approvals) != 1:
    out["status"] = "noncompliant"
    out["reasons"].append("P5 violated")
    return out

  approval_idx = approvals[0]
  pre = labels[:approval_idx]

  for m in POLICY["mandatory_checks"]:
    if m not in pre:
      out["status"] = "noncompliant"
      out["reasons"].append("P2 violated")

  if POLICY["expedite"] in labels:
    exp_idx = labels.index(POLICY["expedite"])
    if exp_idx > approval_idx:
      out["status"] = "noncompliant"
      out["reasons"].append("P4 violated (expedite after approval)")
    for m in POLICY["mandatory_checks"]:
      if labels.index(m) > exp_idx:
        out["status"] = "noncompliant"
        out["reasons"].append("P4 violated (expedite before checks)")

  sourcing = case_attrs.get("sourcing")
  if sourcing == "external" and POLICY["supplier"] not in labels:
    out["status"] = "noncompliant"
    out["reasons"].append("P3 violated (external without supplier)")
  if sourcing == "internal" and POLICY["supplier"] in labels:
    out["status"] = "suspicious"
    out["reasons"].append("Supplier present on internal case")

  seen_correction = False
  seen = set()
  for a in labels:
    if a == POLICY["correction"]:
      seen_correction = True
      continue
    if a in POLICY["mandatory_checks"]:
      if a in seen and not seen_correction:
        out["status"] = "suspicious"
        out["reasons"].append("P7 suspicious repeat without correction")
      seen.add(a)
      seen_correction = False

  if out["status"] == "unknown":
    out["status"] = "compliant"
  return out
```

## 4) Explicit semantics for P1–P7 elements

- **Mandatory checks (P2)**: both `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` must occur before approval; their relative order is unconstrained.
- **Supplier Review (P3)**: modeled as optional in control-flow; enforcement requires a `sourcing` attribute to decide when it is mandatory. Without that attribute, the best-supported stance is “cannot conclude noncompliance solely from absence.”
- **Expedite Review (P4)**: optional; if present, it must occur after both mandatory checks and before approval; its order relative to Supplier Review is unconstrained.
- **Exactly-one approval (P5)**: XOR between `Manager Approval` and `Auto Approval`.
- **Correction/recheck (P6/P7)**: zero or more cycles; each `Correct Dossier` must be followed by at least one re-execution of `Credit Check` and/or `Technical Review` (inclusive OR). Repeated checks without a preceding `Correct Dossier` are not generated by the model and should be flagged as suspicious if observed.

## 5) Limitations from missing attributes

- Without **external/internal**, the model cannot distinguish “missing Supplier Review because internal” from “missing Supplier Review because incomplete logging,” so enforcement must yield an **uncertain** or **needs-review** outcome rather than a definitive violation.
- Without **affected-check**, the model can only represent “one or both checks may repeat after correction,” but it cannot prove that the repeated check matches the true affected set; this should be audited via added attributes or linked work-item data.

# 9. Validation Experiments

## Evaluation program (no fabricated results)

- **Reproducibility**: publish case IDs for train/holdout, random seeds, and preprocessing code (label mapping, lifecycle filtering, timestamp sorting, duplicate handling).
- **Attribute-stratified tests**: hold out strata by approval type (auto/manager), sourcing (external/internal if available), and presence of corrections; ensure each stratum appears in both splits.
- **Curated negative suite**: construct forbidden/suspicious traces for P1–P7 (e.g., missing checks, expedite-before-checks, repeated check without correction, double approval).
- **Missing-event perturbation**: randomly drop Supplier Review and other events to quantify sensitivity and to calibrate “uncertain” classifications.
- **Duplicate-event perturbation**: inject extra checks with and without corrections to validate P7 detection.
- **Unseen-combination tests**: generate “policy-consistent recombinations” (e.g., supplier+auto+alt check order) to measure brittleness to legitimate novelty.
- **Alignment-cost sensitivity**: sweep alignment costs and report metric stability intervals, not single numbers.
- **Soundness/reachability**: check soundness and dead transitions for each model after conversion.
- **Disaggregated reporting**: fitness, precision, generalization, simplicity reported separately by stratum and by the negative suite; never average without governance-approved weights.
- **Stakeholder comprehension protocol**: pre-registered questionnaire of scenario questions (including probes and adversarial traces), measured by accuracy and time-to-answer, not only subjective score.
- **Decision rule**: reject a model even with good aggregate scores if it fails any must-pass policy probes (P1/P2/P4/P5) or exceeds an agreed false-accept rate on the negative suite.

Perturbation tests (missing/duplicate events):

```python
import random
from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace

def drop_events(log, drop_prob, seed):
  rng = random.Random(seed)
  out = EventLog()
  for t in log:
    nt = Trace(attributes=dict(t.attributes))
    for e in t:
      if rng.random() >= drop_prob:
        nt.append(e)
    out.append(nt)
  return out

def duplicate_activity_once(log, activity, seed):
  rng = random.Random(seed)
  out = EventLog()
  for t in log:
    nt = Trace(attributes=dict(t.attributes))
    inserted = False
    for e in t:
      nt.append(e)
      if (not inserted) and e.get("concept:name") == activity and rng.random() < 0.5:
        nt.append(dict(e))
        inserted = True
    out.append(nt)
  return out
```

# 10. Governance Recommendation

- **Exploration**: Model S may be used only as an exploratory/descriptive model, with a prominent warning that it violates mandatory-check and expedite-order policy and cannot represent corrections. It should not be used to label cases as compliant/noncompliant.
- **Compliance enforcement**: neither Model S nor Model C is acceptable as-is. Model S admits forbidden behavior. Model C encodes an incomplete, enumerative view that rejects known-valid probes and is difficult to govern due to duplicate labels and variant sprawl.
- **Preferred path**: adopt the **third alternative** (procedural backbone + explicit rule checks) as the candidate compliance model, because it encodes P1/P2/P4/P5 directly, supports corrections without branch explosion, and is designed to separate “noncompliant” from “uncertain due to missing attributes/events.”

Deployment conditions for the third alternative:

- publish a reproducible evaluation package (seeds, splits, preprocessing, alignment parameters);
- add or link **sourcing** and **affected-check** attributes (or an equivalent evidence source) to resolve P3/P6;
- pass must-pass probe tests for P1/P2/P4/P5 and achieve an agreed low false-accept rate on a curated negative suite;
- demonstrate stability under missing-event and cost-sensitivity tests;
- meet operational runtime and maintainability thresholds with documented ownership.

# 11. Dissenting View

The conservative enforcement stance is to deploy Model C now and treat any unseen combination as noncompliant until explicitly approved. From that perspective, Model C’s strictness is a feature: it minimizes false acceptances, produces crisp “allowed vs not allowed” decisions, and aligns with audit risk management where the cost of a false acceptance is extreme. The third alternative, by design, reintroduces flexibility (e.g., supplier/expedite ordering freedom and correction-driven rechecks) that could be exploited to mask policy drift if the external/internal and affected-check attributes remain missing in practice. In addition, the governance board may prefer an explicit catalog of approved variants for operational stability and change control, rather than a more expressive model whose accepted language is harder for non-experts to enumerate. Finally, low holdout fitness for Model C could simply indicate that the holdout contains genuinely noncompliant behavior that should be rejected.

I would change my recommendation if: (a) a curated negative suite shows Model C has materially lower false-accept rates than the third alternative under consistent alignment settings; (b) the organization cannot realistically add the missing attributes; and (c) a documented change-control process can keep the enumerated-variant library current without exploding duplicate-label ambiguity.
