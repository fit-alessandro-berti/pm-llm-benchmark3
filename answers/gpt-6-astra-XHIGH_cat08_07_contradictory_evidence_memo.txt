# 1. Executive memo

**Decision: adopt a controlled version of E, supported by D.** Expand the simplified path cautiously for verified low-risk houses; restore survey, permit, and activation safeguards for other cases; and test the revised process before broader rollout. Retain coordinator ownership provisionally, with shared case records and backup coverage. Do not scale Redesign R nationally as a package.

R appears to accelerate straightforward orders while making exceptions more costly and difficult to resolve. Median duration fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%. But p95 duration rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right installation fell six percentage points, and complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%. Estimated full cost increased from EUR 198 to EUR 207 despite a EUR 23 saving in visible cost. The cost estimate is incomplete, so the apparent saving cannot support an investment case yet.

The strongest case for selective expansion is segment-specific: simple low-risk houses, 34% of pilot cases, improved on median, p90, first-time-right, and complaints. Apartments and old buildings show substantial deterioration, particularly in the tail. These are observed associations, not causal estimates: pilot building mix differs from baseline, early risk attributes are incomplete, and equivalent follow-up is unconfirmed. A full rollback would discard promising benefits; unrestricted expansion would expose more customers to unresolved harms.

Process evidence suggests several mechanisms worth acting on. Survey skipping coincides with more failed and second visits. Missing expected permit checks concentrate in old apartment buildings. Billing before successful activation coincides with manual activation and billing disputes. High coordinator and second-visit utilization, centralized collaboration, and stalled cases during absences suggest that reduced handovers have concentrated bottlenecks and operational dependency. These mechanisms are plausible, but log completeness and case mix must be checked before assigning causal responsibility.

High model fitness does not establish that R is well controlled. Pilot precision of 0.51 indicates a model permitting substantial behavior beyond the recorded traces. Alignment exceptions, temporal delays, and resource evidence provide more useful operational warnings than fitness alone.

**Act immediately:** pause predictive survey skipping; require verified successful activation before billing; and verify applicable permits before installation. Audit affected open cases, prioritize customers billed without working service, and correct inappropriate billing where confirmed. Treat missing risk information as ineligible for the simplified route. Preserve combined validation/scheduling for verified simple houses and coordinator continuity where capacity and backup coverage are adequate.

Over the next two weeks, repair risk classification and monitoring, establish concurrent comparisons within regions and risk strata, and start complete 60-day downstream cost follow-up. Add structured exception reasons, start timestamps, and shared case context. Test survey policy and ownership arrangements separately so their effects can be distinguished. Do not interpret early reviews as mature outcome evidence.

Release expansion in segment-specific stages only after comparable cohorts demonstrate retained benefit without material deterioration in tail duration, installation quality, complaints, or full cost, and after activation and permit controls work reliably. Predeclare statistical margins and capacity limits before examining trial results. Pending that evidence, cap volumes in stressed queues. This accepts some near-term survey and staffing expense to protect working-service delivery while preserving the pilot’s credible speed gains.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness and low precision measure different properties.** Fitness asks whether the model can reproduce recorded traces. Precision asks how much additional behavior the model permits beyond what those traces demonstrate. A permissive model can replay the frequent simplified route and many exceptional traces while allowing unsupported combinations of survey omission, repeated installation, manual activation, and billing. Fitness of 0.94 therefore does not mean 94% of orders followed an approved operating policy. Nor does precision of 0.51 mean 49% of cases were wrong. The lower generalization score adds concern about transfer to other cases, subject to how it was measured.

Separately discovered baseline and pilot models can also differ because of activity merging, discovery settings, and sample composition. Evaluate both with harmonized activities and comparable validation methods. Use an approved reference model, including conditional survey and permit requirements, for operational conformance. Examine case-level alignments to distinguish genuine deviations from logging omissions and inappropriate reference-model assumptions.

**An improved median can coexist with a much worse tail.** Quantiles describe different locations in a distribution. Enough uncomplicated cases can finish faster to lower the median while a smaller group encounters failed visits, repeat work, and long waits. The segment findings support this explanation: simple houses improve at both median and p90, whereas apartments and old buildings deteriorate sharply at p90. Aggregate quantiles are not weighted averages of segment quantiles, and changed case mix can alter the aggregate further.

Early billing could also make an administrative completion measure look better without shortening time to working service. The cycle-time endpoint is unspecified, so this is a hypothesis to test. Report order-to-verified-working-service separately from order-to-billing and administrative closure. Include unfinished cases using appropriate time-to-event methods; report p95 as not yet estimable if follow-up is insufficient rather than treating still-open cases as completed or excluding them silently.

**Fewer handovers can coexist with lower resilience.** Ownership reduces transfer delays, ambiguity, and repeated explanation. But the handover network counts transfers, whereas the working-together network reveals who must coordinate or supply expertise. Concentrating this work on a few coordinators and activation specialists can reduce formal handovers while increasing dependency on those people. Absence-related stalls provide direct evidence of this weakness. High utilization also leaves little capacity to absorb variable arrivals and rework. Shared case records, designated deputies, and pooled exception queues can preserve accountability while reducing that dependency.

**Lower visible cost can coexist with more expensive outcomes.** Skipping surveys and reducing coordination work can lower upfront expense. Failed visits, manual activation, repeat travel, complaints, and billing corrections occur later or in other teams. The supplied estimates imply downstream additions of EUR 14 per baseline case versus EUR 46 per pilot case, a EUR 32 difference that exceeds the EUR 23 visible saving. This is an arithmetic decomposition of incomplete estimates, not independently measured incremental cost. Measure comparable per-order cost through 60 days after billing, retain open-order costs, and disclose unresolved liabilities beyond that horizon. Do not count earlier revenue collection as an operating-cost saving.

**Simple houses can improve while apartments and old buildings worsen.** The value of preparation depends on address complexity. For straightforward houses, combined validation/scheduling may remove avoidable delay. For apartments and permit-sensitive buildings, surveys and checks may prevent expensive failures. The DFG bypass and recovery edges, alignment exceptions, and apartment activation delays are consistent with this mechanism. They do not isolate it causally: R bundled ownership, task merging, survey policy, and billing changes, while regional and building mix also changed. Segment comparisons and controlled component tests are therefore necessary. Neither national averaging nor the pilot’s favorable simple-house results establish which individual intervention caused the benefit.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks and limitations | Conditions under which reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Extends faster typical completion, fewer handovers, and potential upfront savings quickly. | Exposes more customers to severe delays, rework, billing disputes, and concentrated capacity risk. Pilot representativeness and full economics are unresolved. | Only after representative, mature evidence clears quality, tail, cost, capacity, and activation/permit gates. Current evidence does not justify it. Restoring essential safeguards would also mean scaling a modified process rather than R unchanged. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Restores familiar preparation and activation sequencing; may relieve recovery workloads. | Loses observed simple-house gains and reintroduces coordination friction. Baseline itself has substantial delays and failures; rollback does not prove those problems disappear. | Appropriate if safe routing and billing controls cannot be enforced promptly, serious harms persist under containment, or controlled evidence shows the redesigned components provide no worthwhile benefit. Prefer targeted rollback when harm is segment-specific. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skipping and post-billing activation** | Retains a plausible accountability benefit while removing two suspect changes. | Ownership’s independent effect is unknown; national expansion may amplify high utilization, absence dependency, and unclear specialist responsibility. It may discard useful task merging for simple cases. | Reasonable after capacity, backup coverage, and a component comparison show ownership works across the target segments. Use as a provisional operating pattern locally, not an immediate national mandate. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Best route to distinguishing component effects from selection, mix, and regional differences. | Delays some benefits; may be underpowered for tails or contaminated by shared resource queues. Continuing risky practices merely to collect evidence is unjustified. | Necessary for unresolved components and complex segments after immediate safeguards. Use concurrent controls, adequate independent assignment units, complete follow-up, and prespecified endpoints. |
| **E. Simple-house deployment, complex-case controls, measurement before scale** | Preserves the clearest observed benefit and limits exposure where deterioration is concentrated. Allows staged learning and operational recovery. | Depends on reliable eligibility classification, effective controls, and capacity. Routing mistakes and shared recovery teams can spread harm across segments. | Best current choice if low-risk eligibility is verified, missing attributes default to protective routing, and expansion is capped and reversible. Pair with D; pause survey skipping and post-billing activation across all routes initially. |

The recommended E is deliberately narrower than permission to deploy every element of R to simple houses. Scale the simplified scheduling workflow in controlled increments; retain ownership with backup; restore activation safeguards everywhere. Preserve a concurrent comparison group so early expansion continues to generate evidence.

It is a judgment, not a calculated monetary expected value: implementation costs, outcome uncertainty distributions, and management’s valuations were not provided. Operational urgency differs from information value. In particular, M7 begins immediately even though it ranks below experiments that could change the chosen process design.

**Sequence and comparison design.** Begin M7 immediately and start M4’s long follow-up immediately. Collect M3 and M2 as shared instrumentation; undertake M6 before exposing additional addresses to survey skipping. Run M1 and M5 as separable comparisons once eligibility, recording, and capacity are stable. M8 can accompany customer follow-up at low incremental burden. Ranking does not require waiting for one measurement to finish before starting another.

Use contemporaneous controls within regions and prespecified risk/customer groups. Where resource sharing would contaminate case-level assignment, randomize sufficiently numerous teams or queues and account for clustering; four regions alone do not guarantee adequate independent replication. Record staffing, demand, and shared-team utilization. Analyze assignment as randomized and report actual adherence separately. Historical baseline remains context, not the sole causal comparator.

Reconstruct early missing attributes only from reliable source records. Otherwise report missingness explicitly, use an unknown-risk category operationally, and show sensitivity analyses rather than assuming missing cases resemble measured ones. Present both segment effects and results standardized to the intended rollout mix. Do not add DFG edge shares to estimate pathway probabilities.

Define cohorts by order entry and follow them beyond administrative closure. Verify how the reported 62-day p95 was calculated within a six-week pilot. Use equal opportunity for complaint observation and 60-day post-billing cost follow-up; retain incomplete cases in time-to-event analysis. Power the study for material quality differences and tail uncertainty, accounting for clustering and multiple planned comparisons. No defensible sample size or significance claim follows from the supplied totals alone.

Review safety and queues weekly, but make expansion decisions only when outcome follow-up and precision are sufficient. A null significance test is not proof of equivalence. Continue measurement only while a plausible result could change the action; once a segment clearly fails a gate, remediate its process rather than repeatedly observing the same harmful design.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Choose E with controlled experiments under D.** Preserve the promising simplified route while restoring preparation and activation controls. Do not authorize national deployment of R unchanged.

- **Immediate action:** Give operations one accountable lead for containment and recovery. Review cases with repeated visits, missing expected permits, delayed activation, or billing before verified working service. Prioritize affected customers, correct confirmed inappropriate charges, restore applicable permit checks, and introduce shared case records, deputies, and explicit ownership through technical completion and complaint recovery. Cap intake where recovery queues are overloaded.
- **Pause:** Suspend predictive survey skipping and moving activation checks after billing. Pause expansion into apartments, old buildings, business, and rural segments until their specific failure modes are controlled. Route uncertain-risk addresses through protective validation and survey requirements.
- **Scale:** Expand combined validation/scheduling cautiously for verified simple low-risk houses, retaining a concurrent comparator. Keep coordinator ownership where workload and backup arrangements support it; broader ownership rollout depends on M5. Use available survey capacity to restore necessary preparation, and assess qualified support for congested recovery work.
- **Measure next:** Start activation/permit monitoring and complete cost tracking immediately; validate the risk rule next. Add reason codes and start timestamps, then run separate survey-policy and ownership comparisons. Track order-to-working-service, p90/p95, first-time-right, repeat visits, complaints, SLA breaches, full cost, and absence-related stalls by segment and region.

**Broader-rollout gates:** The following are proposed management thresholds, not thresholds established by the supplied evidence. Agree them before trial results are reviewed, size the comparisons accordingly, and apply them to each segment proposed for expansion against a concurrent control with the same essential safeguards.

1. **Control reliability:** Every billed order has auditable successful activation, and every order requiring a permit has verification before the relevant installation work. Any verified unauthorized bypass blocks the next expansion step until corrected. Missing records do not count as successful compliance. Establish continuous monitoring and case-level source verification; observing zero incidents in a small sample alone is insufficient assurance.
2. **Tail and quality protection:** Using prespecified one-sided 95% confidence bounds with an appropriate plan for multiple comparisons, rule out p90 deterioration above two days, p95 above three days, first-time-right loss above 1pp, complaint increase above 0.5pp, and SLA-breach increase above 1pp. These proposed tolerances are much smaller than the pilot’s adverse aggregate changes. If p95 or complaint outcomes are not mature enough to assess, the gate remains unmet.
3. **Worthwhile benefit and full economics:** Demonstrate a preselected material benefit, such as at least two days faster median time to working service or EUR 5 lower full mean cost per order, with uncertainty supporting a real improvement. In every case, the upper confidence bound on additional full cost should be no more than EUR 5 per order; any accepted premium requires an explicit valuation of the service benefit. Include added survey, backup, and specialist capacity costs.
4. **Operational capacity and transportability:** Show stable recovery backlog and satisfactory absence coverage during representative demand. Use 85% sustained utilization as an initial planning ceiling for constrained teams, then validate the appropriate local limit from arrival variability and service commitments; it is not a universal physical threshold. Reproduce acceptable outcomes across multiple regions representing the intended segment before extending beyond the pilot footprint.

Expand one qualified segment and capacity increment at a time. A successful simple-house result does not authorize apartment or old-building rollout. Persistent failures under restored safeguards warrant targeted rollback; reliable quality, controlled tails, sustainable staffing, and demonstrated net benefit justify broader adoption of the revised process.
