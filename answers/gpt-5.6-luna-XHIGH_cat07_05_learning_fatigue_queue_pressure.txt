# 2. Integrated explanation

The log is consistent with several mechanisms operating in sequence, and the aggregate position table should not be interpreted as a causal learning curve.

At the beginning of a shift, workers face a backlog inherited from another period. Positions 1–5 contain the most complex and urgent work: mean complexity is 0.72, high-priority share is 41%, and the queue is 46. Senior resources also appear to take complex cases early, especially after a night or weekend backlog. Longer service time and higher error/rework at the start therefore reflect case mix, handover conditions, and possibly warm-up. The first 20 events may also include a genuine warm-up or retrieval effect: all human resource groups become faster later, and early errors are higher for juniors. However, the early aggregate is a mixture of difficult cases and first-of-shift behavior, not a clean estimate of learning.

From positions 6–30, service time falls from 15.2 to 13.6 minutes and error/rework fall to 2.5%/4.8%. This is compatible with workers becoming familiar with the current queue and settling into the workflow. It is also compatible with clearing the complex carryover: complexity falls from 0.72 to 0.54, priority falls from 41% to 28%, and the queue falls from 46 to 33. A within-resource comparison of matched cases is needed to decide how much of this change is learning versus selection and stabilization.

Positions 31–50 continue to show shorter service time, but complexity is still falling to 0.50 and priority to 25%. The low error rate of 2.7% is compatible with a favorable case mix and a stable operating period. It should not be used as proof that experience makes people uniformly safer, because the high-risk conditions have not been held constant.

The later pattern is different. At positions 51–75 and 76+, queue length rises to 62 and 88. Service time drops further, to 11.4 and 10.8 minutes, but error rates rise to 4.6% and 7.9%, and rework rises to 8.2% and 13.7%. The WIP-stratified table reinforces this speed-quality tradeoff: relative to low WIP, high WIP reduces mean service time for low-complexity cases from 12.7 to 9.4 minutes and for high-complexity cases from 22.4 to 16.8 minutes, while errors increase from 2.1% to 5.8% and from 4.2% to 10.9%, respectively. This is strong evidence of queue pressure, such as prioritizing closure, reducing verification, switching rapidly, or deferring difficult checks. It is not evidence that workers have simply learned to work faster.

Fatigue may contribute to the late deterioration, particularly after prolonged time-on-task and repeated high-WIP work. A fatigue effect would be expected to make quality worse after controlling for queue pressure, complexity, activity, and shift. But the simultaneous fall in service time makes pure fatigue an incomplete explanation. Under pressure, fatigue and pressure can interact: a tired worker may be more likely to take a shortcut when the queue is high, while a fresh worker may also shorten service under the same target. The data do not contain direct measures of sleep, breaks, alertness, or exertion, so fatigue should be treated as a hypothesis requiring stronger controls.

Clock time adds another layer. Night work may have circadian effects, and Night staff are slower later and have higher high-WIP error rates. Yet Night has fewer events, a 56% high-complexity share in its first hour, more urgent exceptions, and a different workforce. Day demand peaks late, while Early clears the night backlog. Consequently, “last two hours” and “late shift” are jointly determined by time of day, elapsed work, demand, staffing, and inherited queue. Circadian effects cannot be inferred from the shift labels alone; they require comparisons at the same clock time and, ideally, information about prior sleep and roster history.

Case selection and batching can manufacture apparent position effects. A supervisor may route complex cases to seniors early, leave simpler cases for later, and send similar Document Review cases to a junior in a run. Quality Check deferral makes the last 45 minutes contain a burst of corrections, so the correction rate in that interval may represent accumulated pending work rather than a sudden deterioration in every upstream decision. Likewise, the instantaneous bot event lowers recorded service time, but human correction under queue length above 70 moves work downstream and can create rework that is attributed to the reviewer, the bot, or the process depending on the measurement rule.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore conditional: there is likely a modest early warm-up/stabilization effect, a substantial case-mix and selection effect at shift boundaries, and a late queue-pressure speed-quality tradeoff. Fatigue and circadian effects may amplify the late risk, with resource experience modifying susceptibility. The causal question is not “does performance worsen with event number?” but “for the same kind of work, assigned under comparable conditions, how do position, elapsed time, clock time, WIP, and recent workload change service and downstream quality?”

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Data preparation and outcome definitions.**

Create event duration from `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp`, after checking time zones, timestamp ordering, negative or missing durations, and whether `time:timestamp` is an end time. Define service time consistently by activity. Keep waiting time separate from service time; a delayed Quality Check is an operational waiting phenomenon, not necessarily a long checking action. Define error at the event level, and define case-level quality using any error, rework within seven days, downstream correction, escalation, and final outcome. Avoid counting a correction twice as both an original error and a separate independent failure unless the estimand requires it.

Illustrative PM4Py-oriented preparation is:

```python
import pm4py

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw_df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)

# Use the installed PM4Py version's exact keyword names where they differ.
df["service_minutes"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60
df["position_log"] = np.log1p(df["sequence_position_within_shift"])
df["late_shift"] = df["minutes_since_shift_start"] >= 360
df["high_wip"] = df["wip_at_start"] > 70

log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)
log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
log = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(log)
log = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)
```

The actual service and waiting fields produced by these helpers should be inspected against the installed version and validated on a few cases. Do not silently treat an activity’s waiting time as its service time. Also retain the original columns, because derived temporal features can encode information from later events if used carelessly.

**Within-resource comparisons.**

Estimate service time with a generalized linear mixed model or a robust regression, for example a log-duration model with resource random intercepts and activity-family effects. Model error and rework with logistic mixed models or binomial regression. Include resource, activity, complexity, priority, WIP, queue length, arrivals, case age, path, shift, date, day of week, and clock time. Cluster uncertainty at the case and shift/resource level as appropriate. A model can include interactions such as:

```text
log(service_minutes) ~ position_spline + minutes_since_shift_start_spline
    + log1p(wip_at_start) + queue_length_at_start
    + complexity + priority + activity_family
    + shift + clock_time_spline + date + resource_experience_months
    + resource_group:high_wip + position_spline:high_wip
    + (1 | org:resource) + (1 | shift_id)
```

Use a separate model for `error_flag`, `rework_flag`, and downstream correction. Compare predicted outcomes for the same standardized case under early, middle, and late positions. Within-resource analyses reduce confounding from skill and assignment, but they do not eliminate changes in the work assigned to that resource.

**Within-shift position effects.**

Use both sequence position and elapsed time. Position measures queue order and workload progression; minutes since shift start measures time-on-task and operational time. Include nonlinear splines or pre-registered bins so that a single linear “learning” coefficient does not force the curve to be monotonic. Analyze first 20, middle, and final events within each `shift_id`, and exclude or separately model shifts with very few events. A useful falsification is to compare workers at the same position but different clock times, and the same clock time but different positions.

Estimate an early curve and a late curve separately. Learning predicts improvement early; fatigue predicts deterioration with elapsed work after workload controls; pressure predicts changes that track WIP or queue thresholds. These are predictions to test, not labels to assign from the curve alone.

**WIP and queue stratification.**

Reproduce the low/medium/high WIP table with narrower bins or continuous splines, separately for low and high complexity, each activity family, shift, and resource group. Include lagged WIP and queue length because the pressure at the start of an event may affect the event’s processing and downstream correction. Analyze arrivals and staffing at the same time: high WIP caused by a demand surge is not equivalent to high WIP caused by an upstream bottleneck.

Test an interaction between position and WIP. If later service-time compression and error increases occur primarily above WIP 70, that supports pressure as the proximal mechanism. If the late effect persists at low WIP, fatigue, circadian time, or unmeasured selection becomes more plausible. A threshold alert can be evaluated using an interrupted or stepped-wedge design only if alert timing is recorded and implementation is plausibly independent of short-term error changes.

**Complexity adjustment and selection.**

Plot the distribution of `case_complexity_score`, priority, activity path, specialist referral, and prior case age by position and shift. Report standardized mean differences, not only means. Fit matched comparisons or inverse-probability weights for receiving a case in a given position/resource, using variables known before service. Consider doubly robust adjustment, but do not describe it as eliminating unmeasured confounding.

Use stratification by complexity rather than a single adjustment alone: the high-WIP/high-complexity error rate of 10.9% is operationally different from high-WIP/low-complexity error at 5.8%. Examine whether complexity is itself measured after work begins; if so, avoid conditioning on a post-treatment variable or state the estimand clearly. Seniors taking difficult cases early is a selection process that can make senior early service look slow and junior later service look fast without any change in underlying ability.

**Shift and time-of-day controls.**

Include shift type, clock hour, calendar date, weekday/weekend, and inherited queue at shift start. Compare overlapping clock times across Early, Day, Late, and Night shifts and include worker or resource fixed effects when possible. If roster history is available, add hours since prior shift, consecutive shifts, and time since last break; if not, do not claim a biological circadian or fatigue estimate. Use separate estimates for shift-start backlog clearance and end-of-shift demand peaks. A Night-versus-Day coefficient by itself mixes workforce, case mix, staffing, and circadian effects.

**Batching detection and process mining.**

Use the event log to identify runs of the same activity, resource, queue batch, or customer/case segment. The relevant PM4Py starting point is:

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(
    log,
    # supply the installed version's case/event attributes for batch identity
)
```

Because the API and required attributes vary by PM4Py version, supplement it with explicit run features: consecutive same-resource/same-activity count, time between events, same `queue_batch` indicator, and proportion of a shift spent in runs of length at least 5, 9, or 18. Compare batched and non-batched cases after matching on activity, complexity, priority, WIP, and position. Quantify both local service-time savings and system effects: waiting before the batch, Quality Check delay, error, rework, and final completion time.

Use `pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph` or `pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph` to inspect event-volume concentration by hour, resource, and activity. Use `pm4py.view_dotted_chart` or `pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart` with shift and resource coloring to identify end-of-shift bursts and handover patterns. `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` can compare waiting and service performance on variants or time slices, while `pm4py.filter_time_range`, `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values`, `pm4py.filter_case_performance`, and `pm4py.filter_paths_performance` can isolate late windows, activities, slow cases, and paths. `pm4py.cluster_log` can help find recurring behavioral or case-mix clusters, but cluster labels should be treated as descriptive rather than worker-quality labels.

**Resource-experience interactions.**

Estimate a resource-group or continuous-experience interaction with position and WIP. The supplied table suggests that seniors gain speed while retaining comparatively stable quality at low WIP, whereas mids and especially juniors suffer larger high-WIP error increases. Test whether this remains after complexity, activity, shift, and assignment adjustment. Distinguish experience from role, roster, and case allocation; Night staff are a separate operational population, not simply an experience category.

Report conditional effects with confidence intervals and minimum cell sizes. Do not rank individuals from sparse raw error rates. A worker with few high-complexity cases can have a high percentage from one event, while a worker receiving many easy cases can look better through selection.

**Rework and error downstream analysis.**

Link each event to later correction, Quality Check, rework within seven days, escalation, and case completion. Use lagged event features and survival or time-to-rework analysis to determine whether short late service predicts downstream defects. A high immediate error flag may be detected early and repaired, whereas an apparently clean event may generate later rework. Separate original decision error, human override of Bot_Risk, Quality Check detection, and correction work.

Use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` to identify activities and variants associated with repeated work, and inspect performance by path with `pm4py.filter_paths_performance`. Treat the seven-day window carefully across shift boundaries and censor cases not yet observable for seven days. A process graph should show where pressure-related shortcuts move work, not just where the final error is recorded.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair because the observed event is the result of a worker, a case, a queue, an assignment policy, an interface, an automation component, and the surrounding team. A raw late-shift error percentage does not identify an individual’s causal contribution.

- **Selection bias and confounding:** Complex and urgent cases are more common early and at Night, while seniors select or receive difficult work. Workers who handle a high-risk queue can look worse because they are exposed to it. Conditioning only on an imperfect complexity score can leave substantial residual confounding. Assignment should be recorded, and comparisons should be within credible eligibility and case-mix strata.

- **Measurement error:** `start_timestamp`, completion timestamp, WIP, queue length, complexity, and error flags may have different clocks, missing values, or inconsistent operational definitions. A short recorded service time could mean a real shortcut, an unrecorded pause, a batched operation, an automated step, or a timestamping artifact. Rework within seven days can be censored by incomplete follow-up and can reflect detection policy as well as original quality.

- **Queue pressure created by management:** Targets, staffing cuts, priority rules, and end-of-shift closure expectations can create the WIP threshold and encourage speed over verification. It is inappropriate to attribute a pressure-induced system tradeoff solely to workers. Analyze arrivals, staffing, queue ownership, targets, and handovers alongside worker events.

- **Automation quality and accountability:** Bot_Risk’s zero-duration event does not establish zero effort or correctness. Human corrections under high queue may indicate bot degradation, more vigilant review, or rushed review. Preserve bot version, confidence, input quality, human override, and final decision fields. Automation should be audited on stratified samples, and responsibility for defects should not be shifted to the first human who touches the case.

- **Punitive use of error rates:** Do not discipline based only on unadjusted late-shift or individual rates. Small denominators, differential case difficulty, opportunities for detection, and rework visibility can produce unstable or systematically biased scores. Use rates for learning and staffing decisions, with uncertainty intervals, minimum sample sizes, independent quality audits, transparent definitions, and a route for workers to challenge an attribution.

- **Privacy and dignity:** Limit worker data to what is needed, restrict access, avoid publishing rankings, and retain only appropriate granularity. If break or roster data are used, protect health and personal information. Consult workers about whether the measures describe real work and whether an intervention introduces new risk.

- **Process integrity:** Do not defer or omit Quality Check merely to improve throughput. A locally faster process can create delayed defects, customer harm, regulatory risk, and more rework on the next shift. Every speed intervention should have a safety outcome and a handover control.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should show rates and distributions by shift, clock time, elapsed shift position, activity family, complexity band, priority, WIP band, resource group, and batched/non-batched status. It should show sample sizes, confidence intervals, data-quality flags, and a seven-day follow-up status. Worker-level views should be access-controlled and used for coaching or staffing investigation, not public ranking.

1. **Service time:** median and tail service minutes by activity, position bin, WIP band, complexity, shift, and resource group; include the proportion of unusually short records that may indicate batching or timestamp issues.

2. **Waiting time:** queue waiting, time to start, time awaiting Quality Check, and total case elapsed time. Show aging and SLA percentiles separately from service time so throughput is not improved by hiding delay.

3. **WIP and queue pressure:** WIP at event start, queue length, arrival rate, staffing/active-resource count, percentage of events above WIP 70, time above threshold, and threshold-alert response time.

4. **Error rate:** immediate `error_flag` rate and audited error rate, stratified by activity and case type. Display numerator, denominator, detection source, and uncertainty; do not show a raw worker leaderboard.

5. **Rework and downstream quality:** seven-day rework, repeat activity, Bot_Risk override, Quality Check detection, correction, escalation, and final case outcome. Link quality to the original event and report follow-up censoring.

6. **Complexity and priority mix:** distribution and mean of complexity score, high-priority share, case age, referral/exception indicators, and mix by position, shift, resource group, and queue. Include standardized mix differences when comparing periods.

7. **Learning/fatigue curve:** adjusted service, error, and rework predictions across sequence position, minutes since shift start, recent event count, and recent active duration, with separate low- and high-WIP curves. This distinguishes an early warm-up shape from a late pressure shape.

8. **Batching indicators:** consecutive same-activity runs, same-resource runs, same queue-batch runs, batch size distribution, short-service proportion, and end-of-shift burst volume. Show whether batching changes waiting, Quality Check delay, error, and rework.

9. **Handover and queue ownership changes:** count and age of cases transferred between resources or shifts, queue owner at each transition, incomplete handover fields, time from transfer to next action, and errors/rework after transfer. Explicitly show inherited start-of-shift backlog and cases pushed to the next shift.

10. **Worker-load fairness:** active minutes, event count, weighted complexity, high-priority exposure, high-WIP exposure, number of concurrent cases, break coverage, and complexity-adjusted load by resource group. Use distributional measures such as median, interquartile range, and high-load share rather than only an average.

11. **Circadian and shift context:** clock-time performance at matched WIP and complexity, consecutive shifts, hours since prior shift if available, demand peak indicators, and night urgent-exception share. Keep time-of-day association distinct from elapsed shift time.

12. **Automation health:** Bot_Risk volume, latency, confidence or abstention rate, human correction/override rate, audited bot error rate, correction delay, and downstream rework by WIP and complexity. Version the bot and flag changes in its behavior.

13. **Data quality and safety:** missing or inconsistent timestamps, negative durations, duplicate events, incomplete seven-day follow-up, queue-counter freshness, unassigned cases, and Quality Check completion outside the safe deadline. A dashboard that omits data reliability can create false confidence.

Use alerting for process conditions such as sustained WIP above 70, rising rework after service-time compression, or an aging Quality Check queue. Alerts should trigger staffing, routing, verification, or escalation actions, with the action and outcome recorded. The dashboard’s purpose is to improve the system’s speed, quality, and fairness together; it should not convert confounded event-log correlations into punishment.
