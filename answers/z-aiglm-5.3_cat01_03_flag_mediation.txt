## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

**Framework choice.** I do **not** use natural direct/indirect effects: (i) the flag is a deterministic threshold on `risk_score`, so a unit-level counterfactual mediator "had this flagged case been unflagged" requires changing the score — ill-defined; (ii) shared queues create interference, breaking consistency and cross-world assumptions; (iii) the flag changes queue state and resource assignment, producing exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding. Instead I use: **system-level policy (total) effects** (contrasts between fully specified operating policies, because interference makes unit-level totals ill-defined); **controlled direct effects** for components that can be physically held fixed (e.g., priority-lane routing); **randomized-interventional analogues of indirect effects** (draw the mediator from its policy-induced distribution); and **separable effects** to split the flag's "stimulus" (what is displayed) from its "response" (what the routing rules do) — P1 vs P0 manipulates display, P2 vs P0 manipulates routing.

Interference arises because review and escalation queues are shared: each case's routing changes every other case's `manual_queue_wait` and `escalation_queue_wait`, so mediators are jointly determined and unit-level mediation contrasts are ill-defined without specifying the whole routing policy. Exposure-induced confounding arises because the flag changes `priority_lane`, `triage_resource`, and plausibly `review_resource`, altering the queue state and reviewer mix at review — treatment-induced variables that confound every mediator–outcome relation (e.g., flag  M2  longer `manual_service` and more `escalation`). It also entangles waiting mediation with occurrence mediation: entering the queue changes the queue. Treatment–confounder feedback appears because `arrival_load` is one snapshot while congestion evolves; early flagged cases alter conditions for later arrivals, and routing decisions plausibly respond to current queue length. Finally, `risk_score` causes both flag and (via human judgment) `manual_review` and `escalation`, so flagescalation-type paths cannot be separated from score-driven paths observationally.

| Are the stage-decomposition numbers trustworthy? | Negative controls: (a) residual-to-close as negative-control outcome (should be flag-invariant; observed 60–90 min throughout); (b) Low-complexity unflagged as negative-control population (no review  no downstream queue exposure) | none (invariance test) | flag cannot plausibly affect post-decision administration | estimate a "flag effect" on residual-to-close or on Low-unflagged waits | any significant flag effect on residual  timestamp/selection artifact; revisit stage semantics |
| How sensitive is attribution to unmeasured risk? | Rosenbaum/E-value-style sensitivity: posit an unobserved risk attribute (omitted from risk_score) affecting both routing and service/escalation durations; compute the bias needed to null the occurrence-mediated gap | observed risk_score, complexity, arrival_load held fixed | pre-specified sensitivity model () |  required to explain away the occurrence-mediated component |   1.1–1.3 suffices  fragile;  > 2  reasonably robust |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 (status quo) | flagged fast (12 min median access, T1/T2); unflagged slow (150 min) | manual queue ~405 min median for flagged; escalation queue ~420 min for escalated | flagged median 16.5 h vs unflagged 5.75 h | maximal scrutiny of high-score cases; compliance OK today | unflagged triage already taxed by preemption | strong (directly observed, descriptive) | keep as comparator; not scalable if flagged volume grows |
| P1 (suppress display) | risky cases lose the lane unless re-keyed to score: access rises toward ~150 min | little change; humans see the score and likely keep reviewing near-threshold cases (observed 4/10 unflagged reviewed) | neutral to worse for risky cases (+~2 h); small triage gains for unflagged | true high-risk cases lose queue priority — compliance concern if SLAs exist | modest positive for unflagged triage | weak | use only as a randomized experiment separating display from routing; not as standing policy |
| P2 (keep lane, drop mandatory review) | unchanged (fast) | manual entries drop from 100% toward ~40–60% (human-judgment rate); congestion falls for everyone | flagged median could fall from 16.5 h toward 9–11 h if humans re-route High-complexity flagged cases; larger if they do not; unflagged also improve | highest risk: mid-score flagged (0.64–0.78) currently reviewed would skip scrutiny | strongly positive (freed reviewer hours) | strong on the occurrence mechanism; none on quality | do not adopt outright; pilot only with score-stratified exemptions and outcome audit |
| P3 (protected review capacity) | unchanged (fast) | flagged manual queue wait falls from ~405 toward ~100–150 min; escalation unchanged | flagged 3 to 5 h; unflagged unchanged to slightly worse | low — scrutiny preserved | negative if slots carved from shared pool; neutral if capacity is added | moderate (wait gap observed; causal share unquantified) | preferred pilot (below) |
| P4 (escalation criteria/capacity) | unchanged (fast) | added senior capacity cuts escalation queue (~420 min median among escalated flagged); tighter criteria shrink the escalated set (currently scores 0.79) | no change in flagged median (escalated cases are the top half); mean 2 to 3 h; escalated cases 4 to 5 h; criteria-tightening cuts more but removes scrutiny exactly where rejects concentrate | capacity version: low; criteria version: high (top-score cases lose senior review) | freed senior time helps the shared queue | moderate | expand capacity, yes; change criteria only with ground-truth outcome data |

**Selected policy: P3 (protected manual-review capacity), run as a randomized pilot that also measures spillover.**

Eligibility: every case newly flagged at registration during the pilot window; cases already waiting in the review queue at launch are excluded; concurrent unflagged cases form a non-randomized spillover-observation group.
Assignment: at triage completion, flagged cases are randomized 1:1 to a protected reviewer slot (manual review starts within 60 minutes during staffed hours) or the standard shared queue. Permuted blocks, stratified by complexity (High vs Medium/Low) and arrival_load (High vs Normal). Reviewers are blinded to arm.
Queue-capacity guardrail: protected slots capped at 25% of reviewer-hours; weekly monitoring of unflagged median triage_donemanual_start; automatic pause if it exceeds the pre-pilot baseline by more than 25%, or if any reviewer's utilization passes 90%.
Primary outcome: flagged manual queue wait (triage_donemanual_start). Secondary outcomes: flagged total cycle time; unflagged manual queue wait and cycle time (spillover); escalation and rejection rates; reviewer utilization.
Minimum process data: event log with start and completion timestamps for every stage, resource IDs, flag, risk_score, complexity, arrival_load, a rolling census of open reviews, and the staffing/shift calendar.
Stopping conditions: spillover harm triggered, capacity breach, unblinding or reviewer non-compliance, timestamp data-quality failure, or 200 randomized flagged cases reached — whichever comes first.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk.** If an omitted risk attribute (not captured in `risk_score`) drives both routing and downstream durations, the "flag-mediated" delay is actually risk delay; removing mandatory review (P2) would not shorten truly risky cases' cycles and could raise rework — reversing any P2-leaning conclusion and re-centering on improving the score.
2. **Queue interference.** If protected capacity (P3) is carved from the shared reviewer pool and cluster-level analysis shows flagged gains fully offset by unflagged losses, P3's recommendation reverses toward adding capacity (P4-style expansion) or reducing review volume (P2).
3. **Decision quality.** If waived-review flagged cases (scores 0.64–0.78) show unchanged error/approval outcomes under audit, the quality objection to P2 collapses and P2 becomes preferred — shortest cycle at no measurable quality cost.
4. **Timestamp semantics.** If the 8–10 hour manual-wait gaps are largely unstaffed evening/overnight clock time, "queue delay" is a staffing-hours artifact; routing policies P2–P4 would not help, and extended-hours staffing becomes the lever, reversing the pilot choice.
5. **Positivity/overlap.** If prospectively collected near-threshold cases show no discontinuity at the cutoff in routing or cycle time, the flag adds nothing beyond `risk_score`; P1 becomes safe and cheap, and all "flag effects" re-attribute to the score.
6. **Reviewer behavior change.** If reviewers who no longer see the flag (P1) compensate by reviewing at the same ~100% rate using the visible score, P1 only removes the priority lane and makes risky cases worse off — reversing P1. Likewise, if pilot reviewers behave differently because they are observed (Hawthorne), pilot estimates will not transport and the recommendation should be suspended.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed: flagged cases reach and complete triage far sooner (median arrival-to-triage-done 48 vs 210 minutes) but are always sent to manual review (10/10 vs 4/10), wait longer for review (405 vs 105 minutes among reviewed cases), escalate more often (6/10 vs 2/10), and close much later (median 987 vs 345 minutes). Escalated flagged cases are precisely the highest-scoring ones (all 0.79); triage resources are perfectly segregated (T1/T2 for flagged, T3/T4 otherwise); reviewer M2 handles most escalations and every rejection.

Plausible mechanism: the flag is a policy bundle, not a single cause. Its priority lane genuinely accelerates triage — partly by assigning faster resources — while its mandatory-review rule routes every flagged case into a shared, congested queue, and downstream escalation appears driven mainly by risk_score, adding roughly 540 minutes for top-score cases. The cycle-time gap is therefore mostly occurrence-mediated and queue-mediated rather than an intrinsic flag effect.

Still unidentified: how much of the manual-wait gap is congestion versus routing and reviewer mix; whether humans would substitute routing if the flag were hidden; spillover magnitudes onto unflagged cases; and whether scrutiny improves decisions, since no ground truth exists.

Most useful next evidence: the P3 protected-capacity pilot with within-flag randomization. It isolates the waiting-mediated component while holding review occurrence fixed, measures spillover directly, preserves scrutiny, and generates the information needed to test P2 or P4 safely.
