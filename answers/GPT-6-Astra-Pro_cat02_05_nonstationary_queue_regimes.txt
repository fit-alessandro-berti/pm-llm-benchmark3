# 1. Data audit and estimands

**The data support several operating regimes, but not a single calibrated stationary queue.** More importantly, two accounting issues must be resolved before treating the reported WIP and flow counts as measurements of the same conserved system.

For a set of \(n_R\) observed days, define:

$$
T_R=12n_R\ \text{opening-hours},\qquad
\widehat a_R=\frac{\sum_R arrivals}{T_R},\qquad
\widehat d_R=\frac{\sum_R completed}{T_R}.
$$

Here, \(\widehat a_R\) is arrival intensity and \(\widehat d_R\) is **observed completion intensity**, both in cases per opening-hour. Completion intensity is not a server’s service rate or demonstrated sustainable capacity.

The reported mean WIP estimates the number **in the system**, not just waiting. Assuming equal and complete snapshot coverage across days,

$$
\widehat L_R=\frac{1}{n_R}\sum_R mean\_wip.
$$

The service fields describe active handling-time quantiles in minutes. They do not identify mean service time, mean flow time, waiting-time quantiles, or a regime-wide service distribution. A median of daily medians is only a summary of daily medians.

**Clock convention.** Opening-time calculations retain all 12 hours, including an outage occurring during opening hours. An opening-time flow duration excludes overnight closure time but includes waiting during an opening-hours outage. Calendar flow time requires additional accounting.

**Audit findings and threats to naive inference**

| Finding or threat                                                        | Consequence                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hourly totals reconcile, but the peak percentages do not.                | For April 7, 15, 21, and 25, the vectors imply 08:00–09:59 shares of **16.8%, 18.1%, 9.6%, and 11.8%**, versus **31%, 32%, 47%, and 22%** in the daily table. Do not combine these as consistent peak measurements. |

The WIP inconsistency deserves an explicit calculation. **Conditional assumption A1:** arrivals and completions concern the same queue population, all inventory-reducing exits are counted, and there are no unrecorded removals or scope changes. With initial backlog \(B_0\geq0\), at any time on day \(d\),

$$
N_d(t)\geq B_0+\sum_{j<d}(A_j-D_j)-D_d.
$$

By the end of April 24, cumulative recorded net inflow is \(88\). April 25 has only \(43\) completions. Consequently, even setting \(B_0=0\), **WIP could never fall below \(45\) on April 25**, yet its reported mean is \(23\). Analogous lower bounds are \(57\) versus reported \(20\) on April 26, \(59\) versus \(22\) on May 2, and \(65\) versus \(19\) on May 3.

This is not merely a difference between mean and closing WIP. Possible explanations include unrecorded transfers/cancellations, a narrower WIP population, or count-definition errors; none is established. Below, \(A-D\) is therefore called **recorded net inflow**, not reconstructed backlog.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different interpretation is **two structural workload states with a temporary disturbance episode**, rather than separate models for every calendar/staffing combination:

| Alternative regime                | Dates        | Arrivals/hour | Completions/hour | Recorded net inflow | Operational story                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------: | ---------------: | ------------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ordinary lower-workload state     | Apr 6–14     |          7.23 |             7.19 |              \(+5\) | Weekday/weekend differences are covariates within a recurring calendar pattern.                                                        |
| Disruption and relaxation episode | Apr 15–19    |          6.72 |             6.43 |             \(+17\) | Outage and subsequent clearance belong to one transient episode; the exact recovery endpoint is uncertain.                             |
| Persistent higher-workload state  | Apr 20–May 3 |          7.95 |             7.42 |             \(+88\) | The higher-workload/reporting state persists. Staffing changes its performance, but do not necessarily create a new structural regime. |

Under this alternative, day type, hourly demand shape, staffing, and opening backlog would enter as explanatory variables rather than define separate queueing models.

**Strengths:** it is more parsimonious, avoids treating two post-intervention weekdays as a new equilibrium, and distinguishes a workload/reporting change from a capacity intervention.

**Weaknesses:** its aggregate intensities are unsuitable as uniform hourly staffing inputs. It also provisionally groups April 18–19 with recovery despite lacking proof of residual disturbance.

The primary segmentation is better for operational comparisons; the alternative is a better starting point for a small-sample explanatory model. Neither identifies whether the complexity shift was real, recoded, or both.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Across all 28 days there are **2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions over 336 opening-hours**: 7.50 arrivals/hour, 7.17 completions/hour, mean reported WIP 29.86, and mean staffing 5.14 FTE. The arrival-weighted complex share is approximately 33.82%.

These averages obscure at least four important distinctions.

**Calendar composition hides comparable workload per staff-hour.**

| Calendar group | Days | Arrivals/hour | Completions/hour | Mean FTE | Arrivals/FTE-hour | Reported mean WIP |
| -------------- | ---: | ------------: | ---------------: | -------: | ----------------: | ----------------: |
| Weekdays       |   20 |          9.00 |             8.60 |     6.15 |              1.46 |             34.85 |
| Weekends       |    8 |          3.74 |             3.60 |     2.63 |              1.42 |             17.38 |

Weekend volume is much lower, but staffing is also much lower. Arrival load per staff-hour is nearly the same. Lower absolute weekend WIP therefore does not establish spare capacity.

**The workload shift is much larger than the completion-rate shift.**

| Comparable weekday group | Arrivals/hour | Completions/hour | Arrival-weighted complex share | Median of daily service medians | Median of daily service p90s | Reported WIP |
| ------------------------ | ------------: | ---------------: | -----------------------------: | ------------------------------: | ---------------------------: | -----------: |
| LWD                      |          8.35 |             8.30 |                          20.9% |                          25 min |                       47 min |         20.3 |
| HWD                      |          9.50 |             8.38 |                          44.6% |                          35 min |                       83 min |         44.3 |

Average staffing is identical at 5.93 FTE. Arrivals increase about **13.8%**, while completions increase only **1.0%**. The typical daily median rises 40%, and the typical daily p90 rises about 77%.

A useful capacity proxy is available staff-minutes per arrival:

$$
b_R=\frac{720\sum_R staff\_fte}{\sum_R arrivals}.
$$

It falls from **42.6 minutes in LWD to 37.4 in HWD**. **Conditional assumption A2:** staffing is dedicated, fungible capacity and each case has measurable additive labor demand. Under that assumption, 37.4 minutes is a break-even mean labor budget before allowing recovery headroom. It is **not** an estimate of mean service time or utilization. A p90 exceeding that budget does not by itself prove overload.

**The staffing comparison exhibits an actual aggregation reversal.**

Compare April 20–28 with April 30–May 3, excluding the mixed April 29:

| Comparison        | Before: completions/day | After: completions/day | Apparent change |
| ----------------- | ----------------------: | ---------------------: | --------------: |
| All observed days |                   87.33 |                  85.00 |       **−2.7%** |
| Weekdays only     |                  100.57 |                 121.00 |      **+20.3%** |
| Weekends only     |                   41.00 |                  49.00 |      **+19.5%** |

The before window contains seven weekdays and two weekend days; the after window contains two of each. Reweighting post-intervention performance to the before window’s 7:2 composition produces **105 completions/day**, not 85.

Even mean staffing reverses descriptively: pooled staffing falls from 5.17 to 4.88 FTE, although staffing increases within both day types. This is a composition effect, not evidence that the intervention reduced staffing.

The within-type improvements still are **not causal estimates**: post-intervention observations are sparse, weekday composition differs, backlog is changing, and completed-case selection and intraday demand may differ.

**Outage and recovery can average to an apparently normal throughput.**

April 15–17 jointly produce **8.31 completions/hour**, almost identical to LWD’s 8.30. That pooled number conceals a day at **4.83** followed by recovery days averaging **10.04**. A pooled fit could therefore learn “normal throughput” from an abnormal interruption-and-clearance cycle.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For each representative day, let \(X_h\) be hourly arrival counts. Using sample variance with denominator \(12-1\),

$$
F=\frac{s_X^2}{\bar X},
\qquad
CV_{\text{count}}=\frac{s_X}{\bar X}.
$$

These are **count-dispersion indicators**, not interarrival-time variability estimates. Moreover, \(F=\bar X\,CV_{\text{count}}^2\), so they are not independent corroborating tests.

| Representative day              | Arrival Fano factor | Hourly-count CV | Peak/mean hourly arrivals | Arrival lag-1 correlation |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------: | --------------: | ------------------------: | ------------------------: |
| Apr 7: ordinary candidate       |                3.23 |            0.62 |                      2.38 |                      0.68 |
| Apr 15: outage                  |                4.14 |            0.69 |                      2.51 |                      0.71 |
| Apr 21: bursty high-mix weekday |               10.12 |            1.03 |                      3.55 |                     −0.14 |
| Apr 25: weekend                 |                1.29 |            0.55 |                      1.88 |                      0.80 |

April 21 has 34 arrivals during **10:00–11:00** and 24 during **13:00–14:00**, despite averaging only 9.58/hour. Its negative adjacent-hour correlation reflects isolated high bins separated by lower ones; it does not establish weak operational dependence.

Conversely, positive hourly correlations on April 7 and April 25 can arise from a smooth daily rise and fall. A Fano factor above one across unequal clock-hour means does not distinguish stochastic clustering from a deterministic time-of-day profile. Even conditionally independent arrivals could appear overdispersed after mixing hours with different expected counts.

Hourly flow differences also show why daily totals miss congestion. On April 21, cumulative arrivals minus completions reaches **+27 at an hourly boundary**, but closes at +14. On April 15, it reaches +55 before closing at +47. These are changes relative to unknown opening inventory—not absolute queue lengths or within-hour maxima. April 7’s early cumulative difference reaches −3, demonstrating why initializing its queue at zero would be invalid under conserved counting.

**Daily dispersion changes dramatically after segmentation.** The pooled daily-arrival Fano factor is **10.03**, versus **0.66 for weekdays**, **0.74 for weekends**, **0.26 for LWD**, and **0.29 for HWD**. Thus, stable-looking daily totals can coexist with severe intraday concentration; much pooled daily variation is calendar composition.

**Serial indicators are substantial but not stationary-model parameters.** Daily arrivals have lag-1 correlation 0.41 and lag-7 correlation 0.99. Reported WIP has lag-1 correlation 0.61; daily median service and complex share have correlations 0.87 and 0.93. These combine weekly repetition, level shifts, trends, and possibly carry-over. They do not justify an AR model or independent-row confidence intervals.

**Change-point evidence is strongest in the reported complexity field.** Before April 20 its range is 18–27%; thereafter it is 39–47%. A transparent one-split least-squares heuristic, requiring at least five days on each side, selects **April 20** and reduces within-segment squared error by approximately **95.1%**. This is descriptive fit improvement—not a confidence level or proof of a physical change on that date.

Four representative days cannot establish a Poisson, renewal, self-exciting, or other complete stochastic arrival model. Nor do they identify service-time variance, routing dependencies, or the actual peak-hour pattern across each regime.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law relates mean occupancy, throughput, and mean time in a consistently defined system. It does not require Poisson arrivals or exponential service. For finite windows, the central issue is matching occupancy area to case residence times, including boundary contributions; sufficiently well-accounted finite sample paths can support the relationship without a homogeneous stationary model. ([Project Production Institute][1])

For an opening-time window,

$$
H_R=\int_R N(u)\,du
\approx12\sum_R mean\_wip
$$

has units of case-opening-hours. It equals the sum of residence-time contributions **clipped to that window**. Dividing by departures does not generally recover complete flow times: unfinished cases contribute occupancy, while departing carry-in cases have pre-window residence omitted. Equal opening and closing inventory does not eliminate these boundary-age effects. ([Project Production Institute][1])

No supplied window merits an unconditional `applicable` verdict. The two early-window values are **conditional diagnostics**, not measured mean case durations or confidence bounds. Their difference also reflects different calendar composition.

These calculations use departures because they count observed exits, with arrivals providing a sensitivity check in nearly balanced windows. Neither is preferred merely because it produces a more attractive duration.

The estimates cannot be converted to calendar hours by multiplying by two: overnight exposure varies by case. Nor can median service be subtracted from them to obtain mean waiting time.

# 7. Operational implications

**Preserve differentiated staffing while evaluating the intervention.** The higher-staffing observations are consistent with improved clearance within both day types, but do not establish a permanent staffing requirement. Use separate weekday/weekend and workload scenarios, and obtain mean labor demand before estimating utilization or selecting an FTE target.

A particularly risky pooled policy would be **cutting weekend staffing because weekend volumes and WIP are low**. Arrival pressure per staff-hour is comparable to weekdays, handling times are not shorter, the high-mix pre-intervention weekend records \(+12\) net inflow, and the higher-staffing weekend merely balances its counts. Another risky policy would be rolling back the intervention because pooled completions/day fell: that conclusion reverses after calendar stratification.

**Manage recovery separately from steady service.** Reconcile actual outstanding cases and their ages before setting a clearance target. Do not treat April 16’s 129 completions as repeatable normal capacity. A recovery dashboard should distinguish new arrivals, older-case completions, remaining labor, and age-tail reduction; falling service quantiles alone could reflect clearing easier cases while difficult cases remain.

**Validate peak timing before moving shifts.** The vectors suggest important 10:00–11:00 demand and, on April 21, a second 13:00–14:00 burst. The daily field instead claims strong 08:00–10:00 concentration. Resolve the timestamp/window discrepancy before instituting a blanket morning staffing rule. Then test staggered coverage or controlled arrival smoothing against both within-day peaks and next-day carry-over.

**Repair measurement before calibrating a queueing model.** Collect consistent case-level entries and all exits—including transfers and cancellations—plus 07:00/19:00 inventory, lifecycle start/end times, interrupted-work states, resource availability, coding versions, and exact outage/intervention timestamps. Preserve unfinished cases rather than restricting analysis to same-day completions. With more weeks, evaluate interventions using matched calendar/workload conditions and time-blocked comparisons, not randomly split daily rows.

def summarize(g: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:

        raise ValueError("Cannot summarize an empty window")

        "A/h": arrivals / hours, "D/h": departures / hours,

# Transparent one-split least-squares heuristic; five days minimum per side.
# This detects a level shift in the REPORTED complexity field, not its cause.
y = daily["complex_pct"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
min_days = 5
sse_unsplit = ((y - y.mean()) ** 2).sum()
candidates = []
for k in range(min_days, len(y) - min_days + 1):
    left, right = y[:k], y[k:]
    sse = ((left - left.mean()) ** 2).sum() + ((right - right.mean()) ** 2).sum()
    candidates.append({"new_regime_from": daily.date.iloc[k].date(),
                       "left_mean_pct": left.mean(), "right_mean_pct": right.mean(),
                       "SSE": sse, "SSE_reduction_pct": 100 * (1 - sse / sse_unsplit)})
show("TOP THREE HEURISTIC SPLITS (no p-values or causal interpretation)",
     pd.DataFrame(candidates).sort_values("SSE").head(3).set_index("new_regime_from"))
