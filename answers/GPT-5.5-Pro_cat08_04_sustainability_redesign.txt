## 1. System boundary and assumptions

The environmental scope is **order-level operational impact inside the spare-parts fulfilment process**, from `Receive order` through `Close` or `Cancel due to delay`. I include impacts that are directly represented in the enriched event log: production and handling energy through `energy_kwh`, transport emissions through `transport_kgco2e`, material loss through `material_scrap_kg`, packaging material through `package_material_kg`, and indirect waste from `rework_flag`, packaging rework, returns, obsolescence, and cancellations due to delay. This scope is grounded in the supplied event attributes, DFG, path-level sustainability evidence, batching evidence, transport evidence, and conformance findings. 

Inside the boundary:

* Production routing: `Plan production`, `Batch wait`, `CNC production`, `Heat treatment`, `Inspection`, `Rework production`, and `Emergency production`.
* Stock fulfilment routing: `Availability check`, `Pick from stock`, `Partial shipment`, `Backorder wait`, and cancellation due to delay.
* Packaging decisions: `Package standard`, `Package reinforced`, and `Rework packaging`.
* Transport choices: `Ship ground`, `Ship air`, `Dedicated van`, and `Consolidated truck`.
* Service, quality, and resilience outcomes: cycle time, late delivery, rework/return risk, promised lead time, customer segment, criticality, and policy violations.

Outside the boundary:

* Upstream supplier manufacturing of spare parts before the distributor receives or stocks them, except where obsolescence is visible as batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days.
* Full vehicle lifecycle, warehouse building emissions, employee commuting, customer-side emissions, and long-term product-use emissions.
* Monetary cost, except indirectly through service deterioration, rework, customer contact, and resilience risk.

Key assumptions:

* I treat **Critical-downtime** as a service-protection segment where some high-emission actions may be justified, but only when the case is truly downtime-critical and documented.
* I do not assume that the lowest-emission path is always acceptable. For example, forcing all critical orders to ground transport would reduce emissions but would create unacceptable service and resilience risk.
* I treat rework, packaging incompatibility, missed inspection defects, and obsolescence as environmental impacts, not merely quality problems.
* I assume that the enriched log can be used to estimate route reliability and due-date slack, but that causal effects should be validated after implementation rather than inferred only from historical associations.
* Remaining uncertainty is high around true customer damage from late delivery, exact return flows, the environmental weight of obsolescence, and whether some air or dedicated-van shipments were caused by disruptions not fully encoded in the discovered model.

## 3. Intervention options

### S1. Green shipping recommender using promised lead time, route reliability, and customer segment

**Target path or segment:**
Primarily Standard-stock Service-contract orders and Made-to-order orders where `Ship air` is selected despite sufficient slack. It also supports Critical-downtime decisions by distinguishing true emergencies from avoidable expedited transport.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
The recommender would apply SP1 operationally: use ground or consolidated truck when the promised lead time can still be met with probability above 85%. This directly targets avoidable air shipments. The path evidence says stock air is fast but high-emission, with average transport impact of 18.5 kgCO2e versus 3.6 kgCO2e for stock-ground. Standard-stock Service-contract also has higher average transport emissions than Standard-stock Regular, suggesting avoidable service-contract expediting.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Service should remain acceptable if the rule uses promised slack and route reliability rather than a blanket ban on air. Risk is concentrated in cases with unreliable ground service, disruption periods, or tight promises. Resilience improves if the recommender keeps fallback routes available rather than eliminating air capacity entirely.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
SP1 compliance, late-delivery rate after route substitution, p90 cycle time by customer segment, route reliability by carrier and lane, override frequency, and whether air reductions create more cancellations or customer escalations.

---

### S2. Dynamic batching for CNC and heat treatment with maximum waiting-time caps by segment

**Target path or segment:**
Made-to-order Regular and Service-contract cases using `Batch wait -> CNC production`, plus heat-treatment batches.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
CNC_A energy per item falls from 2.8 kWh at batch size 1–3 to 1.3 kWh at batch size 12–20, and heat treatment is lowest-energy above batch size 10. Dynamic batching would keep the environmental benefit of batching while preventing excessive waiting.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
A naïve large-batch rule would worsen service because median batch wait rises from 4h to 38h and heat-treatment waiting is associated with 1.7x higher late-delivery risk. Therefore, the rule should use segment-specific caps: tighter caps for Service-contract and due-soon orders, more batching for Regular Made-to-order orders with slack. Resilience improves if emergency capacity is preserved for critical work rather than consumed by oversized batches.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Batch size distribution, median and p90 `Batch wait`, late-delivery rate by batch size and segment, energy per order, heat-treatment queue age, and obsolescence rate for batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days.

---

### S3. Shift Made-to-order production from CNC_B to CNC_A when queue and due-date slack allow it

**Target path or segment:**
Made-to-order paths currently using CNC_B, especially the variant `MTO -> batch wait -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework -> ground`.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
CNC_B is faster but has a higher rework rate: 18% versus 9% on CNC_A for Made-to-order cases. The CNC_B rework-heavy path has high energy, high scrap, long median cycle time, and 32% late delivery. Moving suitable work to CNC_A reduces rework production loops, scrap, and repeated energy consumption.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Quality likely improves. Service may improve for rework-prone jobs, despite CNC_A possibly being slower, because fewer cases loop through rework. However, shifting too much work to CNC_A could overload CNC_A and create new waiting. Resilience requires CNC_B to remain available as backup for urgent or disruption cases.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Rework rate by `machine_group`, part family, operator/resource if available, CNC_A queue length, CNC_B queue length, due-date slack, energy per completed non-reworked item, and late rate for shifted jobs.

---

### S4. Replace air for Standard-stock Service-contract orders with priority pick + ground unless slack is insufficient

**Target path or segment:**
Standard-stock Service-contract orders, where air is often used even when the promised lead time still allows ground plus priority picking.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
This targets a clearly identified avoidable-emissions pattern. Air cuts median transport time by 24h but emits about 4–6x more kgCO2e per order. Priority picking attacks the internal waiting component while preserving lower-emission ground transport.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Service should remain acceptable for cases with enough slack. The risk is that warehouse priority picking displaces Regular orders or creates local queue instability. It should not be used so broadly that “priority” becomes the default and loses meaning.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Air-to-ground substitution rate, pick waiting time, promised-lead-time slack at routing decision, late rate for substituted cases, late rate for non-priority cases, and customer escalations or cancellations.

---

### S5. Consolidated truck departures twice daily for non-urgent stock orders

**Target path or segment:**
Standard-stock Regular and non-urgent Service-contract orders with sufficient promised lead-time slack.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
Consolidated trucks reduce transport kgCO2e by about 35–55% versus individual ground parcels but require a 12–24h consolidation wait. Twice-daily departures reduce the waiting penalty compared with a once-daily or ad hoc consolidation rule.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
For non-urgent stock orders, service deterioration may be small if consolidation is aligned with promise dates. For tight Service-contract orders, the wait may create late delivery. Resilience improves if consolidated truck is an additional route, not the only route, and if parcel ground remains available during disruption.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Consolidation wait, load factor or shipment count per departure, late rate by consolidation eligibility, p90 cycle time, fallback-route use, and R1 alternative-route availability.

---

### S6. Improve packaging-route compatibility checks before shipment

**Target path or segment:**
`Package standard -> Rework packaging`, `Package reinforced -> Rework packaging`, and routes where packaging is incompatible with route mode or part geometry.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
Packaging rework consumes additional packaging material, handling energy, and time. The DFG shows substantial rework packaging flows: 260 from standard packaging and 360 from reinforced packaging, with loops back to package reinforced or standard. A compatibility check before shipment prevents avoidable repacking.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Service improves by preventing late repacking loops. Quality improves because packaging is better matched to part geometry and route. Material impact should fall by reducing rework, although some cases may receive reinforced packaging earlier. The rule must respect Q1: Made-to-order parts over 8 kg need reinforced packaging unless inspection certifies low damage risk.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Packaging-rework rate, route-mode/package mismatch rate, package material kg/order, damage-related return rate, Q1 compliance, and cycle-time delay from the check itself.

---

### S7. Add inspection quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations

**Target path or segment:**
High-rework Made-to-order combinations, especially CNC_B, rushed inspection queues, and cases that later enter `Rework production`.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
Inspection_cell queues are responsible for many rework-production loops because rushed inspections miss defects. A targeted quality gate prevents defective parts from moving downstream into packaging, shipping, customer returns, and repeat production. This reduces scrap, energy, transport waste, and customer disruption.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Quality should improve. Cycle time may increase for targeted high-risk combinations, but total cycle time may decrease if fewer cases loop through rework. The gate should be risk-based, not universal, to avoid turning inspection into a bottleneck.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Inspection queue length, inspection duration, rework rate after inspection, missed-defect returns, scrap kg/order, late delivery among gated versus non-gated cases, and p90 cycle time.

---

### S8. Replace some dedicated vans with shared emergency courier lanes

**Target path or segment:**
Critical-downtime Service-contract orders currently using `Emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van`.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
Dedicated vans protect downtime service but have very high transport impact and often return empty. Shared emergency courier lanes preserve urgency while improving utilization and reducing empty returns.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
This can reduce emissions without treating all dedicated vans as unjustified. True downtime cases may still need dedicated vans. Precautionary urgent cases and compatible critical shipments can use shared emergency lanes. Resilience improves if shared lanes add options, but worsens if dedicated vans are removed entirely.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Dedicated-van justification rate, SP2 compliance, transport kgCO2e/order for critical cases, median and p90 critical cycle time, late rate, empty-return proxy if available, and carrier disruption performance.

---

### S9. Split Critical-downtime into true downtime and precautionary urgent categories

**Target path or segment:**
Critical-downtime Service-contract cases, especially those using emergency production and dedicated vans.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
The current Critical-downtime segment has very high transport emissions, averaging 38.0 kgCO2e/order. The path `Critical -> emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van` has average transport emissions of 52.0 kgCO2e. Splitting true downtime from precautionary urgent prevents overuse of emergency production and dedicated vans for cases that can tolerate shared courier, air, ground exception, or priority pick.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
True downtime service is protected. Precautionary urgent cases may receive slightly longer but still controlled lead times. Resilience improves because emergency resources remain available for genuinely critical cases.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Critical subcategory assignment, emergency production use rate, dedicated-van use rate, late delivery by subcategory, customer downtime incidents if available, SP2 justification, and override outcomes.

---

### S10. Introduce carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular Made-to-order customers

**Target path or segment:**
Made-to-order Regular orders, especially those otherwise routed to immediate CNC and air shipment.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
The immediate MTO air path has lower waiting but higher energy and transport impact: 24.0 kWh and 23.0 kgCO2e on average. Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation gives enough slack for efficient batching, ground transport, or consolidation without forcing service deterioration after the promise is already made.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
This should reduce late-delivery pressure and reduce rush decisions. It may be unacceptable for Service-contract or true critical cases, so it should be mainly Regular Made-to-order. Customer behavior risk exists: customers may request artificially urgent promises if green options are framed as slower but not valued.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Accepted promise dates, customer segment, route mode after negotiation, batch wait, late rate versus original and negotiated promises, cancellation due to delay, and customer acceptance of greener lead times.

---

### S11. Reduce partial shipments by changing availability-check rules

**Target path or segment:**
Stock orders entering `Partial shipment -> Backorder wait`, especially the path `Stock -> partial shipment -> ground + backorder`.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
Partial shipment creates second shipment emissions, extra packaging, customer contact, and delay risk. The partial-shipment path has higher transport impact than stock-ground, higher energy, higher scrap, a 79h median cycle time, and 24% late delivery. Improved availability checks can delay or combine shipments when service remains acceptable, or split only when the first shipment has real customer value.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
For some customers, reducing partial shipments may worsen perceived service if they need one item immediately. Therefore, the rule should distinguish “partial shipment prevents downtime” from “partial shipment merely creates extra transport.” Resilience may improve by reducing operational complexity during disruptions.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Partial-shipment rate, second-shipment frequency, late rate, cancellation due to delay after backorder, transport kgCO2e/order, package material kg/order, and customer segment/service impact.

---

### S12. Create a sustainability exception log for SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides

**Target path or segment:**
All cases where lower-impact routing or standard policy is overridden: air despite slack, dedicated van, non-reinforced packaging for heavy MTO parts, emergency production, and urgent partial shipment.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement:**
This does not directly reduce emissions by itself, but it makes avoidable exceptions visible and auditable. It also prevents hidden displacement, such as claiming a route was urgent when the promised lead time allowed ground.

**Likely service/quality/resilience effect:**
Service should not deteriorate because justified exceptions remain allowed. Quality improves if Q1 overrides require inspection certification. Resilience improves because exceptions can document disruption context and alternative-route availability.

**Required process-mining evidence to monitor:**
Override reason, approving role/resource, segment, route mode, promised slack, conformance to SP1/SP2/Q1, post-override lateness, rework/return outcome, and repeated exception patterns by lane, product, or customer.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching rebound:** Larger CNC or heat-treatment batches reduce energy per item, but they increase `Batch wait`, raise late-delivery risk, and can create obsolescence. The evidence already shows median batch wait rising up to 38h for larger CNC_A batches and 6% of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong.

2. **Transport displacement from air to ground:** Replacing air with ground reduces emissions, but if applied without promised-lead-time slack and route reliability, it may increase late deliveries, cancellations due to delay, or customer escalations. This is especially risky for Service-contract and Critical-downtime orders.

3. **Dedicated-van reduction risk:** Cutting dedicated vans can reduce very high transport emissions, but a blanket ban would harm true Critical-downtime service. It may also reduce resilience if shared lanes fail during carrier disruption and no fallback remains.

4. **Packaging material trade-off:** Reinforced packaging doubles package material, so using it everywhere would increase material use. However, underusing it for heavy Made-to-order parts violates Q1 and correlates with return/rework loops. The right policy is conditional, not minimal packaging at all costs.

5. **Inspection bottleneck rebound:** Adding inspection gates can reduce rework and scrap, but if the gate is universal or under-resourced, it can create queues, rushed inspections, or late delivery. The current evidence already indicates that inspection_cell queues contribute to missed defects and later loops.

6. **Partial-shipment displacement:** Reducing partial shipments lowers second-shipment emissions and packaging, but if customers need one part immediately, holding the whole order can create service harm. Some partial shipments may prevent downtime and should remain allowed.

7. **Priority-picking spillover:** Replacing air with priority pick plus ground can preserve service with lower transport emissions, but priority picking may delay Regular stock orders if too many Service-contract orders are escalated internally.

8. **Carbon-aware promise-date behavior:** Offering greener, slower promises to Regular Made-to-order customers may work when customers accept them. But some customers may start requesting urgent service by default if they perceive green lead times as second-class service.

9. **Machine-shift congestion:** Moving work from CNC_B to CNC_A reduces rework for suitable cases, but excessive shifting can overload CNC_A, increasing waiting and late delivery. CNC_B should remain available for urgent or compatible work.

10. **Model precision risk during disruptions:** The discovered model has low precision because emergency paths and partial shipments combine in many ways during disruptions. A rigid sustainability rule may misclassify legitimate disruption behavior as nonconforming or block necessary exceptions.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

I would implement a **segmented green-control redesign** rather than a single “lowest-emission route” policy.

The recommended package is:

1. **Slack-based transport control:** Deploy S1 and S4 together. Use a green shipping recommender to replace avoidable air, especially in Standard-stock Service-contract cases where SP1 is currently violated, but keep air available when promised lead time, route reliability, or disruption evidence justifies it.

2. **Controlled consolidation for non-urgent stock:** Add S5 for Standard-stock Regular and slack-rich Service-contract orders. Twice-daily consolidated truck departures should be eligible only when the 12–24h consolidation wait does not threaten the promise.

3. **Critical-order triage and emergency transport redesign:** Combine S8 and S9. Split Critical-downtime into true downtime and precautionary urgent. True downtime can still use emergency production and dedicated vans with documented SP2 justification. Precautionary urgent orders should move to shared emergency courier lanes, air, or priority ground depending on slack and route reliability.

4. **Dynamic production batching with service caps:** Implement S2 for CNC and heat treatment. Use larger batches only where due-date slack supports them. Do not optimize only energy per item; optimize energy, waiting, lateness, and obsolescence together.

5. **Quality-first waste reduction:** Implement S3, S6, and S7 as the main material-waste controls. Shift rework-prone MTO jobs from CNC_B to CNC_A when feasible, add targeted inspection gates for high-risk machine/part combinations, and prevent packaging-route incompatibility before shipment. This attacks hidden material waste without simply reducing packaging below safe levels.

6. **Partial-shipment discipline:** Implement S11 with a customer-value rule. Partial shipment should be used when the first shipment materially protects service or downtime, not as a default response to poor availability checking.

7. **Exception governance:** Implement S12 across SP1, SP2, and Q1. Sustainability policies must permit justified overrides, but those overrides must be visible, reason-coded, and monitored.

This package balances the evidence as follows:

* It targets the largest avoidable transport impacts without banning air or dedicated vans.
* It reduces production energy through batching, but prevents the main batching failure modes: waiting, lateness, and obsolescence.
* It treats rework and packaging loops as environmental hotspots, not just operational defects.
* It preserves Service-contract and Critical-downtime resilience by maintaining alternative routes and documented emergency exceptions.
* It avoids shifting environmental impact from transport to production, from packaging reduction to returns, or from batching efficiency to obsolete inventory.

The highest-priority starting points are **S1/S4**, **S6/S7**, **S8/S9**, and **S2**. These directly address the strongest supplied evidence: avoidable air use in Standard-stock Service-contract orders, packaging incompatibility and Q1-related returns, high-emission dedicated vans without complete justification, and the energy-versus-waiting trade-off in CNC and heat-treatment batching.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

Validation should compare pre- and post-implementation event logs using segment-level, path-level, and policy-level evidence. A simple before/after average is not enough because the redesign changes routing, batching, and exception behavior.

**1. Establish a baseline by segment and path**

Before rollout, freeze baseline metrics for:

* Standard-stock Regular.
* Standard-stock Service-contract.
* Made-to-order Regular.
* Made-to-order Service-contract.
* Critical-downtime Service-contract, later split into true downtime and precautionary urgent.

For each segment, track median and p90 cycle time, late delivery, rework, cancellation due to delay, energy kWh/order, transport kgCO2e/order, material scrap kg/order, package material kg/order, route-mode mix, and variant distribution.

**2. Monitor conformance to SP1, SP2, and Q1**

Create explicit conformance checks:

* **SP1:** For every air, dedicated van, or non-consolidated route, record whether ground or consolidated truck could still meet the promised lead time with probability above 85%. Report violation rate by segment and customer type.
* **SP2:** For every dedicated van, require a Critical-downtime justification event. Track missing justification, repeated approvers, lane patterns, and post-shipment service outcomes.
* **Q1:** For Made-to-order parts over 8 kg, verify reinforced packaging unless inspection certifies low damage risk. Track Q1 violations against returns, packaging rework, and customer complaints if available.

**3. Use guardrail metrics, not only green KPIs**

Each sustainability metric should have service and quality guardrails:

* Transport kgCO2e/order may fall only if late delivery and p90 cycle time remain within acceptable segment-specific limits.
* Energy kWh/order may fall only if batch wait, obsolescence, and cancellation due to delay do not rise.
* Package material kg/order may fall only if damage-related returns and rework packaging do not rise.
* Dedicated-van use may fall only if true Critical-downtime late delivery and disruption performance remain protected.

**4. Validate candidate interventions with controlled rollout**

Use staged rollout where feasible:

* Apply the green shipping recommender first to Standard-stock Service-contract lanes with high SP1 violation and reliable ground performance.
* Pilot dynamic batching on selected CNC_A and heat-treatment queues with explicit waiting caps.
* Pilot CNC_B-to-CNC_A shifting only for part families with historically high CNC_B rework and enough due-date slack.
* Pilot shared emergency courier lanes on precautionary urgent critical cases, not true downtime cases.

Compare matched or stratified cohorts by segment, lane, part family, promised lead time, route reliability, machine group, and batch size.

**5. Monitor process-mining views after rollout**

Use the event log to maintain:

* Frequency DFGs to detect route substitution, new loops, or new bottlenecks.
* Performance DFGs to see whether waiting moved from transport to picking, batching, inspection, or packaging.
* Variant analysis to identify new exception paths.
* Conformance diagnostics for SP1/SP2/Q1.
* Queue and waiting analysis for `Batch wait`, `Inspection`, `Backorder wait`, and packaging.
* Rework-loop analysis for `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production` and `Rework packaging -> Package reinforced/standard`.

**6. Govern exceptions explicitly**

Every override should include:

* Case type and customer type.
* Promise-date slack at decision time.
* Route reliability estimate.
* Sustainability policy affected: SP1, SP2, or Q1.
* Reason code: true downtime, disruption, customer contractual requirement, quality risk, part geometry, carrier outage, or approved management exception.
* Approver or responsible role.
* Outcome: delivered on time, late, reworked, returned, cancelled, or closed normally.

Exception governance should not punish legitimate emergency decisions. Its purpose is to distinguish **necessary high-impact service protection** from **habitual avoidable expediting**.

**7. Sensitivity analysis**

Regularly test whether recommendations remain valid under different assumptions:

* Ground reliability drops during carrier disruption.
* Demand forecasts worsen and batch obsolescence rises.
* Inspection capacity becomes constrained.
* Critical-downtime classification is overused.
* Customers reject longer green promise dates.
* Consolidated truck wait approaches 24h instead of 12h.
* CNC_A becomes overloaded after shifting work away from CNC_B.

The redesign should be considered successful only if it reduces environmental impact while keeping segment-level service, quality, and resilience within agreed guardrails.
