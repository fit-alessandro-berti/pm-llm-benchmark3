## 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Environmental scope:** an operational, order-to-delivery lifecycle view for spare-part orders, using the enriched event log as the primary evidence base.

**Inside the boundary**

- Order receipt through availability check, stock picking, production planning, emergency production, batching, CNC work, heat treatment, inspection, rework loops, packaging, rework packaging, shipping, delivery confirmation, cancellation due to delay, and close.
- Environmental attributes visible in the log: `energy_kwh`, `material_scrap_kg`, `package_material_kg`, `transport_kgco2e`, `batch_id`, `batch_size`, `route_mode`, `machine_group`, `rework_flag`, `expedite_flag`, and `promised_lead_time_hours`.
- Waste from rework loops, packaging rework, partial shipments, backorders, and batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days, because these generate hidden energy, material, handling, or obsolescence burdens.

**Outside the boundary**

- Upstream extraction and supplier manufacturing of purchased parts, inbound supplier logistics, customer use phase, end-of-life treatment, office/IT emissions, and returns that are not linked to the order event log.
- Full COe conversion of `energy_kwh` is not attempted because emission factors are not supplied. Energy and transport kgCOe are treated as separate indicators.

**Key assumptions and uncertainty**

- The log is flattened by order, so multi-line orders can obscure which line item caused partial shipment, rework, or extra transport.
- Route reliability, carrier disruption states, van empty-return distances, package geometry, and true customer downtime cost are not fully observed.
- The discovered process model has low precision because emergency paths and partial shipment combinations are heterogeneous. Recommendations should therefore be validated by segment and path, not by one global average.
- There is no single hidden ground-truth optimum; the objective is a defensible multi-criteria redesign that reduces impact without unacceptable service, quality, or resilience loss.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching-to-latency rebound:** Larger CNC_A or heat-treatment batches lower energy per item, but batch wait can rise from 4h to 38h and heat-treatment waiting has 1.7x higher late-delivery risk. If lateness triggers air shipment, emergency production, or cancellation, the energy gain is displaced into transport emissions, scrap, and lost service.

2. **Batching-to-obsolescence displacement:** Large production batches can leave 6% of batch-produced items unshipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong. This converts apparent energy efficiency into inventory obsolescence and eventual material waste.

3. **Air reduction to expedited production rebound:** Replacing air with ground is beneficial only if promise slack is real. If later recovery actions use emergency production, overtime, or another air shipment, emissions and scrap can return through a different path.

4. **Consolidation-to-missed-promise rebound:** Consolidated trucks reduce transport emissions by 35–55%, but 12–24h waits can push orders past promise, causing customer escalation, expedite flags, or use of dedicated vans.

5. **Packaging-material displacement:** Reinforced packaging reduces damage-related returns by 40% for heavy Made-to-order parts but doubles package material. Universal reinforced packaging would reduce returns but increase material impact; universal lightweight packaging would reduce material but increase Q1 violations, rework, and returns.

6. **Inspection-gate bottleneck:** Adding inspection quality checks can reduce rework loops, but if `inspection_cell` queues grow, rushed inspections may reappear downstream, increasing `Inspection -> Rework production` loops, packaging rework, or late delivery.

7. **Machine-shift capacity displacement:** Moving CNC_B work to CNC_A lowers rework rate, but if CNC_A queues become long, planners may revert to CNC_B or use emergency production. The environmental benefit depends on avoiding both rework and waiting.

8. **Partial-shipment reduction risk:** Fewer partial shipments reduce duplicate transport and customer contact, but holding orders for full availability can increase late delivery or `Cancel due to delay`, especially during disruptions where splitting was a resilience mechanism.

9. **Customer behavior rebound:** Carbon-aware promise dates may encourage customers to request tighterDelivery dates to preserve perceived service, or to escalate when a slower green option is offered. This can increase air, express courier, or expedite production. Behavior must be monitored through accepted promise dates, later change requests, and expedite flags.

10. **Route consolidation resilience displacement:** Moving many orders to consolidated trucks or shared emergency lanes improves utilization but can reduce route diversity. For Service-contract customers, R1 requires at least one alternative route during carrier disruption.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a **service-tiered low-impact redesign**, not a blanket lowest-emission policy.

1. **Use slack-based green routing first.**  
   Deploy S1 with S4 and S5: replace avoidable air for Standard-stock Service-contract orders with priority pick plus ground or consolidated truck where SP1 on-time probability remains above 85%. Keep air where slack is insufficient, and keep alternate routes available for Service-contract resilience.

2. **Make batching conditional, not maximal.**  
   Deploy S2 with segment-specific waiting caps. Use larger CNC_A batches only when due-date slack is sufficient and obsolescence risk is low. Avoid large heat-treatment batches where late risk rises materially. For uncertain Made-to-order demand, prefer smaller or capped batches over maximum energy-per-item efficiency.

3. **Reduce rework before chasing transport or packaging savings.**  
   Deploy S3 and S7 together: prefer CNC_A when queue and slack allow, and add inspection gates for high-rework machine/part combinations. This addresses the 470 inspection-to-rework transitions and 390 rework-to-CNC loops, where energy, scrap, and cycle time compound.

4. **Right-size packaging and enforce compatibility.**  
   Deploy S6 with Q1. Reinforced packaging remains required for Made-to-order parts over 8 kg unless inspection certifies low damage risk, but compatibility checks should prevent `Rework packaging` loops and avoid reinforced packaging where it is not needed.

5. **Protect true critical service.**  
   Deploy S9 before S8. Split true downtime from precautionary urgent cases. True Critical-downtime orders may still justify emergency production and dedicated vans despite high transport emissions. Precautionary urgent cases can move to shared lanes or stock/ground where service evidence permits. Require SP2 justification for every dedicated van.

6. **Reduce avoidable split and promise failures.**  
   Deploy S11 and S10 for Regular and Made-to-order segments: tighten availability rules for partial shipments and negotiate realistic, carbon-aware promise dates instead of relying on late recovery actions.

7. **Govern trade-offs explicitly.**  
   Deploy S12 as the control layer. Every override of SP1, SP2, or Q1 should record reason, approver, affected segment, promised slack, expected service benefit, and estimated emissions consequence.

This package balances objectives because it removes the clearest waste first—avoidable air, rework, packaging rework, partial-shipment duplication, and unjustified van use—while preserving urgent transport where service or resilience requirements genuinely demand it.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

**Validation design**

- Establish a baseline by `case_type`, `customer_type`, path variant, `route_mode`, `machine_group`, `batch_size`, and rework status. Do not rely only on global averages because emergency and partial-shipment variants are heterogeneous.
- Pilot interventions in controlled segments first:
  - S4/S5 on Standard-stock Regular and Standard-stock Service-contract orders with verified slack.
  - S3/S7 on Made-to-order orders with CNC_B or high rework history.
  - S2 only where due-date slack and obsolescence exposure can be bounded.
  - S8/S9 only after an urgency-class attribute is reliable.
- Use matched comparisons where possible: compare similar orders with and without the intervention by promised lead time, segment, route, machine group, and disruption state.

**Conformance monitoring**

- **SP1:** Track air shipments where ground or consolidated truck could have met promise with >85% probability. Monitor violations by segment and require reason codes for justified exceptions.
- **SP2:** Require explicit Critical-downtime justification for every `dedicated_van` event. Alert on missing justification and review recurring approver or route patterns.
- **Q1:** Monitor Made-to-order parts over 8 kg for reinforced packaging or inspection-certified low damage risk. Correlate violations with return, packaging rework, and damage events.

**Segment-level service monitoring**

- Track median and p90 cycle time, late delivery, cancellation due to delay, partial shipment, expedite flag, and customer escalation by the full segment matrix: Standard-stock Regular, Standard-stock Service-contract, Made-to-order Regular, Made-to-order Service-contract, and Critical-downtime Service-contract.
- Set agreed guardrails before rollout, especially for Critical-downtime and Service-contract orders; any degradation should trigger rollback or exception review rather than being absorbed into average performance.

**Environmental and quality monitoring**

- Validate that energy reductions persist after including rework loops and cancellations.
- Validate that transport reductions are not offset by later air, van, or emergency production events.
- Monitor `material_scrap_kg`, `package_material_kg`, packaging rework, inspection-to-rework loops, and 14-day unshipped batch share.
- Track route diversity and alternative-route availability during disruption events to satisfy R1.

**Exception governance**

- Review exception logs weekly for repeat SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides, especially where the claimed service benefit is not supported by late-delivery avoidance.
- Require time-limited approvals for new exception patterns, with explicit review of emissions, service, quality, and resilience impacts.
- Feed exception reasons back into the recommender, batching caps, promise-date logic, and package compatibility rules so that recurring exceptions become process improvements rather than permanent side channels.
