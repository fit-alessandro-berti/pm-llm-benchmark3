- **G1:** exact start and end equality at second precision, with nearly immediate individual records; strongest fit is a machine fixture/cycle batch.
- **G2:** common start plus increasingly staggered ends; canonical start-batch signature.
- **G3:** staggered starts plus common end and common subsequent insertion; canonical end-batch/submission signature.
- **G4:** exact adjacency, no overlap; canonical serial flow.

# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

Definitions used below:

- **Observed waiting time:** `observed start  queue_arrival`.
- **Observed service time:** `complete  start`.
- **Observed resource utilization:** occupied interval time divided by available time, subject to a specified capacity model.
- **Bottleneck detection:** identifying a step/resource whose capacity constrains queue, delay, throughput, or work-in-process.

These are estimates from event semantics, not direct measurements of physical work. In particular, a lifecycle field can represent a UI action, a group-level action, a rounded time, or an actual execution boundary.

### G1 — RBT-7 fixture batch

- **Waiting time:** Item-level observed waits are 6, 5, 4, and 2 minutes. If the cell genuinely waits for a four-item fixture, these waits partly reflect **batch formation**, not lack of productive capacity. Treating each case independently can make earlier arrivals look disadvantaged even though they are waiting for an economically or technically required batch.
- **Service time:** Each item receives an observed three-minute service interval. Summing across cases gives 12 item-minutes, while the machine cycle is only three elapsed minutes. For machine occupancy, the relevant cycle occupancy is about three minutes, not 12; for item-level lead time, three minutes can remain valid.
- **Utilization:** Naively adding overlapping item intervals can produce utilization above 100% for a single machine. Capacity must be modeled as a four-position fixture or one batch-capable cell. Utilization can be calculated either as fixture-cycle utilization or as occupied item-slots divided by available item-slots.
- **Bottleneck detection:** Case-level activity counts may overstate RBT-7’s elapsed workload if each item is treated as a separate serial job. Conversely, ignoring fill waiting can hide a batching-policy bottleneck. Distinguish cycle capacity, fixture fill rate, and machine cycle time.

### G2 — T-B group start

- **Waiting time:** Observed waiting is 18, 14, 11, and 6 minutes. Because all starts are likely a single “begin group” action, these are best interpreted as **wait to group initiation**, not necessarily wait to individual inspection. If individual work began later than 10:00, true per-item waiting is overstated or unknown.
- **Service time:** Observed service times are 8, 14, 21, and 27 minutes. Since all start at the same group action, later-completed items have mechanically longer apparent service. This likely inflates item-level service time, especially for S03 and S04, and can manufacture overlap/multitasking.
- **Utilization:** Union-of-intervals suggests T-B is continuously occupied from 10:00 to 10:27. That may be reasonable at screen/workstation level, but summing item durations overstates one-person utilization. If the worker inspected items serially after group start, actual item work may be sequential within the window.
- **Bottleneck detection:** A model may identify inspection as long-service and highly parallel, or as a one-person resource with impossible utilization, depending on aggregation. The more defensible conclusion is that the current start field is insufficient for item-level capacity inference.

### G3 — T-C common submission/end

- **Waiting time:** Observed waiting is 22, 18, 19, and 19 minutes. These are less distorted than service time if starts are genuine, although a form workflow may still make “start” a data-entry action rather than the first review action.
- **Service time:** Observed durations are 28, 20, 13, and 6 minutes. If physical reviews were performed throughout the half-hour and only submitted at 11:30, the common end inflates service for cases completed earlier. It also compresses completion throughput into a false 11:30 burst.
- **Utilization:** Interval union indicates occupation from 11:02 to 11:30. That may approximate the folder-review session, but overlapping individual case intervals should not be summed as independent concurrent work without validation. The resource may be one reviewer working serially, a reviewer switching among cases, or multiple review tasks under one interface.
- **Bottleneck detection:** End batching can create a false downstream bottleneck at 11:30 and false upstream starvation before 11:30. It may also obscure when review capacity actually became available. Use submission time for audit/conformance milestones, not necessarily for operational completion.

### G4 — serial pack flow

- **Waiting time:** Observed waits rise from 7 minutes to 19 minutes as arrivals accumulate: P01=7, P02=10, P03=13, P04=16, P05=19. This is consistent with a one-capacity serial pack station and a queue arriving faster than the observed four-minute service cadence.
- **Service time:** Four minutes per item appears credible because starts and ends are second-resolution and each completion is followed by the next start. It still measures scanner-defined process boundaries, not necessarily all physical handling, but it is operationally useful.
- **Utilization:** The observed resource is occupied continuously from 12:05 to 12:25, with no gaps. Over the displayed active span, utilization is near 100%; over a wider shift it cannot be estimated without availability/schedule data.
- **Bottleneck detection:** This is strong evidence of a serial capacity constraint during the observed interval. However, five cases are too few to establish a persistent bottleneck across shifts or products. Queue growth may also originate upstream if the arrival burst is exceptional.

### G5 — analyst_pool shared login

- **Waiting time:** Observed waits are 15, 15, 16, and 15 minutes. These could be genuine team-queue waits, but cannot be assigned to an individual analyst. If each analyst independently starts tasks, the pool-level wait may be valid while individual workload attribution is not.
- **Service time:** Observed durations are 25, 15, 20, and 10 minutes. They may represent real work spans, multitasking spans, time including interruptions, or work by different analysts. They should not be interpreted as serial person-minutes for a single worker.
- **Utilization:** If `analyst_pool` is mistakenly modeled as one server, overlapping intervals yield impossible utilization and false apparent multitasking. If treated as a pool of up to three analysts, concurrency becomes plausible, but exact capacity is still uncertain because “up to three” is not an attendance record.
- **Bottleneck detection:** A single-resource model can falsely flag analyst_pool as overloaded. A pooled-resource model can understate bottlenecks if the actual number of active analysts was one. Robust reporting should provide bounds for capacity 1 through 3, or exclude person-level utilization until badge identity is available.

### G6 — bulk closure at 17:00

- **Waiting time:** Observed waits are extraordinarily large: from 12:55 for C01 down to 35 minutes for C07. If `start=17:00` is a bulk closure timestamp rather than actual work commencement, these values are **inflated waiting times**. They measure “arrival to recorded closure,” not “arrival to work start.”
- **Service time:** All observed service times are zero because `start=complete=17:00`. This creates **near-zero service** despite potentially substantial real work having occurred before 17:00. Therefore, G6-like data can simultaneously show inflated waiting and zero service: the same late closure timestamp is used as both the apparent start and completion.
- **Utilization:** A lifecycle-interval method assigns zero occupied time, falsely implying an idle resource. A count-based method could instead report a burst of seven completions at 17:00, falsely implying extreme instantaneous capacity. Neither is valid as a physical utilization estimate.
- **Bottleneck detection:** G6 can create contradictory conclusions: a queue-delay analysis flags a severe bottleneck because waiting appears high, while a service-time analysis says closure work is instantaneous and non-bottleneck. Both are artifacts of the same semantic failure. Exclude this cluster from execution-based queue/service/utilization analysis unless actual work-start and work-finish events are recovered. It may still be useful for administrative closure timeliness.

### G7 — coarse and undocumented rounding

- **Waiting time:** Observed waits range from 1 minute (M02) to 2 minutes, including an impossible negative value for M02 if both fields are literal exact times. This demonstrates that lifecycle and arrival timestamps cannot be combined naively. Interval censoring may make the actual order indeterminate rather than truly negative.
- **Service time:** Every case appears to take 15 minutes. This is a measurement-grid artifact until the rounding rule is known. Real durations may vary materially within the rounding intervals.
- **Utilization:** Five identical intervals assigned to T-G can imply fivefold overlap and impossible utilization for a single worker, or a shared group session, or merely rounded timestamps. Capacity inference is not defensible from these fields.
- **Bottleneck detection:** A process-mining model may falsely infer synchronized starts and a fixed 15-minute service standard. It should not identify T-G as a concurrency or service-time bottleneck from this cluster alone. Use interval-censored analyses and retain recording order as weak auxiliary evidence, not as a replacement for event time.

### G8 — likely queue accumulation during capacity loss

- **Waiting time:** Observed waits are 38, 43, 48, 54, and 58 minutes. The increasing waits despite early arrivals and the 15:38 first start are consistent with a queue building during unavailability, then being processed serially. However, the safety and repair clocks differ, so the exact 15:00–15:35 linkage is uncertain.
- **Service time:** Each observed service interval is seven minutes. Given second-resolution scanner records and serial handoff, this is relatively credible after the first start. The first interval may still include post-safety restart/setup behavior if physical work began before the scan-defined start.
- **Utilization:** From 15:38 through 16:13, T-H appears continuously busy. Treating the 15:00–15:35 safety event as resource downtime would lower availability but raise utilization conditional on available time. Without synchronized clocks and resource availability records, report both observed busy time and uncertain downtime separately.
- **Bottleneck detection:** The apparent bottleneck is likely not ordinary processing speed alone; it may be capacity unavailability or a safety hold. A conventional queue model that ignores downtime can incorrectly attribute the long waits to chronic service under-capacity. Operational validation is required before attributing causality to safety.

def overlap_pairs(group):
    """Count pairwise overlapping intervals for a resource, excluding touching intervals."""
    g = group.sort_values(["start", "complete", "sequence_no"])
    intervals = g[["start", "complete"]].to_numpy()
    pairs = 0

    for i in range(len(intervals)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(intervals)):
            s1, e1 = intervals[i]
            s2, e2 = intervals[j]
            if max(s1, s2) < min(e1, e2):

    return pd.Series({"overlap_pairs": pairs, "possible_pairs": possible})

    g["queue_before_start"] = q_before_each_start
    return pd.Series({
        "arrivals_before_first_start": before_first_start,
        "max_queue_before_start_proxy": int(max(q_before_each_start)),
        "first_start_delay_min": (first_start - g["queue_arrival"].min()).total_seconds() / 60,
    })

queue_diag = (
    df.groupby("cluster", as_index=False)
      .apply(queue_diagnostics, include_groups=False)
      .reset_index()
)

cluster_diag = (
    df.groupby("cluster")
      .agg(
          n=("case", "size"),
          resource=("resource", "first"),
          source=("source", "first"),
          precision=("precision", "first"),
          max_start_equality=("start_group_size", "max"),
          max_end_equality=("end_group_size", "max"),
          mean_recording_lag_s=("recording_lag_seconds", "mean"),
          min_wait_min=("wait_minutes", "min"),
          max_wait_min=("wait_minutes", "max"),
          zero_duration_n=("zero_duration", "sum"),
          negative_wait_n=("negative_wait", "sum"),
          suspect_lifecycle_n=("precision_seconds", lambda x: 0),  # filled below
      )
      .reset_index()
)
cluster_diag["suspect_lifecycle_n"] = (
    df.assign(
        suspect_lifecycle=(
            df["zero_duration"]
            | df["negative_wait"]
            | df["precision"].eq("15 min")
            | df["source"].eq("manual_bulk_form")
        )
    )
    .groupby("cluster")["suspect_lifecycle"]
    .sum()
    .to_numpy()
)

cluster_diag = (
    cluster_diag.merge(
        resource_overlap.groupby("cluster", as_index=False)
        .agg(overlap_rate=("overlap_rate", "mean"), overlap_pairs=("overlap_pairs", "sum")),
        on="cluster",
        how="left",
    )
    .merge(queue_diag, on="cluster", how="left")
    .sort_values("cluster")
)

print("\nCompact independent pandas diagnostic table:")
print(cluster_diag.to_string(index=False))

print("\nExamples of equality groups (size > 1):")

      .sort_values(["cluster", "sequence_no"])

# PM4Py batch discovery ----------------------------------------------------------
# Batch labels are pattern detections. They are compared with independent diagnostics
# and are not interpreted here as evidence of physical simultaneous execution.

def call_discover_batches(dataframe, merge_distance, min_batch_size):
    """
    Try common PM4Py signatures. Both requested parameters are supplied whenever
    accepted by the installed PM4Py version.
    """
    fn = pm4py.discover_batches
    try:
        params = inspect.signature(fn).parameters
        kwargs = {}
        if "merge_distance" in params:
            kwargs["merge_distance"] = merge_distance
        if "min_batch_size" in params:
            kwargs["min_batch_size"] = min_batch_size
        return fn(dataframe, **kwargs)
    except TypeError:
        # Compatibility fallback for versions exposing positional parameters.
        try:
            return fn(dataframe, merge_distance=merge_distance, min_batch_size=min_batch_size)
        except TypeError:
            return fn(dataframe, merge_distance, min_batch_size)

def summarize_batches(result, merge_distance, min_batch_size):
    """
    PM4Py versions can return a DataFrame, list, dict, or another tabular object.
    Convert robustly to a compact printable summary without imposing semantics
    on PM4Py's category names.
    """
    if isinstance(result, pd.DataFrame):
        out = result.copy()
    elif isinstance(result, dict):
        out = pd.DataFrame(
            [{"batch_key": str(k), "batch_value": str(v)} for k, v in result.items()]
        )
    elif isinstance(result, (list, tuple)):
        out = pd.DataFrame({"batch_value": [str(x) for x in result]})
    else:
        out = pd.DataFrame({"batch_value": [str(result)]})

    out["merge_distance"] = merge_distance
    out["min_batch_size"] = min_batch_size
    return out

batch_runs = []
for merge_distance in [0, 60]:       # exact temporal merging versus one-minute tolerance
    for min_batch_size in [2, 4]:    # at least two versus at least four events
        detected = call_discover_batches(pm_df, merge_distance, min_batch_size)
        batch_runs.append(summarize_batches(detected, merge_distance, min_batch_size))

batch_results = pd.concat(batch_runs, ignore_index=True, sort=False)

print("\nPM4Py discover_batches output (compact; labels are temporal categories):")
display_cols = [
    c for c in [
        "merge_distance", "min_batch_size", "activity", "resource",
        "batch_type", "type", "batch_key", "batch_value"
    ] if c in batch_results.columns
]
if not display_cols:
    display_cols = list(batch_results.columns)
print(batch_results[display_cols].head(40).to_string(index=False))

print(

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

## Resource IDs trusted versus treated as shared accounts

| Cluster | If resource ID is trusted as one worker/machine | If resource ID may represent a pool/shared account | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | RBT-7 is one batch-capable automated cell; overlapping items are compatible with four fixture positions. | Less relevant; resource is described as an automated cell rather than a personal account. | Physical batch remains plausible, although item-level versus cycle-level accounting still differs. |
| G2 | One T-B resource with overlapping case intervals implies either multitasking, a group-control event, or inaccurate item starts. | If T-B represents a station/team rather than one inspector, actual parallel inspection becomes more plausible. | The shared “begin group” context still makes common setup more likely than literal identical individual work starts. |
| G3 | One T-C worker apparently works several overlapping reviews; this could be multitasking or a form-submission artifact. | A team/pooled account makes concurrent review possible. | Common end and common record insertion remain strong evidence of submission batching regardless of capacity model. |
| G4 | One T-D server gives clean serial processing. | A pooled ID is not needed to explain data, but would weaken one-person workload attribution. | Queue and four-minute cadence remain operationally useful at station level. |
| G5 | Treating `analyst_pool` as one analyst implies sustained overlap and impossible or very high utilization. | Explicit context supports a pool of up to three analysts; apparent overlap may be ordinary parallel team work. | Individual utilization is unidentified; pool capacity should be bounded, not assumed. |
| G6 | T-F does not represent one worker’s continuous zero-duration work; it more likely identifies a closing account. | A pooled/shared closing account makes the artifact even more likely. | No resource-capacity conclusion is justified either way. |
| G7 | One T-G with five overlapping 15-minute intervals looks impossible or multitasked. | A pooled station/team could make concurrent work possible. | Documented 15-minute rounding dominates both interpretations; timing remains too uncertain. |
| G8 | One T-H server gives a serial seven-minute triage sequence after 15:38. | A pooled resource could conceal multiple capacity units, but none is observed in this fragment. | The observed serial starts/ends are stronger evidence than the account interpretation, though capacity availability still needs validation. |

## Exact timestamps versus interval-censored timestamps

**Assumption for interval censoring:** stated precision is treated as an uncertainty scale, not necessarily a known symmetric error distribution. This is conservative because the prompt says precision is documented granularity, not true accuracy. For G7, the rounding direction is explicitly undocumented, so the uncertainty is asymmetric or unknown rather than safely modeled as ±7.5 minutes.

| Cluster | Exact-timestamp interpretation | Interval-censored interpretation | Robustness of diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | Exact common 09:00 and 09:03 strongly supports a synchronized cycle. | One-second censoring barely changes conclusion. | High robustness. |
| G2 | Exact common starts support a group-start event. | One-minute censoring allows small differences within 10:00, but does not explain the explicit group-begin UI context. | High robustness for start batching; low robustness for claims about simultaneous individual work. |
| G3 | Exact 11:30 common end and 11:31 recording support batch submission. | One-minute censoring allows small completion differences, but all end values and insertion values remain highly concentrated. | High robustness for end batching; medium for exact completion ordering. |
| G4 | Exact end-to-start handoffs support serial operation. | One-second censoring preserves ordering and lack of meaningful overlap. | High robustness. |
| G5 | Exact overlap under a single ID suggests multitasking/concurrency. | One-minute censoring can alter narrow boundary overlaps but substantial overlaps remain; shared-login ambiguity remains dominant. | Medium for pooled concurrency; low for individual multitasking. |
| G6 | Exact equality implies zero duration. | Minute-level censoring could permit short nonzero administrative durations, but cannot recover hours of unrecorded work. | Very high confidence that lifecycle fields are inadequate for execution analysis. |
| G7 | Exact values falsely imply synchronized starts/ends. | Under quarter-hour censoring and unknown rounding, ordering and overlap become largely indeterminate; M02’s negative wait may vanish. | Very high confidence in uncertainty/coarsening; low confidence in any physical batch interpretation. |
| G8 | Exact 15:38 start follows apparent safety window by three minutes. | Second-level execution times remain robust, but cross-system clock offset can materially alter the safety causal story. | High confidence in backlog before start; medium confidence in safety as cause. |

## Same temporal pattern, multiple physical explanations

At least the following patterns are observationally non-identifying:

1. **G1’s identical starts and ends**
   - A true four-item fixture cycle.
   - A PLC that assigns common cycle boundaries to items that receive some sequential sub-operations.
   - A system that emits item records from one aggregate machine-cycle event.
   
   The fixture-capacity context makes the first explanation strongest, but timestamp equality alone does not prove it.

2. **G2’s common start and staggered completions**
   - One group-begin UI action followed by serial per-item inspection.
   - Real parallel inspections launched together and completed at different times.
   - A common setup task followed by separate item-specific work, possibly by more than one person.
   
   The application design strongly favors the first or third explanation, but worker assignment and workstation capacity are needed to distinguish them.

3. **G3’s staggered starts and common end**
   - Reviews finish at different times but are submitted together after a folder is completed.
   - A coordinated final approval at 11:30 after earlier preparation.
   - Minute-level rounding that collapses close but distinct completions into 11:30.
   
   The common 11:31 insertion and stated form behavior favor bulk submission, but do not prove no review ended exactly at 11:30.

4. **G5’s overlapping intervals on one resource**
   - Several analysts concurrently using a shared account.
   - One analyst genuinely multitasking across cases.
   - Lifecycle spans include waiting, reading, or interruptions rather than active effort.
   
   The explicit pooled login prevents person-level inference.

5. **G7’s identical quarter-hour lifecycle times**
   - A real group measuring session.
   - Start and completion rounding.
   - Periodic synchronization or retrospective entry at quarter-hour boundaries.
   
   The documented rounding makes coarsening the default explanation, not evidence of a physical group.

6. **G8’s backlog before first start**
   - Safety-related capacity loss.
   - A dispatch/authorization hold.
   - A break, staffing gap, material unavailability, or a clock offset between systems.
   
   The safety event is useful corroboration but not causal proof.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

Priorities are ordered by expected analytical value and by the severity of the current failure modes.

1. **Capture execution timestamps separately from administrative timestamps.**
   - Record at least: queue arrival, actual work accepted/started, paused, resumed, physically completed, quality-approved, and submitted/closed.
   - Preserve `recorded_at` as a database/audit timestamp, but never substitute it for execution time.
   - This is most urgent for G3 and G6, where submission/closure likely replaces execution completion.

2. **Define lifecycle semantics in a source-level data contract.**
   - For each source and activity, specify whether `start` means item work start, group session start, UI button click, machine-cycle initiation, or retrospectively entered time.
   - Define whether `complete` means physical completion, operator confirmation, folder completion, approval, or database closure.
   - Version this contract and make it available to process-mining consumers.

3. **Synchronize source clocks and record clock provenance.**
   - Use a common time service where feasible.
   - Store source clock offset, synchronization status, timezone, and clock-drift monitoring data.
   - This is necessary to evaluate the G8 safety/repair relationship and any cross-system queue calculation.

4. **Capture reliable resource and identity data.**
   - Replace shared logins with badge identity, authenticated session identity, or an explicit mapping of session-to-worker.
   - Where shared accounts must remain, record participating badge IDs or active staffing count.
   - Model machines, stations, worker pools, and named workers as different resource types rather than overloading one `resource` field.

5. **Record explicit group/batch identifiers and batch membership.**
   - For RBT-7, capture fixture ID, cycle ID, position/slot, load time, unload time, and batch capacity.
   - For T-B, capture group/session ID, group-begin time, per-item work-start, and per-item completion.
   - For T-C and manual forms, capture folder/submission ID separately from each item’s substantive completion.

6. **Record workstation and resource capacity/availability.**
   - Store capacity by workstation, fixture slots, number of active analysts, planned shifts, breaks, safety lockouts, maintenance, and downtime reason.
   - Capacity must be time-varying: “up to three analysts” is not equivalent to three active analysts at every instant.
   - This enables utilization estimates that do not confuse one machine cycle, a team pool, and a single person.

7. **Capture pause/resume and blocking events.**
   - Include safety holds, material shortages, dispatch holds, system outages, rework waits, and operator breaks.
   - Link these to the affected resource/workstation and, where appropriate, to queued cases.
   - This supports a defensible capacity-loss explanation for patterns like G8.

8. **Preserve raw timestamp precision and publish precision metadata.**
   - Store raw device timestamps at maximum available resolution; display rounding should not overwrite raw data.
   - Record both documented granularity and measured accuracy/clock uncertainty.
   - For legacy systems, document whether times are floored, ceiled, rounded, backfilled, or derived from periodic polling. This is essential for G7.

9. **Preserve ordering and event provenance.**
   - Keep `sequence_no`, event UUID, source event ID, source system, device ID, ingestion timestamp, and any corrections/amendments.
   - Sequence order is not a substitute for time, but it helps diagnose bulk insertion and reconstruct source behavior.

10. **Implement data-quality flags instead of deleting problematic events.**
    - Flags should identify shared-account use, bulk submission, estimated/rounded time, inferred lifecycle state, late entry, and missing lifecycle fields.
    - Downstream performance dashboards can then exclude, interval-censor, or separately report suspect data rather than silently treating it as exact execution history.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High-confidence operational interpretations**

- **G1:** A real batch-capable machine-cycle interpretation is strongly supported. It is appropriate for fixture/cycle analysis, provided utilization is not calculated by summing four overlapping item intervals as if they were serial machine work.
- **G4:** Serial processing is strongly supported by second-resolution adjacency and short recording lag. It is suitable for queue, service-time, and station-level bottleneck analysis, subject to the usual limitation that this is a small fragment.
- **G8:** A real pre-start backlog followed by serial seven-minute processing is strongly supported. The claim that safety specifically caused the delay is only medium confidence because clocks differ and the context is non-causal.

**Interpretable only with semantic qualification**

- **G2:** The start field likely represents a group-start/common-setup event. Use it for group-session analysis, but not as an item-specific work start without additional instrumentation.
- **G3:** The end field likely represents folder/form submission or common completion. Use it for submission/compliance timing, but not uncritically for item-level service completion.
- **G5:** Analyze at pooled-resource level only. Individual analyst utilization, multitasking, and workload balance are not identifiable from `analyst_pool`.

**Exclude from exact queue/service/utilization analysis, or interval-censor**

- **G6:** Exclude from physical execution waiting, service-time, utilization, and throughput-capacity analysis until actual work timestamps are recovered. It can remain in an administrative closure-timeliness analysis. Its current values create the misleading combination of long waiting and zero service.
- **G7:** Treat lifecycle timestamps as interval-censored and do not infer physical concurrency, exact sequencing, fixed service time, or resource utilization. The recorded sequence may aid auditing, but cannot restore the missing execution timing.

**Operational validation required before performance conclusions**

- Validate whether **G1** PLC intervals are item-specific execution intervals or assignments of a shared machine cycle.
- Validate T-B’s actual number of inspectors/workstations and whether the group-begin event should be treated as group setup rather than individual start.
- Validate T-C’s definition of “complete,” the timing of real review completion, and whether 11:30 is a folder submission, approval gate, or actual final work completion.
- Validate active analyst count and identity/session mappings for G5.
- Validate G8’s clock offset, safety-check scope, and whether the safety event actually made T-H unavailable.

Overall, PM4Py batch discovery should be used here as a screening tool for temporal patterns. The defensible performance model must then combine those patterns with source semantics, precision, resource identity, capacity, recording lag, and independently observed queue behavior.
