# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee, Fiber Operations
**From:** Process Excellence / Process Mining
**Subject:** Redesign R: what to do next
**Decision requested:** Approve **Option E** (deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, with controls elsewhere), run as a **structured experiment (D)**. Do not scale nationally (A). Do not roll back fully (B).

**Bottom line.** R is not one intervention. It is five changes that push in opposite directions. Coordinator ownership and the merged validate/schedule step seem to deliver real speed for simple houses. The survey-skip rule and moving activation checks after billing start seem to create the failure paths behind the worse tail, rework, complaints and billing disputes. We recommend keeping what works where it works, stopping what creates customer and compliance risk, and paying for the specific information that would justify going further.

**What we know**
- Typical orders got faster: median 14.2 to 10.8 days. Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot) improved on every reported metric: median -6 days, p90 -8 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints -1pp, fewer SLA breaches.
- The tail got much worse: p95 went from 45 to 62 days, second visits from 11% to 18%, manual activation from 7% to 15%, complaints from 6.1% to 9.8%, and NPS from +21 to +17.
- The damage is concentrated in apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings and business customers, which together make up 55% of pilot volume.
- The event log shows billing starting before a successful activation test, and permit checks missing in old apartment buildings. These are departures from our own rules, not just inefficiencies. Billing customers for service that does not work carries regulatory and reputational exposure.
- Visible cost fell by EUR 23 per order. However, the partial full-cost estimate is EUR 9 *worse* than baseline, and it leaves out complaint handling after week 4, so the true gap is probably larger.
- Coordinators run at 93% utilization in two regions, and second-visit teams went from 66% to 92%. At those loads, small disruptions create long queues. Cases also stalled when a coordinator was absent.

**What we don't know.** The pilot regions had more apartments and old buildings than the baseline. Risk attributes are missing for the first two weeks, start timestamps are sparse, and downstream cost is cut off. So part of the aggregate deterioration may come from the case mix rather than the design. Part of the median gain may be a measurement artefact: if cycle time ends at billing start, moving billing earlier shortens it on paper.

**The trade-off.** Scaling now would lock in a speed gain for about a third of customers. The price would be more failed installations, billing before service, and a fragile coordinator model for everyone else. Rolling back would throw away a proven gain for simple houses to fix problems we can trace to two specific components. Because the segment evidence separates the two, we do not have to choose all or nothing.

**Decision for the next 12 weeks**
1. **Immediately, in all regions:** no billing start before a passed activation test, a mandatory permit check for permit-sensitive buildings, and automatic conformance alerts for both.
2. **Pause:** survey skipping outside simple low-risk houses, and any national rollout.
3. **Continue and extend in stages:** the full coordinator path for simple low-risk houses, with named backup coordinators, shared case notes and a utilization cap of about 85%.
4. **Measure:** a randomized survey-skip test by risk stratum, together with validation of the risk rule; full 60-day cost per order; coordinator ownership compared with shared-team ownership when a coordinator is absent; and reason codes for failures.
5. **Rollout gate, per segment:** p90 and first-time-right no worse than baseline, complaints no worse, full 60-day cost at or below baseline, near-zero billing before activation, and team loads that can be sustained.

**Cost of this path.** It delays the network-wide benefit for simple houses by about one quarter and gives back some of the survey capacity we freed. We judge that cheaper than scaling a design whose full cost and failure rate we cannot yet bound.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## 3.1 High fitness but low precision
- **Mechanism.** Fitness asks: "can the model replay the log?" Precision asks: "does the model allow behaviour the log never shows?" Discovery algorithms (for example, the inductive miner) respond to a more variable log by producing looser constructs such as optional steps, loops and parallel blocks. These replay almost everything, so fitness rises to 0.94, but they allow many combinations that never happen, so precision falls to 0.51. Generalization also fell (0.70 to 0.56), which fits a log with many rare variants and a shorter observation period (6 vs 10 weeks).
- **What it means here.** The pilot model is a flexible description of a less disciplined process, not evidence that the process conforms better. It "fits" because it allows skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits and early billing in almost any order.
- **The alignments tell the real story.** Against the *intended* process, the log shows frequent move-on-log steps (billing before activation, repeated installation without survey, complaints after billing) and move-on-model steps (missing permit check). Fitness against the normative model would be much lower than 0.94.
- **Implication.** Do not report the pilot's 0.94 fitness as a quality gain. Conformance to a reference model with explicit controls is the right governance metric. A recovery of precision in the scaled variant is useful as a sign of a controlled process, but it should not become a target in its own right.

## 3.2 Improved median but worse tail duration
- **A mixture of paths.** The pilot splits cases into a fast path (simple houses: merged task, no handovers, no survey needed) and a failure path (skipped survey, failed visit, second visit, manual activation, complaint). The median sits in the fast path, and the fast path got faster. p90 and p95 sit in the failure path, which got more frequent (Failed visit -> Second visit 10% to 17%) and slower (second-visit delay grew where coordinators are overloaded).
- **Queueing at high load.** As a rough rule, waiting time grows with ρ/(1–ρ). For the second-visit team, going from 66% to 92% raises that factor from about 1.9 to about 11.5 (roughly 6×). For coordinators at 93% it is about 13. Small increases in rework volume therefore turn into long tail delays.
- **New long-tail relations.** The temporal profile shows Installation visit -> Activation test getting worse for apartments, and a new long gap from Billing start to Complaint escalation.
- **Measurement effects.** If cycle time ends at Billing start, moving billing ahead of activation mechanically shortens the median for exactly the cases that later fail. With only completion timestamps, the merged validate/schedule task also removes a recorded waiting interval. Some of that is real improvement and some is relabelling, and we cannot separate the two without start timestamps (M2). The tail itself may be understated because long cases are censored at the end of the pilot.

## 3.3 Fewer handovers but lower resilience
- **Handovers turned into central hubs.** The handover network got simpler (3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%), but the working-together network became centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. The coordination work did not disappear. It moved into fewer people.
- **Redundancy was removed.** In the baseline, handovers were costly, but they spread case context across several people. Under single ownership without shared notes, one absent coordinator stalls all of their cases. At 93% utilization there is no slack for colleagues to absorb that work.
- **Unclear ownership after billing.** Activation specialists report unclear ownership once billing has started. So the case owner still hands work over, just informally and without a clear accountable party, which may not show up as a handover in the log.
- **Implication.** Accountability and resilience are separate design goals. Keep single ownership but add pairing or pod backup, shared case context and load caps. Test this in M5.

## 3.4 Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost
- **Cost is shifted, not removed.** For every 100 orders, R avoids about 13 surveys (Scheduling -> Field survey 31% to 18%). In exchange it adds about 6 failed visits, 7 second visits, 8 manual activations and 4 complaints. Survey staff utilization fell (78% to 52%) while second-visit staff utilization rose (66% to 92%). Prevention became correction, and a truck roll that fails is usually more expensive than a survey.
- **Accounting truncation.** Visible cost (EUR 161) excludes complaint handling. The fuller estimate (EUR 207, against EUR 198) still excludes complaint handling after week 4. Complaints come after billing, via a long tail, so the costs most affected by R are the ones least captured. The EUR 9 gap is probably a lower bound.
- **Not captured anywhere.** Billing disputes (business customers), goodwill credits, churn, regulatory exposure, and the lower NPS.
- **Implication.** Economic decisions need a cost measure per matured cohort (for example, 60 days after billing; see M4), not a snapshot of per-order process cost.

## 3.5 Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings
- **The effect depends on segment.** Every component of R removes a check or a handover. That is a good trade where checks rarely find anything (simple houses) and a bad one where they often do (apartments: the skip rule is often wrong; old buildings: permits are missed; business customers: activation and billing discipline matters; rural: rescheduling needs local knowledge).
- **The aggregate depends on mix.** The pilot's aggregate is a weighted mixture of these effects. Pilot regions over-represent the losing segments, so the aggregate somewhat overstates the harm of a national R compared with the national mix. Still, the within-segment check in Section 2 shows most of the harm is real and within segments, not just mix. A different national mix would also produce a different aggregate result. The decision should be made per segment, not on the aggregate.
- **Caveat on segment evidence.** Risk labels are missing for weeks 1–2, and the pilot regions differ from the baseline regions, so segment effects are estimates exposed to both misclassification and regional confounding. M1 and M6 address this directly.

## Common thread
R optimizes the steps it controls directly (validation, scheduling, handovers) and pushes risk onto steps it does not control (field execution, activation, billing, customer service). Every "good" metric measures upstream flow. Every "bad" metric measures downstream quality. No single metric settles the question. The segment and path structure does.

# 4. Decision alternatives

## A. Scale Redesign R nationally
- **Advantages.** Captures the median speed-up and handover reduction everywhere, quickly. It is simple to communicate, and the visible cost saving is about EUR 23 per order.
- **Risks.** Spreads the observed failure modes nationally: billing before activation, missing permit checks, a 7pp rise in second visits, 3.7pp more complaints. Likely higher full cost. Coordinators at 93% cannot absorb national variance. There is regulatory exposure. The median gain may be partly an endpoint artefact.
- **Reasonable only if.** Full 60-day cost is proven lower, the skip rule is validated across all strata, activation-before-billing is restored, and coordinator capacity and backup are in place. None of these conditions hold today.
- **Verdict:** Reject for now.

## B. Roll back to the baseline process
- **Advantages.** Quickly removes the known harms (tail, rework, early billing, permit gaps). The team returns to a familiar, more precise process (precision 0.79).
- **Risks.** Discards clear gains for simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot, and possibly a larger share nationally): 6 days faster, higher FTR, fewer complaints. It also brings back the "waiting for appointment" complaints and the long handover chains (34% with 3+ handovers). It punishes the parts of R that work for faults in the parts that do not, and it teaches the organization that pilots are all-or-nothing.
- **Reasonable if.** The problems cannot be contained by targeted controls, even simple houses show net harm once full 60-day cost is counted, or a regulator requires an immediate return to the certified process.
- **Verdict:** Reject as a whole. Roll back the harmful *components* instead.

## C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation
- **Advantages.** Removes the two components most directly linked to the harm (skip rule, early billing) and keeps the accountability benefit. It is simple and applies to all segments.
- **Risks.** Coordinator ownership is the component with the clearest *capacity and resilience* risk: 93% utilization, stalls during absence, slower rescheduling in rural areas. Scaling it nationally without M5 exports that fragility. It also gives up the survey savings for simple houses, where skipping seems harmless. And the merged validate/schedule task, which is low risk, is not explicitly included.
- **Reasonable if.** M5 shows coordinator ownership with backup performs as well as shared teams under absence, and coordinator staffing can hold utilization at 85% or below.
- **Verdict:** Its controls are right, but it scales the wrong thing first. Use its suspensions, not its national scale.

## D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement
- **Advantages.** Deals with the main confounders (regional mix, season, missing risk attributes before week 3, sparse timestamps, truncated cost). It produces causal, segment-level estimates and so the most defensible basis for a national decision.
- **Risks.** Delay: benefits for simple houses wait, and the harms keep running if the experiment carries on R unchanged. Randomizing survey skipping in risky strata puts customers at risk unless there are guardrails. There is also an organizational cost to running two processes side by side.
- **Reasonable if.** Combined with immediate controls, so the experiment does not keep exposing customers to known harms. On its own it is too passive.
- **Verdict:** Needed, but as the measurement engine inside E, not as the standalone decision.

## E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments and old buildings; measure before wider scale
- **Advantages.** Matches the design to the segment where the evidence supports it, captures the observed benefit now, and contains the harm where it is concentrated. It also produces the data needed for the next decision.
- **Risks.** Depends on classifying segments correctly, and the risk rule itself is under suspicion. Running two process variants adds complexity. Coordinators could still overload if simple-house volume grows. A "simple" label may hide real risk (for example, a house that needs a permit).
- **Reasonable if.** Risk attributes are captured at order intake (not imputed), a fallback routes any case with survey or permit flags back to the controlled path, activation-before-billing applies to all segments, and coordinator load is capped.
- **Verdict:** **Recommended, strengthened with C's suspensions and D's experimental design.**

## Summary

| Option | Captures simple-house gain | Contains tail/rework | Fixes billing/permit risk | Resolves key uncertainty | Main exposure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Yes (national) | No | No | No | Customer, cost, regulatory |
| B | No | Yes | Yes | No | Lost benefit, stagnation |
| C | Partly | Partly | Yes | No | Coordinator fragility |
| D | Delayed | Only if controls added | Only if controls added | Yes | Delay |
| E (+C controls, +D design) | Yes (targeted) | Yes (targeted) | Yes | Yes | Segmentation errors, complexity |

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

**How we rank.** Decision value is roughly: (probability the result changes what we do) × (money and customer impact at stake) – (cost and delay of getting it). The open decisions are:
- (i) whether and where survey skipping may be used;
- (ii) whether R is net positive on full cost, even for simple houses;
- (iii) whether coordinator ownership can scale nationally;
- (iv) what drives failures, so we can fix them.

The early-billing question is already decided on risk grounds (it is paused). Information about it would not reverse the decision, so measurements aimed at it have low *decision* value, though they can have high *control* value.

**Illustrative stakes** (based on an assumed volume, not a measured fact). At 150,000 orders a year, each EUR 10 per order error in the full-cost estimate is worth about EUR 1.5M a year. The pilot's +3.7pp complaint rate would mean about 5,500 extra complaints a year. Getting the scale-or-not decision wrong is therefore worth millions a year, while most of the measurements below cost weeks and modest instrumentation effort.

**Sequencing (not the same as the ranking).**
- Week 1: M7 and M6.
- Week 1–2: start M4 cohorts and M3 codes, since both need time to mature.
- Weeks 3–12: M1, with M3 codes in place.
- Weeks 4–12: M5 in two regions; M2 where field-app changes are feasible; M8 alongside.

**Cheap analyses on existing data first.**
- Standardize the baseline to the pilot's segment mix for weeks 3–6, where attributes exist.
- Compare with non-pilot regions over the same 6 weeks (difference-in-differences) to remove seasonal effects.
- Recompute cycle time to the "activation passed" endpoint to remove the billing-endpoint artefact.
- Treat still-open cases with survival methods so the tail is not understated.

These cost days and may already move the estimated effects by several points.

**Where information would not change the decision, act without waiting.** The billing-before-activation pause and the permit-check reinstatement fall into this category.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Option E, executed with C's suspensions and D's experimental rigor.** Keep Redesign R for simple low-risk houses, restore controls everywhere else, and scale further only through measured, segment-specific gates. No option dominates on every metric. This one keeps the demonstrated speed and handover benefit where it is real, and limits the tail, rework and activation-compliance risk where it is concentrated.

## Immediate action (weeks 0–2)
- **Hard control, all regions and segments:** Billing start is allowed only after a passed Activation test. Any manual activation needs an activation-specialist sign-off before billing. For customers already billed before activation, review the cases and credit where appropriate.
- **Reinstate the mandatory Permit check** for old and permit-sensitive buildings, and route any case with a permit or survey flag to the controlled path.
- **Deploy M7 conformance alerts** for both rules, with daily breach reporting to operations leadership.
- **Coordinator safeguards:** name a backup for every coordinator, use shared case notes or a case timeline, set a utilization cap of about 85% (reallocate or add staff in the two regions at 93%), and escalate automatically when a case has been idle for more than 2 working days.
- **Clear ownership after installation:** the activation specialist owns the case until service is confirmed working.
- **Redefine the headline cycle-time metric** to end at "service activated and verified", not at Billing start.
- **Run the cheap re-analyses** of existing data listed in Section 5.

## What to pause
- Survey skipping for apartments, old or permit-sensitive buildings, business customers and rural long-distance installs.
- Post-billing activation checks for all segments.
- Any national rollout of R, including national rollout of coordinator ownership, until M5 reports.

## What to scale
- **Stage 1 (now to week 12):** the full R path (merged validate/schedule, coordinator ownership, survey skip for low-risk houses) continues for simple low-risk houses in the four pilot regions and extends, stepped-wedge, to two or three more regions with a mix closer to the national average.
- **Everywhere:** the merged validate/schedule step for cases classified as simple at intake, since it is low risk and shows a clear improvement on Address validation -> Appointment scheduling.
- **For other segments:** coordinator ownership may continue in pilot regions *with the survey and permit steps restored* (a C-style variant), to feed M5. It is not scaled beyond them.

## What to measure next
In priority order:
1. M6 (weeks 1–3).
2. M4 (60-day cohorts starting now).
3. M1 (randomized, guard-railed, weeks 3–12).
4. M3 (codes from week 2).
5. M5 (two regions, weeks 4–12).
6. M2 and M8 where feasible.
7. M7 runs continuously.

## Evidence thresholds for broader rollout
Each segment is judged separately, against a baseline adjusted for mix and season (or against concurrent control regions), using matured cohorts (at least 8 weeks of data with at least 98% complete risk attributes, 30-day complaint and 60-day cost windows closed).

| Gate | Threshold to scale the segment nationally |
|---|---|
| Tail duration | p90 no more than baseline + 1 day; p95 no more than baseline + 10% (measured to "activation verified") |
| First-time-right | At least baseline – 1pp |
| Second-visit rate | No more than baseline + 1pp, with the 90% confidence interval upper bound under +2pp |
| Complaints within 30 days | No more than baseline + 0.5pp |
| Full 60-day cost per order | At or below baseline, with the 90% confidence interval upper bound no more than baseline + EUR 5 |
| Billing before passed activation | Under 0.5% of cases, all with documented approved exceptions; zero unapproved |
| Permit check for permit-sensitive buildings | 100% compliance (hard gate) |
| Skip rule (where skipping is allowed) | M6 false-negative rate of 5% or less; M1 shows no significant increase in failed or second visits |
| Capacity and resilience | Coordinator utilization at or below 85% and second-visit team at or below 80% for 4 consecutive weeks; no case idle for more than 2 working days because of coordinator absence (M5) |
| Benefit worth the complexity | Median at least 3 days faster than baseline for the segment; NPS no worse than baseline – 2 |
| Process control (diagnostic) | Alignments against the reference model show no systematic move-on-log for billing or activation, and precision of the discovered model for the scaled variant recovers towards baseline levels |

**Expansion path:**
