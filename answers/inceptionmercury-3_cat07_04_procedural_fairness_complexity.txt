# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this process cannot be assessed by approval rates alone. The following dimensions are relevant.

- **Outcome parity**: Raw approval rates differ sharply: A 78%, B 63%, C 69%, D 58%. These differences are a starting point, not a verdict. They may partly reflect genuine differences in eligibility, risk, documentation completeness, and needed support.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity**: After controlling for measured case mix, adjusted approval differences remain: B 5.5 percentage points, C 2.2, D 3.1 relative to A. Adjusted differences also appear for supervisor review, missing-document requests, and appeals. These are concerning because they suggest that recorded complexity does not fully explain group differences.
- **Procedural burden**: Group B, C, and D are less likely to follow the shortest direct path. Group A has 42% of cases on the simple V1 path; B has 19%, C 22%, D 18%. B and C more often pass through language support, accommodation review, missing-document requests, supervisor review, and appeals. These extra steps impose administrative burden on applicants even if each step is individually justified.
- **Timeliness**: Median cycle time is 8.1 days for A, 13.7 for B, 15.9 for C, and 12.1 for D. The 90th percentile is 21.4 days for A, 34.2 for B, 38.8 for C, and 29.5 for D. Waiting times for support steps are particularly unequal: B waits 3.8 days between language support contact and eligibility; C waits 4.7 days after accommodation review.
- **Consistency of routing**: The adjusted probability of supervisor review is higher for B (+8.0 percentage points), C (+5.5), and D (+2.0) relative to A. The adjusted probability of missing-document requests is also higher for B (+9.5) and C (+4.2). Some routing is clearly justified by case mix, but the adjusted differences raise the question of whether similar cases are treated similarly across groups.
- **Quality/accessibility of support**: Language support is provided by only 7 bilingual caseworkers. Accommodation review is provided by 5 specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. The log records that support was given, but not whether it was adequate, timely, dignified, or understandable. This is a large missing dimension.
- **Appeal/rework burden**: Appeal rates are 6% for A, 13% for B, 10% for C, and 16% for D. Reopen/rework rates are 11% for A, 22% for B, 24% for C, and 20% for D. Higher appeal and rework rates may indicate more denials, worse explanations, or more procedural friction.
- **Resource-allocation fairness**: Resource assignment appears heavily concentrated. A is spread across 52 caseworkers. B is disproportionately assigned to 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. C is disproportionately assigned to accommodation specialists. D is assigned to fraud review more often, consistently with higher recorded risk. But concentration can become unfair when it creates systematic queue delays.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate legitimate case-mix differences from unequal treatment. The analysis should compare how cases with similar recorded needs are processed, not simply whether final outcomes are equal.

### Matching or stratification variables

Stratify or match on variables that affect case complexity and legitimate routing:

- risk_score_band
- documentation_completeness
- channel
- household_size_band
- prior_case_count
- region
- month of application

Language preference and disability accommodation request are special: they are legitimate routing factors, but they should not be used to “explain away” poor service for groups that need those rights-preserving steps. They should be treated as needs, not solely as controls.

Use exact matching or propensity-score matching within strata, with calipers, then compare average treatment effects for group membership.

### Path/variant comparison

- Discover variants by group with `pm4py.get_variants`.
- Compare the frequency of each variant within matched strata.
- Compare same-variant performance. For example, among cases that all follow V2, compare waiting time from Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents. This isolates queue and execution differences from path differences.
- Identify substitution patterns: for example, whether B is more likely to receive V4 because of language need, and whether that same need adds waiting time beyond what is necessary.

### Resource-assignment comparison

- Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` and `pm4py.discover_working_together_network`.
- Count, per case, the number of distinct resources and handovers.
- Compare handover counts within matched strata.
- Measure resource concentration using a Gini coefficient for case loads by worker and group.
- Check whether B/C cases are assigned to bilingual/specialist workers because those workers are the only ones able to serve them, or because of arbitrary routing.

### Waiting-time decomposition

- Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `pm4py.get_service_time`.
- Decompose total cycle time into service time and waiting time per activity.
- Build a performance DFG using `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg`.
- Compare edge-level waiting times between groups after controlling for prior activities and resource pool.
- This distinguishes “the step is necessary” from “the step is excessively slow for this group.”

### Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

- Estimate adjusted differences with and without risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.
- If adding those variables attenuates group differences, that may indicate the covariates themselves carry historical or administrative bias.
- Use E-values or bounds to assess how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain remaining disparities.
- Compare models with and without month and region fixed effects.

### Subgroup checks by region and channel

- Repeat the analysis within each region and within each channel.
- B has a high in-person share and a high language-support share; separately compare B online and B in-person.
- C with accommodation requests should be compared to C without accommodation requests.
- D with high recorded risk should be compared to D with lower recorded risk.
- Regional checks can reveal whether disparities are driven by local capacity problems or by uniform treatment differences.

All comparisons should report confidence intervals, use permutation tests where appropriate, and avoid treating small adjusted differences as definitive evidence of discrimination.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most important procedural disparities are visible in the variant and waiting-time data.

### Large share of complex, slow paths for B, C, and D

- Group A has a very high share of the direct V1 path: 42%. Group B has only 19%, C 22%, D 18%.
- Group B has 24% of cases on V4, which includes Language Support Contact. That is by far the highest share. V4 has a median cycle time of 19.2 days, compared with 5.8 days for V1.
- Group C has 31% of cases on V5, which includes Accommodation Review and Supervisor Review. V5 has a median cycle time of 21.6 days.
- Group D has 28% of cases on V3, which includes Risk Screening, Fraud Review, and Supervisor Review. V3 has a median cycle time of 18.4 days.

Some of these differences are justified by case mix. D has a 51% high-risk share, so more fraud review is appropriate. C has a 62% accommodation-request share, so accommodation review is appropriate. B has a 57% language-support need share, so language support is appropriate. The path difference is not itself unfair.

### Excess waiting time at support steps

The waiting-time table shows where the main procedural burdens accumulate:

- B waits 1.6 days between Completeness Check and Request Missing Documents, versus 0.4 days for A.
- B waits 3.8 days between Language Support Contact and Eligibility Assessment.
- C waits 4.7 days between Accommodation Review and Eligibility Assessment.
- B waits 1.9 days between Supervisor Review and Decision Draft, versus 1.0 days for A.
- D waits 2.8 days between Risk Screening and Fraud Review, versus 2.2 days for A.

The language support and accommodation review waits are especially concerning because these steps are supposed to protect rights. A delay caused by a lack of bilingual staff or specialist capacity is not a neutral difference. It is a structural disadvantage imposed on the very applicants who need support.

### Handover burden

Resource assignments show that B and C experience more handovers:

- B is often passed from general caseworker to bilingual caseworker and back to a general caseworker.
- C is often passed from general caseworker to accommodation specialist and then to supervisor.
- Handovers increase the risk of repeated explanations, lost context, and administrative delay.
- They also make it harder to assign responsibility for the case.

Some handovers are necessary because specialists exist. But the pattern of serial handovers, rather than co-working or supported ownership, is a procedural burden.

### Adjusted supervisor-review and missing-document disparities

The adjusted comparison shows:

- Probability of Supervisor Review: B +8.0 percentage points relative to A.
- Probability of Missing Document Request: B +9.5 percentage points.
- Probability of Supervisor Review: C +5.5 percentage points.
- Probability of Missing Document Request: C +4.2 percentage points.

These are not fully explained by risk band, documentation completeness, channel, region, prior cases, or month. They may reflect genuine hidden complexity, but they may also indicate that minority-language applicants and accommodation-request applicants are subjected to stricter scrutiny or more defensive documentation requests.

### What is justified

- D’s fraud-review routing is consistent with its high measured risk share. This is legitimate if the risk score is accurate and unbiased.
- C’s accommodation-review routing is required by policy.
- B’s language-support routing is required by policy.
- B’s higher missing-document rate is partly consistent with its 46% incomplete-documentation share.

### What remains concerning

- The rights-preserving support steps are not timely. B and C pay a large waiting-time penalty for needing language support or accommodation review.
- The lack of separate service-level targets for support steps means these penalties are invisible in performance management.
- The adjusted supervisor-review and missing-document differences for B and C suggest inconsistent escalation and documentation practices even after controlling for recorded complexity.
- The handover pattern, especially B’s general  bilingual  general route, creates extra work for applicants and extra risk of lost information.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms could produce the observed patterns. Some are legitimate; some are concerning.

### Legitimate mechanisms

1. **True case-mix differences**: D has higher recorded risk and prior case counts; C has more accommodation needs; B has more language barriers and incomplete documentation. These factors legitimately trigger additional review steps and longer cycles.
2. **Mandatory rights-preserving steps**: Language support and accommodation review are legal or policy requirements. They cannot be skipped, and they inherently take time.
3. **Complexity of adjudication**: Applications needing supervisor review, fraud review, or repeated document requests may be genuinely harder to decide. Slower processing may reflect more careful decision-making rather than unfairness.
4. **Channel effects**: Group A is mostly online and has the best documentation profile. Online self-service may reduce errors and missing documents, leading to faster processing.

### Concerning mechanisms

5. **Under-resourcing of support services**: Only 7 bilingual caseworkers serve a group with a large language-support need. Accommodation review has only 5 specialists, two of whom also do supervisor review. This creates queues that systematically fall on B and C.
6. **Language bias in documentation completeness**: Documentation completeness may be measured through online forms and materials designed for the majority language. Applicants with minority-language needs may appear incomplete because of language barriers, not because their documents are actually insufficient. This can trigger more missing-document requests and more supervisor review.
7. **Administrative burden as a “support tax”**: Applicants who need assistance must go through extra handovers, waits, and repeated contacts. Even if each step is legitimate, the system penalizes people for needing help. This is a procedural fairness concern independent of final approval rates.
8. **Defensive documentation and escalation**: Overloaded or risk-averse caseworkers may request extra documents and ask for supervisor review more often for groups they find harder to communicate with. This creates longer paths without clear added value.
9. **Bias embedded in risk score**: Group D’s high risk score may reflect historical disparities rather than actual future risk. If so, the fraud-review routing and lower approval rates are not fully neutral.
10. **Appeal/rework cycles**: Higher appeal and rework rates for B, C, and D may indicate poorer explanations, more denials, or more administrative errors. They may also indicate that some applicants are forced into additional steps to resolve problems that should have been handled earlier.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available data are useful but insufficient for a causal verdict on discrimination.

- **Risk score is not neutral**: risk_score_band may encode historical disparities in policing, prior benefit history, or administrative bias. Controlling for it can hide discrimination rather than remove it.
- **Documentation completeness is partly an outcome**: Whether a case is marked complete depends on the applicant’s ability to navigate forms, language requirements, and digital channels. Group B’s higher incomplete share may be caused by language barriers, not by genuine missing evidence.
- **No true eligibility ground truth**: Approval rates cannot be compared to an unbiased standard of who should receive benefits. We know approvals differ, but not whether the correct decisions were made.
- **Case outcome is narrow**: The log records approval, closure, and appeals, but not the quality of explanation, dignity of treatment, or whether the applicant understood the decision.
- **Resource workload is too coarse**: Workload is observed only at daily granularity. We cannot see real-time queue lengths, interruptions, overtime, or whether urgent cases were prioritized.
- **Missing applicant needs**: We do not see language proficiency level, disability severity, accommodation effectiveness, digital literacy, or personal circumstances. Some of the adjusted differences may be due to these unmeasured needs.
- **Appeal information is limited**: Higher appeal rates could mean more errors, more denials, better access to appeal, or more frustration. The log does not show appeal merit or the quality of appeal intake.
- **Censoring and right-truncation**: Some cases may still be open or may not have reached benefit activation/case closure within the observation window. Median and 90th percentile cycle times may be affected.
- **Time period controls are coarse**: Month controls do not capture policy changes, staffing shifts, or seasonal variation within the month.

These limitations mean the analysis can identify credible signs of procedural unfairness, but it cannot prove intentional discrimination or quantify exactly how much of the adjusted gap is due to discrimination.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

The goal should be to make the procedure fair for applicants who need support, not to remove support steps.

### Add capacity for language support and accommodation review

- Hire more bilingual caseworkers, interpreters, and accommodation-review specialists.
- Use remote interpretation services to expand coverage.
- **Side effects**: Increased cost; recruitment may be difficult; new staff require training; if demand fluctuates, staff may be idle at times; moving staff into these roles may take them away from other work.

### Add service-level targets for support steps

- Set separate timeliness targets for Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and related handovers.
- Monitor waiting times at support queues, not just final cycle time.
- **Side effects**: Staff may prioritize support steps to the detriment of other stages; targets can be gamed; extra reporting overhead; if targets are too strict, quality may suffer.

### Redesign routing to reduce handovers

- Assign a single caseworker as owner through the whole process, with specialists joining as co-workers rather than taking over the case.
- Avoid “general  bilingual  general” serial handovers.
- **Side effects**: More coordination burden; caseworkers may lack specialist skills; joint working may require new tools; potential confusion about responsibility.

### Improve decision explanations

- Provide plain-language, multilingual explanations of decisions, document requests, and appeal rights.
- Track whether applicants received and understood the explanation.
- **Side effects**: Longer letters and more staff time; translation costs; better explanations may increase appeals, which are legitimate but require appeal capacity; if explanations are templated, they may become hollow.

### Monitor adjusted disparities

- Build a dashboard of process-mining metrics by applicant group, region, channel, and resource.
- Track adjusted disparities over time for approvals, cycle time, handovers, support-step waits, and appeals.
- **Side effects**: Monitoring can create pressure to hit targets rather than improve real fairness; requires data infrastructure; may raise privacy concerns; false alarms can consume resources.

### Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness

- Conduct fairness audits on risk score calibration across groups.
- Recalibrate the risk score if it encodes historical disparities.
- Redesign documentation completeness assessment so language barriers do not penalize applicants.
- **Side effects**: Changing the risk score may allow more true fraud or ineligible cases to pass; more manual review may be needed; documentation redesign may require additional validation; the process may become slower in the short term.

No mitigation should include removing language support, accommodation review, or other rights-preserving steps simply to make cycle times shorter.

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The following code shows how PM4Py can be used to support the fairness assessment.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# 1. Load and format the log
df = pd.read_csv("benefits_event_log.csv", parse_dates=["time:timestamp"])
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    resource_key="org:resource"
)
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

# 2. Insert service and waiting times
pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log, service_time=True)

# 3. Split by applicant group
group_logs = {}
for g in ["A", "B", "C", "D"]:
    group_logs[g] = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, "applicant_group", g, level="case"
    )

# 4. Variant analysis
for g, glog in group_logs.items():
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(glog)
    print(g, sorted(variants.values(), reverse=True)[:10])

# 5. Directly-follows graph and performance DFG
for g, glog in group_logs.items():
    dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(glog)
    perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(glog)
    # Compare specific edge waits
    edge = ("Completeness Check", "Request Missing Documents")
    print(g, "missing-doc wait:", perf_dfg.get(edge))

# 6. Filter to a common variant and compare within that variant
v1 = [
    "Submit Application",
    "Identity Verification",
    "Completeness Check",
    "Eligibility Assessment",
    "Decision Draft",
    "Applicant Notification",
    "Benefit Activation"
]
v1_log = pm4py.filter_variants(log, variants=[v1])

# 7. Filter cases by performance
long_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(log, min_performance=30)

# 8. Filter paths by performance
long_doc_path = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    log,
    path=("Completeness Check", "Request Missing Documents"),
    min_performance=0,
    max_performance=100000
)

# 9. Service time and total case duration
service_times = pm4py.get_service_time(log)
all_durations = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(log)

# 10. Handover and working-together networks
handover_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
resource_similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)

# 11. Log skeleton / declarative constraints
skeleton = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log)
declare_model = pm4py.discover_declare(log)

# 12. Case-level dataset for adjusted comparisons
event_df = pm4py.convert_to_dataframe(log)
case_df = event_df.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
    applicant_group=("applicant_group", "first"),
    risk_band=("risk_score_band", "first"),
    documentation=("documentation_completeness", "first"),
    channel=("channel", "first"),
    region=("region", "first"),
    prior_cases=("prior_case_count", "first"),
    cycle_days=("time:timestamp", lambda x: (x.max() - x.min()).total_seconds() / 86400),
    approved=("case_outcome", lambda x: int((x == "Approved").any())),
    appealed=("appeal_flag", "max"),
    handovers=("org:resource", lambda x: (x != x.shift()).sum() - 1)
).reset_index()

# 13. Example adjusted model
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.formula.api import logit, ols

approval_model = logit(
    "approved ~ C(applicant_group) + C(risk_band) + C(documentation) + "
    "C(channel) + C(region) + prior_cases",
    data=case_df
).fit()

cycle_model = ols(
    "cycle_days ~ C(applicant_group) + C(risk_band) + C(documentation) + "
    "C(channel) + C(region) + prior_cases",
    data=case_df
).fit()
```

This plan combines variant analysis, performance DFGs, handover networks, waiting-time decomposition, and adjusted regression to separate procedural burden from legitimate complexity.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

On the current evidence, the process appears procedurally unfair in important ways, especially for Group B and Group C. The raw approval differences are not by themselves proof of discrimination, because case mix and legitimate routing differ. However, the procedural evidence points to more than outcome inequality.

Group B and Group C have longer cycles, more waiting at rights-preserving support steps, more handovers, more supervisor review, and more missing-document requests than would be expected from their recorded case mix. The language-support and accommodation-review queues are severely under-resourced, and no separate service-level target protects applicants from those delays. This creates a procedural penalty for applicants who need the very assistance that the process is supposed to provide.

Group D’s situation is more indeterminate. Its higher risk profile justifies more fraud review, and its lower approval rate is not automatically unfair. But because risk_score_band may itself carry historical bias, the adjusted differences for D cannot be interpreted as fully neutral without auditing the risk score.

I would not call the process intentional discrimination on the basis of this log alone. I would call it structurally and procedurally unfair in its current form, with credible evidence that B and C bear disproportionate administrative burdens. The safest conclusion is that the process is not yet fair, and that capacity, routing, service-level targets, and monitoring need to be changed before fairness can be established.
