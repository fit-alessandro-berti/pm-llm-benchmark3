# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

Mira_Coordinator is best interpreted as a cross-cutting exception-routing and case-recovery role, rather than as a member of one ordinary production team. The evidence supports substantial organizational influence, but it does not by itself establish that she has formal authority, that she is inefficient, or that she is exercising inappropriate discretion.

**Handover-of-work network**

This network represents sequential transfer of work between resources, usually by looking at the resource on one event and the resource on the next relevant event in a case. It is useful for identifying routing paths, interfaces, queues, and possible coordination hubs.

For Mira, the high out-degree (0.82), in-degree (0.51), and betweenness (0.77) are consistent with a broker: she receives exception cases from the rules engine, sends them to medical, legal, or adjustment resources, and receives responses or follow-up work. The specific edges show multiple kinds of routing rather than one simple reporting line. Her edges to all four adjusters, both specialists, and the two temporary resources suggest that she crosses team and skill boundaries.

The network does not prove that Mira performs the substantive claims work, manages those people, or is the sole cause of delay. A handover edge is not necessarily a managerial relationship, and a high edge count can reflect a role explicitly designed to route work. The time between events is also not pure handling time: it can include queueing, system delay, or waiting for a person or vendor. The Bot_Rules-to-Mira path may represent an automated exception queue rather than a discretionary human decision in every case.

**Working-together network**

This network measures co-occurrence of resources within cases, here using pairwise case co-occurrence. It captures sustained case-level collaboration or shared ownership more than transient routing. It is therefore informative about team structure and regular working relationships, but not about every organizationally important intervention.

Mira's low weighted degree, and low pairwise co-case rates with team leads and adjusters, are compatible with a short-lived intervention. She may route or reopen a case, after which the case proceeds through another team without her appearing again. A short routing event can have a large effect on the path while contributing little to co-occurrence. The fact that she touches only 22% of claims but most reopened cases and complaints also means that all-case co-occurrence dilutes the signal from the exceptional subset. Her low working-together degree is consequently not evidence that she is unimportant or isolated.

The network does not prove that she lacks collaboration, because co-occurrence depends on the case window, event granularity, and resource logging. It also does not distinguish a person who participates briefly from one who is absent because the handover is recorded at too coarse a level. The low Mira-adjuster rates despite many handovers are a warning to inspect the definitions and denominators, not a contradiction that can be resolved by treating one network as wrong.

**Subcontracting network**

With (n=2), this network detects a pattern in which resource A acts, resource B performs later work, and resource A returns within the next two events. It is useful for finding delegated work, supervision, review loops, and brokered specialist interventions. It is not restricted to legal subcontracting or to external vendors.

Mira's subcontracting betweenness (0.69) and the repeated patterns with Omar and Priya indicate that she often initiates or frames specialist work and then receives the result, performs a follow-up assignment, or reopens the case. This supports a coordination loop around exceptions. The fragments do not establish whether Mira is the decision-maker, merely the queue owner who records the request and response, or an authorized quality gate.

The network does not establish causality, intent, or a formal delegation contract. A return to Mira may be required by the workflow design or by the event logger. The Alice/Carla and Bruno/Diego patterns show that similar return loops exist in ordinary team supervision, so Mira's pattern could be a cross-team version of a legitimate review loop. The `n=2` setting is also sensitive to unrelated intervening events and should be tested for robustness.

**Performance/time statistics**

The activity profile is consistent with Mira handling complexity: 48% of her cases have an exception flag, she touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, and her case cycle-time contribution has high variance. The six-day absence is particularly informative. Ordinary low-value claims did not change, while the 90th percentile for exception cases increased from 8.4 to 13.9 days and queues accumulated. This is evidence that she is a capacity or coordination constraint for a specialized flow, not that she slows the ordinary flow.

Her 4.2-minute median event service time should not be interpreted as the total time she adds. The long periods while she is absent from a case may be waits for specialists, adjusters, approvals, or vendors. Conversely, the absence result is observational rather than a controlled experiment: absence may have coincided with staffing or demand changes, and the increase may reflect work that was not assigned elsewhere. Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay also have substantial waiting-time components, so system-wide delay should not be attributed to Mira without decomposing service and waiting time by activity and path.

**Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity**

Activity-based resource similarity and organizational-role discovery can compare resources by the activities they execute and, where available, by context, sequence, or case type. They can test whether Mira resembles team leads, adjusters, temporary staff, a specialist scheduler, or a distinct exception role. Her observed mixture—missing-information requests, reopens, complaints, and specialist assignment—would plausibly form a separate cluster or be closest to coordinators/team leads rather than to a substantive specialist.

These methods do not, by themselves, recover a job title, authority, competence, or appropriateness of decisions. Similarity is affected by activity naming, event volume, automation, case mix, and the fact that one person can occupy several roles. A distinct activity profile would support role differentiation, not automatically a bottleneck or a control failure. Conversely, similarity to a team lead would not prove that she is an informal or formal team lead. The analysis should include the activity context and exception/complaint strata, not only raw activity counts.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

The hypotheses below are not mutually exclusive. A formal exception-desk role can also be a bottleneck, a boundary-spanning broker, and a control risk if the role is under-specified.

**Hypothesis 1: formal coordinator / exception desk**

**Mechanism.** The organization has assigned Mira ownership of exception routing, missing-information recovery, complaints, reopens, and specialist assignment. Bot_Rules identifies or routes unusual cases; Mira determines the next operational queue; specialists or adjusters do the substantive work; Mira receives the response and closes or reopens the coordination loop.

**Supporting evidence.** She touches only 22% of all claims but 64% of reopened cases and 71% of complaints. Bot_Rules-to-Mira is frequent, and Mira has outgoing links to every major downstream group. Her high betweenness and the specialist return edges fit an exception-desk design. Ordinary low-value claims were unaffected during her absence, while exception cases accumulated, which is what one would expect when a specialized queue owner is unavailable. The process-owner description of a “queue cleaner” is also compatible with a deliberately operational, non-managerial role.

**Limits.** Formal status is not present in the event log. The title, approval rights, service-level targets, backup coverage, and decision rules are unknown. A high concentration of work around one person can arise because the process was designed around her informally rather than because management formally assigned it. The absence observation does not show that she is the best or only way to perform the function.

**Predictions.** A formal role should have stable activity and routing signatures over time, a consistent set of entry conditions, and similar behavior on comparable exception cases. A designated backup should take over with broadly similar transitions during absences. Cases should show recorded reasons or flags for routing, predictable specialist selection, and measurable service-level performance for Mira's queue. Her workload should vary with exception and complaint volume more than with total claims volume.

**Hypothesis 2: hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency**

**Mechanism.** Mira is the effective single-server gateway for multiple exception paths. Even if each intervention is short, work queues behind her, and downstream resources cannot proceed until assignment, clarification, reopen, or escalation is recorded. Her high centrality reflects structural dependency rather than merely high productivity.

**Supporting evidence.** Her 0.82 handover out-degree, 0.77 betweenness, and high-variance cycle-time contribution are unusually high. Exception cases have a high rate of her involvement and experienced a sharp tail increase during her absence. Her high event volume spans otherwise separate teams and specialists. The fact that she disappears from cases after routing can hide the queue wait from a simple per-event service-time metric while preserving the dependency in the path.

**Limits.** The absence evidence shows accumulation but not necessarily a Mira-caused bottleneck; there may have been no trained replacement, a freeze on reassignment, or an unusual exception mix. Her median service time is only 4.2 minutes, and some delays are likely specialist or vendor waits. A routing hub can be central because it efficiently prevents more expensive escalations. The data does not yet show queue length, arrival rate, utilization, or time specifically waiting for Mira.

**Predictions.** Mira-specific queue wait should rise with concurrent exception arrivals and be associated with downstream start delays. Cycle time should have a nonlinear relationship with her workload, especially at the upper tail. Adding a trained backup, parallel routing, or capacity during peaks should reduce exception waiting without materially affecting ordinary claims. Cases near shift boundaries, weekends, or her absence should show stronger delays if this is a capacity problem.

**Hypothesis 3: organizational boundary spanner or triage broker**

**Mechanism.** Mira connects teams with different skills, priorities, and ownership rules. She interprets exception information, chooses whether to request documents, send a case to a specialist, return it to an adjuster, reopen it, or escalate a complaint. The role is not ordinary co-production; it is brokerage across organizational boundaries.

**Supporting evidence.** Her edges span Bot_Rules, team leads, four adjusters, two specialists, temporary staff, reopens, and complaints. Subcontracting loops with Omar and Priya show that she initiates and receives specialist work. Low working-together rates coexist with many handovers, exactly as expected for a transient boundary-spanning role. Adjusters describe her as the person who knows where hard cases should go, and the absence impact is concentrated in exceptional cases.

**Limits.** Routing knowledge could be a property of one experienced employee rather than an intentionally designed boundary-spanning capability. The event log does not reveal the information used, whether choices are rule-based, or whether other coordinators would make the same decisions. The complaint and reopen activities may themselves make Mira appear central even if another actor owns substantive resolution.

**Predictions.** Mira should be disproportionately present on cases that cross team, region, channel, or specialist boundaries, and her paths should be more diverse than those of team leads. Similar cases routed by different people should reveal whether routing is consistent. Her absence should increase cross-team handover latency more than within-team latency. A role or resource that centralizes the same interface should emerge if the function is institutional rather than person-specific.

**Hypothesis 4: quality gatekeeper or automation monitor**

**Mechanism.** Mira reviews exceptions generated by Bot_Rules, corrects routing, verifies missing information, and reopens cases when automated or human processing is inadequate. Her apparent discretion may partly be a control step that prevents bad automated decisions or premature closure.

**Supporting evidence.** Bot_Rules sends 920 cases to her, with a further Bot_Rules-to-Mira-to-Bot_Rules pattern. Her activities include reopen and escalation, and exception-flag prevalence is high. The manager's concern that removing her would increase formal escalations and specialist overload is consistent with a quality buffer. Temp_2's closure-exception path and the specialist return loops provide plausible control points.

**Limits.** There is no direct evidence of a documented quality checklist, sampling policy, defect detection rate, or override outcome. A rule-monitoring role could also be a symptom of poor automation or ambiguous exception definitions. The automated network edges do not identify what Mira changed or whether her intervention improved results.

**Predictions.** Mira's interventions should correlate with Bot_Rules confidence failures, rule exceptions, missing fields, or later corrections. Her involvement should reduce downstream rework, complaints, inappropriate payment, or specialist referrals after controlling for case complexity. A well-designed audit trail should show reason codes and before/after routing states. Improving the rules engine should reduce low-value Mira interventions while preserving the high-risk ones.

**Hypothesis 5: data or logging artifact**

**Mechanism.** The resource field may record a queue owner, reassignment clerk, or system action under Mira's identity, making her look like the actor who made a decision. Multiple event types may be collapsed under generic activities such as “Assign Specialist” or “Reopen Claim,” and handover construction may turn same-case administrative entries into apparent work transfers.

**Supporting evidence.** Her working-together rates are surprisingly low relative to the number of handovers, she may disappear for several events after routing, and some activities are short routing or clarification events. The process owner does not regard her as a real team lead, which raises the possibility that the event resource means queue maintenance rather than substantive work.

**Limits.** The absence result and the high share of reopens and complaints imply a real operational function even if attribution is imperfect. The cross-resource patterns are coherent across several network types, so a pure artifact is not required to explain the evidence. It remains a limitation until event semantics and resource-assignment rules are validated.

**Predictions.** A raw audit log with user IDs, queue IDs, timestamps, and state transitions should show whether Mira performed an action or was merely assigned as owner. Splitting administrative and substantive events should reduce her apparent handover centrality if logging is the main explanation. Duplicate events, batch updates, or inconsistent timestamps should be concentrated around Mira's transitions. Reconstructing the network by true actor rather than queue owner should materially change the result if this hypothesis is strong.

# 3. Risk assessment

**Bottleneck risk.** There is credible risk for exception and complaint flows, especially at the tail of the distribution. The absence period and high betweenness show a capacity dependency, but not that Mira is inefficient. Measure arrivals, queue sizes, concurrency, scheduled availability, and waiting specifically before Mira's first event. Compare with matched exception cases and with periods using other coordinators.

**Coordination risk.** Mira is a useful integration point, but a single routing point can create delayed handovers, unclear ownership, and loops between coordinators, adjusters, and specialists. The return patterns and reopens warrant checking for repeated clarification cycles and whether cases lack a current owner. This is a process-design risk, not evidence of individual failure.

**Key-person risk.** Knowledge of where hard cases should go appears concentrated in Mira. The six-day absence demonstrates operational vulnerability for exceptional work. Risks include absence, turnover, workload spikes, and inconsistent decisions by an untrained replacement. A documented routing policy, cross-training, backup coverage, and a searchable decision history would reduce dependence without eliminating the function.

**Compliance/control risk.** The concern that one person detects a problem and selects the next specialist is a legitimate segregation-of-duties question. It may create uncontrolled discretion, conflict-of-interest exposure, inconsistent treatment, or weak auditability if the decisions are not rule-bound and reviewable. The evidence does not establish wrongdoing. Test whether Mira can both create and approve a material outcome, whether her choices are constrained by policy, whether reasons are recorded, and whether high-risk routes receive independent review. Access rights and approval rights should be examined separately from routing authority.

**Workload fairness risk.** Mira's load is concentrated in difficult cases, so raw case counts or event counts would be a poor fairness measure. A 4.2-minute median event does not capture cognitive complexity, context switching, queue interruptions, complaint handling, or responsibility for stalled cases. Conversely, a high exception share may reflect routing or logging conventions rather than all work she performs. Use complexity-adjusted workload measures—case severity, number of handovers, specialist involvement, reopen count, complaint status, and active minutes—and compare peak load, after-hours work, and leave coverage across coordinators.

**Data-quality risk.** Resource identity may be a human actor, queue owner, service account, or last-touch field. Automated events and vendor events have different semantics and waiting-time behavior. Same-timestamp events, missing lifecycle transitions, duplicate administrative updates, and inconsistent case-resource attribution can inflate or distort network edges. Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay should not be treated as ordinary employees, and Bot_Rules should not be treated as a human coordinator. Validate event definitions, timestamp time zones, resource types, and assignment semantics before making personnel decisions.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

The analyses should be stratified by exception status and case complexity, and should preserve both resource type and activity. The aim is to separate the coordination function from the person and from external or automated waiting.

**Handover-of-work analyses**

- Format the event log with explicit case, activity, timestamp, resource, and lifecycle columns, then construct handover networks for all claims and for `exception_flag=True` separately. Compare edge frequencies, normalized centrality, and path diversity for Mira, team leads, and any backups.
- Compute time from the preceding event to Mira's event, time from Mira to the next substantive event, and time from a routing event to work start. Use activity-specific edges so that Bot_Rules-to-Mira, Mira-to-specialist, and Mira-to-adjuster are not conflated.
- Check whether Mira's centrality remains high after collapsing consecutive events by the same resource, removing administrative duplicates, and distinguishing human, bot, and vendor resources. Use bootstrap or time-window comparisons to assess stability rather than relying on one aggregate graph.
- Calculate edge-level and case-level rework loops, particularly `Mira -> specialist -> Mira`, `Mira -> adjuster -> team lead`, and reopen-to-close paths. Identify whether these loops resolve exceptions or predict complaints, payment delay, or repeated reopen events.

**Working-together analyses**

- Build the co-case network separately for ordinary claims, exceptions, complaints, reopens, high-value cases, and each team or region. Compare Mira's weighted degree and betweenness within the exceptional subgraph; the all-case denominator can mask her relevant collaboration.
- Use sliding time windows and activity-aware co-occurrence, such as resources appearing within a defined event or elapsed-time window, in addition to simple case co-occurrence. Compare whether Mira is briefly present in many cases or jointly active for long periods with a smaller group.
- Evaluate team cohesion and brokerage: remove Mira from the case-resource graph, measure how many cross-team paths disappear, and compare this with removing Alice or Bruno. This is a structural diagnostic, not proof that removal is desirable.

**Subcontracting analyses**

- Re-run `discover_subcontracting_network` with several (n) values, not only 2, and stratify by activity fragment. Test whether Mira's loops with Omar and Priya occur because she requests work, reviews it, or merely records its return.
- Compare the frequency and elapsed time of Mira-mediated specialist paths with direct team-lead-to-specialist or alternative coordinator paths. Measure completion, reopen, complaint, and payment outcomes after each path while controlling for case type and exception status.
- Examine role directionality and return intervals. A legitimate supervisory loop should have identifiable entry criteria and a bounded return path; an unplanned loop may show repeated referrals, missing information, or escalating waits.

**Activity-based similarity and role discovery**

- Create a resource-by-activity matrix, preferably using normalized frequencies and case-type/exception-stratified features, and apply `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity`. Inspect whether Mira is closest to Alice/Bruno, temporary staff, adjusters, or forms a distinct cluster.
- Use `discover_organizational_roles` and `discover_network_analysis` on the event log or derived networks, then validate the discovered roles against known resource types. Include features such as incoming/outgoing handovers, distinct case categories, reopens, complaint involvement, and specialist assignments; raw activity counts alone may identify volume rather than role.
- Test role stability over the 12 weeks. A consistent Mira-specific profile supports a designed role; a sudden profile change may indicate staffing substitution, process change, or logging drift. Similarity should be treated as descriptive and not as evidence of authority or performance quality.

**Filtering, variants, and time ranges**

- Use `filter_event_attribute_values` to compare `exception_flag=True` and `False`, then separately filter complaints, reopens, high amounts, channels, regions, and case types. This tests whether Mira's centrality is concentrated where her role should be needed.
- Use `filter_variants` to identify the most common Mira-mediated paths, rare long-tail variants, and paths with specialist involvement. Compare variant frequency and outcomes before and after Mira's interventions, including `Reopen Claim` and `Escalate Complaint` loops.
- Split the 12 weeks into weekly windows and compare pre-absence, absence, and recovery periods. If timestamps permit, analyze shifts, weekdays, queue saturation, and handover latency. Use matched case cohorts or regression/survival models to avoid attributing all time differences to Mira.
- Check resource substitution during the absence and identify whether work was held, redirected to team leads, or completed by temporaries. A genuine key-person problem should be visible as missing capacity or changed routing, not merely a changed name in the log.

**Service and waiting-time analysis**

- Use `get_service_time` for activity/resource-specific active time, and use `insert_case_service_waiting_time` or an equivalent elapsed-time decomposition to distinguish active service from case waiting. Report Mira's active time, wait before her routing event, wait after routing, specialist service, vendor wait, and approval/payment wait separately.
- Measure queue delay between `Bot_Rules` and the first human action, between Mira and the selected downstream resource, and between specialist completion and Mira's follow-up. Compare medians and upper percentiles by exception status and workload level.
- Use cycle-time decomposition and survival or quantile analysis to test whether Mira's involvement predicts delay after controlling for complexity. Also test whether it predicts fewer complaints, fewer reopens, fewer repeat missing-information requests, or lower downstream rework; a coordination role may increase visible handling steps while improving outcomes.
- Visualize the resulting SNA graphs with `view_sna` or save them with `save_vis_sna`, but preserve the underlying edge tables and confidence intervals. A visual hub is useful for communication, not a substitute for denominator and time analysis.

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately.** Treat Mira's function as a critical exception-coordination capability and put an interim control around it. Assign and train at least one backup coordinator; publish a simple routing matrix for medical, legal, adjustment, complaint, and reopen decisions; and maintain an explicit current-owner field. Record reason codes, selected destination, urgency, and whether the action is a routing decision, a quality check, or an approval. Separate routing authority from material settlement/payment approval, and require independent review where policy or amount thresholds call for it. Establish queue and service-level alerts for exception cases, especially during absence or peak load. Do not remove Mira before a replacement routing path is operating.

**Monitor.** Track exception arrivals, queue age, time waiting for Mira, time from Mira to downstream start, active versus waiting time, upper-percentile cycle time, reopen and complaint rates, specialist utilization, and outcomes by routing path. Review these by case type, priority, amount band, region, channel, and exception flag. Monitor route consistency for comparable cases, use of overrides, after-hours work, leave coverage, and the share of decisions with reason codes. Recalculate handover, co-case, and subcontracting networks in rolling windows, with bots and vendors labeled separately. Audit a sample of high-risk decisions and compare matched cases routed by different people.

**Do not change until there is more evidence.** Do not conclude that Mira is a formal team lead, a poor performer, or acting improperly from centrality alone. Do not remove the role, automate all of its decisions, or redistribute the work broadly until queue data, event semantics, control rights, and outcome comparisons have been validated. Do not treat low working-together centrality as evidence that the function can be ignored, and do not attribute vendor or specialist waiting to Mira without a time decomposition. After measurement and a controlled pilot with a trained backup, management can decide which routing rules to automate, which judgments require human review, and whether the role should be one coordinator, a rota, or a small exception desk.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator appears to be a cross-team exception broker: she receives unusual cases from the rules engine, routes them to adjusters or specialists, and handles reopens, complaints, and missing information. Her high handover and subcontracting centrality means she connects many parts of the process; it does not mean she is a manager or that she performs most of the substantive work. Her low working-together score is compatible with short routing interventions: she may appear briefly, then disappear while another team completes the case. The absence evidence shows a real dependency, because exception-case delays worsened while ordinary claims did not. The main concerns are exception-flow bottlenecks, concentrated knowledge, unclear ownership, and insufficiently documented routing discretion. Management should immediately provide trained backup, decision rules, reason codes, queue monitoring, and independent review of material approvals. It should measure active versus waiting time and validate logging before changing the role. Mira should not be removed until an equivalent coordination function is demonstrably available.
