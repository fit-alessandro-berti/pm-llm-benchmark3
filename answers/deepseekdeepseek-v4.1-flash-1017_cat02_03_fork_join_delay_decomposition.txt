Let's consider "elapsed fork-join stage time" formula. In §1:
- Let \(t^a_c\), \(t^f_c\) arrival, fork_release.
- \(Q^A_c = A_{start,c}-t^f_c\). same.
- \(S^A_c = A_{end,c}-A_{start,c}\).
- \(E^A_c=A_{end,c}\), \(J_c=\max_i E^i_c\).
- \(W^{sync,A}_c = J_c-E^A_c\). Only \(W^{sync,crit}_c=0\) (unless tie; all tied max zero).
- \(T^{FJ}_c = J_c - t^f_c = \max_i(Q^i_c+S^i_c)\). This is the fork-join stage elapsed from release to all branches complete.
- \(D_c = final\_start_c - J_c\).
- \(F^{service}_c=final\_end-final\_start\).
- Total \(T_c = final\_end - arrival = (fork_release-arrival) + T^{FJ}_c + D_c + F^{service}_c\).
Why summing all sync waits double counts: For each noncritical branch, \(W_i\) measures waiting from its completion to J. Intervals \([end_i,J]\) overlap in calendar time across branches and overlap with active work of critical branch; only stage elapsed \(T^{FJ}\) (or critical q+s) should be added. More explicitly, \(T^{FJ} \ne \sum_i(Q_i+S_i)\), because branches run in parallel. \(T^{FJ} = \max_i(Q_i+S_i)\). Sum sync waits is not additive with critical path.

# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

All times are minutes unless noted. For case \(c\) and branch \(i\in\{A,B,C\}\), define:

- Upstream elapsed:  
  \[
  U_c = fork\_release_c - arrival_c
  \]

- Branch queue:  
  \[
  Q^i_c = start^i_c - fork\_release_c
  \]

- Branch service:  
  \[
  S^i_c = end^i_c - start^i_c
  \]

- Branch completion offset from fork release:  
  \[
  R^i_c = Q^i_c + S^i_c = end^i_c - fork\_release_c
  \]

- Join ready under the AND-join rule:  
  \[
  J_c = join\_ready_c = \max_{i\in\{A,B,C\}} end^i_c
  \]

- Critical branch:  
  \[
  k_c = \arg\max_{i\in\{A,B,C\}} end^i_c
  \]
  with deterministic tie-breaking in this answer/code: \(A>B>C\). There are no original-case ties, but the tie rule matters for counterfactual labels.

- Branch synchronization wait after completion:  
  \[
  W^{sync,i}_c = J_c - end^i_c
  \]
  The critical branch has \(W^{sync,k_c}_c=0\), and any tied maximum branch also has zero wait.

- Elapsed fork-join stage time:  
  \[
  T^{FJ}_c = J_c - fork\_release_c
             = \max_{i\in\{A,B,C\}} \left(Q^i_c + S^i_c\right)
  \]
  Equivalently, along the critical branch,
  \[
  T^{FJ}_c = Q^{k_c}_c + S^{k_c}_c + W^{sync,k_c}_c
           = Q^{k_c}_c + S^{k_c}_c
  \]
  since the critical branch has zero synchronization wait.

- Downstream queueing before final release:  
  \[
  D_c = final\_start_c - J_c
  \]
  This is interpreted as downstream queueing only if the stated model and final-station assumptions hold.

- Final service:  
  \[
  F^{service}_c = final\_end_c - final\_start_c
  \]

- Total case flow time:  
  \[
  T_c = final\_end_c - arrival_c
      = U_c + T^{FJ}_c + D_c + F^{service}_c
  \]
  and, along the critical path,
  \[
  T_c = U_c + Q^{k_c}_c + S^{k_c}_c + D_c + F^{service}_c
  \]

Do **not** add all branch queue/service times. The branches run in parallel, so the fork-join duration is a maximum, not a sum. Also, do **not** add all synchronization waits. Each \(W^{sync,i}_c\) is measured from a branch completion to the same join-ready time \(J_c\). These waiting intervals overlap with each other and with the remaining work of the critical branch. For example, in F02 the sync waits are \(10+0+35=45\) minutes, but the whole fork-join stage is only \(60\) minutes; adding all branch service/queue/synchronization terms would count parallel work and overlapping waiting multiple times. Only the fork-join stage \(T^{FJ}_c\), or the critical branch’s queue plus service, enters the total-flow decomposition.

Here `q+s = branch_queue + branch_service = branch_end - fork_release`, i.e. the branch completion offset from fork release. Join ready is the maximum of these offsets.

| A | 50.00 | 50.00 | 50–50 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0–0 | 50.00 | 50 | 2/12 = 16.7% | 11.00 | 10.0 | 25 |
| B | 37.50 | 37.50 | 30–45 | 19.17 | 20.00 | 5–30 | 56.67 | 75 | 8/12 = 66.7% | 4.33 | 0.0 | 20 |
| C | 20.17 | 20.00 | 18–30 | 10.42 | 5.00 | 5–35 | 30.58 | 57 | 2/12 = 16.7% | 30.42 | 32.5 | 52 |

Critical-branch cases:

- A critical: F01, F11.
- B critical: F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12.
- C critical: F04, F08.

**Influence ranking.** B is clearly the most influential branch overall. It is critical in 8 of 12 cases, has the highest mean queue-plus-service offset, and has the largest observed branch completion offset at 75 minutes. Importantly, B’s mean service time is only 37.5 minutes, below A’s constant 50 minutes. If one looked only at service time, A would appear more important, but B’s queue delay plus service makes it the dominant critical-path contributor. A queue reduction on B can shorten many cases, though A or C can still cap the benefit.

A and C are near-tied for second place, with different risk profiles. A is a stable 50-minute floor: it is critical in F01 and F11 and becomes limiting after B queue reductions in several counterfactual cases. C has lower mean queue-plus-service but a heavier tail: F04 has C offset 57 minutes and F08 has C offset 55 minutes, and C is critical in exactly those two cases. Thus, for expected or typical control, A is arguably second because it is a persistent floor. For tail/outlier risk, C is arguably second because its maximum offset exceeds A’s. C’s very large mean synchronization wait, 30.42 minutes, does **not** mean C controls the join; it mainly reflects that C often finishes early and waits for A/B.

Overall: **B > A  C**, with B dominant, A ahead for mean/floor control, and C ahead for rare tail control.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

For CF1 and CF2 below, the reported saving is the reduction in `join_ready`, equivalently the total-flow saving **only if downstream final starts can move earlier by the same amount** while preserving the observed downstream queue behavior. If final starts are fixed by release policy or resource availability, the realized total-flow saving from CF1/CF2 can be zero even when `join_ready` improves, because the improvement is absorbed as extra downstream waiting. CF3 directly measures removal of that downstream waiting.

| Counterfactual | Mechanism | Per-case `join_ready`/potential savings | Aggregate potential saving | Cases with zero or capped benefit | Main interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| CF1 | Reduce A service by 20%: A service \(50\to40\) | F01 \(+5\), F11 \(+10\), all others 0 | 15 total, mean 1.25 | Zero in F02–F10, F12 because B or C remains critical; F01 is capped by B; F11 becomes an A/B tie | A acceleration helps only when A is critical. It is a poor standalone lever in this sample. |
| CF2 | Reduce B queue delay by 50% | F02 \(+10\), F03 \(+10\), F05 \(+10\), F06 \(+15\), F07 \(+15\), F09 \(+12.5\), F10 \(+5\), F12 \(+15\) | 92.5 total, mean 7.71 | Zero in F01, F04, F08, F11; partial/capped by A in F02, F05, F06, F10; C caps F04/F08 | B is the strongest branch lever, but not universal. It frequently shifts the constraint toward A. |
| CF3 | Add final capacity or eliminate downstream queue: set \(final\_start = join\_ready\) | F01 8, F02 15, F03 15, F04 18, F05 10, F06 15, F07 15, F08 15, F09 15, F10 20, F11 20, F12 10 | 176 total, mean 14.67 | None under the assumption of sufficient final capacity; feasibility uncertain because current waits may be release policy rather than pure capacity shortage | The largest single potential flow-time lever in the observed data, but it may be partly a policy/availability issue rather than a capacity issue. |

**CF1 direct benefit and secondary effects.** Reducing A’s service by 20% removes 10 minutes of A active work in every case. However, A is critical only in F01 and F11, and even F01 is capped because B remains at 08:55. Thus most cases receive no join-ready improvement. If downstream final starts are fixed, the additional A speed may simply increase A’s synchronization waiting or reduce A resource utilization without reducing total flow time. Secondary effects: faster A may reduce A’s resource load and can matter after B improvements, because A’s 50-minute active time becomes a hard floor once B queue is reduced.

**CF2 direct benefit and secondary effects.** Halving B queue delay is much more effective because B is critical in 8/12 cases. It produces a substantial aggregate potential saving of 92.5 minutes. But the benefit is not uniform: C-critical cases F04 and F08 remain unchanged, and A-critical cases F01/F11 remain unchanged. Several B cases become A-limited after the B improvement, e.g. F05 reaches A’s 10:00 completion, F06 becomes an A/B tie at 10:15, and F10 becomes limited by A at 11:15. This shows that B acceleration alone exposes the A floor. If final starts are fixed, the B queue reduction may increase downstream waiting instead of improving final completion.

**CF3 direct benefit and secondary effects.** Eliminating downstream final queueing has the largest aggregate potential effect, 176 minutes over 12 cases. This would reduce final completion in every case, with largest savings in F10 and F11, both 20 minutes. However, the observed `final_start - join_ready` must not be blindly interpreted as pure resource-capacity queue; it could reflect final-station release policy, batching, staffing availability, or dispatch rules. Adding a third final resource may create idle capacity if upstream is not ready, while changing release policy may be cheaper but must preserve decision quality and fairness. After removing downstream queueing, the fork-join stage becomes the visible bottleneck, making B/A/C improvements more directly useful. Counterfactual effects should not be assumed additive: max-based branch constraints and finite final capacity interact.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Recommendation 1: Reduce B queue delay and improve B scheduling/capacity.**  
Because B is critical in 8/12 cases and has the largest mean queue-plus-service offset, the first operational target should be B queueing. This could mean adding temporary B capacity during peaks, reducing batching before B, pre-staging machine stress tests, or scheduling B earlier relative to its fork release. A 50% B queue reduction is estimated to reduce join-ready time by 92.5 minutes in aggregate in the sample, but the actual throughput gain depends on whether final release can start earlier. Flow-time tail should improve in B-dominated long cases such as F07 and F12, though tail risk from C remains in F04/F08. Resource utilization: if capacity is added, individual B utilization may fall unless volume grows; if scheduling is improved without extra capacity, B utilization may rise and queue may fall. Synchronization effects: reducing B wait will make A and C wait longer relative to join-ready in some cases, and A becomes the new constraint in several cases. Possible new bottleneck: A’s 50-minute service floor and occasional C tails. Pilot first by measuring B queue causes, B active service, critical-branch frequency before/after, join-ready reduction, and whether final start actually advances under current final-station policy.

**Recommendation 2: Improve final Release Decision scheduling/capacity, but first verify whether downstream waiting is a true queue.**  
The observed downstream wait is large: 176 total minutes across cases. Before adding permanent final capacity, pilot a release-policy change or flexible scheduling that allows final work to start closer to join-ready when a resource is free. If measured final-resource utilization and queueing justify it, add cross-trained or temporary final capacity in peak windows. Throughput may improve if final is a real constraint; if final is not capacity-constrained, the gain will appear mostly as reduced flow time and WIP. Flow-time tail should benefit in cases with large downstream waits, especially F10 and F11. Resource utilization: adding capacity can reduce final utilization and create idle time, so a scheduling/policy fix should be tested first. Possible new bottleneck: the upstream fork-join stage, especially B and the A floor. Synchronization effects: reducing downstream waiting does not change branch synchronization, but it makes branch improvements visible in final completion. Pilot by measuring final-resource busy time, ready-to-start delay, queue by resource, reasons for delayed start, and whether earlier starts are possible without compromising decision quality.

print(cf_agg.round(2).to_string(index=False))

# 7. Limits of inference

These timestamps identify completed events and allow the stated max-based AND-join decomposition only under the supplied process model. They do not identify causality. A long B queue may be caused by B resource capacity, scheduling rules, upstream batching, priority policies, staffing, breakdowns, or data-recording conventions; the data do not distinguish among these.

Branch resource capacity, resource identity, calendars, shifts, multiple parallel servers, preemption, rework, and queue discipline are not supplied. Therefore branch utilization cannot be computed, and the observed queue and service times cannot be cleanly attributed to capacity versus policy. The final station’s waits may be true queueing, but they may also reflect deliberate release policy, final review batching, limited approval availability, or other unobserved constraints. `final_start - join_ready` is therefore an upper-label “downstream queueing” measure only under the stated assumptions.

The one-minute timestamp accuracy can hide sub-minute waits and may make ties or near-ties arbitrary. Counterfactual results assume no changes in other branches, no capacity interactions, and no behavioral response; actual redesigns can shift the critical branch, change downstream congestion, and create new bottlenecks. The sample has only 12 cases, so aggregate means and tail observations are illustrative rather than statistically robust. Finally, synchronization waiting is derived, not directly logged: it is valid as a model-based construct, but it should not be interpreted as observed idle time for a specific branch resource without additional data.
