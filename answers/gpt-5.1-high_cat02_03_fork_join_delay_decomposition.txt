# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

## 1.1 Notation

For each case \(c\) and branch \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\), define the following timestamps:

- \(a_c\): `arrival`
- \(f_c\): `fork_release`
- \(s_{c,i}\): branch start time (`A_start`, `B_start`, `C_start`)
- \(e_{c,i}\): branch completion time (`A_end`, `B_end`, `C_end`)
- \(j_c\): `join_ready` \(= \max_i e_{c,i}\) (latest branch completion)
- \(g_c\): `final_start`
- \(h_c\): `final_end`

All timestamps are on the same calendar day, so elapsed times are well-defined as differences in minutes.

## 1.2 Component definitions

Using the notation above, the delay components for case \(c\) and branch \(i\) are:

- **Upstream elapsed time before fork release**

  \[
  U_c = f_c - a_c
  \]

- **Branch-specific queue waiting** (from fork release until the branch actually starts; non-negative by construction):

  \[
  Q_{c,i} = \max\{0,\, s_{c,i} - f_c\}
  \]

- **Branch active service time**:

  \[
  S_{c,i} = e_{c,i} - s_{c,i}
  \]

- **Branch elapsed time from fork release to branch completion**:

  \[
  L_{c,i} = Q_{c,i} + S_{c,i} = e_{c,i} - f_c
  \]

- **Join-ready time** (latest completion across all branches):

  \[
  j_c = \max_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} e_{c,i}
  \]

- **Branch-specific synchronization waiting after completion** (time a completed branch waits until all branches are finished):

  \[
  W_{c,i} = j_c - e_{c,i} \ge 0
  \]

- **Elapsed fork-join stage time** (from fork release to join ready):

  \[
  FJ_c = j_c - f_c = \max_i L_{c,i}
  \]

- **Downstream queueing before the final activity** (given the modelling assumption in the prompt):

  \[
  D_c = g_c - j_c
  \]

- **Final service time**:

  \[
  F_c = h_c - g_c
  \]

- **Total case flow time**:

  \[
  T_c = h_c - a_c
  \]

These quantities satisfy the per-case identity

\[
T_c = U_c + FJ_c + D_c + F_c.
\]

## 1.3 Why summing branch synchronization waits double counts elapsed time

From the definitions,

\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_{c,i} + S_{c,i} + W_{c,i}
&= (s_{c,i} - f_c) + (e_{c,i} - s_{c,i}) + (j_c - e_{c,i}) \\
&= j_c - f_c \\
&= FJ_c.
\end{aligned}
\]

So **each branch individually experiences the full fork-join stage duration** \(FJ_c\) when we sum its own queue, service, and synchronization waiting.

If we were to sum this over the three branches,

\[
\sum_{i \in \{A,B,C\}} (Q_{c,i} + S_{c,i} + W_{c,i})
  = 3 \cdot FJ_c.
\]

This shows explicitly that the calendar time from `fork_release` to `join_ready` would be **triple-counted** if we simply added the branch-specific synchronization waits. The correct **elapsed** fork-join time is \(FJ_c = j_c - f_c\) (one interval on the clock), while the branch-level waits \(W_{c,i}\) are only meaningful for **attribution and resource-wait analysis**, not for a non-overlapping time decomposition.

Concretely, in case F01 the calendar interval from the first branch completion to `join_ready` is 22 minutes, but two branches wait in parallel (5 and 22 minutes respectively), so the sum of synchronization waits (27 minutes) overstates the actual synchronization period. The decomposition in this answer therefore uses \(FJ_c\) as the elapsed fork-join stage time and treats \(W_{c,i}\) as branch-level diagnostics.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

## 3.1 Summary statistics by branch

From the per-case calculations, the following branch-level summaries (in minutes) are obtained. Queue and service times are measured from `fork_release` onward.

| branch | mean_service_min | min_service_min | max_service_min | mean_queue_min | max_queue_min | mean_queue_plus_service_min | times_critical | critical_frequency | mean_sync_wait_min |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 50.0 | 50 | 50 | 0.0 | 0 | 50.0 | 2 | 0.17 | 11.0 |
| B | 37.5 | 30 | 45 | 19.2 | 30 | 56.7 | 8 | 0.67 | 4.3 |
| C | 20.2 | 18 | 30 | 10.4 | 35 | 30.6 | 2 | 0.17 | 30.4 |

Key observations (all are **observations** or **estimates** from the given 12 cases):

- **Branch A**
  - Service time is long but constant at 50 minutes with zero queue.
  - A is critical (last to complete) in only 2 of 12 cases (F01, F11).
  - It experiences moderate synchronization waits on average (11 minutes), meaning A is frequently *not* the last-completing branch despite its long service.

- **Branch B**
  - Mean service time is shorter than A’s (37.5 minutes), but B builds substantial queue (mean \(\approx 19.2\) minutes, up to 30 minutes).
  - The combined queue + service for B averages about 56.7 minutes, longer than A’s 50 minutes and much longer than C’s 30.6 minutes.
  - B is the critical branch in 8 of 12 cases (two-thirds of the sample), and when it is critical its queue+service time from `fork_release` to completion averages about 65 minutes, with a tail up to 75 minutes (F07 and F12).
  - B’s mean synchronization wait is only about 4.3 minutes, consistent with its frequent role as the last-completing branch.

- **Branch C**
  - C’s own processing (queue + service) is typically short (mean around 30.6 minutes), but it shows occasional long queues (35 minutes in F04 and 25 minutes in F08).
  - C is critical in 2 of 12 cases (F04 and F08), with queue+service from `fork_release` of 57 and 55 minutes respectively when critical.
  - C has the **largest mean synchronization wait** (around 30.4 minutes), indicating documentation often finishes well before the slowest technical branch and then waits for synchronization.

At the **fork-join stage level**:

- The elapsed fork-join durations \(FJ_c = j_c - f_c\) range from 50 to 75 minutes with a mean around 61 minutes.
- Upstream elapsed time \(U_c\) averages about 21 minutes (35 minutes in F01, 20 minutes otherwise), so the fork-join stage dominates the middle of the process.
- Downstream queueing before the final step averages about 14–15 minutes per case, with every case experiencing some final-station queueing.

## 3.2 Influence on join_ready and total completion time

Taking into account the factors requested in the prompt:

- **Frequency of being last-completing**:
  - B is critical in 8/12 cases, A and C in 2/12 each.
  - This already suggests that B most often controls `join_ready` and therefore the earliest possible start of the final step.

- **Average and tail contribution to join_ready**:
  - When A is critical, its `fork_release`–to–completion time is always 50 minutes (no variation).
  - When B is critical, its `fork_release`–to–completion time averages about 65 minutes and reaches up to 75 minutes; these long tails in B directly drive late `join_ready` times in cases like F07 and F12.
  - C is critical only in F04 and F08, with queue+service times of 57 and 55 minutes, substantial but less frequent.

- **Queue delay plus service, not service alone**:
  - Although A has the **largest mean service time**, its **zero queue** and relatively modest synchronization waits mean that A rarely determines `join_ready`.
  - B’s moderate service plus large queue makes its *effective* path (queue + service) the longest in expectation, explaining why B dominates the critical-path frequency.
  - C’s queue + service is usually shortest but with sporadic spikes (F04 and F08) that occasionally make C critical.

- **Downstream congestion interaction**:
  - Downstream queueing adds on average about 14–15 minutes beyond `join_ready` for *every* case.
  - In some cases (for example F10, F11, F12) downstream queueing of 20 minutes significantly contributes to total flow time, partially masking upstream improvements.

**Influence ranking (hypothesis based on the 12 cases):**

1. **Branch B (Machine Stress Test)** most strongly controls total completion time among the three branches, because it:
   - is critical in two-thirds of cases,
   - has the highest mean queue+service time, and
   - produces the longest fork-join tails when it is critical.
2. **Branch C (Documentation Review)** contributes to synchronization waste more than to completion time: it is often early and then waits, but occasionally (F04, F08) becomes critical with long queues.
3. **Branch A (Laboratory Assay)**, despite its long and constant service time, is usually overtaken by B’s queues and only occasionally sets `join_ready`.

However, **final-station congestion** is also an important driver: even when B is dominant at the fork-join stage, the additional 10–20 minutes of downstream waiting per case mean that improvements upstream can be partially hidden unless final-station capacity is also addressed.


# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

This section evaluates three counterfactual changes, all expressed in terms of **direct per-case effects** under the simplifying **assumption** that:

- **Assumption 1 (local queueing effect)**: For the first-order analysis, reducing a branch time changes that case’s `join_ready` but leaves the **observed downstream queue interval** \(D_c = g_c - j_c\) unchanged. In reality, join-time shifts would interact with the shared resources of the final station and possibly change queueing patterns across cases; these are discussed as **likely secondary effects** rather than quantified precisely.

Under this assumption, a reduction in `join_ready` for a case directly translates into an equal reduction in its final completion time, unless another branch becomes critical and limits the gain.

## 4.1 Scenario 1 – Reducing A service time by 20%

**Definition (counterfactual):**

- For all cases, reduce A’s service time from 50 minutes to 40 minutes (20% reduction), keeping:
  - A’s queue \(Q_{c,A}\) unchanged (0 in all cases),
  - all B and C times unchanged,
  - downstream queueing \(D_c\) and final service \(F_c\) unchanged.

**Per-case direct effects on join_ready:**

- In cases where A is **not** critical (10 of 12 cases), `join_ready` is determined by B or C, so pulling A earlier has **zero direct effect** on `join_ready`.
- In **F01** (originally A-critical):
  - Original A completion: 09:00; B and C complete at 08:55 and 08:38.
  - With 20% faster A, A would complete at 08:50.
  - New `join_ready` becomes 08:55 (branch B), a **5-minute** reduction (from 09:00).
- In **F11** (A-critical):
  - Original A completion: 11:30; B and C complete at 11:20 and 11:03.
  - With 20% faster A, A completes at 11:20.
  - New `join_ready` becomes 11:20, a **10-minute** reduction.

**Aggregate direct effect (estimate):**

- Only two cases (F01, F11) gain directly:
  - F01: 5 minutes shorter flow time.
  - F11: 10 minutes shorter flow time.
- Across all 12 cases, the total reduction in flow time is about **15 minutes**, an average of roughly **1.25 minutes per case**.

**Secondary effects (hypotheses):**

- Earlier A completions will slightly increase the average synchronization waiting experienced by B or C when A is no longer critical, but since A is rarely critical, the global synchronization pattern changes little.
- Because downstream queueing is still substantial (10–20 minutes per case), the perceived improvement at the final station will be small; throughput in cases per hour is likely almost unchanged with this isolated A-speed-up.

Overall, **speeding up A alone yields limited benefit**, since A is seldom the bottleneck in the fork-join stage.

## 4.2 Scenario 2 – Reducing B queue delay by 50%

**Definition (counterfactual):**

- For all cases, reduce B’s queue from `fork_release` by 50%:
  - New queue: \(Q'_{c,B} = 0.5 \, Q_{c,B}\).
  - B’s service duration \(S_{c,B}\) is unchanged.
  - All other branches, downstream queueing, and final service remain as observed (Assumption 1).

This shifts B’s completion time earlier by \(0.5 \, Q_{c,B}\) minutes in each case.

**Per-case effects on join_ready (direct):**

- **Zero-benefit cases because another branch remains critical**:
  - **F01**: A is and remains last; even with B’s queue halved, A still finishes later, so `join_ready` is unchanged.
  - **F04** and **F08**: C is critical and remains last; B’s speed-up cannot pull `join_ready` earlier.
  - **F11**: A is critical and remains last; B’s improvement does not change `join_ready`.

- **Positive-benefit cases (B currently critical or becomes critical):**
  - **F02** and **F03**: B is critical, so halving its queue reduces `join_ready` directly by 10 minutes in each case.
  - **F05**: B is critical initially; halving its queue would allow A to become the new last finisher. `join_ready` shifts from B’s old completion to A’s completion, yielding a 10-minute improvement.
  - **F06** and **F07**: B remains critical; `join_ready` is reduced by 15 minutes in each case.
  - **F09**: B remains critical; `join_ready` is reduced by 12.5 minutes.
  - **F10**: B’s faster completion allows A to become limiting; `join_ready` is reduced by 5 minutes.
  - **F12**: B remains critical; `join_ready` is reduced by 15 minutes.

**Aggregate direct effect (estimate):**

- Total direct reduction in flow time across all 12 cases is about **92.5 minutes**, an average of roughly **7.7 minutes per case**.
- 8 of 12 cases benefit directly; 4 cases show **zero direct benefit** because they were and remain constrained by A or C.

**Secondary effects (hypotheses):**

- Shorter B queues will reduce variability and tails of `join_ready`, which may also **smooth downstream queues** at the final station, potentially giving slightly more than the direct per-case gains estimated under Assumption 1.
- As B becomes less of a bottleneck, the effective bottleneck may shift:
  - to **A** in several cases (as already seen in F05 and F10), and/or
  - to the **final decision station**, where downstream queueing is already material.

Overall, **reducing B’s queue by 50% delivers much larger gains than speeding up A’s service**, both in mean flow time and in tail reduction.

## 4.3 Scenario 3 – Adding final-station capacity / reducing downstream queueing

**Definition (counterfactual):**

- Model the effect of adding final-station capacity (or improving scheduling/pooling) as a **50% reduction in downstream queueing** for each case:
  - New downstream queue: \(D'_c = 0.5 \, D_c\).
  - `join_ready` and branch times are unchanged.
  - Final service times \(F_c\) are unchanged.

**Per-case direct effects:**

- Every case experiences some downstream queueing (8–20 minutes), so every case benefits from reducing \(D_c\).
- Direct flow-time reduction per case is exactly half of the original downstream queue:
  - For example:
    - F01: original downstream queue 8 minutes → 4 minutes saving.
    - F07: original downstream queue 15 minutes → 7.5 minutes saving.
    - F10 and F11: original downstream queue 20 minutes → 10 minutes saving each.

**Aggregate direct effect (estimate):**

- The sum of downstream queueing across all cases is 176 minutes.
- A 50% reduction therefore yields about **88 minutes** of aggregate direct savings, averaging roughly **7.3 minutes per case**, similar in scale to the B-queue reduction scenario.

**Secondary effects (hypotheses):**

- With more capacity at the final station, some **upstream bottlenecks become more visible**:
  - Cases whose `join_ready` is already late (especially B-critical cases) will still arrive late; their completion times may continue to dominate tails even after the final step is accelerated.
  - Synchronization waiting upstream (especially for branch C) will not decrease just because final capacity is added.
- However, reduced downstream queueing should:
  - improve **throughput** (cases per hour completed at F1/F2),
  - reduce **overall cycle times uniformly**, and
  - diminish the probability of long tail cases driven primarily by final-station congestion.

## 4.4 Comparative view of the three redesigns

Putting the three scenarios side by side:

- **Scenario 1 (A service –20%)**:
  - Benefits only the few cases where A is critical (F01 and F11).
  - Aggregate saving \(\approx 15\) minutes; about 1.25 minutes per case on average.
  - Leaves both B’s queueing and final-station congestion essentially unchanged.

- **Scenario 2 (B queue –50%)**:
  - Directly improves 8 of 12 cases; 4 have zero benefit because another branch remains critical.
  - Aggregate saving \(\approx 92.5\) minutes; about 7.7 minutes per case.
  - Substantially reduces join-ready tails and shifts the bottleneck toward final-station congestion and, in some cases, branch A.

- **Scenario 3 (downstream queue –50%)**:
  - Benefits every case, because all have positive downstream queueing.
  - Aggregate saving \(\approx 88\) minutes; about 7.3 minutes per case.
  - Does not change which branch is critical at the fork-join stage but directly attacks a large and uniform source of delay.

**Overall conclusion (hypothesis):**

- Among branch-focused changes, **reducing B’s queue** has the largest effect on fork-join performance and a large effect on total flow time.
- Reducing downstream queueing at the final station is **at least as powerful** as halving B’s queue in aggregate, and likely easier to realize as a generic capacity or scheduling improvement.
- Speeding up A’s service alone has **limited leverage** and would not be a first-priority redesign unless there are additional external reasons to accelerate A.


# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

Based on the decomposition and counterfactuals, the following two redesigns are recommended, with attention to throughput, tail behavior, utilization, bottlenecks, and synchronization effects.

## 5.1 Redesign 1 – Reduce B’s queue via capacity and scheduling improvements

**Recommendation (design):**

- Treat the Machine Stress Test (branch B) as a primary internal bottleneck and:
  - increase its effective capacity (more equipment, longer staffed hours, or better maintenance to avoid downtime), and/or
  - smooth its arrival pattern from `fork_release` (e.g., scheduling rules, prioritizing older cases, or limiting batching that causes surges).

**Throughput and flow-time tail effects (hypotheses):**

- Throughput should improve once B is less congested, because fewer cases will be held back at the fork-join stage.
- The long tail cases (especially F07 and F12) are partly driven by B’s large queue+service times; reducing B’s queue is likely to **shorten the upper tail** of total flow times, not just the mean.

**Resource utilization and new bottlenecks:**

- Utilization at B will drop somewhat under this redesign (by design), freeing capacity and shifting the bottleneck:
  - In some cases, **A** will become limiting at the fork-join stage, as seen in the counterfactual behavior of F05 and F10.
  - At the system level, the bottleneck may move to the **final decision station**, making downstream queueing relatively more important.

**Synchronization effects:**

- Faster B completion reduces how long other branches (especially C) must wait for synchronization.
- Some synchronization waste may still remain when A is slower or when final-station congestion causes additional waiting after `join_ready`.

**What to pilot or measure first:**

- Measure:
  - the empirical distribution of B’s queue length and waiting times across more days,
  - correlations between B’s load and overall flow-time tails,
  - whether B shares resources with other processes that are not visible in this snippet.
- Pilot:
  - small-scale capacity increases or scheduling changes at B and monitor: (i) changes in B’s queue, (ii) shifts in the critical-branch frequencies, and (iii) changes in downstream queueing.

## 5.2 Redesign 2 – Increase final-station capacity / pooling to cut downstream queueing

**Recommendation (design):**

- Add capacity or implement better pooling at the final decision station (F1/F2), for example by:
  - adding another decision resource or widening coverage windows,
  - cross-training staff to flex between branches and final decisions,
  - improving dispatch rules to keep both F1 and F2 highly utilized but with shorter queues.

**Throughput and flow-time tail effects (hypotheses):**

- Since every case waits 8–20 minutes downstream, reducing this queue is a **direct and uniform lever** on total flow time.
- The upper tail of flow times, especially for already late B-critical cases, will shrink by the full amount of queue reduction at the final station.
- Throughput (cases released per hour) should increase, unless constrained elsewhere (e.g. B).

**Resource utilization and new bottlenecks:**

- Final-station utilization will decrease somewhat after capacity is added or pooling is improved, but:
  - this may reveal the **fork-join stage (especially B)** as the dominant bottleneck more clearly,
  - the system may become more sensitive to variability in B or in upstream arrival patterns.

**Synchronization effects:**

- Reducing downstream queueing does **not** alter the fork-join synchronization pattern among A, B, and C:
  - branches will still wait for each other up to `join_ready`,
  - but cases will proceed much faster once ready, limiting the total time they spend in the system after all branch work is complete.

**What to pilot or measure first:**

- Measure:
  - actual utilization of F1 and F2 (are they truly saturated or is there avoidable idle time?),
  - the distribution of `final_start - join_ready` over a longer horizon,
  - the sequencing of cases at the final station (to confirm it behaves like a queue rather than a policy-based delay).
- Pilot:
  - adding temporary decision capacity or changing scheduling rules during busy periods,
  - tracking resulting changes in downstream queueing and whether upstream bottlenecks become more prominent.

Overall, a **combined redesign** that first reduces B’s queue and then trims final-station queueing appears most promising, with A-service acceleration as a lower-priority fine-tuning step.

# 7. Limits of inference

Several important aspects of the underlying process cannot be identified from these timestamps alone:

- **Causality of delays**:
  - The data show *when* waiting occurs but not *why* (e.g., machine downtime, operator unavailability, rework, batching, or prioritization rules).
  - We cannot distinguish between random variability and systematic scheduling or policy effects.

- **Branch resource capacity and sharing**:
  - The number of servers or staff in each branch and at the final station is unknown.
  - It is unclear whether branch B shares resources with other processes or whether F1 and F2 are always dedicated to these cases.
  - Without explicit resource logs, we cannot separate **true capacity constraints** from apparent delays caused by external tasks.

- **Preemption and multitasking**:
  - The complete-event timestamps show only start and end times, not whether work is preempted or interleaved with other tasks.
  - An apparent long service time could in reality be fragmented work with interruptions that are not logged.

- **Nature of final-station waiting**:
  - The interval `final_start - join_ready` is interpreted here as **queueing**, but it could partly reflect a **release policy**:
    - batching decisions (e.g., deciding cases in groups),
    - scheduled decision rounds,
    - additional unlogged checks or approvals between branch completion and formal `final_start`.
  - Without more detailed event logs or policy descriptions, we cannot be sure this is a pure FIFO queue.

- **Generalisability and stability**:
  - The analysis is based on 12 cases from a single day; no assumptions of stationarity or long-run averages can be safely made.
  - Apparent bottlenecks on this day might not persist under different loads, case mixes, or staffing levels.

- **Inter-case interactions in counterfactuals**:
  - The counterfactual analyses treat each case’s downstream queue as unchanged when upstream times are modified, isolating **direct effects**.
  - In reality, speeding up branches or adding final capacity would change the arrival pattern to shared resources and thus alter queueing for other cases.
  - A full evaluation would require simulation or broader logs to capture these system-level feedback effects.

These limitations mean that the conclusions here should be treated as **well-motivated hypotheses** based on the supplied timestamps, suitable for guiding pilots and further measurement rather than as definitive causal statements about the underlying process.
