## 1. Interaction-graph description

The evidence is best read as a typed object-interaction graph, not as one process model. The important node types are:

- **Job**: J1–J6, the flow objects whose total elapsed time managers care about.
- **Component**: C1–C6, physical items required by jobs.
- **ComponentLot**: L7 contains C2 and C5; L8 contains C1 and C3. Lots create quality/release coupling.
- **Machine**: CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1. Machines are activity hosts, but not necessarily causal bottlenecks.
- **Tool**: FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3. Tools can constrain machines and jobs even when machine utilization is low.
- **Batch**: B1 batches J1, J2, J3; B2 batches J4, J5. Batches create synchronization barriers.
- **Shipment**: S1 consolidates J1 and J4; S2 consolidates J2 and J3; S3 consolidates J5 and J6. Shipments create end-of-process partner waiting.

Important edge types and qualifiers include:

- `requires`: Job  Component.
- `contains`: ComponentLot  Component.
- `batches`: Batch  Job.
- `consolidates`: Shipment  Job.
- `uses` / `related_to`: Job/Batch  Machine/Tool/Component, often inferred from timestamped events.
- `holds` / `acquires` / `releases`: Job  Tool, with timestamps.
- `quarantined` / `released`: ComponentLot  Component/Job, with timestamps.
- `packed` / `dispatched`: Job/Shipment lifecycle events.
- `ready` / `starts` / `tests` / `recalibrates`: event-level timing anchors.

At least three higher-order motifs involve more than two object types:

1. **Batch synchronization star**:  
   J1, J2, J3  B1  OVEN-1 + RACK-7 + C1/C2/C3.

## 4. What flattening misses

**Job viewpoint**  
A job flattening makes visible the activity sequence for J1–J6: release, staging, machine process, inspection, rework, pack, dispatch. It hides waiting that is not represented as a job event, such as waiting for batch partners, tools, lot release, or shipment partners. It can duplicate rework loops without showing which resource caused the rework. A misleading conclusion is that the process is stable because no job total is more than 1.8 standard deviations above the mean, even though J1’s packed-to-dispatch wait in S1 is 5 h 55 min and J1/J2 wait hours in B1.

**Machine viewpoint**  
A machine flattening makes visible utilization, event gaps, and typical sequences. It hides the object-level reason for gaps. For example, OVEN-1 utilization is 44% and its longest gap is 73 minutes, but B1 creates multi-hour waiting for J1 and J2, and B2 follows an OVEN-1 idle period from 12:55 to 14:50. A misleading conclusion is that OVEN-1 is underutilized and therefore not important. Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting when the machine waits for batches, tools, lots, or shipments rather than for a queue of jobs.

**Batch viewpoint**  
A batch flattening makes visible batch start, cure, unload, and synchronization. It hides individual job readiness and the contribution of component lots or tools. Because there are only two observed Batch–Machine interactions, a flattening may suggest low impact. That is misleading: B1 alone contains 7 h 35 min of early-job synchronization waiting for J1 and J2.

**Tool viewpoint**  
A tool flattening makes visible fixture holds, rack cleaning, calibration, and recalibration. It hides inferred job waiting because there is no explicit `Wait for Tool` event. A misleading conclusion is that FIX-A is not a bottleneck because J3’s job log looks normal, even though J4 waits at CNC-2 while FIX-A is held until 11:40.

**ComponentLot viewpoint**  
A lot flattening makes visible quarantine and release events. It hides job impact if lot events are not consistently related to jobs. The L7 quarantine/release is visible, but the job flattening omits it. A misleading conclusion is that the 3 h 30 min L7 hold had no job-level effect, even though J5 did not proceed until after release and J2 required an override.

**Shipment viewpoint**  
A shipment flattening makes visible `Create Shipment -> Load -> Dispatch`. It hides how long an already packed job waits for its shipment partners. The shipment DFG shows `Load -> Dispatch` median of 0.3 h, which suggests fast dispatch. That is misleading for J1, which is packed at 13:30 and dispatches at 19:25.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization/RACK-7/oven explanation:

1. J1 and J2 ready timestamps are system placeholders and actual readiness occurred after 11:50, making the 3 h 55 min and 3 h 40 min waits much smaller.
2. OVEN-1 was occupied, in temperature ramp, or under maintenance until 12:05, so B1 could not have started earlier regardless of job readiness.
3. RACK-7 was unavailable until 12:05 for B1, not only until 14:35 for B2, making tool availability the primary constraint rather than batch synchronization.
4. Batch policy requires a full three-job batch and J3’s late readiness is caused by an upstream process, so the delay is upstream rather than synchronization.
5. L8 or component inspection blocked C1 or C3 until 12:05, so the batch start was constrained by lot/component quality rather than by waiting for J3.
6. J1 and J2 would have waited downstream at TEST-1 or S1 even if cured earlier, so earlier batch start would not improve end-to-end job flow.
7. B1 start at 12:05 is fixed by oven scheduling, shift change, or a maintenance window, independent of job readiness.

Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:

1. Validated ready-time source for each job, including whether “ready” means component staged, fixture ready, machine available, or batch-eligible.
2. Machine and tool state timelines: OVEN-1 available/unavailable, RACK-7 cleaning start/end, FIX-A state, CAL-3 calibration state.
3. Explicit wait events: `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Lot`, `Wait for Batch Partner`, `Wait for Shipment Partner`.
4. Batch policy parameters: minimum/maximum batch size, allowed partial batches, cure recipe, rack capacity, and start-time rules.
5. Lot inspection and release details: quarantine reason, inspector, release authorization, override audit trail, and whether release was conditional.
6. Shipment constraints: truck ETA, customer time window, freight mode, dispatch rule, and whether partial shipment is allowed.
7. Rework reason codes and quality-hold reasons, to separate rework from synchronization or tool contention.
8. Job due dates and customer priority, to determine whether waiting is operationally harmful or acceptable under promise dates.
