# CAUSAL ASSESSMENT: ORION RESOURCE GROUP AND CASE CYCLE TIME

## 1. Executive causal assessment

**Provisional verdict:** `mainly confounded`, confidence 72.

Orion's 6.7-hour longer mean cycle time (21.96 vs 15.24 hours) reverses substantially within complexity strata, particularly for high-complexity cases (Orion 31.57 vs Nova 35.00 hours). This reversal is the key signal of confounding by case mix: Orion receives 42.9% high-complexity cases versus Nova's 12.5%. However, the confounding is not complete. Three mechanisms appear partially genuine:

1. **Night-shift queueing bottleneck**: Orion is 92.9% night-assigned; night work (especially high-load nights) exhibits elevated wait times independent of complexity.
2. **Compliance-check coupling**: Orion's 64.3% compliance rate vs Nova's 25% adds mandatory routing delays, amplifying wait-before-review.
3. **Rework asymmetry**: Orion's 28.6% rework rate (vs Nova's 18.8%) suggests either harder-case residual confounding or sub-standard review quality; the quality signal (appeals) is too sparse to distinguish.

The proposed 50% reassignment will likely **fail or backfire** because:
- It ignores the legitimate role of Orion in high-complexity work where it actually performs *faster* than Nova.
- Removing high-complexity cases from Orion will improve Orion's mean artificially (regression to lower-complexity mean), not because reviewers work better.
- Nova's night-shift capacity is not demonstrated; shifting work risks overloading day operations and degrading quality.

**Safest first step:** Reduce queueing and compliance delays rather than reallocate cases.

## 2. Causal graph

```
complexity  routing_rule
                                    
complexity  compliance_required  queue_wait  cycle_time
                                    
                              compliance_performed
                                    
arrival_load  queue_wait  resource_group
                                      
                 review_service  unobserved_reviewer_skill
                      
                    rework  cycle_time
                      
                quality_outcome
                      
                  review_service
                      
unobserved_case_risk  {complexity, compliance_required, rework, quality_outcome}

shift  arrival_load
   
queue_wait (separate night-shift bottleneck)
   
resource_group (Orion  night, Nova  day)

case_completed  {cycle_time, resource_group, routing_rule}
                  [selection bias: we observe only completed cases]
```

**Explanation of arrows:**

*Supported by context:*
- `complexity  routing_rule`: Orion explicitly selected for difficult cases.
- `complexity  compliance_required`: Policy links complexity to mandatory compliance checks.
- `compliance_required  queue_wait`: Compliance checks introduce mandatory sequence steps.
- `shift  arrival_load`: Night shifts have different load profiles (stated in context).
- `resource_group  routing_rule`: Assignment is deterministic post-classification.
- `review_service`, `queue_wait`  `cycle_time`: Timing components compose the total.
- `rework  cycle_time`: Repeated review activities visible in traces.

*Assumptions (not directly tested here):*
- `unobserved_case_risk`: True case difficulty (beyond recorded complexity) drives both routing and reviewer effort.
- `unobserved_reviewer_skill`: Within-group reviewer heterogeneity affects service time and rework.
- `shift  queue_wait` (separate): Night operations may have independent congestion or staffing issues.
- `case_completed` collider: Survivor bias—abandoned or escalated cases are invisible; if Orion has higher completion rates, sample composition is distorted.

**Static-DAG limitation:**
The graph suppresses queueing dynamics: queue length at arrival influences wait, which affects reviewer utilization and downstream queueing. A time-ordered event log with queue-state snapshots would reveal whether Orion queues are chronically long (skill/staffing deficit) or episodically long (case clustering or shift transitions).

**Final Recommendation (120–200 words):**

**Do not implement the proposed 50% reassignment immediately.** The crude difference in cycle times (21.96 vs 15.24 h) is primarily driven by Orion's legitimate assignment to harder, high-complexity cases—a routing choice, not an operational deficiency. Within-complexity strata, Orion's performance is equal or superior.

Instead, pursue a three-phase learning agenda:

1. **Diagnose the mechanism (Phase 0–1):** Establish whether Orion's slightly longer wait times and higher rework are caused by queueing bottlenecks (tunable), compliance-check overhead (process redesign), or genuine skill gaps (mentoring). Phase 1b's micro-randomized experiment on simple cases will isolate any intrinsic resource-group effect.

2. **Improve operations (Phase 2):** If queueing or compliance are the bottlenecks, increase night-shift capacity and streamline compliance checks. These improvements benefit Orion directly and strengthen its role as a high-complexity specialist. If mentoring helps, scale it.

3. **Reassign selectively, if justified (Phase 3):** Only after operational improvements are in place, reassign 20% of eligible medium-complexity cases (non-compliance, low-risk) to Nova, with tight monitoring. This test is safer and more informative than a blanket 50% shift.

**Expected outcome:** Cycle-time reduction of 10–20% through operational improvements, with Orion and Nova better aligned to their respective strengths (night/hard cases vs. day/simple cases). If selective reassignment helps, expand; otherwise, retain status quo. This approach respects the data's complexity and avoids the trap of treating association as causation.

## 8. Residual uncertainty

1. **Escalation and case completion rates.** *Most valuable data:* Count of cases escalated, abandoned, or transferred out per group and complexity tier, and reason for escalation (e.g., unresolvable, requires litigation, exceeds authority). *Why:* If Orion has lower escalation rate, sample composition is distorted, and observed cycle times conflate completion with speed. If Nova escalates hard cases to Orion, within-complexity comparisons are biased.

2. **Unobserved case risk and routing rule details.** *Most valuable data:* The deterministic or discretionary decision rule used to assign cases to Orion vs. Nova after Classify step (e.g., IF complexity = High OR prior_litigation_flag = True THEN Orion; ELSE Nova). Quantify what fraction of routing decisions are deterministic vs. discretionary, and what features drive discretionary decisions. *Why:* If routing is partially discretionary (e.g., reviewer chooses Orion for cases with similar complexity but different risk profiles), unmeasured case risk is a major confounder, and within-complexity strata remain biased.

3. **Reviewer-level skill and effort heterogeneity.** *Most valuable data:* Service time and rework rate per reviewer within each group, controlling for case complexity and compliance status. Identify whether Orion has a higher-skill mix or lower, and whether review times vary more within Orion or Nova. *Why:* High-level group comparisons cannot detect whether Orion's longer times are driven by individual reviewers (actionable via mentoring) or systemic issues (queueing, policy). Reviewer effects could also bias within-group case-mix adjustment.

4. **Queue-state time series and shift-specific dynamics.** *Most valuable data:* Event log with sub-hourly timestamps for Classify, Wait-Queue-Entry, Compliance-Check (if required), Wait-After-Compliance, Review-Start, Review-End, Request-Info-Trigger, Review-Start-2, Review-End, Close. Reconstruct queue length and wait times for each shift and each hour of day. *Why:* Queueing is inherently dynamic; a single wait_before_review_h value masks periods of high congestion and periods of idle capacity. Time-series patterns reveal whether night-shift bottleneck is chronic (staffing) or episodic (case clustering), and whether Orion and Nova queues are separate or shared.

5. **Post-process factors affecting 30-day appeals.** *Most valuable data:* Case outcome follow-up data including: appeal filing date, appeal decision, reason for appeal (e.g., claimant dissatisfied with case handling, external legal claim, regulatory investigation). Do appeals trace back to review quality (e.g., missing evidence, wrong legal interpretation) or external factors (claimant expectations, case dynamics outside reviewers' control)? *Why:* Current 30-day appeal indicator (2/30 cases, 6.7%) is too sparse to infer quality. If appeals are driven by post-process factors, quality is not measured by cycle time or rework, and Orion's safety is not compromised by faster/slower service. If appeals trace to review errors, high-complexity rework (50% in both groups) may reflect insufficient first-pass quality, and longer times may not guarantee better outcomes.
