# 1. Data audit and estimands

**Opening-hours convention and assumptions.**

- The center is open for 12 hours per day, 07:00–19:00, including weekends. Unless stated otherwise, hourly intensities below are totals divided by \(12D\), where \(D\) is the number of selected days. They are average opening-hour intensities, not claims of a constant intraday rate.
- **A1 — WIP scope:** I assume the 15-minute WIP snapshots refer to the same case population and queue boundary as arrivals and completions. It is not stated whether snapshots cover only opening hours or include closed-hour carry-over. This matters for Little’s Law.
- **A2 — Aggregation:** Regime service summaries use ranges or unweighted means of the supplied daily quantiles. A mean of daily medians is not a case-level median, and neither it nor the p90 supplies mean service time.
- **A3 — Net flow:** \(A-C\) denotes arrivals minus completions over a stated window. It is a flow-imbalance diagnostic, not a directly measured or age-resolved backlog change. Initial and final backlog, and any other entries or exits, are unavailable.
- **A4 — Capacity proxies:** Staff FTE is used only to construct descriptive pressure ratios. It is not automatically the number of productive parallel servers.

**Estimands and definitions.**

- Arrival intensity: \(\lambda_A=\sum A/(12D)\), cases per opening hour.
- Completion intensity: \(\lambda_C=\sum C/(12D)\), cases per opening hour. This is observed output, **not** necessarily service capacity or a service-rate parameter \(\mu\): output can be arrival-limited when WIP is low or backlog-fed during recovery.
- Service behavior: daily median and p90 active handling time in minutes, excluding waiting. These do not identify mean handling time, waiting time, or mean case flow time.
- WIP: \(L\) is the equally weighted mean of daily `mean_wip`, in cases. With equal day lengths and complete, comparable snapshots, this approximates an opening-period snapshot average.
- Capacity pressure:
  - arrivals per FTE-open-hour, \(\sum A/(12\sum \mathrm{FTE})\);
  - WIP per FTE, calculated as the ratio of mean WIP to mean FTE, equivalently \(\sum L_d/\sum \mathrm{FTE}_d\) here.
  
  Neither is utilization. A conventional offered-load calculation would require at least mean service time, effective productive capacity, and an appropriate staffing/model structure.

**Threats to naive inference.**

1. **Stock–flow and censoring problems.** The 07:00 backlog is generally missing; cases carry overnight; completions need not have arrived that day. Daily WIP means are not endpoint backlogs.
2. **Wrong time summary.** Median or p90 active handling cannot be substituted for mean flow time or mean service time. Utilization and waiting-time formulas therefore cannot be identified from these fields alone.
3. **Several superimposed non-stationarities.** These include weekday/weekend demand, a 180-minute outage, recovery, the April 20 mix/service change, intraday bursts, and the April 29 staffing intervention.
4. **Serial dependence.** Backlog, staffing responses, persistent case mix, and weekly seasonality connect adjacent days. Twenty-eight daily rows are not 28 independent experiments.
5. **Small and transient regimes.** The outage has one observation; recovery, intervention weekdays, and several weekend regimes have only two or three. Confidence labels below concern descriptive separation, not statistical significance.
6. **Ambiguous intervention exposure.** Staffing changed during April 29, but the implementation time is unknown. April 29 is only partially exposed, and the post-period has no concurrent control.
7. **Case-mix ambiguity.** The increase in `complex_pct` could be a real mix shift, a coding change, or both. The simultaneous service-quantile shift does not distinguish those explanations.
8. **WIP sampling and capacity ambiguity.** Snapshot coverage is not fully specified, and FTE does not reveal breaks, multitasking, productive minutes, skill mix, or outage-specific availability.
9. **An internal hourly-field mismatch.** The hourly vectors sum to the stated daily arrivals and completions, but their 08:00–09:59 arrival shares do not reproduce `peak_08_10_pct`. For example:
   - April 7: \((5+12)/101=16.8\%\), versus 31% reported;
   - April 21: \((3+8)/115=9.6\%\), versus 47% reported;
   - April 25: \((2+4)/51=11.8\%\), versus 22% reported.
   
   If the vectors and daily field are intended to describe exactly the same dates, their definitions or alignment need reconciliation. I therefore use them as separate evidence rather than merging them uncritically.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different alternative is a **backlog-state segmentation**, using flow balance and WIP state rather than treating each calendar/mix era as the main unit:

| Candidate state | Dates and evidence | Operational story |
|---|---|---|
| Near flow balance, subject to endpoint uncertainty | Apr 6–14: \(\sum(A-C)=5\); Apr 18–19: 0; May 2–3: 0 | These may be candidates for approximate congestion calculations, although they still differ greatly in demand and service mix |
| Backlog accumulation | Apr 15: imbalance 47 during outage; Apr 20–28: imbalance 106 during the high-mix era | Both are non-stationary accumulation states, but their causes—capacity loss versus demand/service pressure—must remain separate |
| Backlog draining | Apr 16–17: completions exceed arrivals by 30; Apr 29–May 1: excess completions 18, with declining daily mean WIP | High completion intensity is interpreted as recovery output rather than a new steady-state service regime |

**Strengths relative to the primary segmentation.** This alternative directly addresses Little’s-Law boundary conditions, highlights carry-over, and prevents outage recovery from being interpreted as normal productivity. It also offers a useful operational distinction between “load is rising” and “backlog is being cleared.”

**Weaknesses.** The states are provisional because daily mean WIP is not an endpoint backlog. Grouping by an outcome such as \(A-C\) can induce selection bias, and the accumulation/drain groups combine different arrival and service processes. It is therefore poorly suited to estimating a regime-specific demand or service model without further splitting.

A second defensible sensitivity is a **two-persistent-era model**: before versus after April 20, with weekday/weekend effects and outage, recovery, and staffing interventions as overlays. That story emphasizes a possible coding or service-process change rather than a new demand regime. It is parsimonious, but pooling April 20–28 with April 29 onward would hide the subsequent backlog drain. A hybrid—primary calendar/mix regimes plus a backlog-state overlay—is safer than either segmentation alone.

- The weekly sawtooth in arrivals is unmistakable: weekday totals range from 92 to 121, while weekend totals range from 36 to 53. Raw daily autocorrelation therefore mixes day-of-week structure with shorter-lag dependence.
- WIP displays persistent state: 475235 around outage/recovery; 37, 41, 46 during April 20–22; 4950 immediately before intervention; and 433632 afterward. These are daily means rather than endpoint inventories, but they are inconsistent with treating daily performance as independent.

- The service evidence moves at the same boundary: April 19 has median/p90 of 29/56 minutes, while April 20 has 34/78. Weekday peak percentages also move into the 40–50% range. Nevertheless, a coding change, a real mix shift, and an accompanying service-process change remain competing explanations.

Four representative hourly days cannot identify a full stochastic model. They cannot distinguish a time-varying Poisson process from clustered arrivals, establish the arrival-count distribution, estimate a reliable autocorrelation function, or validate invariant weekly seasonal patterns. Raw interarrival times and substantially more regime-specific days would be needed.

The major non-stationarity sources should remain separate:

- **Demand:** weekday/weekend volume, changing peak concentration, and isolated hourly bursts.
- **Service mix/process:** complex percentage and active-handling quantiles.
- **Capacity:** FTE differences, the outage, and the staffing intervention.
- **Backlog:** arrival–completion imbalance and WIP persistence, which alter the work available for completion.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law does not require Poisson arrivals or a particular service distribution. The difficulty here is finite-window accounting: consistent case boundaries, comparable WIP and throughput, and initial/final carry-over. Near flow balance is a useful practical condition for these aggregates, not a universal mathematical requirement of every form of the law.

Where shown, \(L/\lambda\) uses cases per **opening hour**, so the result is an opening-hour congestion diagnostic. It is not automatically elapsed clock time across overnight closures, and it is not an observed mean case flow time. Completions are the preferred departure-throughput proxy only when the window is close to flow balance; arrivals provide a sensitivity calculation. No median service time is used.

# 7. Operational implications

**Staffing and capacity.**

- Staff separately by day type, service era, and backlog state. A model staffed to the pooled 90 arrivals/day would understate ordinary weekday volume and overstate weekend volume, while also ignoring the much longer high-mix handling times.
- Do not derive a numeric FTE target from the supplied medians and p90s. Estimate mean productive handling time by variant, resource and staffing calendars, and then evaluate time-varying capacity scenarios.
- The intervention comparison supports further investigation of added weekday capacity: arrivals and coded complex mix were nearly unchanged, FTE rose about 15%, completions rose about 20%, and WIP fell. It does not establish a stable causal elasticity. Evaluation should use several more weeks, exact treatment timing, day-of-week controls, starting backlog, and case mix.
- Weekend decisions need capacity-normalized measures. Although absolute weekend WIP is low, WIP/FTE is higher than on weekdays overall, and the pre-intervention high-mix weekend reaches 8.60.

**Backlog recovery.**

- Maintain a distinction between ordinary capacity and temporary recovery capacity. April 16–17 and April 29–May 1 both show high output while backlog is being drained; neither output rate should become the baseline promise without further evidence.
- Track endpoint backlog and its age distribution, not just WIP mean and daily completions. A rise in completions can coexist with poor performance for old or difficult cases if short jobs are completed preferentially.
- The outage day has a 47-case arrival–completion deficit and the following two recovery days a 30-case excess of completions. These figures help size recovery scenarios, but they do not measure how many aged outage cases remain.
- The April 20–28 imbalance of 106 cases and still-elevated post-intervention weekend service times argue against declaring the system fully recovered merely because May 2–3 have balanced daily flows.

**Peak smoothing and scheduling.**

- First reconcile `peak_08_10_pct` with the hourly vectors. Until then, the daily field and hourly evidence should not be used interchangeably.
- Where demand can be scheduled, evaluate smoothing only against validated 15-minute or hourly arrivals. April 21’s largest bursts occur outside 08:00–09:59, so a single early-morning intervention could miss much of the load.
- If demand cannot be shifted, consider targeted cross-trained capacity around validated burst windows rather than a uniform all-day staffing increase. Any such proposal still needs service-mean and skill-mix data.

**Policies that look attractive pooled but are risky segmented.**

1. **Hold staffing flat because completions per FTE-open-hour look stable.** Pooled, ordinary, and high-mix values are all around 1.39–1.41. Under segmentation, high-mix weekdays nevertheless have WIP 44.3, arrivals per FTE-open-hour 1.602, and a 94-case weekday arrival–completion deficit. Backlog-fed output can conceal deteriorating flow performance.
2. **Cut weekend staffing because absolute WIP is low.** This looks reasonable from pooled absolute WIP but is risky when weekend WIP/FTE exceeds weekday WIP/FTE and high-mix weekend handling times remain long.
3. **Use the pooled 3.98–4.16-hour \(L/\lambda\) arithmetic as a customer-flow-time target.** It combines transient recovery, outage, weekly composition, and an accumulating high-mix regime, and it omits closed-hour elapsed time.

**Data collection.**

- Capture case identifier, arrival/queue-entry time, service start and end, completion time, resource, variant, and variant-coding version. Preserve multiple service steps for complex cases.
- Record staffing intervals and productive minutes, exact outage and intervention start/end times, and any other case entries or exits.
- Record 07:00 and 19:00 backlog, backlog age, and WIP snapshot timestamps/scope, including whether closed-hour inventory is represented.
- Store raw event timestamps so that arrival dispersion can be modeled conditionally on time of day, rather than inferred from four daily profiles.
- Reconcile hourly-bin conventions and the peak metric, and collect additional weeks before fitting seasonal, autocorrelation, or causal intervention models.

def dispersion_metrics(values):
    """Descriptive dispersion across bins, not a fitted count-process model."""
    x = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    mean = x.mean()
    variance = x.var(ddof=0)
    return {
        "fano": variance / mean,
        "cv": np.sqrt(variance) / mean,
        "max_share": x.max() / x.sum(),
    }

def lag_corr(values, lag=1):

    return float(np.corrcoef(x[:-lag], x[lag:])[0, 1])

# Transparent one-split heuristic: minimize within-segment SSE for complex_pct.
# It is deliberately not called a validated or unique change point.
def best_two_segment_split(values, min_len=5):
    x = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    candidates = []
    for k in range(min_len, len(x) - min_len + 1):
        left, right = x[:k], x[k:]
        sse = ((left - left.mean()) ** 2).sum() + ((right - right.mean()) ** 2).sum()
        candidates.append((sse, k, left.mean(), right.mean()))
    return min(candidates, key=lambda z: z[0])

# Little's-Law calculations are restricted to explicitly chosen diagnostic windows.
# No flow-time values are calculated for outage, recovery, accumulating high-mix,
# or intervention-transient windows.
def little_diagnostic(name, g):
    hours = OPEN_HOURS * len(g)
    L = g["mean_wip"].mean()
    lambda_a = g["arrivals"].sum() / hours
    lambda_c = g["completed"].sum() / hours
    return {
        "window": name,
        "days": len(g),
        "L": L,
        "A_minus_C": int(g["arrivals"].sum() - g["completed"].sum()),
        "arr_h": lambda_a,
        "comp_h": lambda_c,
        # Opening-hour L/lambda diagnostics, NOT observed elapsed flow times.
        "W_arr_open_h": L / lambda_a,
        "W_comp_open_h": L / lambda_c,
    }
