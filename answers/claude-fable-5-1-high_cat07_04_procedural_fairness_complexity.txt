# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this process is multi-dimensional. Equal approval rates are neither necessary nor sufficient for fairness: a process can produce identical approval rates while imposing very different burdens, and it can produce different approval rates for legitimate reasons. The dimensions below should be assessed separately, because the evidence supports different conclusions on each.

**1.1 Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates are 78% (A), 63% (B), 69% (C), 58% (D); raw gaps of 9 to 20 percentage points. Median cycle time ranges from 8.1 days (A) to 15.9 days (C), and the 90th percentile from 21.4 to 38.8 days. These are real disparities in what applicants experience. They are not, on their own, evidence of discrimination, because the case-mix table shows that the groups differ strongly in risk band, documentation completeness, channel and support needs. Raw parity is the starting point for the assessment, not the criterion.

**1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region and month, the approval gap shrinks to -5.5 pp (B), -2.2 pp (C) and -3.1 pp (D). Roughly two thirds of the raw gap for B and D, and most of the gap for C, is statistically associated with the recorded case-mix. The residual gaps are smaller but not zero, and they must be read with two cautions: (i) some controls are plausibly *mediators* rather than confounders (documentation_completeness is affected by language barriers; risk_score_band may be affected by historical disparities), so adjusting for them can absorb part of the very effect we want to detect; (ii) the residual may also reflect unmeasured legitimate complexity. The adjusted gap is therefore a bounded estimate, not a point measure of unfair treatment.

**1.3 Procedural burden.** How many steps, requests, contacts and handovers does an applicant have to endure to reach a decision? Group B has +9.5 pp probability of Request Missing Documents *even after controlling for recorded documentation completeness*, +8.0 pp probability of Supervisor Review, and 24% of its cases run through V4 with a language-support handover chain. Group C has 31% of cases in V5 (Accommodation Review plus Supervisor Review). Rework/reopen rates are 22% (B) and 24% (C) against 11% (A). Burden is a fairness dimension in its own right: an applicant who is eventually approved after three document requests, two handovers and a supervisor escalation has not received the same procedure as one approved in the straight-through V1 path.

**1.4 Timeliness.** Adjusted cycle-time differences are +2.6 days (B), +4.1 days (C) and +0.9 days (D). The waiting-time table localises this: the largest group-specific waits are Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment (3.8 days, only B) and Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment (4.7 days, only C). Both are queues in front of rights-preserving steps, and the policy sets no timeliness target for them. Delay in a benefits process is not neutral: it delays income support, and it falls on the groups whose rights the steps are meant to protect.

**1.5 Consistency of routing.** Are applicants with the same recorded characteristics routed the same way? Fraud-review routing for Group D (28% in V3 against a 51% high-risk share) is roughly proportional to its risk-band share and to the other groups' ratio (A: 9%/18%), so it appears rule-driven. Supervisor Review is different: the policy lists no rule that sends language-support or accommodation cases to a supervisor, yet V5 contains Supervisor Review as a standard step for Group C and Group B shows +8.0 pp adjusted probability of Supervisor Review. Discretionary escalation that correlates with protected-group membership after adjustment is a consistency concern, whether or not it is well-intentioned.

**1.6 Quality and accessibility of support.** Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review exist to make the process accessible. Whether they achieve that is not observable in the log: the log records that the contact happened and how long it took, not whether the applicant understood the document request, whether the accommodation was actually provided, or whether the explanation of the decision was comprehensible. The fact that Group B still receives more document requests at equal recorded completeness is indirect evidence that the support step may not be fully closing the communication gap.

**1.7 Appeal and rework burden.** Appeal rates are 6% (A), 13% (B), 10% (C), 16% (D); adjusted +4.4 pp (B), +1.1 pp (C), +5.7 pp (D). V6 (appeal path, 30.7 days median) is 13% of Group D. High appeal rates can mean wrongful or poorly explained first-instance decisions, or a group that is more able or more motivated to appeal, or a group whose cases are genuinely contestable. Without appeal *outcomes* (reversal rates) the direction cannot be determined. Rework rates of 22-24% for B and C, roughly double Group A, indicate that a large share of these applicants go through parts of the process twice.

**1.8 Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers; Group B is concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers; Group C on 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. Concentration has three consequences: capacity bottlenecks (the 3.8 and 4.7 day waits), dependence of outcomes on a handful of individual decision-makers (less averaging-out of individual judgement), and role conflicts (a specialist who also reviews as supervisor may effectively be reviewing their own team's work, or may be a single point of failure in both roles). The pattern also suggests that the organisation has sized general capacity for Group A demand and treated support capacity as residual.

**1.9 Dignity and explanation (partially observable).** case_outcome does not record whether the applicant was treated respectfully, whether notices were in the applicant's language, or whether the decision explanation was adequate. This dimension is acknowledged as largely unmeasurable here but should not be dropped from the framework, since it is often the dimension applicants themselves weigh most.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate four things: **disparity** (groups experience different things), **legitimate routing** (differences that follow from policy rules applied to genuinely different cases), **potentially discriminatory or unjustified treatment** (differences that remain when legitimate drivers are held fixed, or that arise from how the rules and resources are designed), and **uncertainty** (what the data cannot resolve). No single model does this; the strategy is a set of mutually checking analyses.

**2.1 Matching or stratification variables.**
- *Legitimate routing triggers* (policy-defined): risk_score_band (Fraud Review), disability_accommodation_requested (Accommodation Review), language_preference (Language Support Contact). These define which steps a case *should* pass through.
- *Complexity indicators*: documentation_completeness at submission, household_size_band, prior_case_count.
- *Operational context*: channel, region, month (workload seasonality), and ideally submission week to absorb backlog effects.
- Build exact or coarsened-exact matched strata on (risk band × documentation completeness × channel × region × prior-case band) and compare groups *within strata*. Where strata are thin, use propensity-score weighting as a secondary check, but report the number of comparable cases per stratum so that comparisons resting on very few Group A in-person, incomplete-documentation, high-risk cases are flagged as weak.
- Treat documentation_completeness and risk_score_band as *both* confounders and potential mediators: run the model with and without them and report both estimates as bounds (see 2.5). Do not include downstream process variables (e.g., whether Supervisor Review occurred) as controls in the outcome model, since they are part of the treatment under study.

**2.2 Path/variant comparison.**
- Define the *policy-expected path* for each case from its triggers: e.g. a minority-language case with incomplete documents should follow Submit → Verify → Language Support → Completeness → Request Docs → Eligibility → Decision → Notify. Compare the observed variant to the expected one and count *unexpected* steps (Supervisor Review, repeated Request Missing Documents, Fraud Review without high risk band) and *missing* steps (no Language Support Contact despite language preference).
- Compare variant distributions within matched strata, not across whole groups. The question is not "does Group C use V5 more" (of course it does; 62% request accommodation) but "among accommodation-requesting cases, does routing differ by group, and among non-accommodation cases, do Group C applicants still have longer paths?"
- Compare cycle times of *structurally similar* variants: V4 vs V2 isolates the marginal cost of the language step (19.2 vs 14.9 days); V5 vs V1 isolates accommodation plus supervisor review (21.6 vs 5.8 days); V3 vs V1 isolates the fraud-review branch.
- Compute per-case counts of Request Missing Documents and Supervisor Review (repetitions matter), and rework loops (activity revisits).

**2.3 Resource-assignment comparison.**
- For each caseworker and supervisor, compute the group composition of their caseload and their approval, Request-Docs and Supervisor-Review rates *within strata*. Identify whether the Group B/C residual is spread across resources or concentrated in a few.
- Compare outcomes for Group B cases handled by bilingual caseworkers vs Group B cases handled by general caseworkers (there will be some), and for Group C cases where the specialist is also the supervisor vs where they are different people.
- Use handover-of-work and working-together networks per group and per region to quantify handover counts and ping-pong patterns (general → bilingual → general).
- Use activity-based resource similarity and organisational role discovery to check whether the "bilingual" and "accommodation" roles are de facto isolated clusters with little overlap with the general pool.

**2.4 Waiting-time decomposition.**
- Decompose each case's cycle time into per-transition waiting times and attribute them to (a) steps everyone passes (Submit → Verify, Decision → Notify), (b) policy-triggered steps (Language Support, Accommodation, Fraud Review), and (c) discretionary steps (Supervisor Review, repeated Request Docs).
- Express the adjusted cycle-time gap as a sum: gap = Σ (extra probability of step × step wait) + Σ (extra wait on shared steps). For Group C, for instance, the 4.7-day accommodation queue times a high V5 share is probably the dominant term; for Group B the 3.8-day bilingual queue and the 1.6-day Completeness → Request Docs wait.
- Distinguish *queue* waiting (resource unavailable) from *applicant* waiting (waiting for documents). The current log has only one timestamp per event, so service and waiting cannot be separated directly; if start timestamps or "documents received" events exist elsewhere they should be merged.

**2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.**
- *risk_score_band*: re-estimate adjusted disparities (i) with the band as a control, (ii) without it, (iii) with the band replaced by its "objective" inputs if available (e.g., prior case count alone). If Group D's adjusted approval gap moves from -3.1 pp to something much larger when the band is removed, the band is doing most of the explanatory work and its own fairness becomes the key question. Check calibration: among cases with the same band, do actual fraud-review findings differ by group? If high-band Group D cases are cleared by Fraud Review more often than high-band Group A cases, the band over-flags Group D.
- *documentation_completeness*: re-estimate with completeness measured only at submission (before any caseworker judgement), and test whether the "incomplete" label is applied at different rates by caseworker for observationally similar cases. Report bounds.
- Use a simple bias-bounding approach (e.g., how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to eliminate the residual -5.5 pp for Group B?) to communicate how fragile or robust the residual is.

**2.6 Subgroup checks by region and channel.**
- Repeat the stratified comparisons within each region, especially the two backlog regions and the two regions with elevated handovers. A residual that exists only where the backlog is may be a capacity problem; a residual that exists everywhere is more likely structural.
- Compare online vs in-person within each group. If Group B online applicants look like Group A online applicants after adjustment, the in-person channel (and the bilingual queue behind it) is the mechanism; if Group B online applicants still show extra Request Docs and Supervisor Review, the mechanism is closer to the decision itself.
- Test interaction terms (group × region, group × channel) rather than assuming additive effects.

**2.7 What each finding would mean.**
- Difference disappears within strata and is fully explained by policy-triggered steps → *legitimate routing* (but the timeliness of those steps remains a design fairness question).
- Difference persists within strata and is concentrated in discretionary steps or in specific resources → *potentially unjustified treatment*, warranting case review.
- Difference depends heavily on whether risk band / completeness are controlled → *uncertainty*, resolvable only by auditing those attributes.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**3.1 Language-support path (Group B).** 24% of Group B cases follow V4 versus 1-3% elsewhere; this is expected given 57% need language support (it also implies that many language-preference cases do *not* get a Language Support Contact, which itself should be checked). The concern is the cost of the step: V4 has a 19.2-day median against 14.9 days for V2, the same path without the language contact, so the language step adds roughly 4.3 days, of which 3.8 days is the queue in front of only 7 bilingual caseworkers. The Completeness → Request Docs wait is also four times longer for B (1.6 vs 0.4 days), attributed to waiting for bilingual staff. *Assessment:* the routing is justified; the waiting is not justified by case complexity, it is a capacity allocation choice, and it falls exclusively on the group with a protected characteristic. Further, the +9.5 pp adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents at equal recorded completeness is concerning: either the completeness attribute is recorded inconsistently, or Group B applicants are being asked for more or repeated documents, possibly because the initial request was not understood, which would mean the support step is not fully doing its job.

**3.2 Accommodation path (Group C).** 31% of Group C in V5 versus 2-3% elsewhere, consistent with 62% requesting accommodation. V5 has a 21.6-day median against 5.8 days for V1, i.e. about 15.8 extra days; the 4.7-day Accommodation Review → Eligibility queue explains a third of that. The rest is the Supervisor Review step embedded in V5 (with 1.6-day wait) and the general slowness of a path handled by 5 specialists. *Assessment:* Accommodation Review is legitimate. The systematic inclusion of Supervisor Review in the accommodation path is *not* grounded in the stated policy and should be checked: if it is an unwritten "always escalate accommodation cases" rule, it is a procedural burden and delay imposed on disabled applicants without a documented justification; if it reflects that two of the five specialists are also supervisors and review each other's work, it is a role-design problem. Group C also has the highest rework rate (24%) and the longest 90th percentile cycle time (38.8 days), suggesting the specialist queue is fragile under load.

**3.3 Fraud-review path (Group D).** 28% in V3 versus 9% for A; Group D's high-risk share is 51% versus 18%, so the routing ratio (~0.55 vs 0.50) is comparable. Risk Screening → Fraud Review wait is slightly longer (2.8 vs 2.2 days). *Assessment:* conditional on the risk band, routing appears consistent and the adjusted cycle-time gap is small (+0.9 days). The concern shifts upstream to the band itself (49% high prior-case count in D; prior contact with the system is partly a function of past decisions) and downstream to appeals: Group D has the highest raw (16%) and adjusted (+5.7 pp) appeal probability and 13% in the appeal variant V6. Whether those appeals succeed is the decisive missing fact. A high appeal rate with a high reversal rate would indicate that fraud-route decisions are frequently wrong; a low reversal rate would suggest contestation of correct decisions.

**3.4 Supervisor Review as a discretionary escalation.** Adjusted +8.0 pp (B) and +5.5 pp (C) after controls. Neither language preference nor accommodation is a listed escalation trigger. The Supervisor Review → Decision wait is also longer for B (1.9 vs 1.0 days) due to backlog in two regions. *Assessment:* this is the disparity least explained by documented complexity and most in need of explanation. Possible legitimate explanations (e.g., supervisors must sign off whenever an interpreter was used) should be documented if they exist; otherwise this looks like differential scrutiny.

**3.5 Resource concentration and handovers.** 52 caseworkers for A versus 7 + 3 for B and 5 for C. Handover chains general → bilingual → general (B) and general → specialist → supervisor (C) in two regions add at least two queue entries per case. Concentration also means that if one of the 7 bilingual workers is on leave, roughly 14% of Group B capacity disappears; workload is only observed at daily granularity, so intra-day bottlenecks cannot be seen. *Assessment:* the assignment rule (bilingual case → bilingual worker) is reasonable; the *sizing* of the pools relative to demand is where the unfairness materialises. The 3 "overloaded regional officers" handling Group B cases are an unexplained assignment pattern that deserves specific attention.

**3.6 Shared steps.** Submit → Verify (0.8 vs 1.1-1.4 days) differs modestly and is attributed to in-person channel; Decision → Notify differs little (0.5-0.8 days). These shared steps are not a major disparity source, which helps localise the problem to the support-step queues and discretionary escalations.

**Summary of what is justified vs concerning:**
- Justified by complexity/policy: *presence* of Language Support, Accommodation Review and Fraud Review steps; higher Request Docs and V2 share for groups with more incomplete documentation; Group D's V3 share given its risk band.
- Concerning despite complexity: wait times specific to rights-preserving steps (3.8 d, 4.7 d) with no service-level target; Supervisor Review at elevated adjusted rates without a policy trigger; Request Docs at elevated adjusted rates at equal recorded completeness; rework rates double the baseline for B and C; resource concentration and role overlap; residual approval gaps after adjustment (largest for B, -5.5 pp).
- Indeterminate: whether the risk band is neutral; whether appeals reflect error; whether support quality is adequate.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate: policy-mandated routing on genuinely different cases.** Groups differ in risk band, documentation and support needs; the policy requires Fraud Review, Accommodation Review and Language Support Contact accordingly. This alone produces different variants, more steps and longer cycle times, and would do so in a perfectly fair process. It explains most of Group D's raw gap and the *existence* of V3, V4 and V5.

2. **Concerning (structural): under-resourced rights-preserving steps.** The organisation staffs the support steps with 7 and 5 people respectively, sets no timeliness target for them, and so converts a right (language support, accommodation) into a queue. Nobody decides to treat B or C worse; the delay is a by-product of capacity decisions, but it is systematic, foreseeable and falls on protected groups. This mechanism explains the 3.8 and 4.7 day waits and much of the adjusted cycle-time gap for B and C.

3. **Concerning (behavioural): differential scrutiny or risk aversion by caseworkers.** Caseworkers may escalate to Supervisor Review or issue additional document requests more readily when they are less confident, e.g. because of communication difficulty, unfamiliar documents from other jurisdictions, or a perception that support cases are "complicated". This would produce elevated adjusted probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Docs for B and C, longer paths, more rework and slightly lower approval, without any explicit discriminatory rule.

4. **Concerning (measurement): biased or endogenous covariates.** documentation_completeness may be judged "incomplete" more often when the caseworker cannot read the documents or the applicant misunderstood the instructions; risk_score_band may be built on prior-case history and region, which encode past decisions and enforcement intensity. If so, the adjusted model partly "explains away" disparity that is in fact produced by the process, and the true unjustified component is larger than the adjusted estimates suggest (most relevant for D via risk band and for B via completeness).

5. **Concerning (communication): support steps that do not fully close the gap.** If the Language Support Contact establishes contact but the subsequent document request is still issued in the majority language, or the accommodation is recorded but not delivered, applicants will fail to comply correctly, generating repeat Request Docs, rework loops and appeals. This is consistent with B's +9.5 pp Request Docs at equal recorded completeness and B/C rework rates of 22-24%.

6. **Mixed: regional capacity and backlog effects.** Two regions have supervisor backlogs and elevated handovers. If Group B and C applicants are geographically concentrated in those regions, part of the disparity is regional rather than group-driven. The model controls for region, but a group × region interaction (e.g., the only bilingual staff are in one region, so cases are transferred) could still produce group-specific delay.

7. **Mixed: appeal propensity.** Higher appeal rates for D and B may reflect more incorrect or poorly explained first decisions (concerning), or greater awareness of appeal rights among applicants with prior system contact (neutral), or both. Without appeal outcomes the mechanism cannot be identified.

8. **Legitimate-in-form but concerning-in-effect: risk-based routing with an uncalibrated score.** Even if the fraud-routing rule is applied consistently, a score that over-predicts risk for Group D produces more fraud reviews, more delay, more denials and more appeals for that group while looking "consistent" in every routing check.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in covariates.** documentation_completeness is not an objective property of the application but a caseworker's judgement recorded at some point in the process. If the judgement is systematically harsher for applicants whose documents are in another language or format, the variable is contaminated by the very process being evaluated, and adjusting for it hides rather than reveals unfair treatment. It is also unclear whether the value reflects the state at submission or after the first document request, which changes its status from confounder to outcome.

**Historical bias in risk_score_band.** The band correlates strongly with prior_case_count (49% high for D) and presumably with region and other proxies. Prior contact with the benefits system depends on past eligibility decisions, past enforcement intensity and the applicant's economic situation; if any of those were unequal in the past, the score reproduces that inequality and legitimises it as "risk". The log gives no information on the score's calibration (does a high band predict confirmed fraud equally across groups?) or on its inputs. Treating it as neutral is therefore not defensible; treating it as entirely biased is equally unsupported. It should be audited, not assumed.

**Missing applicant needs.** language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested are binary requests, not measures of need. An applicant who needed support but did not know to request it, or who requested it and did not receive it, is indistinguishable from one without needs. The type of accommodation (large print, extended deadlines, in-person meeting) is not recorded, so "Accommodation Review" covers heterogeneous work. Literacy, digital access, caregiving constraints and precarious housing, all of which affect ability to respond to document requests within deadlines, are unobserved.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity and, apparently, only through the log itself. Whether a bilingual caseworker's 3.8-day queue is due to volume, part-time schedules, other duties (e.g., interpreting for other teams) or leave cannot be determined. Individual caseworker experience, training and discretion levels are not in the log. Without these, it is hard to separate "this resource is slow" from "this resource is overloaded".

**Appeal quality and outcome.** appeal_flag records that an appeal was filed, not its result, its grounds, or its duration in the appeal stage. The single most informative fairness statistic, the reversal rate by group, is absent. Nor is there information on why applicants did *not* appeal: fear, lack of information, or acceptance of a correct decision.

**Outcome granularity.** case_outcome does not capture partial approvals, benefit amounts, effective dates, the quality of the written explanation, whether notices were issued in the applicant's language, or whether the applicant understood them. A process can be outcome-fair and still humiliating or opaque.

**Single timestamp per event.** With only completion timestamps, service time and waiting time cannot be separated; a long Accommodation Review → Eligibility interval could be queue time, processing time or waiting for the applicant. Direct use of service-time functions requires start timestamps that the schema does not include.

**Group definitions.** The four groups are defined by overlapping characteristics (language, accommodation, risk history). An applicant can be minority-language *and* disabled *and* high-risk. The group labels flatten intersectional cases, which may be exactly where burden compounds.

**Selection into the log.** Applicants deterred from applying, or whose applications were never registered (e.g., abandoned in-person visits), are absent. Access barriers before Submit Application are invisible.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**6.1 Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** (more bilingual caseworkers, interpreter contracts, more accommodation specialists, cross-training general staff).
- Expected effect: directly reduces the 3.8 and 4.7 day queues and the Completeness → Request Docs delay; reduces concentration risk.
- Side effects: cost; recruitment lead time; cross-trained generalists may deliver lower-quality support than specialists; if capacity is added only in some regions, regional disparity may grow; contracted interpreters may lack case knowledge and introduce a different handover.

**6.2 Service-level targets for support steps** (e.g., Language Support Contact within 1 working day of Verification; Accommodation Review within 2 days), reported alongside existing targets.
- Expected effect: makes the delay visible and managed; creates accountability for the rights-preserving steps rather than treating them as outside the clock.
- Side effects: targets can be gamed (superficial contact logged to stop the clock, "review" completed without substantive assessment); pressure to hit the target may reduce quality; measuring without adding capacity simply produces red dashboards. Targets should be paired with quality checks and with capacity.

**6.3 Routing redesign to reduce handovers** (assign a bilingual or accommodation-capable caseworker as case owner end-to-end rather than general → specialist → general; allow specialists to perform Completeness Check and Eligibility Assessment; run Language Support Contact in parallel with Identity Verification rather than in series).
- Expected effect: fewer queue entries, fewer context losses, shorter V4 and V5 paths without removing the support content.
- Side effects: increases the load on already scarce specialists unless combined with 6.1; may reduce the general pool's exposure to these cases and deepen segregation of expertise; parallelisation may cause wasted work if the case is withdrawn; end-to-end ownership by a small pool increases dependence on individual judgement, so pair with 6.5.

**6.4 Separate supervisor-review triggers from support steps and clarify the role conflict.** Document explicit criteria for Supervisor Review; remove any implicit "escalate all accommodation/language cases" practice unless justified; ensure accommodation specialists do not act as supervisors on their own team's cases.
- Expected effect: reduces unjustified escalation (+8.0 / +5.5 pp), shortens V5, removes a segregation-of-duties problem.
- Side effects: if escalation was catching genuine errors in complex cases, removing it could lower decision quality for exactly these applicants; replace discretionary escalation with risk-neutral random quality sampling rather than none at all.

**6.5 Decision-explanation improvements** (notices in the applicant's preferred language and accessible format; specific listing of missing documents with examples; plain-language reasons for denial; a contact point for clarification).
- Expected effect: fewer repeat document requests, fewer misunderstanding-driven appeals, better dignity outcomes; would also generate data on explanation quality.
- Side effects: translation and accessibility cost; longer notices may be less read; more detailed reasons may increase *well-founded* appeals in the short term (which is a good outcome but looks like a worse metric); risk of templated explanations that satisfy the form but not the substance.

**6.6 Monitoring adjusted disparities** (a recurring fairness dashboard reporting raw and adjusted approval, cycle time, step probabilities, rework and appeal rates per group, per region, with bounds under alternative covariate sets).
- Expected effect: converts a one-off study into ongoing accountability; detects drift after changes.
- Side effects: metric fixation (managing the number rather than the process); adjusted metrics can be misread as "fair" when they merely control for biased covariates, so always show unadjusted and adjusted side by side; group labels raise privacy and data-protection concerns; small groups in small regions produce noisy estimates that may be over-interpreted.

**6.7 Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.** For the risk band: document inputs, test calibration by group (fraud-review confirmation rate at equal band), test whether removing proxy inputs changes group distributions, consider a fairness constraint or a human-review safeguard for band-driven routing. For completeness: define objective completeness criteria, record the value at submission and after each request separately, and measure inter-rater consistency across caseworkers.
- Expected effect: determines whether the adjusted estimates are trustworthy; may reduce Group D's over-routing to Fraud Review if the score is miscalibrated.
- Side effects: recalibrating the score may increase genuine fraud leakage if the score was actually accurate; audits take time and expertise; changing the completeness definition breaks time-series comparability; a more rigid completeness checklist may itself disadvantage applicants with non-standard documents unless it allows equivalents.

**6.8 Do not remove or shortcut support steps.** Any redesign should be evaluated on the burden and timeliness *of applicants who use the steps*, not on the average cycle time. Eliminating Language Support Contact would shorten V4 and worsen the fairness problem.

**6.9 Rebalance assignment of Group B cases away from the 3 overloaded regional officers** and review why that pattern exists.
- Side effects: if those officers have unique regional or language knowledge, redistribution may lower quality; requires workload data at finer granularity than daily.

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The plan is organised as: prepare the log and per-case attributes → build stratified comparison groups → variant and path analysis → performance DFG and waiting-time decomposition → resource and handover analysis → declarative/skeleton checks of routing consistency → assemble the adjusted comparison. Code is illustrative PM4Py-style Python; column names follow the schema given.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# ---------- 7.1 Load and format ----------
df = pd.read_csv("benefits_log.csv")
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)
df = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])

CASE_ATTRS = ["applicant_group", "channel", "language_preference",
              "risk_score_band", "documentation_completeness",
              "household_size_band", "disability_accommodation_requested",
              "prior_case_count", "region", "case_outcome", "appeal_flag"]

# One row per case with attributes taken at the first event (submission-time values)
case_df = (df.groupby("case:concept:name").first()[CASE_ATTRS]
             .reset_index())
case_df["month"] = (df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"]
                      .min().dt.to_period("M").astype(str).values)

# Stratum key used for within-stratum comparisons throughout
STRATA = ["risk_score_band", "documentation_completeness", "channel",
          "region", "prior_case_count"]
case_df["stratum"] = case_df[STRATA].astype(str).agg("|".join, axis=1)

# ---------- 7.2 Case-level durations and step counts ----------
durations = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(df)          # list aligned to case order
case_df["cycle_time_days"] = (
    df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].agg(lambda s: (s.max()-s.min()).total_seconds()/86400).values
)

def step_count(activity):
    return (df[df["concept:name"] == activity]
            .groupby("case:concept:name").size()
            .reindex(case_df["case:concept:name"], fill_value=0).values)

for act in ["Request Missing Documents", "Supervisor Review", "Fraud Review",
            "Language Support Contact", "Accommodation Review", "Appeal Intake"]:
    case_df["n_" + act.replace(" ", "_").lower()] = step_count(act)

# Rework: any activity executed more than once in a case
case_df["rework"] = (df.groupby(["case:concept:name", "concept:name"]).size()
                       .gt(1).groupby(level=0).any()
                       .reindex(case_df["case:concept:name"]).values)

# ---------- 7.3 Group and stratum filters ----------
def log_for(group=None, stratum=None, region=None, channel=None):
    sub = df
    if group is not None:
        sub = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(sub, "applicant_group", [group], level="case", retain=True)
```

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence does not support a finding that decision-makers are discriminating against Groups B, C or D, and it does not support a finding that the process is procedurally fair. On the available data the most defensible characterisation is: **procedurally unequal in ways that are only partly justified by case complexity, with the unjustified portion concentrated in the timeliness and burden of rights-preserving steps and in discretionary escalation, and with the size of any residual outcome disparity remaining uncertain because key covariates may themselves be biased.**

More specifically, and with appropriate confidence levels:

- **Reasonably well supported:** The presence of Language Support, Accommodation Review and Fraud Review steps is legitimate routing, and it explains most of the raw variant differences. Group D's fraud-review routing is consistent with its recorded risk band. Most of the raw approval gap for Groups C and D is associated with recorded case-mix.
- **Well supported and concerning:** Applicants who exercise the rights-preserving steps wait substantially longer (3.8 and 4.7 day queues, roughly +4 to +16 days on comparable paths) because those steps are staffed by very small pools and are excluded from service-level targets. This is a structural, foreseeable burden on protected groups, and it is not explained by case complexity; it is explained by capacity design. Rework rates for B and C are about double the baseline.
- **Moderately supported and concerning:** After adjustment, Group B still shows higher Supervisor Review (+8.0 pp) and Request Missing Documents (+9.5 pp) probabilities with no policy trigger, and a residual approval gap of -5.5 pp; Group C shows Supervisor Review embedded in its accommodation path without a documented rule. These patterns are consistent with differential scrutiny or with support steps that do not fully close the communication gap, but the current data cannot distinguish those from unmeasured legitimate complexity.
- **Indeterminate:** Whether risk_score_band is fair to Group D; whether elevated appeal rates for D and B reflect erroneous first decisions; whether support is of adequate quality; whether the residual gaps would grow or shrink under unbiased covariates. The adjusted estimates should be read as a range whose lower bound is the reported figure and whose upper bound is closer to the raw gap, depending on how much of documentation_completeness and risk_score_band is itself produced by the process.

The appropriate response is therefore not to declare the process fair on the strength of the adjusted approval gaps, nor to declare it discriminatory on the strength of the raw ones, but to treat the timeliness and burden of the support steps and the unexplained escalation patterns as established procedural-fairness deficits requiring remediation now, while the covariate audits and appeal-outcome analysis needed to settle the remaining questions are carried out.
