## 1. What is observed

The automation flag records branch-level availability, not case-level receipt of automated triage. Alpha and Beta are flagged from April, Gamma from July, and Delta and Epsilon remain unflagged through August. Actual use is represented by automated_fast_path_pct: it rises gradually from 25–27% in the first treated month to 32% for Gamma and 48–50% for Alpha and Beta in August. Thus exposure has both a rollout component and a changing intensity component.

Rollout was targeted: Alpha and Beta were selected for high volume and poor historical cycle time, while Gamma followed a readiness pilot. The rollout therefore was not randomized. It coincided with a roughly 10% routing increase to Alpha and Beta, a roughly 5% increase to Gamma, a May product-driven increase in high-complexity cases, branch-specific FTE changes, and a centralized fraud desk that received somewhat more borderline cases from automated triage. Managers could also change local practices.

The outcomes are branch-month aggregates. Medians and p90s describe different parts of the case-time distribution, while rework and escalation are percentages of cases. CSAT is a survey mean with 20–45% response rates but no response counts or case-level linkage. Fraud-desk waits, queue states, individual case paths, and variance estimates are absent. These facts describe measurement and exposure; they do not by themselves establish an automation effect.

## 2. Causal estimands

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | population | time horizon | outcome | unit of analysis | interpretation | identifiable from supplied data? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ITT-A | Branch has automation available versus the same branch without availability, regardless of uptake | All eligible branches and months under the rollout policy | Immediate, 1–4 months, and mature 5–6 months | Each operational and customer outcome | Branch-month | Policy effect of making the capability available, including training, adoption, routing, and practice changes | Not point-identified; estimable only under a credible staggered parallel-trends/no-spillover design |
| TOT-U | A case uses the automated fast path versus an otherwise comparable case using manual triage | Cases eligible for the fast path | Within-case episode | Cycle time, rework, escalation, CSAT | Ideally case; supplied data only provide branch-month intensity | Effect of actual use, distinct from merely offering the system | No; intensity is endogenous and case-level comparability and outcomes are unavailable |
| MCT | Availability versus no availability | Eligible branch-months | 0–1, 2–4, and mature post months | median_cycle_h | Branch-month median | Change in the typical branch-month case time, not the mean individual effect | No without assumptions; only aggregate medians are observed |
| TAIL | Availability versus no availability | Eligible branch-months, especially high-volume or complex branches | Same dynamic horizons | p90_cycle_h or probability of exceeding an SLA | Branch-month p90 | Effect on severe delays and service reliability | No; p90s alone do not reveal queue distributions or case-level tail composition |
| RW | Availability versus no availability | All registered cases in eligible branches | Monthly and cumulative post period | rework_pct | Branch-month percentage | Whether loops are genuinely reduced or merely displaced | Not point-identified; rework definition and case-level event sequence are not supplied |
| ESC | Availability versus no availability | All registered cases in eligible branches | Monthly and cumulative post period | escalation_pct | Branch-month percentage | Change in specialist-routing demand; not automatically a service-quality effect | Not point-identified; fraud-desk interference and changes in appropriate routing are unresolved |
| CSAT | Availability versus no availability among the eligible customer population | Survey-eligible customers, with survey response as part of measurement | Monthly and 1–4 month post period | csat_1to5 | Branch-month survey mean | Customer-experience effect in the responding population, or in all customers if response is ignorable | No; response counts, composition, and case linkage are missing |
| HET-D | Availability or use intensity versus the counterfactual, separately by branch readiness, complexity, or post-adoption month | Branches/cases in prespecified complexity or readiness strata | Ramp versus mature months | Median, p90, rework, and CSAT | Branch-month by stratum if available | Whether benefits or harms vary with learning, demand pressure, or case mix | No for case-level heterogeneity; only weak branch-level dynamic patterns are available |

The availability estimand includes a treatment version consisting of system access, training, local implementation, routing, and any induced queue effects. The fast-path estimand is a different, dose-like treatment and should not be substituted for availability. Cases sent to the common fraud desk create interference: one branch's intervention can change another branch's outcome, so the usual no-interference interpretation is not automatically valid.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: cohort-specific staggered event study or group-time ATT | Alpha/Beta cohort begins April; Gamma cohort begins July; event time is months relative to each branch's first availability; estimate cohort and horizon effects separately rather than naive TWFE | Not-yet-treated branches at each cohort date plus never-treated Delta/Epsilon; do not reuse already-treated branches as controls | Branch fixed effects, calendar-month fixed effects, pre-period outcome levels/trends, and baseline volume/FTE/complexity; contemporaneous arrivals, FTE, and complexity are sensitivity covariates because they may be mediators | ITT-A, including dynamic and branch/cohort-specific effects | Conditional parallel trends, no differential anticipation, treatment consistency, limited fraud-desk interference, and adequate overlap; cluster dependence is handled honestly despite only five clusters | Plot and test pre-treatment leads, placebo adoption dates, cohort-specific pre-fit, leave-one-branch-out estimates, control-group sensitivity, and randomization or wild-bootstrap inference | Endogenous targeting, only three treated branches, short post periods, spillovers, and simultaneous operational shocks |
| Alternative: controlled interrupted time series focused on Gamma | One July level/slope change, with separate ramp and mature indicators | Delta and Epsilon over the same April–August calendar interval; Alpha/Beta excluded as already treated | Branch-specific level and trend, month effects, and predetermined baseline covariates | Gamma short-run ITT-A and outcome-specific trajectory change | Gamma would have followed the control trajectory absent rollout; no concurrent Gamma-only shock or spillover | Compare pre-slopes, inspect April–June placebo breaks, vary the pre/post window, and compare level versus slope specifications | Gamma's readiness selection and June FTE loss make the counterfactual fragile |
| Alternative: matched or synthetic-control time series | Each adopter gets a branch-specific post indicator; construct weights from pre-period outcomes and baseline volume, FTE, and complexity | Weighted Delta/Epsilon and, for earlier cohorts where possible, not-yet-treated observations | Pre-period trajectories and only predetermined baseline characteristics; do not condition on post-treatment mediators in the primary fit | Branch-specific ITT-A, median and tail outcomes | A weighted donor reproduces the untreated trajectory and remains unaffected by the intervention | Pre-period RMSPE, placebo treatment dates and placebo branches, donor-weight sensitivity, and post-period prediction intervals | There are too few donors and short pre-periods to guarantee a credible synthetic counterfactual |
| Alternative for the next wave: randomized stepped-wedge phase-in | Randomly assign eligible nonadopters to first versus later activation, with a fixed implementation date per sequence | Branches assigned to later sequences and, temporarily, not-yet-treated branches | Branch and month effects; baseline covariates for precision, not to repair randomization | Future ITT-A and dynamic rollout effect | Assignment is genuinely random after readiness gating, compliance is recorded, and spillovers are measured or controlled | Allocation audit, baseline balance, adherence, sequence-by-time interaction, and branch-clustered/randomization inference | Operational exceptions, contamination through shared queues, and too few branches if randomization is not expanded |

The primary strategy is preferable because availability is the policy decision, timing is staggered, and uptake increases over time. A cohort-specific event study can preserve the distinction between the April and July cohorts, display pre-trends, and show whether a change is a ramp effect or a mature effect. A conventional two-way fixed-effects coefficient would mix early and late cohorts and can use already-treated branches as controls; with heterogeneous dynamic effects, that weighting can be misleading. The design still cannot manufacture identification: there are only five branches, two never-treated controls, and at most two post months for Gamma. I would therefore report cohort-specific arithmetic, sensitivity intervals, and small-cluster or randomization-based uncertainty rather than a single precise pooled percentage. The controlled ITS and synthetic-control analyses are useful falsification and robustness checks, but their donor pools are especially thin. A randomized stepped-wedge phase-in is the strongest alternative for future decisions because it directly addresses endogenous selection and generates a clean availability contrast while retaining operational rollout.

## 4. Process-aware causal mechanisms

| mechanism | process artifact or metric affected | expected signature if beneficial | expected signature if harmful | evidence in supplied data | unresolved ambiguity |

| Learning and implementation fidelity | Month-by-month use, overrides, manual fallback, path mix | Use and benefit improve together; late performance stabilizes | Ramp is slow, local workarounds persist, or higher use exposes failure modes | Intensity climbs monotonically; Gamma improves after activation, while Alpha/Beta show mixed late outcomes | Calendar shocks and changing case mix move with learning |

## 5. Quantitative evidence

The following are arithmetic contrasts, not causal estimates. For Alpha and Beta, “pre” is January–March and “post” is July–August, so the ramp months are not silently treated as mature performance. For Gamma, “pre” is April–June and “post” is July–August. Changes in cycle time are relative to the pre-period mean; percentage outcomes are percentage-point changes.

| branch | pre window | post window | median change | p90 change | rework change | escalation change | CSAT change | mean fast-path use, pre -> post |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alpha | Jan–Mar | Jul–Aug | −3.3 h (−6.7%) | +6.9 h (+7.0%) | +1.3 pp | +3.1 pp | +0.07 | 4% -> 47% |
| Beta | Jan–Mar | Jul–Aug | +0.9 h (+1.9%) | +9.5 h (+10.2%) | −0.1 pp | +2.6 pp | 0.00 | 6% -> 49% |
| Gamma | Apr–Jun | Jul–Aug | −6.5 h (−14.8%) | −9.3 h (−9.8%) | −1.2 pp | +1.2 pp | +0.14 | 4% -> 28.5% |

For a same-calendar comparison around Gamma's adoption, I computed each branch's April–June to July–August change and averaged the two never-treated branches without weighting by arrivals. Gamma changed by −6.5 h in the median, versus −2.6 h for Delta/Epsilon on average, an arithmetic residual of about −3.9 h. For p90 the corresponding figures are −9.3 h versus −2.2 h, or about −7.1 h residual. Rework changed −1.2 pp for Gamma versus +0.5 pp for the controls, while escalation changed +1.2 pp versus +0.2 pp. CSAT rose +0.14 for Gamma versus about +0.04 for controls. This is a useful comparison, but not a formal identified difference-in-differences: the branches differ in readiness, staffing, complexity, and pre-trends, and the controls may receive fraud-desk spillovers.

An intensity view gives a different descriptive picture. From the first treated month to August, Alpha's fast-path use rose 25% to 48% while its median fell 5.0 h, p90 rose 3.9 h, and rework rose 3.3 pp. Beta rose 27% to 50%, with median −2.3 h, p90 +3.6 h, and rework +2.3 pp. Gamma rose 25% to 32%, with median −2.4 h, p90 −4.4 h, and essentially no rework change. These patterns are compatible with a dose relationship, but intensity is jointly determined by learning, month, routing, and case composition; they are not causal dose-response estimates.

Pre-trends are not reassuring enough for a universal claim. Alpha's January–March median is 50.4, 46.9, 49.4 h; Beta's is 45.4, 45.9, 46.4 h; Gamma rises from 36.4 in January to 39.7 in April before the May shock. Delta and Epsilon also worsen in May–June and improve in July–August without automation. The improvement in targeted branches can therefore include regression to the mean, especially because Alpha and Beta were selected for poor historical performance. Lead tests would have low power with five clusters.

Two sensitivity scenarios illustrate the uncertainty. For the median, the raw adopter range is −14.8% to +1.9%; if the Gamma control comparison is credible, the incremental signal is roughly −3.9 h, but the Alpha/Beta range does not support a common effect. A reasonable operational scenario is therefore “near zero to roughly 9–15% improvement for a Gamma-like ready branch, with no assurance for a high-volume branch,” not a bound on a population effect. For p90, the raw range is −9.8% to +10.2%; the Gamma comparison suggests about −7.1 h beyond controls, while Alpha and Beta are worse late. A neutral, beneficial-tail, and harmful-tail scenario all remain plausible. Rework and CSAT are similarly sensitive to window choice and measurement selection.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle time; Gamma was readiness-selected | Often overstates benefit through regression to the mean, but a continuing negative shock could bias toward harm | Model and plot long pre-period trends; compare matched baseline performance and placebo adoption dates | Do not generalize adopter contrasts to all branches; require randomized phase-in |
| Nonparallel trends | Branches have different trajectories and May deterioration | Ambiguous; can mimic either improvement or deterioration | Event-study leads, branch-specific slopes, controlled ITS, and leave-one-branch-out checks | Treat estimates as descriptive and avoid a single pooled effect |
| Time-varying confounding | Product, routing, demand, and staffing changes occur near treatment | Complexity and demand pressure likely mask benefits; other changes can reverse the sign | Collect case-level covariates; adjust only predetermined variables in the primary design and use mediator-adjusted models as sensitivity | No clean attribution without a design change |
| Treatment intensity and compliance | Availability is not actual use; use ramps from 25–27% to 32–50% | Direction ambiguous because high use reflects both learning and selected cases | Record eligibility, automated decisions, overrides, fallback, and estimate ITT separately from dose associations | Do not advertise a fast-path dose effect from the availability flag |
| Fraud-desk spillover | Automated triage sends borderline cases to a shared desk serving all branches | Can make controls worse and exaggerate benefit, or congest adopters and hide benefit | Capture desk arrivals, queue age, service times, and branch of origin; run spillover-adjusted sensitivity | Interpret branch comparisons as system-level effects or redesign controls |
| Staffing changes | Alpha and Gamma lose productive FTE while demand rises; Beta gains one | Alpha/Gamma staffing pressure likely masks benefit; Beta's gain can exaggerate it | Use productive FTE per arrival, schedules, backlog, and specialist capacity; pre-specify staffing interactions | Add staffing guardrails and do not compare raw branch changes |
| Case-mix change | High-complexity share rises after the product change and differs by branch | Usually masks speed benefits in adopters, but automated routing can also select easier fast-path cases | Stratify by registration risk, channel, issue type, and eligibility; inspect within-stratum path rates | Any global X% claim is invalid; target versions of treatment to strata |
| Anticipation | Preparation or informal use may begin before the availability date | Contaminates pre-period and usually biases effects toward zero | Check training, shadow-mode, login, and manual-workflow changes before activation; add anticipation leads | Move the treatment date or exclude contaminated months |
| Survey nonresponse | CSAT response rates vary widely and counts are unavailable | Ambiguous; positive-response selection can overstate CSAT | Obtain respondent counts and composition, response propensity, and case linkage; report weighted and unweighted CSAT | Do not use a small CSAT rise as evidence of customer benefit |
| Aggregated outcome measurement | Medians, p90s, percentages, and means collapse case distributions | Can hide subgroup harm, nonlinearity, and displacement; uncertainty is understated | Obtain case-level event logs and bootstrap within branch-months before aggregating | Limit conclusions to branch-month summaries and add SLA/complaint metrics |
| Very small number of branches | Five clusters make ordinary cluster-robust standard errors unreliable | Not a directional bias, but creates false precision and unstable signs | Wild-cluster bootstrap, randomization inference, permutation/placebo tests, and explicit cluster count | Use intervals and decision guardrails, not conventional p-value thresholds |
| Dynamic treatment effects | Use and learning change each month; Gamma has only two post months | Early estimates may understate mature benefit or capture temporary ramp harm | Estimate event-time effects, separate ramp/mature periods, and follow branches for at least six months | Delay scale-up until mature tail and rework behavior are observed |
| Local practice changes | Managers could alter work allocation during rollout | Ambiguous and may be inseparable from the policy package | Log staffing, SOP, queue-priority, and training changes; interview and audit implementation | Define the estimand as the full rollout package, not software alone |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Use Delta and Epsilon as the next randomized-order pair: after the same objective readiness gate is met, randomly assign one branch to activate first and keep the other not-yet-treated for the initial evaluation window. If the readiness gate is necessary for safety, define it before looking at post-assignment outcomes and record any exception. This gives one operational expansion while preserving a contemporaneous control. The first activation should not be coupled with a new routing campaign, product change, or discretionary staffing change. If a safety gate excludes Epsilon because its complexity and rework are too high, activate Delta first and retain Epsilon as the control; the exclusion rule should be documented rather than chosen after seeing results.

Use at least six monthly pre-periods and six monthly post-periods, with weekly operational monitoring during the first eight weeks. Preserve the current monthly branch panel, but add case-level event logs with case ID, registration and completion timestamps, every triage and specialist transition, automated recommendation, confidence, override, fallback, rework loop, escalation destination, and closure. Record arrivals by channel and risk class, eligibility for automation, productive FTE and schedules, backlog, service rate, SLA breach, complaints, reopenings, and customer survey invitation, response, and score.

For each shared specialist or fraud queue, capture origin branch, arrival time, queue entry and exit, service start, queue length, and waiting time. Monitor spillovers to the non-treated branch and all other branches, including fraud-desk load. Guardrails should pause further sequence advancement if p90 or SLA breaches rise by at least 10% for two consecutive weeks, rework rises by 2 percentage points, unresolved backlog grows materially, or complaints/low CSAT show a sustained deterioration. A modest median improvement cannot offset tail or customer harm.

Scale only if the treated branch shows a pre-specified mature-period improvement in median and no material p90, rework, queue-wait, complaint, or CSAT deterioration, while the control remains comparable and spillovers are bounded. Analyze availability as the primary ITT; use intensity and pathway outcomes to explain implementation, not to replace the randomized contrast.

for outcome in outcomes:
    print("\nTREND:", outcome)
    print(df.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values=outcome).round(3))
    ax = df.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values=outcome).plot(
        marker="o", title=outcome, figsize=(8, 4)
    )
    ax.set_xlabel("Month")
    ax.set_ylabel(outcome)
    ax.figure.tight_layout()
    plt.show()
    plt.close(ax.figure)

# 3. Transparent adopter-specific contrasts.
windows = {
    "Alpha": (["2026-01", "2026-02", "2026-03"], ["2026-07", "2026-08"]),
    "Beta": (["2026-01", "2026-02", "2026-03"], ["2026-07", "2026-08"]),
    "Gamma": (["2026-04", "2026-05", "2026-06"], ["2026-07", "2026-08"]),
}
df["month_s"] = df["month"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m")
for branch, (pre_months, post_months) in windows.items():
    b = df[df["branch"] == branch]
    pre = b[b["month_s"].isin(pre_months)][outcomes].mean()
    post = b[b["month_s"].isin(post_months)][outcomes].mean()
    print("\nPRE/POST:", branch)
    print(pd.DataFrame({"pre_mean": pre, "post_mean": post, "change": post - pre}).round(3))

# 4. Relative-time indicators for the staggered-adoption description.
adoption = {"Alpha": "2026-04", "Beta": "2026-04", "Gamma": "2026-07"}
adoption_period = pd.to_datetime(pd.Series(adoption))
df["adoption_month"] = df["branch"].map(adoption_period)
df["relative_time"] = (
    (df["month"].dt.year - df["adoption_month"].dt.year) * 12
    + df["month"].dt.month - df["adoption_month"].dt.month
)
df.loc[df["adoption_month"].isna(), "relative_time"] = np.nan
event_times = [-2, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4]  # relative time -1 is the omitted reference
for k in event_times:
    name = "rt_m2" if k == -2 else "rt_" + ("p" + str(k) if k >= 0 else "m" + str(abs(k)))
    df[name] = (df["relative_time"] == k).astype(int)
print("\nRELATIVE-TIME COUNTS (never-treated rows are all zero):")
print(df[["branch", "month", "relative_time"] + [
    "rt_m2", "rt_0", "rt_p1", "rt_p2", "rt_p3", "rt_p4"
]].to_string(index=False))

# 5. Descriptive fixed-effects panel models, with branch-clustered SEs.
# These are descriptive associations. They do not prove causality.
# A causal interpretation would require explicit assumptions about selection,
# parallel trends, anticipation, treatment consistency, and spillovers.
def fit_clustered(formula, label):
    print("\nMODEL:", label)
    try:
        model = smf.ols(formula, data=df).fit(
            cov_type="cluster",
            cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"], "use_correction": True},
        )
        print(model.summary().tables[1])
        return model
    except Exception as exc:
        print("Model could not be fit:", repr(exc))
        return None


for outcome in outcomes:
    fit_clustered(
        f"{outcome} ~ automation + C(branch) + C(month)",
        f"descriptive availability model for {outcome}",
    )
    fit_clustered(
        f"{outcome} ~ automated_fast_path_pct + C(branch) + C(month)",
        f"descriptive treatment-intensity model for {outcome}",
    )

# An event-study-style descriptive model for the median.
event_terms = " + ".join(

)
fit_clustered(
    f"median_cycle_h ~ {event_terms} + C(branch) + C(month)",
    "descriptive staggered relative-time model for median_cycle_h",
)

if df["branch"].nunique() < 30:
    warnings.warn(
        f"Only {df['branch'].nunique()} branch clusters: clustered SEs and "
        "ordinary p-values are not reliable; consider wild-bootstrap or "

    )
print(

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automation availability was followed by rising automated-fast-path use, higher escalation shares in all adopters, and heterogeneous median, p90, rework, and CSAT movements.
- Suggestive: Gamma's July–August improvement in both median and p90 exceeded the simple change in never-treated branches, while Alpha and Beta showed late median improvement or stability but worse p90s.
- Conditionally causal: Under parallel trends, limited spillovers, stable case mix and staffing, and valid treatment timing, a ready branch may obtain a short-run median and possibly tail improvement from availability.
- Not supported: A universal statement that “automation reduces cycle time by X%,” a causal fast-path dose effect, or a claim that customer outcomes and specialist queues improve without further measurement.
