Likely consequences include overselling, wrong lot/location selection and later reconciliation or cancellation; T3 synchronization problems prolonged the risk. | All; particularly controlled inventory, cold-chain stock and recall-sensitive lots | **Modify** | Treat the snapshot as a hint, not a reservation. Time-box it, record its age, maintain a single offline decrement ledger and require physical verification when stale or for high-risk stock. |
| **E. Manual pick list** | Maintain physical picking and packing | Physical flow continued despite WMS loss. | Reconstructed scans can conceal missing lot, serial, expiry, quantity, picker or timing evidence. Backfilled events may falsely appear contemporaneous. | All; highest traceability impact for controlled and recall-sensitive items | **Modify** | Retain emergency paper picking only with unique IDs, real-time lot/quantity records, picker/checker signatures and exception labels. Later uploads must be identified as manual or reconstructed, never as original scans. |
| **F. Temporary release control** | Add professional oversight during degraded operations | A positive adaptation: pharmacist review covered all controlled-substance orders in its design and reduced controlled-substance errors. | Sampling other orders left gaps; cold-chain documentation was still missed. Missing dual control remained 2.9%, and many temporary-release paths still dispatched without scan. | Controlled substances directly; sampled orders across other products; cold-chain gap | **Modify** | Apply mandatory, product-specific release checklists. Sampling may supplement low-risk assurance, but cannot replace required cold-chain, controlled-substance or recall controls. |
| **G. Dispatch without scan** | Remove a courier-handoff bottleneck | Allowed dispatch to continue when scanning was unavailable. | Created the largest traceability gap: courier identity, handoff time, seals, destination and chain of custody may be absent. Phone confirmation alone is inadequate for controlled products and may not establish cold-chain receipt conditions or recall destination evidence. | All; weakest for controlled products and other high-risk shipments | **Modify** | Permit only as a flagged interim fallback for eligible low-risk orders, with verified recipient/callback and supporting courier evidence. Require signed chain-of-custody evidence where applicable. |
| **I. ## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience is controlled degradation followed by trustworthy recovery.** It protects the most important service while preserving mandatory controls or implementing an approved, evidenced equivalent. It also makes delays, exceptions, staffing costs and residual uncertainty visible. By contrast, concealed noncompliance produces output or apparent closure while required controls are absent, evidence is weak, or risk has been moved to a later queue.

In this case:

- **Most clearly resilient:** **B**, because it substitutes a substantive authorization decision rather than omitting it. **K** is necessary recovery resilience when performed transparently. The mission-prioritization component of **A**, and explicit forms of **I/J**, can also be resilient.
- **Risky shortcuts in their observed form:** **C** and **G**. A’s authorization bypass belongs in the same category. So do reconstructed scans presented as original events under E, phone-only POD for controlled products under H, and retrospective sign-offs presented as proof that a past check occurred under L.
- **Ambiguous and dependent on implementation:** **D** was reasonable early but deteriorated with snapshot age; **E** depends on whether contemporaneous manual evidence existed; **F** was valuable but incomplete; **H** may be a limited low-risk fallback; **I/J** depend on explicit rules and their effects on other customers; **L** is either transparent deviation management or cosmetic closure. **A** is similarly resilient only when fast-lane status changes sequencing, not mandatory control requirements.

Management risk appetite can determine how much standard-order delay, cancellation, courier expense and manual staffing to accept. It cannot, by itself, convert a missing mandatory control into compliance. A formal legal or regulatory emergency provision could change the permissible response, but that must be established explicitly.

Conformance results also require care:

- New move-on-log activities are not automatically bad. A spreadsheet invoice hold may be prudent, and emergency triage may be excellent resilience despite reducing model fitness.
- Missing model events do not alone prove that a physical check never occurred; they may reflect unavailable logging. Conversely, a later reconstructed event does not prove that the check occurred at the required time.
- Thus T2’s 44% fitting traces should not be read as “56% noncompliant,” and T4’s 0.90 fitness should not be read as recovery. Fitness diagnoses process change; product risk, control evidence and outcomes determine its meaning.

The central trade-off was therefore not simply “speed versus compliance.” Risk was transferred **from Hospitals to Pharmacies/Wholesale customers, from release to delivery/reconciliation, and from T2 operations to T4-and-later audit work**. Retrospective remediation remains valuable: an independent temperature logger or signed courier record may recover actual facts. But if no dual check occurred, or no temperature evidence exists, later documentation cannot recreate that control.

## 4. Recovery process model

**Trigger conditions and operating modes**

1. **Declare degraded operations immediately** when any of the following affects an upcoming release:
   - WMS, authorization or required scanning is unavailable;
   - inventory synchronization or records cannot be trusted;
   - cyber integrity concerns affect orders, stock or evidence;
   - required temperature, security, traceability or release evidence cannot be captured.
2. Establish incident command with operations, QA/pharmacist leadership, compliance, IT/security, inventory and customer service.
3. Use **controlled manual mode** only when the minimum controls below can still be performed. Otherwise enter **restricted/hold mode** for the affected orders. Hospital urgency does not waive this gate.
4. Explicit stop-ship conditions include unverified authorization, unidentified recall-sensitive lot, unreliable high-risk inventory, absent required dual control, unresolved temperature/security requirements, or inability to establish an acceptable courier/recipient chain.
5. Return to normal system operation only after IT/security validates system integrity, authorization and scanning work through a full dispatch cycle, inventory synchronization passes reconciliation checks, and QA accepts the manual-event queue. This is **operational recovery**, not necessarily incident closure.

**Triage and release eligibility**

- Triage may precede authorization, but **no picking or release occurs before validated authorization**.
- Prioritize only release-eligible orders by:
  1. Verified patient or essential-service harm, including urgent Pharmacy cases and bulk orders supplying critical providers;
  2. Time constraints such as remaining safe cold-chain transit time or expiry;
  3. Routine replenishment;
  4. Demonstrably deferrable bulk demand.
- Product risk determines the controls and specialist effort required; it does not justify bypassing them.
- Use documented override reasons, order age and FIFO within equivalent priority. Add an aging escalation so lower-priority orders cannot starve indefinitely.
- Record deferral, expected review time, customer notification and any safe substitution or partial-shipment decision.

**Minimum controls by risk**

| Order type | Minimum degraded-mode controls |
|---|---|
| **All orders, including ambient** | Unique incident/order ID; approved offline customer/product authorization with source version; reliable physical inventory reservation; required quality/release check; controlled courier handoff; acceptable delivery evidence. Customer history alone is insufficient. |
| **Hospital-critical** | Exactly the same mandatory controls, with faster access to authorizers, pharmacists, inventory verification and couriers. Any emergency release must follow a formally approved policy and remain visibly exceptional. |
| **Cold-chain, any customer** | Lot/expiry and pack-out records; validated shipper/coolant and lane duration; required temperature readings or logger identity at their actual milestones; documented handoff. Required pre-dispatch evidence must exist before release. Later in-transit/receipt evidence remains an open obligation until received; missing evidence triggers QA disposition, not assumption of compliance. |
| **Controlled-substance, any customer** | Verified eligibility, independent dual control at required stages, quantity reconciliation, secure storage, courier/seal identity and signed recipient chain of custody. Phone-only POD is insufficient. If required controls cannot be staffed, hold the order. |
| **Recall-sensitive, any customer** | Current recall-status check and contemporaneous lot/serial, quantity and destination linkage sufficient to identify affected recipients. Do not dispatch if that linkage cannot be maintained. |

Product controls are cumulative: a Hospital-critical cold-chain controlled order receives all applicable controls.

**Manual evidence capture**

- Issue controlled, sequentially numbered fallback packs containing order ID, authorizer and source version, inventory snapshot ID/age, physical count/reservation, product/lot/serial/expiry/quantity, picker/checker, release decision, temperature/device data where applicable, courier/seal handoff and recipient evidence.
- Record the **actual event time**, recorder and later entry time separately. Corrections preserve the original entry and reason.
- Maintain one controlled offline reservation ledger and one evidence index; uncontrolled local spreadsheets are not independent systems of record.
- Label imported evidence as **contemporaneous manual**, **independently recovered**, or **unverified/reconstructed**. Never generate synthetic scans or signatures.
- Begin reconciliation and high-risk exception review during T2/T3, rather than waiting for full system restoration.

**BPMN-style operational flow**

1. **Receive and register  triage.**
2. **Authorization valid?** If no, route to compliance hold; do not ship.
3. **Inventory and product evidence reliable?** If no, perform controlled physical verification or hold/quarantine.
4. **Pick/pack with manual evidence  independent quality/release gate.** Failure returns to hold, rework or formal deviation handling.
5. **Controlled courier handoff  delivery  product-appropriate POD.** The resulting state is “operationally delivered,” not automatically process END.
6. Required invoice and evidence conditions are checked. A spreadsheet invoice hold remains an owned open state, not an endpoint.

**Backlog and recovery flow**

- Reassess backlog whenever inventory, authorization or staffing changes. Report age and cancellations by priority and customer group.
- At system restoration, freeze and register the manual ledgers, then replay transactions once using duplicate-protection controls. Do not trust partial synchronization merely because interfaces are available.
- Reconcile **order  authorization  offline reservation  physical stock/lot  pick/pack  dispatch/POD  invoice**. Investigate mismatches and obtain customer review before avoidable cancellations.
- Review all high-risk manual releases and all logged exceptions; use risk-based sampling only for otherwise complete low-risk flows.
- For recoverable evidence, obtain independent source records and mark their late recovery. For unrecoverable controls, open a formal deviation and perform QA/pharmacist risk assessment, including customer notification, reporting, quarantine or recall where required.
- Track **operational, financial and compliance closure separately**. Normal operations may resume with a visible, owned and resourced low-risk remediation queue, but full incident closure requires disposition of high-risk exceptions and approved CAPA.

## 5. Resilience indicators

Leading indicators should be reviewed before each dispatch wave or shift; lagging indicators should be evaluated by cohort and after recovery.

| Indicator | Type and definition | Interpretation/escalation |
|---|---|---|
| **Control-complete throughput** | Leading: dispatches per labour hour and percentage of orders due that are dispatched with every mandatory control. Report raw dispatches separately. | Rising raw throughput alongside falling control-complete throughput is a stop-and-investigate signal, not resilience. |
| **Critical-service protection** | Leading/lagging: on-time fill and unfilled critical demand; median/p90 cycle time by segment and product against T0. Include projected critical stockouts for the next wave. | Hospital fill and median cycle time must not obscure the unmet 9%, Pharmacy deterioration or long Wholesale tails. |
| **Authorization and release completeness** | Leading: percentage authorized before pick/release, percentage passing quality release before dispatch, number of unapproved bypasses, and trained-authorizer/pharmacist coverage versus upcoming demand. | Target zero unapproved bypasses. Staffing-queue growth is an early warning of pressure to repeat C. |
| **Contemporaneous traceability and control evidence** | Leading at handoff and lagging at closure: complete lot/destination links, manual/offline handoff evidence, cold-chain records and controlled-substance dual control. Separate contemporaneous, recovered and reconstructed evidence. | Required high-risk evidence must be complete at its designated milestone. An improving retrospective percentage does not erase earlier uncontrolled releases. |
| **Inventory and evidence readiness** | Leading: snapshot age, unposted manual transactions, physical-to-ledger reservation mismatches, synchronization failures and manual-evidence upload lag. | Exceeding approved snapshot age or encountering unexplained high-risk discrepancies triggers physical verification or stop-ship. |
| **Backlog volume and age** | Leading: open/deferred demand, median/p90/oldest age, percentage over service limit, and backlog-to-pick time by priority. | Include demand deferred or cancelled; otherwise deferral artificially improves throughput. Compare with baseline p95 once instrumented. |
| **Rework and reconciliation load** | Leading/lagging: corrections per 100 started cases, release failures, manual cases awaiting reconciliation, and estimated person-hours to clear them. | Compare correction rate with baseline 3.5%, T2 12.5%, T3 9.0% and T4 5.8%; use the open workload to plan recovery staffing. |
| **Fairness across customer groups** | Leading/lagging: fill, deferral, cancellation, p90 cycle time and backlog age for Hospital, Pharmacy and Wholesale, stratified by urgency and product mix; document overrides. | Equal outcomes are not required, but gaps must be explainable by explicit priorities. The 30-point Hospital/Pharmacy fill-rate gap is a review trigger, not proof of unfairness by itself. |
| **Lagging service and compliance outcomes** | Traceability exceptions, missing temperature documentation, missing dual control, cancellations, confirmed unauthorized releases, temperature excursions, controlled-inventory discrepancies and customer harm/complaints. | Compare with T0 rates of 0.8%, 0.4%, 0.2% and 2.1% cancellation, while separately reporting severe individual events that averages can hide. |
| **Recovery and audit tail** | Time to controlled operations; open reconciliation/audit cases and their p90 age; percentage operationally or financially closed with compliance still open; time to deviation disposition. | A persistent or ageing queue prevents a claim of full recovery even when Hospital cycle time and WMS availability look normal. |

Conformance fitness and fitting-trace percentage should accompany these measures as diagnostics, not be optimized as stand-alone resilience targets. All measures should be reported by customer segment and product risk, with denominators that include deferred and cancelled demand.

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept visible, temporary Pharmacy/Wholesale delay, slower release, invoice holds and substantial reconciliation labour when these are the cost of protecting critical supply while preserving authorization, quality, cold-chain, controlled-substance and recall controls. It should not accept raw dispatch volume, the restored 8-hour Hospital median or audit completion as proof of resilience; nor should it accept assumed authorization, uncontrolled courier handoff, informal permanent deferral or retrospective entries presented as real-time compliance. Normal operations may resume with a disclosed and resourced low-risk remediation queue, but unexplained high-risk deviations keep the incident open. The recommendation would soften if contemporaneous offline authorizations, signed custody records, logger data or dual-control evidence show that controls actually occurred, allowing some deviations to be reclassified as logging/model differences. It would harden—and could require broader stop-ship, notification or recall action—if review finds unauthorized supply, temperature excursions, controlled-stock discrepancies, untraceable destinations or evidence that deferral caused unforeseen patient harm.
