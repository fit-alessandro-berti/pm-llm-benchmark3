# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

This review treats the reported metrics as **unreproduced claims**, not as established facts. Neither submission supplies the random seed, exact case identifiers, preprocessing, event ordering rules, alignment costs, PM4Py version beyond “the same installation,” or definitions for several measures. Consequently, the board should not select a model by ranking the numbers in the results table.

Four distinct uses must be separated:

| Intended use | Primary question | Acceptable trade-off | Consequence for these models |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exploration / hypothesis generation** | What behavior might exist, including incomplete or noisy traces? | High fitness and permissiveness can be acceptable; precision is a warning, not an automatic rejection. Findings must not be presented as rules. | Model S may be used as a deliberately weak exploratory lens, with its admitted forbidden behavior disclosed. Model C is poor for exploration because it fragments behavior into memorized variants. |
| **Conformance enforcement / audit** | Does this case violate agreed policy P1–P7? | Mandatory behavior, ordering, exclusivity, and correction rules must be enforced. Precision against forbidden behavior, conditional data rules, and explainable violations matter more than raw alignment fitness. | Neither original is deployable as-is: S admits clear violations (F1); C rejects plausible valid combinations (F2, F4) and encodes no transparent correction rule (F6). |
| **Simulation / prediction / resourcing** | Are branching probabilities, repetitions, timing, and attribute-dependent routing realistic? | Needs data attributes, frequencies, durations, soundness, and reachability—not merely control-flow fitness. | Neither model is validated for simulation. S has no `Correct Dossier` at all; C hard-codes a few paths without probabilities or attributes. |
| **Stakeholder communication / ownership** | Can accountable owners understand, change, and assign responsibility for each rule? | A small backbone plus named constraints is preferable to branch enumeration, even if a formal metric such as simplicity is imperfect. | S is readable but misleading because readability hides missing mandates. C is difficult to own or update (F3). |

**Assumptions made explicit**

1. P1, P2, and P5 are hard control-flow requirements unless stakeholders revise them.
2. P3 is a **data-conditioned** requirement: without the external/internal attribute, no purely control-flow model extracted from that log can prove Supplier Review was required or forbidden.
3. P4 is hard on ordering—Expedite Review after both mandatory checks and before approval—but optional on occurrence.
4. P6 is currently under-specified operationally: the process may repeat only affected checks, but the log lacks the “affected check” attribute. A model may allow selective rechecks structurally, but cannot validate that the *right* subset was repeated without more data.
5. P7 makes repeated mandatory checks without an intervening `Correct Dossier` nonconforming for enforcement purposes, subject to investigation for duplicate/missing-event data quality problems.
6. Behavior probes B1–B8 are policy tests, not a complete ground truth. “Accepts” below means exact membership in the model language, not a high alignment fitness after deleting or inserting events.

The fitness/precision trade-off is therefore use-dependent. Advocates’ positions F7 and F8 are each partially coherent—S for exploration, C for conservative audit—but neither converts its preferred metric into evidence of policy correctness.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

Model S:

```text
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  +(
    X('Credit Check', tau),
    X('Technical Review', tau),
    X('Supplier Review', tau),
    X('Expedite Review', tau)
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

**Strongest case for Model S.** It is compact, fast to align, and robust as an exploratory device when events may be missing (F4, F7). Its parallel block captures the genuine insight that several checks can occur in different orders, and its high holdout alignment fitness warns against assuming the training variants exhaust reality. For early discovery workshops it can start a conversation: “these activities cluster between registration and approval.” Its simplicity also makes its semantics easy to inspect—ironically, that inspectability is what lets reviewers see its flaws.

**Strongest case against Model S.** It is not merely noisy-tolerant; it changes the normative meaning of P2, P4, and P3. Mandatory checks become optional, Expedite Review loses its required precedence, Supplier Review is detached from external sourcing, and correction behavior is absent. Low precision is not an incidental metric blemish but the expected result of these semantic choices. Using it for enforcement would certify cases stakeholders explicitly call forbidden.

At least five concrete admitted behaviors are undesirable or policy-ambiguous. “Admits” means exact language membership:

1. **Skips both mandatory checks — forbidden by P2/F1.**
   `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` (B3). All four parallel branches choose `tau`.

2. **Skips one mandatory check while appearing checked.**
   `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order`. Technical Review is silently skipped, violating P2.

3. **Expedite Review before mandatory checks — forbidden by P4/B4.**
   `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`. Parallel interleaving permits Expedite first.

4. **Expedite Review with no mandatory checks at all.**
   `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`. This compounds P2 and P4 violations: urgency processing substitutes for checks.

5. **Supplier Review treated as universally optional and unconditional.**
   `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` is valid for an external case (B7), but S also admits the same trace for an internal order and admits omitting Supplier Review for an external order—for example B1’s trace shape—because the attribute is absent and the model simply makes Supplier Review optional everywhere.

6. **Approval immediately after registration with only optional decoration.**
   `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`: both mandatory checks skipped, yet an approval and release occur.

7. **No correction semantics.** Any trace containing `Correct Dossier`, including B6, is outside the exact language; the model does not distinguish legitimate selective recheck from suspicious repetition (P6/P7).

**Intentional noise tolerance vs accidental semantic flaws.** Optionalizing Supplier Review could be defended as missing-attribute tolerance, and optional Expedite Review matches P4’s “optional occurrence.” But optionalizing Credit/Technical Review is not a noise model unless the team explicitly assumes mandatory events may be missing and routes such cases to data-quality review. Likewise, placing all four activities in one parallel block is stronger than “checks may complete in either order”: it erases Expedite’s precedence constraint. The absence of `Correct Dossier` is an omission, not robustness; alignments may delete it as a log move and thereby look “fit” while explaining nothing. In short, S confuses *alignment tolerance for possibly missing observations* with *permission for the underlying process to skip required work*.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

Model C is an exclusive choice among eight fully sequenced variants. It includes only Manager Approval except in the final Auto Approval branch, only one correction pattern, and only selected combinations of Supplier/Expedite and check order.

**Strongest case for Model C.** It encodes several real constraints better than S: registration first, release last, both mandatory checks present in every branch, Expedite after checks where it appears, and exactly one approval in each listed path. Its high training precision reflects that it does not casually admit B3 or B4. For a narrow, stable, fully enumerated subprocess, such a model could be auditable branch-by-branch. The C advocate’s conservatism (F8) is understandable in regulated settings: unseen behavior should trigger review rather than automatic acceptance.

**Strongest case against Model C.** C is principally a **variant catalogue**, not a compositional process model. It memorizes combinations that happened to be frequent rather than representing independent dimensions: check order (2), Supplier present/absent, Expedite present/absent, approval type (2), and correction pattern. Those dimensions multiply; enumerating them guarantees missing valid combinations and makes every policy change a branch-editing exercise (F2, F3).

Specific structural failures:

- **Branch enumeration instead of factors.** B5 combines known features—alternate check order, Supplier, Expedite, Auto Approval—yet is rejected because that exact conjunction was not listed (F4). This is the signature of memorization, not rule learning.
- **Approval asymmetry.** Auto Approval appears only in `Credit, Technical, Manager-order` form in branch 8: `Register, Credit, Technical, Auto, Release`. Thus B2 (`Technical, Credit, Auto`) is rejected even though P2 allows either order and P5 allows Auto Approval. C silently invents a rule that Auto Approval requires Credit before Technical.
- **Correction handling is rigid and opaque.** The sole correction branch requires **both** checks to repeat after correction: `... Correct, Credit, Technical, Manager, Release`. It rejects B6’s selective Technical-only recheck and cannot express Credit-only recheck (F6). It also lacks Supplier/Expedite interplay after correction and multiple distinct correction cycles beyond replaying that one pattern.
- **Duplicate labels.** Activities recur across exclusive branches; the reported count is 33 under an undocumented definition. In a Petri-net conversion, same-label transitions in different branches complicate alignment explanations, token replay, impact analysis, and ownership: an event labelled `Credit Check` does not identify which conceptual branch instance is responsible (F3).
- **Holdout degradation.** Fitness falls from 0.973 training to 0.744 holdout while S remains high. That gap is consistent with overfitting to frequent variants and with F4’s unseen legitimate combinations. F8’s reply—“unseen is noncompliant”—is a governance stance, not evidence; it would convert discovery omissions and missing events into violations.
- **Precision may be an artifact.** Training precision 0.941 can be high because the model’s language is close to the observed positive variant set. F5 notes there was no curated negative suite, so we do not know whether C rejects the *right* forbidden traces for the right reasons, nor whether it rejects valid-but-unobserved traces excessively—which B2 and B5 already demonstrate.
- **Maintainability and runtime.** Adding Auto Approval to external/urgent cases or selective correction multiplies branches. The 0.440s median alignment time and 0.281 simplicity are consistent with that blow-up, though confounded.
- **Missing Supplier conditionality.** C includes Supplier in some branches and omits it in others, but without the external attribute it cannot enforce P3; it merely correlates Supplier with listed variants.

C is therefore not “audit-ready because it mirrors frequent variants.” Audit readiness requires rules that remain correct under recombining known dimensions, explicit treatment of data conditions, and explainable rejection. C offers traceability to examples at the expense of generalization and changeability.

Governance must therefore report “accepted / not accepted” separately from alignment cost.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by likely decision impact:

1. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior (highest impact).** S makes Credit/Technical optional and omits correction, yet reports higher fitness. If the board rewards fitness, it directly rewards violating P2. This pathology can turn a forbidden trace such as B3 into an alignment success. Decision rule must therefore include hard behavior probes that veto enforcement use regardless of fitness.

2. **Positive-log-only precision and absence of a negative suite.** Precision computed against observed traces cannot distinguish “unobserved because forbidden” from “unobserved because rare, new, conditional, or missing” (F5). C’s 0.941 may mean policy precision or merely variant memorization; S’s 0.584 understates/overstates different risks depending on which extra traces are truly forbidden. Curated forbidden traces (B3, B4, B8, duplicate approval, release before approval) are essential.

3. **Train/holdout leakage or split sensitivity.** Without seeds and case IDs, the 0.744 C holdout result could reflect a genuinely brittle model, a variant-clustered split, temporal drift, or preprocessing differences. Conversely, S’s stable fitness could reflect leakage of similar variants or simply permissiveness. F4’s description—holdout recombines training features—suggests the split tested compositional generalization, which is exactly what an enforcement model needs, but this must be reproduced with published splits: random, variant-stratified, feature-combination, and temporal splits.

4. **Missing-event bias.** If Supplier or check events are sometimes not logged (F4), fitness penalizes models that correctly require them and rewards models that optionalize them. Missingness may also be non-random—e.g., external orders integrate with a supplier system that drops events—which biases both discovery and evaluation. Evaluation needs missing-data perturbation and, ideally, source-system reconciliation rather than assuming the log is complete.

5. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness.** Token-based generalization (S 0.907) can be high because broad parallel places are visited often. It does not check whether generalized behavior respects P2/P4. Similarly, holdout fitness for S may measure “can align” rather than “should accept.” Generalization must be tested on *labelled valid unseen combinations* and *labelled forbidden recombinations*, not only on unlabelled logs.

6. **Duplicated-label effects.** C’s repeated labels across branches can distort alignments (multiple transitions with the same label), token replay, generalization, precision escaping edges, runtime, and simplicity after conversion. They also create ambiguity in diagnostics: which branch instance fired? The undocumented count prevents exact comparison, but qualitatively this is a major maintainability pathology (F3).

7. **Alignment-cost sensitivity.** Fitness/precision can move materially depending on costs for log moves, model moves on silent transitions, and duplicate labels. With undocumented costs, S may cheaply skip mandatory activities via `tau`, while C may incur large costs for one missing/reordered event. Sensitivity analysis over cost schemes is mandatory before quoting differences such as 0.981 vs 0.744 as stable.

8. **Silent-transition effects.** S uses `tau` inside each `X(activity,tau)`. Silent transitions can provide cheap alignment paths and affect precision/generalization by enabling behavior without observable events. They are semantically necessary for optionality but must be costed and reported; otherwise “skipping work” and “not observing work” are indistinguishable.

9. **Runtime confounding by model size and search space.** C’s slower median alignment likely reflects branch count, duplicate labels, net size, and trace length—not an intrinsic property stakeholders should weigh equally with correctness. Still, worst-case runtime matters for audit at scale. Report runtime by trace length, variant, and percentile, with hardware and caching fixed.

10. **Stakeholder-score methodology.** The 4.4 vs 1.9 scores lack protocol. A model can score high because it is visually clean while users fail to predict that B3 is admitted. Comprehension must be measured by tasks—trace classification, rule identification, change impact—not preference alone. This is ranked last not because usability is unimportant, but because the current numbers are too weak to overturn behavioral evidence.

# 7. Third Alternative

## Strategy chosen

A **procedural backbone plus explicit declarative/data constraints and post-hoc policy checks**. The backbone compositionally encodes what control flow can encode honestly: start/end, mandatory parallel checks, optional Supplier/Expedite after checks with free relative order, zero or more correction cycles with selective observable rechecks, exactly one approval, and release. Rules that depend on missing attributes are **not** silently weakened; they produce a three-valued outcome: `PASS`, `FAIL`, or `UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE` / `POLICY_REVIEW`.

This avoids S’s flaw—do not make mandatory checks optional—and avoids C’s flaw—do not enumerate combinations of independent choices.

## 1. Compact formal description

Let:

- `R` = Register Dossier, `CC` = Credit Check, `TR` = Technical Review, `SR` = Supplier Review, `ER` = Expedite Review, `CD` = Correct Dossier, `MA` = Manager Approval, `AA` = Auto Approval, `RO` = Release Order.
- Initial phase: `R` then parallel composition `CC  TR` (both exactly once, either order).
- Post-check optional phase: `(SR or skip)  (ER or skip)`, sequenced **after** both mandatory checks; SR and ER may be in either relative order.
- Correction loop: zero or more cycles of `CD` followed by a parallel selective-recheck block in which each of `CC, TR, SR, ER` may occur at most once in that cycle. Declarative checks below require at least one affected mandatory recheck when the attribute exists, and flag empty or unexplained rechecks.
- Approval/release: exactly one of `MA, AA`, then `RO`, with no approval before the initial checks and any started correction cycle is completed.

In process-tree notation, the backbone is:

```text
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  +('Credit Check', 'Technical Review'),
  +(X('Supplier Review', tau), X('Expedite Review', tau)),
  *(tau,
    ->('Correct Dossier',
       +(X('Credit Check', tau), X('Technical Review', tau),
         X('Supplier Review', tau), X('Expedite Review', tau)))),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

The loop `*(tau, redo)` means: execute `tau`, then either exit or execute a correction cycle and repeat. Thus zero, one, or many correction cycles are possible; each correction is followed by an observable recheck block (possibly empty in the backbone, but rejected/flagged by constraints below rather than silently certified).

## 3. Declarative dictionaries and external checks

```python
MANDATORY_CHECKS = {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}

DECLARE_LIKE_CONSTRAINTS = {
    "P1_first_last": {
        "first": "Register Dossier",
        "last": "Release Order",
    },
    "P2_mandatory_before_approval": {
        "activities": ["Credit Check", "Technical Review"],
        "before_any_of": ["Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"],
        "min_occurrences_before_approval": 1,
    },
    "P4_expedite_order": {
        "activity": "Expedite Review",
        "after_all": ["Credit Check", "Technical Review"],
        "before_any_of": ["Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"],
        "note": "Within a correction cycle, Expedite must also follow any rechecked mandatory check in that cycle.",
    },
    "P5_exactly_one_approval": {
        "one_of": ["Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"],
        "total_count": 1,
        "before": "Release Order",
    },
    "P7_no_repeat_without_correction": {
        "activities": ["Credit Check", "Technical Review"],
        "repeat_requires_intervening": "Correct Dossier",
    },
    "P6_correction_recheck": {
        "after": "Correct Dossier",
        "recheck_parallel_any_subset_of": [
            "Credit Check", "Technical Review", "Supplier Review", "Expedite Review"
        ],
        "requires_at_least_one_recheck_before_approval": True,
        "if_affected_attribute_available": (
            "rechecked mandatory checks must equal the affected mandatory-check set; "
            "unaffected mandatory repeats are violations, affected non-repeats are violations"
        ),
        "if_affected_attribute_missing": "POLICY_REVIEW, not silent PASS",
    },
    "P3_supplier_conditional": {
        "activity": "Supplier Review",
        "if_case_attribute": {"sourcing": "external"},
        "then": "required at least once before approval (initial or justified recheck cycle)",
        "if_sourcing_internal": "absence expected; presence is POLICY_REVIEW unless business confirms otherwise",
        "if_sourcing_missing": "UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE",
    },
}

APPROVALS = {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"}

def activities_of(trace):
    """trace: iterable of activity-name strings, in recorded order."""
    return list(trace)

def check_trace_policy(trace, case_attrs=None):
    """
    Returns (status, reasons).
    status in {"PASS", "FAIL", "UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE", "POLICY_REVIEW"}.
    This is a transparent post-hoc layer; it does not alter model_third's language.
    """
    case_attrs = case_attrs or {}
    acts = activities_of(trace)
    reasons = []
    status = "PASS"

    def escalate(new_status, msg):
        nonlocal status
        reasons.append(msg)
        priority = {"PASS": 0, "UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE": 1,
                    "POLICY_REVIEW": 2, "FAIL": 3}
        if priority[new_status] > priority[status]:
            status = new_status

    if not acts or acts[0] != "Register Dossier" or acts[-1] != "Release Order":
        escalate("FAIL", "P1: Register must be first and Release last.")

    approval_idx = [i for i, a in enumerate(acts) if a in APPROVALS]
    if len(approval_idx) != 1:
        escalate("FAIL", "P5: exactly one approval is required before Release.")
        first_approval = len(acts)
    else:
        first_approval = approval_idx[0]
        if "Release Order" in acts[:first_approval]:
            escalate("FAIL", "P1/P5: Release precedes approval.")

    pre_approval = acts[:first_approval]

    for chk in MANDATORY_CHECKS:
        if chk not in pre_approval:
            escalate("FAIL", f"P2: mandatory {chk} missing before approval.")

    # P4: first Expedite must be after first completion of both mandatory checks.
    if "Expedite Review" in pre_approval:
        try:
            both_done = max(pre_approval.index("Credit Check"),
                            pre_approval.index("Technical Review"))
            if pre_approval.index("Expedite Review") < both_done:
                escalate("FAIL", "P4: Expedite Review precedes completion of mandatory checks.")
        except ValueError:
            escalate("FAIL", "P4/P2: Expedite present while a mandatory check is missing.")

    # P7 / correction segmentation
    segments = [[]]  # split trace by Correct Dossier for repeat analysis
    for a in acts:
        if a == "Correct Dossier":
            segments.append([])
        else:
            segments[-1].append(a)

    # Repeated mandatory check within a segment = no intervening correction.
    for seg in segments:
        for chk in MANDATORY_CHECKS:
            if seg.count(chk) > 1:
                escalate("FAIL", f"P7: {chk} repeated without intervening Correct Dossier.")

    # P6: each correction must be followed by >=1 observable recheck before approval/end.
    for i, a in enumerate(acts):
        if a == "Correct Dossier":
            following = acts[i + 1:first_approval] if first_approval > i else acts[i + 1:]
            rechecks = [x for x in following if x in (
                "Credit Check", "Technical Review", "Supplier Review", "Expedite Review")]
            # stop at next correction: only rechecks before it belong to this cycle
            cycle = []
            for x in acts[i + 1:]:
                if x == "Correct Dossier" or x in APPROVALS:
                    break
                cycle.append(x)
            cycle_rechecks = [x for x in cycle if x in (
                "Credit Check", "Technical Review", "Supplier Review", "Expedite Review")]
            if not cycle_rechecks:
                escalate("POLICY_REVIEW", "P6: Correct Dossier with no observable recheck.")
            affected = case_attrs.get("affected_checks")  # e.g. {"Technical Review"}
            if affected is None:
                if cycle_rechecks:
                    escalate("POLICY_REVIEW",
                             "P6: selective recheck observed, but affected_checks attribute is missing.")
            else:
                rechecked_mandatory = {x for x in cycle_rechecks if x in MANDATORY_CHECKS}
                if rechecked_mandatory != set(affected):
                    escalate("FAIL",
                             "P6: rechecked mandatory checks do not match affected_checks.")

    # P3 conditional Supplier rule
    sourcing = case_attrs.get("sourcing")  # "external" | "internal" | None
    has_supplier = "Supplier Review" in pre_approval
    if sourcing == "external" and not has_supplier:
        escalate("FAIL", "P3: external order lacks Supplier Review before approval.")
    elif sourcing == "internal" and has_supplier:
        escalate("POLICY_REVIEW", "P3: Supplier Review present for internal order; confirm policy.")
    elif sourcing is None:
        escalate("UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE",
                 "P3: sourcing attribute missing; Supplier requirement cannot be certified.")

    return status, reasons
```

## 4. Explicit semantics

- **Mandatory checks (P2):** In the backbone, `+(CC,TR)` requires both exactly once in the initial phase, in either order. They are not optional. Silent skipping is impossible in the backbone language.
- **Optional Supplier Review (P3):** Backbone-wise, Supplier is optional because the log lacks sourcing. Semantically the hybrid does **not** conclude “optional for everyone.” `check_trace_policy` returns `FAIL` for known-external without Supplier, `POLICY_REVIEW` for known-internal with Supplier (until stakeholders confirm whether presence is forbidden or merely unnecessary), and `UNKNOWN_REQUIRES_ATTRIBUTE` when sourcing is missing.
- **Expedite Review ordering (P4):** Backbone sequence places the initial Supplier/Expedite block after `+(CC,TR)` and before approval; within that block SR and ER are parallel, so ER may precede or follow SR but not the mandatory checks. The post-hoc checker also flags Expedite before completion of both checks and, by policy note, requires cycle-level review if Expedite appears in a correction cycle before that cycle’s rechecked mandatory checks.
- **Exactly-one approval (P5):** `X(MA,AA)` in sequence before Release gives exactly one approval in the backbone. The checker independently counts approvals to make audit output explicit.
- **Correction/recheck (P6/P7):** The loop permits zero or more `Correct Dossier` cycles. In each cycle, any subset of checks may observably repeat at most once per activity within that cycle’s parallel block. This represents selective recheck structurally without inventing which check was affected. If `affected_checks` is later captured, the checker enforces exact match for mandatory rechecks. If it is missing, a selective recheck is routed to `POLICY_REVIEW`, and a correction with no recheck is not silently passed. A mandatory repeat without an intervening correction cannot be produced by moving within one backbone segment and is flagged by P7 logic; B8 is rejected by both backbone and checker.

## 5. Limitations caused by missing attributes

The missing external/internal attribute means Supplier Review cannot be validated as required, forbidden, or irrelevant for unlabelled cases. The honest output is conditional or unknown, not optional-for-all. Similarly, without `affected_checks`, the model can represent “only Technical repeated” but cannot know whether Technical was the affected check (B6). It also cannot determine whether Auto vs Manager Approval should depend on amount, risk, sourcing, or urgency—P5 only states exclusivity—so no approval-routing rule is invented. Finally, if events are missing in the source log, backbone rejection may indicate data incompleteness rather than process violation; conformance output must separate `FAIL` from `UNKNOWN` and data-quality review.

# 9. Validation Experiments

No new metric results are asserted here. The following program is designed to produce decision-grade evidence.

## Reproducible case splits and preprocessing

- Freeze an event-log snapshot with hash, source query, case/activity/timestamp columns, sorting tie-breakers, and deduplication rules.
- Publish preprocessing code: incomplete-case handling, timestamp ordering, activity-name normalization, whether first/last incomplete cases are removed, and treatment of repeated events.
- Use multiple splits, each with published case IDs and seeds:
  1. random case split 80/20 (seed fixed);
  2. variant-stratified split;
  3. feature-combination holdout: hold out combinations such as alternate order × Auto × Supplier × Expedite (B5-like);
  4. temporal holdout if timestamps permit;
  5. attribute-stratified holdout once sourcing/affected attributes are recovered.
- Repeat random splits over at least 30 seeds and report distributions, not point estimates.

## Attribute-stratified holdout tests

Recover or instrument `sourcing  {external, internal}`, `affected_checks`, approval rationale/risk tier, urgency, and source-system completeness flags. Evaluate separately for external/internal, urgent/non-urgent, corrected/uncorrected, and approval type. A model must not be certified on strata where the deciding attribute is missing; report coverage and `UNKNOWN` rate.

## Curated negative and positive suites

Build a versioned trace suite with stakeholder-signed labels: B1–B8, A1–A4, plus forbidden traces (missing each mandatory check, Expedite first, both approvals, no approval, Release before approval, repeat without correction, correction with no recheck, Supplier missing for external synthetically labelled cases) and valid recombinations. Report per-probe pass/fail before any aggregate metric. Any `FAIL` on a veto probe (B3/B4 type) rejects enforcement use.

## Missing-event and duplicate-event perturbation

For held-out traces, randomly and systematically delete Supplier/check/Expedite events at several rates; duplicate events; swap adjacent parallel events; delay timestamps. Measure: acceptance change, alignment fitness change, violation-type change, and `UNKNOWN` routing. The goal is to distinguish process violation from observation failure. A mandatory-check model should degrade into data-quality review under plausible missingness, not silently become S.

## Unseen-combination tests

Enumerate the factorial space of independent choices—check order × Supplier × Expedite × approval × correction subset (with sourcing/affected labels assigned by policy)—and generate synthetic labelled traces. Test whether the model accepts exactly the policy-labelled combinations. This directly tests C’s memorization weakness and the third model’s compositional claim.

## Alignment-cost sensitivity

Run alignments under multiple cost schemes: unit costs; higher model-move cost for mandatory activities; higher log-move cost; standard PM4Py defaults documented explicitly. Report whether rankings and per-case violation classifications are stable. Publish PM4Py version and parameters.

## Soundness and reachability

Check Petri-net soundness, dead transitions, unreachable branches, approval reachability, Release reachability, and whether correction loops can bypass approval or repeat indefinitely without progress. For the hybrid, also unit-test the checker.

## Metrics reported separately

Report fitness, precision (with negative-suite precision in addition to log-based precision), generalization, simplicity, runtime distributions, duplicate-label counts with code, unknown/review rates, and per-stratum results. Do not publish a single weighted score unless governance pre-registers normalization and weights.

## Stakeholder comprehension protocol

Recruit defined roles (compliance, operations, IT, process owner). Give counterbalanced model presentations. Tasks: classify B1–B8/A1–A4, identify which rule causes a rejection, predict effect of adding selective correction, and locate the owner of Supplier rule. Score accuracy, time, confidence calibration, and inter-rater agreement; compare against chance. Preference ratings may be collected but cannot substitute for task accuracy.

## Decision rule for rejection despite good aggregates

Reject for enforcement if any holds: (a) accepts a signed forbidden veto trace; (b) rejects a signed valid trace without an `UNKNOWN`/data-quality route; (c) cannot explain violations by named policy rule; (d) holdout compositional tests fail materially; (e) required attributes are missing for a stratum being certified; (f) soundness/reachability fails; (g) results are not reproducible under published splits/costs. High fitness cannot cure a veto failure.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

**Model S:** May be used only for clearly labelled exploration and workshop hypothesis generation. It must carry a visible warning that it admits skipping mandatory checks and early Expedite Review. It must not be used for compliance decisions, audit evidence, automated approval, simulation of compliant behavior, or management reporting of conformance rates. Retire it as a candidate enforcement model immediately; retain it, if at all, as a discovery artifact.

**Model C:** Must not be used for compliance enforcement in its current form. Its rejection of B2 and B5 shows false noncompliance risk, and its correction semantics contradict F6. It may be retained temporarily as a regression suite of historically frequent variants—i.e., examples to explain, not rules to enforce—and as input to root-cause analysis of holdout failures. Retire it as a standalone model once the third alternative passes validation; do not “fix” it by appending more branches except as an emergency, explicitly temporary patch with expiry.

**Third alternative:** Recommended direction, but **not yet deployable for enforcement**. Before deployment it must meet these conditions:

1. Reproducible evaluation under §9 with published log hash, preprocessing, splits/seeds, PM4Py version, and alignment costs.
2. Zero acceptance of signed forbidden veto probes (including B3, B4, B8, dual approval, release-before-approval) under the hybrid verdict.
3. Acceptance of signed valid recombinations (B1, B2, B5, B7) subject only to explicit attribute conditions; B6 resolved according to `affected_checks` once available, and routed to review until then.
4. Soundness and reachability checks pass; correction loop cannot bypass mandatory checks or approval.
5. External/internal and affected-check attributes are captured at source, or the affected strata remain `UNKNOWN` and are excluded from automated certification.
6. Missing/duplicate-event perturbation tests show violations are triaged into process noncompliance vs data-quality review according to a pre-registered rule.
7. Stakeholder task-based comprehension meets a pre-set threshold across compliance, operations, IT, and process ownership—not merely a preference score.
8. Named owners are assigned for backbone, each DECLARE-like constraint, attribute feeds, and change control; adding a correction type must not require branch enumeration.
9. Runtime and diagnostics meet operational limits at agreed percentiles, with duplicate-label count for the third model reported by published code.

Until those gates pass, use the third alternative in **shadow mode**: run it alongside human review, compare disagreements, and treat `POLICY_REVIEW`/`UNKNOWN` as mandatory human triage. If governance forced a binary choice today between only S and C for enforcement, neither is acceptable; the least-bad interim is human review informed by C’s frequent variants as examples and S explicitly quarantined from decision-making.

# 11. Dissenting View

The proposed hybrid mistakes stakeholder preference for a clean backbone for evidence that it is correct. Its most important rules — selective rechecking, conditional Supplier Review, and data-dependent approval — live outside the executable model, precisely because the attributes needed to enforce them are absent. Calling this safer may create false assurance: users will see a tidy diagram, while consequential decisions remain hidden in Python checks few managers can inspect. Model C, by contrast, at least exposes what the organisation actually approved and did repeatedly; its low holdout fitness may indicate genuinely unauthorised innovation, missing events, or an unrepresentative split, not necessarily model failure. Retiring it before curating negative examples and recovering source-system attributes risks replacing auditable conservatism with elegant speculation. Model S likewise has a legitimate exploratory role that the recommendation understates. Until conditional validity is observable, enforcement should remain conservative, human-reviewed, and explicitly provisional rather than delegated to a newly invented hybrid whose own fitness, precision, soundness, and comprehension have not yet been measured at all.

This dissent is strongest on one point the recommendation must concede: the third alternative is currently a **design hypothesis**, not a validated model, and moving complexity from a process diagram into code can reduce transparency for non-technical governors if the checker is not governed as a first-class artifact.

I would change my recommendation if additional evidence showed any of the following: that holdout “valid recombinations” such as B5 were in fact unauthorized when reviewed by compliance with complete attributes; that sourcing and affected-check data cannot be recovered and shadow-mode `UNKNOWN` rates make the hybrid operationally useless; that C, under reproducible stratified splits and a signed negative suite, rejects forbidden behavior without rejecting labelled valid recombinations after only minimal, maintainable amendment; or that stakeholders perform materially better on rule-prediction and change-impact tasks with C or S than with the backbone-plus-constraints presentation. Conversely, evidence that missing events explain B6/B8-style anomalies would shift enforcement further toward data-quality triage rather than stricter control flow.
