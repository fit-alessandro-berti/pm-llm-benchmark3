## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation
The gap between raw and mapped model fitness in March–April reflects a primarily semantic/logging shock rather than an immediate behavioral overhaul. Raw fitness collapses from about 0.92 to 0.55 when `Validate Eligibility` becomes `Eligibility Screen` and duplicated `Open Case` events appear, inflating apparent model violations. Once we map `Eligibility Screen` back and remove duplicates, mapped fitness returns close to baseline (~0.9), showing that most of the structural mismatch was representational rather than a real redesign of the eligibility stage. The residual 2–5 point decline in mapped fitness later in the year indicates additional, smaller behavioral and case-mix changes.

However, successful label mapping does **not** prove that the business process is unchanged. It shows that a particular normalization restores conformance with respect to the old control-flow model along observed edges, but it remains blind to new optional steps (e.g., more consistent `Senior Assessment` logging), subtle timing differences, or shifts in the mix of variants.

Duplicated technical events mainly distort ordering and conformance statistics, while missing resource identifiers hinder workload and efficiency analysis (e.g., queueing by team) but leave activity-level DFG counts mostly intact. Because the post-May changes involve large shifts in supplier mix, rework variants, queueing-based cycle times, and normalized DFG edges that are **not** directly touched by the migration labels, they cannot be dismissed as logging drift alone. The alignment between Supplier Z disruption, staffing loss, priority rule, and worsening performance strongly suggests genuine process and workload drift layered on top of the initial semantic issues.

**3.2 Normalized DFG-distance analysis**

Using the 7-edge vectors of normalized relation rates \(v_m\) for each month \(m\), define an L1 distance to January:
\[
d(m) = \frac{\sum_i |v_{m,i} - v_{Jan,i}|}{\sum_i v_{Jan,i}}
\]
where omitted edges are assumed unchanged (implicitly contributing 0 to the numerator). This underestimates true structural drift but transparently captures changes along the given backbone.

Approximate distances are:

| month | distance_vs_2026-01 (L1 / sum) | interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-03 | 0.01 | Structure almost unchanged; March drift dominated by semantics/logging, not control-flow |
| 2026-05 | 0.10 | Clear shift toward more rework and slightly weaker direct flow to decisions |
| 2026-07 | 0.15 | Strongest mid-year control-flow drift, with higher `Request Info` and `Senior Assessment` usage and fewer direct decisions |
| 2026-09 | 0.07 | Partial structural reversion as Supplier Z recovers and rework falls |
| 2026-10 | 0.16 | Peak deviation: heavy `Senior Assessment` edges and sustained rework under renewal-season load |
| 2026-12 | 0.09 | Improvement relative to October but not a full return to baseline structure |

**3.3 Cycle-time deterioration decomposition**

From January to October, median cycle time increases by about 16.3 hours (31.5→47.8). Median service time for `Assess` rises modestly (roughly 4.8→5.4 hours), so direct service effects likely contribute <10–15% of the deterioration. The sharp growth in reconstructed waiting before `Assess`, combined with staffing cuts and rising volumes, suggests queueing accounts for the majority, plausibly 60–70% of the added delay. Rework-related paths (higher `Assess→Request Info→Collect Documents→Assess` rates, fast_variant_pct falling, rework_variant_pct more than doubling) reasonably explain another 15–25%, as cases loop through document collection and re-assessment. The remaining 10–20% is consistent with case-mix drift: high_complexity_pct rises from 21% to 41% by October and Supplier Z’s share spikes mid-year, both associated with longer, more document-intensive trajectories. These ranges are qualitative but anchored in the relative magnitudes of queueing vs. service-time changes and the sizable shifts in high-complexity and rework metrics.

**3.4 Candidate change points by drift type**

- **Logging drift:** (1) 2026-03 (system migration), marked by raw fitness collapse, new activity labels, higher duplicate_open_case_pct, and missing_resource_pct jump. (2) Around 2026-10, when `Assess→Senior Assessment` edges rise sharply and Senior Assessment outcomes are logged more consistently, suggesting a semantic/logging improvement for high-complexity handling in addition to genuine routing changes.
- **Behavioral (control-flow) drift:** (1) 2026-05, with doubled rework variant share, increased Assess→Request Info rates, and weakened direct decision edges in the DFG. (2) 2026-07, when the priority rule starts, raising Assess→Senior Assessment rates and further reducing direct Assess/SeniorAssessment→Decision probability.
- **Performance drift:** (1) 2026-05, where median and p90 cycle times first jump materially above baseline while service time barely changes. (2) 2026-07–2026-10, as p90_cycle_h climbs from 95 to 116 hours and mapped fitness does not improve, indicating sustained congestion rather than pure logging anomalies.

**3.5 Global vs. mechanism-specific change points**

The evidence strongly favors **multiple mechanism-specific change points** rather than one global breakpoint. March reflects a largely semantic/logging shock; May introduces supplier-driven rework and performance deterioration; June–July add staffing-driven queueing and priority-induced redistribution; October overlays seasonal case-mix drift under an already stressed system; December combines load relief with targeted interventions. The staggered timing of distinct signals (fitness, DFG distance, rework, queueing, and case mix) is inconsistent with a single global change point and supports a layered, evolving drift narrative.

| Seasonality segments | `renewal_season_flag` (Oct–Nov vs other months) | Separate structural drift from recurring seasonal load effects | ≥400 cases per month; compare Oct–Nov only to prior-year Oct–Nov where available | Seasonal vs non-seasonal performance; stability of seasonal patterns across years | Seasonal flag may coincide with policy changes; small number of seasonal months reduces statistical power |
| Team/region segments | `assessor_team`, `region` (coarser groups) | Understand workload distribution and team-level behavior/performance | ≥150 cases per team-region per quarter; merge sparse cells | Cross-team DFGs, service times, and queueing under similar case mix; impact of staffing loss | Team assignment may correlate with unobserved difficulty; migration may have changed team IDs or logging coverage |

The **primary monitoring population** should be all **non-renewal months for standard-complexity, non-Supplier Z cases under normalized labels**. This segment is large enough for stable statistics, less exposed to supplier-specific and seasonal shocks, and therefore most sensitive to emerging structural or behavioral drift in the core process. Other segments (Supplier Z, renewal season, high complexity, priority cases) should be monitored as targeted overlays with tailored thresholds.

Seasonality should be handled by comparing seasonal months (e.g., Oct–Nov) primarily to the **same months in prior years** and by maintaining separate baselines for seasonal vs non-seasonal periods. Multiple testing across monitors can be managed with tiered alert severity (e.g., requiring persistence over multiple months and focusing on families of related metrics) and, where formal testing is used, by applying simple false-discovery-rate control rather than a single universal p-value or threshold.

def add_baseline_relative_changes(monthly: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Calculate simple baseline-relative changes vs. first month for key metrics."""
    baseline = monthly.iloc[0]
    for col in [
        "cases", "high_complexity_pct", "supplier_z_pct",
        "fast_variant_pct", "rework_variant_pct",
        "median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h", "mapped_model_fitness",
    ]:
        delta_col = f"delta_{col}_vs_baseline"
        monthly[delta_col] = monthly[col] - baseline[col]
    return monthly


def compute_dfg_l1_distances(dfg_fragment: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Compute transparent L1 distances between monthly DFG fragments and January."""
    edge_cols = [
        "reg_elig", "elig_collect", "collect_assess",
        "assess_reqinfo", "reqinfo_collect", "assess_senior",
        "assess_to_decision",
    ]
    baseline = dfg_fragment.iloc[0][edge_cols].astype(float)
    baseline_sum = float(baseline.sum())
    assert baseline_sum > 0, "Baseline DFG has zero total weight"

    def l1_dist(row) -> float:
        diff = (row[edge_cols].astype(float) - baseline).abs().sum()
        return float(diff / baseline_sum)

    dfg_fragment["l1_dist_vs_baseline"] = dfg_fragment.apply(l1_dist, axis=1)
    return dfg_fragment


def compute_fitness_recovery(monthly: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Calculate raw-to-mapped fitness gap and recovery."""
    monthly["fitness_gap"] = monthly["mapped_model_fitness"] - monthly["raw_model_fitness"]
    # Simple diagnostic ratios
    monthly["fitness_recovery_ratio"] = (
        monthly["mapped_model_fitness"] / monthly["raw_model_fitness"].replace(0, np.nan)
    )
    return monthly


def flag_candidate_change_points(monthly: pd.DataFrame,
                                 dfg_fragment: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Flag candidate change points using simple, explainable rules:
    - large changes in logging-related metrics (fitness_gap, distinct labels, duplicates),
    - large shifts in performance (median/p90 cycle),
    - large shifts in structure (DFG L1 distance).
    """
    monthly = monthly.copy()
    dfg_fragment = dfg_fragment.copy()
    monthly = monthly.merge(dfg_fragment[["month", "l1_dist_vs_baseline"]], on="month", how="left")

    flags = []
    for idx, row in monthly.iterrows():
        reasons = []
        if idx > 0:
            prev = monthly.iloc[idx - 1]
            # Logging/semantic signals
            if (row["distinct_activity_labels"] - prev["distinct_activity_labels"]) >= 2:
                reasons.append("distinct_labels_jump")
            if (row["duplicate_open_case_pct"] - prev["duplicate_open_case_pct"]) >= 2:
                reasons.append("duplicate_open_case_jump")
            if (row["fitness_gap"] - prev["fitness_gap"]) >= 0.20:
                reasons.append("fitness_gap_increase")

            # Performance signals
            if (row["median_cycle_h"] - prev["median_cycle_h"]) >= 4.0:
                reasons.append("median_cycle_jump")
            if (row["p90_cycle_h"] - prev["p90_cycle_h"]) >= 10.0:
                reasons.append("p90_cycle_jump")

        # Structural (DFG) signals vs. January
        if row.get("l1_dist_vs_baseline", 0.0) >= 0.10:
            reasons.append("dfg_distance_high")

        flags.append(",".join(reasons))

    monthly["change_point_flags"] = flags
    return monthly

def format_event_log_df(df: pd.DataFrame,
                        case_id_col: str = "case:concept:name",
                        activity_col: str = "concept:name",
                        timestamp_col: str = "time:timestamp",
                        resource_col: str | None = "org:resource") -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Format a raw event-level dataframe using pm4py.format_dataframe(...).
    The function returns a dataframe suitable for subsequent PM4Py APIs.
    """
    params = {
        "case_id": case_id_col,
        "activity_key": activity_col,
        "timestamp_key": timestamp_col,
    }
    if resource_col is not None:
        params["resource_key"] = resource_col
    formatted = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, **params)
    return formatted


def slice_time_window(df: pd.DataFrame,
                      start: datetime,
                      end: datetime,
                      timestamp_col: str = "time:timestamp") -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Slice a time window using pm4py.filter_time_range(...).
    Assumes the dataframe has been formatted with the given timestamp column.
    """
    sliced = pm4py.filter_time_range(
        df,
        start_time=start,
        end_time=end,
        timestamp_key=timestamp_col,
        include_start=True,
        include_end=True,
    )
    return sliced


def compute_monthly_dfgs(df: pd.DataFrame,
                         case_id_col: str = "case:concept:name",
                         activity_col: str = "concept:name",
                         timestamp_col: str = "time:timestamp") -> dict:
    """
    Compute monthly DFGs using pm4py.discover_dfg(...).
    Returns a dict: {period (YYYY-MM): (dfg, start_activities, end_activities)}.
    """
    df = df.copy()
    df["month"] = df[timestamp_col].dt.to_period("M")

    monthly_dfgs: dict[str, tuple] = {}
    for month, sub_df in df.groupby("month"):
        # pm4py.discover_dfg can operate on dataframes formatted with the standard keys.
        dfg, start_acts, end_acts = pm4py.discover_dfg(
            sub_df,
            case_id_key=case_id_col,
            activity_key=activity_col,
            timestamp_key=timestamp_col,
        )
        monthly_dfgs[str(month)] = (dfg, start_acts, end_acts)
    return monthly_dfgs

## 9. Provisional attribution
The strongest conclusion is that **March’s conformance collapse is primarily due to semantic/logging drift from the system migration, while mid-to-late-year deterioration is driven by real performance and case-mix changes.** The sharp drop in raw fitness and rise in distinct labels and duplicate technical events, combined with near-baseline mapped fitness and minimal DFG distance, make the migration the dominant explanation for early drift with high confidence (≈0.9). A second strong conclusion is that **Supplier Z’s disruption from May materially increased rework and cycle times**, as evidenced by the simultaneous jump in supplier_z_pct, rework_variant_pct, and normalized rework-edge frequencies; confidence here is also high (≈0.85–0.9).

Moderately strong but more mediated is the attribution of **queueing-driven performance drift to staffing loss and rising load**. The fact that Assess service time barely changes while reconstructed waiting and p90_cycle_h climb suggests that capacity shortfall and volume growth, not slower work, explain much of the delay (confidence ≈0.7–0.8). The **priority rule and seasonal renewal effects** plausibly redistribute delay towards standard cases and concentrate Senior Assessment in high-complexity cases, but monthly aggregates cannot fully separate these from concurrent supplier and staffing pressures (confidence ≈0.6–0.7).

Several mechanisms remain only plausible. **Persistent behavioral adaptation** by assessors (e.g., more conservative use of `Request Info`) could explain residual mapped-fitness drift and structural changes even after Supplier Z and seasonality are controlled, but current aggregates cannot isolate behavior from mix and policy (confidence ≈0.5). **Measurement missingness and resource drift** clearly affect resource-level metrics but are unlikely to account for the magnitude and timing of DFG and cycle-time changes.

Attribution is tangled by mediation and interaction: supplier issues feed rework, which amplifies queueing under constrained staffing and seasonal volume, while the priority rule redistributes who waits. I would revise this account if event-level segmentation showed, for example, that standard, non-Supplier-Z cases outside renewal months experienced similar performance deterioration, or if normalized DFG distances remained near zero after May despite worsening performance, which would suggest more subtle timing or outcome drift rather than structural control-flow changes.

## 10. Monitoring decision rules
1. IF raw_model_fitness drops by ≥0.3 in a single month while mapped_model_fitness remains within 0.05 of its baseline and distinct_activity_labels increases, THEN trigger an immediate schema and label-mapping review plus replay-based conformance on the normalized log, BECAUSE this pattern is characteristic of logging/semantic drift rather than true process redesign.
2. IF supplier_z_pct increases by ≥15 percentage points over two months and rework_variant_pct or Assess→Request Info rates rise by ≥8 points in the same segments, THEN open a supplier-quality incident, sample cases for document-completeness review, and negotiate corrective actions, BECAUSE the joint shift strongly indicates supplier-driven rework and avoidable looping. 
3. IF median or p90 waiting time before `Assess` grows by ≥25% above the six-month baseline while Assess service times remain stable, THEN treat this as a queueing and capacity issue and adjust staffing, overtime, or scheduling, BECAUSE stable service time with longer waits is a signature of congestion, not slower work.
4. IF, after 2026-07, high-complexity priority cases maintain or improve cycle times but standard cases’ p90_cycle_h worsens by ≥20% relative to pre-rule baselines, THEN review the priority rule thresholds and potential side-effects on standard cases, BECAUSE this indicates redistribution of delay rather than overall efficiency gain.
5. IF Oct–Nov performance metrics (cycle times, rework, fitness) breach their historical renewal-season bands but non-seasonal months remain stable, THEN treat the issue as seasonal amplification and adjust seasonal staffing and supplier expectations, BECAUSE this pattern points to recurring load-sensitive drift rather than a permanent structural break.
6. IF missing_resource_pct for any critical activity doubles or exceeds 8% for two consecutive months, THEN investigate logging pipelines and identity mappings and temporarily de-emphasize resource-level performance dashboards, BECAUSE high missingness can bias team comparisons and obscure true workload and queueing patterns.
7. IF multiple monitors (DFG distance, rework share, queueing time, and SLA breach rate) signal sustained deterioration for at least two consecutive months in the primary monitoring population, THEN convene a cross-functional review to jointly analyze supplier, staffing, policy, and logging factors, BECAUSE concurrent, persistent multi-metric drift is unlikely to be noise and typically reflects interacting mechanisms rather than a single isolated issue.
