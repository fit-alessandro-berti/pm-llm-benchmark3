# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** `Submit` ($t=0$).  
**Horizon:** 72 hours (SLA threshold).  
**Event of interest:** `Resolve`.  
**Competing events:** `Withdraw`, `Reject` (internal terminal), `Transfer` (external terminal with unknown outcome).  
**Censoring:** `open` cases at observation cutoff (right-censored at `duration_or_age_h`).

**Estimand 1: Conditional SLA breach probability**  
$S_{\text{resolve}}(72) = P(T_{\text{resolve}} > 72 \mid T_{\text{resolve}} < \infty)$  
Interpretation: Among cases that *eventually* resolve, what fraction exceed the 72-hour SLA? This matches operational decisions around *process efficiency* and *resource allocation for active cases*.

**Estimand 2: Cumulative incidence of resolution by SLA**  
$\text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72) = P(T \le 72, \text{cause} = \text{resolve})$  
Tail risk = $1 - \text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72)$.  
Interpretation: Probability that a case both resolves and does so within the SLA window, accounting for competing exits. This matches *SLA compliance reporting* and *contractual risk assessment*, where withdrawal/rejection/transfer remove cases from the resolution denominator.

**Case contributions under each estimand:**
- **S03 (76h resolved):** Contributes to the tail risk in both estimands. In Estimand 1, it is a resolved case that breached. In Estimand 2, it contributes to resolution incidence at $t=76 > 72$, reducing $\text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72)$.
- **S09 (92h open):** Right-censored at 92h. Already past the SLA horizon, so it is a known breach of *time-to-disposition*, but resolution status remains unknown. Contributes to the risk set up to 92h; in Estimand 2, it reduces the estimated CIF because it hasn't resolved by 72h.
- **S06 (27h withdrawn):** Competing event at 27h. Removes from the resolution risk set at $t=27$. Does not contribute to resolution tail risk; correctly handled by competing-risk estimators.
- **S08 (68h transferred):** Censored/competing at 68h. Under non-informative censoring, it leaves the risk set at 68h. Under competing-risk treatment, it contributes to the transfer CIF. Under pessimistic bounds, it may be treated as a potential breach. Its contribution depends on the transfer assumption (see Section 2 & 6).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive approach that computes $\frac{\text{# resolved} > 72\text{h}}{\text{# resolved total}}$ suffers from multiple biases:

1. **Survivor bias:** Excludes `open`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, `transferred` cases. Fast-resolving cases may be systematically simpler, inflating apparent SLA compliance.
2. **Informative censoring:** `transferred` cases (e.g., S08, S21, S32) are often complex or stalled. Dropping them assumes they would have resolved at the same rate as retained cases, which is implausible.
3. **Competing risks:** `withdrawn` (S06, S12, S22, S30) and `rejected` (S05, S14, S23) remove cases from the resolution risk set. If withdrawal correlates with low resolution probability, ignoring it overestimates $\text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72)$.
4. **Cohort imbalance:** Cohort B introduces `AutoCheck` early in the path. Pooling A and B ignores structural process differences that affect hazard rates.
5. **Open cases >72h:** S09 (92h), S11 (81h), S25 (79h), S26 (104h), S32 (96h transferred) are already past the SLA horizon. A naive completed-case analysis either drops them or misclassifies them, severely underestimating tail risk.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

We compare three transfer-treatment assumptions alongside competing-risk handling for withdrawal/rejection:

1. **Transfer as non-informative right-censoring:** Cases leave the risk set at transfer time. Assumes transferred cases would have resolved at the same rate as retained cases. *Likely underestimates tail risk if transfers are systematically stalled.*
2. **Transfer as competing risk:** Standard Aalen-Johansen treatment. Removes from resolution risk set, contributes to transfer CIF. *Balanced assumption; matches internal process termination.*
3. **Transfer as pessimistic breach bound:** Treat all transfers as resolved at $t=72^+$ (or censored at 72h with event=1). *Upper bound on tail risk; useful for worst-case SLA planning.*

**Qualitative conclusions that could reverse:**  
- If Cohort B transfers are disproportionately P1 cases with long `last_event_gap_h`, treating transfers as censored (Scenario 1) may show Cohort B as having *lower* tail risk than A. Under Scenario 3, Cohort B's tail risk could exceed A's, reversing cohort performance rankings.
- Withdrawal/rejection treated as censoring vs competing risks shifts the denominator. If withdrawal correlates with low complexity, ignoring it as competing inflates resolution probability by ~5-10% in this sample.

# 7. Operational interpretation

- **Alerts:** Use landmarked CIF at $t=48$h. If $P(\text{resolve} > 72 \mid \text{state at 48h}) > 0.35$, trigger automated escalation or supervisor review. Thresholds should be calibrated on historical rolling windows.
- **Workload actions:** Multi-state hazard estimates identify bottleneck transitions (e.g., `Review  Escalate` delay). Reallocate capacity to stages with highest cause-specific hazard for resolution.
- **SLA governance:** Track $\text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72)$ monthly by priority and cohort. Report tail risk as $1 - \text{CIF}_{\text{resolve}}(72)$, not conditional survival, to align with contractual definitions.
- **Calibration & fairness:** Plot predicted breach probability vs empirical breach rate in deciles. Check that P1 cases do not exhibit systematically higher tail risk after adjusting for complexity/cohort. If P1 tail risk > P3, investigate policy rollout compliance and assignment mechanisms.
- **Causal caution:** Survival outputs are associative. Do not claim "escalation reduces breach risk" without addressing confounding by indication. Use these scores for triage and monitoring, not as proof of intervention efficacy.

def compute_cif_resolve(group, censor_mask):
    """Simplified CIF approximation for demonstration (Aalen-Johansen style)"""
    n = len(group)
    if n == 0: return np.nan
    times = group['time'].values
    events = group['event_resolve'].values
    competing = group['competing'].values
    censor = censor_mask.values
    
    # Sort by time
    order = np.argsort(times)
    times, events, competing, censor = times[order], events[order], competing[order], censor[order]
    
    # Risk set and hazard calculation
    h_resolve = np.zeros_like(times, dtype=float)
    risk_set = n
    cif = 0.0
    for i in range(n):

        if events[i] == 1:
            h_resolve[i] = 1 / risk_set
            cif += (1 - cif) * h_resolve[i]
        if events[i] == 1 or competing[i] == 1 or censor[i]:
            risk_set -= 1
    return cif

# 9. Limitations and additional data

- **Exact event timestamps:** Only total duration and final gap are provided. Precise hazard estimation, multi-state transition rates, and time-dependent covariate modeling require per-activity timestamps.
- **External outcomes after transfer:** Unknown resolution status post-transfer prevents validation of transfer assumptions. Partner system data or follow-up audits are needed.
- **Cutoff/start dates:** `duration_or_age_h` lacks absolute dates, preventing seasonal/workload adjustments and exact age-at-cutoff calculations.
- **Time-varying workload/capacity:** Queue lengths, agent availability, and daily volume strongly influence process duration but are unobserved.
- **Assignment mechanism & policy compliance:** Cohort B assignment was non-randomized with incomplete rollout. Propensity scoring or instrumental variables would be needed for causal cohort comparisons.
- **Case complexity features:** Clinical/administrative complexity, document completeness, and customer responsiveness are missing but likely confound priority/cohort effects.
- **Impact:** These gaps limit precise hazard modeling, causal inference, and dynamic resource planning. Survival estimates should be treated as associative bounds until granular event logs and external follow-ups are integrated.
