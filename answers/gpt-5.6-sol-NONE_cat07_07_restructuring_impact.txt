# 1. Mechanism map

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech into Technical Shared Service | Replaces embedded specialist relationships with hub-and-spoke routing | Fewer distinct local caseworkerlocal-tech edges; stronger edges to/from shared resources; greater network centralization | More consistent routing and workload pooling | Nominal cross-team handovers fall, but specialist queue entries rise from 36 to 41 and return handovers from 31 to 36 per 100 cases |

The principal structural trade-off is therefore **pooling versus coordination**. Pooling can relieve East and smooth specialist demand, but it adds formal queue boundaries and concentrates operational risk. A reduction in named local-to-local transfers is not necessarily a reduction in meaningful coordination load.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Routing rules are accurate, advice is complete on first return, and explicit cross-team transfers fall to about 3 per 100 cases. Shared-service edges replace inconsistent local and absence-cover edges without creating additional clarification loops.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Technical wait remains close to the estimated 4.0 hours and approval near 3.0 hours. Pooling removes East’s local overload, while capacity planning prevents legal/risk demand from crowding out technical work.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median cycle time improves, especially in East. The 90th percentile is stable or improves because lower East tails and fewer misrouted cases outweigh added North/South queue time. Variance across teams narrows.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standard advice and approval reduce inconsistent decisions. Rework after shared-service handovers is no worse than current local loops, and difficult Quality Check catches errors without creating repeated QA cycles.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Each case retains a named local owner; the shared service owns advice quality and deadlines; approvers record reason codes. Reassignment declines despite broader resource pooling.
- **Fairness implications:** East and customers previously dependent on scarce local availability gain access. Segment-specific outcomes remain comparable after controlling for complexity and specialty. Accommodation needs are preserved in routing.
- **Early warning signs:** None beyond short-lived transition effects. Queue age stabilizes, first-time-complete advice remains high, specialist utilization rises without sustained backlog, and North/South rework does not increase.

## Central scenario

- **Handover pattern:** Visible cross-team transfers fall, but hub-and-spoke exchanges increase. Technical referrals rise to around 41 per 100 cases, returns to 36, approvals to 49, and legal/risk referrals to 12. Some cases require clarification or reassignment, offsetting part of the nominal simplification.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Median waits approximate management estimates, but weekend, absence, and demand-peak waits increase. East improves from its overloaded baseline; North and South experience modestly slower access than with embedded specialists. Legal/risk remains the tightest constraint.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Overall median is stable or modestly better because simple cases and East improve. The 90th percentile is flat or somewhat worse, especially for high-complexity and multi-specialty cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Decision consistency improves, while context-related rework rises. Aggregate rework may be stable because these effects offset one another. Rework becomes concentrated in complex cases, regional exceptions, and cases requiring repeated customer contact.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Formal roles become clearer at the activity level but weaker at the case level. Staff understand who provides advice, yet ownership of follow-up, incomplete advice, and exceptions remains occasionally disputed.
- **Fairness implications:** East gains more than North/South. Customers with common, well-recorded needs benefit from standardization; customers needing language, regional, or nonstandard accommodation face additional contacts and delay.
- **Early warning signs:** Rising queue-age variance, increasing resources per case, more Specialist ReviewCustomer Contact loops, declining first-time-complete advice, and widening North/South-versus-East trends.

## Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern:** The shared service becomes a high-centrality hub with repeated referrals, returns, reassignment, and quality loops. Formal cross-team transfers decline, but total effective handovers and resources per case rise.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Specialist utilization approaches saturation. Legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Check crowd out technical work. Median waits exceed estimates and the upper tail grows rapidly; backlogs persist across days rather than clearing between peaks.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Simple cases may still improve enough to hold down the overall average, while high-complexity, high-value, and specialty cases deteriorate substantially. The 90th/95th percentiles and SLA misses rise. East’s expected benefit may disappear if central congestion exceeds its former local constraint.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Loss of informal advice causes over-referral and preventable errors. Advice lacking regional context generates customer recontact, reassessment, and QA rework. Staff may bypass queues, reducing log completeness and control.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases circulate between local owners, specialists, QA, and shared approvers. Reassignment counts and idle gaps rise; staff disagree over who must resolve incomplete or conflicting advice.
- **Fairness implications:** Complex customers, small segments, language-accommodation groups, and regions with distinctive conventions bear disproportionate delays. Priority overrides may accelerate visible or high-value cases while displacing ordinary cases.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue inflow persistently exceeds completions; utilization remains above roughly 85–90% without buffer; oldest-case age rises; legal/risk waits exceed the current 9.6 hours; specialist and approval SLA breaches cluster; local escalation rates rise; staff report shadow work or informal workarounds.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** Fast simple cases can reduce the mean while the 90th or 95th percentile worsens. High utilization often affects queue tails before it materially shifts the average.

2. **High-complexity deterioration:** All high-complexity work enters the same service. Its delay can rise substantially even if automated or simple cases improve. Complexity-specific distributions are essential.

3. **Local knowledge loss:** North and South currently use stable local loops and regional conventions. More standardized processing can appear faster while generating poorer advice, avoidable customer contact, or later rework not immediately reflected in cycle time.

4. **Unequal team effects:** East may benefit from pooled capacity because E_Tech is at 82% utilization and East has the slowest current performance. North and South may lose a working local arrangement. The aggregate can conceal winners and losers.

5. **Customer-segment fairness:** Customers requiring language, regional, accessibility, or nonstandard accommodation may need additional handovers. A stable overall mean can coexist with systematically worse waits, rework, or outcomes for a small segment.

6. **Learning and deskilling:** Routing all hard work away from local teams reduces opportunities to build technical judgment. Early throughput may improve while long-run escalation dependence and recovery risk increase.

7. **Accountability gaps:** Shared queues can reduce time recorded as active work while increasing ambiguity over who follows up. Cases may sit between activities, be reassigned, or receive technically complete but operationally unusable advice.

8. **Exception-handling fragility:** Standard routing may work well for common variants but fail on multi-specialty, high-value, or unusual regional cases. These low-frequency variants can have extreme delays without materially changing the average.

9. **Common-mode resilience risk:** Pooling improves cover for the absence of one local specialist but makes all teams dependent on one queue, routing mechanism, and management structure. A shared-service outage affects the whole organization.

10. **Hidden work and logging gaps:** Local staff may seek unofficial advice in messaging or batch administrative updates. Recorded cycle time can improve while actual effort, coordination, and waiting remain invisible.

11. **Priority displacement:** Priority cases can meet targets by repeatedly overtaking normal work. The average may remain stable while ordinary customers accumulate long waits.

12. **Survivorship and open-case bias:** Early post-change reporting based only on completed cases excludes the cases still trapped in the new queue. Open-case age and cohort analysis are therefore necessary.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope

Use a staged pilot rather than moving all specialist, legal/risk, difficult QA, and approval work simultaneously.

- Pilot the Technical Shared Service first for a defined subset, while maintaining separate capacity and ownership for Legal/Risk.
- Include **East plus a randomized or rule-based sample from North and South**. East tests the capacity-pooling benefit; North/South test the cost of losing effective embedded expertise.
- Initially route medium/high-complexity technical cases centrally. Preserve a local consultation channel and log it explicitly.
- Introduce shared approval in a later phase or for a separate sample so technical-centralization and approval effects can be distinguished.
- Assign a named end-to-end local case owner and a named shared-service owner for each referral.

## Comparison group

Use contemporaneous matched controls:

- Non-pilot cases within each team, matched on priority, complexity, specialty, customer segment, location, and arrival week.
- If operationally possible, randomize eligible cases within strata.
- Otherwise use a phased stepped-wedge rollout and difference-in-differences analysis, checking pre-change parallel trends.
- Avoid comparing East pilot cases only with North/South controls because their baselines differ materially.

## Observation windows

- **Baseline:** At least 12 weeks before pilot; preferably 16–26 weeks to capture seasonality and absences.
- **Stabilization:** First 2–4 weeks reported separately as transition.
- **Pilot evaluation:** At least 12 weeks after stabilization.
- **Learning/resilience follow-up:** 6 months or more to observe deskilling, repeat demand peaks, absence cover, and outcome accumulation.
- Track arrival cohorts, including open cases, rather than relying only on completion-period averages.

## Required segmentation

Every principal metric should be segmented by:

- Team: North, South, East
- Complexity: low, medium, high
- Specialty: technical, legal, risk, security, multiple/none
- Customer segment and location
- Priority and, where available, language/accommodation need
- Pilot/control status and arrival cohort

Report absolute differences, relative differences, sample sizes, and uncertainty. Do not treat small segments as satisfactory merely because results are statistically inconclusive.

## Guardrail metrics

- 90th/95th percentile specialist and approval waiting time
- Oldest open case and backlog age distribution
- SLA miss rate overall and by segment
- High-complexity cycle-time tail
- Rework after Specialist Review and difficult Quality Check
- Reassignment count and distinct resources per case
- Repeat specialist/contact loops
- Legal/Risk waiting time and utilization
- First-time-complete advice
- North/South deterioration versus baseline
- Customer-segment disparity in waits, outcomes, and rework
- Local technical exposure and escalation rates
- Staff overtime, absence sensitivity, and unlogged workaround rate

## Staff feedback collection

- Weekly short surveys for local caseworkers, specialists, approvers, and supervisors.
- Structured questions on ownership, advice completeness, routing accuracy, regional context, workload, and shadow work.
- Biweekly review of a stratified sample of delayed, reworked, and exception cases.
- Focus groups after stabilization, separated by team and role to avoid hierarchy effects.
- Add structured reason codes for referral, reassignment, returned advice, override, and handover reason to the event log.

## Rollback or adjustment triggers

Pre-register thresholds based on baseline variation. Example triggers include:

- High-complexity 90th-percentile cycle time rises more than 15% for two consecutive reporting periods.
- Shared-service 90th-percentile wait exceeds the pilot target by more than 25%.
- SLA misses rise by more than 2 percentage points overall or materially in any team/segment.
- Rework rises by more than 2 percentage points or by 20% relative to baseline.
- Backlog grows for three consecutive weeks or oldest-case age breaches a fixed limit.
- Legal/Risk wait materially exceeds its already-high baseline of 9.6 hours.
- North or South deteriorates without a compensating organization-wide benefit.
- A protected/accommodation segment shows persistent disparity after case-mix adjustment.
- Staff report sustained ownership ambiguity or material shadow processing.

Responses can include restoring local consultation capacity, separating QA from the technical queue, reserving legal/risk capacity, revising routing thresholds, adding surge capacity, or pausing the next rollout stage.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first with modifications; do not approve an immediate full rollout.**

The restructuring has a credible upside: pooling may particularly help East, standardize technical and approval decisions, reduce poor ad hoc absence cover, and make specialist advice more auditable. However, management’s own routing estimates do not demonstrate an unambiguous reduction in effective handovers. They instead show more formal technical referrals, more shared approvals, and more legal/risk demand. Placing technical work, legal/risk triage, and difficult Quality Check in one service also concentrates several demand streams around resources already near capacity.

Proceed conditionally if the pilot shows:

- no material deterioration in high-complexity and tail performance;
- sustainable queue age rather than utilization alone;
- stable or improved rework and outcome quality;
- an actual benefit for East without disproportionate loss in North/South;
- no material customer-segment disparity;
- clear end-to-end case ownership;
- preserved local learning through consultation, rotation, or co-working;
- sufficient capacity buffer and separation of legal/risk from avoidable competing work.

Management should **not infer success from an early decrease in mean or median cycle time**. Early completed cases will overrepresent simple work, while difficult cases remain open. Nor should higher specialist utilization automatically be interpreted as productivity: near-saturation utilization can indicate insufficient resilience and predict nonlinear queue growth. Decisions should rely on cohort-based tails, open-case backlog, quality, fairness, role clarity, and capability trends before expansion.

def case_level_table(log, sla_days_by_priority):
    ordered = log.sort_values([CASE, TS])

    cases = (
        ordered.groupby(CASE)
        .agg(
            start=(TS, "min"),
            end=(TS, "max"),
            team=("team", "first"),
            location=("location", "first"),
            complexity=("case_complexity", "first"),
            specialty=("required_specialty", "first"),
            priority=("priority", "first"),

        (cases["end"] - cases["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    )
    cases["sla_days"] = cases["priority"].map(sla_days_by_priority)
    cases["sla_miss"] = cases["cycle_days"] > cases["sla_days"]
    return cases

# Handover counts per 100 cases and resources per case
def handover_metrics(log):
    x = log.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    x["prev_resource"] = x.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift()
    x["handover"] = (
        x["prev_resource"].notna() &
        x[RES].notna() &
        (x["prev_resource"] != x[RES])
    )
    n_cases = x[CASE].nunique()

    return {
        "cases": n_cases,
        "handovers_per_100_cases": 100 * x["handover"].sum() / n_cases,
        "median_resources_per_case":
            x.groupby(CASE)[RES].nunique().median(),
        "p90_resources_per_case":
            x.groupby(CASE)[RES].nunique().quantile(.90)
    }
