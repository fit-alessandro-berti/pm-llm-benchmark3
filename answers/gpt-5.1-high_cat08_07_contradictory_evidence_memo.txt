## 1. Executive memo

Redesign R has delivered real benefits on speed, handovers, and visible cost, but at the price of worse tails, more rework, more complaints, and emerging compliance-like risks around activation and permits. The evidence is heterogeneous across segments and based on imperfect data and a low-precision pilot model, so we should neither scale blindly nor discard the improvements that clearly work. I recommend **a targeted hybrid deployment (Alternative E) with guard-rails, plus a focused measurement program before any broader rollout**.

Specifically:
- **Immediately keep R for simple, low-risk houses**, where median duration, tail, first-time-right, and complaints all improved.
- **For apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business and rural segments, roll back the risky parts of R**: suspend survey skipping, require permit checks, and forbid billing start before a successful activation test except for tightly controlled exceptions.
- **Retain coordinator ownership in all regions, but de-risk it**: cap coordinator utilization, add backup ownership rules for absence, and give activation specialists clearer pre-billing authority.

This direction preserves the speed and accountability gains where they are proven (simple houses), while reducing exposure where the pilot shows clear harm (apartments, old buildings, business customers) and where process-mining diagnostics reveal systematic misalignments (billing before activation, missing permit checks, repeated visits without surveys). It also acknowledges that our current cost view is incomplete: visible cost per case fell (EUR 184 → 161) but estimated full cost (including complaints and revisits) worsened and is based on truncated complaint data.

Process mining shows the pilot model has **high fitness but low precision**: many cases follow the intended simplified coordinator path, yet the model also admits too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visits, and early billing. Alignment diagnostics and temporal deviations highlight new failure modes: a longer “installation → activation” delay for apartments, frequent “billing before activation”, more failed visits and second visits, and a new long-tail relation from billing start to complaint escalation. Social-network changes show fewer handovers but greater centralization on coordinators and activation specialists, with 93% coordinator utilization and stalls when they are absent—suggesting reduced resilience despite simpler flows.

Given this, the next 6–9 months should focus on **(1) targeted scaling with guard-rails, (2) structured experiments to validate the risky rules, and (3) better measurement of downstream cost and customer experience**. Only once we can show, segment by segment, that R does not worsen tails, first-time-right, complaints, or full cost should we consider national scaling beyond simple houses. Until then, the company should treat R as a promising but incomplete design that needs tightening, not as a finished template.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision**: In the pilot, many cases follow the intended simplified coordinator path, so the discovered model can replay most observed behavior (high fitness). However, to accommodate a mix of skipped surveys, manual activations, repeated visits, missing permit checks, and early billing, the model has to allow many alternative paths, including ones rarely or undesirably executed. This broad allowance lowers precision: the model accepts more behavior than is actually normative, matching the reported fitness 0.94 but precision 0.51.

- **Improved median but worse tail duration**: Median performance improves mainly because simple low-risk houses—34% of pilot cases—benefit from merged validate/schedule steps, fewer handovers, and no unnecessary surveys, shortening the typical path. At the same time, apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, and other harder segments experience more failed visits, second visits, and post-install complaints, especially when surveys are skipped or permits missed. These problematic cases accumulate in the tail, driving p90 and p95 durations up even as the median falls.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience**: Coordinator ownership and task merging reduce handovers dramatically (cases with 3+ handovers fall from 34% to 12%), simplifying the formal flow. Yet the working-together network becomes more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists, with coordinator utilization at 93% and evidence of stalled cases when a coordinator is absent. Fewer formal handovers mean less shared context and fewer parallel actors who can step in, so when the central role is overloaded or unavailable, the process becomes brittle rather than resilient.

- **Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost**: Skipping surveys, compressing steps, and moving some activation checks after billing reduce early labor and survey costs, yielding lower mean cost before complaint handling (EUR 184 → 161). However, these shortcuts increase failed visits, second visits, manual activations, and complaints (including billing disputes), which show up as rework and downstream service cost. Because complaint-handling costs after week 4 are missing, the estimated full cost (198 → 207) likely underestimates the true downstream burden, reconciling lower visible cost with higher total cost.

- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings**: The same redesign levers have very different effects by segment. For simple, low-risk houses, survey skipping and merged validate/schedule steps are mostly correct bets, improving median and tail times, first-time-right, and complaints. For apartments and old, permit-sensitive buildings, the predictive rule misclassifies risk: skipping surveys and omitting permit checks lead to more failed and repeat visits, longer “installation → activation” intervals, and more complaints. Thus, averaged metrics look mixed, but segment-level metrics clearly diverge.


## 4. Decision alternatives

- **A. Scale Redesign R nationally**  
  - *Advantages*: Captures median speed gains, handover reduction, and lower visible cost across the footprint; standardizes around coordinator ownership and simplified flows.  
  - *Risks*: Exposes all regions and segments to worse tails, more rework, more complaints, and potential non-compliance with activation and permit expectations. Low-precision model and incomplete cost data mean risks may be underestimated.  
  - *Reasonable only if*: Guard-rails are added first (survey rules tuned, mandatory permit checks for sensitive buildings, strict “activation before billing” policy except for defined exceptions), and evidence shows no deterioration in tails, first-time-right, complaints, or full cost by segment. Under current evidence, this is **not recommended**.

- **B. Roll back to the baseline process**  
  - *Advantages*: Reduces exposure to the observed harms in higher-risk segments and restores a more precise, more controlled process model; removes emerging billing/activation and permit misalignment patterns.  
  - *Risks*: Sacrifices proven gains for simple low-risk houses (faster cycle, better first-time-right and complaints) and loses the organizational learning from coordinator ownership. May send a signal that experimentation is discouraged.  
  - *Reasonable if*: Hybrid controls and targeted deployment fail to contain tails and complaints, or compliance/regulator feedback requires immediate full rollback. Given current segment-level benefits, a total rollback is **too blunt** as a first choice.

- **C. Scale only coordinator ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**  
  - *Advantages*: Preserves accountability and handover reduction while removing the riskiest rule changes (skip survey, activation after billing), likely mitigating tails, rework, and complaint escalation. Simplifies the process model and improves precision.  
  - *Risks*: Coordinator utilization and network centralization still threaten resilience; without additional capacity or backup ownership, the process may remain fragile. Some potential cycle-time gains from well-targeted survey skipping are also lost.  
  - *Reasonable if*: The company prioritizes stability and quality but still wants structural accountability. This is close to the recommended direction but still too coarse because it ignores segment-level differences where survey skipping is safe.

- **D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**  
  - *Advantages*: Produces cleaner causal evidence on survey skipping, permit checks, and activation timing; can address current data gaps (segment attributes, downstream cost, start timestamps). Supports better tuning of predictive rules and guard-rails.  
  - *Risks*: Slower realization of benefits; may be seen as “analysis paralysis” if not combined with practical improvements where evidence is already clear. Requires experimental design capability and operational discipline.  
  - *Reasonable if*: Conducted in parallel with targeted deployment (e.g., in a subset of non-simple segments), rather than as the only action. On its own, D underuses the strong evidence we already have for simple houses.

- **E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and run measurement before wider scale**  
  - *Advantages*: Aligns deployment scope with demonstrated benefits; shields high-risk segments from known harms; allows continued learning via experiments and conformance monitoring; balances speed, quality, and risk. It also uses process-mining diagnostics (segment outcomes, alignments, temporal profiles, social networks) to target interventions.  
  - *Risks*: Operational complexity of running differentiated rules by segment; risk of misclassification if segment data remain incomplete; requires strong governance and monitoring to avoid silent scope creep.  
  - *Reasonable if*: Management is willing to invest in better measurement and governance. Under current evidence and constraints, **E is the most defensible path**.


## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranking by decision value (highest first):

1. **M4 – Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: True total economics of R, including complaints, revisits, billing disputes, and potential regulatory costs.  
   - *Decision impact*: Could show that apparent cost savings are illusory, justifying tighter controls or rollback of risky elements; alternatively, it could confirm that tails and rework are affordable in some segments, supporting broader rollout with guard-rails.

2. **M6 – Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: Whether the rule that skips surveys is accurate by building type and segment, especially for apartments and old buildings.  
   - *Decision impact*: If the rule is weak, survey skipping should be heavily restricted or redesigned; if strong in certain strata, R can be safely expanded there, enabling more precise hybrid deployment.

3. **M1 – Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: Causal effect of survey skipping on first-time-right, tails, complaints, and cost within each risk stratum.  
   - *Decision impact*: Provides robust evidence on where skipping is acceptable vs harmful, informing whether to widen or shrink R’s scope beyond simple houses.

4. **M7 – Conformance monitoring for Billing start before Activation test and Permit check absence**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: Scale and persistence of the most concerning misalignments (early billing, missing permits) and whether controls are effective.  
   - *Decision impact*: If misalignments remain frequent despite guard-rails, this would argue against scaling R and might require reverting certain design elements; if they fall to rare exceptions, it supports broader adoption with confidence.

5. **M8 – Collect customer experience separately for “speed to appointment” and “working service after installation”**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: Trade-off between faster appointments and the quality of the working service, currently only visible via aggregate NPS.  
   - *Decision impact*: If customers value working service much more than speed, management should favor quality-focused variants (tighter activation and survey controls); if both dimensions improve for some segments, that supports scaling there.

6. **M5 – Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership under coordinator absence**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: How much centralization harms resilience when coordinators are overloaded or absent.  
   - *Decision impact*: If shared ownership performs better under shocks with modest cost to handovers, management might adjust the coordinator model (e.g., backup coordinators or pooled ownership) before scaling.

7. **M3 – Add structured reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, and Second visit**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: Root causes behind rework and complaints (e.g., wrong address data, technical issues, missing surveys, scheduling problems).  
   - *Decision impact*: Enables more targeted redesign of the riskiest parts of R (e.g., activation rules, survey decisions), but is slightly lower priority than directly quantifying cost and rule accuracy.

8. **M2 – Instrument start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, and Manual activation**  
   - *Uncertainty resolved*: More precise activity-level durations and waiting times, beyond completion timestamps.  
   - *Decision impact*: Improves diagnostics and capacity planning, but is less directly tied to the core go/no-go decision on scaling R than cost, rule accuracy, and conformance; thus it ranks last, though still useful.


## 6. Final recommendation

- **Immediate action**: Adopt **Alternative E**. Keep Redesign R for simple, low-risk houses where it clearly improves speed and quality; in all other segments, retain coordinator ownership but revert to baseline rules for surveys, permit checks, and activation ordering (activation test before billing), with clear ownership for activation specialists. Add limits and backups to reduce coordinator overload and absence-related stalls.
- **What to pause**: Pause any further national rollout of survey skipping and post-billing activation beyond simple houses. Suspend the use of the current predictive rule to skip surveys for apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and rural long-distance installs until validated.
- **What to scale**: Scale the **coordinator-ownership model with guard-rails** (capacity caps, backup ownership, clearer activation responsibility) and the simplified validate/schedule step for simple houses nationally. Use process-mining conformance monitoring to ensure that billing-before-activation and missing-permit patterns decline sharply.
- **What to measure next**: Prioritize M4 and M6, followed by M1 and M7, with M8 and M5 as supporting studies and M3/M2 as enablers. Integrate these into a 6–9 month measurement plan with segment-level dashboards for tails, first-time-right, complaints, SLA breaches, and full cost.
- **Threshold for broader rollout**: Consider expanding R beyond simple houses only when, for each additional segment, (a) p90 duration is no worse than baseline and p95 is not materially worse, (b) first-time-right is within ~2 percentage points of baseline or better, (c) complaint rate and SLA breaches are not higher than baseline by more than ~1 percentage point, (d) full 60-day cost per case (including rework and complaints) is at least cost-neutral, and (e) conformance monitoring shows rare and controlled instances of billing before activation and missing permit checks. Only under those conditions is broader national scaling defensible.
