## 1. What is observed
Automation was made available at Alpha and Beta from 2026-04 and at Gamma from 2026-07, while Delta and Epsilon remained manual through 2026-08. Availability (`automation=1`) is distinct from actual use: the fraction of cases finishing via the automated fast path (`automated_fast_path_pct`) starts around 4–6% everywhere, then rises sharply in adopter branches after go-live (e.g., Alpha from 4% pre to mid‑40s% later, Gamma from 4% to around 30%), while non-adopters stay near 4%. Rollout was explicitly non-random: leadership selected high-volume, historically weaker branches (Alpha, Beta) first, with Gamma added later after a pilot; Delta and Epsilon are better-performing comparators. Several contemporaneous operational changes affect demand and capacity: a centralized fraud desk begins in March; a routing campaign shifts 10% more volume to Alpha/Beta and later 5% more to Gamma; FTE staffing changes by branch (e.g., Alpha loses two FTE in May, Beta gains one in April, Gamma loses one in June); and a May product change increases the share of high-complexity work. Outcomes are monthly branch aggregates: cycle-time medians and p90s, rework and escalation percentages, and mean CSAT from surveys with unknown branch-month response counts. There is no micro-level event data, queue length information, or fraud-desk waiting-time series, and only eight months of observations, limiting precision and trend assessment.

Interference arises because cross-branch routing and the centralized fraud desk link branches; treatment versions differ by intensity and local work practices, so E1–E7 average over heterogeneous implementations rather than a single “automation” effect.

## 3. Identification strategy

### Primary strategy: staggered-adoption event-study with intensity adjustment

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S1-event-study | Relative-month indicators for `automation` onset per branch, with separate post periods and optional interaction with automated-fast-path intensity | Never-treated branches (Delta, Epsilon) and not-yet-treated periods of Gamma as controls; optionally down-weighting early Alpha/Beta months with large staffing shocks | Branch and calendar-month fixed effects; `high_complexity_pct`; arrivals-per-FTE; indicators for months after product change and routing campaign | E1, E3, E4, E6, E7 (ITT-style and dynamic effects of availability on medians, tails, rework, escalation, and CSAT) | In the absence of automation, adopters and controls would have followed parallel relative trends conditional on controls; no major unmeasured time-varying shocks differentially aligned with adoption; spillovers via routing and fraud desk are modest or captured by controls; treatment-intensity differences either modeled or not strongly outcome-relevant. | Visual inspection of pre-trends in event-study plots; placebo “pseudo-adoption” dates for never-treated branches; sensitivity to excluding individual branches, to alternative pre/post windows, and to adding/removing covariates; robustness across outcomes. | With only five branches and multiple concurrent shocks (staffing, complexity, routing), parallel-trend assumptions are weak; spillovers and targeted selection into early adoption can bias ITT estimates, especially for tails. |

### Alternative strategy 2: branch-level interrupted time series with synthetic controls

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S3-ITS-synth | For each adopter (especially Gamma), model a level/slope break at adoption month, constructing a synthetic control from weighted combinations of Delta/Epsilon (and pre-adoption Gamma) | Synthetic control built from never-treated branches and earlier months, matched on pre-adoption outcome trajectories and complexity | `high_complexity_pct`, arrivals-per-FTE, and FTE levels included in matching or regression; allow different pre-trends via synthetic weights | Branch-specific causal effect trajectories on medians, p90s, and rework (E1, E3, E4, E7 at branch level) | Post-adoption outcomes for each adopter would have followed its synthetic-control trajectory absent automation; no unmodeled shocks at adoption unique to treated branch; spillovers are negligible or symmetric. | Check pre-period fit between adopter and synthetic control; placebo adoption dates in controls; leave-one-branch-out sensitivity of synthetic weights; compare ITS break sizes with those from S1. | Extremely short pre-adoption series (especially for Gamma) limits synthetic-control quality; overlapping system-wide shocks (product change, routing) make it hard to attribute deviations from synthetic to automation alone. |

S1 is preferable because it uses the full panel structure, explicitly represents staggered timing, and can incorporate both binary availability and treatment intensity. It allows transparent visualization of pre-trends and dynamic effects by relative month, and it naturally accommodates never-treated branches as controls while including Gamma as both control (before July) and treated (after July). Compared with aggregated-window DiD, S1 spreads information across all months, reducing sensitivity to any single shock (e.g., May’s complexity increase) and permitting covariate adjustment at the month level rather than collapsing everything into two numbers. Relative-time indicators let us detect anticipation and learning curves, which are plausible given gradual adoption of the fast path. While the small number of branches still makes statistical inference fragile and identification assumptions remain strong, S1 provides the clearest framework for articulating those assumptions, checking them graphically, and layering in intensity-based analyses, whereas S2 and S3 rely even more heavily on unverifiable functional-form and extrapolation choices in an already tiny panel.

## 5. Quantitative evidence

### 5.1 Branch-specific pre/post changes for adopters (descriptive arithmetic)

Define pre and post windows as follows: for Alpha and Beta, pre = 2026-01–03 and post = 2026-06–08 (three months before adoption vs three relatively stabilized post months, skipping the immediate April–May transition and product-change shock month); for Gamma, pre = 2026-04–06 and post = 2026-07–08 (closest pre-period to its July adoption and its two available post months).

Approximate window averages (arithmetic only, not causal effects):

| branch | pre window | post window | pre automated_fast_path_pct | post automated_fast_path_pct | pre median_cycle_h | post median_cycle_h | pre p90_cycle_h | post p90_cycle_h | pre rework_pct | post rework_pct | pre escalation_pct | post escalation_pct | pre csat_1to5 | post csat_1to5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha | Jan–Mar | Jun–Aug | ≈4% | ≈44% | ≈48.9 h | ≈48.2 h | ≈98.5 h | ≈110.2 h | ≈13.2% | ≈14.5% | ≈8.5% | 11.6% | ≈3.85 | ≈3.87 |
| Beta | Jan–Mar | Jun–Aug | ≈6% | ≈46% | ≈45.9 h | ≈47.5 h | ≈92.9 h | ≈103.8 h | ≈12.8% | ≈12.7% | ≈8.2% | 10.8% | ≈3.95 | ≈3.94 |
| Gamma | Apr–Jun | Jul–Aug | 4% | ≈28.5% | ≈43.7 h | ≈37.2 h | ≈94.2 h | ≈84.9 h | ≈13.8% | 12.6% | ≈9.5% | 10.7% | ≈3.95 | ≈4.09 |

These arithmetic contrasts show that Automation availability plus increased fast-path use is associated with: (i) fairly flat medians at Alpha/Beta with worsening tails and higher escalation; and (ii) noticeably better medians and tails at Gamma with a modest CSAT improvement.

### 5.2 Comparisons to never-treated branches (descriptive contrasts)

Using the same Jan–Mar vs Jun–Aug windows for non-adopters:

- Delta’s median cycle rises from ≈34.5 h to ≈40.4 h (≈+6 h), and p90 from ≈71.8 h to ≈90.7 h (≈+19 h), with rework increasing from ≈11.7% to ≈13.9% and CSAT drifting slightly down, then back up around 4.0.  
- Epsilon’s median cycle increases from ≈40.3 h to ≈45.0 h (≈+4.7 h), and p90 from ≈82.6 h to ≈99.3 h (≈+16.7 h), with rework rising from ≈13.5% to ≈16.2% and CSAT dipping to ≈3.75 in June before partially recovering.

Compared with these controls, Alpha and Beta see medians that are roughly stable rather than worsening by 5–6 h, and tails that increase by roughly 10–12 h, somewhat less than Delta/Epsilon’s ≈17–19 h. Gamma’s post-adoption medians and tails actually improve relative to its closer pre-period, while non-adopters see deterioration. These are descriptive differences; they are consistent with automation partially offsetting system-wide pressure (higher complexity and arrivals) but cannot by themselves distinguish automation from other concurrent branch-specific changes.

### 5.3 Treatment-intensity patterns

Across all months, non-adopters remain at ~4% automated fast-path share with gradually worsening medians and tails after May. Among adopters:

- Alpha: average median is ≈48.9 h when `automated_fast_path_pct` is 4% (Jan–Mar) and ≈48.5 h when it is ≥25% (Apr–Aug), while p90 increases from ≈98.5 h to ≈108–112 h; rework and escalation both rise.  
- Beta: median increases from ≈45.9 h at 6% fast-path share to ≈47.8 h when share is ≥27%, with p90 up by ≈11 h and escalation increasing from ≈8.2% to ≈10.8%, while rework is flat.  
- Gamma: when fast-path share is 4% (Apr–Jun), median and p90 average ≈43.7 h and ≈94.2 h; when share rises to 25–32% (Jul–Aug), they drop to ≈37.2 h and ≈84.9 h, with rework falling back toward ~12.6%.

Thus, higher intensity is strongly associated with better performance at Gamma but not at Alpha/Beta. These intensity correlations are descriptive and confounded by branch, complexity, and staffing differences.

### 5.4 Pre-trends and regression to the mean

Over January–March, adopters and non-adopters already differ: Alpha and Beta start with somewhat worse medians and tails than Delta but not dramatically worse than Epsilon, consistent with historical underperformance but not an extreme outlier status in this eight-month window. All branches show some upward drift in medians and p90s around May, coinciding with higher `high_complexity_pct` and the product change, suggesting common shocks. Because automation is targeted to weaker, higher-volume branches, some of the post-period stabilization at adopters could be regression to the mean rather than a true treatment effect; conversely, if routing shifts extra load to adopters, naive pre/post contrasts might understate benefits. With only three genuine pre months for Alpha/Beta and two for Gamma (relative to its adoption), standard pre-trend tests would have very low power, and any apparent deviations from parallel trends could be noise or unmeasured shocks.

### 5.5 Sensitivity and bounded conclusions

For median cycle time, a simple DiD-style contrast comparing adopter vs non-adopter changes (using the windows above) yields roughly: adopters’ medians change by near 0–+1.5 h on average, while controls worsen by ≈+5–6 h. Interpreted causally under parallel-trend and no-time-varying-confounding assumptions, this would suggest automation reduces median cycle time by on the order of 5–10% relative to what would otherwise have occurred. However, adjusting informally for plausible confounding from staffing changes and added routing to adopters could shrink this advantage or even eliminate it; a conservative bounded conclusion is that the median effect is likely between roughly −15% (substantial benefit) and +5% (slight harm) relative to a no-adoption counterfactual. For CSAT, differences between adopters and controls over time are within about ±0.1 points on a 1–5 scale. Even under generous assumptions, any true effect on `csat_1to5` is likely small, on the order of −0.1 to +0.1, and could easily be zero once survey-selection bias is considered. For tails and escalation, available evidence is consistent with automation contributing to higher escalation rates and at most modest tail mitigation for some branches; sign and magnitude relative to counterfactuals remain highly uncertain.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Given the mixed but somewhat encouraging evidence—especially at Gamma—and the substantial unresolved confounding, the next step should be an expansion explicitly designed as a learning experiment. Delta and Epsilon are natural candidates, but to maximize causal learning and manage risk, the organization should randomize the timing and intensity of automation within and across these branches. For example, leadership could randomize which branch adopts first, and within each branch randomly assign subsets of products, risk classes, or arrival channels to higher versus lower automated-fast-path availability. This stepped-wedge, stratified design would generate credible counterfactuals while gradually exposing customers to the new system.

The embedded evaluation should include at least six months of pre-period data (already partly available) and a minimum of six to nine months of post-period observations for each treated unit, with clear demarcation of go-live and any major configuration changes. Process-mining outcomes should go beyond branch-month medians and p90s to include per-case path variants (manual vs automated, escalated vs not), queue-state measures at manual triage, specialist, and fraud-desk queues (e.g., daily backlog, waiting-time distributions), and routing flows between branches. Spillover monitoring should track cross-branch arrivals, fraud-desk load, and whether non-adopter branches’ tails or CSAT deteriorate as adopters scale automation.

Guardrails for customer harm should be pre-defined: for instance, triggers to pause or reduce automation intensity if a branch’s `p90_cycle_h` or rework rate rises more than a set threshold (e.g., 20%) relative to its randomized controls for two consecutive months, or if `csat_1to5` falls below an agreed floor. A decision rule for scale-up might require that automated branches achieve at least a modest median-cycle improvement (e.g., 5–10%) or equivalent volume-handling gain, with no statistically or practically significant degradation in tails or CSAT, under the randomized design. Meeting these criteria would justify wider deployment; failure would point toward redesigning triage rules, specialist capacity, or routing before further expansion.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Under rising arrivals and case complexity, adopter branches maintained or modestly improved median cycle times compared with never-treated branches, while tails generally worsened across the system and escalation to specialists increased in adopter branches.
- Suggestive: Higher automated-fast-path use, particularly at Gamma, is associated with faster medians, slightly better tails, and stable or improved CSAT without clear rework inflation, although similar intensity patterns at Alpha and Beta do not consistently translate into performance gains.
- Conditionally causal: Under strong parallel-trend and no-time-varying-confounding assumptions using Delta and Epsilon as controls, making automation available likely reduces median cycle time for adopter-type branches by on the order of single-digit percentages relative to a no-adoption counterfactual, with ambiguous effects on tail performance.
- Not supported: A simple, system-wide statement that “automation reduces cycle time by X%” uniformly across branches, case types, and percentiles is not justified by the current staggered, confounded rollout data.
