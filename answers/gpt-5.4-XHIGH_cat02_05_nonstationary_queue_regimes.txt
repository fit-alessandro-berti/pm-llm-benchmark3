# 1. Data audit and estimands
Observation: the center is open `07:00-19:00`, so one operating day is `12` opening hours. Let `A_d` be daily arrivals during opening hours, `C_d` daily completions during opening hours, `L_d` mean work-in-process from 15-minute opening-hours snapshots, and `c_d` mean concurrent staffed FTE. Useful rate estimates are `lambda_A = A_d / 12` and `lambda_C = C_d / 12`, both in cases per opening hour.

Observation: `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min` are active handling times only. They exclude waiting and are not means. They are therefore service-time descriptors, not flow-time estimates and not direct substitutes for `W` in Little's Law.

Estimate: capacity pressure can be summarized only indirectly here, for example by `L_d / c_d` (WIP per staffed FTE), by the sign of `C_d - A_d`, and by completions per staffed opening hour `C_d / (12 c_d)`.

If I write `L / lambda` later, the estimand is only an open-hours-equivalent mean time in system for a locally stable window under the same opening-hours convention. It is not full clock-time elapsed flow time, because overnight backlog can exist while `L` is not observed when the center is closed.

Threats to naive inference:

- Opening-hours censoring: arrivals, completions, staffing, and WIP are summarized only while open. Cases may remain in system overnight.
- Unknown boundary states: 07:00 opening backlog and 19:00 closing backlog are not given, so a day with `C > A` is not necessarily “over-capacity”; it may simply be draining earlier backlog.
- Completion-arrival mismatch: completed cases need not have arrived the same day, so same-day `A` and `C` are not paired cohorts.
- WIP is sampled, not continuous: 15-minute snapshots can miss short spikes and smooth short outages or bursts.
- Service summaries are not flow-time summaries: median and p90 active service times exclude queueing delay and are not means.
- Serial dependence is expected: backlog, case mix, staffing, and weekday/weekend structure persist across days, so daily rows are not IID.
- The `complex_pct` jump after `2026-04-20` could be a real mix change, a coding change, or both. That uncertainty matters for causal claims.
- The staffing intervention on `2026-04-29` has no exact timestamp, so that day is intrinsically a transition day, not a clean post-change regime.
- The outage on `2026-04-15` creates a shock-and-recovery pattern. Treating `2026-04-16` to `2026-04-17` as normal high productivity would be wrong.
- The representative hourly vectors do not reproduce the stated `peak_08_10_pct` values for the same labeled dates. For example, the `2026-04-21` vector gives about `9.6%` in the `08:00-09:59` bins, while the daily field says `47%`. So those vectors are useful for shape and burstiness, but not for validating the exact daily peak-share field.

# 2. Primary regime segmentation
Primary segmentation: a date-ordered operational segmentation works best here. It separates weekday/weekend scale, the explicit outage shock, the recovery flush, the persistent high-complexity/high-burst phase that begins on `2026-04-20`, and the staffing intervention that starts during `2026-04-29`.

Why one pooled queueing model is misleading under this segmentation:

- Demand scale changes sharply by day type: weekdays average `108` arrivals across the full sample, weekends `44.9`.
- Service-time behavior shifts with the high-complexity phase: median active service moves from roughly `25-29` minutes to roughly `34-36` minutes, and p90 from roughly `47-56` to roughly `78-88`.
- Capacity state changes matter: the same queue can be balanced, outage-constrained, backlog-draining, overloaded, or intervention-assisted.
- Backlog state changes the meaning of completions: `120.5/day` during `2026-04-16` to `2026-04-17` is a recovery flush, not a sustainable stationary completion rate.

# 3. Alternative segmentation
A serious alternative is a state-based segmentation that ignores weekday/weekend boundaries unless they coincide with a different operating state. This tells a more causal story: the queue alternates among low-complex normal operation, shock, unassisted high-complex stress, and assisted high-complex recovery.

| Alternative state | Dates | Operational story | Compact summary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-complex normal | `2026-04-06` to `2026-04-14`, plus `2026-04-18` to `2026-04-19` | Normal low-complex process family, scaled down on weekends | `A≈78.9/day`, `C≈78.5/day`, `WIP≈17.9`, `complex≈21.5%`, `peak≈25.4%` |
| Shock outage | `2026-04-15` | Capacity failure dominates demand behavior | `A=105`, `C=58`, `WIP=47` |
| Shock recovery flush | `2026-04-16` to `2026-04-17` | Backlog-drain regime, not normal production | `A≈105.5/day`, `C≈120.5/day`, `WIP≈43.5` |
| Unassisted high-complex | `2026-04-20` to `2026-04-28`, plus `2026-04-25` to `2026-04-26` and the post-period weekends without weekday staffing uplift | High-complex and morning-heavy work arrives faster than the old staffing pattern can absorb it | `A≈90.0/day`, `C≈80.4/day`, `WIP≈35.8`, `complex≈43.1%`, net `-106` |
| Assisted high-complex | `2026-04-29` to `2026-05-01` | Same stressed mix, but with additional weekday capacity | `A≈115/day`, `C≈121/day`, `WIP≈37.0`, `complex≈44.0%`, net `+18` |

Strengths of the alternative segmentation:

- It aligns more directly with suspected drivers: outage, case-mix change, and staffing uplift.
- It makes the intervention story clearer: under high-complex conditions, added weekday staffing coincides with a sign reversal from backlog growth to backlog drain.
- It is less likely to mistake the weekend/weekday scale effect for a structural process change.

Weaknesses relative to the primary segmentation:

- It deliberately mixes weekdays and weekends inside the same state, so it is weaker for roster design.
- Its “unassisted high-complex” bucket still contains heterogeneous weekends and weekdays, so it is not a single stationary queue either.
- It depends more heavily on interpreting `complex_pct` as meaningful; if that field partly changed because of coding, the state labels become less causal.

So the primary segmentation is better for operations planning, while the alternative segmentation is better for explaining what changed.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity
Observation from representative hourly vectors:

- `2026-04-07` looks like an ordinary weekday bulge, but still not Poisson-smooth. Arrival Fano is about `3.23`, arrival CV about `0.62`, and the within-day cumulative net reaches about `+6` cases relative to start-of-day.
- `2026-04-15` is not primarily a demand burst problem. Arrivals are moderately bursty (`Fano≈4.14`, `CV≈0.69`), but the key feature is three zero-completion hours. The cumulative within-day net reaches about `+55`, consistent with the outage causing queue growth.
- `2026-04-21` is the burstiest arrival pattern supplied. Arrival Fano is about `10.12`, arrival CV about `1.03`, the largest hour contains about `29.6%` of the day’s arrivals, and the within-day cumulative net peaks around `+27`. Completions are much smoother (`Fano≈0.98`), suggesting the burst is on the demand side more than the completion side.
- `2026-04-25` is calmer: arrival Fano about `1.29`, arrival CV about `0.55`, with a smaller maximum hour share (`15.7%`).

Observation from the daily sequence:

- Pooled daily arrivals show a Fano factor of about `10.0`, but all weekdays together are only about `0.66` and all weekends about `0.73`. That means apparent overdispersion in pooled daily counts is dominated by regime mixture and day-type composition, not just within-regime noise.
- Lag-1 autocorrelation is materially positive for arrivals (`0.41` overall, `0.54` on weekdays only), stronger for WIP (`0.61` overall, `0.79` on weekdays), and extremely strong for `complex_pct` (`0.93`). That is strong evidence of serial dependence and persistent state.
- Relative backlog, using cumulative `C-A` with an arbitrary zero reference, is close to balanced before the outage, drops to roughly `-51` after `2026-04-15`, recovers to about `-21` by `2026-04-17`, then worsens again to about `-115` by `2026-04-28` before the intervention starts to reverse it.

Change-point evidence:

- A transparent 3-day standardized split heuristic scores `2026-04-20` and `2026-04-21` highest. That supports a real regime change around the mix/peak shift.
- `2026-04-15` also scores highly, which is expected because the outage is an explicit shock.
- `2026-04-27` to `2026-05-01` also score highly, consistent with a second transition around the staffing intervention and subsequent backlog reduction.

Why stationarity is weak:

- The queue is not stationary across the full 28-day sample because demand scale, burstiness, service-time behavior, staffing, and backlog state all change.
- Even within candidate subperiods, weekends and weekdays are different enough that separate treatment is usually safer.
- Four representative days cannot identify a full stochastic arrival or service model. They are too few to infer a renewal process, a time-varying Poisson model, or a service-time distribution family.
- Because the supplied hourly vectors conflict with the daily peak-share field, they should be treated as illustrative shapes rather than exact calibration data.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix
All implied `W` values below are open-hour-equivalent times in system, because both `L` and `lambda` are built from opening-hours aggregates. They are not full elapsed clock-time flow times.

| Candidate window/regime | Boundary stability | Throughput proxy | WIP comparability | Carry-over risk | Stationarity assessment | Implied flow-time calculation if defensible | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `2026-04-06` to `2026-04-14`, weekdays only | Moderate: 7-day net flow `-4`, so arrivals and completions are close | `lambda_A≈8.35/h` or `lambda_C≈8.30/h` | Moderate: same opening-hours sampling convention throughout | Medium: overnight backlog still unobserved | Locally the most stable weekday window | `20.3 / 8.35 ≈ 2.43 h` or `20.3 / 8.30 ≈ 2.44 h` | `approximate only` |
| `2026-04-11`,`2026-04-12`,`2026-04-18`,`2026-04-19` | Moderate: 4-day net flow `-1` | `lambda_A≈3.48/h` or `lambda_C≈3.46/h` | Moderate | Medium | Plausible local weekend regime, but short window | `13.8 / 3.48 ≈ 3.95 h` or `13.8 / 3.46 ≈ 3.98 h` | `approximate only` |
| `2026-04-15` outage day | Poor: explicit intra-day outage breaks boundaries | Neither same-day `A` nor `C` is a steady throughput proxy | Poor: WIP responds to outage shock | High | Clearly nonstationary | Not defensible | `not defensible` |
| `2026-04-16` to `2026-04-17` recovery flush | Poor: backlog is being drained | `C` is inflated by old work; `A` understates the work being cleared | Poor | High | Recovery transient, not steady state | Not defensible | `not defensible` |
| `2026-04-20` to `2026-04-28`, weekdays only | Poor: 7-day net flow `-94` and WIP rises to `50` | `lambda_A≈9.50/h`, `lambda_C≈8.38/h`, but the gap is exactly the problem | Moderate measurement, poor state comparability | High | Persistent overload, not stationary | Raw ratios `44.3/9.50≈4.66 h` and `44.3/8.38≈5.28 h` should not be interpreted as steady-state `W` | `not defensible` |
| `2026-04-29` transition day | Poor: intervention starts during the day at unknown time | Mixed pre/post-change day | Poor | High | By definition transitional | Not defensible | `not defensible` |
| `2026-05-02` to `2026-05-03` post-intervention weekends | Moderate: 2-day net flow `0` | `lambda_A≈4.08/h` or `lambda_C≈4.08/h` | Moderate | Medium | Short but locally balanced weekend window | `20.5 / 4.08 ≈ 5.02 h` | `approximate only` |

The key rule is that Little's Law is most believable only when boundary conditions are approximately stable and arrivals and completions nearly agree over the same window. That condition is plainly violated for outage, recovery, overloaded high-complex weekdays, and the full pooled sample.

# 7. Operational implications
- Staff to regime, not to the pooled average. A staffing plan built around the pooled `5.14` FTE or pooled `90/day` arrivals would under-resource high-complex weekdays, where arrivals are about `114/day`, active service is slower, and WIP/FTE rises to about `7.47` before the intervention.
- Treat outage recovery as a separate playbook. The `2026-04-16` to `2026-04-17` completion surge is backlog liquidation, not a new sustainable target. It should trigger temporary recovery staffing, not a permanent productivity benchmark.
- Add targeted morning flexibility in the high-complex regime. The daily peak field jumps from about `30%` in the ordinary weekday regime to about `47%` in high-complex weekdays. Even with the caveat on the exact hourly vectors, the operational signal is that demand concentration increased materially.
- Preserve or formalize the staffing uplift used on `2026-04-29` to `2026-05-01`. Under comparable high-complex weekday demand, completions move from `100.6/day` pre-intervention to `121/day` after staffing increases, while mean WIP falls from `44.3` to `34.0`.
- Track backlog explicitly. A simple daily backlog ledger with opening backlog, arrivals, completions, and closing backlog would prevent recovery days from being misread as normal throughput.
- Audit the variant definition behind `complex_pct`. If the field changed because of coding, then some of the apparent service-time and burstiness shift may be classification drift rather than pure operational deterioration.
- Improve data collection before fitting a queueing model. Needed fields are exact intervention timestamps, exact outage intervals, hourly staffing by role, actual wait time, full case flow time, opening/closing backlog, and an event log with per-step timestamps.

One policy that looks beneficial under pooled averages but is risky under a segmented view:

- “Hold staffing near the pooled average because the queue averages only `90` arrivals per day.” This looks economical in pooled data, but it is risky because the queue actually alternates between about `108` weekday arrivals and about `45` weekend arrivals, with a separate stressed weekday regime at about `114` arrivals plus slower service and heavier morning concentration. The pooled average would institutionalize understaffing on exactly the days that create backlog.

Another policy that pooled data can misstate:

- “The staffing intervention did not help, because pooled stabilized completions (`85/day`) are slightly below the pre-intervention high-complex all-day mean (`87.3/day`).” That conclusion is wrong after segmentation: weekday completions rise from `100.6` to `121`, and weekend completions rise from `41` to `49`; the pooled decline is only a weekday/weekend composition effect.

    "high_complex_weekends_pre_intervention": daily["date"].isin(pd.to_datetime(["2026-04-25", "2026-04-26"])),
    "intervention_transition_day": daily["date"].eq(pd.Timestamp("2026-04-29")),
    "post_intervention_weekdays": daily["date"].isin(pd.to_datetime(["2026-04-30", "2026-05-01"])),
    "post_intervention_weekends": daily["date"].isin(pd.to_datetime(["2026-05-02", "2026-05-03"])),
    "pooled_all_days": pd.Series(True, index=daily.index),
}

primary_summary = pd.DataFrame({k: summarize(v) for k, v in primary_masks.items()}).T

def alt_state(row):
    if row["outage_min"] > 0:
        return "shock_outage"
    if row["date"] in pd.to_datetime(["2026-04-16", "2026-04-17"]):
        return "shock_recovery_flush"
    if row["complex_pct"] >= 39 and row["staff_fte"] >= 6.5:
        return "assisted_high_complex"
    if row["complex_pct"] >= 39:
        return "unassisted_high_complex"
    return "low_complex_normal"

def burstiness(values):
    s = pd.Series(values, dtype=float)
    mean = s.mean()
    sd = s.std(ddof=1)
    return {
        "mean": mean,
        "sd": sd,
        "cv": sd / mean if mean else np.nan,
        "fano": s.var(ddof=1) / mean if mean else np.nan,
        "lag1": s.autocorr(),
    }

hourly_metrics = []
peak_lookup = daily.set_index(daily["date"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m-%d"))["peak_08_10_pct"].to_dict()
for day, vals in hourly.items():
    arr = np.array(vals["arrivals_by_hour"])
    comp = np.array(vals["completions_by_hour"])
    arr_b = burstiness(arr)
    comp_b = burstiness(comp)
    cum_net = (arr - comp).cumsum()
    hourly_metrics.append({
        "day": day,
        "arrivals_fano": arr_b["fano"],
        "arrivals_cv": arr_b["cv"],
        "arrivals_lag1": arr_b["lag1"],
        "completions_fano": comp_b["fano"],
        "completions_cv": comp_b["cv"],
        "max_single_hour_arrival_share_pct": arr.max() * 100.0 / arr.sum(),
        "cum_net_peak": cum_net.max(),
        "cum_net_end": cum_net[-1],
        "vector_peak_08_10_pct": (arr[1] + arr[2]) * 100.0 / arr.sum(),
        "daily_peak_08_10_pct": peak_lookup[day],
        "note": vals["note"],
    })

# Transparent heuristic, not ground truth:
# compare standardized 3-day windows before and after each candidate split.
features = [
    "arrivals", "completed", "mean_wip", "staff_fte",
    "complex_pct", "peak_08_10_pct", "median_service_min", "p90_service_min"
]
z = daily[features].copy()
for col in features:
    std = z[col].std(ddof=1)
    z[col] = (z[col] - z[col].mean()) / std if std else 0.0

win = 3
scores = []
for i in range(win, len(daily) - win + 1):
    left = z.iloc[i - win:i].mean()
    right = z.iloc[i:i + win].mean()
    score = (right - left).abs().sum()
    scores.append({
        "candidate_start": daily.iloc[i]["date"].date().isoformat(),
        "score": score,
        "left_dates": f"{daily.iloc[i-win]['date'].date()}..{daily.iloc[i-1]['date'].date()}",
        "right_dates": f"{daily.iloc[i]['date'].date()}..{daily.iloc[i+win-1]['date'].date()}",
    })
change_scores = pd.DataFrame(scores).sort_values("score", ascending=False).head(8)

# Little's Law is only computed for windows that are locally balanced enough
# to support a rough approximation. Because both L and lambda come from

        "L_open": L,

        "lambda_comp_open_hr": lambda_comp,
