# 2. Integrated explanation

The trajectory is not a single learning or fatigue curve but a composition of three regimes that overlap within every shift, amplified by how management stages work:

**Regime A (positions 1-30, ~0-2.5h): complexity-dominated, then warm-up.** Shifts start with inherited backlog (WIP 46 Early, 70 Late, 61 overall at pos 1-5). First hour carries 41% high-priority, mean complexity 0.72, vs 0.50 by mid-shift. Seniors selectively take these (noted post-weekend/night backlog), so mean service time 18.8 min is inflated by harder cases, not incompetence. As backlog clears (WIP 39  33, complexity 0.61  0.54), service time falls to 13.6 min and errors fall to 2.5%. This *appears* as learning but is mostly case-mix normalization plus genuine warm-up (procedure re-activation, system logins). Evidence: stabilization note, junior early error 5.1% partly reflects being handed complex carryover beyond skill level. Pure tenure effect after adjusting for complexity is modest (~1-2 min).

**Regime B (positions 31-50, ~2.5-6h): efficient plateau under balanced flow.** Complexity 0.50, queue 31, high-priority 25%. Service time 12.9 min, error 2.7% - the system's best operating point. Case-mix, staffing and demand align, especially for Early shift (clears to 42) and Day mid-period. Within-resource variance is lowest here; low vs medium WIP differences are small (12.7 vs 11.6 min for low complexity). This plateau is misread as "peak learning" if WIP not controlled.

**Regime C (positions 51+, ~6-8h+: pressure-driven speed-accuracy tradeoff).** Demand peaks late, especially Day (38  75 queue) and inherited by Late. After position 51, complexity continues falling to 0.43-0.46 - cases *should* get easier - yet service time drops sharply to 11.4  10.8 min and errors jump 4.6%  7.9% (rework 8.2%  13.7%). This is not learning, not pure fatigue, and not circadian alone. It is workers responding rationally to visible queue: cut service time, batch similar work (Junior_H/I Document Review 9-18 event runs), defer Quality Check to shift end, and satisfice on Bot_Risk verification. Crucially, the tradeoff is *conditional*: under low WIP late, seniors 2.2% error and mids 2.9% error - essentially flat - proving time-on-task alone does not compel error. Under high WIP (>70) the same late tenure produces 4.9% (seniors), 6.8% (mids), 12.4% (juniors), 8.2% (nights). Complexity interaction intensifies it: high WIP high complexity 10.9% error vs low WIP high complexity 4.2%.

Why errors rise only in some conditions? Because two filters intervene:
1. **Experience buffer:** Seniors maintain quality by slowing less but preserving checks - their high-WIP late error 4.9% is < junior low-WIP early error 5.1%. Mid and junior collapse under same pressure.
2. **Workaround opportunities:** Low-complexity tasks allow shortcuts (9.4 min under high WIP) that hide in average service time but leak as later rework/Quality Check bursts in last 45 min. Waiting time falls due to batch completion, not true processing efficiency.
3. **Automation propagation:** Bot_Risk classifications are not independently worse late, but human verification is thinned under pressure, so corrections spike and uncaught bot errors surface as downstream rework within 7 days.

In short: service time falls monotonically for three different reasons - early: easier mix; mid: warm-up + flow; late: pressure + batching - while errors trace a U-shape because early complexity errors are offset by caution and senior coverage, mid plateau benefits from good conditions, and late pressure overwhelms caution except for experienced staff at low load. Attributing the late fall in service time to learning overstates skill gain and legitimizes understaffing.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker-level conclusions are fragile and potentially unfair in this setting for five compounding reasons:**

* **Selection bias created by operations, not workers.** The log is not a randomized trial. Seniors appear faster early (15.9 min) than juniors (19.6 min) but handle disproportionate complex backlog (complexity 0.72 early). Early high service time is management's sequencing choice, not a skill deficit. Ranking resources on mean service time without `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, `queue_length_at_start` rewards those given easy cases late (complexity 0.43) and punishes those who clear backlog. Random assignment would change leaderboards.

* **Queue pressure is a management decision, not individual failure.** High WIP (>70) low complexity error 5.8% is 2.8× baseline; high complexity 10.9% is 2.6× baseline. This tradeoff appears *within* the same worker late vs early when WIP rises, and across shifts (Day 3875, Late 7066). Blaming final-2-hour errors (Day 7.5%) without logging staffing ratios, arrival rates, and overtime mandates misattributes systemic understaffing to personal fatigue. Penalizing workers for management's demand curve is both inaccurate and demoralizing.

* **Measurement error and detection bias.** `service_time = time:timestamp - start_timestamp` is logged work, not cognitive effort: batch opening (9-18 events same queue batch) artificially compresses service time, inflating "productivity" while deflating quality. `error_flag` depends on Quality Check intensity, which itself is batched in final 45 min; errors occurring early may be caught later, errors occurring late may escape to `rework_flag` 7 days later or remain undetected. Night urgent exceptions (56% high complexity) have different error definitions. Comparing raw `error_flag` across resources without adjusting for who gets checked, when, and by whom, amplifies visibility bias.

* **Automation quality confounded with human verification load.** Bot_Risk is instant but not infallible. Human corrections rise at WIP>70 - this could mean bots degrade, or humans over-correct under stress, or humans correctly catch more because they finally look. Without independent bot confidence and audit labels, attributing downstream risk-classification errors solely to `org:resource` who last touched the case scapegoats humans for automation limits and reduced double-check capacity.

* **Punitive use of error rates creates perverse incentives and harm.** As shown, Juniors 12.4% under high WIP late vs Seniors 4.9% is not a fixed trait but a load × experience interaction; Night staff fewer cases but more urgent exceptions. Punitive ranking (Intervention 8) and public leaderboards will drive defensive behaviors observed already: batching Document Review, deferring/diluting Quality Checks, avoiding complex cases, under-reporting errors, or rejecting high-priority backlog. This improves metrics briefly but raises true rework (13.7% at 76+), customer harm, and turnover among most pressured staff. Any performance use must be risk-adjusted, developmental, and accompanied by workload fairness guarantees, transparency, and appeal.

**Operational cautions:** Do not use raw late-shift error rates for discipline, promotion, or pay. Validate constructs (replicate audit of errors, observe actual batch work), share aggregate - not individual - dashboards with workers, and involve them in diagnosing queue causes (rostering, inflow throttling, bot thresholds) rather than imposing top-down caps.
