# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

**Handover of work.** A directed edge records that one resource’s event follows another’s in the same claim under the chosen event-ordering rule. Mira has high normalized out-degree (0.82), in-degree (0.51) and betweenness (0.77). The directions matter. Bot_Rules routes exceptions to her, both temps send work to her, and she routes onward to adjusters in both teams and to medical and legal specialists. This is consistent with a desk linking otherwise separate work queues. Betweenness describes paths in the aggregate graph. It establishes neither managerial authority nor the fraction of claims that actually followed those paths. An adjacent event can also be an automated status update rather than a personal instruction.

**Working together.** This network gives an undirected tie to resources recorded on the same claim, even if their events are days apart. The reported pattern has recurring North and South pairs, such as Alice–Carla and Bruno–Diego. Mira’s weighted degree is 0.16, versus 0.46 for Alice and 0.43 for Bruno. A broker can touch only 22% of claims and send each one to a different subset of handlers. Each particular pairing then covers relatively few claims, while Mira still has many distinct onward routes. Repeated handovers on one claim can add several transitions but only one co-case for a pair. The brevity of a routing event explains how little handling time it requires. It does **not** reduce binary co-occurrence, and Mira’s disappearance for several events does not erase her earlier touch. Low co-case strength is weak evidence about informal advice or influence.

**Subcontracting.** The short return pattern A → B → A captures an intervening resource followed by a return to the first. The reported Mira–Omar–Mira and Mira–Priya–Mira fragments, together with Mira’s subcontracting betweenness of 0.69, suggest that some referrals return to her desk for a next decision. This adds information beyond seeing both directions in an aggregate handover graph, where the two directions could come from different claims. A return could also record an answer acknowledgment, an automatic reopen or repeated clarification. It does not establish a reporting line or a contractual delegation. The Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules and Temp_1–Vendor_Verify–Temp_1 patterns illustrate the range of mechanisms the same measure captures.

**Performance and time.** Mira’s median service time is 4.2 minutes, and 48% of the claims she touches are flagged exceptions. She appears on 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Those last two percentages describe who handles those claims, not who caused the problem. During her six-day absence, exceptions queued and their 90th-percentile cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days, while the ordinary low-value median held steady. This is evidence of a possible coverage problem concentrated in the exception tail. The two statistics describe different populations and quantiles. Neither the absence comparison nor the 42- and 51-minute mean gaps from Mira to the specialists identifies whose queue consumed the time. Specialist review, external verification and payment can all extend elapsed time independently of a short routing action.

**Activity profile and role discovery.** Missing-information requests overlap with junior adjusters’ and Temp_1’s work. Reopens overlap with Alice’s work, while specialist assignment and complaint escalation distinguish Mira from the leads’ routine triage and settlement approval. An activity-frequency profile could identify a distinct exception-handling function or a blend of clerical and coordination work. No similarity or role-discovery result is supplied. Even a stable cluster would describe recorded tasks, leaving decision rights and employment grade to be verified separately.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk.** The observed signal concerns slow exceptional claims. A queue can grow before a four-minute task if nobody is available to take it. Locate the growing queue before adding coordinator or specialist capacity.
- **Coordination risk.** Bypassing the desk could send incomplete claims to expensive specialists or leave returned opinions without an owner. Conversely, routing every clarification back through Mira could add avoidable transfers. Referral acceptance and the reason for each return distinguish these risks.
- **Key-person risk.** The service may depend on Mira’s knowledge, permissions, or simply having someone rostered at the desk. These imply different remedies. The absence supports testing cover and documenting decisions, without establishing that Mira alone can perform them.
- **Compliance and control risk.** Detecting a problem and choosing its reviewer can concentrate discretion, particularly on complaints or high-value claims. Check actual permissions, referral criteria, overrides, and independence of review and settlement approval. The log shows selections and sequences, with no direct evidence of improper referrals or approvals.
- **Workload fairness risk.** Compare difficult assignments, time spent, rework and available hours across the desk, teams, specialists and temps. Omar and Priya handle exception-heavy work with long service times. The temps’ lower exception shares may reflect their remit. Ranking staff by raw throughput could penalize difficult assignments or conceal repeated overload.
- **Data-quality risk.** The six listed outbound Mira frequencies sum to 1,640 against 960 Mira events. A single direct-successor graph over those same events could contribute at most 960 such transitions. Likewise, if all 4,100 ingestion cases form the denominator, Alice’s 665 cases cap any Alice co-case rate near 16%, below the reported 27% with Carla. Different scopes, normalization or edge definitions could explain the discrepancies. The broad role hypotheses are worth testing, but exact rates and rankings need reconstruction.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

1. **Establish a common log and case population.** Use `pm4py.format_dataframe` with the supplied case, activity and timestamp columns. Verify resource identities, desk versus personal accounts, bot and vendor event origins, duplicate records, time zones, ties and lifecycle transitions. Reconcile “Assign Specialist” with the activity catalogue. Build an ordered completion trace for sequence analysis and retain matched start/complete instances for timing. Document the 12-week inclusion rule and flag claims already open at the start or unfinished at the end.
2. **Rebuild directed handovers.** Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` and a same-case direct-successor table to verify direction and raw counts. For Bot_Rules → Mira, Mira → each handler, and specialist → Mira, report transitions, distinct claims, share of source transitions and underlying activity pairs. Recalculate centrality with stated thresholds and path-weight conventions. Keep bots and vendors in the primary graph. For a human-only view, hide their nodes in the constructed graph so their removal does not manufacture direct human handovers.
3. **Rebuild working-together ties.** Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` and independently count each resource pair once per claim. State the denominator for each reported percentage. Compare the fraction of all claims containing a pair with the fraction of Mira’s claims containing each partner and with Jaccard overlap (shared claims divided by claims involving either partner). Recalculate within exceptions and within each team. Stronger conditional ties alongside weak whole-log ties would support selective brokerage.
4. **Inspect short returns.** Use `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with `n=2`, verify its window against explicit trace fragments, and vary the window as a sensitivity check. Count qualifying fragments and distinct claims. Among eligible Mira-to-specialist referrals, measure the fraction returning to Mira, the elapsed time, the next activity and whether another referral follows. Compare with lead–adjuster approval loops and bot and vendor loops. Sample linked referral records to distinguish completed consultations from misrouting or acknowledgments.
5. **Compare activities and matched pathways.** Run `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` or `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` and inspect the underlying activity proportions. Compare Mira with leads, adjusters, temps and any substitute across weeks. Use complete-case filters for exception status and `pm4py.filter_variants` for ordinary, specialist and reopen pathways. For incident-triggered cohorts, select case IDs containing the activity and retain the full trace. Deleting intervening activities first would create false adjacencies. Compare like case types, priorities, amount bands, regions and channels, using information available at routing time.
6. **Locate the delay and test the absence explanation.** Use paired intervals with `pm4py.get_service_time` and case service/waiting measures such as `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` where timestamp coverage permits. Break the exception path into detection to desk start, desk handling, assignment to specialist start, review, and return to next action. Identify external verification and payment waits separately. With completion-only data, report inter-event gaps and obtain queue-entry or start records before attributing waiting. Compare exception medians and 90th percentiles, arrivals, open backlog and staffing before, during and after the absence against comparable weekdays and covered shifts. Account for parallel work, case mix, vendor or specialist disruption and unfinished claims. Track claims already waiting when the absence began as well as new arrivals.

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately.** Preserve the exception desk and name a trained deputy with the access needed to handle referrals and returns. Have Mira, the leads and specialists document common routing criteria, who owns a returned claim and when escalation is appropriate. Record referral reasons and overrides. Have compliance review a sample of discretionary decisions against existing policy.

**Monitor.** Pilot deputy coverage on scheduled shifts. Compare time to accepted assignment, aged backlog, exception tail cycle time, repeat referrals, formal escalations, specialist load and control exceptions against comparable Mira-covered shifts. Track ordinary claims and vendor turnaround alongside these measures to detect displaced work or external delays.

**Defer structural changes.** Keep current reporting lines and approval rights pending the log audit and coverage pilot. Avoid eliminating the desk, making Mira the mandatory approver for all exceptions, or automating specialist selection on the strength of network rank alone. Any redesign should identify who will triage ambiguous claims, receive specialist answers and own unresolved returns.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira appears to help difficult claims reach the right people across both teams. She touches about one claim in five, yet appears on nearly two thirds of reopened claims and seven in ten complaints. That helps explain why many transfers pass through her even though she seldom appears alongside the same adjuster across the full caseload. During her six-day absence, the slowest tenth of exception claims took at least about 14 days, up from about eight. That raises a coverage concern, although claim mix and specialist availability could also explain some of the delay. Management should keep the desk in place, train a deputy, document routing decisions and review a sample of discretionary referrals. A coverage trial should measure how long claims wait for assignment, how often specialists redirect them and whether queues move elsewhere. Check the underlying counts before using network rankings to change reporting lines or assess individual performance.
