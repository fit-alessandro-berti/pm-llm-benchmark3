# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness should be assessed across several dimensions, not reduced to whether groups have identical approval rates.

1. **Outcome parity**
   - Raw approval rates differ substantially: 78% for Group A, 63% for Group B, 69% for Group C, and 58% for Group D.
   - These differences alone do not establish unfair treatment because the groups differ in measured risk, documentation completeness, channel, prior cases, and use of support processes.
   - They are nevertheless an important signal for investigation, especially when paired with higher appeal, rework, or delay rates.

2. **Adjusted outcome disparity**
   - After adjustment for listed case-mix variables, Groups B, C, and D still have lower predicted approval probabilities than Group A:
     - Group B: -5.5 percentage points
     - Group C: -2.2 points
     - Group D: -3.1 points
   - These residual differences are not, by themselves, proof of discrimination. They could reflect omitted legitimate factors, measurement error, model specification choices, or process differences not captured in the adjustment.
   - However, the Group B residual disparity is large enough to warrant focused review, particularly because it co-occurs with language-support routing, longer waits, more document requests, and more appeals.

3. **Procedural burden**
   - A fair process should not impose disproportionate administrative burden on applicants merely because they need language access or accommodation.
   - Groups B and C experience more frequent requests for missing documents and supervisor review even after adjustment:
     - Group B: +9.5 percentage points for document requests; +8.0 points for supervisor review.
     - Group C: +4.2 points for document requests; +5.5 points for supervisor review.
   - Some extra review may be justified by legitimately different case needs. But if the additional steps arise from inaccessible forms, inadequate translation, fragmented routing, or inconsistent handling, the burden is procedurally concerning.

4. **Timeliness**
   - Group B and Group C have materially longer adjusted cycle times than Group A:
     - Group B: +2.6 days
     - Group C: +4.1 days
   - Their long waits are concentrated at rights-preserving support steps:
     - Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment: Group B median 3.8 days.
     - Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment: Group C median 4.7 days.
   - Legitimate support steps should not be treated as unfair merely because they add activity time. The concern is the queueing delay, avoidable handoffs, and lack of explicit service-level targets for those steps.

5. **Consistency of routing**
   - Differential routing can be fair when it follows clear, relevant, and consistently applied rules:
     - High-risk cases may appropriately receive Fraud Review.
     - Accommodation requests may appropriately receive Accommodation Review.
     - Minority-language cases may appropriately receive Language Support Contact.
   - The key question is whether similarly situated cases are routed consistently and whether support-related routing produces avoidable repeat checks, redundant supervision, or unequal treatment.
   - Group D’s high use of fraud review is plausibly related to its higher risk-score distribution, but fairness still depends on whether the risk score is accurate, calibrated, and free from historical bias.

6. **Quality and accessibility of support**
   - Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving processes, not discretionary inconveniences.
   - The evidence indicates that people using them may face longer queues, extra handovers, and potentially more document requests.
   - A process may formally provide support while still being procedurally unfair if support is too delayed, insufficiently accessible, or causes applicants to repeat information across staff members.
   - The log cannot measure whether translation was accurate, whether accommodations were effective, whether communications were understandable, or whether applicants were treated respectfully.

7. **Appeal and rework burden**
   - Group B has a 13% appeal rate and 22% rework rate; Group C has a 10% appeal rate and 24% rework rate; Group D has a 16% appeal rate and 20% rework rate.
   - Adjusted appeal differences remain elevated for Group B (+4.4 points) and Group D (+5.7 points).
   - Appeals and rework can be legitimate where decisions are contested or additional evidence emerges. But elevated appeal or rework may also signal unclear notices, inconsistent first-pass decisions, inaccessible communications, or procedural errors.
   - Rework is especially relevant to fairness because it shifts administrative work and delay onto applicants who may already face barriers.

8. **Resource-allocation fairness**
   - Resource allocation appears uneven:
     - Group B cases are concentrated among seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers.
     - Group C cases are concentrated among five accommodation specialists, two of whom also conduct supervisor reviews.
   - Specialization may be necessary for quality and legal compliance, but unequal staffing capacity can turn legitimate routing into unequal service.
   - If the system provides legally required support but systematically under-resources it, the process may be formally compliant while operationally inequitable.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should distinguish: (a) observed disparity, (b) disparity explained by measured legitimate complexity, (c) potentially unjustified treatment differences, and (d) uncertainty caused by incomplete or biased data.

## Define the estimands

Analyze several outcomes separately rather than treating approval as the only endpoint:

- Approval and denial probability.
- Time to decision, notification, activation, and closure.
- Time spent waiting versus active service time.
- Probability of support routing, document requests, supervisor review, fraud review, appeal, and rework.
- Number of handovers and number of distinct resources.
- Accessibility-sensitive measures, such as delay after Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review.
- Reversal or modification rates following appeal, if obtainable.

For each measure, distinguish:

- **Total disparity:** observed group difference.
- **Complexity-adjusted disparity:** difference conditional on measured case attributes.
- **Within-path disparity:** difference among cases taking the same or comparable route.
- **Resource-conditional disparity:** difference among cases handled by comparable staff, teams, regions, or workloads.
- **Support-step disparity:** differential delay associated with language or accommodation routing.

## Matching or stratification variables

Use matching, coarsened exact matching, entropy balancing, inverse-probability weighting, or stratification across combinations of:

- `risk_score_band`
- `documentation_completeness`
- `channel`
- `household_size_band`
- `prior_case_count`
- `region`
- calendar month or processing period
- `disability_accommodation_requested`
- `language_preference`
- possibly baseline case type or program category, if available

Important design choices:

- Do not automatically adjust away `language_preference` or `disability_accommodation_requested` when assessing the fairness of support-step timeliness. These attributes define the population entitled to support.
- Use risk score and documentation completeness in sensitivity analyses, not just as fixed “neutral” controls, because both may reflect upstream inequities.
- Avoid controlling for variables that may themselves be downstream consequences of unequal treatment. For example, if documentation completeness is reduced because forms are inaccessible or translation is delayed, full adjustment for it can conceal part of the procedural disadvantage.

A useful approach is to report nested models:

1. Minimal adjustment: region, month, channel, household size, and prior case count.
2. Add documentation completeness.
3. Add risk score band.
4. Add intermediate process variables such as document requests or supervisor review only for descriptive mediation analysis, not as a primary “fairness-adjusted” model.

The change across models reveals how much of the disparity is associated with potentially contested covariates.

## Path and variant comparison

Compare groups on:

- Variant frequencies.
- Transition probabilities between activities.
- Conditional variant use within matched or stratified case-mix cells.
- Cycle time, service time, and waiting time within variants.
- Rework loops, such as repeated completeness checks, repeated requests for documents, or repeated supervisor review.
- Whether a support step is followed by unnecessary re-entry into general processing.

Examples:

- Compare Group B and Group A cases with similar documentation completeness, channel, region, and risk band. Is Group B still more likely to take the “Language Support  Completeness  Request Documents” path?
- Compare Group C and Group A cases with similar complexity. Is Group C still more likely to have Accommodation Review followed by Supervisor Review and delayed eligibility assessment?
- Compare Group D and Group A within the same risk band. Does Group D receive more fraud review or supervisor review even after risk is held constant?

## Resource-assignment comparison

Analyze assignment conditional on case mix:

- Probability of assignment to each resource or resource class.
- Concentration metrics: number of eligible staff, workload per staff member, and share assigned to high-delay staff.
- Handover count and sequence, particularly:
  - general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker
  - general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor
- Time between handovers.
- Within-resource comparisons: whether groups experience different waits when assigned to the same worker or team.
- Region-specific assignment patterns, especially the two regions with known high handovers.

A stronger fairness concern arises if similarly complex Group B or C cases are assigned to fewer-capacity queues without compensating staffing, while comparable Group A cases are processed by broader, less congested pools.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Decompose total duration into:

- Time before first review.
- Active service time per activity.
- Waiting time between activities.
- Waiting time at support steps.
- Waiting time before decision and notification.
- Appeal-related duration.
- Delay attributable to rework and handovers.

For example, estimate:

\[
\text{Total cycle time} =
\text{pre-processing wait} +
\text{service time} +
\text{inter-activity waiting} +
\text{rework delay} +
\text{appeal delay}.
\]

Then compare group differences in each component after stratification or matching.

This is important because Group B and C disparities appear primarily queue-related rather than solely caused by longer substantive work. A longer service interaction may be appropriate; a longer wait for that interaction may indicate capacity or routing inequity.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

Because risk score and documentation completeness may encode structural disadvantage:

- Re-estimate results excluding `risk_score_band`.
- Re-estimate with broader risk categories or within-risk-band comparisons.
- Test whether risk-score distributions differ by group within comparable observed case circumstances.
- Examine calibration: among applicants with the same risk band, do downstream fraud findings, denial outcomes, or appeal reversals differ by group?
- Treat documentation completeness as both:
  - a case attribute, and
  - a possible outcome of accessibility barriers.
- Compare results before and after adjusting for documentation completeness.
- Conduct quantitative sensitivity analysis for an unmeasured confounder, reporting how strong an omitted factor would need to be to eliminate the adjusted gap.
- Check whether group differences are concentrated in categories where risk scores or completeness assessments are most subjective.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

Report results separately by:

- Region.
- Online versus in-person channel.
- Language preference.
- Accommodation requested versus not requested.
- Risk score band.
- Documentation completeness level.
- Month or staffing period.

This can identify whether aggregate disparities are driven by:

- The two regions with overloaded bilingual routing.
- In-person processing delays.
- Specific support queues.
- Particular regions or staff teams.
- A subset of high-risk or incomplete-documentation cases.

Do not infer fairness from overall averages if a subgroup experiences materially worse treatment.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## Group B: language-support pathway and document burden

Group B has the clearest pattern of support-associated procedural burden.

- Group B uses V4, the language-support variant, in 24% of cases, compared with 1% for Group A.
- V4 has a median cycle time of 19.2 days, compared with 5.8 days for the common direct path V1.
- The wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment is 3.8 days.
- Group B waits 1.6 days from Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents, compared with 0.4 days for Group A.
- It also has a higher adjusted probability of:
  - Request Missing Documents: +9.5 points.
  - Supervisor Review: +8.0 points.
  - Appeal: +4.4 points.
- Its adjusted approval probability remains 5.5 points below Group A after the preliminary controls.

Some of this is plausibly explained by legitimate language-access needs, more in-person applications, and higher rates of incomplete documentation. Yet the concentration of Group B cases among only seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers is concerning. The 3.8-day support-to-eligibility delay appears more consistent with constrained capacity and fragmented routing than with the inherent time needed to provide language support.

The additional general  bilingual  general handovers in two regions are also concerning. They may create repeated explanation, inconsistent information transfer, unnecessary waiting, and more opportunities for missing-document requests. The evidence does not prove that language support causes denials or that any individual was treated discriminatorily, but it supports a substantial concern about unequal procedural burden.

## Group C: accommodation pathway and specialized-team queue

Group C has the greatest adjusted cycle-time difference: +4.1 days compared with Group A.

- Group C uses V5, the accommodation-review pathway, in 31% of cases, compared with 2% for Group A.
- V5 has the longest listed median time among non-appeal variants: 21.6 days.
- Group C’s wait from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment is 4.7 days.
- Group C has elevated adjusted probabilities of:
  - Supervisor Review: +5.5 points.
  - Request Missing Documents: +4.2 points.
- Its rework rate is 24%, the highest reported.
- It is concentrated among five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor reviews.

Accommodation Review is legitimate and should be retained. It may require specialized work and therefore some additional active processing time. The fairness concern is the extent to which Group C bears queue delay and multiple handovers because the specialized capacity is small and partly shared with supervisory work.

The pattern general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor may be substantively justified where an accommodation affects eligibility evidence or requires review. It becomes concerning if the process routinely returns cases to general staff without clear ownership, duplicates assessments, or uses supervisor review as a routine substitute for adequate specialist capacity.

## Group D: risk-based routing with unresolved fairness questions

Group D’s higher use of the fraud and supervisor pathway is largely consistent with measured case mix.

- Group D has the highest high-risk share: 51%.
- It uses V3 in 28% of cases, much higher than other groups.
- V3 includes Risk Screening  Fraud Review  Supervisor Review and has a median cycle time of 18.4 days.
- Group D waits 2.8 days from Risk Screening to Fraud Review, somewhat above Group A’s 2.2 days.
- Its raw approval rate is lowest, 58%, and its appeal rate is highest, 16%.
- Even after adjustment, Group D has:
  - -3.1 percentage-point approval difference.
  - +5.7-point appeal difference.
  - +2.0-point supervisor-review difference.

Fraud review for higher-risk cases is a legitimate policy route. Therefore, the existence of a higher V3 share is not evidence of unfairness by itself. The residual gaps are still important because risk-score bands may be coarse, imperfect, or historically biased. A fairness audit should ask whether applicants with the same risk band and comparable observed circumstances receive similar fraud-review intensity, delay, decision outcomes, and appeal outcomes across groups.

## Group A: lower burden and broader staffing access

Group A is much more likely to follow V1, the direct route, at 42%, with a 5.8-day median cycle time. It is spread across 52 caseworkers, rather than concentrated in small specialist pools.

This may reflect legitimate differences in online submission, documentation completeness, language needs, and case complexity. It also illustrates the structural comparison: Group A appears to receive the benefits of broad capacity, fewer handoffs, and greater access to the fast path. A fairness assessment should determine whether similarly situated non-Group-A applicants have equal access to a comparably efficient process.

## Cross-cutting resource disparity

The most important cross-cutting disparity is not simply that support users take extra steps. It is that support users appear routed into bottlenecked specialist pools without service-level protections.

- Bilingual capacity: seven caseworkers, with additional burden on three regional officers.
- Accommodation capacity: five specialists, with two also performing supervisor review.
- Identified handover-heavy regions.
- No separate timeliness thresholds for language or accommodation steps.

This combination makes it plausible that rights-preserving processes are producing unequal delay and administrative burden. The evidence supports concern about system design and capacity allocation more strongly than it supports a conclusion about intentional individual-level discrimination.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity and specialized review**
   - Higher risk cases may require fraud review and supervisor review.
   - Accommodation requests may require tailored assessment.
   - Language support may require interpreter coordination, translated communications, or specialized staff.
   - These mechanisms can legitimately increase the number of activities and some active service time.

2. **Under-capacity specialist queues**
   - Seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists may be insufficient for the volume and complexity of Group B and Group C cases.
   - Queueing can produce long waiting time even if staff perform support work appropriately.
   - The lack of separate service-level targets may make these delays operationally invisible.

3. **Fragmented routing and avoidable handovers**
   - General  specialist  general routing can create loss of context, duplicated verification, repeated applicant contact, and delays waiting for the next owner.
   - If no one has end-to-end ownership, specialized support may become an interruption rather than an integrated part of case processing.

4. **Accessibility-related documentation barriers**
   - Higher incomplete-documentation rates may reflect genuine missing evidence, but may also arise because forms, notices, upload systems, instructions, or document requirements are not adequately translated or accessible.
   - This could generate more Request Missing Documents events, rework, and appeals.

5. **Risk-score measurement or historical bias**
   - Group D’s routing may be justified by higher measured risk, but the score may reflect prior enforcement patterns, historical administrative contact, unequal access to documentation, or biased labels.
   - A risk score can be operationally predictive while still creating disparate and potentially unjustified procedural burdens.

6. **Regional staffing and operational variation**
   - The two regions with more handovers may have local staffing shortages, different practices, or different interpretations of policy.
   - This could explain why disparities are concentrated geographically rather than being uniform across the organization.

7. **Communication quality and decision explanation**
   - Higher appeal rates may arise from substantively incorrect decisions, but also from notices that are unclear, insufficiently translated, not accessible, or fail to explain missing evidence and next steps.
   - Poor explanations can cause unnecessary appeals and rework without necessarily changing initial approval rates.

8. **Different applicant constraints**
   - In-person applicants may face scheduling, transport, document-scanning, or office-capacity constraints.
   - These can legitimately affect elapsed time, but the agency still has a responsibility to avoid designing channels that systematically disadvantage people who cannot use the online route.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The event log is useful for detecting patterns, but it is insufficient to determine whether specific decisions were discriminatory or whether all observed disparities are unjustified.

- **Measurement bias in risk scores**
  - `risk_score_band` may reflect historical surveillance, enforcement, prior denials, neighborhood effects, or other inequities.
  - Treating it as a neutral control can obscure disparate treatment created upstream.
  - The banded form may also hide important variation and make calibration checks difficult.

- **Documentation completeness may not be exogenous**
  - `documentation_completeness` may reflect applicant circumstances, but it can also be influenced by inaccessible instructions, language barriers, disability-related access barriers, staff guidance, or channel design.
  - Adjusting for it may remove part of the very procedural inequity under review.

- **Missing applicant needs and eligibility facts**
  - The available attributes do not capture many potentially legitimate factors: program type, benefit category, legal eligibility details, document type, urgency, interpreter modality, accommodation type, literacy, internet access, immigration or identity-verification complexity, and changes in household circumstances.
  - Omitted legitimate factors can exaggerate apparent unfairness; omitted barriers can hide it.

- **Coarse resource-workload measurement**
  - Workload is observed only daily, not at the time a case entered a queue.
  - It cannot show intraday queues, staff absence, priority handling, case batching, actual availability, or the distinction between nominal assignment and active work.
  - Therefore, workload explanations are plausible but not fully demonstrable from these data.

- **No measure of support quality**
  - An event labeled “Language Support Contact” does not reveal whether interpretation was timely, accurate, culturally appropriate, or sufficient.
  - “Accommodation Review” does not show whether the requested accommodation was granted, effective, or delayed.

- **No decision-quality or dignity measure**
  - `case_outcome` records an endpoint, not whether the decision was legally correct, accurately explained, respectful, comprehensible, or based on complete evidence.
  - A similar approval rate could mask unequal treatment quality; a different approval rate could reflect legitimate eligibility differences.

- **Appeal data are incomplete as a quality indicator**
  - `appeal_flag` shows that an appeal occurred, not whether it was timely, accessible, successful, withdrawn, resolved, or caused by an error.
  - Appeal rates may also be affected by unequal knowledge of appeal rights or barriers to filing.

- **Causal ambiguity**
  - The event log is observational. Differences in activity paths, waits, and outcomes may reflect applicant needs, operational constraints, policy rules, staff behavior, or unmeasured factors.
  - It can establish disparity patterns and identify plausible mechanisms, but it cannot independently establish intent or legal discrimination.

- **Group labels are broad**
  - Groups may be heterogeneous. For example, Group B combines language preference, mixed channel use, regional variation, and possibly many distinct communities.
  - Aggregate comparisons can hide both severe local harms and areas with equitable performance.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation | Purpose | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add bilingual language-support capacity | Reduce the 3.8-day wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment and reduce assignment concentration among seven workers. | Hiring or contracting interpreters can be costly; poorly trained contractors may reduce quality; capacity increases should preserve confidentiality and interpretation standards. |
| Add accommodation-review capacity | Reduce Group C’s 4.7-day post-accommodation-review wait and relieve the five-specialist bottleneck. | Additional specialists may be difficult to recruit; accelerating review without adequate expertise could lead to poor accommodations or inconsistent decisions. |
| Separate specialist support from supervisor-review workload | Prevent the same accommodation specialists from becoming a dual bottleneck. | More specialization can increase staffing costs and may create new handoff points unless ownership is redesigned. |
| Establish service-level targets for support steps | Set explicit targets for time to language contact, time to accommodation review, and time from support completion to eligibility assessment. Monitor median and high-percentile delays. | Targets can encourage superficial completion, premature closure, or gaming unless paired with quality metrics and exception handling for genuinely complex cases. |
| Introduce queue-priority protections for rights-preserving steps | Ensure that applicants requiring support are not penalized through longer waits merely because they need support. | Priority rules may increase waits for other cases; design should be transparent, capacity-aware, and based on legal and service obligations. |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers | Use case ownership, integrated specialist consultation, shared case notes, warm handoffs, or specialist co-review to reduce general  specialist  general transfers. | End-to-end ownership may increase training needs and workload for generalists; overly broad access to specialist information can raise privacy concerns. |
| Improve accessible and translated intake materials | Reduce avoidable incomplete-documentation findings and repeated document requests. | Translation, usability testing, accessible formatting, and continuous content maintenance require investment; simpler forms may not eliminate genuinely necessary evidence requirements. |
| Improve decision explanations and notices | Provide plain-language, translated, and accessible explanations of decisions, missing documents, appeal rights, and next steps. | More detailed notices may require staff time and legal review; explanations must avoid revealing sensitive fraud-detection logic or creating security risks. |
| Monitor adjusted disparities routinely | Track outcome, timing, routing, handover, rework, and appeal disparities within comparable case strata and regions. | Repeated monitoring can create false precision if data quality is weak; metrics may be gamed if used punitively without contextual review. |
| Audit risk-score bands | Test calibration, disparate impact, error rates, stability, and routing consequences by group and region. Consider whether the score is appropriate for procedural triage. | Removing or weakening a risk score may increase missed fraud or shift discretion to staff; replacement systems can reproduce similar bias if not carefully governed. |
| Audit documentation-completeness determinations | Examine whether completeness standards and staff coding are consistent across language, channel, disability, and region. | More review can slow processing if implemented as an additional mandatory step; targeted sampling and feedback may be preferable to universal second review. |
| Add quality review of appeals and rework | Analyze appeal outcomes, reversals, reasons, notice quality, and repeated-document patterns. | Review capacity is needed, and appeals may take longer initially if the organization expands quality checks without resourcing them. |
| Publish internal fairness dashboards by region and pathway | Make bottlenecks and support-step disparities visible to managers. | Small subgroup counts can create privacy or statistical-instability concerns; use suppression rules and uncertainty intervals. |

The central design principle is not to remove Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review to shorten cycle times. Those are rights-preserving functions. The goal is to make them timely, integrated, sufficiently staffed, and less administratively burdensome.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Expected columns
CASE_ID = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

df = pd.read_csv("benefits_event_log.csv")
df[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(df[TIMESTAMP], utc=True, errors="coerce")

# Preserve event-level and case-level attributes.
# PM4Py's dataframe formatting uses the selected key columns.
log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id=CASE_ID,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP
)

# Confirm chronological event order within each case
log_df = log_df.sort_values([CASE_ID, TIMESTAMP]).copy()
```

Examples of fairness-relevant questions:

- Does Language Support Contact consistently precede substantive eligibility decisions for cases needing it?
- Does Accommodation Review occur before Decision Draft when accommodation is requested?
- Are document requests followed by a renewed completeness or eligibility assessment?
- Are appeals followed by Appeal Review?
- Are there group-specific loops such as repeated document requests or repeated supervisor review?
- Are support activities skipped more often in certain regions or channels?
- Do support cases show unexpected sequences, such as decision drafting before support completion?

These checks should be interpreted cautiously. A constraint violation may be an error, a logging gap, a legitimate exception, or an alternative valid process. Investigate with case review rather than assuming nonconformance equals unfairness.

## I. Statistical analysis outside PM4Py

PM4Py identifies process patterns; regression, matching, survival analysis, and uncertainty estimation should be performed with statistical tools such as `statsmodels`, `scikit-learn`, `lifelines`, or causal-inference libraries.

Recommended models:

- Logistic or doubly robust models for approval, document request, supervisor review, and appeal.
- Survival or accelerated-failure-time models for time to decision and time between specific activities.
- Multilevel models with random effects for region, worker/team, and month.
- Matched within-stratum comparisons for Group A versus B, A versus C, and A versus D.
- Bootstrap confidence intervals for group and pathway differences.
- Sensitivity analyses with and without risk score and documentation completeness.

The reporting output should include effect estimates, confidence intervals, sample sizes, overlap diagnostics, and explicit statements about residual confounding.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence indicates meaningful procedural-fairness concerns, especially for Groups B and C, rather than a basis for a definitive conclusion of intentional discrimination.

Some routing differences appear legitimate: Group D’s greater fraud-review use is consistent with higher measured risk, Group B’s language-support routing is appropriate, and Group C’s accommodation review is rights-preserving. Raw approval-rate differences therefore should not be treated as proof of discrimination.

However, the process appears to impose disproportionate burden on applicants who need language support or accommodations. Groups B and C have longer adjusted cycle times, concentrated waits after support steps, more handovers, limited specialist capacity, and no dedicated timeliness targets for those rights-preserving services. Group B also retains a notable adjusted approval and appeal disparity, while Group C has the largest adjusted delay and highest rework rate.

For Group D, the evidence is more indeterminate: risk-based routing may be justified, but the risk score cannot be assumed neutral, and the elevated adjusted appeal rate warrants audit.

Overall, the most supportable conclusion is that the process shows credible signs of procedurally inequitable design and resource allocation, particularly in support-related pathways. Further matched, region-specific, resource-specific, and sensitivity analyses are needed before attributing residual disparities to discrimination or concluding that all differences are explained by legitimate complexity.
