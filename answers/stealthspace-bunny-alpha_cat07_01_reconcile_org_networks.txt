# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

**Overall interpretation:** Mira_Coordinator’s strongest provisional description is a **cross-team exception and triage coordination point**. She connects automated routing, adjusters, senior approvers, and specialists, particularly in difficult cases. This is a functional interpretation—not evidence of a formal job title, managerial authority, or undesirable performance.

The apparent network contradiction is largely a distinction between **making connections between resources** and **sharing a case with the same resources**.

## handover-of-work network

**What it indicates**

- A handover edge represents resource A being followed by resource B in the event sequence. Mira’s handover out-degree of **0.82**, in-degree of **0.51**, and betweenness of **0.77** identify her as a major structural routing point.
- Her incoming edge from Bot_Rules has frequency **920**, compared with 610 to Alice and 590 to Bruno. She also connects to medical and legal specialists and to all four adjusters.
- Her return edges from Omar and Priya—160 and 140 occurrences—support a pattern of routing difficult work to specialists and subsequently receiving their responses.
- Thus, her organizational importance may consist largely of **matching work to resources and reconnecting dispersed expertise**, rather than continuously working alongside those resources.

**What it does not establish**

- A next-event relationship does not necessarily prove an explicit assignment, actual work handoff, or formal supervisory authority.
- The network does not measure whether routing decisions were correct, timely, equitable, or compliant.
- Her centrality partly reflects her position between several resource groups. The graph alone cannot distinguish a necessary coordination function from excessive intermediation.
- Automation and vendors belong in this network too. Bot_Rules is itself a routing hub, while Vendor_Pay is a terminal dependency with substantial inbound connectivity but no outbound handover.

## working-together network

**What it indicates**

- Mira’s working-together weighted degree is **0.16**, compared with **0.46** for Alice, **0.43** for Bruno, and approximately **0.47–0.49** for the two senior adjusters.
- Her co-case rates with the team leads are only 5% and 4%, and her strongest adjuster co-case rates are 8% and 7%. She is therefore less embedded in the recurring North and South case-handling pairs.
- This is consistent with a **transition-based role**: she enters a case, performs a routing or exception-handling event, and leaves while another resource performs the next substantive activity.

**What it does not establish**

- Low case co-occurrence is not evidence of low contribution. A resource can coordinate a case without remaining actively involved alongside its handler.
- The reported percentages appear to use **all claims as the denominator**, not only the approximately 22% of claims Mira touches. Consequently, the percentages understate her interaction intensity within her own case portfolio.
- A low score does not establish that she is merely administrative, nor that her coordination is unnecessary.
- Short event durations can help explain low **event-based** collaboration measures, but do not automatically explain low **case-based** co-occurrence: a short event still makes the resource present in that case. Both measurement granularity and actual case participation need examination.

## subcontracting network

**What it indicates**

- The Mira–Omar and Mira–Priya patterns—126 and 98 occurrences—show that her resource sequence often returns to her after specialist activity.
- Her fragments connect missing-information or complaint handling with medical/legal review and subsequent reopening or assignment. This supports expertise in **specialist coordination and returning difficult cases to an active queue**.
- Her subcontracting betweenness of **0.69** reflects the importance of these connections in the return-pattern network.
- Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules, occurring 62 times, provides an additional possible automation-monitoring or exception-feedback mechanism.

**What it does not establish**

- These patterns do not establish financial subcontracting, employment relationships, or a subordinate–supervisor hierarchy.
- A return to Mira may represent legitimate exception handling rather than repeated unnecessary work.
- The counts are modest relative to her total activity, and n=2 patterns are sensitive to event ordering and logging conventions.
- The fragments do not prove that she personally possesses specialist medical or legal expertise; they may show that she knows whom to involve.

## performance/time statistics

**What it indicates**

- Mira handles 960 events across 905 distinct cases. Her involvement in 22% of all claims, alongside involvement in 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, demonstrates concentration in problematic or atypical work.
- The reported **48% exception share** should be read as applying to her case portfolio, not as saying she handles 48% of all exception cases.
- During her six-day absence, exception cases accumulated in queues and their 90th-percentile cycle time increased from **8.4 to 13.9 days**. This is a meaningful warning about dependency on her coordination function.
- Her case-count percentile of 0.91 indicates high caseload relative to other resources, but not necessarily excessive individual workload.

**What it does not establish**

- The unchanged median for ordinary low-value claims limits any claim that Mira is a universal cycle-time bottleneck. The evidence is more consistent with a possible **exception-tail bottleneck** than an across-the-board delay.
- Her median of 4.2 minutes per event does not show that a case requires only 4.2 minutes of coordination. Investigation, queue time, informal communication, and work outside the log may be omitted.
- Handover elapsed times are not her service times. For example, 42 minutes from Mira to Omar may include other events, specialist availability, or waiting.
- High variance in cycle-time contribution could reflect her difficult case mix rather than inconsistent performance.
- Other resources contribute substantial time: Vendor_Verify has a 2.1-hour median per event, Vendor_Pay 1.3 hours, Priya 51 minutes, and Omar 37 minutes. These are not all human active-work times, and they show why a complete queue analysis must include external and automated resources.
- Medical and legal cases have higher reported exception shares than Mira’s portfolio. Her involvement is therefore not the sole source of exception routing or specialist workload.

## role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

**What it could indicate**

- Activity-profile similarity could test whether Mira resembles formal coordinators, exception handlers, or a hybrid group. Her recorded activities include clarification, reopening, complaint escalation, and specialist assignment.
- Organizational role discovery could identify whether similar work is performed by other resources, particularly temporary staff or team leads during exceptions.
- Including non-event attributes such as `case_type`, priority, and region may help distinguish an exception-desk role from a claims-adjuster role.

**What it does not establish**

- No discovered role clustering is actually supplied, so no role label should be asserted from these tools.
- Mira’s assignment-heavy, relatively sparse activity profile could place her in a mixed cluster, a separate coordinator cluster, or an outlier category, depending on features and parameters.
- A role cluster is descriptive, not a job title, performance rating, or ground truth.
- Bots and vendors should not automatically be interpreted as members of human organizational roles merely because they share activity profiles.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

These hypotheses are **not mutually exclusive**. Mira could be a formal exception coordinator, an informal knowledge broker, and the main human fallback for automation.

## 2.1 Formal coordinator / exception desk

- **Mechanism:** She handles missing information, reopening, complaints, and assignment within defined escalation procedures, delegating work to teams and specialists.
- **Supporting evidence:** Her exception-related activity profile, ownership of 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, and routing immediately after Bot_Rules fit an exception-desk function.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** Her limited team-lead co-case participation, relatively sparse recorded involvement per case, and absence of formal decision-rights documentation do not establish a formal coordinating position. The process owner’s description also challenges that interpretation.
- **Future predictions:** Most of her work would concentrate in exception variants; routine claims would rarely require her; exception demand would track incoming exception volume; and a suitably authorized substitute could process similar exceptions without a comparable increase in ordinary-case delays.

## 2.2 Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

- **Mechanism:** Although individual routing events are short, she absorbs the decisions and coordination needed to restart cases, locate specialists, and manage exceptions. Incoming demand may therefore exceed her effective coordination capacity.
- **Supporting evidence:** High handover centralities, the high reported cycle-time variance, and the exception-queue deterioration during her absence are consistent with constrained coordination capacity.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** There is no direct queue-utilization series showing sustained overload before the absence. The absence is observational, and her 4.2-minute median does not capture all of her work.
- **Future predictions:** Exception arrivals would build a visible queue ahead of her; delays would increase disproportionately when arrival volume or case complexity rises; and transferring her work without backup capacity would lengthen queue residence or time to specialist assignment. Capacity pressure could appear despite short individual service events.

## 2.3 Organizational boundary spanner or triage broker

- **Mechanism:** She translates between automated decisions, regional teams, adjusters, and specialist functions, using knowledge of where difficult cases should go rather than performing a fixed queue-cleaning task.
- **Supporting evidence:** Her many transient handovers, low case-level collaboration, specialist return patterns, and the adjusters’ description of her as the person who knows where hard cases belong all support this interpretation.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** The strongest collaboration is instead observed within the North and South pairs. The evidence does not yet show which boundaries she spans most effectively, or whether her matching decisions improve outcomes.
- **Future predictions:** Her bridges would connect resources with otherwise limited direct collaboration; routes involving both adjuster and specialist groups would disproportionately pass through her; and reassigning boundary-crossing cases without transferring her knowledge would increase re-escalations or specialist search time.

## 2.4 Automation monitor / quality gatekeeper

- **Mechanism:** She reviews automated exception decisions, resolves ambiguous routing, supplies missing information, and returns appropriate cases to automated or manual processing.
- **Supporting evidence:** Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules appears 62 times, and her activity profile contains several problem-resolution events.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** Bot_Rules handles more exception-associated events overall, and the evidence does not show that Mira overrides automated decisions frequently or correctly. Reopening and complaint handling also extend beyond simple automation monitoring.
- **Future predictions:** Her return-to-Bot_Rules patterns would concentrate in cases with recorded overrides, missing automation inputs, or later complaints/rework. Comparable exception decisions made by leads or rules would follow similar documented criteria.

## 2.5 Instrumentation or activity-granularity explanation

- **Mechanism:** Assignment, clarification, and reopening are recorded as separate events, while substantive work is performed elsewhere. This can increase handover degree without implying continuous involvement or a uniquely large decision-making role.
- **Supporting evidence:** Mira has 960 events but only 905 distinct cases, a pattern compatible with sparse touches in many cases and repeat intervention in exceptional ones. The separate “Assign Specialist” event is especially relevant.
- **Contradicting or limiting evidence:** The low co-case rate is a genuine case-level observation; short durations alone cannot explain it. Nothing establishes that the event capture is inaccurate.
- **Future predictions:** Consolidating routing events or distinguishing assignment from execution would substantially reduce her apparent handover degree only if apparent centrality came mainly from event fragmentation. Stable decision responsibility after such consolidation would support a genuine coordinating role.

# 3. Risk assessment

## Bottleneck risk

There is a plausible **exception-queue bottleneck**, but it is not established as a general claims bottleneck. Her outgoing hub position concentrates coordination work, and the absence-period queue increase is a warning signal. The unchanged ordinary-claim median shows that the impact is selective. Queue arrival rates, service capacity, and residence times are needed before labeling her “the bottleneck.”

## Coordination risk

Low sustained co-case participation creates a possible risk of lost context: the person identifying the problem may not be the person executing the next step. Her return patterns suggest feedback is possible, but do not prove that information is complete or decisions are consistently reapplied. Conversely, a persistent coordinator may deliberately reduce information loss by standardizing difficult transitions.

## Key-person risk

The absence evidence indicates a resilience concern. Automated rules and vendors perform identifiable processing tasks, but they are not demonstrated substitutes for the contextual knowledge needed to resolve ambiguous exceptions. A backup must be able to make and record routing decisions—not merely forward messages.

## Compliance and control risk

The compliance concern is a **design risk**, not evidence of wrongdoing. Mira may help detect problems and select a destination without herself authorizing settlement or payment. Human-in-the-loop routing can be entirely legitimate.

Nevertheless, a broad discretion over specialist selection could create inconsistent treatment, undocumented overrides, or conflicts of interest. Compliance should test whether routing follows documented policy, whether the necessary authorization is present, and whether similarly situated cases receive consistent treatment. Actual evidence of circumvention or favoritism has not been supplied.

## Workload fairness risk

Mira’s disproportionate involvement in reopened and complaint cases may reflect expertise, but it may also concentrate cognitively difficult work in one person. Her 48% exception rate should not be interpreted as 48% of all exceptions, and high activity volume does not establish overload without active hours, case complexity, and unrecorded work.

Conversely, the 62% and 71% exception rates for medical and legal portfolios show that other resources also receive difficult work. Queue-cleaning or escalation activity should not automatically be treated as low-value or discouraged through performance targets. The present event log also lacks demographic or similarly relevant attributes needed for a fairness assessment.

## Data-quality and interpretation risk

Several issues can distort the role assessment:

- Event time, service time, queue time, and complete-case cycle time are different measures.
- Explicit assignment events can inflate apparent handover activity.
- Case-level co-occurrence and event-level collaboration have different denominators.
- Normalized scores from different graphs are not directly comparable.
- The observed activity **“Assign Specialist”** is not in the enumerated activity catalog and should be reconciled.
- A resource name may represent a person, service, or account rather than an individual worker.
- The reported average cycle-time contribution needs a documented calculation and treatment of parallel or repeated events.

These risks call for better measurement, not an assumption that Mira’s recorded behavior is unrepresentative.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

## 4.1 Construct comparable, validated logs

Use `pm4py.format_dataframe` to standardize timestamps, resource identifiers, case identifiers, and lifecycle fields. Check start/complete pairing, invalid intervals, missing destinations, resource renaming, and concurrency.

Reconcile “Assign Specialist” with the activity catalog. Preserve complete cases for outcome analysis; a log containing only exception-flagged events is suitable for some exception-event questions but not for normal end-to-end cycle-time comparisons.

## 4.2 Repeat the handover analysis with time and context

Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` on the full directed graph and on explicitly defined exception subsets.

Retain all relevant resources—including bots, temporaries, and vendors—and record filtering and minimum-support parameters. Examine:

- Bot_Rules  Mira routing volume.
- Queue time before Mira.
- Mira  adjuster and specialist routing.
- Specialist and temporary-staff return paths.
- Handover distributions, not only averages.
- Network changes before, during, and after her absence.

Use `pm4py.discover_network_analysis` and `pm4py.view_sna` or `pm4py.save_vis_sna` for complementary degree, betweenness, and path inspection. Ensure that direction and edge definitions remain comparable across periods; otherwise apparent centrality changes may be artifacts.

## 4.3 Distinguish weak ties from lack of contribution

Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network`, but also construct case-level co-occurrence sets with closely related Python tools.

For each pair, compare:

- Co-case rate over all claims.
- Co-case rate among Mira’s cases.
- Co-case rate among the partner’s cases.
- Conditional overlap or Jaccard similarity.
- Co-case rates restricted to the same exception, case type, and time period.

This tests whether Mira is a weak-tie broker rather than merely absent from collaboration. It also prevents stable regional pairings from being mistaken for evidence of superior performance.

## 4.4 Test the robustness of subcontracting patterns

Use `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with n=2 and additional window sizes, such as n=3 or n=4, and compare overlapping with disjoint patterns.

Check whether:

- Mira’s specialist patterns persist at larger windows.
- The Bot_Rules return pattern is associated with documented exceptions.
- The patterns remain within the same case under concurrent-event handling.
- Alternative coordinators show comparable return behavior.
- Results remain stable under minimum-support thresholds and temporal resampling.

The aim is to identify a recurring coordination mechanism, not to label resources as subcontractors.

## 4.5 Test organizational roles and resource similarity

Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` on human resources, with bots and vendors analyzed separately or explicitly tagged.

Compare results across:

- Activity frequencies and case-normalized frequencies.
- Activity-profile and event-count-based similarity.
- Different role counts and time periods.
- Ordinary and exception cases.
- Stable versus changing assignments.

Validate candidate roles through a facilitated review of actual responsibilities. Do not treat the algorithm’s cluster as the sole organizational design.

## 4.6 Make filtered comparisons explicit

Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` for `exception_flag`, priority, amount band, region, channel, and case type. Use `pm4py.filter_variants` to retain complete traces matching specified activity sequences—for example, standard handling versus missing-information, reopening, or complaint variants.

Compare networks and outcomes within the **same cohort definition**. For exception-routing questions, distinguish filtering to flagged events from filtering to whole cases that contain a flagged event.

Analyze several six-day or weekly windows, including the absence period and comparable weekdays, rather than relying on one before/after comparison.

## 4.7 Separate service time from waiting time

Use `pm4py.get_service_time` with explicit lifecycle-pairing rules, and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` for consistent waiting-time calculations.

Measure, by resource and activity:

- Active service or completion intervals.
- Time awaiting assignment.
- Time awaiting specialist or adjuster availability.
- Case-level elapsed and waiting time.
- Median, 90th, and 95th percentiles.

Do not automatically interpret every gap between successive events as queue time; first establish what happened during that gap. Include Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay waiting as well as human queues. Repeated events within a case must not be summed as if they were independent claims.

## 4.8 Test capacity and change effects

If staffing or scheduling data are available, relate event and exception volume to active hours, shifts, specialist capacity, and concurrent case load. Use case-level bootstrapping for uncertainty and an interrupted time-series or matched-period analysis around the absence.

Such comparisons remain observational, but they can test whether:

- Queue residence, not coordinator service time, explains the tail.
- Ordinary and exception cases respond differently.
- Similar complexity and priority cases experience similar outcomes.
- A backup reduces the tail without increasing rework, complaints, or specialist overload.

A queue-state event or case-state history is needed before treating time between events as time spent unassigned.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

- **Retain and document the coordination function; do not remove Mira from routing as an isolated intervention.**
- Establish a provisional role description covering exception triage, clarification, reopening, complaints, specialist matching, and escalation tracking. This need not imply a reporting-line change.
- Define decision rights and an auditable record: exception reason, information requested, owner, due date, destination, routing rationale, policy or authorization reference, and any override.
- Have compliance review whether routing criteria and authorization controls are consistent, especially for medical, legal, and complaint cases. Distinguish detection, recommendation, and approval where necessary; do not impose separation on every routine decision without evidence.
- Create and train at least one qualified primary backup or backup pool. The backup must be able to exercise the same documented decision rights.
- Introduce queue-age and workload alerts for exception cases, including time awaiting Mira and time awaiting a specialist.

## What should be monitored

Track ordinary and exception cases separately, using consistent definitions:

- Queue arrival rate, residence time, backlog, and staffing coverage.
- Median and upper-percentile case cycle time.
- Reopening, repeat assignment, complaint, and escalation rates.
- Service and waiting time for Mira, team leads, adjusters, specialists, and vendors.
- Routing consistency, documented overrides, and later rework.
- Workload per active hour and specialist capacity.
- Performance of the backup during shadow coverage or planned absences.

Treat formal escalation as a potentially necessary protective action, not automatically as inefficiency. Vendor delays and automated exception quality should be monitored alongside human coordination.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

Do not yet:

- Remove Mira, eliminate her authority, or reduce her headcount based on centrality or the owner’s “queue cleaner” characterization.
- Declare her a formal team lead, quality gatekeeper, or management role based solely on network position.
- Replace her with automation without testing exception accuracy, override handling, and queue capacity.
- Create another single point of failure while removing her.
- Judge performance from co-case rates, raw event counts, or role-discovery clusters.
- Infer misconduct or unfairness from exception concentrations.

Any future removal or automation should first demonstrate a replacement for **exception recognition, missing-information coordination, specialist selection, escalation tracking, and contextual routing knowledge**. A practical pilot could transfer a controlled share of decisions to trained backups under common decision records, with predefined checks for delay, quality, compliance, and specialist overload.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator appears to be the cross-team exception and triage point for difficult claims. Although involved in only 22% of claims, she handles 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Her many handovers, return patterns, and increased exception delays during her absence suggest a coordination dependency. Her low working-together score is therefore not contradictory: she often connects people briefly rather than handling a claim alongside them.

This does not prove that she is formally a team lead, intentionally delays cases, or bypasses controls. Her recorded events may not capture all investigation, waiting, and informal coordination. High exception rates for medical and legal cases also show that difficult work is concentrated in specialist handling.

Management should retain and document her routing function, clarify decision rights and audit trails, train qualified backup coverage, and measure queues and specialist capacity. Automation, role redesign, or staffing reductions should wait until the hidden work and routing rules are measured and a tested replacement is in place.
