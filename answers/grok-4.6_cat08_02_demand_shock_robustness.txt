# 1. Stress scenarios

The lab is not a single-volume system. Peak weeks already show that **weekly volume rises only about 43% (2,300 → 3,300) while Urgent volume more than doubles (~414 → ~957/week) and Complex-panel volume more than doubles (~299 → ~660/week). Routine weekly volume is almost unchanged.** Any redesign that treats “peak” as “more of the same” will protect the wrong work.

**S1 — Winter infection wave (mix shift, not just volume).**  
Case mix moves toward the observed peak: Routine ~42%, Urgent ~29%, Complex-panel ~20%, Repeat-after-failure ~9%. Recollection after pre-analytical check rises from 4.2% to 8.0%; batch-wait incidence from 4.4% to 12.7%; assay-repeat from 6.7% to 14.8%; technical-validation → medical-validation from 24.5% to 42.3%; hold-for-specialist from 1.8% to 7.5%. Medical-validation completions roughly 2.5× weekly; specialist-hold entries ~5–6× weekly. Urgent p90 is already 33h against an 18h penalty SLA.

**S2 — Assay-machine degradation or M1 outage.**  
M1/M2 already sit at 98% peak utilization with an 18h median queue. M2 is faster but fails more; Repeat-after-failure cases already cluster after night shifts and M2 usage and consume assay capacity twice. An M1 outage, or an M2 failure spike, immediately lengthens `Batch wait → Assay run` (9h → 22h in peak) and `Repeat assay → Assay run` (10h → 26h). Repeats then re-enter the same scarce machines.

**S3 — Medical-validator shortfall or specialist-review absence.**  
Specialist review is already one person down every second Friday. Peak specialist utilization is 87% with a 36h median queue; `Hold for specialist review → Medical validation` stretches from 18h to 48h. Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need medical validation and 2.4× more likely to enter specialist hold. Urgent cases also require medical validation. Absence here does not slow Routine technical-release cases as much as it parks contractual and complex work in a long, invisible queue.

**S4 — Courier compression or delay.**  
Intake already spikes at 10:00 and 17:00. Peak courier utilization is 88% with a 7.5h median queue; `Receive sample → Pre-analytical check` grows from 2h to 9h. A weather/route event that merges those spikes, or delays morning tubes until afternoon, starts the Urgent 18h clock before the sample is even on a bench. Pre-analytical check (96% peak, 9.5h queue) then manufactures recollection loops (`Request recollection → Receive sample` 42h / 60h), and clerks lose intake capacity explaining errors to physicians.

**S5 — Compound Friday (realistic joint stress).**  
S1 mix + S4 morning delay + specialist off (known calendar) + elevated M2 repeats after a night shift. This is the scenario in which a policy that is “fine on average” fails: Urgent tokens, specialist holds, and assay repeats collide in the same 12–24h window.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Use the DFG frequencies, performance edges, and station table as a causal chain, not as an average cycle-time target.

**S1 — Mix shift.**  
Arrival smoothing does **not** fire first. The first overloaded stations are **pre-analytical check (96%, 9.5h)** and **assay machines (98%, 18h)**, then **technical validation (101%, 16h)** and **medical validation (93%, 22h)**. Evidence: more cases take `Technical validation → Medical validation` and `Hold for specialist review`; Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%, so even Urgent work is absorbed into `Test preparation → Batch wait` and `Batch wait → Assay run` (14h + 22h). Relieving registration (I8) or enlarging Routine batches (I2) without a downstream gate dumps more WIP onto benches and M1/M2. Strict Urgent-first (I1) clears the 18h tail for some Urgent orders but starves Routine (already 62h median / 120h p90 vs a 72h target) and Complex-panel (111h / 210h), raising total WIP. Adding only evening medical capacity (I5) shortens `Medical validation → Release result` (already only 2h / 5h) **after** a 24h tech-to-medical wait; the queue simply moves back to technical validation and assay repeats. Outsourcing Complex overflow (I10) removes specialist-hold volume (610 peak entries) but inserts a 12h transport delay into a path whose specialist wait is already 48h median—net-positive only when the hold queue dominates transport.

**S2 — Machine degradation.**  
The constraint is **M1/M2**, then **repeat-assay re-entry**, then **technical validation**. Peak `Assay run → Repeat assay` is 1,260 vs 7,240 clean handoffs (14.8%). Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex and sending all Routine to M2 (I3) protects critical slots **only if** M2 quality holds. The log says the opposite: repeats cluster on M2 and night shifts. A full I3 split therefore migrates the bottleneck from “Urgent waiting for M1” to “Routine-driven repeat storm that steals M1 back.” Larger batches (I2) improve setup efficiency at preparation (89%, 11h) but increase the cost of a failed batch and make Urgent bypass harder. Cross-training technical validators into accession (I4) does nothing for a machine constraint and removes people from a station already at 101%.

**S3 — Validator / specialist absence.**  
Bottleneck sequence: **specialist review (36h queue)** → **medical validation (22h)** → **technical validators holding cases they cannot release** → apparent assay idle-with-WIP (machines look busy because finished assays cannot leave). Complex-panel and Urgent concentrate here. I5 (evening medical shift) is the direct relief for medical-validator scarcity; it does **not** drain specialist hold (`Hold → Medical` 48h). If evening medical validators pull work that still needs the specialist, they idle or rubber-stamp. I10 is the specialist-specific relief. I1 does not help a medical/specialist constraint: Urgent cases still stop at medical validation. I4 is actively harmful: technical validation is the only automatic-release path for many Routine cases (11,700 normal `Technical validation → Release result` vs 4,050 in peak—the automatic-release share collapses). Removing technical validators lengthens the only path that does **not** need the missing specialist.

**S4 — Courier compression / delay.**  
First queue is **courier intake (7.5h)** and **receive → pre-analytical (9h)**. Pre-analytical failures then open two expensive loops: recollection (780 peak, 47% of which cancel rather than return) and manual accession correction (420 cases, only two specialists, 14h queue). Those loops re-enter `Receive sample` and consume clerks who should be clearing the next courier spike. I7 (slot smoothing) attacks the arrival shape. I6 (2-hour defect triage) cuts the 42–60h recollection cycle before tubes occupy preparation and assay. If I8 accelerates registration into this spike, pre-analytical utilization at 96% becomes a hard block and accession specialists (91%, 14h) become the second bottleneck. I1 at preparation cannot recover an Urgent sample that has not yet passed pre-analytical check.

**S5 — Compound Friday.**  
Bottlenecks appear **in parallel**, not in a single sequence: morning courier lump → pre-analytical/accession; night-shift M2 repeats still on the machines; specialist off → hold queue; Urgent SLA clocks already running. Any single-station heroics migrate load onto the next saturated resource. The only stable pattern is **contain defects early, meter arrivals, reserve a small amount of machine and medical capacity, and switch on overflow policies by trigger**—not a permanent Urgent-first or M2-for-all-Routine rule.

# 3. Robust redesign package

Do not add capacity everywhere. Build a **metered, multi-class flow** that stays acceptable when the mix, the machines, or the people move.

**R1 — Always-on early defect containment (I6, strengthened).**  
Reject or flag poor samples within 2 hours of receive, before test preparation. Peak recollection doubles (4.2% → 8.0%) and the recollection cycle is the longest common edge (42h / 60h). Every bad tube that reaches assay is a future repeat and a stolen Urgent slot.  
- *Why robust:* Helps S1–S5. It reduces wasted occupancy on the two scarcest peak resources (assays at 98%, technical validation at 101%) and reduces clerk time spent on physician explanations.  
- *Downstream risk:* Short-term rise in recollection requests and courier callbacks. Cap recollect communications (script + digital notice) so registration clerks are not absorbed.  
- *Assumption:* Pre-analytical staff can make a binary accept/reject in minutes; physicians accept a fast “recollect” over a late “failed assay.”  
- *Failure mode:* If triage is slow or timid, it becomes another 9.5h queue.

**R2 — Smooth and meter arrivals (I7 + gated I8).**  
Shift courier pickups off the 10:00 / 17:00 spikes. Automate registration only for complete digital metadata, **and release registered orders into receive/pre-analytical only when pre-analytical WIP is below a cap.**  
- *Why robust:* Attacks S4 directly and stops I8 from migrating the bottleneck onto a 96% station. Registration is only 85% / 2.8h—not the constraint.  
- *Downstream risk:* If clinics refuse new slots, smoothing fails; if the WIP cap is too tight, Urgent digital orders wait at registration while the 18h clock runs—so Urgent orders get a reserved intake token (see R3).  
- *Assumption:* Courier contracts and clinic draw times are negotiable.  
- *Failure mode:* Unmetered automation (raw I8).

**R3 — Protected slots, not a global Urgent-first rule (modified I1 + partial I3).**  
Create three virtual lanes with **daily reserved capacity**, not a strict heap:

| Lane | Prep / assay rule | Validation rule |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent | May bypass batch wait, but only inside a daily Urgent slot budget on M1 (and a small M2 reserve if M1 is down) | Evening medical capacity when triggers fire |
| Complex-panel | Booked into mixed M1 batches; never dumped entirely onto M2 | Specialist calendar reservation; overflow via I10 only on trigger |
| Routine | Batched on remaining M1 + M2 capacity; default batch size stays 12 | Technical validation → release remains the happy path |

Keep the existing Urgent bypass (74% normal) as a **controlled exception**, not as fragmentation of every batch. Do **not** dedicate all Routine volume to M2.  
- *Why robust:* Peak already shows what happens when bypass falls to 58% and machines sit at 98%: everyone waits 18h for assay. Slot reservation protects the 18h SLA without starving Routine/Complex the way I1 does. Partial M1 protection avoids the I3 repeat storm.  
- *Downstream risk:* Reserved empty Urgent slots look like “waste” on quiet mornings; over-reserving starves Routine. Technical validation remains at 101% unless assay outflow is paced.  
- *Assumption:* A simple token board (or LIS rule) can cap Urgent interruptions per batch.  
- *Failure mode:* Turning the slots into I1 (Urgent always jumps the queue) or into I3 (Routine only on M2).

**R4 — Accession flexibility without stripping technical validation (conditional I4).**  
Cross-train two technical validators so they **can** do accession correction, but treat them as a surge pool. Standing work of accession stays with the two specialists; digital-metadata checks from I8 and fast reject from I6 should shrink correction demand (peak 420 cases).  
- *Why robust:* Accession is a tiny pool (n=2) whose 14h queue blocks many otherwise-ready cases (`Manual accession correction → Test preparation` 5h / 16h). Capability is robust; permanent reassignment is not.  
- *Downstream risk:* Peak technical validation is already 101%. Deploying the pool on the wrong day lengthens `Assay run → Technical validation` (5h / 15h) and grows medical/specialist queues because cases never reach a releasable state.  
- *Assumption:* 4-week cross-training is real; deployment is gated (Section 4).  
- *Failure mode:* Using I4 as standing FTE transfer.

**R5 — Repeat containment at the source (operating rule, not a manager I-item).**  
After any night shift or M2-heavy window, require extra QC on the assay families that historically generate Repeat-after-failure. Prefer those assays on M1 when M1 has unreserved capacity. Repeat-after-failure is only 8–9% of mix but uses the machine twice and already has the worst times (70h / 145h median; 132h / 240h p90).  
- *Why robust:* Directly attacks S2 and the peak repeat rate jump (6.7% → 14.8%).  
- *Downstream risk:* Slightly lower M1 throughput for first-pass Routine; better than a 26h requeue.  
- *Assumption:* Repeat clustering by night/M2 is causal enough to steer scheduling.  
- *Failure mode:* Ignoring QC and “solving” repeats by adding I1 priority for repeats—those would then preempt fresh Urgent work.

**Explicitly reject as standing design:**  
- **I2 as a global 24-tube rule** — efficiency at prep, longer waits for anyone who cannot bypass; only a scenario policy.  
- **I9** — release is not the bottleneck (`Medical validation → Release result` is 2h / 5h). Holding Routine reports adds waiting to a class already missing its 72h p90 in peak.  
- **Permanent I1 or full I3.**  
- **Standing I5 / I10** — they are capacity/overflow and must be triggered, or they become idle cost and traceability debt.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

| Policy | When it turns ON | When it turns OFF | Intent and side-effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P-Urgent storm (time-boxed I1)** | ≥15% of in-lab Urgent cases have elapsed >12h, **or** Urgent median queue at prep+assay >6h | Urgent in-lab >12h share back under 8% for 24h | Temporary Urgent-first at prep/assay only. Accept Routine slip toward 72h. Do not apply I1 at medical validation (that would starve Complex). |
| **P-Routine efficiency (I2 = 24)** | Urgent arrivals <20% of daily mix **and** no P-Urgent storm **and** M1/M2 utilization <90% | Urgent mix ≥25% or any machine utilization ≥95% | Larger Routine batches only when they will not trap Urgent work. |
| **P-M1 critical reservation (stronger I3)** | M1 or M2 down, **or** assay utilization ≥95% **and** Urgent+Complex share ≥40% | Both machines up and assay utilization <90% for 48h | Raise M1 reservation for Urgent/Complex (e.g. 60–70% of slots). Keep a Routine trickle on M1 so M2 does not inherit all failure-prone volume. Watch repeat rate hourly. |
| **P-Accession surge (deploy I4 pool)** | Accession median queue >8h **and** technical-validation utilization <95% **and** tech-val queue <8h | Accession queue <4h **or** tech-val utilization ≥98% | Move at most one cross-trained validator, not two, unless both conditions are wide. |
| **P-Evening medical (I5)** | Medical-validation median queue >8h **or** count of Urgent cases waiting for medical validation > N (set N so 18h SLA is still reachable) **or** Complex-panel waiting for medical >1 working day | Queues below those bands for two consecutive shifts | Adds medical time without touching specialist. If the queue is mostly `Hold for specialist review`, I5 will idle—do not pay overtime for the wrong work. |
| **P-Complex overflow (I10)** | Specialist-review queue >24h **or** specialist absent (known Friday / sick) **and** Complex-panel not yet past assay **and** remaining time budget >12h transport | Specialist queue <12h and specialist present next two days | Send **unassayed or early-stage** Complex overflow only. Do not outsource cases already in specialist hold—you pay 12h and still need local sign-out. Require a chain-of-custody checklist; refuse overflow if partner cannot return structured results into the LIS. |
| **P-Intake shock (I7 emergency + pre-anal overtime)** | Courier queue >4h or two arrival waves merge into one | Next-day arrivals back on slotted times | Short overtime at pre-analytical **plus** freeze non-Urgent automated registration releases. Protects S4 from becoming S5. |

No policy may declare all four case types “priority.” Repeat-after-failure gets machine access only after first-pass Urgent slots, except when the repeat is itself an Urgent clinical sample.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Technical validation remains the hidden ceiling.** Peak utilization is already 101%. Every successful upstream fix (I6, I7, better assay yield, evening medical pulling work forward) increases finished assays arriving at a station with no slack. Cross-training (I4) makes this worse on the wrong day. Four-week cross-training **into** technical validation from a less scarce pool is the missing capability; the candidate list only trains the other direction.

2. **Specialist review is still a single-point calendar failure.** I10 only helps cases that have not already entered the 48h hold. A Friday absence during a Complex surge still parks in-flight panels locally.

3. **Urgent and Complex cannot be protected at once at medical validation.** Both need the same scarce medical/specialist time. R3 can split machines; it cannot split a missing specialist.

4. **Recollection physics.** I6 shortens detection but not geography. `Request recollection → Receive sample` stays a 42–60h external loop. Rural or delayed couriers will still blow Urgent SLAs for redrawn samples.

5. **Redesign-induced vulnerability: reserved capacity and overflow complexity.** Protected M1 slots will idle on a quiet morning and tempt supervisors to “lend them to Routine,” recreating I1/I3 thrash the next Urgent wave. I10 introduces a second chain of custody; a partner-mislabeled Complex panel is a new, low-frequency, high-severity failure mode that the current DFG does not contain. Gated I8 can hide a growing registration backlog that is not yet in the lab queues, so the dashboard must include **not-yet-received digital orders**.

6. **M2 quality vs speed is not designed out.** Until night-shift/M2 repeats fall, any plan that leans on M2 for volume (including partial I3) can re-create the Repeat-after-failure cluster that already shows the worst tail (240h p90).

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Monitor **daily in peak, intra-day on S5 days**. Leading indicators drive policy switches. Lagging metrics judge whether the redesign stayed acceptable.

**Leading (act the same day)**

| Signal | Warning band | Danger / policy trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Courier arrivals vs slot plan (counts at 10:00 and 17:00) | Wave >1.5× slotted capacity | Wave merge or queue >4h → P-Intake shock |
| Pre-analytical queue median / utilization | >4h or >90% | >8h or >95% → freeze non-Urgent I8 release; consider pre-anal overtime |
| Pre-analytical reject/recollect rate (I6) | >6% | >9% (above peak 8%) → sample-quality intervention with draw sites, not more assay time |
| Accession queue **and** tech-val utilization (paired) | Acc. >6h while TV <90% | Acc. >8h **and** TV <95% → P-Accession surge; **reverse** if TV ≥98% |
| Assay utilization + queue; Urgent bypass % | Util >90%; bypass <70% | Util ≥95% or bypass ≤58% (peak failure mode) → P-M1 reservation |
| Repeat rate by machine and shift | >10% | >14% or night/M2 cluster → tighten R5; block full I3 |
| Urgent elapsed-time histogram (in-lab, not yet released) | Share >12h exceeds 10% | Share >12h exceeds 15% **or** prep+assay Urgent wait >6h → P-Urgent storm |
| Medical-validation queue and Urgent-waiting-for-medical count | Queue >6h | Queue >8h → P-Evening medical **unless** most age is in specialist hold |
| Specialist-review queue / specialist present (Fri flag) | Queue >12h or next-day absence | Queue >24h or specialist out → P-I10 for early-stage Complex only |
| Reserved-slot waste (empty M1 Urgent tokens) | >20% tokens unused by 14:00 | Release unused tokens to Routine after 14:00 so R3 does not idle into a fake shortage |

**Lagging (review daily, decide weekly — do not steer hourly)**

- Urgent: % released ≤18h; median and p90 (peak reference 17h / 33h).  
- Routine: % ≤72h; median and p90 (peak 62h / 120h).  
- Complex-panel: median and p90 (peak 111h / 210h); % outsourced and partner TAT.  
- Repeat-after-failure cycle time (peak 145h / 240h).  
- Overall rework (7.5% → 11.8% is the peak warning); assay-repeat % (6.7% vs 14.8%).  
- % cases with at least one queue >12h (22% vs 54%).  
- Cancel rate after recollection (38% → 47% in peak): a quality-of-care and wasted-intake metric.  
- End-to-end throughput vs starts (peak release 8,040 / 9,900 = 81% vs 86% normal): WIP accumulation.

Acceptable under stress does **not** mean “restore the 38h Routine median.” It means: Urgent p90 back under contractual 18h; Routine p90 not left at 120h; Complex-panel not left in a 48h specialist hole; rework not allowed to stay at 11.8%; and no policy that fixes one class by silently parking the others.
