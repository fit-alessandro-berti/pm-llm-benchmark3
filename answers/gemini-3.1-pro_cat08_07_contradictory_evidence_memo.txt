# 1. Executive memo
To: Management Committee
From: Process Mining & Strategy Team
Subject: Decision Memo: Next Steps for Fiber-Installation Process Redesign R

Management must decide the future of the fiber-installation Redesign R. While the pilot successfully reduced median cycle times and handovers, it generated severe unintended consequences: worse tail durations, increased rework, premature billing, and lower customer satisfaction. The process-mining evidence reveals that Redesign R is not a universal solution; its effects bifurcate sharply based on customer segment and building risk.

We recommend **Alternative E: a hybrid approach**. Management should immediately scale the Redesign R framework (coordinator ownership and streamlined scheduling) exclusively for simple, low-risk houses, where it delivers clear value. Concurrently, we must roll back the risky elements of the pilot—specifically, survey skipping for apartments/old buildings and post-billing activation checks—while deploying targeted measurements to resolve remaining uncertainties.

The fundamental trade-off is between the speed and efficiency of a streamlined "happy path" versus the resilience and compliance required for complex installations. Treating all cases with a fast-track approach breaks down in the face of complexity. 

Process-mining artifacts highlight this breakdown clearly. The pilot's discovered model shows high fitness (0.94) but extremely low precision (0.51), indicating an under-constrained process. Skipping upfront checks (surveys and permits) leads to unstructured, ad-hoc rework loops (failed visits, manual activations). This is corroborated by the alignment diagnostics: frequent instances of billing preceding successful activation and missing permit checks for older buildings severely impact quality. 

Financially and operationally, the redesign trades visible upfront costs for hidden downstream costs. While visible cost dropped to EUR 161, the incomplete total cost estimate rose to EUR 207 due to second visits jumping from 11% to 18% and complaints rising to 9.8%. Furthermore, the organizational shift to single-coordinator ownership successfully eliminated handovers but created bottlenecks, pushing coordinator utilization to 93% and destroying resilience when staff are absent.

However, a complete rollback (Alternative B) would be a costly overreaction. Segment-level data proves that for simple houses, Redesign R reduced median duration by 6 days and improved first-time-right rates by 3 percentage points. These gains are too substantial to abandon. 

By adopting Alternative E, we secure the proven efficiency gains on simple cases while halting the operational bleeding on complex ones. The recommended immediate actions and measurement plan will establish the strict controls and cost transparency needed before any further scaling is considered.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

*   **High fitness but low precision:** The process model fits the log perfectly (high fitness) because it has devolved into a highly permissive "flower model." By allowing skipped steps (surveys) and relaxed constraints, the model accommodates almost any unstructured sequence of rework, manual intervention, and second visits, which inherently destroys precision.
*   **Improved median but worse tail duration:** The "happy path" for simple cases (which make up a large portion of the volume) is much faster due to merged scheduling and reduced handovers, driving down the median. However, when complex cases skip necessary prerequisites (surveys/permits), they enter chaotic rework loops or stall entirely, drastically inflating the tail (p90/p95).
*   **Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Single-coordinator ownership consolidates tasks, drastically reducing formal handovers. However, context is now siloed. At 93% utilization, coordinators have no slack; if a coordinator is absent, no one else has the context to advance the case, stalling work and destroying systemic resilience.
*   **Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost:** Upfront costs decreased because surveys were skipped and handover administration was eliminated. However, these savings merely shifted the cost downstream. Failed visits, manual activations, and complaint escalations are far more expensive to resolve than conducting a proactive survey, driving the true total cost higher.
*   **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** Simple houses are highly standardized, making predictive skipping and fast-tracking highly effective. Apartments and old buildings contain hidden complexities (permits, distinct wiring). Bypassing the survey in these segments guarantees that installers arrive unprepared, triggering failures, delays, and compliance breaches.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
*   **Advantages:** Maximizes median speed improvements and visible upfront cost reductions; simplifies the handover network universally.
*   **Risks:** Massively inflates tail durations, rework costs, and customer complaints; scales non-compliant behaviors like premature billing.
*   **Conditions:** Reasonable only if downstream rework was cheap and customer satisfaction was largely unaffected by early billing.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process.**
*   **Advantages:** Restores predictability, limits tail durations, and ensures compliance with necessary permit and activation steps.
*   **Risks:** Forfeits significant, proven median cycle time gains and first-time-right improvements for simple houses; returns to high-handover inefficiencies.
*   **Conditions:** Reasonable if the organization lacks the IT or operational capacity to run segment-specific process variants.

**C. Scale only the coordinator-ownership part, but suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
*   **Advantages:** Retains accountability benefits of single ownership while mitigating the severe rework and compliance risks of the pilot.
*   **Risks:** Coordinators remain a severe bottleneck (93% utilization) and a single point of failure; loses the scheduling speed for simple cases.
*   **Conditions:** Reasonable if handovers were the absolute primary driver of delays and coordinator capacity could be easily expanded.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
*   **Advantages:** Resolves data gaps (especially downstream costs and segment risk) before committing to structural changes.
*   **Risks:** Delays banking the clear, proven gains for simple houses; extends decision paralysis while absorbing experiment costs.
*   **Conditions:** Reasonable if there is deep uncertainty about whether the simple-house benefits are genuine or an artifact of the pilot.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and run measurement before wider scale.**
*   **Advantages:** Secures massive efficiency gains where proven (simple houses), stops the bleeding on complex cases, and enables targeted learning.
*   **Risks:** Running a bifurcated process increases operational complexity, routing logic overhead, and training requirements.
*   **Conditions:** Reasonable and optimal when segment differences are stark, identifiable upfront, and drastically alter the success of the process (as seen here).

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

1.  **M4. Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start:** Highest value. Resolves the critical ambiguity between visible savings (EUR 161) and actual costs (EUR 207+). Will definitively determine if Redesign R is financially viable.
2.  **M7. Run conformance monitoring for Billing start before Activation test and Permit check absence:** Crucial for immediate risk mitigation. Resolves uncertainty about the scale of non-compliance and allows management to enforce rules that prevent NPS destruction and regulatory issues.
3.  **M3. Add structured reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, and Second visit:** Highly valuable for root-cause analysis. Explains *why* the tail is blowing up and exactly what drives rework, dictating how to fix the complex-case process.
4.  **M6. Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes:** Essential for future scalability. Resolves whether the skip logic is fundamentally flawed or just poorly calibrated, determining if survey-skipping can ever be expanded to medium-risk cases.
5.  **M2. Instrument start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, and Manual activation:** Replaces assumptions with facts regarding bottleneck wait times. Resolves where exactly the delays occur within the active rework loops.
6.  **M5. Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership under coordinator absence:** Resolves the resilience vs. efficiency trade-off. Informs how to scale the organizational model without creating single points of failure.
7.  **M1. Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum:** Lower immediate value. The pilot already showed the rule failed for medium-risk cases; validating the rule (M6) should precede further randomized trials.
8.  **M8. Collect customer experience separately for "speed to appointment" and "working service after installation":** Lowest value. Complaints already indicate that "installed but not working" and early billing are the core issues; separating the NPS score provides lagging confirmation rather than actionable operational direction.

# 6. Final recommendation

We recommend executing **Alternative E (Hybrid Approach)**.

*   **Immediate action:** Deploy Redesign R's fast-track flow exclusively for simple, low-risk houses.
*   **What to pause:** Immediately suspend the predictive survey-skipping rule for apartments and old buildings. Immediately halt the practice of initiating Billing start before a successful Activation test network-wide.
*   **What to scale:** Scale the coordinator-ownership model and the merged address validation/appointment scheduling step nationally, but strictly limit this to the simple house segment.
*   **What to measure next:** Execute M4 (full downstream costs), M7 (conformance monitoring on billing/permits), and M3 (structured reason codes for rework).
*   **What threshold or evidence would justify broader rollout:** Broader rollout of survey skipping requires M6 validation showing >90% accuracy in predicting zero-rework installations. Scaling coordinator ownership to complex cases requires M4 to prove end-to-end costs remain lower than baseline, and M5 to demonstrate that absence-handling protocols prevent SLA breaches.
