# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed trajectory of service time and error rates cannot be attributed to a single mechanism. Instead, it emerges from the dynamic interplay of selection bias, learning, queue pressure, fatigue, and automation dependencies across the shift lifecycle.

**Early shift (positions 1–15):** Service times are highest (~18.8 min) and error rates moderate (~3.4%). This period is dominated by **case-mix/selection** and **learning**. Backlog from previous shifts arrives first, carrying higher complexity (0.72) and priority (41%). Seniors front-load complex cases to clear urgent items. Resources are still warming up, but low queue pressure (WIP ~46) allows thorough processing. The combination of difficult cases and initial calibration explains the slower throughput without excessive errors.

**Mid shift (positions 16–50):** Service times drop to their minimum (~12.9–15.2 min) and error rates reach their lowest (~2.5–2.8%). **Learning/warm-up** peaks, complexity naturally declines (0.540.50), and queue stabilizes (31–33). This is the optimal operating zone: resources have internalized routines, cases are simpler, and pressure is manageable. Speed and quality align.

**Late shift (positions 51–76+):** Service times fall further (~10.8–11.4 min) but error rates spike dramatically (4.6%7.9%, rework 8.2%13.7%). This divergence occurs because **queue pressure** overrides learning and complexity effects. WIP surges to 88, forcing workers to truncate steps, batch activities, or skip thorough validation. The speed is not pure learning; it is pressure-induced throughput acceleration. Simultaneously, **fatigue** erodes cognitive control, and **automation interaction** amplifies errors: Bot_Risk outputs are corrected more frequently under high queue load, creating correction bursts. **Batching/workarounds** (e.g., delayed Quality Checks) concentrate rework into the final hours. Circadian dips further degrade attention during these high-pressure windows.

Thus, service time falls continuously due to learning, complexity reduction, and pressure, but errors only rise when pressure and fatigue cross a threshold that compromises process adherence. The system shifts from complexity-dominated early on, to learning-stabilized mid-shift, to pressure-fatigue-automation dominated late.

Move complex cases away from the first hour** | Smooths workload distribution; reduces early error risk and senior burnout. | Delays high-priority resolution; may increase early WIP and backlog carryover. | Complexity distribution over shift, early-hour error rate, queue length at 60 min, priority SLA adherence. |
| **3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70** | Triggers proactive resource reallocation or pacing; prevents speed-quality collapse. | Alert fatigue; may cause premature breaks or artificial pacing that reduces throughput. | Alert trigger frequency, post-alert error rate, service time stability, rework rate in alert windows. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases** | Eliminates selection bias; reveals true capacity and learning curves. | Ignores priority/complexity routing rules; may increase rework and SLA breaches. | Case completion time, error rate by resource, priority adherence rate, senior utilization balance. |
| **5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in final two hours** | Reduces fatigue-driven errors; improves cognitive reset without full shift cuts. | Temporarily increases service time; may not address root cause (pressure); scheduling complexity. | Error rate post-break, service time during/after break, rework rate, worker self-reported fatigue scores. |
| **6. Defer low-priority quality checks to next shift** | Smooths end-of-shift QC bursts; reduces correction clustering. | Delays feedback loop; may let errors propagate to downstream processes; compliance risk. | Downstream error rate, QC backlog size, rework cycle time, compliance audit findings. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Absorbs complex/urgent cases; reduces pressure on mid/junior staff during peaks. | Higher labor cost; may create dependency on seniors; scheduling complexity. | Senior utilization %, error rate during peaks, queue length at shift end, rework distribution by resource type. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | Incentivizes quality maintenance. | Encourages gaming (e.g., case deferral); ignores systemic pressure; unethical; increases attrition. | Error rate trends, attrition/turnover, quality vs speed balance, rework root cause analysis, worker sentiment surveys. |

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level performance conclusions drawn from raw event logs are frequently misleading when mechanisms are conflated. **Selection bias** is pervasive: early-shift cases are systematically more complex and urgent, while later shifts inherit stabilized or simpler workloads. Attributing speed differences to worker skill ignores this case-mix shift. **Measurement error** in timestamps, error flags, and queue snapshots can distort trajectories; automated error detection may miss latent defects, while human flags may be applied inconsistently under pressure. **Queue pressure is often a management-created condition**: staffing ratios, WIP targets, and shift handover policies directly dictate workload intensity. Blaming workers for late-shift errors ignores that pressure forces process truncation, which is a system design failure, not individual negligence. **Automation quality** introduces hidden dependencies: Bot_Risk outputs require human validation, and correction rates spike under load. This amplifies error counts without reflecting worker competence. Finally, **punitive use of error rates** is ethically unsound and operationally counterproductive. Raw late-shift error rates conflate fatigue, pressure, batching, and automation corrections. Punishment incentivizes case deferral, reduces transparency, and increases turnover. Performance management must separate individual behavior from systemic constraints, use adjusted metrics, and focus on process redesign rather than worker accountability.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

| Indicator | Definition & Calculation | Visualization & Placement |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean Service Time** | Average duration between `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp` per activity/resource/shift. | Line chart (shift position), heat map (resource × shift) |
| **Case Waiting Time** | Time cases spend in queue before processing; computed via `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`. | Histogram + rolling average by shift hour |
| **Work-in-Progress (WIP)** | Real-time count of open cases; tracked via `wip_at_start` and live queue snapshots. | Gauge + time-series trend with threshold line at 70 |
| **Rework Rate** | Proportion of cases flagged `rework_flag` within 7 days of initial completion. | Stacked bar (by activity), trend over time |
| **Error Rate** | Proportion of events with `error_flag` = 1, stratified by resource, shift, and WIP band. | Dual-axis chart (error rate + service time) with WIP overlay |
| **Complexity Mix Index** | Weighted average of `case_complexity_score` across active cases; tracks case difficulty drift. | Area chart (shift progression), color-coded by priority share |
| **Batching Indicator** | Run-length variance of service times < 3 min; flags sequential batching behavior. | Scatter plot (service time vs sequence position), anomaly highlights |
| **Handover/Queue Ownership Changes** | Count of cases transferred or re-assigned at shift boundaries; tracks handover friction. | Timeline view at shift transitions, correlation with late-shift error spike |
| **Worker-Load Fairness** | Gini coefficient of cases/event time across resources; measures equity of distribution. | Gauge + threshold alert (>0.3 indicates imbalance) |

Dashboard should update hourly, allow drill-down by shift/resource/activity, and include automated alerts for WIP > 70, error rate > 5%, or batching anomalies. All metrics must be adjusted for complexity and priority to prevent misleading conclusions.
