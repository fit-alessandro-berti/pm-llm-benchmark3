# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

**Notation and assumptions**

Let \(c\) index cases and \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\) index branches. All durations below are in minutes. Times are treated as exact to the supplied one-minute resolution.

- **Assumption 1 — fork semantics:** `fork_release` is the earliest permissible branch start for all three branches. It need not mean that resources were available then.
- **Assumption 2 — join semantics:** `join_ready_c = max(A_end_c, B_end_c, C_end_c)`. This follows the stated rule that F may start only after all branches complete, but `join_ready` itself is inferred, not observed.
- **Assumption 3 — no hidden branch delay:** Within a branch interval, time is split only into pre-start waiting and start-to-end service. If branch work were preempted or paused, the observed start–end interval would overstate pure service; that cannot be checked here.
- **Assumption 4 — downstream definition:** `downstream_queue = final_start - join_ready` is a *residual waiting time under the stated rule*. Calling it a physical queue requires that no release policy, batching, setup, transport, assignment rule, or unlogged task intervenes. That is not verifiable from timestamps alone.

**Component formulas**

For case \(c\) and branch \(i\):

- Upstream elapsed time:
  \[
  upstream_c = fork\_release_c - arrival_c
  \]
  This is elapsed time before the fork, not necessarily all queueing; it may include preparation or service already completed upstream.

- Branch queue waiting:
  \[
  queue_{c,i} = start_{c,i} - fork\_release_c
  \]

- Branch active service, more precisely observed branch processing interval:
  \[
  service_{c,i} = end_{c,i} - start_{c,i}
  \]

- Branch completion time from fork:
  \[
  response_{c,i} = queue_{c,i} + service_{c,i} = end_{c,i} - fork\_release_c
  \]

- Join-ready time:
  \[
  join\_ready_c = max_i(end_{c,i}) = fork\_release_c + max_i(response_{c,i})
  \]

- Branch synchronization waiting after completion:
  \[
  sync_{c,i} = join\_ready_c - end_{c,i} \ge 0
  \]
  For the critical branch, \(sync=0\) apart from ties.

- Elapsed fork–join stage time:
  \[
  forkjoin_c = join\_ready_c - fork\_release_c = max_i(queue_{c,i}+service_{c,i})
  \]

- Downstream waiting before F:
  \[
  downstream\_queue_c = final\_start_c - join\_ready_c
  \]

- Final service:
  \[
  final\_service_c = final\_end_c - final\_start_c
  \]

- Total case flow time:
  \[
  total_c = final\_end_c - arrival_c
  \]

**Non-double-counting decomposition**

The additive decomposition is:

\[
total_c = upstream_c + forkjoin_c + downstream\_queue_c + final\_service_c
\]

Equivalently:

\[
total_c = upstream_c + max_i(queue_{c,i}+service_{c,i}) + downstream\_queue_c + final\_service_c
\]

This does **not** double count because the three branches run in parallel. The fork–join stage contributes elapsed time through the **maximum** branch response time, not through the sum of branch times.

**Why summing synchronization waits double counts**

For a given case,

\[
sync_{c,A}+sync_{c,B}+sync_{c,C}
= 3\cdot join\_ready_c - (A\_end_c+B\_end_c+C\_end_c)
\]

The synchronization waits overlap in clock time. Example: if A finishes at 09:00 and is critical, B finishes at 08:55 and waits 5, C finishes at 08:38 and waits 22, then the elapsed fork–join time is still governed by A’s completion at 09:00. Adding B’s 5 and C’s 22 to A’s branch time would count overlapping parallel time as if it were serial. Therefore branch-level \(queue + service + sync\) is informative per branch, but summing those three-branch totals across A, B, and C is not elapsed time.

The correct interpretation is:

- Non-critical branches experience *idle-after-completion* while waiting for siblings.
- Only the critical branch’s \(queue+service\) defines elapsed fork–join time.
- Synchronization wait is a diagnostic of slack, not an additive serial stage.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

Branch means are arithmetic means over 12 cases. “Response” means queue + service measured from `fork_release` to branch end; this is the quantity that determines criticality.

| branch | service mean | service min–max | queue mean | queue median | queue min–max | response mean | response min–max | critical frequency | mean sync wait | sync range |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 50.0 | 50–50 | 0.0 | 0 | 0–0 | 50.0 | 50–50 | 2/12 = 16.7% | 11.0 | 0–25 |
| B | 37.5 | 30–45 | 19.2 | 20 | 5–30 | 56.7 | 35–75 | 8/12 = 66.7% | 4.3 | 0–20 |
| C | 20.2 | 18–30 | 10.4 | 5 | 5–35 | 30.6 | 23–57 | 2/12 = 16.7% | 30.4 | 0–52 |

Additional aggregates:

- Mean upstream elapsed: \(235/12 = 19.6\) minutes; F01 is 15 and all others are 20.
- Mean fork–join elapsed: \(732/12 = 61.0\) minutes.
- Mean downstream queue: \(176/12 = 14.7\) minutes, range 8–20.
- Final service is constant at 20 minutes in all cases.
- Mean total flow time: \(1383/12 = 115.25\) minutes, range 93–130.

**Interpretation**

A has the largest service time, 50 minutes in every case, but A is **not** the strongest controller of completion. Its response time is perfectly constant at 50 because A never queues. That makes A a fixed floor: any branch finishing after 50 minutes from fork will dominate A. B does so frequently because B’s queue delay is large and variable.

B is the dominant branch:

- B is critical in 8 of 12 cases.
- B has the largest mean response, 56.7 minutes, despite lower mean service than A.
- B drives both 75-minute fork–join maxima: F07 and F12 both have B response 75 and the two long tail outcomes with downstream included.
- B has the lowest mean synchronization wait, 4.3 minutes, consistent with B usually being late rather than waiting for siblings.

A is second in influence, but in a different way:

- A is critical only twice, F01 and F11.
- In several B-critical cases, A is the next constraint and caps the benefit of improving B. For example, in F05, B ends at 10:10 and A at 10:00, so accelerating B by more than 10 minutes cannot reduce join time further unless A is also improved.
- Because A’s response is a constant 50, A creates a hard floor for fork–join time. Even perfect B and C performance cannot make fork–join below 50 under current A timing.

C is usually non-critical but occasionally decisive:

- C has the largest mean synchronization wait, 30.4 minutes, meaning C usually finishes early and waits for A/B.
- But C is critical in F04 and F08, driven mainly by queue spikes of 35 and 25 minutes rather than by normally large service.
- C therefore matters for tail risk and reliability, not for average completion in most cases.

**Ranking**

1. **B — strongest average and tail controller**, mainly through queue delay plus moderately large service.
2. **A — structural floor and frequent secondary constraint**, critical in only 2 cases but present as the binding cap once B is improved in several others.
3. **C — low average influence but non-negligible episodic criticality**, especially when its queue spikes.

Ties and near-ties: there are no exact ties for `join_ready`. The closest critical margins are F01, where A beats B by 5 minutes, and F11, where A beats B by 10 minutes. F10 has B ahead of A by only 5 minutes, so small B improvements there are capped quickly by A.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

Counterfactuals below are deterministic timestamp-shift calculations, not causal forecasts. Unless stated otherwise, the assumed propagation rule is:

- branch start times shift only where the counterfactual says so;
- service durations remain unchanged except where explicitly reduced;
- `join_ready` is recomputed as the new maximum branch end;
- downstream queue duration and final service are held fixed, so a change in `join_ready` shifts final start/end one-for-one.

That last assumption is important: if the final station is congested in a queueing sense, earlier join readiness may not shift final start one-for-one. Conversely, if downstream wait is mostly fixed policy or scheduling latency, it may shift fully.

**Counterfactual 1: Reduce A service by 20%**

A service falls from 50 to 40 minutes, a nominal 10-minute reduction per case, with A start unchanged.

Per-case join savings:

| case | original critical | new A end effect | join saving |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| F01 | A | A moves 09:0008:50, but B ends 08:55 | 5 |
| F11 | A | A moves 11:3011:20, tying B at 11:20 | 10 |
| All other 10 cases | B or C | A was not critical | 0 |

- Direct benefit: only when A is critical.
- Zero-benefit cases: 10 of 12. This is the fork–join masking effect in its purest form.
- Partial-benefit case: F01 saves only 5 of the nominal 10 because B becomes the new critical branch.
- Aggregate join saving: 15 minutes over 12 cases; mean 1.25 minutes per case.
- Secondary effects: If final-station waiting is a true queue with resource contention, earlier arrival at F might change downstream queue duration; under the fixed-wait assumption it does not. The model also assumes A’s start does not move; if faster A service freed shared upstream resources, there could be additional benefits not represented in branch timestamps.

Conclusion: A 20% A-service reduction is surprisingly weak for total flow time under current B behavior, even though A has the largest service time. It becomes more valuable only after B is improved enough that A is frequently the binding floor.

**Counterfactual 2: Reduce B queue delay by 50%**

B start moves earlier by half of B’s observed queue; B service duration is unchanged, so B end moves earlier by the same amount unless another branch caps the benefit.

Per-case join savings:

| case | B queue | nominal B end shift | realized join saving | reason |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| F01 | 5 | 2.5 | 0 | A already critical |
| F02 | 20 | 10 | 10 | B moves 09:2509:15, tying A |
| F03 | 20 | 10 | 10 | B remains critical: 09:4509:35 |
| F04 | 10 | 5 | 0 | C critical |
| F05 | 25 | 12.5 | 10 | capped by A at 10:00 |
| F06 | 30 | 15 | 15 | B moves 10:3010:15, tying A |
| F07 | 30 | 15 | 15 | B remains critical: 10:5510:40 |
| F08 | 5 | 2.5 | 0 | C critical |
| F09 | 25 | 12.5 | 12.5 | B remains critical: 11:1511:02.5 |
| F10 | 20 | 10 | 5 | capped by A at 11:15 |
| F11 | 10 | 5 | 0 | A critical |
| F12 | 30 | 15 | 15 | B remains critical: 12:1011:55 |

- Direct benefit: 8 of 12 cases improve at the join.
- Zero-benefit cases: F01, F04, F08, F11, where B is not critical.
- Capped-benefit cases: F05 and F10, where A becomes the binding constraint before the full B gain is realized; F02 and F06 exactly reach the A boundary.
- Aggregate join saving: 92.5 minutes; mean 7.71 minutes per case.
- Tail effect: F07 falls from fork–join 75 to 60 before downstream effects; F12 falls from 75 to 60. This is much stronger tail control than Counterfactual 1.
- Secondary effects: Earlier B starts could increase contention for B resources if the queue reduction is achieved by releasing more work concurrently rather than by adding capacity or smoothing arrivals. If B shares staff/equipment with A or C, branch interactions could offset gains. Also, one-minute timestamp precision means fractional 2.5/12.5-minute shifts are modelling conveniences.

Conclusion: B queue reduction is the strongest branch-level lever. However, after this improvement, A’s constant 50-minute response becomes the floor in multiple cases, so further B-only gains have diminishing returns.

**Counterfactual 3: Add final-station capacity or reduce downstream queueing**

Because the true mechanism behind `final_start - join_ready` is unknown, two scenarios are useful:

- **Scenario 3a, conservative:** reduce downstream queue by 50%.
- **Scenario 3b, upper bound under stated definitions:** eliminate downstream queue entirely.

For Scenario 3a, per-case savings equal half of downstream queue:

| case | downstream queue | saving at 50% reduction |
|---|---:|---:|
| F01 | 8 | 4.0 |
| F02 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F03 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F04 | 18 | 9.0 |
| F05 | 10 | 5.0 |
| F06 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F07 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F08 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F09 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F10 | 20 | 10.0 |
| F11 | 20 | 10.0 |
| F12 | 10 | 5.0 |

- Direct benefit: every case benefits if downstream waiting is truly reducible and final service remains 20 minutes.
- Aggregate saving at 50% reduction: 88 minutes; mean 7.33 minutes per case.
- Upper-bound saving if eliminated: 176 minutes; mean 14.67 minutes per case.
- Cases with zero branch-masking: none at the branch level, because this lever acts after `join_ready`. But it can be masked by a different mechanism: if the wait is actually a mandatory cooling, review, batching, transport, or release-policy delay, “capacity” will not remove it.
- Secondary effects: Added final capacity could raise throughput if F1/F2 are truly contended. But the observed intervals raise doubt: for example, F03 becomes join-ready at 09:45 and starts F on F1 at 10:00 even though F1’s previous case F01 ended at 09:28. Likewise F02 is F2’s first observed final task and still waits 15 minutes after join. That pattern is more consistent with scheduling latency, assignment rules, unlogged work, setup, or policy delay than with simple two-server saturation. Therefore the expected benefit of physical capacity is uncertain; reducing the *release mechanism delay* may matter more than adding a third resource.

**Comparison of the three counterfactuals**

| counterfactual | aggregate saving over 12 cases | mean saving per case | cases with zero join/total benefit under model | main masking mechanism |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|

Branch improvements are masked by fork–join maxima. Downstream improvements are not masked by branch maxima, but they are exposed to mechanism risk: the wait may not be a queue in the resource-contention sense.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Recommendation 1 — Reduce B start delay through scheduling/capacity control for the Machine Stress Test branch**

Practical forms could include earlier B release prioritization, reserved B slots aligned to fork release, smoothing of B arrivals, or added B capacity. The timestamp evidence alone cannot choose among those mechanisms; it only shows that B start delay is the high-value target.

- **Throughput:** Should improve end-to-end throughput if B is on the critical path for two-thirds of cases and if earlier B completion is not blocked downstream. Fork–join throughput is governed by the slowest branch, so B queue relief raises the effective completion rate of the parallel stage.
- **Flow-time tail:** Strongest expected tail benefit among branch options. F07 and F12 both have B response 75; halving B queue reduces their fork–join times to 60 under the model, and the worst total flow times should compress if downstream waiting is unchanged.
- **Resource utilization:** If achieved by adding B capacity, B utilization may fall while system throughput rises; that is acceptable if B was the constraint. If achieved only by starting B earlier without capacity, B utilization and internal congestion may rise, potentially lengthening B service or creating queue displacement to other times.
- **Possible new bottlenecks:** A becomes the floor at 50 minutes from fork in capped cases such as F05 and F10 and exactly binding in F02/F06 after improvement. After B relief, further gains require A improvement or fork-release/branch-start redesign. C remains an episodic bottleneck when its queue spikes.
- **Synchronization effects:** Reducing B lateness reduces A/C synchronization waiting in many cases, but once B is no longer critical, extra B speed has zero marginal value for those cases. The benefit curve is therefore piecewise: full benefit while B is strictly critical, capped benefit at the A boundary, zero after that.
- **Pilot/measure first:** Pilot a B priority/reserved-slot rule on a subset of cases and log B resource identity, queue-entry time, preemption, setup, and whether B shares resources with A/C. Measure B queue, B service stability after earlier starts, join-ready time, and the proportion of cases where A becomes critical.

**Recommendation 2 — Diagnose and reduce the post-join release delay before F, rather than immediately buying final capacity**

The final station shows a consistent 8–20 minute post-join wait, mean 14.7, while the specific assigned final resource often appears idle from the observed intervals. The first redesign step should be to identify whether this is assignment delay, batching, staff availability not captured by F1/F2 labels, setup, transport, system release rules, or unlogged tasks.

- **Throughput:** If the delay is true resource queueing, added capacity or better F1/F2 balancing could raise throughput. If it is fixed scheduling latency, capacity may add cost with little throughput gain; removing latency or parallelizing release checks would be more effective.
- **Flow-time tail:** Downstream waits are less dispersed than B response times, range 8–20, so tail gains are smaller than B-tail gains but still material: eliminating half the wait saves up to 10 minutes in F10/F11.
- **Resource utilization:** Observed final service is only 20 minutes per case and resources alternate across cases with visible gaps. Naively adding a third final resource risks low utilization unless arrival bursts to F are denser outside this sample. Improving release responsiveness may raise effective utilization of existing F1/F2 without adding idle capacity.
- **Possible new bottlenecks:** If post-join delay is removed while B remains unimproved, cases will simply arrive earlier at a still B-dominated join; total flow improves by the removed wait, but the process tail remains governed by B-critical cases such as F07/F12. Conversely, if B is fixed first, more cases may reach F in bursts and final-station contention could become real.
- **Synchronization effects:** This lever acts after synchronization is complete, so it does not interact with branch slack except through arrival patterns to F. Earlier joins from a B redesign could synchronize arrivals at F and create secondary queueing not present in the fixed-wait counterfactual.
- **Pilot/measure first:** Add timestamps for “ready for final assignment,” “final resource assigned,” “setup start,” and any release-policy hold reason. Pilot a ready-to-start pull rule or expedited assignment for join-ready cases and measure whether `final_start - join_ready` falls without increasing final service or errors.

**Combined sequencing view**

Do B first for critical-path relief, but instrument F release delay in parallel. If B is fixed without watching F, the bottleneck may shift downstream in arrival bursts or expose A as the parallel-stage floor. If F is fixed without B, every case gets a modest benefit but the worst cases remain B-driven.

# 6. Reproducible Python

```python
import io
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Data are embedded so the script is self-contained.
csv_data = """case,arrival,fork_release,A_start,A_end,B_start,B_end,C_start,C_end,final_start,final_end,final_resource
F01,07:55,08:10,08:10,09:00,08:15,08:55,08:20,08:38,09:08,09:28,F1
F02,08:05,08:25,08:25,09:15,08:45,09:25,08:30,08:50,09:40,10:00,F2
F03,08:20,08:40,08:40,09:30,09:00,09:45,08:45,09:03,10:00,10:20,F1
F04,08:35,08:55,08:55,09:45,09:05,09:35,09:30,09:52,10:10,10:30,F2
F05,08:50,09:10,09:10,10:00,09:35,10:10,09:15,09:35,10:20,10:40,F1
F06,09:05,09:25,09:25,10:15,09:55,10:30,09:40,10:00,10:45,11:05,F2
F07,09:20,09:40,09:40,10:30,10:10,10:55,09:45,10:03,11:10,11:30,F1
F08,09:35,09:55,09:55,10:45,10:00,10:30,10:20,10:50,11:05,11:25,F2
F09,09:50,10:10,10:10,11:00,10:35,11:15,10:15,10:35,11:30,11:50,F1
F10,10:05,10:25,10:25,11:15,10:45,11:20,10:30,10:50,11:40,12:00,F2
F11,10:20,10:40,10:40,11:30,10:50,11:20,10:45,11:03,11:50,12:10,F1
F12,10:35,10:55,10:55,11:45,11:25,12:10,11:00,11:18,12:20,12:40,F2
"""

df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(csv_data))

date = "2026-05-19 "
time_cols = ["arrival", "fork_release", "A_start", "A_end", "B_start", "B_end",
             "C_start", "C_end", "final_start", "final_end"]
for col in time_cols:
    df[col] = pd.to_datetime(date + df[col].astype(str), format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M")

def minutes(later, earlier):
    return (later - earlier).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

branches = ["A", "B", "C"]

# Core decomposition: upstream + max(queue+service) + downstream + final service.
df["upstream_elapsed"] = minutes(df["fork_release"], df["arrival"])
for b in branches:
    df[f"{b}_queue"] = minutes(df[f"{b}_start"], df["fork_release"])
    df[f"{b}_service"] = minutes(df[f"{b}_end"], df[f"{b}_start"])
    df[f"{b}_response"] = df[f"{b}_queue"] + df[f"{b}_service"]

end_cols = [f"{b}_end" for b in branches]

# Deterministic tie handling: idxmax returns the first maximum in A,B,C order.
# We also record all tied branches and the tie margin so ties are not hidden.
df["join_ready"] = df[end_cols].max(axis=1)
df["critical_branch"] = df[end_cols].idxmax(axis=1).str.replace("_end", "", regex=False)
df["critical_tie_branches"] = df.apply(
    lambda r: ",".join([b for b in branches if r[f"{b}_end"] == r["join_ready"]]), axis=1
)
df["critical_margin_min"] = df.apply(
    lambda r: sorted([r[f"{b}_response"] for b in branches], reverse=True)[0]
              - sorted([r[f"{b}_response"] for b in branches], reverse=True)[1],
    axis=1
)

for b in branches:
    # Synchronization wait is slack after branch completion until all branches finish.
    df[f"{b}_sync_wait"] = minutes(df["join_ready"], df[f"{b}_end"])

df["forkjoin_elapsed"] = minutes(df["join_ready"], df["fork_release"])
df["downstream_queue"] = minutes(df["final_start"], df["join_ready"])
df["final_service"] = minutes(df["final_end"], df["final_start"])
df["total_flow_time"] = minutes(df["final_end"], df["arrival"])

# Internal consistency check: decomposition must reconstruct total flow time.
df["decomp_check"] = (
    df["upstream_elapsed"] + df["forkjoin_elapsed"]

# Counterfactual helper.
# Direct branch-end shifts are applied first; join_ready is then recomputed as a max.
# Downstream queue duration is held fixed, so total saving equals join saving for branch CFs.
def join_savings(new_end_by_branch):
    new_ends = pd.DataFrame({
        b: new_end_by_branch.get(b, df[f"{b}_end"]) for b in branches
    })
    new_join = new_ends.max(axis=1)
    return minutes(df["join_ready"], new_join)  # positive means earlier join

cf1_saving = join_savings({"A": df["A_end"] - pd.to_timedelta(0.20 * df["A_service"], unit="m")})
```

# 7. Limits of inference

**What the timestamps identify**

Under the stated fork–join rule, the timestamps identify elapsed upstream time, branch start delays relative to `fork_release`, branch start–end durations, inferred `join_ready`, synchronization slack, post-join waiting, final duration, and total flow time. They also identify which branch is last in each observed case and permit deterministic max-based counterfactual arithmetic.

**What they do not identify**

- **Causality:** The data show association in one small sample, not that B queue delay is caused by capacity, priority rules, arrival bursts, staffing, equipment failure, case mix, or upstream release timing. Counterfactual savings are arithmetic scenarios, not estimated treatment effects.
- **Branch resource capacity:** Branch resource identities, number of parallel servers, calendars, utilization, and whether A/B/C share people or equipment are not logged. A constant 50-minute A duration could reflect one deterministic task, a saturated resource with stable processing, rounding, or a standard work package. B queue could be true waiting, scheduled start offset, batching, or dependency on an unlogged input.
- **Work preemption and hidden waiting inside service:** `service = end - start` may contain interruptions, rework, waiting for information, or multitasking. If preemption occurs, speeding “service” and adding capacity are not equivalent interventions.
- **Concurrency inside branches:** It is unknown whether each branch is a single activity or an aggregate. Discovery from timestamps alone would flatten internal structure.
- **Whether final waiting is truly a queue:** `final_start - join_ready` is only a residual gap. It may be queueing for F1/F2, but idle-looking gaps for the assigned resource suggest alternatives: release policy, shift or break timing, setup, inspection, transport, batching, decision authorization, data-system delay, or unlogged work. Adding final capacity is therefore not guaranteed to reduce this gap.
- **Queue discipline and assignment:** It is unknown whether cases are processed first-come-first-served, prioritized, assigned to F1/F2 by specialization, or held for a preferred resource. Resource-specific waiting cannot be reliably estimated without assignment-ready timestamps.
- **Small-sample and tail uncertainty:** Twelve cases on one day cannot support stable tail estimates, seasonality claims, or capacity sizing. Values such as B criticality 8/12 are descriptive of this sample; confidence intervals would be wide.
- **Timestamp granularity:** One-minute resolution and fractional counterfactual shifts are approximations. Exact ties for `join_ready` may be hidden by rounding.
- **Generalization across case mix:** If accelerated B cases differ in complexity, service times may change after queue redesign. Earlier starts can also move congestion elsewhere, so queue reduction is not automatically capacity creation.
- **Process-model limits:** A PM4Py directly-follows graph can summarize observed ordering and interval performance, but unless explicitly modelled, it does not know that A/B/C form an AND-split/AND-join. Synchronization waiting and critical-branch identity must come from the custom max-based logic, not from DFG edges alone.

The defensible conclusion is narrow but strong within the sample: B controls completion most often because queue delay makes its fork-to-completion response largest, A forms a constant 50-minute floor, C is usually early but occasionally critical, and post-join delay is a consistent separate loss whose physical mechanism remains unidentified.
