## 1. Interactiongraph description  

**Node types**  
- **Job** (J1J6) – final product objects.  
- **Component** (C1C6) – required subassemblies.  
- **ComponentLot** (L7, L8) – containers that bundle components (e.g., L7 holds C2 & C5).  
- **Machine** (CNC2, OVEN1, TEST1) – processing resources.  
- **Tool** (FIXA, RACK7, CAL3) – ancillary resources that may be shared.  
- **Batch** (B1, B2) – groups of jobs processed together on a machine.  
- **Shipment** (S1S3) – consolidation bundles for final dispatch.  

**Edge types & qualifiers**  
| Source  Target | Qualifier | Observation status |
|----------------|-----------|--------------------|
| Job  requires  Component | *requires* | Directly observed in the logs (e.g., J1requiresC1). |
| ComponentLot  contains  Component | *contains* | Directly observed (L7containsC2,C5). |
| Job  batches  Batch | *batches* | Directly observed (J1J3 in B1). |
| Batch  uses  Machine | *operates on* (implicit) | Inferred from machineprocess timestamps for the same batch. |
| Batch  uses  Tool | *uses* | Inferred (RACK7 used by both B1 and B2). |
| Job  consolidated  Shipment | *consolidates* | Directly observed (J1 & J4 in S1). |
| Component  released  ComponentLot | *released* | Inferred from quarantine/release events that affect J2 & J5. |
| Tool  used_by  Job | *acquire/release* | Directly observed for FIXA and CAL3. |
| Job  tested_by  Machine | *final test* | Directly observed (TEST1 with CAL3). |

**Higherorder motifs (3 node types)**  

1. **Batchsharing motif** – *Batch* B1  *Machine* OVEN1  *Tool* RACK7  *Jobs* J1J3.  
   - All three edges are direct observations (batchmachine, tooljob, jobbatch).  
   - Captures synchronization: all jobs in B1 start only when the batch is loaded onto the oven and the rack is ready.  

2. **ComponentLot coupling motif** – *ComponentLot* L7  *Components* C2,C5  *Jobs* J2,J5  *Batch* B1B2.  
   - Links the lotrelease events (10:20 quarantine  13:50 release) to the job readiness times of J2 and J5.  
   - Inferred because the extraction pipeline omitted the lot events for jobs; the alternative integration would surface the threeway coupling.  

3. **ToolCalibration cascade motif** – *Tool* CAL3  *Machine* TEST1  *Jobs* J5,J6  *Shipment* S3.  
   - Shows that a single calibration resource creates a ripple: J5 tests early, CAL3 is then busy recalibrating, delaying J6, which in turn pushes back the shipment S3 dispatch.  
   - Edge *Tool  used_by  Job* is direct; *Job  consolidated  Shipment* is direct; *Machine  tested_by  Job* is direct; the *Job  consolidated  Shipment* edge captures the finalstage coupling.  

**Direct vs. inferred edges**  
- Direct observations: all Job  requires  Component, ComponentLot  contains  Component, Job  batches  Batch, Batch  uses  Machine (inferred from timestamps), Tool acquire/release, Machine test events, Job  consolidated  Shipment.  
- Inferred edges: Batch  uses  Tool (RACK7 shared), Job  requires  ComponentLot (via the quarantine/release chain), Job  consolidates  Shipment (waiting for partner jobs).

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition  

**Counterfactual assumptions** – each calculation imagines the same job progressing as if the identified coupling were absent (e.g., batch synchronization removed, tool idle, shipment partner already present, etc.).

1. **Synchronization waiting for J1 and J2 in BatchB1**  
   - *Observed*: J1 ready 08:10  batch start 12:05  waiting **3h55min**.  
   - *Observed*: J2 ready 08:25  batch start 12:05  waiting **3h40min**.  
   - *Counterfactual*: Jobs start as soon as their component is ready (i.e., independent processing).  
   - *Decomposed waiting*  **3h55min** – **setup & load time** of the oven/rack (0.15h)  net **3h40min** of synchronization delay attributable to batch formation.  

2. **Delay imposed on J4 by FIXA contention**  
   - *Observed*: J3 acquires FIXA 09:00  releases 11:40.  
   - *Observed*: J4 reaches CNC2 at 09:50 but its `Machine Process` starts at 11:45 (5min idle at CNC2)  **5min** “feasibility” wait.  
   - *Additional inferred wait*: CNC2 idle 10:05–10:47 (42min) coincides with J4 arrival; however, FIXA was already released, suggesting the **actual delay** is the **5min** wait due to fixture availability.  

3. **Delay on J1 caused by ShipmentS1 consolidation**  
   - *Observed*: J1 packed 13:30, J4 packed 19:10, S1 dispatch 19:25.  
   - *Counterfactual*: Dispatch as soon as the last packed job (J1) is ready.  
   - *Extra wait* = **19:25 – 13:30** = **5h55min** of waiting for J4 to finish packing, i.e., **shipmentpartner synchronization** delay of **5h55min** for J1.  

4. **Delay for J5 due to BatchB1B2 sharing of RACK7**  
   - *Observed*: J4 ready 13:10, J5 ready 14:40, batchB2 starts 14:50 (RACK7 cleaning/quarantine until 14:35).  
   - *Waiting* = **14:50 – 14:40** = **10min** of rack inactivity, plus the **5min** idle at OVEN1 before batch start after rack cleared.  
   - *Decomposed rackrelated delay*  **15min** for J5.  

5. **Delay for J6 due to CAL3 calibration sharing**  
   - *Observed*: J5 final test 16:1016:35  CAL3 recalibrated 16:3517:20.  
   - *Observed*: J6 test starts 17:25  waiting **5min** for CAL3 to become free plus **25min** idle of TEST1 from 16:3517:25 (machine capacity)  net **30min** delay for J6.  
   - *Counterfactual*: If CAL3 were dedicated to J5 only, J6 could start immediately after J5 finishes testing, removing the CAL3related portion (**25min**).  

**Summary of calculations**  
- Batchsynchronization accounts for **3.4h** of combined waiting (J1 & J2).  
- Toolcontention (FIXA) adds **5min** for J4.  
- Shipmentconsolidation postpones J1 for **5.9h** (5h55min).  
- Racksharing adds **15min** for J5.  
- Calibrationtool sharing adds **30min** for J6.  

These numbers illustrate that *crossinstance* couplings (batch, tool, shipment, lot, rack) dominate the observed variance, even though machine utilizations appear moderate.

**Rationale for ranking**  
- The shipmentconsolidation mechanism directly explains the largest observed waiting time (6h for J1) and is visible in only the shipmentflattened view, confirming that standard flattenings miss it.  
- Batch synchronization follows because it produces noticeable delays across several jobs and is inferred from timestamps; however, it is secondary to shipment coupling.  
- Tool contention and componentlot coupling contribute but are smaller in magnitude and more likely to be artifacts or secondary effects.  
- Machine capacity appears underutilized because the *crossinstance* couplings (batch, tool, shipment, lot) fragment the resource, leading to idle gaps that do not translate into shortening job times.

## 4. What flattening misses  

| Viewpoint | Visible | Hidden / duplicated | Misleading DFG / performance |
|-----------|---------|---------------------|------------------------------|
| **Job flattening** | Direct *Release  Machine* events, inspectiontopack transitions | Waits due to batch synchronization (B1B2 overlap), tool acquisition gaps, shipment partner waiting, componentlot release lags | Median elapsed times suggest normal processing; the DFG shows no “wait” edges, so it underestimates total job duration. |
| **Machine flattening** | Utilization percentages, activity sequences (Setup  Process  Test) | Interjob synchronization delays (e.g., J4 delayed by FIXA), tool contention not linked to machine events, batchlevel load changes | Low utilization appears as “capacity free”, but long idle gaps are interpreted as normal downtime, masking the real waiting caused by shared resources. |
| **Batch flattening** (not directly given) | Batchmachine coupling, shared rack usage | Individual joblevel waiting, toolrelated delays, lotrelease timing | No explicit `Batch` edge; inferred batch synchronization would be invisible, leading to the false impression of independent job flows. |
| **Tool flattening** | Acquire / release timestamps for FIXA, RACK7, CAL3 | Contention periods that map to job delays, policy conflicts (e.g., simultaneous fixture use) | Tool edges often omitted from performance DFGs; thus toolinduced delays are conflated with machine idle time. |
| **ComponentLot flattening** | Containment and release events (L7, L8) | Mapping of lot events to specific jobs (J2, J5) without direct edge; thus joblot coupling is lost | Job flattening may appear “normal” because lotrelated quarantine/release events are filtered out, hiding a source of variability. |
| **Shipment flattening** | `Create  Load  Dispatch` timeline | Waiting for consolidation partners, timing of shipment creation vs. lastjob pack, impact of shipment delays on earlier jobs | Only final dispatch timings are shown; earlier waiting for shipment partners is invisible, causing the misconception that dispatch is instantaneous. |

**Why low machine utilization coexists with long waiting**  
- **Crossinstance coupling**: Even when a machine sits idle (e.g., OVEN1 idle 73min), the *next* job cannot start until a shared resource (rack, batch, tool, shipment) becomes available.  
- **Batchlevel gating**: The batch does not advance until *all* jobs in the group are ready for the next operation, so the machine may sit idle while the batch is waiting for a synchronized start.  
- **Tool & shipment constraints**: A tool (FIXA, CAL3) or a shipment partner may monopolize capacity, causing the machine to idle while the job is poised to run.  
- **Data integration artifacts**: Events that represent resource contention (acquire/release, quarantine) are sometimes omitted from the raw logs that feed the flattening algorithms, so the idle machine time is recorded without a corresponding “wait” edge, reinforcing the illusion of free capacity.

## 5. Objectlifecycle evidence  

### Narrative 1 – BatchB1  OVEN1  Jobs J1J3  Shipment S2  

1. **Temporal precedence**  
   - 08:10J1 component ready  08:25J2 ready  11:55J3 ready.  
   - 12:05OvenRACK batch load (B1) initiates.  

2. **Resource feasibility**  
   - Oven1 has capacity > job load; rack RACK7 is shared but becomes available at 12:00 (after previous batch).  
   - All three jobs need the same ovenrack combo, thus they are *gated* by the batch start time.  

3. **Policy constraints**  
   - Company policy mandates *simultaneous* curing for batched jobs to ensure uniformity.  

4. **Causal speculation**  
   - Without the batch policy, J1 and J2 could have started as soon as their components were ready, shaving **3.5h** of waiting.  

### Narrative 2 – ComponentLot L7  Jobs J2, J5  Batch B1B2  

1. **Temporal precedence**  
   - 10:20Quarantine of L7 (C2,C5) begins  13:50Release of L7.  

2. **Resource feasibility**  
   - Only after release can J2 (needs C2) and J5 (needs C5) proceed to their batch stages.  

3. **Policy constraints**  
   - Safety protocol requires quarantine before any component leaves the lot.  

4. **Causal speculation**  
   - If the quarantine could be shortened or the release event were linked directly to job readiness, the **5min** delay for J5 (and analogous for J2) could be eliminated.  

### Narrative 3 – Tool CAL3  Job J5  Job J6  Shipment S3  

1. **Temporal precedence**  
   - 16:00J5 ready for final test  16:1016:35J5 tested  16:3517:20CAL3 recalibrated  17:2517:50J6 tested  17:55S3 dispatched (after J6 pack).  

2. **Resource feasibility**  
   - CAL3 is a singleslot calibration tool; only one test can occupy it at a time.  

3. **Policy constraints**  
   - Calibration schedule mandates a short cleanup after each test to meet quality standards.  

4. **Causal speculation**  
   - Providing a backup CAL3 or staggering calibration intervals could cut **30min** from J6’s wait, reducing overall shipment delay by ~30min.  

These narratives illustrate how *multiple object types* interlock through temporal order, resource constraints, and policy rules, generating cascading delays even when individual machines appear lightly loaded.

### Spillover considerations  

- **Intervention1 (batch split)** could increase overall batchload frequency, potentially raising energy consumption on OVEN1 despite lower waiting.  
- **Intervention2 (dual FIXA)** might create a *new* contention point if both fixtures are used concurrently without proper scheduling, leading to hidden delays.  
- **Intervention3 (shipment change)** may improve ontime delivery but could trigger downstream fulfillment processes (e.g., carrier scheduling) to reoptimize, causing external spillovers.  
- **Intervention4 (logging improvement)** indirectly affects all other interventions by providing clearer evidence; missing lot events could otherwise mislead timing calculations.  
- **Intervention5 (bufferedshipment)** may shift the bottleneck from dispatch to storage, requiring monitoring of buffer turnover rates.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline  

```python
import pm4py

# Assume `ocel` is the loaded ObjectCentric Event Log (OCEL)
# 1. Get frequency of interactions between Batch and Machine
batch_machine_freq = pm4py.objects_interactions_summary(ocel, interaction_type="Batch-Machine")
print("Batch  Machine frequency:", batch_machine_freq)

# 2. Get frequency of interactions between Job and Tool (FIXA)
job_tool_freq = pm4py.objects_interactions_summary(ocel, source_type="Job", target_type="Tool",
                                                  qualifier="uses")
print("Job  Tool (FIXA) frequency:", job_tool_freq)

# 3. Discover an ObjectCentric Graph (OCG) highlighting the motifs
ocg = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel, variant="dot")
# 4. Discover an ObjectCentric Transition System (OTG)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
# 5. Discover an EventCentric Object Directed Graph (EC-ODG)
ec_odg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)

# 6. Flatten the OCEL for machinelevel performance analysis
machine_flattening = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, flattening_type="Machine")
print("Machine flattening summary:", machine_flattening)

# 7. Compare two interaction metrics: BatchMachine vs. JobTool
batch_machine_metric = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, focus="Batch-Machine")
job_tool_metric = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, focus="Job-Tool")
print("BatchMachine mean interaction time:", batch_machine_metric["mean_time"])
print("JobTool mean interaction time:", job_tool_metric["mean_time"])

# 8. Optional: convert OCG to NetworkX for custom centrality analysis
import networkx as nx
ocg_nx = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(ocg)
betweenness = nx.betweenness_centrality(ocg_nx)
print("Betweenness centrality of tools (e.g., FIXA):", betweenness)
```

**Explanation of the script**  
- Lines14 compute interaction frequencies that directly reflect the batchmachine and jobtool couplings observed in the narrative.  
- Lines35 generate higherorder structures (OCG, OTG, ECODG) to visualize the motifs identified in Section1.  
- Line6 produces a machinecentric flattening to verify that low utilization does not hide synchronization delays.  
- Lines78 compare two distinct interaction metrics, demonstrating that batch synchronization impacts interaction latency far more than jobtool usage, consistent with the ranked hypotheses.  
- The final conversion to NetworkX allows centrality metrics (e.g., betweenness) to highlight the *hubs* (FIXA, CAL3, RACK7) that propagate delays across multiple jobs.

## 8. Falsification and additional data  

**Observations that would weaken the topranked hypothesis (shipmentconsolidation coupling)**  
1. System logs showing *dispatch* immediately after the *last* job is packed, with no intervening “waiting” timestamps for the consolidated shipment.  
2. A policy change that forces *individual* dispatch without consolidation, yet overall job completion times remain unchanged.  
3. Shipmentlevel performance metrics (e.g., average dispatchtime after packing) that are already minimal, indicating the shipment process is not a bottleneck.  
4. No correlation between *shipmentcreation* timestamps and *jobcompletion* timestamps across different orders.  
5. External carrier reports indicating ontime deliveries despite the observed long waiting for partners.  
6. Detecting that the “consolidation” step is a dataentry artifact (e.g., same `Dispatch` event timestamp for all partners without actual loading time).  

**Fields or event types that would discriminate among the hypotheses**  
1. **BatchStart** event with explicit *batchreadytimestamp* (records when the batch is fully assembled).  
2. **ToolAcquire / ToolRelease** timestamps with *resourceID* (distinguishes FIXA, RACK7, CAL3).  
3. **ComponentLotQuarantine / ComponentLotRelease** events linked to the *jobID* (captures lotcoupling precisely).  
4. **ShipmentPartnerReady** event indicating when each consignee’s packing is finished, allowing calculation of true partnerwaiting time.  
5. **PolicyTag** field (e.g., “batchpolicy”, “consolidationpolicy”) to trace managerial decisions that enforce synchronization.  
6. **ResourceUtilizationperJob** metric (machine time consumed by each individual job, not just aggregated utilization), revealing if a job is starved while the machine is “idle”.  

These observations and data enhancements would either confirm or refute the dominant coupling mechanisms, guiding future operational improvements.

---  

**Overall conclusion:**  
The unpredictable waiting and cascading delays stem primarily from *crossinstance* coupling—especially shipment consolidation, batch synchronization, and shared tool/componentlot constraints. Traditional flattenings miss these interactions, leading to an apparent “normal” process. By employing objectcentric analysis (motif detection, interactionfrequency comparison, and targeted interventions), managers can target the true sources of variability while monitoring spillover effects.
