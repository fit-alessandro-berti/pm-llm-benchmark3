## 1. Analytical assumptions

### Interpretation of key terms
- **Case notion**: The comprehensive view of a business scenario as captured by the OCEL snapshot. It comprises all activities, objects, and their relationships.
- **Convergence**: The tendency of multiple flattened views (e.g., Order-centric, Item-centric) to align or match on the same underlying events and objects.
- **Divergence**: The situation where flattened views produce differing sets of events, objects, or relationships, indicating fragmentation in the traceability of business processes.

### Treatment of copied events
Copied shared events across object types (e.g., the dispatch event e15 appearing for each order) are treated as evidence of **convergence** rather than duplication. They indicate that the same underlying business activity is shared among multiple objects and should be considered a single logical occurrence within the broader fulfillment process.

### Analytical transformations
Allowed transformations:
- **Relation propagation**: If a shared event involves multiple objects, we can propagate the event across those objects to reflect the shared nature of the activity.
- **Event aggregation**: Grouping multiple events that pertain to the same logical action (e.g., consolidating the two payment records for V2 into a single “Payment Received” event).

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

### 1. Order-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Loss of intra-order item relationships (e.g., P3 contains items from O2 and O3). 
- **Effect**: Operational insight into item grouping is lost; packages and deliveries appear disconnected from their origin orders.
- **DFG impact**: May inflate event frequency due to duplication of events like packing and dispatch across multiple orders.
- **Performance**: Potentially efficient for order lifecycle analysis but may miss logistics intricacies.

### 2. Item-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Loss of order-level context; items appear isolated from their orders.
- **Effect**: Understanding of order composition becomes difficult; relationships like "P3 packs I21 and I31" become ambiguous.
- **DFG impact**: Event duplication is moderate; the traceability of items to orders is weakened.
- **Performance**: Efficient for item-level analytics but may obscure the overall fulfillment process.

### 3. Package-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Loss of order and item context; packages are treated as standalone entities.
- **Effect**: Hard to trace which items were packed together or which orders they belong to.
- **DFG impact**: High duplication of packing events, leading to inflated event counts.
- **Performance**: Good for logistics analysis but may not reflect the broader fulfillment narrative.

### 4. Delivery-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Consolidation of multiple deliveries under a single delivery event (e.g., D2 carries P2 and P3).
- **Effect**: While consolidation simplifies delivery tracking, it hides individual delivery details and their association with specific orders/items.
- **DFG impact**: Event duplication is high; the granularity of delivery events is lost.
- **Performance**: Useful for monitoring delivery status but may obscure order and item relationships.

### 5. Invoice-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Invoicing is consolidated across multiple orders (e.g., V2 for O2 and O3).
- **Effect**: Financial reconciliation becomes straightforward but loses the detailed fulfillment steps.
- **DFG impact**: Moderate duplication of invoice events; may inflate financial transaction counts.
- **Performance**: Effective for financial analysis but may not align with operational timelines.

### 6. Payment-centric flattening
- **Distortion**: Multiple payment records (e.g., M2 partially settles V2) can lead to confusion about settlement completeness.
- **Effect**: Payment reconciliation becomes complex; it’s unclear which payments fully settle which invoices.
- **DFG impact**: High duplication of payment events, leading to potential overestimation of transaction volume.
- **Performance**: Robust for financial tracking but may obscure the fulfillment process.

### 7. Singleton lifecycle (e.g., focusing solely on Order)
- **Distortion**: Lifecycle appears truncated; downstream events like packing, dispatch, invoicing, and payment are not considered.
- **Effect**: The process appears overly simplified; critical downstream activities are ignored.
- **DFG impact**: Minimal DFG distortion but significant loss of operational context.
- **Performance**: Not suitable for comprehensive process analysis; may lead to incomplete insights.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

### Recommended flattened logs
- **Order-centric**: Essential for end-to-end fulfillment analysis, capturing the sequence of order creation, processing, and closure.
- **Item-centric**: Useful for detailed inventory and packaging analysis, providing insights into how items are grouped within orders.
- **Package-centric**: Important for logistics and supply chain visibility, showing how items are bundled for transportation.
- **Invoice-centric**: Necessary for financial reconciliation and payment processing analysis.
- **Payment-centric**: Critical for financial tracking and reconciliation processes.

### Non-flattened logs
- **Full OCEL snapshot**: Should be retained for comprehensive analysis, allowing for the exploration of all object relationships and events in their natural context.

### Questions best answered by flattened logs
- **Order-centric**: “What is the order fulfillment timeline?”
- **Item-centric**: “How are items grouped within each order and package?”
- **Package-centric**: “Which items are packed together and dispatched in each delivery?”
- **Invoice-centric**: “How are payments reconciled across multiple orders?”
- **Payment-centric**: “What are the payment flows for each invoice?”

### Questions requiring object-centric analysis
- **Cross-order package relationships**: Analyzing how items from different orders are consolidated into packages requires an object-centric view.
- **Consolidated delivery analysis**: Understanding how deliveries combine items from multiple orders demands an object-centric perspective.
- **Consolidated invoicing**: Financial reconciliation across multiple orders necessitates an object-centric view.
- **Partial payment scenarios**: Tracking partial payments and their impact on invoicing requires an object-centric analysis.

### Reconciliation approach
- **Comparison**: Compare flattened logs against the full OCEL snapshot to identify discrepancies and validate the completeness of each flattened view.
- **Integration**: Use object interactions summaries to reconcile differences, ensuring that all object-centric relationships are accounted for in the final analysis.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles**

### Decision criteria
1. **Coverage**: Ensure the lead object captures the majority of business activities while acknowledging its limitations.
2. **Contextual completeness**: Maintain secondary lifecycles to provide context for abstracted objects (e.g., items within orders).
3. **Data integrity**: Avoid over-reliance on any single object type to prevent loss of critical insights.

### Strongest supporting evidence
- Orders are central to business processes, covering nearly all fulfillment steps.
- The OCEL snapshot shows a clear order lifecycle (e.g., e01 to e31), making it a natural choice for a primary lead object.

### Strongest counterargument
- Item, package, delivery, invoice, and payment events are crucial for detailed process understanding and may be overlooked when focusing solely on orders.

### Residual risk
- Relying solely on the Order object may lead to incomplete insights into item grouping, packaging logistics, and detailed financial reconciliation.

### Conditions for changing the decision
- If the analysis reveals that crucial business insights are consistently missing from the Order-centric view, consider incorporating additional lead objects (e.g., Item, Package) to provide a more holistic view.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

### Sensitivity checks

1. **Removing or down-weighting copied shared events**
   - **Approach**: Temporarily exclude shared events (e.g., D2’s dispatch) and re-run the analysis.
   - **Expected outcome**: Reduced event duplication; clearer distinction between unique and shared activities.
   - **Benefit**: Improves data integrity and focuses on unique process steps.

2. **Comparing direct E2O relations with a propagated-relation variant**
   - **Approach**: Analyze events directly related to objects versus those propagated through object relationships (e.g., P3’s involvement in multiple orders).
   - **Expected outcome**: Divergence in event counts and object relationships; identification of shared activities.
   - **Benefit**: Provides a deeper understanding of how relationships influence process tracing.

3. **Changing the lead object**
   - **Approach**: Re-run the analysis using different lead objects (e.g., Item, Package) and compare results.
   - **Expected outcome**: Variation in preserved vs. lost information; identification of which object type best captures the desired business insights.
   - **Benefit**: Ensures robustness of the chosen lead object and highlights potential blind spots.

4. **Comparing frequency metrics based on events, unique objects, and total objects in the OC-DFG**
   - **Approach**: Calculate and compare event frequencies, unique object occurrences, and total object counts across flattened logs.
   - **Expected outcome**: Insights into data distribution and potential biases introduced by each flattening approach.
   - **Benefit**: Helps in selecting the flattening method that aligns best with analytical goals.

These checks will help validate the robustness of the chosen flattened logs and ensure comprehensive coverage of the fulfillment process.
