# 1. System boundary and assumptions

The objective is to reduce the environmental burden of fulfilling spare-part demand while delivering usable, conforming parts within the required service window. The functional unit is a complete, customer-accepted order, supported by line-item measures because one order may contain several parts and shipments. Report both absolute impacts for the incoming-order cohort and impacts per completed order; otherwise cancellations, undelivered backorders, or growing inventory can make performance look greener without satisfying demand.

The primary boundary covers attributable procurement and replenishment, warehousing, picking, production and heat treatment, inspection, packaging, all delivery legs, and subsequent returns, repair, replacement, and disposal of packaging, scrap, and obsolete stock. Include energy consumed during setups, standby, and rework, and empty vehicle travel where attributable. Upstream material and packaging production matter even where their emissions are not in the current log. Moving production into purchased stock must not make its embodied burden disappear.

Use two layers of assessment: a measured operational dashboard using kWh, transport kgCO2e, scrap kg, and packaging kg; and an extended lifecycle assessment as supplier, electricity, material, and waste-treatment factors become available. Do not add these unlike units together or treat operational electricity as total lifecycle carbon. Material mass also does not establish toxicity, resource scarcity, or recyclability. Capital equipment and the delivered part's eventual use and end-of-life are outside the initial quantified comparison unless a redesign materially changes them; assess such changes separately. Customer downtime is a binding service consideration, with any associated environmental effects examined as scenarios rather than assumed carbon credits.

The evidence is observational. Product geometry, weight, routing, urgency, geography, and production complexity can explain part of the differences between paths. The selected variants cover 89% of orders, and the DFG counts do not fully reconcile: for example, 470 incoming cancellation transitions are shown versus 530 cancellation-to-END transitions. Repeated activities and flattened line items further limit interpretation. Reconcile case completeness, event coverage, and allocation before estimating total savings. Energy-emission factors, transport accounting boundaries, actual part weights, damage-return rates, and disposal outcomes remain uncertain. Items unshipped after 14 days are inventory at risk, not automatically waste.

# 3. Intervention options

**S1. Introduce a shipping recommender at release and dispatch.** Target Package standard/reinforced -> ground, air, van, or consolidated truck. Select the lowest-impact feasible route using remaining promised time, picking/packaging queues, departure cutoffs, route reliability, and disruption information. SP1's probability above 85% is a policy floor, not a sufficient service target for every segment: stricter contractual and critical-part requirements also apply. Environmental gains come from avoiding unnecessary premium shipments; poor reliability estimates could increase lateness. Monitor predicted versus actual on-time probability, dispatch slack, route_mode, transport_kgco2e, overrides, and segment-level outcomes. Preserve R1 fallback capacity.

**S2. Use dynamic batching with segment-specific waiting caps.** Target Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC and queues before heat treatment. Group compatible, demand-backed work until the batch is efficient or the earliest safe release time is reached. Set that time from the tightest order deadline minus a conservative allowance for all remaining processing, inspection, packing, and transport. CNC_A's observed processing energy falls from 2.8 to 1.3 kWh/item for larger batches, but median waiting rises from 4h to 38h. Release smaller batches when necessary and reserve an urgent escape path. Monitor batch_id/size, queue age, energy per accepted item, end-to-end lateness, subsequent expedites, and inventory aging. Do not produce speculative extras merely to reach a batch threshold.

**S3. Route eligible Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A.** Target compatible parts currently following CNC_B -> Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC. CNC_B's 18% rework rate versus CNC_A's 9% suggests an opportunity to prevent repeat machining, scrap, and downstream delay. Switch only when CNC_A capacity, process qualification, and due-date slack support it; retain required heat treatment and the ability to use CNC_B. Loading CNC_A indiscriminately could create late orders and remove useful capacity diversity. Monitor matched part-family outcomes, machine queues, total route energy, scrap, first-pass yield, and delivery performance. The observed rework gap is a hypothesis to test, not a guaranteed causal reduction.

**S4. Pair priority picking with ground for eligible Standard-stock Service-contract orders.** Target Stock -> standard pack -> air where earlier picking leaves enough time for reliable ground delivery. SP1 violations in 22% of this segment provide a focused review population, although some may reflect missing decision evidence. Reduce warehouse delay before changing mode, while preserving priority access to air when slack is insufficient. Monitor Availability check -> Pick -> Package elapsed time, dispatch cutoffs, route outcomes, and the segment's current 5% lateness and 41h p90 cycle time. Also monitor Regular orders so priority picking does not simply transfer delay to them.

**S5. Pilot twice-daily consolidated departures for non-urgent stock orders.** Target standard-packed ground parcels with compatible destinations and adequate slack, initially among Standard-stock Regular orders. Consolidated trucks offer approximately 35–55% lower transport emissions than individual ground parcels, but the observed 12–24h consolidation wait must fit within the full remaining service window. Two departures per day are a proposed operating schedule, not proof that all geographic lanes will have short waits or sufficient loads. Monitor ready-to-depart dwell, utilization, missed departures, transport_kgco2e, and lateness. Keep parcel departures available for orders that cannot wait or for an unreliable consolidation lane.

**S6. Check part, packaging, and route compatibility before packing.** Target the 260 standard-pack and 360 reinforced-pack transitions into Rework packaging. Use part weight and geometry, route requirements, and damage history to choose packaging once; revalidate if the shipping mode changes. This prevents discarded packaging, repeated work, and missed dispatches. Enforce reinforced packaging for Made-to-order parts above 8 kg unless inspection certifies low damage risk under Q1. Monitor packaging-rework loops, package_material_kg, dispatch delay, damage returns, and Q1 certifications. Packaging reduction is beneficial only when protection remains adequate.

**S7. Strengthen inspection for high-rework machine/part combinations.** Target the 470 Inspection -> Rework production transitions and defects discovered later during packaging or by customers. Introduce first-piece/process checks where they prevent a defective batch, plus a reliable pre-pack quality gate, supported by sufficient inspection_cell capacity and protected inspection time. A gate without capacity could worsen the existing rushed-inspection problem. Early detection can initially raise recorded internal rework while reducing downstream damage and replacement production. Monitor inspection queues, defect origin and detection point, repeated loops, escapes, returns, and total energy/scrap through final acceptance.

**S8. Use shared emergency courier lanes for suitable Critical-downtime orders.** Target Critical -> emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van, especially recurring corridors with empty return travel. Pool compatible urgent deliveries or arrange backhauls when pickup timing, custody, capacity, and destination commitments remain feasible. Retain a dedicated van when sharing would jeopardize restoration deadlines. The dedicated-van variant's 14h median and 3% lateness versus the critical ground exception's 23h and 9% make an unconditional substitution inappropriate. Monitor complete-trip emissions, loading, detours, empty distance, restoration-part arrival, and availability of an independent fallback.

**S9. Distinguish confirmed downtime from precautionary urgency.** Add an urgency classification and required restoration deadline at Receive order/Availability check. Route precautionary urgent orders through normal stock or planned production when slack allows; preserve immediate escalation for confirmed downtime and uncertainty that cannot safely be resolved. This can avoid unnecessary emergency production, air, and dedicated vans. Classification must not delay genuine emergencies or remove contractual entitlements. Monitor classification changes, expedite_flag, emergency-production frequency, decision time, actual service consequences, and cases requiring rescue expedites. Customer_type alone is insufficient evidence of urgency.

**S10. Offer carbon-aware promise choices to Regular Made-to-order customers.** At order acceptance, offer an explicit service choice whose longer deadline permits compatible batching and ground shipment. This can replace CNC-immediately -> air with a feasible planned route. Communicate the actual timing trade-off and use only credible environmental estimates. Record the original requested date, accepted promise, subsequent changes, and consent; do not relabel lateness by moving promises after an order is committed. Monitor acceptance, cycle time, cancellation, batch energy, final transport, and later urgent requests. A longer promise does not justify extra speculative production.

**S11. Improve availability checks and control partial shipments.** Target Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Package/Backorder wait. The partial-plus-backorder variant has 79h median cycle time, 24% lateness, and 7.2 kgCO2e transport per order, indicating both service and duplicate-shipment burdens. Use line-level available-to-promise checks, synchronized reservation, and reliable replenishment dates. Combine fulfillment where all required items can arrive on time; dispatch a usable critical item separately when waiting would prolong downtime. Monitor complete-order delivery, line readiness, shipment count, packaging, backorder age, cancellations, and customer use of partial deliveries. Avoid solving availability errors simply by inflating inventory.

**S12. Record and review sustainability and quality exceptions.** Add a decision-linked exception event before an SP1 override, an explicit Critical-downtime justification before a dedicated van under SP2, and inspection certification for any permitted Q1 exception. Store the decision-maker, reason, alternatives, deadline, predicted service, disruption state, and estimated impact. This exposes preventable waiting that later causes expediting and supports corrective action. It does not directly save a fixed amount of energy or emissions. Keep emergency entry fast and review promptly; an administrative bottleneck must not delay critical dispatch. Missing SP2 justification in 31% of van cases is a documentation/control gap, not proof that all those trips were unnecessary.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching saves machine energy but consumes service slack.** Waiting for CNC or heat batches can trigger air shipment, emergency work, or cancellation, outweighing processing savings. Optimize through delivery, cap cumulative waiting across both stages, and measure subsequent premium transport.
2. **Batch efficiency creates obsolescence.** Filling batches with forecast demand can reduce kWh per produced item while increasing energy and material per shipped item. The 6% unshipped-after-14-days signal warrants aging and write-off tracking; it is not a proven 6% scrap rate. Use demand-backed quantities and count eventual disposal and storage.
3. **Ground and consolidation move burdens into queues and rescue shipments.** A missed departure can create a last-minute van, duplicate shipment, or customer-arranged collection. Count all legs, including partner/customer expedites where observable, and retain feasible departures when consolidation fails.
4. **Vehicle sharing reduces empty travel but adds detours and handling.** Savings allocated to one order may conceal a longer total route, damage, or slower critical restoration. Evaluate the whole vehicle trip and worst affected service commitment, including backhaul travel and carrier operations outside the distributor's gate.
5. **Lighter packaging can create heavier downstream waste.** Damaged heavy parts can require new production, packaging, and express redelivery. Conversely, reinforcing every parcel unnecessarily increases material use. Choose protection by geometry, weight, route, and evidenced damage risk, with Q1 enforced.
6. **Rework reduction can become defect displacement.** Lower internal rework may mean inspection is missing faults; moving work to CNC_A can also move congestion into heat treatment or inspection. Track customer escapes, total repeated work, and all downstream queues, not just a machine's reported yield.
7. **Fewer partial shipments can worsen customer downtime or inflate stock.** Waiting to complete a basket may hold a usable urgent part, while blanket inventory increases shift impacts to replenishment and obsolete stock. Use line-level criticality, reliable reservation, and total stock-lifecycle accounting.
8. **Customer behavior can erase scheduling gains.** Customers may order earlier and duplicate precautionary stock, select longer promises and later demand an expedite, or label routine demand as downtime to obtain priority. Monitor later upgrades, duplicate orders, cancellations, realized urgency, and customer-level ordering changes without obstructing genuine emergencies.
9. **Efficiency savings can increase absolute activity and weaken buffers.** Cheaper fulfillment may stimulate smaller, more frequent orders; higher machine utilization or fewer carriers can remove recovery capacity. Report absolute cohort/period impacts alongside normalized intensity, and preserve tested route and production alternatives.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a coordinated release-to-delivery redesign with three connected elements.

First, prevent avoidable work through S6 packaging compatibility, S7 inspection improvement, and S11 better availability/reservation, supported by S12 exception records. These address visible sources of repeated production, packaging, shipping, and delay. Improve inspection capacity and first-piece controls before adding gates, and retain beneficial critical partial shipments. These changes make later transport decisions more reliable because fewer orders lose their slack in rework.

Second, implement S1 with S4 for Standard-stock Service-contract orders and a limited S5 consolidation pilot for non-urgent stock lanes. Priority picking recovers time that can make ground feasible. Consolidate only when departure reliability and load conditions support both environmental improvement and the order deadline. S9 supplies meaningful urgency information, enabling a limited S8 shared-courier pilot while preserving dedicated vans for true downtime that shared service cannot meet.

Third, implement S2 and S3 together for Made-to-order work: route compatible parts to CNC_A where capacity permits, batch against actual demand, and release at the latest safe time across CNC, heat treatment, inspection, and delivery. Offer S10 prospectively to Regular customers who value a longer, lower-impact service option. Do not chase the largest batch or lowest machine energy independently of final delivery and quality.

Use a constrained decision hierarchy: satisfy safety/quality, genuine restoration deadlines, contractual service, and R1; then compare the feasible alternatives on lifecycle energy, transport emissions, materials, and operational cost. Treat the observed service performance as an initial reference to preserve or improve, with contractual requirements taking precedence where stricter. In particular, SP1's above-85% threshold must not become permission to move a Service-contract segment from 5% lateness toward 15%. Record a governed SP1 exception when a stricter service requirement makes the nominally greener route unsuitable.

No wholesale air ban, van replacement, machine shutdown, or compulsory shipment consolidation is justified. The recommended package protects justified premium service while attacking its preventable causes and verifying that savings persist through returns and inventory aging.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

1. **Rebuild an auditable baseline.** Reconcile starts, terminations, missing events, repeated edges, and open/censored cases. Retain the incoming-order cohort and follow unresolved orders rather than dropping them. Add linked order-line, part, batch, shipment, inventory-lot, and return/replacement objects. Meter each batch or vehicle movement once, allocate by a documented physical basis, and reconcile allocated totals to source totals. Validate transport factors and distinguish measured values from estimates. Add weights, geometry, material type, inspection certification, queue/resource state, original and accepted deadlines, route alternatives, and decision-time predictions where absent.

2. **Use explicit policy checks alongside process discovery.** The low-precision discovered model is useful for exploration, but its permissive combinations must not define acceptable behavior. Segment normal, emergency, and disruption variants and test the following constraints directly:

   - **SP1:** At the actual routing decision, reconstruct whether ground or consolidation could meet the deadline with probability strictly above 85%, using information available then. Check that a qualifying route was selected or a documented, justified exception applies, including stricter contractual service. Audit reliability calibration by lane and segment; do not use hindsight as the prediction.
   - **SP2:** Every dedicated-van dispatch must have a prior, explicit Critical-downtime justification tied to the order and operational need. Separate a missing record from an unjustified use and investigate both.
   - **Q1:** Every Made-to-order part over 8 kg must have reinforced packaging or the required inspection certification of low damage risk. A generic sustainability override is insufficient. Recheck after route or packaging changes.
   - **R1:** Service-contract routing must retain at least one feasible alternative during carrier disruption. Verify capacity, cutoff, geography, and transit feasibility, and exercise failover rather than relying on a carrier list.

3. **Pilot in stages with valid comparisons.** Begin with a shadow shipping recommender and data-quality checks, then test eligible stock lanes, packaging/availability changes, and compatible Made-to-order families. Use randomized eligible groups where practical, or matched controls and staged rollout controlling for part family, weight, distance, urgency, demand, and disruption. Batch, inspection, and carrier interventions create spillovers, so compare appropriate machine shifts, lanes, or rollout clusters instead of assuming orders are independent. Measure the package effect and avoid summing overlapping option estimates. Run until there is sufficient service evidence and follow-up for returns and obsolescence, including the 14-day inventory signal and subsequent disposition.

4. **Monitor all five segments and vulnerable tails.** Compare median/p90 cycle time, complete-order lateness, cancellation, first-pass yield, and returns against each segment's baseline and contractual targets. Give particular attention to Standard-stock Service-contract's 5% lateness/41h p90, Critical-downtime's 4%/30h, and the already weak Made-to-order Regular 18%/180h. Review true downtime separately from precautionary urgent orders while retaining the original aggregate series so relabeling cannot manufacture improvement. Check Regular-order starvation, critical restoration deadlines, and performance under disruption, not just overall averages.

5. **Predefine acceptance and rollback rules.** Agree service/quality non-deterioration limits and disruption-capacity requirements before launch, using no deterioration as the default pending an explicit business decision. Report uncertainty intervals and exposure counts; a small sample with no detected harm does not establish safety for rare critical failures. Expand only when environmental improvement survives full-path accounting and service, quality, and resilience criteria are met. Pause the relevant rule if it causes unacceptable lateness, a Q1 failure, rising customer defects, excessive queue aging, or loss of R1 fallback. Restore established routing or smaller-batch release while correcting the cause.

6. **Make exceptions operationally useful.** Assign ownership across service operations, production/quality, logistics, and sustainability/data teams. Capture concise reason codes and decision evidence at the event, allow authorized rapid emergency action, and promptly review outcomes. Review repeated overrides by root cause—late picking, inspection congestion, unavailable packaging, carrier failure, or misclassified urgency—and change the upstream process where warranted. Maintain versioned rules and prediction models, audit exception patterns for gaming, and review absolute impacts, normalized impacts, stock aging, and supplier/carrier displacement together before claiming sustained improvement.
