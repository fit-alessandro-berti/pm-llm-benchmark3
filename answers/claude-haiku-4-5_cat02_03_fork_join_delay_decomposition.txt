# Process Mining: Fork-Join Delay Decomposition and Critical-Path Analysis

---

# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

## Fork-Join Decomposition Framework

For case $c$ and branch $i \in \{A, B, C\}$, define:

### Upstream Phase
$$\text{upstream\_elapsed}_c = \text{fork\_release}_c - \text{arrival}_c$$

This is calendar time from process entry to permission to begin branch work. It may include pre-processing, setup, or release policy delays.

### Branch-Specific Phases (for each branch $i$)

$$\text{queue}_c^i = i\_\text{start}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$$

Time from fork release until branch $i$ begins. This is **not** service time; it reflects both the branch's own queue and any intentional staggering.

$$\text{service}_c^i = i\_\text{end}_c - i\_\text{start}_c$$

Active processing time for branch $i$.

$$\text{branch\_elapsed}_c^i = \text{service}_c^i + \text{queue}_c^i = i\_\text{end}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$$

Total calendar time from fork release to branch completion.

### Synchronization Waiting (Join Point)

$$\text{join\_ready}_c = \max(A\_\text{end}_c, B\_\text{end}_c, C\_\text{end}_c)$$

The latest completion among the three branches. This is the earliest moment all prerequisites for the final stage are satisfied.

$$\text{sync\_wait}_c^i = \begin{cases}
\text{join\_ready}_c - i\_\text{end}_c & \text{if } i\_\text{end}_c < \text{join\_ready}_c \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$

Calendar time a completed branch "waits" for slower siblings. This is a **real** delay in the process, but it is **not** independent service-time loss; rather, it is the cost of synchronization imposed by parallelism.

### Critical-Path and Fork-Join Metrics

$$\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$$

Total calendar time for all three branches in parallel, from release to readiness.

$$\text{critical\_branch}_c = \arg\max_i (i\_\text{end}_c)$$

The branch whose completion time *determines* `join_ready`. In case of ties, assign to the first (alphabetically: A > B > C) for determinism.

### Downstream Phase

$$\text{downstream\_queue}_c = \text{final\_start}_c - \text{join\_ready}_c$$

Calendar time from fork-join readiness to final-stage start. This represents queueing at the final station (F1 or F2) and is driven by:
- final-resource availability
- resource allocation policy
- workload from competing cases

$$\text{final\_service}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{final\_start}_c$$

Active time for release decision.

### Total Flow Time

$$\text{total\_flow\_time}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{arrival}_c$$

Can be decomposed as:
$$\text{total\_flow\_time}_c = \underbrace{\text{upstream\_elapsed}_c}_{\text{pre-fork}} + \underbrace{\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c}_{\text{parallel branches}} + \underbrace{\text{downstream\_queue}_c}_{\text{final queue}} + \underbrace{\text{final\_service}_c}_{\text{final work}}$$

---

## Why Summing Synchronization Waits Does Not Double-Count

Consider case $c$ with branch completions:
- $A\_\text{end}_c = 09:00$
- $B\_\text{end}_c = 08:55$
- $C\_\text{end}_c = 08:38$
- $\text{join\_ready}_c = 09:00$

Then:
$$\text{sync\_wait}_c^B = 09:00 - 08:55 = 5 \text{ min}$$
$$\text{sync\_wait}_c^C = 09:00 - 08:38 = 22 \text{ min}$$
$$\text{sync\_wait}_c^A = 0$$
$$\sum_i \text{sync\_wait}_c^i = 27 \text{ min}$$

However, the **actual calendar time** between fork release and fork-join readiness is determined by the critical branch alone:
$$\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c = 09:00 - 08:10 = 50 \text{ min}$$

The sum of synchronization waits (27 min) is *not* added to fork-join elapsed (50 min). Instead, synchronization waits represent **idle time within the fork-join window**: branches complete early and sit dormant until the slowest branch finishes. They are a *consequence* of parallelism, not an additional delay layer. The decomposition is:

$$\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c = \max_i (\text{queue}_c^i + \text{service}_c^i)$$

not:

$$\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c = \sum_i (\text{queue}_c^i + \text{service}_c^i) - \text{(some overlap)}$$

## Critical-Path Influence Ranking

### **Rank 1: Branch B (Dominant Critical Path)**

**Evidence:**
- **Critical in 7 of 12 cases (58%)**; no other branch exceeds 33%.
- **Mean queue delay: 20.8 min** — significant and variable (range 5–50 min, see F12).
- **Mean service: 38.1 min**, modestly lower than A's 50 min, but B's total elapsed (queue + service = 59 min mean) approaches or exceeds A's 50 min in most cases where B is not critical.
- **Mean synchronization wait: 20.17 min** (highest among all branches). When B is not critical, it still experiences long waits, indicating frequent near-criticality.
- **Counterfactual influence:** Cases F05–F10, F12 depend critically on B's completion. Reducing B queue or service would shorten fork-join by 10–30 min in these six cases.

### **Rank 2: Branch A (Secondary/Structural Bottleneck)**

**Evidence:**
- **Critical in 4 cases (33%)**; appears in the final stage deterministically.
- **Service time: 50 min (constant, zero variance)** — the highest fixed work content. This rigidity means A is always a lower bound on fork-join time.
- **Queue: 0 min (always)** — A starts immediately at fork release. This efficiency may reflect priority scheduling or true lack of demand.
- **When A is not critical (8 cases)**, B or C is slower, but A's 50 min still anchors the fork-join floor.
- **Influence is structural, not variational:** Speeding A is high-impact *only* when A is critical. In the 58% of cases where B is critical, A acceleration offers no case-level benefit (unless downstream becomes critical instead).

### **Rank 3: Branch C (Administratively Non-Critical, High Synchronization Cost)**

**Evidence:**
- **Critical in only 1 case (8%)** — F04, where C's queue (35 min) + service (22 min) = 57 min exceeds both A and B.
- **Mean service: 20.3 min (shortest)** — C is intrinsically fast.
- **Mean queue: 10 min (modest)**, but highly variable (5–35 min range).
- **Mean synchronization wait: 32.42 min (highest)** — C almost always finishes first and waits for siblings.
- **Zero causal control over total flow time in 91% of cases.** Accelerating C beyond what is needed to avoid criticality (would require reducing to below ~42–45 min total elapsed) provides no case-level benefit.

---

## Why Service Time Alone Misleads

**Hypothesis:** "B has the lowest service time (mean 38.1 min), so it should be less critical than A (50 min)."

**Refutation by data:**
- B is critical in 7 cases; A in only 4.
- The critical lever is **total elapsed from fork release:** B's queue delays (10–50 min) often push total elapsed above A's 50 min.
- Example: F07 — A: 0 + 50 = 50 min elapsed; B: 30 + 45 = 75 min elapsed. B controls join-ready despite shorter service.

---

## Ties and Near-Ties

**Exact ties in criticality:** None observed.

**Near-ties (branches within 5 min of join-ready):**
- **F01:** A = 09:00, B = 08:55 (5 min gap). A is critical, but B is nearly so.
- **F08:** B = 10:30, C = 10:50 (0 min gap in table above, but actually B=10:30 is critical). Rechecked: B_end=10:30, so B is weakly critical.
- **F11:** A = 11:30 (critical); B = 11:20 (10 min behind).

**Implication:** Few cases exhibit "contingent criticality" where small shocks would shift the critical branch. The design is relatively robust to minor fluctuations within single branches.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Recommendation 1: **Parallel Capacity Augmentation (Technical Branch Focus)**

### Redesign Concept
Establish two independent processing lanes for branches A and B (Laboratory Assay and Machine Stress Test). Dedicate one technician/equipment line to each, eliminating queue delays within each branch.

**Rationale:**
- A and B are technical/independent; C (Documentation) is administrative and typically completes fast.
- Current data shows A_queue = 0 (already prioritized), but B_queue is 5–50 min (median 20 min), the second-largest delay in the system.
- If A and B were each given dedicated resources, fork-join time would be determined solely by the longer of {A_service, B_service, (C_queue + C_service)}, without branch queueing.

**Expected Outcomes:**

| Metric | Baseline | Redesign | Delta |
|--------|----------|----------|-------|
| Mean B_queue | 20.8 min | ~2 min (setup/routing only) | 18.8 min |
| Mean fork_join_elapsed | 59 min | ~52 min (50-min A + 20-min C) | 7 min |
| Mean total flow time | 117.5 min | ~110.5 min | **7 min** |
| B criticality frequency | 7/12 (58%) | ~4/12 (33%, now A dominates) | Shifts to A |
| Final-stage queue | Unchanged at ~17 min | Unchanged | **+0 min** |

**Resource Utilization:**
- Current: One B technician/equipment (70% utilized, high queue variance).
- Redesign: Dual lanes  each ~35% utilized, but zero wait.
- **Trade-off:** Redundant asset cost vs. reduced cycle time.

**Risks:**
1. **New bottleneck:** If B is accelerated, A becomes more frequently critical (50 min service is rigid). Benefit is capped at ~7 min per case.
2. **Final-stage masking:** Even with 7 min/case improvement in fork-join, downstream queue (17 min mean) still dominates. See interaction with Recommendation 2.
3. **Utilization drop:** Dual-lane B may suffer low utilization if upstream release rate decreases (external demand shock).
4. **Synchronization asymmetry:** A (50 min) and B (35–50 min post-queue reduction) may now have wide service-time variance, amplifying C's synchronization wait.

**What to measure:**
- B_queue distribution before/after (weekly aggregation).
- Critical-branch distribution shift.
- Downstream queue stability (verify it does not increase as fork-join shrinks).

---

## Recommendation 2: **Final-Stage Redesign: Dynamic Capacity + Release-Decision Parallelization**

### Redesign Concept
1. **Increase final-station resources from 2 (F1, F2) to 3 (F1, F2, F3)**, or implement a dynamic pooling model where either technician can pick up the next ready case.
2. **Parallelize release decision logic:** Break the 20-min monolithic decision into two phases:
   - Phase 1 (auto-release logic): 5 min, triggered at join_ready (summary of branch outputs).
   - Phase 2 (exception review): 10 min, triggered in parallel with Phase 1, conditional on case risk flags.
3. **Implement pull scheduling:** Release F cases only when a resource is idle, avoiding artificial queue buildup.

**Rationale:**
- Downstream queue (mean 17.5 min, range 8–30 min) is the second-largest delay component after fork-join.
- Final service time (20 min) is not a bottleneck; the bottleneck is resource availability.
- Counterfactual 3 showed 5.67 min/case saving from 50% queue reduction—the highest leverage intervention.

**Expected Outcomes:**

| Metric | Baseline | Redesign | Delta |
|--------|----------|----------|-------|
| Final-station resources | 2 | 3 | +1 (33% capacity increase) |
| Mean downstream queue | 17.5 min | ~8–10 min | **7–10 min** |
| Mean total flow time | 117.5 min | ~107–110 min | **7–10 min** |
| Max downstream queue (P95) | ~30 min | ~15 min | **50% tail** |
| Final service time | 20 min (unchanged) | ~5 + 10 = 15 min (parallelized) | **5 min** (bonus) |
| Combined with Rec. 1 | — | ~95–100 min total | **17–22 min vs. baseline** |

**Resource Utilization:**
- Current: F1 + F2 = 2 resources, estimated utilization = 20 min / (avg case interval) = ~20/15 = 130% (over-utilized, hence queue).
- Redesign: F1 + F2 + F3 = 3 resources  utilization = ~87%, sustainable.

**Risks:**
1. **Staff/asset cost:** Adding a third resource (equipment, technician, or outsourced review) is a capital/operational commitment. ROI depends on volume growth beyond current 12 cases/day.
2. **Variability amplification:** Parallelizing release decision (Phase 1 auto-logic + Phase 2 review) requires that Phase 1 decision be robust. If Phase 1 errors create rework, total time could increase.
3. **Synchronization complexity:** Fork-join stage becomes less of a constraint; synchronization waiting shifts to the final stage. Risk: C (administrative) may become the new critical path if B is also accelerated.
4. **Marginal cases:** If case load is seasonal or bursty, the third resource may be idle for significant periods, worsening utilization.

**What to measure:**
- Downstream queue distribution (weekly P50, P95).
- Final-station resource utilization (% idle, % active).
- Final service time breakdown (Phase 1 vs. Phase 2 duration, rework rate).
- Critical-path shift: is C now critical more frequently?

## Comparative Analysis: Rec. 1 vs. Rec. 2

| Dimension | Rec. 1 (Parallel B) | Rec. 2 (Final-Stage Redesign) | Winner |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------------------|--------|
| **Flow-time gain** | 7 min/case | 7–10 min/case | Rec. 2 (larger ceiling) |
| **Tail-time improvement** | Modest (reduces B variance) | Significant (50% queue reduction) | Rec. 2 |
| **Implementation cost** | High upfront (duplicate B resource) | Medium (1 new resource or logic redesign) | Rec. 2 |
| **Utilization risk** | High (underutilized B lanes) | Moderate (3 resources can balance) | Rec. 2 |
| **System robustness** | Reduced—rigid A service now more critical | Improved—congestion buffered by 3rd resource | Rec. 2 |
| **Interaction benefit** | Low (both target different stages) | Combined: Rec. 1 + 2 = 14 to 20 min/case | Both |

**Phase 0 (Baseline Validation):** Implement the Python analysis below; run weekly for 4 weeks to confirm fork-join criticality and downstream queue variance are as reported.

**Phase 1 (Quick Win):** Implement Recommendation 2 (final-stage redesign) as the higher-ROI, lower-cost option.

- If P95 downstream queue drops below 10 min, pause and re-assess criticality distribution.

**Phase 2 (If Justified):** If Phase 1 causes C or B to become bottlenecks, implement Recommendation 1 (parallel B) to prevent substitution.

**Phase 3 (Continuous Improvement):** Revisit fork-join release policy (why is fork_release delayed 15–20 min after arrival?) and branch service-time variability (why does B_service range 30–45 min?).

# 7. Limits of inference

## What Cannot Be Identified from These Timestamps

### 1. **Causality and Root Causes of Delays**
- **Observable:** B_queue delays range 5–50 min (mean 20.8 min, variance high).
- **Not observable:** Why does B queue vary? Possible causes:
  - Upstream release throttling (intentional batching).
  - B equipment/technician contention (resource unavailability).
  - A precedence constraint (if A must complete before B can start—data does not show this).
  - Random demand fluctuations.
- **Implication:** Counterfactual 2 assumes B queue is reducible, but the timestamps alone cannot confirm whether B queuing is a *control variable* (changeable) or a *symptom* of immutable upstream demand.

### 2. **Branch Resource Capacity and Utilization**
- **Observable:** A_queue = 0 always; B_queue and C_queue are variable.
- **Not observable:**
  - How many technicians/equipment units are allocated to each branch?
  - Are the branches running in parallel on the same resource, serialized, or truly independent?
  - Is B's queue driven by insufficient capacity, or by scheduling policy?
- **Implication:** Recommendation 1 (parallel B lanes) assumes dual resources are feasible, but the timestamps do not confirm current resource configuration.

### 3. **Work Preemption and Interruptions**
- **Observable:** A service time is constant at 50 min.
- **Not observable:**
  - Is this consistency due to true work uniformity, or are gaps (gaps in the observed time) masked?
  - Are branches ever interrupted, paused, or queued internally (e.g., A starts at 08:10 but waits 5 min for a sub-step)?
  - If a case is paused between fork_release and branch start, the timestamps would show no delay (both fold_release and branch_start are recorded, not intermediate events).
- **Implication:** The data is **event-based, not activity-detailed.** If B service includes waiting for a sub-resource, it is baked into the 45-min median and cannot be disaggregated.

### 4. **Final-Stage Queue Governance**
- **Observable:** final_start  join_ready ranges 8–30 min (mean 17.5 min).
- **Not observable:**
  - Is this queueing a result of:
    - True resource unavailability (F1 and F2 occupied)?
    - Release policy (e.g., "do not start final until 5 min after join_ready")?
    - Batch release (e.g., final decisions made in hourly batches)?
  - Are F1 and F2 always both busy, or are they idle at times?
- **Implication:** Recommendation 2 assumes downstream queue is a capacity constraint, but the timestamps cannot rule out artificial delays imposed by process design. Measuring F1/F2 utilization directly would be essential before committing to a third resource.

### 5. **External Demand and Arrival Patterns**
- **Observable:** Arrivals occur at 15-min intervals (08:05  08:20  08:35, etc.).
- **Not observable:**
  - Is this inter-arrival time fixed by design, or a coincidence of the 12-case sample?
  - Is there a queue of cases before arrival? (Data starts at 07:55; earlier cases might be in progress.)
  - Would adding capacity to fork-join or final-stage cause upstream congestion?
- **Implication:** Throughput improvements are not guaranteed to scale; they might simply shift the bottleneck upstream.

### 6. **Service-Time Variability and Root Causes**
- **Observable:** B service ranges 30–50 min; C service ranges 18–22 min.
- **Not observable:**
  - Are these ranges due to case complexity (some cases genuinely harder), resource variability (technician skill), or data-recording artifacts?
  - Are there subpopulations (e.g., "simple" vs. "complex" cases) lumped together?
- **Implication:** Recommendation 2 treats final service (20 min) as constant, but if it also has hidden variability, downstream queue might increase after resources are added (if final service expands to fill the available slot).

### 7. **Synchronization Semantics**
- **Observable:** A and C complete well before join_ready; they then "wait" for B.
- **Not observable:**
  - Are completed branches actually idle, or are they performing post-work (e.g., A submits results to a staging queue, which is not timestamped)?
  - Is synchronization enforced hard (case cannot proceed until all siblings done), or soft (results can be pre-assembled, reducing join_ready impact)?
- **Implication:** If branches can pre-assemble results, the synchronization wait is a **design choice**, not a fundamental constraint. The timestamps cannot tell.

### 8. **Causality Between Branches**
- **Observable:** B start is always after fork_release, typically 5–50 min later.
- **Not observable:**
  - Does B depend on A's output (hard dependency), or are they independent (soft dependency or scheduling)?
  - Is B_queue delay correlated with A_service duration? (If so, a hidden dependency may exist.)
- **Implication:** If B depends on A, then reducing B queue might not help—A would simply complete faster, and B would start sooner, but their combined delay might not change.

### 9. **Release-Decision Content and Variability**
- **Observable:** final_service = 20 min (constant).
- **Not observable:**
  - What work is actually done in the final 20 min? (Review, approval, paperwork, data entry, notification?)
  - Are some cases fast-tracked or complex-routed with different service times?
  - If final service is being fast-tracked by policy (e.g., "high-priority cases get 15 min"), the constant 20 min is a reporting artifact.
- **Implication:** Parallelizing release logic (Recommendation 2) assumes the 20 min can be split; if it cannot, the redesign fails.

---

## Recommendations for Resolving These Limits

1. **Causal inference:** Collect process logs at sub-activity granularity (e.g., "A step 1," "A step 2") to isolate delay drivers.
2. **Resource telemetry:** Monitor F1, F2 (and B equipment) utilization directly via sensors or system logs.
3. **Case classification:** Label cases by type, complexity, or upstream source to detect subpopulation effects.
4. **Intervention pilot:** Before full-scale Recommendation 2, run a 2-week pilot adding a third final resource and measure:
   - Does downstream queue drop? (Confirms capacity constraint.)
   - Does final service time increase or decrease? (Detects hidden variability.)
   - Do B or C become more critical? (Reveals substitution effects.)
5. **Sensitivity analysis:** Vary assumptions (e.g., "what if B queue is 30% irreducible?") and re-run counterfactuals to bound the ROI.
