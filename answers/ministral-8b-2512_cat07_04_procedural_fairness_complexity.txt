# 4. Plausible mechanisms

### Legitimate mechanisms
1. **Case complexity**: Groups B, C, and D have higher `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, and `prior_case_count`, leading to longer paths (e.g., V3 for Group D).
2. **Policy requirements**: Disability accommodation and language support are rights-preserving but create procedural steps (e.g., V4 for Group B).

### Concerning mechanisms
3. **Resource constraints**: Limited bilingual/accommodation specialists force longer waits (e.g., 3.8 days for language support) and handovers.
4. **Historical bias**: `risk_score_band` may encode past disparities (e.g., Groups B/D have higher scores but not necessarily higher fraud risk).
5. **Procedural friction**: Support steps (e.g., Language Support Contact) add delays without clear benefit to outcome quality.
6. **Evaluative bias**: Supervisor Review is more frequent for Groups B/C, possibly reflecting unconscious bias in "complexity" assessment.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

### Measurement bias
- `risk_score_band` may reflect historical disparities (e.g., Groups B/D scored higher due to past under-resourcing).
- `documentation_completeness` could be affected by language barriers (e.g., Group B’s 46% incompleteness vs. Group A’s 21%).

### Missing applicant needs
- No data on **cognitive disabilities**, **mental health support needs**, or **cultural navigation assistance**.
- `case_outcome` does not capture **quality of explanation** or **dignity of treatment**.

### Unobserved resource workload
- Daily granularity of `org:resource` may mask sudden spikes in workload (e.g., seasonal surges).
- No data on **caseworker turnover** or **training quality** for support steps.

### Appeal quality
- `appeal_flag` does not indicate **grounds for appeal** (e.g., procedural error vs. substantive disagreement).
- No data on **appeal success rates** or **quality of appeal decisions**.

### Historical bias
- `prior_case_count` may reflect past procedural barriers (e.g., Groups B/C had more rejections in the past).
- `region` could encode systemic inequities (e.g., rural regions have fewer bilingual specialists).

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process exhibits **procedural disparities** that persist even after controlling for case complexity. While some routing differences (e.g., Fraud Review for Group D, Accommodation Review for Group C) are justified by policy and documented complexity, other disparities are concerning:

1. **Support-step delays** (e.g., 3.8 days for language support, 4.7 days for accommodation review) create unnecessary procedural burden for Groups B and C, even after accounting for resource constraints.
2. **Handover inefficiencies** (e.g., General  Bilingual  General for Group B) add friction without clear benefit.
3. **Resource imbalances** (e.g., 7 bilingual caseworkers handling 57% of Group B cases) suggest inequitable workload distribution.
4. **Adjusted outcome disparities** (e.g., Group B’s 5.5 percentage-point lower approval probability) hint at potential evaluative biases beyond objective complexity.

**The process is not procedurally fair** in its current form, as it imposes disproportionate burdens on Groups B and C without clear justification. However, the evidence is **indeterminate** regarding whether these disparities stem from legitimate complexity, resource constraints, or systemic inequities. Further investigation (e.g., audits of `risk_score_band`, qualitative interviews, or experimental interventions) is needed to distinguish between these possibilities.

**Key recommendations**:
- Prioritize **resource capacity** for support steps (language/accommodation) to reduce delays.
- Implement **service-level targets** for support steps to ensure timeliness.
- Redesign **routing** to minimize handovers and improve consistency.
- Monitor **adjusted disparities** over time to track progress.
- Audit **risk scoring and documentation thresholds** for bias.
