## 1. Analytical assumptions

**Interpretation of key terms:**
- *Case notion*: A perspective that defines how events are grouped into traces, typically by selecting a "lead" object type whose lifecycle events form the backbone of the trace. In OCEL, this involves flattening the event log by copying shared events into all relevant cases.
- *Convergence*: The artificial merging of distinct business processes into a single trace due to shared events involving multiple objects of the chosen case notion. This can obscure true process variants.
- *Divergence*: The artificial splitting of a single business process into multiple traces due to the chosen case notion not capturing the full lifecycle of related objects. This can truncate lifecycles or hide dependencies.

**Treatment of copied shared events:**
- Copied shared events are treated as *both evidence and duplication*. They provide evidence of cross-object interactions (informative) but also introduce redundancy (duplication) that can inflate frequencies and distort performance metrics. The duplication is not inherently harmful if acknowledged and accounted for in analysis.

**Analytical transformations:**
- *Explicit relation propagation*: I permit propagating object relations through the O2O graph (e.g., inferring that an event related to `P3` is also related to `O2` and `O3` via the `packs` relation) *only if this propagation is explicitly documented and justified*. This is useful for capturing indirect dependencies but risks overgeneralization if not carefully controlled.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

### 1. Cross-order packages: `P3` in Order flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Order (`O2`, `O3`).
- **Events/objects**: `e13` (Pack Package), `e16` (Dispatch), `e24` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: In the Order flattening, `e13` appears in both `O2` and `O3` traces, but the DFG will show a direct edge from "Add Item" to "Pack Package" for both orders, hiding the fact that `P3` contains items from both orders. This inflates the frequency of the "Pack Package" activity and distorts performance metrics (e.g., time between "Add Item" and "Pack Package" appears shorter than reality because `P3` is packed once for both orders).
- **Effect**: Harmful for order-centric analysis, as it obscures cross-order consolidation. Informative for logistics analysis if acknowledged.

### 2. Cross-order packages: `P4` in Item flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Item (`I32`, `I41`).
- **Events/objects**: `e14` (Pack Package), `e17` (Dispatch), `e27` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: In the Item flattening, `I32` and `I41` appear to be packed and delivered together in `P4`, but the order-level context (e.g., `I32` belongs to `O3` and `I41` to `O4`) is lost. The DFG will show a direct edge from "Add Item" to "Pack Package" for both items, but the shared package is not visible.
- **Effect**: Harmful for item-level tracking, as it hides cross-order dependencies. Context-dependent for logistics analysis.

### 3. Consolidated delivery: `D2` in Order flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Order (`O1`, `O2`, `O3`).
- **Events/objects**: `e16` (Dispatch), `e24` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: In the Order flattening, `e16` and `e24` appear in all three order traces, but the DFG will show "Dispatch" and "Confirm Delivery" as separate events for each order, hiding the consolidation in `D2`. This inflates the frequency of these activities and distorts delivery performance metrics (e.g., delivery time appears shorter because `D2` is confirmed once for all orders).
- **Effect**: Harmful for order-centric analysis, as it obscures delivery consolidation. Informative for logistics analysis if acknowledged.

### 4. Consolidated invoicing: `V2` in Order flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Order (`O2`, `O3`).
- **Events/objects**: `e19` (Issue Invoice), `e22` (Record Payment), `e25` (Record Payment).
- **Distortion**: In the Order flattening, `e19` appears in both `O2` and `O3` traces, but the DFG will show "Issue Invoice" as a separate event for each order, hiding the consolidation in `V2`. This inflates the frequency of invoicing and distorts payment performance metrics (e.g., payment time appears shorter because `V2` is paid once for both orders).
- **Effect**: Harmful for financial analysis, as it obscures invoice consolidation. Informative for order-centric analysis if acknowledged.

### 5. Partial payments: `V2` in Invoice flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Invoice (`V2`).
- **Events/objects**: `e22` (Record Payment), `e25` (Record Payment).
- **Distortion**: In the Invoice flattening, `V2` shows two payment events (`M2` and `M3`), but the DFG will show a direct edge from "Issue Invoice" to "Record Payment" twice, inflating the frequency of payment activities. Performance metrics (e.g., payment time) may be distorted because the partial payments are treated as separate events.
- **Effect**: Harmful for financial analysis if not accounted for. Informative for tracking payment instalments.

### 6. Near-singleton lifecycle: `I41` in Item flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Item (`I41`).
- **Events/objects**: `e10` (Add Item), `e14` (Pack Package), `e17` (Dispatch), `e27` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: `I41` has a near-singleton lifecycle (only 4 events), but the DFG will show a complete path from "Add Item" to "Confirm Delivery." However, the order-level context (e.g., `I41` belongs to `O4`) is lost, and the lifecycle appears artificially short compared to other items (e.g., `I11` and `I12` are split across packages).
- **Effect**: Harmful for item-level tracking, as it hides dependencies. Context-dependent for logistics analysis.

### 7. Truncated lifecycle: `P1` in Package flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Package (`P1`).
- **Events/objects**: `e11` (Pack Package), `e15` (Dispatch), `e23` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: `P1` has a truncated lifecycle (only 3 events), missing order creation/closure and payment events. The DFG will show a direct path from "Pack Package" to "Confirm Delivery," but the lack of order-level context hides why `P1` was created (e.g., to split `O1`).
- **Effect**: Harmful for logistics analysis, as it obscures the rationale for package creation. Informative for package-level tracking if acknowledged.

### 8. Performance bias: `D2` in Delivery flattening
- **Affected flattening**: Delivery (`D2`).
- **Events/objects**: `e16` (Dispatch), `e24` (Confirm Delivery).
- **Distortion**: In the Delivery flattening, `D2` shows a single dispatch and confirmation event, but the DFG will treat this as a single delivery, hiding the fact that `D2` consolidates `P2` and `P3` (and thus `O1`, `O2`, `O3`). Performance metrics (e.g., delivery time) may appear artificially fast because the consolidation is not visible.
- **Effect**: Harmful for logistics optimization, as it obscures consolidation. Informative for delivery-level tracking if acknowledged.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

### Flattened logs to create:
1. **Order-flattened log**:
   - *Safe questions*: Order fulfillment time, order closure rate, item addition patterns.
   - *Unsafe questions*: Cross-order package consolidation, delivery consolidation, invoice consolidation.
   - *Use case*: Order-centric KPIs (e.g., "What percentage of orders are split across packages?").

2. **Item-flattened log**:
   - *Safe questions*: Item-level delays, item damage rates, item packing patterns.
   - *Unsafe questions*: Order-level context, cross-item dependencies, payment patterns.
   - *Use case*: Item-level tracking (e.g., "How often are items from the same order split into different packages?").

3. **Invoice-flattened log**:
   - *Safe questions*: Invoice payment time, partial payment patterns, invoice consolidation.
   - *Unsafe questions*: Order/item/package/delivery context.
   - *Use case*: Financial analysis (e.g., "What percentage of invoices are paid in instalments?").

### Questions requiring object-centric views:
1. **Cross-order package consolidation**:
   - *Tool*: OC-DFG or OTG (Object Type Graph) to show how `P3` and `P4` consolidate items from multiple orders.
   - *Example*: "How often are items from different orders packed together?"

2. **Delivery consolidation**:
   - *Tool*: ET–OT graph (Event Type–Object Type graph) to show how `D2` consolidates `P2` and `P3`.
   - *Example*: "What is the average number of orders per delivery?"

3. **Invoice consolidation**:
   - *Tool*: Object interaction summary to show how `V2` consolidates `O2` and `O3`.
   - *Example*: "What percentage of invoices consolidate multiple orders?"

4. **Partial payments**:
   - *Tool*: OC-DFG or equivalent-execution clustering to group events by invoice (`V2`) and show partial payments (`M2`, `M3`).
   - *Example*: "What is the average number of payments per invoice?"

5. **End-to-end process variants**:
   - *Tool*: Equivalent-execution clustering to group traces by shared objects (e.g., orders split across packages, consolidated deliveries).
   - *Example*: "What are the most common process variants for orders with split packages?"

### Reconciling contradictory findings:
- **Example**: The Order-flattened log shows "Dispatch" as a frequent activity, while the Delivery-flattened log shows fewer "Dispatch" events. This contradiction arises because the Order log duplicates shared delivery events (e.g., `e16` for `D2` appears in `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` traces), while the Delivery log shows `D2` as a single event.
- **Reconciliation**: Use the OC-DFG to show that `D2` consolidates multiple orders, and use the Order log to quantify how often orders are involved in consolidated deliveries. The contradiction is resolved by acknowledging that the Order log inflates frequencies due to duplication, while the Delivery log hides consolidation.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**Position**: **B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

### Decision criteria:
1. **Business intent**: The order-fulfilment process is fundamentally order-centric (customers place orders, not items or packages). Orders are the primary unit of value and accountability.
2. **Convergence/divergence trade-offs**: Order flattening has low convergence risk (orders are mostly independent) but medium divergence risk (shared packages/deliveries truncate lifecycles). This is preferable to high convergence risk (e.g., Delivery flattening) or high divergence risk (e.g., Item flattening).
3. **Completeness**: Order flattening captures the full lifecycle (creation to closure), while other object types truncate lifecycles (e.g., Item, Package) or converge excessively (e.g., Delivery, Invoice).
4. **Secondary perspectives**: Cross-order interactions (e.g., shared packages, consolidated deliveries) require secondary lifecycles (e.g., Package, Delivery) to avoid hiding critical dependencies.

### Strongest supporting evidence:
- Orders are the only object type that captures the full end-to-end process (from `Create Order` to `Close Order`).
- Business facts emphasize order-level splits (e.g., `O1` split across `P1` and `P2`) and consolidations (e.g., `V2` billing `O2` and `O3`), which are best understood from an order-centric perspective.
- Order flattening preserves the most natural business questions (e.g., "How long does it take to fulfill an order?").

### Strongest counterargument:
- Order flattening hides cross-order interactions (e.g., `P3` packing `I21` and `I31` from `O2` and `O3`). This can lead to misleading performance metrics (e.g., underestimating packing time due to shared packages).
- Secondary lifecycles (e.g., Package, Delivery) are required to capture these interactions, adding complexity to the analysis.

### Residual risk:
- The primary lead object (Order) may still distort performance metrics for shared events (e.g., `e16` for `D2` appears in three order traces, inflating dispatch frequencies). This risk is mitigated by:
  1. Using the OC-DFG to quantify duplication.
  2. Supplementing with secondary lifecycles (e.g., Delivery) to analyze shared events.
  3. Explicitly documenting the distortion in reports.

### Conditions for changing the decision:
1. **Shift in business focus**: If the analysis prioritizes logistics (e.g., delivery optimization) over order fulfillment, Delivery could become the primary lead object, with Order as a secondary perspective.
2. **Process changes**: If cross-order interactions (e.g., shared packages) become rare, Order flattening would have even lower convergence risk, strengthening its suitability.
3. **Tooling limitations**: If object-centric tools (e.g., OC-DFG, OTG) are unavailable, a single lead object may be necessary despite its limitations.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

### 1. Removing or down-weighting copied shared events
- **Method**: For the Order-flattened log, identify events that are shared across multiple orders (e.g., `e13`, `e16`, `e19`, `e22`) and either:
  - Remove them from all but one order trace (e.g., keep `e16` only in `O1`).
  - Down-weight their frequency in the DFG (e.g., divide edge weights by the number of orders involved).
- **Validation**: Compare the original and modified DFGs to quantify the inflation of shared event frequencies. For example, the edge from "Pack Package" to "Dispatch" should have a lower weight in the modified DFG for `O2` and `O3` (since `e16` is shared).

### 2. Comparing direct E2O relations with propagated-relation variant
- **Method**: Create two versions of the Order-flattened log:
  - *Direct*: Only include events where the order is directly related (e.g., `e01` for `O1`).
  - *Propagated*: Include events where the order is indirectly related via O2O relations (e.g., `e13` for `O2` via `P3` packing `I21`).
- **Validation**: Compare the two logs to assess how propagation affects lifecycle completeness and convergence. For example, `O2` in the propagated log will include `e13` (Pack Package) and `e16` (Dispatch), while the direct log will not.

### 3. Changing the lead object
- **Method**: Re-run the analysis with Delivery as the primary lead object (instead of Order) and compare:
  - The number of unique traces (Delivery will have fewer traces due to consolidation).
  - The frequency of "Dispatch" and "Confirm Delivery" activities (Delivery will show lower frequencies due to consolidation).
  - The average trace length (Delivery traces will be shorter due to truncated lifecycles).
- **Validation**: Use the OC-DFG to reconcile differences. For example, the Order log shows "Dispatch" as a frequent activity, while the Delivery log shows fewer "Dispatch" events due to consolidation.

### 4. Comparing frequency metrics based on events, unique objects, and total objects in the OC-DFG
- **Method**: For the "Dispatch" activity, compute three frequency metrics:
  - *Event-based*: Count of `Dispatch` events in the Order-flattened log (e.g., 3 events: `e15`, `e16`, `e17`).
  - *Unique object-based*: Count of unique deliveries involved in `Dispatch` events (e.g., 3 deliveries: `D1`, `D2`, `D3`).
  - *Total object-based*: Count of all objects involved in `Dispatch` events (e.g., 15 objects: `O1`, `I11`, `P1`, `D1`, etc. for `e15`; `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, etc. for `e16`; `O3`, `O4`, etc. for `e17`).
- **Validation**: Compare the metrics to assess how duplication and consolidation affect frequency counts. For example, the event-based count (3) is inflated due to shared events, while the unique object-based count (3) reflects the actual number of deliveries.

### Additional sensitivity checks:
5. **Partial payments**: Compare the Invoice-flattened log with and without splitting partial payments (`M2` and `M3` for `V2`) into separate events. Assess how this affects payment time metrics.
6. **Cross-order packages**: Compare the Package-flattened log with and without propagating order relations (e.g., `P3` related to `O2` and `O3`). Assess how this affects package assembly metrics.
7. **Lifecycle truncation**: For the Item-flattened log, compare the average trace length with and without including order creation/closure events (via propagation). Assess how this affects item-level performance metrics.
