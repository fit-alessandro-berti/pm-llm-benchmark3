# Compliance Assessment — Northbridge Sterile Systems, Aachen, ECD-2 Contamination Event (July 2026)

## 1. Interpretive Assumptions and Decision Method

**Facts** (taken from the materials, not argued): the text of GPIP-4, DE-AAC-PROC-17 Rev. 6, ECD-2, and the CFO email; the effective dates and windows; the recorded event times and amounts; the ON-44 outage parameters; the organizational facts, including that the Chief Risk Officer neither issued nor ratified any A7 exception.

**Interpretations** (my reasoning, contestable):

- **I1 — Authority is issue-specific, not global.** A source binds only where its issuer has authority over the control at stake. The COO/Incident Commander have operational authority; GPIP-4 A7 reserves *formal exceptions to GPIP-4* to the Chief Risk Officer. ECD-2 is therefore a valid and authoritative overlay on **local operational workflow** (SOP timing, PO sequencing, supplier-register use) and an invalid instrument for **waiving GPIP-4 integrity controls** (segregation of duties, the two-approver financial control, Compliance clearance for new suppliers or >EUR 100,000). This is the single most consequential interpretive choice in this report; §5 states the counter-reading.
- **I2 — ECD-2 is nevertheless not a nullity.** Under B7 the SOP itself concedes that a corporate incident directive may supersede "ordinary local workflow steps." B1 (PO-before-commit) and B3 (approved-supplier register) are ordinary local workflow/onboarding steps and are within C4's stated reach. B2 (Plant + Finance approval) duplicates a corporate financial control and is *not* ordinary in that sense; ECD-2 does not touch it (C5).
- **I3 — Specificity does not defeat superiority.** The Aachen SOP is more specific than GPIP-4, but A5 permits local procedures only to be *stricter* or evidential. Where the SOP is silent or laxer, GPIP-4 governs.
- **I4 — Later-in-time is not self-executing.** ECD-2 (July 2026) is later than GPIP-4 (January 2026), but a later instrument from a *lower* authority does not repeal a higher one. Later-in-time resolves conflicts only between instruments of equal authority and scope.
- **I5 — The CFO email is weak in form but real in substance, within one lane.** It is uncontrolled (not in the policy repository) and its own D4 disclaims removal of essential integrity controls. But D3 records that the CFO owns and may interpret Finance controls. I therefore treat D1 as a **valid interpretation of who may exercise the Finance-approval control up to EUR 150,000** during the window — i.e. joint Incident Command authorization may stand in for the Finance-Controller sign-off — and as **incapable** of touching Compliance clearance, segregation of duties, or anything above EUR 150,000. D2 adds a Legal condition above EUR 150,000; it does not silently extend D1's sufficiency rule past D1's own ceiling.
- **I6 — Emergency necessity mitigates, it does not authorize.** A4 confines the automatic emergency effect to competitive tendering. Genuine, documented necessity lowers severity and supports discretionary ratification; it does not convert a breach into compliance.
- **I7 — Rules apply at the moment of the act.** The controlling question is always what was in force, and what had been done, at the instant of `Commit_to_Supplier`. Instruments that came into existence later (ECD-2 for a 08:50 commitment on 07-14; the CFO email of 07-15 07:20 for anything before it) are irrelevant to that act.
- **I8 — Retrospective evidence vs. retrospective authorization.** Late *recording* of a timely act is a logging matter and is curable by better evidence (signed PDFs, call recordings, supplier acknowledgments), per the ON-44 note. Late *performance* of a control is not curable: A6 is explicit. Post-hoc Plant/Finance/Compliance events are treated as mitigation and as evidence of eventual review, never as satisfaction of a pre-commitment requirement.
- **I9 — Burden of proof.** The organization bears the burden of showing that a control was performed and that an exception applied. Where a claimed authorization exists only in oral testimony with no artifact (P-506), the burden fails and the case is assessed as if the exception did not exist — not as INDETERMINATE, because the *absence* of the required record is itself the finding.
- **I10 — Scope of ECD-2.** "Critical sterilization-line lots required to contain the current contamination event" is read as: components whose absence would, within the incident horizon, impair containment of the *current* event. This sits between Operations' reading (any backup-line part) and Internal Audit's (24-hour need). Under my reading, P-504 (cafeteria) is plainly outside; P-503 (three days of stock, backup line) is outside on Audit's reading and marginal on mine; P-506 is outside because the window had expired regardless of the goods' purpose.
- **I11 — INDETERMINATE is reserved** for cases where a decisive fact cannot be established from any available evidence and where the outcome genuinely turns on it. No case here qualifies at the top level, because every case has at least one independently decisive, evidentially secure finding. Sub-issues that are indeterminate are flagged as such inside the rows.

## 5. Strongest Alternative Interpretations

**P-502 → JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION.** The premise that must change: that `Compliance_Clearance` for a *new supplier* is an integrity control rather than a **supplier-onboarding requirement**. C4 expressly claims precedence over "supplier-onboarding requirements," and A3's new-supplier trigger is, functionally, the corporate onboarding gate. On that reading C2 and C4 together relieve the pre-commitment clearance, the clearance at 18:30 becomes timely completion of a deferred onboarding step, and only the A2 Finance leg remains — itself arguably de minimis at 62k with two IC authorizers. *Credible* because it takes C4's own words seriously and because the alternative renders C2 nearly useless (one may use an unregistered supplier but may not commit to one). *Weak* because A3's new-supplier trigger sits in a clause about clearance thresholds and integrity, C5 records that ECD-2 never mentioned Compliance, and the organizational facts state that directives have **never** previously been treated as overriding new-supplier Compliance clearance — precedent is absent exactly where it is needed. On balance I retain NONCOMPLIANT, but this is the closest call in the report and my confidence is only medium-high.

**P-505 → NONCOMPLIANT.** Two premises could change, and either suffices. (i) *Evidence*: if the ERP clock skew is unverified, the ERP's 18:06 stands as the system of record and the commitment falls outside the window — after which ECD-2 relief evaporates and the missing pre-commitment Finance approval becomes a plain A2 breach. The counter is that the supplier's own acknowledgment at 17:59:31 is third-party and independent of Northbridge's clocks, and a call cannot conclude before it begins at 17:57. (ii) *Authority*: if the CFO email is treated as a nullity because it is uncontrolled and self-limiting (D4), then no instrument substitutes for the Finance Controller and the 19:00 approval is A6-non-curative. This is a respectable formalist position; I reject it because D3 grants the CFO substantive authority over exactly this control and form should not defeat conceded authority within its own lane. Note the honest asymmetry: this is my only classification that depends on crediting an uncontrolled email.

**P-508 → JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION.** The premise that must change: that D2 states a *condition* rather than an *extension*. Read as an extension — "IC authorization suffices up to 150k; above that, IC authorization plus Legal suffices" — the ladder is coherent, the buyers plainly understood it that way, and every step of it was executed (Compliance, Legal, two IC authorizers, clean segregation of duties, all before commitment). *Credible* because it is the natural reading of a hurried email and because P-508 is otherwise the most carefully controlled purchase in the population. *Weak* because a CFO cannot extend his own authority by implication past a ceiling he himself set, and because A2 at 155k is a corporate control the CFO may interpret only as to the Finance leg — the independent budget owner remains missing on any reading. The severity here is genuinely arguable down to low.

**P-501 → JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION.** The premise that must change: either that ECD-2's protection extends to acts in the immediate run-up to its 09:00 start (the incident was live; the directive was being signed), or that B4's substitution clause tolerates *constructive* absence — a Plant Manager who is nominally reachable but not practically available in a four-hour stoppage window. *Credible* on necessity: the purchase was cheap, from an approved supplier, genuinely urgent, and fully ratified within hours. *Weak* on both limbs: a time-limited directive that could be back-dated by argument would have no limits at all, and the record states affirmatively that the manager *was* reachable and *did not* delegate — B4 conditions substitution on active delegation, which is a documentary act, not a state of mind.

**P-506 → INDETERMINATE.** The premise that must change: the allocation of the burden of proof. If a buyer's contemporaneous good-faith belief that an extension existed is treated as evidence to be weighed rather than a claim to be proved, the case turns on facts no one has yet gathered (IC members' recollections, call records), and INDETERMINATE is the honest label. *Weak* because C3's four-hour recording rule exists precisely to make such claims verifiable, and treating its absence as mere evidential uncertainty rewards the omission. But if any artifact of the extension surfaces, I would revise to INDETERMINATE pending review, and potentially to JUSTIFIED_EXCEPTION given that Finance approval genuinely preceded commitment here.

**P-503 → partial defence, but not a reclassification.** The strongest available argument is scope: Incident Command labelled the pumps "critical contingency," Operations' reading of C6 covers backup lines, and pump lead times may exceed the three-day stock horizon. Even granting all of it, A1 is untouched — a person cannot authorize their own commitment — so the alternative moves severity, not classification. I note this to show that some cases are robust to the scope dispute and others are not.

## 6. Evidential Sufficiency and Remediation

**Four distinct failure types, deliberately kept separate:**

- **Policy breach (a control was not performed when required):** P-503 (A1, A2, A3), P-504 (B3, B1), P-502 (A3, A2 pre-commitment), P-508 (A2), P-506 (B1, budget-owner timing), P-501 (unauthorized committer). These are substantive and are not curable by any subsequent document.
- **Logging defect (control performed, recorded late or in the wrong place):** P-507's authorization reaching the ERP at 14:30 while the signed PDF existed at 12:40; P-501's emergency form filed at hour 23 of 24 — compliant but with no margin.

**Monitoring actions**

- A directive-boundary monitor: any purchase citing an incident directive within ±2 hours of its start/end, or after its expiry, routes to Audit automatically (P-501, P-505, P-506 would all have been caught).
- A scope-drift monitor comparing the cost centre and goods category of directive-cited purchases against the directive's stated scope (P-504 would have fired immediately).
- A self-approval detector across Request/Authorize/Commit/Approve actor identities, running continuously rather than at case closure (P-503).

- A quarterly emergency-usage report: count, value, and share of purchases invoking any emergency route, with a governance review triggered if the share rises above a set baseline.

## 7. Recommended Policy Clarification

**Proposed GPIP-4 clause A8 — Incident Directives, Emergency Authority, and Expiry** *(replacing A4 and A7 and superseding B7 and C4)*

1. **Two-tier authority.** An **Operational Incident Directive** (OID), signed by the COO or an Incident Commander, may suspend or reorder *operational workflow steps* only: purchase-order sequencing, competitive tendering, local approval routing, and use of a supplier outside the approved register. An OID may **never** suspend the following **Core Integrity Controls**: (a) segregation of duties under A1; (b) Compliance clearance under A3, including the new-supplier trigger; (c) the requirement that at least one approver be independent of the requester and committer. Suspension of a Core Integrity Control requires a **written, scoped, time-limited exception signed by the Chief Risk Officer or, if the CRO is unavailable for more than four hours, by any two members of the executive committee acting jointly, one of whom must not be the requesting function.** Such an exception must be ratified or revoked by the CRO within five business days; failure to ratify converts the affected purchases into recorded control failures.
2. **Scope must be stated, not inferred.** Every OID must list the covered production lines, cost centres, and goods categories, and must state whether contingency and backup-line items are included. Goods outside the stated list are governed by ordinary policy regardless of incident context. Where scope is contested at the time of the act, the buyer must record a one-line written scope justification before commitment; an unrecorded scope claim is not a defence.
3. **Thresholds and who may approve.** Below EUR 50,000: joint authorization by two distinct Incident Command members suffices. EUR 50,000–150,000: two distinct IC members **plus** a Finance Controller or a delegate named in writing by the CFO; the CFO may name such delegates but may not remove the second-signature requirement. Above EUR 150,000: the EUR 50,000–150,000 requirements **plus** Compliance and Legal clearance, both before commitment. Thresholds apply to the committed value, not the invoiced value, and may not be met by splitting a purchase.
4. **Segregation of duties is absolute.** No person may occupy more than one of the roles requester, committer, and authorizer on the same purchase, in any emergency, at any value. A purchase breaching this clause is void as to its authorization and must be escalated to the Audit Committee within 24 hours.
5. **New suppliers.** An OID may authorize *use* of an unregistered supplier. It does not waive Compliance clearance, which must be obtained before commitment; where that is impossible, an **expedited clearance** (sanctions, ownership, and conflict screening only) must be obtained before commitment and completed to full standard within 72 hours.
6. **Retrospective records.** Authorization given verbally must be recorded within four hours by any tamper-evident artifact — signed PDF, call recording, or system entry — with the **artifact's creation time**, not its system-ingestion time, governing. Where a system outage is formally logged, contemporaneous external evidence (supplier acknowledgment, telephone record, access log) takes precedence over internal database timestamps in both directions. Late recording of a timely act is a logging defect; late performance of a control is a breach and is never cured by subsequent approval.
7. **Expiry is strict.** An OID lapses at its stated end time. It may be extended only in writing, before expiry, by the original signatories. No verbal, implied, or purpose-based extension has effect, and a continuing operational need is not an extension. Purchases committed after expiry are assessed under ordinary policy in full.
8. **Safeguards against emergency normalization.** (a) **Hard duration cap:** no OID may run longer than 96 hours; a third consecutive extension requires CRO and Audit Committee approval. (b) **Mandatory post-incident review** within ten business days of every OID, listing every purchase made under it, its value, its scope justification, and each control not performed — published to the executive committee. (c) **Usage ceiling with automatic review:** if OID-cited purchases exceed 10% of site purchase value in any rolling quarter, or if any single requester invokes an OID more than three times, procurement authority for that scope reverts to ordinary policy until the CRO reinstates it. (d) **No blanket sufficiency statements:** communications asserting that one approval is "sufficient for all approvals" have no normative effect unless entered in the controlled policy repository; uncontrolled emails may clarify who exercises a control within the sender's own authority, never which controls apply.
