

# 1. Fairness dimensions

To evaluate procedural fairness, we must look beyond simple outcome metrics. The following dimensions capture the quality and equity of the process itself:

- **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates differ (78% for Group A vs. 58% for Group D). While important, this metric alone cannot isolate process fairness from case complexity.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After controlling for risk, documentation, and channel, Group B still shows a -5.5 percentage point approval gap and longer cycle times. This suggests residual inequity in how decisions or processing are made.
- **Procedural burden:** Differences in the number of steps, handovers, and required actions. Group B and C require more support steps (Language Support, Accommodation Review), adding cognitive and operational load.
- **Timeliness:** Median and 90th percentile cycle times show substantial lags for Groups B, C, and D. Wait times at specific support steps (e.g., 3.8 days for Language Support) indicate resource bottlenecks affecting specific demographics disproportionately.
- **Consistency of routing:** Variants paths differ significantly by group. Group A flows largely through V1 (direct path), while B and C are funneled into longer paths (V4, V5). If routing is not strictly driven by verified need, it may introduce inconsistency.
- **Quality/accessibility of support:** Service-level targets do not include separate timeliness thresholds for support steps. This risks treating mandatory rights-preserving steps (Language, Accommodation) as secondary, potentially affecting the quality of interaction and dignity.
- **Appeal/rework burden:** Higher appeal and reopen/rework rates for Groups B, C, and D suggest either more contentious decisions or dissatisfaction with the initial process quality.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Group B cases are concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers, while Group A is spread across 52. Concentration on limited resources increases workload and delays for minority groups.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty, the following analysis strategy is proposed:

- **Matching or stratification variables:** Control for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. Create propensity scores to balance groups on observable complexity.
- **Path/variant comparison:** Calculate adjusted rates for variant selection (V1–V6). Determine if groups are routed to complex paths solely by risk, or if similar-risk cases in different groups take different paths.
- **Resource-assignment comparison:** Analyze `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` per group. Measure how often cases switch roles (e.g., general  bilingual  general) compared to the baseline.
- **Waiting-time decomposition:** Separate service time (processing) from wait time (queue). Insert service waiting time via `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to identify if delays occur in processing or queuing.
- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates:** Vary the `risk_score_band` variable to assess how much residual disparity remains if risk scores are adjusted or removed (given potential historical bias).
- **Subgroup checks by region and channel:** Split data by region and channel (online vs. in-person) to ensure aggregated results do not mask localized fairness issues (e.g., backlog in specific regions for Group B).

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Analysis of the variant and resource data reveals several key disparities:

- **Path selection:** Group A relies heavily on V1 (42%), the shortest path. Group B uses V4 (Language Support) at 24% and V2 (Request Docs) at 34%. Group C uses V5 (Accommodation) at 31%. While some divergence is expected due to rights (language/accommodation), the adjusted data shows Group B has +8.0% higher Supervisor Review probability even after controlling for mix. This suggests supervisory thresholds may differ by group.
- **Waiting times:** Significant disparities exist at support points. Group B waits 3.8 days between Language Support Contact and Eligibility Assessment. Group C waits 4.7 days between Accommodation Review and Eligibility. These exceed standard waiting times for other steps.
- **Resource allocation:** Group B is handled by a small set of bilingual caseworkers (7), whereas Group A has 52 caseworkers. This structural constraint directly correlates with the observed waiting time. Group C is funneled into a specialized queue with fewer specialists (5).
- **Justification vs. Concern:** Routing Group D to Fraud Review is justified by high risk_score_band. However, the adjusted waiting time difference for Group B (+2.6 days) cannot be fully explained by case complexity, given the resource constraints on bilingual staff.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1.  **Legitimate routing requirements:** Rights-preserving steps (Language Support, Accommodation) legally mandate review, necessitating path changes and additional processing time (V4, V5).
2.  **Resource capacity constraints:** Understaffing in specialized queues (7 bilingual caseworkers for 1250 cases in Group B) creates bottlenecks, causing delays even for cases that are not inherently more complex.
3.  **Documentation barriers affecting completeness:** Language barriers may lead to higher rates of incomplete documentation (46% for Group B), triggering "Request Missing Documents" (V2) even when applicants have valid eligibility, adding procedural steps.
4.  **Implicit bias or policy gaps:** The policy sets service-level targets for general processing but not for support steps. Caseworkers may subconsciously deprioritize language/accommodation queues or supervisors may apply stricter scrutiny to complex paths involving support steps.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available data has gaps that prevent definitive conclusions:

- **Measurement bias:** `documentation_completeness` may reflect language barriers rather than applicant diligence. If incomplete docs are a proxy for language need, controlling for them may over-control and hide unfairness.
- **Missing applicant needs:** The log captures `language_preference` and `disability_accommodation_requested`, but not the quality of interaction or whether accommodations were actually provided effectively.
- **Unobserved resource workload:** Workload is observed at daily granularity. We cannot see peak-hour delays or individual caseworker capacity limits that might drive waiting times.
- **Appeal quality:** `appeal_flag` is binary. We do not know if appeals are driven by procedural errors, lack of explanation, or substantive eligibility disputes.
- **Historical bias in risk scores:** `risk_score_band` may reflect historical enforcement disparities. If Group D is over-flagged as high risk due to past practices, routing them to Fraud Review perpetuates systemic inequality.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review:** Hire more bilingual caseworkers and specialists.
  - *Side effect:* Increased operational costs; risk of underutilization if demand fluctuates.
- **Service-level targets for support steps:** Define SLAs specifically for Language Support and Accommodation Review queues.
  - *Side effect:* May encourage rushing reviews if targets are too tight, reducing quality.
- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers:** Minimize handovers between general  bilingual  general caseworkers. Allow bilingual staff to own cases end-to-end where possible.
  - *Side effect:* Reduces cross-training opportunities; requires specialized skill matching.
- **Decision-explanation improvements:** Provide clearer reasons for decisions, especially for denials or appeals.
  - *Side effect:* Increased processing time per case initially; requires training.
- **Monitoring adjusted disparities:** Track adjusted approval and cycle time gaps quarterly to detect regressions.
  - *Side effect:* Requires continuous analytical effort and access to sensitive data.
- **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness:** Regularly review the risk scoring algorithm for bias and verify if documentation completeness correlates with language.
  - *Side effect:* May require changes to core fraud systems and risk models.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process appears procedurally indeterminate but carries significant fairness concerns. Adjusted analysis reveals persistent disparities in approval probability, cycle time, and supervisory review rates for minority and accommodation-needing groups, even after controlling for risk and documentation complexity. While some divergence in paths is justified by legitimate support requirements (language, accommodation), the resource allocation and waiting-time bottlenecks suggest these rights-preserving steps function as barriers rather than facilitators. We cannot definitively attribute this to discrimination due to potential measurement bias in risk scores and documentation completeness. However, the data does not support dismissing these disparities as solely legitimate complexity effects. Procedural fairness requires addressing resource capacity and SLAs for support steps, not just outcome parity.
