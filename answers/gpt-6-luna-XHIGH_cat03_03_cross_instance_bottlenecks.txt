## 1. Interaction-graph description

Represent the evidence as a typed, temporal hypergraph rather than a single job trace. Nodes include Job, Component, ComponentLot, Batch, Machine, Tool, and Shipment objects; event nodes carry activities and timestamps. A multi-object event is a hyperedge joining all participating objects. Keeping event nodes matters: the 12:05 `Batch Cure` joins three jobs and their components to one batch, oven, and rack; it is one cure event, not three independent cures.

The selected O2O edges (`requires`, `contains`, `batches`, `consolidates`) are direct relation observations. Event-to-object links stated in the timing evidence (for example, `Machine Process` to J4/C4/CNC-2/FIX-A, or `Quarantine Lot` to L7/C2/C5/J2/J5) are also direct as reported, subject to the stated inconsistent job linkage. Sharing of an object is direct evidence of a potential coupling; an edge such as “J4 waited for FIX-A” or “J1 was held for J4” is an inference, not a recorded relation. The acquire/release interval for FIX-A supports a possible occupancy interval, but there is no explicit wait event. Likewise, “ready” times and machine idle intervals alone do not prove a queue or its cause.

Compact edge list (event labels stand for event nodes, not extra object types):

```text
J1 -requires-> C1       J2 -requires-> C2       J3 -requires-> C3
J4 -requires-> C4       J5 -requires-> C5       J6 -requires-> C6
L7 -contains-> C2,C5    L8 -contains-> C1,C3
B1 -batches-> J1,J2,J3  B2 -batches-> J4,J5
S1 -consolidates-> J1,J4   S2 -> J2,J3   S3 -> J5,J6
BatchCure@12:05 -> {B1, OVEN-1, RACK-7, J1,J2,J3,C1,C2,C3}
Quarantine/ReleaseLot@10:20/13:50 -> {L7,C2,C5,J2,J5}
MachineProcess(J4) -> {J4,C4,CNC-2,FIX-A}
FinalTest(J5/J6) -> {J5/J6, TEST-1, CAL-3}
```

Three higher-order motifs make the coupling concrete. First, the B1 synchronization motif is Job–Component–ComponentLot–Batch–Machine–Tool: J1/J3 components C1/C3 are in L8, J2's C2 is in quarantined L7, and the jobs meet in B1's OVEN-1/RACK-7 cure. Second, a resource-transfer motif links J3–FIX-A–CNC-2–J4–C4–B2: J3 holds the fixture as J4 reaches CNC-2, and J4's later process feeds a batch and shipment lifecycle. Third, the J5/J6 tail motif links Job–ComponentLot–Batch–Shipment–Machine–Tool: J5's C5 is in L7 and J5 shares B2 and S3 with J4/J6, while J5 and J6 both require TEST-1/CAL-3 in sequence. These are graph paths through observed relations and events; they suggest mechanisms, not causation by themselves.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

All figures below are elapsed-time decompositions. They are conditional counterfactuals, not causal estimates; timestamps do not establish that a particular resource or policy was the only constraint.

- **B1 observed waits:** From readiness to the common 12:05 start, J1 waits 12:05−08:10 = **3 h 55 min** and J2 waits 12:05−08:25 = **3 h 40 min**. J3 waits 12:05−11:55 = **10 min**. Assumption: the recorded readiness times are accurate and these jobs could not start their batch cure before B1 started. The first two waits are then synchronization exposure, but may include other unrecorded constraints.
- **B1 avoidable synchronization component:** The latest job becomes ready at 11:55. If B1 could start exactly then, with the same cure duration and no other setup or release gate, J1 would wait 3 h 45 min less than it did and J2 3 h 30 min less; J3 would wait zero. That leaves **7 h 15 min of combined synchronization wait** for J1 and J2 relative to each starting at its own readiness. The actual batch also started 10 minutes after the latest readiness, adding 10 minutes for each of the three jobs (30 job-minutes); that part remains unexplained. This counterfactual assumes smaller/earlier batching is technically and economically feasible.
- **J4 and FIX-A:** J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and starts its process at 11:45: **1 h 55 min** elapsed. FIX-A is held by J3 until 11:40, overlapping J4's post-arrival interval for **1 h 50 min**. If J4 was otherwise ready, needed FIX-A continuously, and could start promptly when it was released, as much as 1 h 50 min is plausibly fixture-associated; the remaining five minutes could be setup, handoff, or another queue. CNC-2's idle period 10:05–10:47 is 42 minutes, showing why a machine gap alone cannot explain or measure the tool wait. Counterfactual: J4 has priority/eligibility and an available fixture at 09:50.
- **J1 and S1 consolidation:** J1 is packed 13:30; J4 is packed 19:10; S1 dispatches 19:25. J1's pack-to-dispatch interval is **5 h 55 min**, versus J4's **15 min**. J4's later packing is **5 h 40 min** after J1's, a plausible partner-wait component for J1. Counterfactual: S1 could dispatch 15 minutes after J1 was packed if J4 were not required as a shipment partner, using the observed post-last-pack interval as the dispatch allowance. Actual loading/dispatch readiness is not timestamped here, so this is a policy scenario rather than a proven dwell attribution.
- **J6 and CAL-3:** J6 is ready at 16:05 and starts at 17:25, so it waits **1 h 20 min**. J5's test occupies 16:10–16:35; CAL-3 is then in recalibration until 17:20, followed by five minutes until J6 starts. The recorded calibration interval is **45 min**, plausibly gating J6, with an additional 5-minute post-calibration interval. Counterfactual: J6 is otherwise eligible and could test as soon as CAL-3 becomes available; if a spare calibrated tool existed, the 45-minute recalibration interval could be removed from its queue. The preceding J5 service and any priority rule remain alternative contributors to the total 80 minutes.
- **B2 / RACK-7 cross-batch interval:** OVEN-1 is idle 12:55–14:50 (**1 h 55 min**), while RACK-7 remains in cleaning/quarantine until 14:35. The overlap from oven-idle onset to rack release is **1 h 40 min**, followed by 15 minutes to B2's start. If the rack is mandatory and B2 otherwise ready/eligible, the rack state plausibly explains much of that idle window. This assumes the oven's idle timestamp and rack status are complete and there was no independent batch-release constraint.
- **L7 quarantine duration:** 10:20–13:50 is **3 h 30 min**. J5 does not proceed until after release, consistent with a lot gate; J2's override before release means the same lot state may have been bypassed for one job. Counterfactual: a valid release event is required before either component may be consumed. The evidence does not show exactly when J5 would otherwise have been ready, so the full 3 h 30 min cannot be assigned as J5 delay.

## 4. What flattening misses

- **Job viewpoint:** It can show each job's activity path and its own elapsed transitions. It hides shared-object state unless those events are explicitly linked to the job. Here L7 quarantine/release is absent in the supplied extraction; including one shared event in several job cases can instead duplicate its frequency and elapsed time. A Job DFG can misleadingly imply `Inspect -> Pack` is the only large wait, while partner wait before dispatch and pre-process resource waits are outside that edge. Repeating the B1 cure in three cases can also make one batch look like three process events.
- **Machine viewpoint:** It exposes machine service sequence, utilization, and machine event gaps. It hides a waiting job that is blocked by a tool, lot status, batch rule, or shipment policy while the machine is free; it may also aggregate distinct jobs' events into a deceptively simple machine path. Concluding “no queue because utilization is modest” is misleading: J4 can wait for FIX-A while CNC-2 sits idle, and TEST-1 can be idle because CAL-3 is unavailable. Machine-event gap is not a measure of job waiting.
- **Batch viewpoint:** It makes batch membership, starts, and cure cycles visible. It can hide each member's readiness, component-lot release, and rack state unless those links and timestamps are retained. Counting one shared cure as one batch event is correct for capacity but does not describe the three different job waits; copying it per job overstates cure frequency. A normal batch activity trace can conceal a long synchronization barrier before the batch starts.
- **Tool viewpoint:** It can expose acquire/release, cleaning, quarantine, and calibration lifecycles. If waiting is not explicit, it still cannot establish which job was queued or its eligibility; inferred holds may overstate exclusive use. A tool-only sequence can make FIX-A or CAL-3 look reasonably occupied while omitting CNC/TEST idle time and blocked jobs. Conversely, machine-only utilization can imply spare capacity where the tool is the gating resource.
- **ComponentLot viewpoint:** It makes L7's C2/C5 sharing and the 10:20–13:50 quarantine visible. Inconsistent relations to J2/J5 can orphan lot events or make one shared event appear separately under each job. A Lot DFG can reveal a long hold yet cannot establish which job was ready, whether J2 consumed material, or whether the supervisor override authorized use. Treating missing job links as no lot effect is a misleading conclusion.
- **Shipment viewpoint:** It shows S1–S3 and their internal create/load/dispatch sequence. It hides how long each packed job waits before a shipment is loadable or dispatchable. Consolidation can copy one shipment event across member jobs, while a shipment case can omit job packing times. Thus the reported 0.8 h and 0.3 h medians say little about J1's 5 h 55 min packed-to-dispatch interval.

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because capacity is conjunctive: a job may need a machine, a fixture/calibration tool, released material, a batch cohort, and a shipment decision. A free machine does not make the job executable if any other required object is unavailable or policy says to wait. Utilization also measures busy time over a chosen denominator, not queue length or blocked-job time.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

1. **B1 and L7/L8.** Temporal precedence: J1/J2 are ready at 08:10/08:25; the L7 quarantine begins at 10:20; J3 becomes ready at 11:55; B1 cures at 12:05. Resource feasibility: one OVEN-1/RACK-7 event serves all three jobs, while L7 links J2's C2 with J5's C5 and L8 links C1/C3. Policy: B1's common start is consistent with a batch barrier; lot release would normally govern material use, but J2 has an override. Causal speculation: batch synchronization plausibly accounts for the early-ready jobs' wait, and L7 may have complicated J2 eligibility. Evidence does not show that lot quarantine delayed the batch start, since J2 entered B1 before release and readiness alone does not prove batch eligibility.
2. **FIX-A from J3 to J4.** Temporal precedence: J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00; J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50; J3 releases the fixture at 11:40; J4's process starts 11:45. Resource feasibility: the machine is idle for 42 minutes within this period and the J4 process event names FIX-A, making simultaneous fixture contention plausible. Policy: tool reservation priority, setup, or a requirement to keep the fixture with J3 may govern transfer; none is stated. Causal speculation: FIX-A likely constrained J4, but the missing `Wait for Tool` event and unknown J4-ready time prevent assigning all elapsed time to it. J4's later B2 and S1 relationships show how a local hold could propagate downstream, though propagation is not established by these timestamps.
3. **J5/J6 through test and S3.** Temporal precedence: both are test-ready around 16:00; J5 tests 16:10–16:35, CAL-3 recalibrates until 17:20, and J6 tests 17:25–17:50. Resource feasibility: TEST-1 is idle during the recalibration interval, but both jobs require CAL-3. Policy: the calibration interval may be mandatory; S3 later consolidates J5 with J6. Causal speculation: CAL-3 plausibly gates J6 despite idle TEST-1. S3 might then hold J5 after packing until J6 is packed, but no J5 packing time is supplied, so that downstream delay cannot be quantified.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# `ocel` is an already loaded PM4Py OCEL. Discovery outputs are retained
# for inspection because their detailed result formats can vary by release.
interaction_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
objects_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
features = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel)

print("Object-type interaction summary:", interaction_summary)
print("Object graph / OTG / ETOT / OCDFG:",
      type(objects_graph), type(otg), type(etot), type(ocdfg))
print("Extracted feature shape:", getattr(features, "shape", None))

# Compare two event-derived object-centric frequency metrics using the
# event-object relation table: event co-participations vs distinct object
# pairs ever co-participating. A multi-object event creates many pairs;
# neither count establishes a causal dependency.
rel = ocel.relations[["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid", "ocel:type"]].drop_duplicates()
pairs = rel.merge(rel, on="ocel:eid", suffixes=("_a", "_b"))
pairs = pairs[pairs["ocel:oid_a"] < pairs["ocel:oid_b"]].copy()
pairs["type_pair"] = pairs.apply(
    lambda r: tuple(sorted((r["ocel:type_a"], r["ocel:type_b"]))), axis=1
)
event_coparticipations = pairs.groupby("type_pair").size().sort_values(ascending=False)
distinct_object_pairs = (
    pairs.drop_duplicates(["type_pair", "ocel:oid_a", "ocel:oid_b"])
         .groupby("type_pair").size().sort_values(ascending=False)
)
print("Event co-participation counts:\n", event_coparticipations)
print("Distinct object-pair counts:\n", distinct_object_pairs)

# Job flattening is useful for its own DFG but can omit shared lot events
# unless relations were integrated. It is not a waiting-time ground truth.
job_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, object_type="Job")
job_performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(job_log)
print("Job performance DFG:", job_performance_dfg)

# Optional if NetworkX conversion is available in this PM4Py version.
if hasattr(pm4py, "convert_ocel_to_networkx"):
    nx_graph = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(ocel)
    print("NetworkX graph:", type(nx_graph))
```

I would compare the event co-participation metric with distinct object-pair counts and the interaction summary, then inspect OTG/OCDFG frequencies by object type and activity. High frequency identifies repeated coupling, not severity: rare B1/B2 interactions can still produce large delays. Performance DFGs from flattened logs describe recorded activity-to-activity durations. Waiting should instead be measured from explicit ready/request/block timestamps to grant/start/release events and joined to the relevant object state; machine-event gaps alone do not assign waits to jobs.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the leading batch/rack/tool explanation include:

1. Audited readiness records show J1/J2 were not eligible for B1 until close to 12:05, or the readiness timestamps were entered late.
2. Process specifications require B1 to wait for a mandatory 12:05 quality, material, or operator release independent of member readiness.
3. RACK-7 was not required for B2, or an available compatible rack could not be used for a documented technical reason unrelated to cleaning/quarantine.
4. FIX-A was not required by J4 before 11:40, J4 was not ready to process at 09:50, or its CNC start was deliberately scheduled for 11:45 despite a free tool.
5. CAL-3 was not exclusive or mandatory during 16:35–17:20, or J6 was held by a priority/authorization rule that would persist with another calibrated tool.
6. Repeated complete histories show little wait when batches, racks, fixtures, or calibration tools are shared, while delays track another state such as operator shifts or engineering holds.
7. S1's dispatch was constrained by a carrier appointment or paperwork completed at 19:25 regardless of J4, and comparable early-packed jobs do not wait for shipment partners.
8. L7's override was a valid, fully documented disposition and C2 was never consumed before release; in that case the apparent lot gate is not evidence of unsafe or delaying coupling.

Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among mechanisms:

1. Per-job `ready`, `eligible`, `request`, `blocked`, and `unblocked` timestamps, with reason codes and precedence status.
2. Tool request/queue, reservation, acquire, transfer, release, cleaning, quarantine, calibration-start, and calibration-complete events, including tool compatibility and owner.
3. Machine planned calendar, setup start/end, queue, blocked/starved state, downtime reason, operator assignment, and job-specific start eligibility.
4. Batch membership decision time, minimum/maximum batch rules, member-ready snapshots, batch release/approval, cure recipe, and rack assignment/availability.
5. Component-lot status history, affected component quantities, sampling/inspection result, disposition/override reason and approver, and actual component consumption by job.
6. Shipment membership changes, each job's pack-complete and load-ready times, load start/end, carrier appointment, paperwork/customs holds, dispatch authorization, and split-shipment decisions.
7. Stable source event IDs, object IDs, relation provenance, source-system name, event creation versus occurrence time, clock offset, and correction/version history to resolve inconsistent integration.
8. Outcome fields such as defect/rework, due date, service level, shipment cost, energy, and safety/quality incidents to evaluate spillovers rather than optimizing wait in isolation.
