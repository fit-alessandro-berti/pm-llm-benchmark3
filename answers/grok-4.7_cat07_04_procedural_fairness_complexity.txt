# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness here is about whether similarly situated applicants move through required steps with comparable burden, delay, access to support, and opportunity to contest a decision. Approval rates are one input to that judgment, not the judgment itself.

**Outcome parity.** Raw approval is 78% for Group A, 63% for Group B, 69% for Group C, and 58% for Group D. Those gaps are large, and they line up with very different case mix: Group D is 51% high risk and 49% high prior-case count; Group B is 46% incomplete on documentation and 57% in need of language support; Group C requests accommodation in 62% of cases. Equal approval would not be the fair benchmark when eligibility inputs differ this much. Unequal approval is also not evidence that the process treated people equivalently.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** A preliminary model that controls for risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior-case count, region, and month leaves smaller approval gaps: about −5.5 percentage points for Group B, −2.2 for Group C, and −3.1 for Group D, relative to Group A. Most of the raw approval gap is associated with measured case mix. A residual gap remains in every group. The adjustment set omits language preference and accommodation request, and it conditions on covariates that may themselves carry bias or sit downstream of access barriers. The residual is a reason to keep investigating, not a completed estimate of unjustified denial.

**Procedural burden.** Group A reaches the short path V1 in 42% of cases (median 5.8 days). Groups B, C, and D reach it in 18–22%. Group B is concentrated on document-request and language-support paths (V2 34%, V4 24%). Group C is concentrated on accommodation review (V5 31%, median 21.6 days). Group D is concentrated on fraud and supervisor review (V3 28%). Some of that routing matches written policy. The same paths also add activities, handovers, and rework (22% for B, 24% for C, 20% for D, versus 11% for A). After controls, Request Missing Documents remains higher by 9.5 points for B and 4.2 for C, and Supervisor Review remains higher by 8.0 points for B and 5.5 for C. Those are extra procedural steps, not just different outcomes.

**Timeliness.** Median cycle time is 8.1 days for A, 13.7 for B, 15.9 for C, and 12.1 for D. The 90th percentiles spread further: 21.4, 34.2, 38.8, and 29.5 days. Adjusted cycle time remains longer by 2.6 days for B, 4.1 for C, and 0.9 for D. The longest single waits sit on rights-preserving edges: Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment is 3.8 days for Group B, and Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment is 4.7 days for Group C. Supervisor Review to Decision Draft is 1.9 days for B and 1.6 for C, against 1.0 for A. Decision Draft to Notification differs only modestly (0.5–0.8 days). Delay is concentrated before the decision is issued, in support queues and in a few regional backlogs, rather than in the final notice.

**Consistency of routing.** Policy requires Fraud Review for higher risk, Accommodation Review when requested, and Language Support Contact for minority-language cases. Group D’s heavier V3 share is directionally consistent with a 51% high-risk share. Group C’s V5 share and Group B’s V4 share track the corresponding needs. Consistency fails in other places. Group B’s Supervisor Review rate stays 8 points higher after risk is controlled, and V4 itself does not include supervisor review, so that increment is not the language step. Group B’s document-request rate stays 9.5 points higher after completeness is controlled. In two regions, Group B cases bounce general caseworker to bilingual caseworker and back, and Group C cases bounce general caseworker to accommodation specialist to supervisor. Applicants with the same recorded profile are not on the same route.

**Quality and accessibility of support.** Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving steps, and they are present in the log, so the agency is not simply skipping them. Accessibility is weak in practice. Seven bilingual caseworkers handle a group in which 57% of cases need language support. Five accommodation specialists handle a group in which 62% request accommodation, and two of those specialists also perform supervisor review. Service targets do not set a separate clock for either step. The log does not record interpretation quality, whether the accommodation granted matched the request, or whether notices were usable in the applicant’s language. A step that exists and a step that is timely and adequate are different facts. Only the first is observed.

**Appeal and rework burden.** Appeal rates are 6% (A), 13% (B), 10% (C), and 16% (D). Adjusted appeal probability remains higher by 4.4 points for B, 1.1 for C, and 5.7 for D. Variant V6, which runs through appeal intake and appeal review, has median duration 30.7 days and accounts for 13% of D and 8% of B versus 4% of A. Rework is roughly doubled for B, C, and D relative to A. Appeals can be a healthy check. They are also extra time, extra hearings, and extra burden on people who already waited longer. The log does not show whether appeals corrected errors, which would make a higher appeal rate partly protective, or whether weak explanations and inaccessible notices pushed people into a slow remedy.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A work is spread across 52 caseworkers. Group B work is concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. Group C work is concentrated on 5 specialists. Group D work is routed into the fraud queue in line with measured risk. Equal headcount would be the wrong test, because specialized rights require specialized staff. The observed pattern is a narrow specialist queue placed in front of a right, plus extra handovers, plus shared duty between accommodation review and supervision. That allocation makes the cost of using a right fall on the applicant as waiting time.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The aim is to separate four claims that the raw tables mix together: disparity (rates differ), legitimate routing (policy requires a different path because of a relevant fact), unjustified differential treatment (the path or the wait differs among cases that policy treats as alike), and uncertainty (the covariate is a bad control, a mediator, or a poor measure). No single regression does that.

**Matching or stratification variables.** Build strata, or matched sets, that policy actually uses. Primary strata should be risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, because those are the covariates in the preliminary model and the factors the agency can point to as complexity. Report approval, cycle time, appeal, rework, Supervisor Review, and Request Missing Documents inside each stratum, with cell counts. Then add the two variables the preliminary model left out: language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested. Compare Group B with Group A among minority-language cases and, separately, among majority-language cases; compare Group C with others among accommodation requests and among cases with no request. A gap that appears only where the right is triggered is a capacity and design problem. A gap that remains where the right is not triggered is harder to attribute to the support step. Prefer stratification or matching over one saturated regression when cells allow, and pool only sparse cells with a pre-specified rule. Publish confidence intervals and the number of cases per cell. Groups C and the cross-cells will be small.

**Path and variant comparison.** Hold the variant fixed and then compare groups. V1 against V1 compares the short path. V2 against V2 compares document-request cases. V3 against V3 compares fraud-review cases at similar risk. V4 and V5 have almost no Group A mass, so they should be described as the support pathways and compared with the closest policy-compliant alternative, not forced into a within-variant parity test they cannot support. Also test activities that policy does not tie to the group: extra Supervisor Review on V4, a second Request Missing Documents after a file is already marked complete, and appeal after a fully documented low-risk approval or denial. Variant share differences and within-variant duration differences answer different questions and should be reported separately.

**Resource-assignment comparison.** Within region, channel, and risk band, compare who receives the case: share of cases held by the 7 bilingual caseworkers, the 5 accommodation specialists, and the fraud queue; handovers per case; whether the specialist also acts as supervisor on the same case; and daily workload of the assigned resource. The fairness contrast is workload and handover count among cases that require the same specialist step, plus whether non-specialist steps are slower once a case has been tagged for language or accommodation. A handover-of-work network and a working-together network, split by group and region, make the general-to-specialist-to-general bounce visible.

**Waiting-time decomposition.** Split every case duration into activity service time and waiting time between activities, then sum waits by edge. Report the contribution of Submit to Identity Verification, Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents, Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment, Risk Screening to Fraud Review, Supervisor Review to Decision Draft, and Decision Draft to Applicant Notification. Do this within channel and within variant so in-person intake is not credited to language support, and fraud-queue time is not credited to accommodation. Where the log allows, separate applicant response time after a document request from agency queue time before the request is even issued. Medians in the current table cannot be added across edges and treated as an average effect; a formal decomposition should use means or a share-of-total-time breakdown with medians shown alongside.

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.** Estimate the adjusted contrasts three ways: with the full control set; without risk_score_band; and without both risk_score_band and documentation_completeness. If the approval or routing gap grows when those covariates are removed, part of the “explained” disparity was sitting inside a measure that may encode language barriers or historical enforcement. If the gap is stable, the result is less dependent on those two fields. Treat both variables as potential mediators of access problems as well as potential confounders of eligibility. Show the range across specifications rather than a single adjusted number. A further check is to re-estimate within the most favorable measured cell only: low risk, complete documentation, online channel, low prior-case count. Residual gaps in that cell are the ones case mix is least able to absorb.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.** Repeat the stratified contrasts for each region and for online versus in-person. The supervisor backlog is already described as concentrated in two regions, and Group B’s extra handovers are described in those same regions. A statewide average can hide a local routing practice. Channel cuts matter because in-person cases wait longer at identity verification (Group B’s Submit to Verification median is 1.4 days versus 0.8 for A) and Group B is 38% in-person. The preliminary model already includes channel and region, so the residual cycle-time gaps are not only composition across those margins. The subgroup view shows where an operational fix would have to land. Intersections — minority language and accommodation, high risk and minority language — should be reported even if they are only descriptive, because the four group labels are coarse and may conceal compounded routes.

What this strategy can support is a statement about remaining differences among cases that look alike on recorded policy inputs, and a statement about how much time is spent inside mandatory support queues. What it cannot support is a finding about motive, a legal conclusion of discrimination, or a claim that the adjusted percentage points are free of confounding.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The largest procedural disparities are the support-path delays for Groups B and C, the residual document-request and supervisor-review increments for Group B, and the appeal path for Group D. They do not all have the same standing.

**Support routes that match policy and still impose a heavy clock.** V4 is 24% of Group B and 1% of Group A, with median duration 19.2 days against 5.8 days on V1. V5 is 31% of Group C and 2% of Group A, with median duration 21.6 days. Those shares track need: 57% of Group B needs language support and 62% of Group C requests accommodation. The waits on the specialist edges are 3.8 days and 4.7 days, in queues staffed by 7 and 5 people. A rough share-weighted illustration, not a formal mediation result, shows why these edges matter: if 57% of Group B cases absorb a 3.8-day language-edge wait, the average contribution is on the order of two days, close to the +2.6-day adjusted cycle-time gap; if 62% of Group C cases absorb a 4.7-day accommodation-edge wait, the average contribution is on the order of three days, a large part of the +4.1-day adjusted gap. Other edges also differ, medians do not add, and not every supported case is an extra case relative to Group A, so this is a magnitude check only. It is enough to say that specialist queue time is a plausible main component of the residual delay. The routing into the step is justified by the stated right. The length of the queue, the absence of a service target, and the handovers around the step are not justified by the existence of the right.

**Document requests beyond recorded incompleteness.** V2 is common in every group and slower than V1 (14.9 versus 5.8 days). Group B’s higher V2 share fits a 46% incomplete-documentation rate, and Group C’s 36% incomplete rate fits a higher V2 share as well. The residual after controlling for documentation_completeness does not fit as neatly: +9.5 points for B and +4.2 for C. The wait from Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents is 1.6 days for B versus 0.4 for A, and the note on that edge is that language-support cases wait for bilingual staff. V4 also embeds a document request after language contact. Two different facts are tangled: more files start incomplete, which can be a legitimate reason to request documents, and files with the same completeness flag are still more often sent into that request and wait longer for it. The second fact is a procedural disparity. It is consistent with a language barrier that the completeness flag measures badly, with a stricter practice toward Group B files, or with both.

**Supervisor review.** V3 and V5 include supervisor review by construction. Group D’s V3 share of 28% against Group A’s 9% lines up with high-risk policy and with only a +2.0-point residual in supervisor review after controls, so most of D’s extra supervision is the complexity the model measured. Group C’s +5.5-point residual is ambiguous because accommodation was not a control and V5 routes accommodated cases through supervisor review. If policy requires that sign-off, the extra review is designed into the right and still lengthens the case (Supervisor Review to Decision Draft is 1.6 days for C). If policy does not require it, the department has attached an extra approval to accommodation. Group B’s +8.0-point residual is the more concerning supervisor finding: language cases are not described as requiring supervisor review, V4’s path summary does not include it, and risk band is already in the model. Combined with a 1.9-day median wait into Decision Draft and a backlog located in two regions, Group B is both more often in that queue and slower once there.

**Fraud routing for Group D.** V3’s 18.4-day median and Group D’s 28% share are the pattern one would expect when half the group is high risk and high risk requires fraud review. Risk Screening to Fraud Review waits are similar across groups (2.0–2.8 days), with Group D at the top of that range. Adjusted cycle time for D is only +0.9 days. The raw approval gap (58% versus 78%) shrinks to about 3 points after controls. This is the disparity most readily accounted for by recorded complexity. It stays concerning for two other reasons: appeal probability remains 5.7 points higher, and the risk band used to justify the route is not yet shown to be neutral.

**Identity verification and final notice.** Submit to Identity Verification is slower for B (1.4 days) and C (1.2) than for A (0.8), in a pattern the log attributes partly to in-person filing. That is a real access difference and it is smaller than the specialist-queue waits. Decision Draft to Applicant Notification differs by at most a few tenths of a day. The process is relatively even at the last step and uneven at intake and in the middle.

**Resources and handovers.** Concentration, not just path shape, drives the waits. Group A’s 52-caseworker spread is compatible with an 8.1-day median. Group B’s dependence on 7 bilingual workers and 3 regional officers, and Group C’s dependence on 5 specialists of whom two also supervise, produce single points of delay and extra handovers. Each handover is another queue. Rework rates of 22% and 24% fit a process that changes hands more often and waits longer for a scarce skill. Group D’s fraud-queue assignment is consistent with risk-based routing; fairness there depends on whether the risk label is earned by the case in front of the worker.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Mandatory specialist routing with too little specialist capacity.** Language support and accommodation review are supposed to happen. Demand for them is structurally higher in Groups B and C. Supply is 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists, with no separate timeliness standard. Cases that use the right wait behind a short queue, accumulate handovers, and exit with longer cycle times and more rework. This mechanism treats the path difference as legitimate and the delay as an organizational failure. It fits V4 and V5, the 3.8- and 4.7-day edges, and the residual cycle-time gaps.

**Complexity-appropriate controls.** Higher risk triggers fraud review and, on V3, supervisor review. Incomplete files trigger document requests. In-person filing slows identity verification. High prior-case volume marks Group D. This mechanism accounts for a large share of raw approval gaps, for Group D’s V3 share, for the common V2 path, and for why adjusted approval differences are much smaller than raw ones. It does not account for residual document requests at the same completeness flag, residual supervisor review for Group B, or multi-day waits after the required support step has already been reached.

**Communication barriers that the completeness flag only partly records.** Applicants who need language support may be asked for documents more often because letters are unclear, portals are monolingual, or completeness is judged by staff who do not share the applicant’s language. They may also take longer to respond once a request goes out. The agency-side version of this mechanism is a fairness problem in the design of notices and forms. The applicant-response version is still fairness-relevant when the request itself is hard to understand or hard to satisfy in person. The 1.6-day wait before the request is issued, and the +9.5-point residual request rate, point to agency processing, not only to slow replies. Both can operate together. The log as summarized cannot split them.

**Uneven scrutiny and regional backlog.** Supervisor review may be used as informal risk management for unfamiliar applicants, minority-language files, or accommodated cases, beyond the written fraud rule. In two regions the supervisor queue is backlogged, and those regions also show more handovers for B and C. A case can be both properly complex and more heavily reviewed because of who the applicant is or which office received the file. The +8-point supervisor residual for Group B, the 1.9-day supervisor wait, and the regional note fit this mechanism. It is not established by the tables alone, because local case mix inside those regions could still differ.

**Measurement feedback in the risk score, and appeal as a remedy for opaque decisions.** If risk_score_band was trained or tuned on past cases that were themselves routed more often to fraud review, Group D’s “higher complexity” is partly a history of the same process. Fraud review then looks justified by a label the process helped create. Separately, thin explanations, especially across language, raise appeals without raising the quality of the original decision. Group D’s +5.7-point adjusted appeal gap and Group B’s +4.4-point gap, together with V6’s 30.7-day median, fit a remedy path that absorbs disagreement the front-end process did not resolve. Appeal volume can also mean applicants in these groups are more willing or more organized to contest a denial. The tables do not separate meritorious appeals from friction.

These mechanisms can run at the same time. A Group B case can be legitimately sent to language support, wait because only seven people can do that work, receive an extra document request because the notice was unclear, and pick up a supervisor review in a backlogged region. Attributing the whole gap to any one of them will misstate the evidence.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The attributes support a careful disparity analysis. They do not identify unjustified denials case by case, and they do not clear the process of unfairness just because several covariates were included.

**Measurement bias.** documentation_completeness is a staff judgment made inside a process that already struggles with language. A file can be coded incomplete because the applicant did not understand what to send, or complete because a bilingual worker repaired it before the flag was set. Conditioning on that flag then removes part of the barrier from the residual and, at the same time, fails to capture remaining communication trouble. Channel and language preference are correlated in Group B, so each one partly stands in for the other. household_size_band and prior_case_count are coarse bands. Applicant group itself bundles traits: Group B mixes minority language with channel mix, Group D mixes risk with repeat cases, and Group C is defined largely by an accommodation request. Contrasts between group labels are not pure contrasts of one protected circumstance.

**Missing applicant needs.** Nothing in the log records literacy, digital access, housing instability, representative or advocate involvement, the specific accommodation requested, whether it was granted, interpreter presence, or quality of the notice. Dignity of treatment and quality of explanation are explicitly outside case_outcome. Two cases with the same cycle time can differ sharply in whether the applicant could participate. Conversely, some long cycle times may be requested pauses or time the applicant used to gather evidence. Without a reason code on waits, agency delay and applicant time are only partly separable.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed at daily granularity. That is too coarse to see within-day priority, batching, or whether a bilingual caseworker’s queue was long because of this case type or because the same seven people cover every language. It also hides idle time, absence, and the double role of the two accommodation specialists who supervise. Handover counts are described for two regions, not as a full distribution. Resource fairness can be shown in concentration and in edge waits; it cannot be shown as a precise excess delay per additional open case.

**Appeal quality.** appeal_flag and the V6 path show that an appeal happened and that it was slow. They do not show the issue appealed, the reversal rate, whether the applicant had language support or an accommodation during the appeal, or whether the original decision letter stated reasons in the applicant’s language. A higher appeal rate after controls is compatible with more errors, with harder cases, and with worse explanations. Rework rate has the same ambiguity: rework can be correction of agency error or a repeat of an applicant-driven document cycle.

**Historical bias in risk scores.** risk_score_band is an allowed routing key in current policy and a possible carrier of past disparity. If past fraud referrals, past denials, or past incomplete files differ by group for reasons of access rather than eligibility, today’s high-risk label reproduces that pattern and makes V3 look policy-compliant. The preliminary model’s decision to control for the band answers the question “given this label, what remains?” It does not answer “is this label a fair summary of eligibility risk?” Group D’s residual approval gap of about 3 points and residual appeal gap of about 6 points should be read with that circularity in view. Small-sample and specification uncertainty also remain: the adjusted table has no intervals, no interactions, and no statement of how completeness and risk were coded inside the model.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.** Staff the bilingual queue and the accommodation queue so that a rights-preserving step does not cost multiple extra days, and separate supervision from accommodation review where the same two people hold both roles. The plausible effect is shorter Language Support to Eligibility and Accommodation Review to Eligibility waits, fewer bounce-back handovers, and a narrower residual cycle-time gap for B and C. Trade-offs are real. Hiring and training take time, and a thin labor market for bilingual benefits work can raise cost without immediately raising quality. Pulling experienced generalists into specialist roles shrinks the 52-person pool that currently keeps Group A fast unless those seats are backfilled. More specialists who are under-trained will shorten the queue and weaken the quality of interpretation or accommodation. Capacity should be added to perform the step well, with quality review of a sample of supported cases, not only to clear it faster.

**Set service-level targets for the support steps.** Give Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and the wait into the following eligibility step their own timeliness thresholds, reported by region and by group, beside the overall cycle-time target. This makes the current policy gap explicit: the steps are mandatory rights, and the clock ignores them. Trade-offs: a speed target can be met by closing the support activity before the applicant has actually understood the notice or received the accommodation, by steering borderline cases away from requesting support, or by prioritizing easy support cases so the median looks better while the 90th percentile stays long. Targets should include tail time, completion of the support need, and a prohibition on treating a withdrawn support request as a success. They should not be hit by skipping the step.

**Redesign routing to reduce handovers.** Assign a bilingual caseworker or an accommodation specialist as the continuing owner through eligibility and decision draft, instead of general caseworker to specialist and back to general caseworker, and then to a supervisor who may be the same specialist. Keep fraud review on the high-risk rule. The expected gain is fewer queues and less rework for B and C. Trade-offs: end-to-end ownership reduces the second pair of eyes that sometimes catches errors, and it can overload specialists with ordinary eligibility work they do more slowly. A clean design keeps one substantive specialist review, keeps supervisor review for the cases policy names, and stops reflexive returns to a generalist queue. Cutting the support step itself would improve the median and violate the right; that is not an available remedy.

**Improve decision explanations.** Issue reasons in the applicant’s language, state what document or eligibility factor drove the result, and record that language support or an accommodation was offered before a denial for incompleteness. This is aimed at Group B’s and Group D’s residual appeal gaps and at V6’s month-long path. Better reasons can reduce unnecessary appeals and make meritorious appeals easier to file. Trade-offs: good explanations add time at Decision Draft, translation capacity is the same scarce bilingual capacity already in the queue, and boilerplate text in the preferred language will not reduce appeals. If explanations reveal errors, appeal rates can rise in the short run. That rise is a correction, not a failure of the control, and it should not be punished in staff metrics.

**Monitor adjusted disparities on a fixed schedule.** Publish, by group and region, raw rates and stratified or matched rates for approval, cycle time and its 90th percentile, support-step wait, document request, supervisor review, appeal, and rework, using the sensitivity set in section 2 (with and without risk band and completeness). Review residual gaps that persist inside low-risk, complete, online cells. Trade-offs: a dashboard that conditions on a biased risk score will report a comforting residual and miss the route by which the score moves people. A dashboard that ignores case mix will treat legitimate fraud review as a fairness failure and create pressure to equalize approvals by approving unlike cases. Metric fixation is a further risk: if offices are scored only on approval parity or only on median days, they can avoid hard cases, discourage accommodation requests, or rush specialist steps. Monitoring should keep raw, stratified, and sensitivity views side by side and should include the rate at which eligible people receive language support and accommodation, not only how fast files close.

**Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.** Test whether the risk band is calibrated by group against subsequent confirmed fraud or eligibility findings, whether it uses inputs that proxy language, disability, or region, and whether a file’s completeness flag depends on who coded it and in what language. Rewrite document requests and checklists that systematically flag minority-language files. Trade-offs: weakening a score that truly tracks fraud will let some improper payments through; tightening documentation to “fix” Group B’s incomplete share will raise burden on people who already struggle to produce papers. An audit that freezes routing while every score is rebuilt delays cases in the fraud queue. The practical path is to keep fraud review for bands that still predict confirmed problems after the proxy inputs are removed, to stop using the band as if it were a neutral fact in fairness reporting until that test is done, and to measure completeness from a checklist the applicant can see in their language.

Across these options, the constraint that matters most is that language support and accommodation review stay in the process. Fairness work here is capacity, ownership, clocks, explanations, and cleaner inputs. Faster medians achieved by dropping those steps would reproduce the Group A path by withdrawing a right.

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The snippets below assume a pandas dataframe `df` with the columns in the event log, including `case:concept:name`, `concept:name`, `time:timestamp`, and `org:resource`. They are an analysis plan, not a claim that these queries have already been run.

```python

import pm4py

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)
# keep org:resource and the case attributes on the formatted frame
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

GROUPS = ["Group A", "Group B", "Group C", "Group D"]

    "applicant_group", "channel", "language_preference", "risk_score_band",
    "documentation_completeness", "household_size_band",
    "disability_accommodation_requested", "prior_case_count", "region",
    "case_outcome", "appeal_flag",
]

def cases_of(log):
    return {trace.attributes["concept:name"] for trace in log}

# --- 1. Variant shares and durations by group ---
rows = []
for g in GROUPS:
    lg = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, "applicant_group", [g], level="case", retain=True
    )
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(lg)  # variant -> list of traces
    durations = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(lg, parameters={
        "timestamp_key": "time:timestamp", "case_id_key": "concept:name"
    })
    # map case id -> duration using the same unit for every group
    for variant, traces in variants.items():
        rows.append({
            "group": g,
            "variant": " → ".join(variant),
            "n": len(traces),
            "share": len(traces) / max(len(lg), 1),
        })
variant_table = pd.DataFrame(rows)

# Compare a named path across groups by filtering to that variant
v1 = [("Submit Application", "Identity Verification", "Completeness Check",
       "Eligibility Assessment", "Decision Draft", "Applicant Notification",
       "Benefit Activation")]
# pm4py.filter_variants expects the variant keys produced by get_variants;
# build those keys from the full log, then filter.
all_variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
v1_keys = [k for k in all_variants if list(k) == v1 or tuple(k) == tuple(v1)]
log_v1 = pm4py.filter_variants(log, v1_keys, retain=True)

# --- 2. Within-stratum logs for the adjustment strategy ---
def filter_case_attribute(log, attr, values):
    return pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, attr, values, level="case", retain=True
    )

strata = []
for risk in ["low", "high"]:          # use the log's actual band labels
    for docs in ["complete", "incomplete"]:
        for channel in ["online", "in_person"]:
            sl = filter_case_attribute(log, "risk_score_band", [risk])
            sl = filter_case_attribute(sl, "documentation_completeness", [docs])
            sl = filter_case_attribute(sl, "channel", [channel])
            strata.append(((risk, docs, channel), sl))
# Sensitivity slices: drop risk, drop risk and completeness, and the
# favorable cell only. Repeat after also splitting language_preference
# and disability_accommodation_requested.

# --- 3. Performance DFG and slow-path filter, per group ---
for g in GROUPS:
    lg = filter_case_attribute(log, "applicant_group", [g])
    dfg, start_act, end_act = pm4py.discover_dfg(lg)
    perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(lg)
    # perf_dfg[(a, b)] is a performance aggregate on that directly-follows edge
    # Focus edges:
    #   ("Language Support Contact", "Eligibility Assessment")
    #   ("Accommodation Review", "Eligibility Assessment")
    #   ("Completeness Check", "Request Missing Documents")
    #   ("Supervisor Review", "Decision Draft")
    #   ("Risk Screening", "Fraud Review")

# Cases that linger on a support edge
```

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process appears procedurally unfair in timeliness, handover burden, and resource allocation for Groups B and C. Rights the agency itself requires — language support and accommodation review — are delivered through queues of 7 and 5 people, with median waits of 3.8 and 4.7 days on the edges into eligibility, extra handovers, no service target of their own, and residual cycle times about 2.6 and 4.1 days longer than Group A after the measured complexity controls. Group B also faces more document requests and more supervisor review than those controls account for.

The process does not, on this evidence, support an equation of the raw approval gaps with discrimination. Group D’s lower approval rate and heavier fraud path largely track a much higher risk band and the written fraud rule; after controls, D’s cycle-time gap is under a day, while D’s appeal gap remains material. Group B’s and Group C’s approval residuals are smaller than their raw gaps and are still not zero. Those residuals are unresolved: they fit remaining unfairness, bias or over-control in risk and completeness, and ordinary confounding.

A calibrated conclusion is that procedural burden and delay are affirmatively uneven, concentrated on the support steps that should protect Groups B and C, and that outcome fairness is not established in either direction. The evidence is strong enough to justify capacity, routing, explanation, and audit work now. It is not strong enough to declare that adjusted approval differences are proved unjustified denials, or that Group D’s fraud routing is proved illegitimate. Until risk scores and completeness flags are audited, and until waits are decomposed inside matched cells by region and channel, the size of unjustified disparity should be reported as a range, not as a single percentage-point fact.
