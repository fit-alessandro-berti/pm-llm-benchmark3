# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let $c$ index cases ($1 \dots 12$) and $i \in \{A, B, C\}$ index branches. Timestamps are treated as absolute time in minutes.

**Core formulas:**
- Upstream elapsed time: $T_{up}^{(c)} = \text{fork\_release}^{(c)} - \text{arrival}^{(c)}$
- Branch queue waiting: $Q_i^{(c)} = \text{start}_i^{(c)} - \text{fork\_release}^{(c)}$
- Branch active service: $S_i^{(c)} = \text{end}_i^{(c)} - \text{start}_i^{(c)}$
- Fork-join stage elapsed time: $T_{join}^{(c)} = \max(\text{end}_A^{(c)}, \text{end}_B^{(c)}, \text{end}_C^{(c)}) - \text{fork\_release}^{(c)}$
- Branch synchronization waiting: $W_i^{(c)} = \max(\text{end}_A^{(c)}, \text{end}_B^{(c)}, \text{end}_C^{(c)}) - \text{end}_i^{(c)}$ (clamped to $\ge 0$)
- Downstream queueing: $T_{down}^{(c)} = \text{final\_start}^{(c)} - \max(\text{end}_A^{(c)}, \text{end}_B^{(c)}, \text{end}_C^{(c)})$
- Final service: $S_F^{(c)} = \text{final\_end}^{(c)} - \text{final\_start}^{(c)}$
- Total case flow time: $T_{total}^{(c)} = T_{up}^{(c)} + T_{join}^{(c)} + T_{down}^{(c)} + S_F^{(c)}$

**Why summing synchronization waits double counts:**
Synchronization waiting $W_i^{(c)}$ measures the idle gap between each branch’s completion and the join point. Summing $\sum_i W_i^{(c)}$ counts the parallel overlap region multiple times. For example, if A finishes at $t+10$, B at $t+20$, C at $t+30$, then $W_A=20, W_B=10, W_C=0$. The sum is $30$, which equals $T_{join}$ in this case, but algebraically $\sum_i W_i = 3 \cdot T_{join} - \sum_i (\text{end}_i - \text{fork\_release})$. The term $\sum_i (\text{end}_i - \text{fork\_release})$ itself double-counts parallel execution time. The additive decomposition avoids this by using the $\max$ operator for $T_{join}$, which inherently isolates the critical path’s $Q_{crit} + S_{crit}$, while $W_i$ remain residual gaps that do not extend flow time.

**Assumptions:**
1. *Non-preemptive execution*: Start/end blocks imply continuous work. Needed to equate $S_i$ with active service.
2. *Deterministic tie-breaking*: If two branches finish at the exact same minute, the critical branch is assigned alphabetically (A > B > C). Needed for unambiguous case-level attribution.
3. *Downstream waiting is queueing, not policy*: $T_{down}$ is treated as waiting for final resources, not a batch-release rule. Needed to interpret it as a bottleneck candidate.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Recommendation 1: Pre-allocate B intake capacity (queue compression)**
- *Mechanism*: Shift administrative pre-checks upstream or use a dedicated B-intake buffer to decouple B_start from fork_release.
- *Throughput*: Increases effective B resource utilization by reducing idle gaps between cases.
- *Flow-time tail*: Compresses the right tail of $T_{total}$ since B drives 75% of criticality.
- *Utilization*: B resource utilization may rise initially; monitor for overtime or spillage.
- *New bottlenecks*: If B queue drops below ~10 min, A’s 50-min service becomes binding more frequently. Risk of shifting criticality to A.
- *Sync effects*: Reduces $W_B$ to 0, but increases relative $W_A$ and $W_C$ in cases where B is no longer critical.

**Recommendation 2: Dynamic final-station routing with real-time load balancing**

- *Sync effects*: Decouples final station from upstream timing, making $T_{down}$ more responsive to real-time capacity.

# 7. Limits of inference

- **Causality vs. Correlation**: Timestamps show temporal ordering but cannot prove that B’s queue delay causes flow-time inflation. Unobserved factors (e.g., material readiness, operator skill) may drive both.
- **Branch resource capacity**: The data assumes one dedicated resource per branch, but branches could share pools or work in shifts. Unknown capacity limits prevent precise utilization or bottleneck identification.
- **Work preemption**: Start/end blocks imply non-preemptive execution. If preemption occurs, $S_i$ underestimates total work content and overestimates effective service time.
- **Final-station waiting nature**: $T_{down}$ is interpreted as queueing, but it could reflect a batch-release policy, quality hold, or managerial gatekeeping. Without explicit policy logs, we cannot distinguish operational congestion from procedural delay.
- **Sample size & representativeness**: $n=12$ captures a single day’s snapshot. Seasonality, shift changes, or case mix variability are unobserved. Statistical bounds on tail risk are wide.
- **Upstream preparation**: `arrival` to `fork_release` variation is minimal (15–20 min), but its drivers are unknown. If upstream prep is stochastic, it could interact with fork timing in ways not visible here.
- **Sync wait attribution**: $W_i$ is a residual metric. It indicates how early a branch finishes relative to the join point but does not reveal whether early completion is due to efficiency, lower complexity, or resource idling.

These limits suggest that redesigns should be piloted with continuous monitoring of queue dynamics, resource utilization, and release policies before scaling.
