# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Management, Fiber Installation Operations
**From:** Process Analytics – Decision Support
**Subject:** Do not scale R nationally; deploy selectively for simple houses, pause risky skips, and measure before wider rollout
**Recommendation: Option E (Hybrid + controlled measurement).**

Pilot R improved the common path but broke the exceptional path. Median cycle time fell 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with 3+ handovers fell 34% to 12%, and visible cost fell EUR 184 to EUR 161. At the same time p90 rose 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 rose 45.0 to 62.0 days, first-time-right fell 82% to 76%, complaints rose 6.1% to 9.8%, second visits rose 11% to 18%, manual activation rose 7% to 15%, and SLA breach rose 13% to 16%.

This is not a uniform success or failure. It is segment-split: simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot) gained -6.0 days median, -8.0 days p90, +3pp first-time-right, -1pp complaints. Apartments with medium risk, old permit-sensitive buildings, business and rural cases all improved median slightly but suffered large p90 increases (+5 to +25 days), lower first-time-right (-4 to -11pp) and higher complaints (+2 to +7pp).

Process-mining diagnostics show why: pilot fitness 0.94 with precision 0.51, frequent log-moves for billing before successful activation, installation repeats without survey, and complaint after billing, plus missing permit checks for older apartments. DFG shifts confirm survey skipping, more direct scheduling to installation, and growth in manual activation to billing and failed visit to second visit. Temporally, validate-to-schedule improved for simple houses, but visit-to-activation worsened for apartments and a new billing-to-complaint tail emerged. Organizationally, work centralized around highly utilized coordinators (93%) and activation specialists, reducing resilience when coordinators were absent.

National scaling (A) would institutionalize downstream rework, billing-before-working-service, and permit non-observance. Full rollback (B) would destroy proven value for simple houses.

We should **scale only what worked, pause what harmed, and close measurement gaps before any expansion.** Keep coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple low-risk houses only. Suspend predictive survey-skipping and post-billing activation for all medium-risk apartments, old buildings, and business customers. Enforce activation-test-before-billing and permit-check guardrails with conformance monitoring. Run a stratified, better-measured experiment to prove tail, cost, and quality can be controlled. Broader rollout only if tail, rework, and full cost return to at least baseline. Details in Sections 4-6.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision:** Fitness asks: can the model replay the log? Pilot fitness 0.94 is high because most traces follow the new coordinator fast path, so replay succeeds. Precision asks: does the model forbid behavior not seen? Pilot precision 0.51 is low because after removing/skipping survey and decoupling activation from billing, the log contains many variable interleavings – survey or no survey, test or manual, single or repeated visit, billing before or after activation, complaint or not. The discovered model must allow all those combinations to maintain fitness, becoming over-permissive. Baseline was tighter (0.87/0.79) because sequence was more enforced. Low generalization 0.56 further warns the pilot model may not transfer beyond the 6-week sample.

**Improved median but worse tail:** Median reflects the typical case. With 34% simple houses accelerated by merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers, the 50th percentile shifts down strongly. The tail reflects exceptions. Apartments, old buildings and business cases hit failed visits, second visits with longer Failed->Second delays under high coordinator load, manual activations, and Billing->Complaint loops. A small share with very long durations pulls p90/p95 and SLA breach up even as median improves. Truncated 6-week pilot plus incomplete downstream cost further understates tail.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Handover count measures transfers, not robustness. Coordinator ownership cut transfers 34% to 12% and simplified the handover network, improving accountability in the happy path. It also centralized the working-together network, concentrated context in one person, and pushed utilization to 93% with second-visit team at 92%. Queuing theory predicts waiting explodes at >85-90% utilization. When a coordinator was absent or overloaded, no shared context existed to pick up work, so cases stalled. Redundancy was traded for ownership.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost:** Visible cost stops before complaint handling and captures saved surveys and merged admin, hence EUR184 to EUR161. Full cost adds revisits, manual activation effort, billing disputes, and complaints, reversing to EUR198 to EUR207. Since cost after week 4 is excluded and pilot is only 6 weeks, late complaints and second visits for late-pilot cases are censored, so even EUR207 likely understates. Unit cost fell, system cost per resolved working connection rose.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** Effect heterogeneity. Simple houses need little survey/permit judgment and benefit from merging and ownership. Medium-risk apartments were often misclassified by the unvalidated predictive rule: Appointment->Survey 31% to 18% and Field->Install 29% to 17% show skips, followed by Visit->Activation test 78% to 60% and Install->Manual 8% to 19% and Install->Failed 13% to 19%. Old permit-sensitive buildings missed expected permit checks, creating +25d p90 and +7pp complaints. Business customers suffered manual-activation-to-billing disputes. Pilot over-representation of apartments/older buildings amplifies the average harm while masking simple-house benefit.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale R nationally.**
*Advantages:* Fastest median gains, captures visible cost saving, leverages coordinator momentum, simple message.
*Risks:* Scales tail explosion, rework, complaint growth, billing-before-activation and missing permit exposure to all regions; overloads coordinators nationally; locks in unvalidated risk rule. No guardrail for compliance-like issues.
*Reasonable only if:* Tail, quality and full-cost harms are proven to be temporary measurement artefacts and guardrails are already enforced – which current evidence does not support.

**B. Roll back to baseline.**
*Advantages:* Restores tail, first-time-right, permit and activation discipline; stops complaint/billing harm; lowest compliance risk.
*Risks:* Sacrifices proven -6d median / -8d p90 and quality gain for simple houses; demoralizes pilot teams; returns to high handovers (34%) and waiting-for-appointment complaints; learns nothing about fixable components.
*Reasonable only if:* Further harm is intolerable, regulator/customer trust requires immediate stop, or organization cannot enforce selective controls.

**C. Scale only coordinator-ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
*Advantages:* Isolates likely beneficial mechanism (ownership) from likely harmful ones (skip + early billing); reduces rework while keeping handover gains; simpler to govern.
*Risks:* Loses some speed for simple cases where skip may be safe; does not by itself solve coordinator overload or segment targeting; still needs measurement to prove ownership is causal vs merged task effect.
*Reasonable if:* Management prioritizes safety over maximum speed and wants a clean, reversible step that preserves accountability.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
*Advantages:* Produces causal, stratified evidence; fixes attribute incompleteness, start-timestamp sparsity, and cost truncation; avoids premature lock-in.
*Risks:* Delays benefits for simple houses; costs measurement effort; requires discipline to randomize and hold controls; leaves current pilot harm running if not paused.
*Reasonable if:* Uncertainty is too high to choose scope of scaling and value of information exceeds delay cost – true for survey rule and full cost.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, run measurement before wider scale.**
*Advantages:* Keeps demonstrated winner segment, contains loser segments with explicit guardrails (activation-before-billing, mandatory permit check, survey required except validated low-risk); combines C + D; addresses tail, rework and compliance while learning.
*Risks:* More complex triage and governance; needs accurate building-risk classification and coordinator capacity management; requires temporary dual process.
*Reasonable if:* Organization can enforce triage criteria and conformance monitoring – the most defensible given segment-split evidence. **Recommended.**

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by ability to change the scale/pause decision and cost-to-learn:

**1. M4 – Full 60-day downstream cost.** Resolves whether R saves or loses money. Current EUR161 vs EUR207 comparison is censored after week 4. If M4 shows simple-house full cost stays below baseline but apartment/old-building cost far exceeds it, it justifies E over A/B. If even simple-house full cost exceeds baseline, it forces pause. Highest decision value; cheap to collect retrospectively.

**2. M6 – Validate predictive rule vs field-survey outcomes.** Resolves whether skip failures are rule error or execution error. If rule accuracy is poor for medium-risk apartments/old buildings, it justifies suspending skip outside low-risk houses and blocks A. Fast, observational, prerequisite for any re-enablement.

**3. M1 – Randomized skip by risk stratum.** Provides causal estimate of skip effect on second visit, FTR, complaint and p90 per stratum. M6 shows correlation; M1 proves it. Could reopen selective skipping if low-risk stratum shows no harm, expanding E safely. Higher cost/slower than M6, so ranked after.

**4. M7 – Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and missing permit.** Resolves compliance-like exposure and enables safe operation of E. Low cost, immediate guardrail; violation rate near zero is a necessary condition for any scaling. If violations persist despite policy, scale must halt.

**5. M3 – Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit.** Resolves *why* rework happens (e.g., no access vs technical vs permit vs signal). Distinguishes fixable execution issues from structural skip harm. Needed to design targeted fixes and interpret M1/M6. Medium cost, high diagnostic value.

**6. M8 – Separate CX for speed-to-appointment vs working-service-after-installation.** Resolves NPS ambiguity (+21 to +17): are customers trading faster appointment for installed-but-not-working and early billing? If working-service scores collapsed while speed improved, it vetoes post-billing activation and supports E’s controls. Low cost survey split.

**7. M2 – Start timestamps for visit, activation, manual activation.** Resolves waiting vs service time and coordinator-queue contribution to Failed->Second delay. Could shift remedy from process redesign to capacity (hire/deputize coordinators) vs rule fix. Important but does not change segment scope as directly as M4/M6/M1.

**8. M5 – Coordinator vs shared-team ownership under absence.** Resolves resilience design: deputy, shared context, load cap. Valuable for sustainable scaling of ownership, but longer-term organizational test; does not resolve immediate tail/cost/compliance question, so lowest short-term decision value.

Together M4+M6+M7+M3 are minimum to justify broader rollout; M1+M8+M2 refine it; M5 hardens it.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: E – Selective deploy with hard guardrails + controlled measurement (incorporating C and D). Neither national scale nor full rollback.**

* **Immediate action (next 2 weeks):** Keep pilot running but constrain it. Enforce two non-negotiable controls everywhere: no Billing start before successful Activation test, and no skip of Permit check for apartment/old-building addresses. Cap coordinator caseload and assign named deputies to cover absence. Communicate to customers that billing follows working service.
* **What to pause:** Pause predictive survey-skipping for all medium-risk apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business and rural cases. Pause post-billing activation entirely. Pause any national rollout planning for R as-is. Do not disband field-survey capacity despite 52% utilization – it will be needed for reinstated surveys.
* **What to scale:** Scale only the proven bundle – single coordinator ownership + merged validate/schedule – **only for simple low-risk houses** where evidence shows median -6d, p90 -8d, FTR +3pp, complaint -1pp. Route all other segments to baseline path with coordinator support but with survey, permit, and pre-billing activation intact.
* **What to measure next (in order):** (1) M4 full 60-day cost per closed case; (2) M6 rule validation + M3 reason codes; (3) M7 continuous conformance dashboards for the two guardrails; (4) M8 split CX; (5) M1 stratified randomized skip trial in 2-3 regions with balanced case-mix and complete risk attributes; (6) M2 start timestamps to size coordinator capacity; (7) M5 absence-coverage test.
* **Threshold for broader rollout:** Require over a balanced 8-week window: p90 31d and p95 45d, first-time-right 82%, complaint 6.5%, SLA breach 13%, permit compliance 98% and billing-before-activation 0, full 60-day cost per case baseline, and coordinator utilization <80% with no absence stall, with benefits replicated outside simple houses or with validated low-risk criteria. If thresholds are met only for simple houses, retain E permanently and treat other segments as separate improvement track. If full cost or tail remains worse even for simple houses after complete measurement, roll back.
