The incident therefore shows two different recovery curves:

1. **Operational recovery:** hospital service and throughput recovered relatively quickly.
2. **Control and evidence recovery:** traceability, documentation, reconciliation, and audit closure lagged materially behind.

The case volumes should not be compared as simple throughput rates without accounting for the different observation-window lengths and backlog mix. More importantly, improved case completion in T4 does not mean the recovery was complete when audit remediation, proof-of-delivery remediation, and manual reservation reconciliation remained open.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

A deviation from the baseline process is not automatically bad. During a cyber incident, resilience means preserving essential service **while retaining an auditable, risk-equivalent control or using a formally approved exception path**. Concealed noncompliance occurs when the organization appears to preserve throughput by deleting, assuming, or postponing controls that are required to establish legal, quality, safety, or traceability status.

In this case, the difference is visible in the contrast between hospital service protection and control degradation:

- Hospital-critical fill rate remained **91%** in T2, which is evidence of operational resilience.
- Yet T2 traceability exceptions rose from **0.8% to 9.5%**, cold-chain documentation gaps rose from **0.4% to 4.8%**, and controlled-substance dual-control gaps rose from **0.2% to 2.9%**.
- T2 fitness fell to **0.71** and only **44%** of traces fit the normal model.
- The conformance results show not just alternative routing but missing required model events: quality/release, scan, POD, and temperature-control records.

Low conformance alone does not prove misconduct: the normative model was designed for normal operations, not necessarily for a declared contingency mode. However, when low conformance coincides with missing controls for cold-chain and controlled substances, it is evidence of material compliance exposure rather than merely a different process variant.

## True or conditionally true resilience

The following mechanisms can be resilient when governed properly:

- **B, manual authorization check:** a credible substitute if based on controlled offline data and documented decisions.
- **A, hospital-critical fast lane:** resilient when it changes queue order and resource allocation, not authorization or release requirements.
- **E, manual pick lists:** resilient when they preserve lot, expiry, quantity, picker, verifier, and chain-of-custody evidence at the time of work.
- **I, deferral of lower-priority work:** resilient when triage rules are explicit, pharmacy clinical urgency is protected, and deferral ages are bounded.
- **J, backlog prioritization:** resilient when it is transparent, risk-based, and fairness-tested.
- **K, reconciliation of manual reservations:** recovery resilience, because it restores inventory integrity before final closure.

## Risky shortcuts or concealed noncompliance

The strongest examples are:

- **C, skipped authorization:** customer history cannot replace a current authorization control.
- **G, dispatch without scan or offline equivalent:** speed was achieved by weakening chain-of-custody evidence.
- **Uncontrolled H, phone POD:** especially inadequate for controlled substances and cannot establish cold-chain integrity.
- **Unbounded D, stale inventory snapshots:** initially useful, but progressively unsafe as inventory state diverged from the snapshot.
- **Release sampling for high-risk products under F:** helpful but insufficient when legal or quality controls require 100% evidence.

These shortcuts may improve visible throughput while transferring risk to customers, quality teams, auditors, and future recall investigations.

## Ambiguous adaptations

A, D, E, F, H, I, and J are not inherently good or bad. Their acceptability depends on product risk, customer urgency, data integrity, and whether a minimum control set remains intact.

That said, some matters are not subject to ordinary risk appetite:

- A hospital emergency may justify expedited handling.
- It does **not** justify shipment of a controlled substance without required dual control and secure custody.
- It does **not** justify release of a cold-chain product without contemporaneous temperature assurance.
- It does **not** justify shipping a recall-sensitive product when lot status cannot be confirmed.

Finally, **L, audit remediation, is necessary but is not resilience by itself**. It is evidence that the organization noticed the gap. It cannot turn missing contemporaneous temperature, custody, or authorization evidence into proof that the control actually occurred.

# 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned model should create an approved **incident-mode process**, rather than allowing teams to improvise separate local variants. It should have its own conformance model, evidence requirements, roles, and exit criteria.

## Incident modes and explicit trigger conditions

| Mode | Trigger | Operational rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal mode** | WMS, authorization service, inventory synchronization, scanners, and courier interfaces are available and trusted. | Standard process applies. |
| **Declared degraded mode** | A cyber alert affects WMS integrity; a critical service is unavailable beyond the approved threshold; scanner or inventory synchronization failures exceed the approved threshold; or cybersecurity cannot confirm the trustworthiness of current data. | Incident commander, Quality/Responsible Person, warehouse operations, cybersecurity, and compliance activate the fallback plan. Normal system records are not assumed trustworthy merely because they are visible. |
| **Manual fulfillment mode** | WMS cannot safely support authorization, reservation, scanning, or dispatch confirmation, but controlled offline data and manual controls are available. | Only approved fallback routes may be used. Every fulfillment event must have an offline evidence record. |
| **Partial recovery mode** | Order intake returns, but inventory synchronization, scanning, or transaction replay is unreliable. This matches T3. | New and manual transactions are reconciled against a controlled manual ledger. No automatic return to normal dispatch, release, or invoice closure. |
| **Normal recovery exit** | Cybersecurity approves system integrity; end-to-end tests pass; inventory variances are within approved tolerance; manual transactions are ingested and reconciled; all high-risk shipments have complete evidence or an approved incident disposition; and the high-risk audit queue is zero. | Resume normal mode. Lower-risk administrative exceptions may remain as tracked CAPA work, but they must not be reported as closed compliance cases. |

A cyber integrity alert should trigger degraded mode immediately. A simple temporary screen outage may have a short operational threshold, such as 15 minutes, but the exact threshold should be approved in advance and should not delay action where data integrity is in doubt.

## Per-order recovery flow

```text
Receive order
  -> Create incident order record and unique contingency ID
  -> Classify customer urgency, product risk, and order age
  -> Verify customer/product authorization using approved fallback source
  -> Verify inventory using controlled snapshot plus manual reservation ledger
  -> Triage to fulfil / alternate route / defer / cancel
  -> Manual pick and pack with point-of-work evidence
  -> Product-specific quality and release gate
  -> Offline dispatch-custody event
  -> Verified proof of delivery
  -> Provisional invoice hold
  -> System ingestion and reconciliation
  -> Compliance evidence review
  -> Final invoice and order closure
```

The key design principle is that triage may happen early, but **release cannot occur until the applicable authorization, inventory, quality, and product-specific controls are complete**.

If required controls cannot be achieved, the order should follow one of these approved alternatives:

```text
Unable to meet minimum controls
  -> validated alternate warehouse / validated courier / manufacturer or supplier route
  -> clinically justified deferment with customer communication
  -> cancellation with documented reason and escalation where clinically relevant
```

There should be no direct contingency equivalent of:

```text
Skip authorization -> Dispatch without scan -> Phone proof later
```

## Triage rules

1. **Apply hard safety and regulatory gates first.** Product risk determines the controls required; customer criticality determines queue priority only after those controls can be met.

2. **Priority 0: documented patient-care emergency or hospital-critical demand.**
   - Expedite compliance staffing, pharmacist release, inventory verification, alternate logistics, and management escalation.
   - Do not waive controlled-substance, cold-chain, recall, or customer-authorization requirements.

3. **Priority 1: clinically urgent pharmacy demand and eligible high-risk product orders.**
   - Include urgent pharmacy orders where patient care would be harmed by delay.
   - Prioritize short-dated, recall-sensitive, and cold-chain products when safe to release.

4. **Priority 2: Pharmacy-standard ambient demand.**
   - Fulfil through controlled manual processing and age-based escalation.

5. **Priority 3: Wholesale-bulk and non-urgent replenishment demand.**
   - Defer first when capacity is constrained, subject to maximum age and communication commitments.

6. **Fairness safeguard.**
   - Review each shift for aging Pharmacy-standard orders, cancellation concentration, and any customer group receiving materially worse service than the declared priority policy permits.
   - Allow a documented clinical escalation route for pharmacies and other non-hospital customers.

## Minimum controls by product and customer risk

| Risk category | Minimum controls before release |
|---|---|
| **All orders** | Unique contingency order ID; verified customer/product authorization from an approved fallback source; inventory source and timestamp; manual reservation entry; pick, pack, release, dispatch, and POD evidence; named operator and reviewer; exception reason and approver where applicable. |
| **Hospital-critical, any product** | Priority handling, rapid escalation, and alternate validated logistics if needed. Hospital status does not reduce product-specific control requirements. |
| **Ambient, lower-risk products** | Current fallback authorization, controlled inventory check, documented pick/pack/release, offline dispatch custody record, and authenticated POD. A single trained release check may be acceptable if approved. |
| **Cold-chain products** | Approved authorization; product and lot traceability; validated storage and packaging; temperature device or logger identifier; temperature evidence at handoff and receipt where required; documented release; verified receiving party. If contemporaneous temperature assurance cannot be established, use an alternate validated route or do not release under the normal distribution process. |
| **Controlled substances** | Current authorization against the controlled-product register; full lot/quantity traceability; dual control at pick and release; independent count; secure storage and secure courier custody; named and verified recipient; no phone-only POD; no release based on stale inventory alone. |
| **Recall-sensitive products** | Confirmed current lot and hold/recall status from a trusted source; full lot and recipient traceability; Quality approval where status cannot be confirmed. No shipment from uncertain stock. |
| **Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk customers** | Explicit deferral reason, promised review time, status communication, aging threshold, and escalation route. Customer segment affects priority, not authorization or product controls. |

## Manual evidence capture

The organization should replace ad hoc spreadsheets and informal notes with a controlled contingency packet or offline application. Each packet should be pre-numbered and linked to one order ID.

At minimum, it should capture:

- order ID, customer ID, product, lot, expiry, quantity, and location;
- customer/product authorization source, version, timestamp, and verifier;
- inventory snapshot version and timestamp;
- manual reservation entry and any physical count;
- picker, packer, release approver, dual-control witness where required;
- temperature device ID, readings, packaging confirmation, and custody events for cold-chain items;
- controlled-substance count, secure handoff, courier identity, and recipient verification;
- dispatch time, courier or route reference, and handoff signature;
- POD method, recipient identity, timestamp, and follow-up status;
- exception reason, approving role, and remediation owner.

Offline authorization and inventory extracts should be signed or integrity-protected, time-stamped, and subject to a defined validity period. The manual reservation ledger should be centrally controlled so that two teams cannot allocate the same stock.

## Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

The T4 DFG shows why reconciliation cannot be treated as an afterthought. A redesigned flow should be:

```text
Manual or partial-recovery shipment
  -> provisional operational completion
  -> transaction ingestion and matching
  -> inventory, authorization, lot, POD, temperature, and custody reconciliation
  -> evidence classified as:
       verified
       documentary gap remediable without safety concern
       potential quality/compliance breach
  -> final order and invoice closure only after required evidence is verified
```

For potential breaches:

```text
Potential breach
  -> Quality / Responsible Person review
  -> customer, carrier, and inventory trace investigation
  -> containment or quarantine where necessary
  -> regulatory assessment and notification where applicable
  -> CAPA and final disposition
```

The reconciliation sequence should prioritize:

1. controlled substances;
2. cold-chain shipments;
3. recall-sensitive lots;
4. hospital-critical shipments with missing evidence;
5. inventory mismatches and duplicate reservations;
6. remaining ambient and lower-risk documentation gaps.

An invoice may be placed in a **provisional hold** while evidence is reconciled, but invoice closure should not create the appearance that the order is compliance-complete. A separate “operationally delivered” status and “compliance-complete” status prevents the T4 pattern in which Invoice close is followed by a long Audit remediation tail.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Indicators should be calculated by incident mode, customer segment, product risk, and priority class. Aggregate performance can hide the pattern seen in T2: strong hospital service alongside severe pharmacy degradation and elevated traceability risk.

| Dimension | Leading indicators during the incident | Lagging indicators after or during recovery |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughput** | Orders received, released, dispatched, and completed per hour or shift; work in progress by process stage; manual-packet capacity; ratio of inflow to clearance rate. | Completed cases per day; median and p90 cycle time by Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk segment; recovery time to baseline service levels. |
| **Service protection** | Priority-0 demand volume; percentage of hospital-critical orders with a feasible validated route; staffing availability for release and compliance checks; alternate-carrier capacity. | Hospital-critical fill rate; missed clinical-service commitments; p90 cycle time and cancellation rate for urgent orders. |
| **Traceability** | Percentage of dispatches with unique contingency ID, lot/expiry, courier custody record, and POD capture at dispatch; age of inventory snapshot; dispatch-to-POD delay distribution. | Traceability exception rate; percentage of shipments for which lot-to-customer reconstruction succeeds; conformance to the approved contingency model; post-incident recall simulation results. |
| **Control completeness** | Percentage of orders with authorization complete before release; percentage of cold-chain orders with a contemporaneous temperature record; percentage of controlled-substance orders with documented dual control; release-gate rejection count. | Missing authorization, temperature, dual-control, quality/release, scan, and POD rates; late discovery of invalid authorization or custody gaps. High-risk control completeness should be effectively 100% at release. |
| **Backlog age** | Queue size, oldest order age, p50/p90 backlog age, deferred-order count, manual reservations awaiting reconciliation, and inflow-versus-clearance trend. | Number and age of orders cancelled after deferral; backlog-related expiry, stockout, or lost-sales outcomes; time to clear manual reservations and POD gaps. |
| **Rework and inventory integrity** | Manual-record discrepancies found at shift review; cycle-count variance; duplicate reservation alerts; number of orders requiring repick or re-release before dispatch. | Rework/correction rate, inventory-adjustment volume, wrong-item/wrong-lot events, stock variance, and complaint or safety-event rate. |
| **Fairness across customer groups** | Capacity allocation by declared priority; aging and deferral rates by segment after adjusting for product risk and clinical urgency; escalation requests from pharmacies; communication timeliness. | Fill rate, median/p90 cycle time, cancellation rate, and backlog age for Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk customers, stratified by comparable product risk. Fairness does not require equal service; it requires policy-consistent, explainable differences. |
| **Recovery and audit tail** | Number of manual cases not yet ingested; percentage reconciled within target; count of high-risk cases with missing evidence at each shift close; audit queue inflow versus closure rate. | Audit-remediation queue size and age; time from dispatch to compliance-complete status; cases closed financially but not compliantly; CAPA recurrence rate. |

Two conformance measures should be maintained:

1. conformance to the normal model when normal systems are operating; and  
2. conformance to the approved contingency model during incident mode.

That distinction avoids treating every approved manual path as failure while still identifying prohibited deviations such as skipped authorization, missing release, uncontrolled dispatch, or absent cold-chain evidence.

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept controlled service degradation, including slower Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk fulfillment, where that is necessary to preserve hospital supply and maintain non-negotiable product controls. It should not accept throughput that depends on assumed authorization, missing controlled-substance dual control, absent cold-chain temperature assurance, unverified recall status, or unrecorded courier custody. It should also not treat invoice closure or later audit remediation as proof of compliant completion. The recommendation could be widened only if evidence shows that the fallback controls are contemporaneous, independently verifiable, product-risk appropriate, and produce traceability, control-completeness, fairness, and audit-tail outcomes close to the approved baseline.
