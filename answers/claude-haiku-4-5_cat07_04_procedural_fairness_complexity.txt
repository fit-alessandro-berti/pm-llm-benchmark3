# 1. Fairness dimensions

## 1.1 Outcome parity
**Raw approval rates** range from 58% (Group D) to 78% (Group A), a 20 percentage-point spread. However, raw parity is not a fairness criterion when case complexity differs legitimately. The question is whether the differential is justified by documented complexity and risk.

## 1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity
After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, Groups B, C, and D retain approval disadvantages of 5.5, 2.2, and 3.1 percentage points respectively. These residual disparities persist *after* accounting for case complexity, suggesting either:
- Unobserved case complexity or legitimate factors not in the model.
- Procedural differences that disadvantage these groups independent of the case facts.
- Measurement bias in the control variables (see Section 5).

## 1.3 Procedural burden
Beyond approval, applicants experience different burdens in *how* decisions are made:
- **Group B**: 9.5 percentage-point higher probability of "Request Missing Documents" step; 24% route through Language Support Contact (V4), creating handovers.
- **Group C**: 5.5 percentage-point higher probability of Supervisor Review; 31% route through Accommodation Review (V5), with median 4.7-day wait between that step and Eligibility Assessment.
- **Group D**: 5.7 percentage-point higher probability of Appeal, suggesting dissatisfaction despite documented complexity.

Procedural burden is fair if imposed proportionately to case complexity, but unfair if support rights become bottlenecks.

## 1.4 Timeliness
Adjusted cycle-time disparities (controlling for case complexity):
- **Group B**: +2.6 days median.
- **Group C**: +4.1 days median.
- **Group D**: +0.9 days median.

Notably, Groups B and C experience longer adjusted delays even though their high cycle times can partly be explained by case mix (higher incomplete docs, in-person applications, prior cases). The residual delay suggests resource constraints (7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B; 5 accommodation specialists for Group C) rather than proportional complexity.

## 1.5 Consistency of routing
The variant distributions show systematic routing patterns:
- **V1** (fast, no-support path): 42% of Group A vs. 19% of Group B and 18% of Group D.
- **V4** (language support): 24% of Group B vs. 1% of Group A.
- **V5** (accommodation review): 31% of Group C vs. 2% of Group A.
- **V3** (fraud review + supervisor): 28% of Group D vs. 9% of Group A.

These are consistent with documented needs (e.g., Group B has 57% language support need), but consistency does not ensure fairness if the support pathways are under-resourced or introduce unequal delay.

## 1.6 Quality and accessibility of support
The data do not directly measure whether language support or accommodation review are responsive, intelligible, or respectful. However:
- The 4.7-day wait between Accommodation Review and Eligibility Assessment suggests accommodation decisions may be treated as sequential gatekeeping rather than integrated into eligibility.
- No separate service-level targets for language support or accommodation steps suggest these rights-preserving steps are not operationally prioritized.
- Handover patterns (general  bilingual  general, general  accommodation specialist  supervisor) may fragment decision-making.

## 1.7 Appeal and rework burden
- **Group B**: 13% appeal rate vs. 6% for Group A (adjusted +4.4 pp); 22% rework rate.
- **Group C**: 10% appeal rate; 24% rework rate.
- **Group D**: 16% appeal rate (adjusted +5.7 pp); 20% rework rate.

Higher appeal and rework rates among Groups B, C, and D suggest either (a) decisions are harder to understand or apply fairly to their cases, or (b) applicants from these groups lack resources to contest, making appeals only the most egregious cases. The appeal flag field should be analyzed for reversal rates and appeal-stage approval rates.

## 1.8 Resource-allocation fairness
- **Group A**: 3600 cases across 52 caseworkers  69 cases per worker.
- **Group B**: 1250 cases, 7 bilingual + 3 regional officers overloaded; implied severe concentration.
- **Group C**: 820 cases to 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also do supervisor review (dual-role bottleneck).
- **Group D**: routed to fraud-review queue (appropriate for risk, but queue depth not reported).

The concentration of specialized resources on smaller populations, without reported capacity expansion, generates bottlenecks that delay even straightforward cases in those groups. This is a resource-allocation fairness issue independent of decision quality.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

## 2.1 Matching and stratification variables

**Primary stratifiers** (measure case legitimacy):
- `risk_score_band`: Band-based matching within groups to control for measured risk.
- `documentation_completeness`: Incomplete docs may explain request-missing-docs, but may also be caused by language or accessibility barriers (Section 5).
- `household_size_band`: Complexity proxy; ensure balanced across groups within strata.
- `prior_case_count`: Repeat applicants may need faster or slower processing; stratify.
- `region`: Regional workforce capacity varies; stratify to isolate regional effects.

**Secondary stratifiers** (measure process exposure):
- `channel` (online vs. in-person): Controls for application modality but in-person may require longer verification.
- `language_preference`: Non-English as separate stratum; do not collapse into Group B.
- `disability_accommodation_requested`: Separate stratum; do not rely on group membership alone.

**Approach:**
1. Stratify by `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` percentile.
2. Within each stratum, compute approval rate, cycle time, supervisor review rate, and request-missing-docs rate for each applicant group.
3. If adjusted disparities shrink, the case mix explanation is stronger; if they persist or grow, procedural explanations are more plausible.
4. Repeat stratification within each `region` to check for geographic heterogeneity.

## 2.2 Path and variant comparison

**Hypotheses to test:**

**H1 (Legitimate routing):** Within each risk_score_band stratum, Groups B, C, D are routed more to support-intensive paths (V2, V4, V5) because their cases require those steps. Approval rates should not differ materially within path.

**H2 (Support step bottleneck):** Within path, groups routed through Language Support Contact (V4) or Accommodation Review (V5) experience extra delay not proportional to case complexity.

**H3 (Supervisor review as screen):** Disproportionate Supervisor Review for Groups B and C (adjusted +8.0 pp and +5.5 pp) acts as an additional barrier even after initial eligibility assessment, independent of risk_score_band.

**Analysis:**
1. Filter log by variant using `pm4py.filter_variants()`.
2. For each variant and each risk stratum, compute:
   - Approval rate by applicant group.
   - Median cycle time by applicant group.
   - Time spent waiting between support steps (Language Support  Eligibility; Accommodation Review  Eligibility) by applicant group.
3. Perform Kaplan-Meier or Cox regression to estimate hazard of approval, controlling for variant and risk stratum, and test for group interaction.
4. If approval hazard does not differ by group *within variant and risk stratum*, routing is likely legitimate. If it does, procedural bias is more likely.

## 2.3 Resource-assignment comparison

**Data needed (computed from log):**
- For each caseworker resource, compute caseload (number of cases) and workload (sum of service times).
- Compute specialization: fraction of a caseworker's cases in each activity (e.g., Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review).
- Aggregate by group: what fraction of Group B's cases touch the 7 bilingual caseworkers?

**Analysis:**
1. Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network()` to map case flow between resource pairs.
2. For Groups B and C, count handovers: (general caseworker  specialist  general) is 2 handovers; (general  specialist  decision) is 1.
3. Compare handover counts by group, controlling for path and complexity.
4. Compute caseworker workload concentration using Herfindahl index: $H = \sum_i (w_i / W)^2$, where $w_i$ is caseworker $i$'s workload and $W$ is total. Higher $H$ indicates concentration.
5. Hypothesis: Groups B and C have higher $H$, meaning a smaller set of caseworkers processes their cases, creating bottlenecks.

## 2.4 Waiting-time decomposition

**Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time()` and `pm4py.get_service_time()` to separate:**
- **Service time** (activity duration): how long the activity itself takes.
- **Waiting time** (between activities): queue delay before the activity starts.

**Decompose total cycle time:**
$$\text{Cycle time} = \sum_{\text{activities}} (\text{service time} + \text{waiting time})$$

**Analysis:**
1. Compute service time and waiting time for each activity by group.
2. Identify which activities contribute most to cycle-time disparity:
   - Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review appear in Table (Section 1) as high-wait steps.
   - Use `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg()` to measure edge-wise waiting times.
3. Estimate counterfactual: if waiting times for Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and Supervisor Review for Group B/C matched Group A (controlling for risk stratum), how much would cycle time shrink?
4. Compare to case complexity: if the counterfactual reduction is large relative to the case-mix difference in documentation_completeness or risk_score_band, resource constraints are the likely cause.

## 2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

**Risk and concern:**
- `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities against Group D (if prior rejections or appeals are risk factors).
- `documentation_completeness` may be measured with language bias (e.g., forms marked "incomplete" if signatures are unclear or language non-standard).

**Sensitivity approach:**
1. Re-run adjusted comparison *excluding* risk_score_band. Do approval disparities shrink, stay the same, or grow?
   - If shrink: risk_score_band explains the disparity (but may itself be biased; see Section 5).
   - If grow: other factors (procedural, resource-based) are primary.
2. Re-run adjusted comparison *excluding* documentation_completeness. Same logic.
3. Run adjusted comparison on the subsample of cases with documentation_completeness = "complete" only. If disparity shrinks relative to full sample, documentation_completeness is a confounder; if it persists, the disparity is not driven by document defects.
4. Compare approval rates for cases routed through Request Missing Documents (V2, V4) *within* each group. If Group B cases in V2 have lower approval rates than Group A cases in V2 (same complexity), the extra document requests may be punitive rather than clarifying.

## 2.6 Subgroup checks by region and channel

**Regional heterogeneity:**
1. Table (Section 1) notes "backlog in two regions" for Supervisor Review. Stratify by region.
2. For each region, recompute approval rate, cycle time, and adjusted disparities for each group.
3. If disparities are concentrated in the two backlog regions, resource investment is the primary solution; if uniform across regions, structural bias is more likely.

**Channel heterogeneity:**
1. Stratify by channel (online vs. in-person).
2. Group A is 9% in-person; Groups B and C are 38% and 24% in-person, respectively.
3. Among in-person applicants, are approval and cycle-time disparities smaller than overall?
   - If yes: in-person application is a confounding factor (e.g., more complex cases apply in-person).
   - If no: disparities persist even controlling for channel, suggesting group-based procedural differences.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## 3.1 Most important path disparities

**Disparity 1: Fast-track eligibility (V1)**
- **Group A**: 42% of cases.
- **Groups B, C, D**: 19%, 22%, 18%.
- **Implication**: Group A disproportionately skips support and complex steps, taking 5.8 days. Others are pushed into longer paths.
- **Legitimacy check**: Is the 42% vs. 19% difference explained by risk_score_band and documentation_completeness?
  - Group A: 18% high-risk, 21% incomplete docs.
  - Group B: 29% high-risk, 46% incomplete docs.
  - Partial explanation: Group B's higher incomplete-docs share justifies more document requests. But 42% vs. 19% is a 23 pp difference; incomplete-docs difference is 25 pp, suggesting case mix explains most of the path disparity. *However*, if language barriers cause the incompleteness, the "fast track" is not available to language-supporting applicants even when their risk is actually low.

**Disparity 2: Language support as mandatory step (V4)**
- **Group B**: 24% (median 19.2 days).
- **Other groups**: 1–3%.
- **Waiting time**: 3.8 days median between Language Support Contact and Eligibility Assessment.
- **Legitimacy check**: Is the 3.8-day wait proportional to the complexity of serving 7 bilingual caseworkers for ~400 Group B cases needing language support?
  - Expected queue: 400 cases / 7 caseworkers  57 cases per caseworker.
  - If service time per language-support case is 1 day, expected queue wait is  8 days.
  - Observed 3.8 days is *lower* than expected queue, suggesting cases are being prioritized or batched. *However*, 3.8 days is still 4× the 0.8–1.4 day wait between Submit and Identity Verification.
  - **Concern**: The step is necessary and legitimate, but under-resourcing (7 caseworkers) creates a bottleneck that slows the entire V4 pathway and contributes +2.6 days to adjusted cycle time.

**Disparity 3: Accommodation review as sequential gate (V5)**
- **Group C**: 31% (median 21.6 days).
- **Other groups**: 2–3%.
- **Waiting time**: 4.7 days median between Accommodation Review and Eligibility Assessment.
- **Resource constraint**: 5 accommodation specialists, 2 of whom also do supervisor review.
- **Implication**: Accommodation decisions are treated as a precondition to eligibility (sequential) rather than an accommodation *during* eligibility assessment (parallel). This adds 4.7 days of queue time.
- **Procedural fairness concern**: If accommodation review is a *right-preserving* step (policy statement), making it a sequential gate that delays eligibility assessment may burden the applicant to obtain the accommodation they are entitled to. A fair process would integrate accommodation into eligibility so that applicants are not penalized for requesting reasonable accommodations.
- **Legitimacy check**: Is 31% of Group C routed through V5 proportional to the 62% accommodation-request share?
  - 31% < 62%, suggesting not all accommodation-requesting applicants go through the full V5 path. Some may be accommodated ad-hoc. But the 31% who do go through V5 experience a 21.6-day median cycle, vs. 5.8 days for V1.
  - If case complexity (risk, incomplete docs) within the accommodation-requesting population is high, the longer cycle may be justified. Stratification by risk within Group C accommodation-requesters is needed.

**Disparity 4: Fraud review as escalation (V3)**
- **Group D**: 28% (median 18.4 days).
- **Other groups**: 8–11%.
- **Legitimacy check**: 51% of Group D is in high-risk band vs. 18% of Group A. So V3 routing is proportional to risk.
- **Residual concern**: Even controlling for risk_score_band, Group D cases have +5.7 pp higher appeal rate and +2.0 pp higher Supervisor Review rate. This suggests that within the high-risk stratum, Group D applicants face additional procedural hurdles (fraud review, supervisor review) beyond their risk profile. Possible mechanisms (Section 4):
  - Risk_score_band is coarse and correlates with group membership; finer risk stratification might reveal that Group D's risk is overstated.
  - Fraud review decisions are less transparent, making appeals more likely.
  - Supervisor review is applied unevenly, disadvantaging Group D within the high-risk category.

## 3.2 Resource disparities most clearly concerning

**Resource concentration:**
| Group | Population | Caseworkers | Caseload per worker | Specialization |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3600 | 52 general | 69 | Low (spread across general staff) |
| B | 1250 | 7 bilingual + 3 regional | ~100–150 per bilingual | High (concentrated in specialists) |
| C | 820 | 5 accommodation + backlog | ~160 per specialist | Very high (dual-role dual bottleneck) |
| D | 980 | Fraud-review queue (size unknown) | Unknown | High (concentrated in fraud queue) |

**Concern**: Groups B and C have far fewer caseworkers per applicant, creating queues. The "specialization" means that cases cannot easily be redirected to other caseworkers if one is absent or overloaded. This is an operational fairness issue: applicants have no control over their group, yet they are subject to unequal resource availability.

**Handover burden** (from the qualitative notes):
- Group B: general  bilingual  general (2 handovers, case fragmentary).
- Group C: general  accommodation specialist  supervisor (2 handovers, decision split across roles).

Handovers introduce coordination costs, information loss, and delayed decision-making. If Group A cases stay with one caseworker 42% of the time (V1 path), while Group B and C are handover-heavy, that is a procedural-fairness disadvantage.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

## 4.1 Legitimate mechanisms

**Mechanism 1: Case complexity (true case mix).**
- Groups B, C, D genuinely have more complex cases (higher risk, incomplete docs, prior cases, larger households).
- Longer processing and higher Supervisor Review reflect the need for additional scrutiny, not bias.
- **Evidence supporting**: Case-mix table shows substantial differences (e.g., Group D 51% high-risk vs. Group A 18%; Group B 46% incomplete vs. 21%).
- **Evidence constraining**: Adjusted disparities persist after controlling for these variables. The -5.5 pp approval gap for Group B and +2.6 day cycle-time gap suggest case mix explains 60–70% of raw disparities but not all.

**Mechanism 2: Legitimate routing to required support steps.**
- Language support and accommodation review are legal rights, not luxuries. Group B and C must be routed to these steps, creating longer paths.
- Within a support-intensive path (V4 or V5), approval rates should not differ by group if the underlying case is treated fairly.
- **Test**: Variant-stratified analysis (Section 2.2). If approval rates within V4 path are equal across groups controlling for risk, routing is legitimate. If Group B approval is lower within V4 path, the support step may be acting as a gatekeeping filter rather than an accommodation.

**Mechanism 3: Risk-appropriate fraud scrutiny.**
- Group D has 51% high-risk share and 49% high prior-case count. Fraud review is appropriate.
- **Constraint**: Adjusted analysis controls for risk_score_band, yet disparities remain (+5.7 pp appeal rate). This suggests either:
  - Risk_score_band is too coarse; finer stratification would explain the disparity.
  - Fraud review process applies different standards to Group D even within the same risk band.

## 4.2 Concerning mechanisms

**Mechanism 4: Under-resourcing of support steps as de facto discrimination.**
- If 57% of Group B needs language support but only 7 bilingual caseworkers exist, the process cannot deliver fair timeliness.
- The decision to employ 7 bilingual caseworkers is not group-specific animus, but the operational consequence is that Group B applicants experience 3.8-day queues.
- This is *systemic* unfairness: the process has a built-in constraint that disproportionately burdens a protected class.
- **Parallel**: Disability accommodation in employment law is an individual right, but under-resourcing it creates de facto discrimination (e.g., inaccessible facilities delay all disabled employees).

**Mechanism 5: Sequential vs. integrated processing of support steps.**
- Policy states language support and accommodation review are "rights-preserving," but operational practice treats them as preconditions to eligibility.
- If accommodation review is held at Accommodation Review  Eligibility  Decision, the applicant waits 4.7 days for accommodation decision before eligibility can even start.
- A fair process would integrate: Eligibility Assessment *with* accommodation accommodations considered, so timeliness is not sacrificed.
- **Mechanism**: The sequential structure may result from organizational siloing (a separate "accommodation" team) rather than intentional discrimination, but the effect is unfair.

**Mechanism 6: Risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as proxies for protected attributes.**
- If risk_score_band is constructed from prior case history, and Group D has higher prior-case count, the band may be circular: high prior cases  high risk score  more fraud review  more delays  more appeals  more prior cases.
- If documentation_completeness is assessed by first-language staff, and minority-language applicants' documents are marked "incomplete" due to language difference (e.g., signatures, formatting), the measure conflates case complexity with language barriers.
- **Effect**: Controls for these variables may not fully isolate "case complexity" from group-based disadvantage.

**Mechanism 7: Bias in supervisor review or appeal decisions.**
- Groups B, C, D have higher adjusted probability of Supervisor Review (even controlling for risk). Supervisors may apply different decision standards.
- Group D has highest appeal rate. Appeals may be filed because decisions lack explanation, or because applicants perceive bias, or both.
- **Data gap**: The analysis does not have appeal *reversal* rates or appeal-stage approval rates. If Group D's appeals are reversed at high rates, that is evidence of initial bias in the main process.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

## 5.1 Measurement bias in control variables

**risk_score_band:**
- Likely constructed from applicant history: prior denials, prior cases, fraud flags, etc.
- **Bias risk**: If Group D has more prior cases due to chronic low income or disability (real need, not real risk), the band conflates legitimate entitlement need with fraud risk.
- **Bias risk**: If prior denials themselves were subject to bias, risk_score_band perpetuates that bias.
- **Implication**: Controlling for risk_score_band does not prove fairness; it may hide historical discrimination.

**documentation_completeness:**
- May be subjectively assessed by first-contact caseworker.
- **Bias risk**: Language-barrier cases (missing signatures, translations, unclear handwriting) marked "incomplete" when a bilingual review would find them adequate.
- **Bias risk**: In-person applicants (Group B 38%, Group C 24%) may be marked "incomplete" due to quality of verbal information vs. written, even if substantively complete.
- **Implication**: Higher "incomplete" rate in Group B and C may partly reflect assessment bias, not case deficiency.

## 5.2 Missing applicant needs and context

**Not captured:**
- **Literacy and numeracy**: Applicants' ability to read forms, understand instructions, and prepare documents. Language-supporting applicants may have low literacy in any language.
- **Disability type and severity**: The flag is binary (accommodation requested/not). Some accommodations are minor (e.g., large-print), others fundamental (e.g., in-person interviews due to cognitive disability). Group C's 62% accommodation share may mix these.
- **Access to external support**: Lawyers, nonprofit advocates, family support. Group A may have more access, reducing errors and appeals.
- **Digitalization barriers**: Group A is 9% in-person (91% online), while Group B is 38% in-person (62% online). Online application requires device, internet, digital literacy. In-person may be a choice due to digital exclusion, or actual preference for staff interaction.
- **Household composition**: household_size_band is included, but not dependency type (children, elderly, disabled household members). This could affect application complexity, document requirements, and risk assessment.

## 5.3 Unobserved resource workload and unavailable capacity

**Data limitations:**
- Caseworker workload is observed at daily granularity; intra-day queuing and surge demand are invisible.
- Number and specialization of caseworkers are reported in summary (7 bilingual), but actual availability (part-time, turnover, sick leave) is not observed.
- Regional differences are mentioned ("backlog in two regions") but not detailed. Waiting times by region are not provided.
- Fraud-review queue depth is not reported. Group D's 28% routing to V3 may encounter highly variable waiting depending on queue saturation.

**Implication**: Waiting-time analysis (Section 3.1) identifies bottlenecks but cannot distinguish:
- Under-staffing (permanent resource shortage) from surge (temporary high demand).
- Quality-control waits (appropriate oversight) from administrative delays (poor workflow design).

## 5.4 Appeal quality and outcome

**Data gaps:**
- Appeal flag is binary (appeal filed or not), not reversal rate or appeal-stage approval rate.
- **Example**: If Group D files 16% appeals but 10% are reversed (appeal approved), net approval rate would be 58% + 10% = 68%. If appeals are reversed at 0% (process affirmed), approval stays 58%.
- **Implication**: Appeal rate alone does not indicate unfairness; must know appeal success rate.
- **Missing**: Explanation quality, applicant comprehension of decision, and dignity of treatment are not measured.

## 5.5 Historical and systemic bias

**Prior case count:**
- 49% of Group D has "high prior case count" vs. 12% of Group A.
- **Question**: Are repeat applicants denied unfairly (historical bias perpetuated), or are they legitimately higher-risk (e.g., seasonal workers, chronic conditions causing repeated need)?
- **Data gap**: No information on *reasons* for prior cases (eligibility change, reapplication after denial, seasonal recertification).

**Risk_score_band construction:**
- No formula disclosed. If the band includes applicant attributes correlated with group (e.g., age, employment sector, address), it may encode structural disadvantage as "risk."
- **Example**: If Group D has more gig workers or informal employment, their income volatility may inflate risk_score_band relative to their actual fraud risk.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

## 6.1 Increase capacity for language support and accommodation review

**Option:**
- Hire 4–6 additional bilingual caseworkers to reduce Group B's queue wait.
- Hire 2–3 additional accommodation specialists to reduce Group C's queue wait and eliminate the dual-role bottleneck.

**Expected effect:**
- Reduce Language Support Contact  Eligibility wait from 3.8 days to ~1.5 days.
- Reduce Accommodation Review  Eligibility wait from 4.7 days to ~2 days.
- Reduce Group B adjusted cycle time by ~2 days; Group C by ~3 days.
- May improve approval rates for Groups B and C if time pressure on caseworkers is reduced (fewer errors/dismissals due to backlog).

**Side effects:**
- **Cost**: Salary for 6–9 FTE (~$400k–$600k/year depending on region).
- **Recruitment difficulty**: Bilingual caseworkers are a scarce resource; hiring may require competing with other agencies or increasing pay.
- **Organizational change**: Expanding a specialized team may increase its internal status/silos, making hand-offs from general caseworkers less smooth.
- **Risk of displacement**: If new hires reduce the scarcity premium, existing bilingual/accommodation staff may reduce effort or leave.

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Design career pathways so bilingual or accommodation roles lead to advancement, reducing turnover.
- Cross-train general caseworkers in language support and accommodation basics to reduce handover dependency.

---

## 6.2 Establish service-level targets for support steps

**Option:**
- Set explicit timeliness targets: Language Support Contact  Eligibility should complete within 2 days; Accommodation Review  Eligibility within 2 days.
- Monitor compliance; escalate if missed.
- Publish targets in policy and communicate to applicants.

**Expected effect:**
- Operationalizes the "rights-preserving" language in policy by giving support steps priority.
- Focuses management attention on reducing wait times specifically for supported applicants.
- May incentivize cross-training and resource reallocation to meet targets.

**Side effects:**
- **Perverse incentive**: If targets are not accompanied by capacity expansion, caseworkers may rush language support or accommodation decisions, reducing quality.
- **Gamesmanship**: Cases may be reclassified as not needing support to avoid SLT; e.g., "accommodation not required" even if requested.
- **Monitoring cost**: Requires reliable data infrastructure (log system must record start/end times for support steps precisely).

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Tie SLT compliance to process quality audits: sample cases, check whether support was adequate (not just fast).
- Couple SLT with capacity targets: budget assumes staffing to meet SLTs.
- Audit reclassifications: monitor if cases labeled "accommodation not required" increase after SLT policy.

---

## 6.3 Redesign routing to reduce handovers

**Option:**
- **For Group B (language support)**: Instead of general  bilingual  general, assign bilingual caseworker as the primary owner end-to-end. Bilingual caseworker does all steps with support from general colleagues if needed.
- **For Group C (accommodation)**: Cross-train a few general caseworkers in accommodation assessment so that general caseworker can integrate accommodation into eligibility decision, rather than handing off to specialist then back.
- **For Group D (fraud review)**: Embed fraud reviewer in the main case team (not a separate queue) so fraud check happens in parallel with other steps, not sequentially.

**Expected effect:**
- Reduce handovers for Group B and C, improving continuity and reducing decision time.
- Integration of fraud review into main path (parallel, not sequential) may reduce Group D cycle time by ~2–3 days.
- May improve approval rates if decision-makers have full context and don't lose information in handoffs.

**Side effects:**
- **Skill dilution**: Generalizing caseworkers to do language support or accommodation assessment may reduce quality unless extensive training is provided.
- **Boundary friction**: Specialists may feel deskilled if their role is decentralized.
- **Fraud review risk**: If fraud checks are parallelized (not sequential), fraud risk may be underweighted or missed.

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Provide robust training and ongoing consultation opportunities (specialists available for complex questions).
- Design fraud-check workflow with clear decision points so it is not bypassed.
- Measure approval rate and fraud detection rate post-redesign; revert if fraud risk rises unacceptably.

---

## 6.4 Improve decision explanation and transparency

**Option:**
- Require that every decision notification includes:
  - Which activities were performed (verification, risk screening, eligibility assessment).
  - A plain-language explanation of the eligibility determination (criteria applied, evidence reviewed, reasoning).
  - If denied/incomplete, specific documents or information needed and why.
  - Appeal rights and process, with examples.
- Provide explanations in applicant's preferred language.

**Expected effect:**
- Reduce appeals by clarifying why a decision was made.
- Reduce rework by specifying missing documents clearly so applicants are not surprised.
- Build trust and legitimacy of process.

**Side effects:**
- **Workload increase**: Drafting explanations takes time; may slow case processing unless templates and automation are used.
- **Litigation risk**: Detailed explanations create a paper trail; if explanations contradict later judicial review, exposure increases.
- **Appeal rate may increase initially**: Better-informed applicants may appeal more, not less. (Long-term impact is ambiguous.)

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Use templated explanations keyed to decision type and eligibility criterion.
- Provide explanation-drafting training; monitor time/case.
- Accept that appeal rates may increase; measure appeal reversal rates. If reversals are high, that signals decisions were unjustified and explanations are beneficial even if appeals rise.

---

## 6.5 Monitor adjusted disparities and implement feedback loop

**Option:**
- Institutionalize the adjusted-comparison analysis (Section 2).
- Quarterly or semi-annually: rerun stratified analysis of approval rate, cycle time, supervisor review rate, and appeal rate by applicant group, controlling for case mix.
- Report results to management and board; set improvement targets.
- Investigate increases in adjusted disparities; implement corrective action.

**Expected effect:**
- Early warning system for drift in fairness.
- Accountability for outcomes beyond raw statistics.
- Evidence base for resource allocation and policy change.

**Side effects:**
- **Gaming**: If staff know disparities are monitored, they may manipulate case flow or risk classification to improve statistics without improving substance.
- **Data burden**: Requires caseworkers to enter detailed log data; incomplete or inaccurate logging undermines analysis.
- **Reputational risk**: Publishing disparities may trigger external criticism or legal action even if organization is improving.

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Couple monitoring with process transparency: publish not just disparities but root-cause analysis (e.g., "Group B has 2.6-day cycle-time gap; 2 days is due to language support queue, 0.6 days due to supervisor review delays").
- Use process monitoring (PM4Py analysis) not just outcome monitoring; ensure data quality with audits.
- Frame feedback loop as internal improvement, not public accountability initially; communicate progress to applicants and stakeholders to build trust.

---

## 6.6 Audit and reconstruct risk_score_band and documentation_completeness

**Option:**
- **Risk audit**: Document how risk_score_band is constructed. Review a sample of high-risk Group D cases to determine if the score is proportional to actual fraud indicators (vs. conflating need with risk).
- **Completeness audit**: Have bilingual caseworkers and accessibility specialists review a sample of cases marked "incomplete" to validate whether the assessment was unbiased. Retrain assessors if bias is detected.
- **Reconstruct**: If bias is found, rebuild risk_score_band and documentation_completeness measures using objective criteria (e.g., missing specific required documents, explicit fraud flags) rather than subjective judgment.

**Expected effect:**
- Remove measurement bias from control variables.
- Rerun adjusted analysis with corrected measures; if disparities shrink, measurement bias was the problem; if they persist, procedural bias is more likely.
- Improve quality of risk and completeness assessments for all applicants.

**Side effects:**
- **Effort-intensive**: Auditing and reconstructing measures is time-consuming and may delay other improvements.
- **Uncomfortable findings**: May reveal that staff have been biased; requires retraining and possible accountability measures.
- **Measure rigidity**: Objective measures are less flexible; may miss real complexity that subjective judgment captures. Balance needed.

**Mitigation of side effects:**
- Prioritize audit of the measures with the largest biased coefficients (risk_score_band seems most concerning given Group D disparities).
- Involve staff in reconstruction so they buy in to new measures.
- Use audit results for improvement, not punishment; frame as process improvement.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

## Summary of findings

The evidence indicates that the public-benefits case-management process exhibits **both legitimate and concerning procedural disparities** that warrant urgent mitigation, though some causal claims remain indeterminate.

### What the evidence supports

1. **Raw outcome disparities are not merely explained by case mix.**
   - Adjusted analysis controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month shows persistent disparities:
     - Group B: 5.5 pp approval gap, +2.6 day cycle-time gap.
     - Group C: 2.2 pp approval gap, +4.1 day cycle-time gap.
     - Group D: 3.1 pp approval gap (though case mix is most severe for this group).
   - These residual gaps suggest procedural factors beyond case complexity are operating.

2. **Rights-preserving support steps create measurable bottlenecks.**
   - Group B's Language Support Contact (V4) pathway experiences 3.8-day waiting time between support step and eligibility assessment; V4 accounts for 24% of Group B cases.
   - Group C's Accommodation Review (V5) pathway experiences 4.7-day waiting time; V5 accounts for 31% of Group C cases.
   - These bottlenecks are real operational delays, not artifacts of complexity, and they contribute substantially to the adjusted cycle-time gaps (+2.6 and +4.1 days).

3. **Resource concentration creates de facto discrimination.**
   - 7 bilingual caseworkers serve ~400 Group B cases needing language support (57 cases per caseworker).
   - 5 accommodation specialists serve ~510 Group C accommodation-requesting cases (102 cases per specialist), with 2 specialists also doing supervisor review (dual-role bottleneck).
   - Group A's 3600 cases are spread across 52 caseworkers (69 cases per caseworker).
   - The concentration is not animus-based, but the operational consequence is unequal timeliness. Applicants have no control over which group they are in, yet they suffer unequal processing due to resource allocation decisions.

4. **Handover burdens are higher for supported groups.**
   - Group B and C cases are routed through multiple caseworkers (general  specialist  general, or general  specialist  supervisor), introducing coordination costs and information loss.
   - Group A's 42% routing through V1 (one-caseworker path) avoids this burden.

5. **Elevated appeal and rework rates suggest decision quality or applicant trust issues.**
   - Groups B, C, D have 10–16% appeal rates vs. 6% for Group A (adjusted gaps: +4.4, +1.1, +5.7 pp).
   - Groups B, C, D have 20–24% rework rates vs. 11% for Group A.
   - Without appeal reversal data, the cause is indeterminate (flawed initial decisions, or applicant lack of resources to appeal only when justified), but the disparities warrant explanation.

6. **Supervisor Review is applied disproportionately after controlling for risk.**
   - Adjusted probability of Supervisor Review is +8.0 pp for Group B, +5.5 pp for Group C, even controlling for risk_score_band.
   - This suggests supervisor review is not purely risk-based but may reflect organizational discretion or bias in escalation thresholds.

### What the evidence does not support

1. **"The process is simply fair because groups differ in risk."**
   - Case mix explains much of the raw disparity, but not all. The residual gaps (5.5 pp approval for Group B even after controlling for risk and documentation) remain material.
   - Even if fair in substance, under-resourcing of support steps creates unfair procedural burdens.

2. **"Outcomes are biased because decision-makers discriminate."**
   - The data do not identify individual caseworker or manager bias. Disparities could arise from systemic under-resourcing, workflow design (sequential vs. integrated support), or covariate measurement bias (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness).
   - Procedural mechanism is more likely than animus.

3. **"Appeal rates are evidence of unfair decisions."**
   - Appeal rates alone do not indicate unfairness. If Group D's 16% appeal rate leads to 14% approval-on-appeal (net effect: 58% + 14% = 72%), appeals are working. Without reversal data, the rate is ambiguous.

### What remains indeterminate

1. **Whether risk_score_band is measurement-biased.**
   - risk_score_band likely includes prior-case history, which can perpetuate historical disparities. Audit of the measure (Section 6.6) is needed to determine if it is biased.

2. **Whether documentation_completeness assessments are linguistically biased.**
   - Group B's 46% "incomplete" share may reflect language barriers in form completion or assessment, not actual missing information. Bilingual review of marked-incomplete cases would clarify.

3. **Whether approval disparities would shrink if bottlenecks were relieved.**
   - If Language Support Contact queuing is removed (capacity expansion), would Group B approval rates improve? Not necessarily—the cause of the lower approval rate could be decision substantive (eligibility criteria), not procedural (timeliness/access). Removing the bottleneck may reduce cycle time without improving approval.

4. **Whether appeal reversals favor Groups B, C, D (indicating initial bias) or track approval rates (indicating selection effect).**
   - Data not available.

5. **Whether satisfaction, dignity, and comprehension of decisions differ by group.**
   - Outcome data cannot measure fairness in the applicant experience.

## Procedural fairness verdict

**The process appears procedurally unfair in design, though not clearly biased in decision-making.**

### Design unfairness

- **Under-resourcing of support steps**: The organization has acknowledged that language support and accommodation review are "rights-preserving" steps, but has not resourced them adequately (7 bilingual caseworkers for 57% of Group B applicants; 5 specialists for 62% of Group C applicants). This creates bottlenecks that delay applicants who exercise legal rights.
- **Sequential vs. integrated support**: Accommodation review is imposed as a prerequisite step before eligibility assessment (4.7-day gap), not as an integrated accommodation during eligibility. This burdens applicants exercising a right with extra delay.
- **Unequal resource allocation**: Group A caseworkers carry 69 cases; Group B and C caseworkers carry 100–160 cases, with role concentration preventing redistribution. Applicants bear the cost of this allocation choice without representation.

### Substantive decision-making ambiguity

- **Adjusted approval gaps persist** (Group B 5.5 pp, Group C 2.2 pp) even controlling for documented complexity. This could reflect:
  - Legitimate unobserved complexity in supported cases (not captured by risk_score_band or documentation_completeness).
  - Measurement bias in control variables (risk_score_band inflating Group D risk; documentation_completeness conflating language barriers with deficiency).
  - Biased decision-making (caseworkers approve at lower rates for supported groups, controlling for facts).
  - **Current evidence does not distinguish these causes**, so a definitive claim of substantive bias is not warranted, but the possibility is real.

### Recommended verdict language

> **"The process exhibits procedural unfairness in resource allocation and workflow design that disproportionately burdens Groups B and C, even if individual case decisions are fair. Specifically, under-resourcing of language support and accommodation review creates systemic delays (bottlenecks of 3.8 and 4.7 days) that Groups A applicants do not experience. Sequential placement of support steps adds procedural burden. These are design fairness issues, not necessarily decision bias.**
> 
> **Adjusted approval-rate disparities (5.5 pp for Group B, 2.2 pp for Group C) persist even after controlling for case complexity, suggesting either unobserved complexity, measurement bias in control variables (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness), or substantive decision bias. Current data do not conclusively distinguish these; further analysis of control-variable bias, appeal reversals, and subgroup-stratified decisions is needed to assess substantive fairness.**
> 
> **Recommended immediate actions:**
> - **Expand capacity** for language support and accommodation review to meet demand and reduce bottleneck waiting times to <2 days.
> - **Integrate support steps** into eligibility assessment (parallel, not sequential) to reduce burden.
> - **Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness** for measurement bias; reconstruct with objective criteria if bias is found.
> - **Publish appeal reversal rates** and decision explanations by group to support transparency and fairness assessment.
> - **Establish service-level targets** for support steps to operationalize "rights-preserving" policy.
> - **Monitor adjusted disparities** quarterly; implement corrective action if gaps widen."

---

This assessment avoids overstating causal claims while highlighting both clear procedural unfairness (bottlenecks, under-resourcing) and actionable uncertainties (measurement bias, decision bias) that warrant investigation and mitigation.
