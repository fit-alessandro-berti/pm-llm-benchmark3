# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

The board should separate four uses that impose different costs for mistakes:

- **Exploration:** find recurring patterns and generate hypotheses. A broad model can be useful, but its admitted traces are leads for review, not evidence of policy compliance.
- **Conformance enforcement:** decide whether a case satisfies policy. False acceptance can bypass controls; false rejection can block legitimate work. Mandatory rules and attribute-dependent rules need explicit, testable treatment.
- **Simulation:** estimate possible paths, workload, or delay. A model needs realistic frequencies, concurrency, and routing probabilities; neither reported model supplies those.
- **Stakeholder communication:** explain the process and its exceptions. A model needs to be legible and stable enough for people to inspect and maintain.

For exploration, high fitness and coverage may be useful even at lower precision. For enforcement, neither fitness nor precision on positive traces is enough: policy probes, negative traces, data conditions, and uncertainty handling matter more. Simulation additionally needs calibrated behavior frequencies. Stakeholder comprehension is evidence about usability only when measured with a defined protocol.

Assumptions used below:

1. A trace is a linear event sequence. Process-tree parallel branches can interleave in any order.
2. The listed activities are the scope of this review. Resource, timing, and authorization rules were not supplied, so neither model can be judged on them.
3. The process tree in the third alternative is a structural envelope. A separate policy check must decide the external/internal supplier condition and the affected-check condition. Missing required attributes produce an indeterminate result, never an automatic pass.
4. For a deployable third model, assume at most one Supplier Review and one Expedite Review per case. The policy does not state repeat counts for those activities, so owners must confirm this boundary.
5. P6 places corrections after the first mandatory checks and before approval. If the business permits correction at other points, that placement must be revised.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

**Strongest case for S.** It is compact, readable, quick to align, and admits combinations of the listed one-off activities without enumerating every ordering. That can be useful as a rough discovery envelope when missing events and future combinations are expected. Its training and holdout fitness are high, and the reported comprehension score is better than C's. It also enforces Register first, Release last, and exactly one of the two approvals.

**Strongest case against S.** Its permissiveness is not limited to tolerating noisy or missing observations. It changes policy semantics by making every check optional and by allowing Expedite Review before the mandatory checks. Missing-event tolerance is not a valid reason to encode mandatory checks as optional in an enforcement model. An event absent from the log should be handled as uncertain evidence or data quality, not silently erased from policy.

Concrete undesirable or ambiguous admitted traces include:

1. Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order — skips both mandatory checks.
2. Register Dossier, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order — skips Technical Review.
3. Register Dossier, Technical Review, Auto Approval, Release Order — skips Credit Check.
4. Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order — Expedite precedes both checks.
5. Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order — skips both checks and accepts Supplier Review without knowing whether the order is external.
6. Register Dossier, Credit Check, Supplier Review, Technical Review, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order — permits Supplier Review before the second mandatory check.
7. Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order — skips both mandatory checks while treating Supplier Review as arbitrary optional behavior.

These are consequences of the expression: after Register, four parallel branches independently choose their activity or tau. Thus Credit, Technical, Supplier, and Expedite may each be omitted or occur in any interleaving order. The approval XOR correctly prevents both approvals in one trace, and the sequence puts Release last.

Some permissiveness may be intentional for exploration, such as tolerating an absent Supplier Review when the case's sourcing attribute is unknown. The mandatory-check omissions and Expedite ordering flaw are semantic errors for policy conformance, not merely noise tolerance. S also contains no Correct Dossier path, so it cannot represent the stated correction behavior.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

**Strongest case for C.** Every enumerated branch contains Register, both mandatory checks before approval, exactly one approval, and Release last. It does not permit the obvious B3 or B4 violations. The exact branch language is auditable as a finite list of known patterns and may serve as a regression fixture for those patterns. Its high training precision is consistent with a narrow model.

**Strongest case against C.** It represents eight complete sequences rather than reusable policy rules. A new legal combination is rejected merely because the combination was not enumerated. It is missing, among others, the alternate check order with Auto Approval, Supplier Review with Auto Approval, Expedite followed by Supplier Review, and combinations of Supplier Review, Expedite, and Auto Approval. F4 describes precisely this gap. Whether unseen behavior is allowed should be determined by policy and attributes, not by whether it happened to appear in a training variant.

C's correction branch models one correction followed by both Credit Check and Technical Review. It cannot transparently represent a correction affecting Technical Review alone or Credit Check alone, repeated correction cycles, or a valid correction trace combined with Supplier Review or Expedite Review. This conflicts with F6 and P6. The branch also has no visible representation of the missing affected-check attribute.

The 0.941 training precision may be an artifact of memorizing the positive training variants: many deviations from those sequences are excluded by construction. It does not show that the excluded paths are policy violations, especially given F5's lack of curated negatives. Training fitness of 0.973 alongside holdout fitness of 0.744 and token generalization of 0.412 is consistent with a model that fits listed variants but covers little beyond them. It does not prove overfitting without the split and settings, but it makes that explanation plausible.

Branch enumeration also multiplies repeated labels across alternatives, complicating transition ownership, impact analysis, and updates. The duplicate count of 33 is too poorly defined to quantify this precisely, but the expression itself clearly reuses visible activity labels across branches. The reported low simplicity and runtime are consistent with a larger alignment search space; they are not independent proof of the cause. C can be a useful catalog of observed traces, but the phrase “audit-ready” overstates what the model establishes.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by likely impact on the governance decision, subject to the missing experimental details:

1. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior.** S can skip all four optional branches, which naturally helps replay when events are missing. This makes its very high fitness misleading for policy correctness. Missing observations should be surfaced as uncertainty, not converted into optional policy.
2. **Missing-event bias and unobserved attributes.** Supplier Review's condition is unavailable in the discovery log. A log-only method cannot distinguish a legitimate internal case without Supplier Review from an external case with a missing Supplier Review. The same issue arises for affected checks after correction. Metrics on this log cannot resolve the policy.
3. **Split sensitivity and possible leakage.** An 80/20 split is not reproducible without seed, exact case IDs, split unit, preprocessing, or time/entity grouping. Correlated cases or duplicated variants across partitions can inflate holdout results; rare combinations can make them unstable.
4. **Positive-log-only precision.** Precision against observed positive traces says little about whether prohibited behavior is accepted. It cannot support F5's compliance claim without curated negative traces such as B3, B4, and B8.
5. **Overfitting versus over-permissive generalization.** C's low holdout/generalization scores fit an undercoverage story. S's high generalization score may reward permissiveness. Neither metric distinguishes a valid unseen combination from an invalid one.
6. **Alignment-cost sensitivity.** Substitution, insertion, deletion, and silent-move costs change the optimal alignment and fitness/precision interpretation. Missing-event penalties are especially consequential here. No costs or settings are supplied.
7. **Duplicate-label effects.** C repeats activity labels across branches. Depending on conversion and metric implementation, duplicate visible transitions can affect precision, alignment choices, simplicity, and diagnostic attribution. The undocumented count cannot be compared or interpreted safely.
8. **Silent-transition effects.** S's tau alternatives encode skipping as silent paths, which are central to its high replay tolerance. Silent routing introduced during process-tree-to-net conversion can also affect alignment search. The number and treatment of silent transitions were not reported.
9. **Runtime confounding by model size.** C's higher runtime is plausible given branch expansion and duplicate labels, but case length, hardware, caching, and settings can explain part of the gap. A single median without a controlled benchmark is not a causal comparison.
10. **Stakeholder-score methodology.** The 4.4 versus 1.9 result is an important usability warning, but an informal score cannot separate actual comprehension from familiarity, training, role mix, or task design.

These rankings describe likely decision impact, not proven causes. Re-running the experiment with a published protocol could reorder several of them.

# 7. Third Alternative

## Formal description

Use a **procedural structural envelope plus an attribute-aware policy validator**. The process-tree backbone is:

Register → (Credit Check ∥ Technical Review) → (optional Supplier Review ∥ optional Expedite Review ∥ zero-or-more correction cycles) → XOR(Manager Approval, Auto Approval) → Release

The correction cycle is Correct Dossier followed by an optional Credit Check and an optional Technical Review. The structural envelope makes the first two checks mandatory and permits only one of each check per correction cycle. The policy validator requires the exact recheck set declared for that correction. It also conditions Supplier Review on the external/internal attribute. Expedite is after both initial checks and before approval; it may interleave before or after Supplier Review. Approval is exclusive.

The parser's tau choices in the correction cycle are only a structural way to represent varying affected-check subsets. They do not make initial mandatory checks optional, and they do not authorize a correction cycle to pass policy unless its metadata and observed rechecks agree.

## PM4Py model

~~~python
import pm4py

model_third = pm4py.parse_process_tree("""
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  +('Credit Check', 'Technical Review'),
  +(
    X('Supplier Review', tau),
    X('Expedite Review', tau),
    *(tau,
      ->(
        'Correct Dossier',
        +(
          X('Credit Check', tau),
          X('Technical Review', tau)
        )
      ),
      tau
    )
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
""")
~~~

The loop is a standard process-tree do/redo/exit loop with silent do and exit branches, giving zero or more correction cycles. The parallel optional Supplier and Expedite branches and correction loop can interleave after the initial checks. This deliberately allows correction before, between, or after those two optional reviews, subject to the stated requirement that all precede approval.

## Required external checks

Required case/event data:

- Case attribute **external_order**: true or false. Unknown means supplier-policy status is indeterminate.
- Each Correct Dossier event has **affected_checks**, a set drawn from Credit Check and Technical Review. Unknown means correction-policy status is indeterminate.
- A correction's affected-check declaration is authoritative only if sourced from the business correction record and validated for completeness.

Policy decisions are conjunctive: the structural model must fit, and all applicable external checks must pass. In pseudocode:

~~~python
def policy_check(events, external_order):
    names = [event["concept:name"] for event in events]
    approvals = [
        name for name in names
        if name in {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"}
    ]

    if not names or names[0] != "Register Dossier":
        return "FAIL"
    if names[-1] != "Release Order":
        return "FAIL"
    if names.count("Register Dossier") != 1 or names.count("Release Order") != 1:
        return "FAIL"
    if len(approvals) != 1:
        return "FAIL"

    approval_index = names.index(approvals[0])
    if names.index("Credit Check") > approval_index:
        return "FAIL"
    if names.index("Technical Review") > approval_index:
        return "FAIL"

    if external_order is None:
        return "INDETERMINATE"
    supplier_count = names.count("Supplier Review")
    if external_order and supplier_count != 1:
        return "FAIL"
    if not external_order and supplier_count != 0:
        return "FAIL"

    if names.count("Expedite Review") > 1:
        return "FAIL"
    if "Expedite Review" in names:
        expedite_index = names.index("Expedite Review")
        if not (
            names.index("Credit Check") < expedite_index < approval_index
            and names.index("Technical Review") < expedite_index < approval_index
        ):
            return "FAIL"

    # The process-tree envelope places rechecks only in a cycle after Correct.
    # For each correction, compare check events up to the next correction or
    # approval with that correction event's declared affected_checks.
    correction_indexes = [
        i for i, name in enumerate(names) if name == "Correct Dossier"
    ]
    for j, correction_index in enumerate(correction_indexes):
        correction = events[correction_index]
        expected = correction.get("affected_checks")
        if expected is None:
            return "INDETERMINATE"
        if not set(expected) <= {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}:
            return "FAIL"

        next_corrections = [
            i for i in correction_indexes[j + 1:]
            if i > correction_index
        ]
        end = next_corrections[0] if next_corrections else approval_index
        observed = [
            names[i] for i in range(correction_index + 1, end)
            if names[i] in {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}
        ]
        if sorted(observed) != sorted(expected):
            return "FAIL"

    return "PASS"
~~~

This policy checker is a compact skeleton: production code should also return reason codes and event indices, validate event ordering through the Petri-net alignment, and verify that the affected_checks value is linked to the correct correction record. A repeated check without a preceding correction is structurally rejected; within a correction segment, only the declared checks may repeat. Missing attributes return INDETERMINATE, which blocks an enforcement pass pending data repair or an authorized manual review.

**Limitations.** The control-flow discovery data lacks external_order, so historical Supplier Review decisions cannot be fully validated. It also lacks affected_checks, so historical correction cycles cannot be resolved as valid or invalid from the trace alone. Collect both attributes before deployment. Do not impute either from the observed activity sequence. If Supplier Review or Expedite Review can legitimately repeat, revise the explicitly stated single-occurrence assumption.

# 9. Validation Experiments

Run an evaluation whose inputs, policy, and outputs can be reproduced.

1. **Freeze data preparation.** Publish the event classifier, lifecycle filtering, timestamp sorting and tie-breaking, case construction, duplicate-event handling, and treatment of incomplete cases. Preserve raw event labels and attributes so transformations can be audited.
2. **Make splits reproducible.** Split by case with a fixed seed and publish case IDs or a cryptographic manifest. Also report repeated seeded splits and a temporal holdout. Group by customer/order/source entity if related cases could leak across partitions.
3. **Stratify holdouts.** Report separate results for internal/external orders, approval type, check order, Supplier/Expedite combinations, and correction scopes. Hold out combinations, not just random cases, to test compositional generalization.
4. **Curate positive and negative suites with policy owners.** Include B1–B8, the three correction traces above, missing attributes, and traces violating each P1–P7 rule. Tag policy-dependent cases as indeterminate until their required attributes are supplied.
5. **Perturb events.** Delete one mandatory event, Supplier Review, or Expedite Review; duplicate each mandatory check with and without a preceding correction; insert duplicate approvals and misplaced events. Measure false acceptance, false rejection, and indeterminate rates separately.
6. **Test unseen combinations.** Hold out combinations such as alternate check order + Supplier + Expedite + Auto Approval. Evaluate the policy validator's decision against owner adjudication. Do not label “unseen” as “invalid” by default.
7. **Sweep alignment costs.** Fix a baseline and publish alternative insertion, deletion, substitution, and silent-move costs. Report rank/order changes in decisions, not only a single score.
8. **Check net properties.** Convert each model and check soundness, reachability, dead transitions, and completion for each required approval path. Inspect duplicate visible labels and silent transitions in the converted net.
9. **Report metrics separately.** For each split and stratum report alignment fitness, alignment precision, token-based generalization, arc-degree simplicity, runtime distribution, negative-suite acceptance, and policy-check outcomes. Do not collapse these into a weighted score.
10. **Measure comprehension.** Recruit role-stratified Compliance, Operations, IT, and Audit participants. Give all models the same short orientation; then randomize scenarios and ask each person to label traces allowed/forbidden/indeterminate and explain why, plus perform one change-impact task. Record accuracy, time, confidence, and a fixed 1–5 ease rating. Publish prompts, rubric, participant counts, and role mix.
11. **Pre-register rejection gates.** Reject a compliance candidate if it accepts any owner-confirmed prohibited negative, admits an approval without both checks, treats unknown required attributes as pass, or has unsound/unreachable required paths, regardless of aggregate fitness. Set false-rejection and indeterminate-rate limits with process owners before deployment.

PM4Py metric and conversion skeleton:

~~~python
import pm4py

def metric_bundle(log, model):
    net, im, fm = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model)
    soundness = pm4py.check_soundness(

    return {

        "fitness": pm4py.fitness_alignments(log, net, im, fm),

            net, im, fm, variant="arc_degree"
        ),
        "diagnostics": pm4py.conformance_diagnostics_alignments(
~~~

# 10. Governance Recommendation

- **Exploration:** S may be retained as a clearly labeled, non-enforcing over-approximation for finding candidate sequences. Every result should be annotated that mandatory checks are optional in the model. Do not use it for simulation or business decisions without replacing those semantics. C can be retained as a list of exact historical patterns or regression fixtures, not as the general process policy.
- **Compliance:** Neither original is fit for enforcement. S admits direct violations; C falsely rejects plausible combinations and cannot express the conditional Supplier and correction rules.
- **Retirement:** Retire both original models as authoritative policy models. Preserve their files and reported results as experiment artifacts, with the claims narrowed to what they measure.
- **Third alternative:** Treat it as a candidate design, not yet deployable. Before deployment, obtain and validate external_order and correction affected_checks; settle repeat rules and correction placement; pass soundness/reachability checks; pass the curated negative suite with zero confirmed prohibited acceptance; meet a pre-agreed false-rejection and indeterminate-rate threshold; demonstrate stable results across seeded and attribute-stratified holdouts and alignment-cost sweeps; and pass the documented stakeholder comprehension/change-impact review. Unknown attributes must route to an explicit pending/manual-review state, not a compliant result.

# 11. Dissenting View

The board could reasonably reject this recommendation as too conservative and operationally expensive. S has excellent replay fitness on both training and holdout, the best reported generalization and simplicity, the fastest alignment time, and the strongest comprehension score. In a process where source data are incomplete, a permissive model may be the least misleading representation of what actually happens. C, by contrast, has high precision on observed training behavior and could support a strict interim policy: unlisted behavior is stopped until an owner approves it. That approach creates a clear audit boundary, even if it blocks some valid cases. The third design adds a policy engine, mandatory attributes, and an indeterminate state, increasing integration work and creating a new source of implementation defects. Until that engine is implemented and independently checked, C's narrowness might be safer than an unvalidated hybrid. The metric gaps may also be artifacts of an unlucky holdout rather than structural failures. If operational teams can manually handle exceptions, the cost of false rejection may be lower than the cost of an undocumented false acceptance. Keeping both originals available during a staged rollout could preserve evidence while the board learns which errors matter in practice.

I would change the decision if a reproducible, policy-adjudicated evaluation showed that S's apparent false acceptance is negligible under realistic missing-event patterns, or that C's rejected combinations are in fact prohibited by approved policy. I would also reconsider the third model if attribute completeness is unattainable or if its policy validator cannot be independently verified, tested, and monitored. Conversely, stable stratified tests, complete attributes, and successful negative-suite results would strengthen the third alternative's deployment case.
