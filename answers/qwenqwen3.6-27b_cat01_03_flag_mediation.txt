| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P1 vs P0 | Cases arriving in similar congestion windows | Triage completion time | Interventional direct effect (IDE) on triage | Yes | Time-varying confounding by queue state |

| E6 | P3 vs P0 | Flagged cases | Total cycle time | Interventional effect with protected capacity | Yes | Spillover to unflagged queue not isolated |
| E7 | P1 vs P0 | All cases | Decision outcome / error rate | Total effect on quality | No | Outcome depends on unobserved true risk |

Framework: I use **Interventional Direct/Indirect Effects (IDE/IEE)** rather than natural effects. Natural direct/indirect effects require cross-world counterfactuals and fail here because (1) the flag induces exposure-induced confounding (manual review changes queue length, which confounds the reviewcycle path), and (2) shared queues violate SUTVA, making individual-level natural effects ill-defined. IDE/IEE randomizes mediators independently of treatment, yielding well-defined estimands even under interference and feedback.

Interference arises because manual-review and escalation queues are shared; one case’s flag-induced routing changes wait times for others. Exposure-induced confounding occurs because the flag increases manual-review probability, which lengthens the review queue; this queue length then confounds the association between manual review and downstream cycle time. Treatment-confounder feedback appears when complexity and reviewer judgments (influenced by the flag) alter subsequent queue states and escalation decisions, violating static causal graph assumptions and requiring longitudinal or g-method approaches.

| Is the flag capturing unobserved risk or creating artifacts? | Negative-control outcome/exposure | Use `triage_resource` ID or `arrival_load` as negative controls | Negative controls unaffected by flag except through measured pathways | Regress negative control on flag; expect zero coefficient | Significant association implies omitted variable bias or data leakage |
| How sensitive are results to hidden risk? | Sensitivity analysis for unobserved risk | E-value or Rosenbaum bounds on `U_hidden_risk` | Bounded influence of unmeasured confounder on treatment and outcome | Compute minimum correlation needed to nullify flag effect | Low threshold (<0.2) suggests conclusions are fragile |
| Does interference invalidate individual-level estimates? | Interference-aware network/queue modeling | Model queue length as state variable; use group-level estimands | Interference limited to queue sharing; Markovian arrivals | Simulate queue dynamics; compare observed vs simulated waits | Large mismatch implies complex interference (e.g., reviewer batching, priority preemption) |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 (current) | Fastest triage | High manual/escalation congestion | Longest overall | Low (thorough review) | High (shared queues bottleneck) | High (observed) | Baseline |
| P1 (suppress flag) | Slower triage | Reduced manual queue length | Shorter for low-risk, longer for high-risk | Moderate (missed high-risk) | Low (queue stabilizes) | Medium (observational proxy) | Not recommended alone |
| P2 (remove auto review) | Unchanged | Lower manual queue, higher triage-to-close direct | Shorter median, longer tail for true high-risk | High (compliance gaps) | Low | Medium | Pilot for routing calibration |
| P3 (protected review capacity) | Unchanged | Isolated manual queue, reduced interference | Slightly shorter flagged, stable unflagged | Low | Moderate (dedicated lane may idle) | Low (requires capacity change) | Consider if compliance critical |
| P4 (change escalation criteria) | Unchanged | Reduced escalation queue, faster close | Shorter for escalated cases | Moderate (if criteria too loose) | Low | Medium | Pair with P2 for efficiency |

**Pilot design (P2 variant):** Eligibility: Flagged cases with `risk_score  [0.60, 0.80]` and `complexity=Medium`. Assignment: 50% randomized to standard triage routing (no automatic manual review); 50% remain on current policy. Guardrail: Manual review queue length must not exceed 12 cases; if exceeded, pause randomization. Outcomes: Primary: total cycle time; Secondary: manual-review wait, escalation rate, outcome accuracy (audit sample). Data needed: Full timestamps, queue lengths at each stage, reviewer IDs, audit flags. Stopping: Early stop if flagged control group shows >15% increase in adverse outcomes or if queue guardrail breaches >3 times in 2 weeks.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk dominates routing:** If `U_hidden_risk` strongly predicts both flagging and downstream delays, the flag’s apparent effect is spurious; suppressing it would not shorten cycles but would increase adverse outcomes.
2. **Queue interference is asymmetric:** If flagged cases displace unflagged ones into faster parallel lanes (not observed), the current system may already be optimizing total throughput; protecting flagged capacity (P3) would worsen overall performance.
3. **Decision quality degrades without review:** If manual review catches >30% of critical errors that triage misses, removing automatic review (P2) would reduce cycle time but violate compliance thresholds, reversing the recommendation.
4. **Timestamp semantics reflect batching:** If `manual_start` timestamps capture reviewer shift-starts rather than actual case pickup, observed waits are administrative artifacts; queue interventions would have no real effect.
5. **Positivity violation at threshold:** If no unflagged cases exist near `risk_score=0.70`, local-threshold designs fail; causal claims about the flag’s direct effect become untestable without external data.
6. **Reviewer behavior adapts to flag suppression:** If hiding the flag causes reviewers to overcompensate by manually flagging borderline cases, P1 would replicate current routing and delays, nullifying expected gains.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed: Flagged cases experience ~2-hour faster triage initiation and ~0.3-hour shorter triage service, yet total cycle time doubles. Manual-review waits are 3–4× longer for flagged cases, and escalation rates are higher. Complexity and `arrival_load` correlate strongly with both flagging and downstream routing. Plausible mechanism: The flag triggers a policy bundle (priority triage + automatic review) that accelerates early stages but funnels cases into a shared, capacity-constrained review queue. Exposure-induced confounding arises because review routing changes queue length, which then confounds the reviewcycle path. Unobserved risk and complexity further entangle treatment and outcome. What remains unidentified: The separable effect of the flag versus underlying risk, the exact interference structure of the shared queue, and whether extended review improves decision quality enough to justify delays. The most useful next evidence comes from a randomized encouragement pilot (P2 variant) that decouples the flag from automatic review while preserving priority triage, paired with real-time queue monitoring. This isolates the routing mediation, tests compliance tradeoffs, and generates interventional estimates robust to confounding and interference.
