# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this context extends beyond equal approval rates and requires evaluating how the process treats applicants across multiple dimensions:

- **Outcome parity**: Raw approval rates range from 58% (Group D) to 78% (Group A). These differences are expected given substantial case-mix variation and should not be interpreted as discrimination in isolation.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity**: After controlling for risk, documentation, channel, household size, prior cases, region, and month, Groups B, C, and D still face lower approval probabilities (5.5, 2.2, 3.1 pp) and longer cycle times (+2.6, +4.1, +0.9 days). This residual gap indicates that documented complexity does not fully explain the differences.
- **Procedural burden**: Rights-preserving steps (language support, accommodation review) and risk-based steps (fraud review) introduce additional interactions, handovers, and documentation requests. Groups B and C experience significantly more handovers and rework, increasing cognitive and administrative load.
- **Timeliness**: Waiting times at specialized steps are structurally asymmetric. Language support adds a 3.8-day median wait; accommodation review adds 4.7 days. These delays are not captured by standard service-level targets and disproportionately affect vulnerable applicants.
- **Consistency of routing**: Variant distribution shows highly divergent paths. Group B relies heavily on V4 (language path), Group C on V5 (accommodation path), and Group D on V3 (fraud path). While routing follows policy, the structural asymmetry creates predictable procedural penalties for groups exercising rights or flagged by risk models.
- **Resource-allocation fairness**: Specialized pools (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists) are disproportionately loaded, while general caseworkers handle the majority of Group A cases. This creates workload inequity and bottleneck risk, potentially degrading service quality for supported groups.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Capacity mismatch in specialized queues**: Legitimate demand for language/accommodation support exceeds specialist capacity, creating artificial delays. This is a resource allocation issue, not a case complexity issue.
2. **Risk score feedback loop**: `risk_score_band` routes cases to fraud review, increasing scrutiny and cycle time. If the risk model encodes historical disparities or proxies for language/disability status, it creates a self-reinforcing procedural penalty that appears legitimate but may be biased.
3. **Documentation completeness as a proxy for support needs**: Incomplete documentation triggers `Request Missing Documents`. If language barriers or disability-related challenges cause incompleteness, applicants face a double penalty: rework steps plus specialized-step waits. This compounds procedural burden.
4. **Supervisor review escalation bias**: Groups B and C show higher probabilities of supervisor review (+8.0pp, +5.5pp). This may reflect legitimate complexity, but could also indicate implicit bias, stricter scrutiny for vulnerable groups, or inconsistent first-line decision quality, leading to higher appeal rates.
5. **Channel and regional operational constraints**: In-person applications (more common in Group B) inherently require scheduling and physical handoffs. Regional backlogs affect `Supervisor Review  Decision Draft` waits. These are legitimate operational factors but exacerbate existing disparities.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias**: `documentation_completeness` may reflect applicant literacy, digital access, or language proficiency rather than actual eligibility. `risk_score_band` may encode historical decision patterns, proxy variables, or algorithmic bias, making it unsuitable as a neutral control.
- **Missing applicant needs**: The log lacks granular data on actual language proficiency, disability severity, digital literacy, or socioeconomic constraints. Binary flags (`disability_accommodation_requested`, `language_preference`) cannot capture the spectrum of support needs.
- **Unobserved resource workload**: Daily granularity masks intra-day bottlenecks, concurrent case loads, specialist burnout, or queue prioritization rules. Handover counts do not capture coordination time or information loss during transitions.
- **Appeal quality**: `appeal_flag` and `case_outcome` do not indicate appeal success rates, grounds for appeal, or whether appeals correct procedural errors (e.g., poor explanation, missed accommodation) vs. substantive eligibility disputes. High appeal rates may signal process failures rather than applicant dissatisfaction.
- **Historical bias in risk scores**: Risk models trained on past approvals/denials may perpetuate systemic disparities. Using `risk_score_band` as a control in adjusted models may mask unfairness rather than explain it, as the score itself may be a downstream effect of biased routing or documentation practices.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review**: Hire or cross-train additional specialists to reduce queue waits. *Side effect*: Higher staffing costs; rapid hiring may dilute expertise; requires careful onboarding to maintain quality.
- **Service-level targets for support steps**: Introduce explicit timeliness thresholds for language/accommodation reviews. *Side effect*: May pressure staff to rush complex reviews, increasing rework or appeal rates; requires robust monitoring to prevent metric gaming.
- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**: Embed bilingual/accommodation competencies in general caseworkers or use parallel processing instead of sequential handoffs. *Side effect*: Training costs; potential quality trade-offs if specialists are removed from critical reviews; requires process re-engineering.
- **Decision-explanation improvements**: Standardize clear, accessible, multilingual explanations with explicit reasoning and next steps. *Side effect*: Increases drafting time initially; requires translation compliance and quality assurance; may temporarily slow notifications.
- **Monitoring adjusted disparities**: Implement dashboards tracking post-control cycle times, appeal rates, and handover frequency by group. *Side effect*: May create perverse incentives if staff are evaluated on adjusted metrics without addressing root causes; requires transparent reporting to avoid metric manipulation.
- **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**: Validate models for bias, recalibrate thresholds, and decouple documentation requests from penalty routing. *Side effect*: May temporarily reduce fraud detection accuracy; requires legal/policy review to adjust routing rules; needs stakeholder alignment to maintain trust.
