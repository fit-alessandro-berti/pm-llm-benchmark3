# 1. Executive memo

**To:** COO and Executive Committee
**From:** Process Excellence / Process Mining
**Decision requested:** Approve **E (segment-gated deployment), executed together with D (controlled regional experiment)**. Reject A (national scale) and B (full rollback). Hold C as the fallback if intake risk-classification proves unreliable.

**Bottom line.** R is not one intervention; it is five. Two components — coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule for simple houses — remove *waiting* and appear to work. Two — predictive survey skipping and activation checks after billing start — remove *information and controls* and are the most plausible source of the worsened tail, rework and complaints. The fifth, fewer handovers, bought accountability by consuming the slack that used to absorb exceptions. Scaling or reverting the bundle destroys value in both directions. **Unbundle it.**

**What we can defend today.** Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot) improve on every dimension that matters: median 6 days, p90 8 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints 1pp. Apartments and permit-sensitive old buildings (47% of pilot) deteriorate sharply: p90 +12 and +25 days, first-time-right 8pp and 11pp. A segment-weighted reconstruction of the pilot deltas reproduces most of the aggregate movement, so this is **within-segment damage, not just an unfavourable case mix** — though the pilot regions were deliberately harder than the national average.

**What we cannot yet defend.** The economics. Visible cost fell EUR 23 (184161), but the all-in estimate rose EUR 9 (198207). Arithmetically, R wins only if true 60-day downstream cost is below **EUR 37/case** versus the EUR 46 currently estimated — i.e. if our estimate overstates by ~20%. That is entirely possible: downstream cost is unmeasured after week 4, complaint windows are immature for recent cohorts, and a six-week window truncates long cases more than the ten-week baseline did. **Most measurement defects flatter R.** We should not scale on numbers whose known biases point in the convenient direction.

**The control issue is separate from the cost issue.** Alignments show frequent *Billing start before successful Activation test* and *Permit check not observed* for older buildings. Some of this may be a logging artefact — we hold completion timestamps only, and "not observed" is not "not performed". But billing a customer before a verified working service is a revenue-assurance and conduct exposure we should not carry while we investigate. Block it in the order system now; verify the exposure in parallel.

**The trade-off, stated plainly.** R converts cheap planned work (survey, permit check, pre-billing test) into expensive unplanned work (failed visit, second visit, manual activation, complaint after billing). For predictable addresses that trade is profitable. For uncertain addresses it is not, and the cost lands outside the pilot's measurement boundary and on the customer. Meanwhile coordinator utilisation at 93% and second-visit utilisation at 92% mean the tail is partly a queueing problem we created — fixable with capacity, not with process reversal.

**Immediate actions (weeks 0–2).**
1. Hard-block billing start until activation confirmation; restore permit checks for permit-sensitive addresses.
2. Suspend survey skipping for medium-risk and old-building addresses; keep it only where the rule is validated.
3. Keep coordinator ownership and the merged task for simple houses; cap coordinator utilisation at ~85% and mandate a shared-context backup.
4. Redeploy released field-survey capacity toward second visits and pre-installation checks.

**Learning plan (weeks 2–12).** Prioritise M4 (true 60-day cost), M6 (risk-rule validation), M3 (reason codes) and M7 (conformance monitoring); then M2 and a stratified randomised test of survey skipping (M1) only where genuine equipoise exists.

**Gate for wider rollout.** No segment worse than baseline on p90/p95, first-time-right and mature complaints; measured all-in cost below EUR 198; zero billing-before-activation events; coordinator utilisation 85%. If those hold at week 12, scale by segment — not nationally, and not as a bundle.

**Cost of delay is modest; cost of a wrong national scale is not.** We forgo roughly six weeks of broader median improvement to avoid institutionalising a 7pp second-visit rate and a billing-control gap across the full order book.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### 3.1 High fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51)
Fitness asks "can the model replay what happened?"; precision asks "does the model forbid things that never happen?". A model that allows almost any interleaving of *skip survey*, *manual activation*, *second visit* and *billing start* replays everything (high fitness) while forbidding nothing (low precision). Three mechanisms can produce this simultaneously, and they are not mutually exclusive:

- **Real control loss.** The redesign removed gating steps, so execution genuinely branched into many combinations. This is corroborated *independently of precision* by the alignments: repeated move-on-log on billing-before-activation and on visits without preceding survey.
- **Log artefacts.** The merged validate/schedule activity changes trace granularity; sparse start timestamps collapse ordering; incomplete attributes before week 3 prevent meaningful stratification. All depress precision without any process change.
- **Estimation instability.** Six weeks, four regions, 9,200 cases and heterogeneous execution reduce generalisation (0.700.56), which is exactly what one expects when a model is fitted to a short, unrepresentative sample.

**Managerial implication:** precision is a *model property*, not a process KPI, and must not be presented to the board as "the process got worse by 28 points". The decision-relevant signal is the *specific* conformance violations, which stand on their own. Re-discover on weeks 3–6 with complete attributes and a consistent activity granularity before drawing conclusions.

### 3.2 Improved median, worse tail
Cycle time is a **mixture distribution**, not one number. The pilot contains a fast, low-variance sub-process (simple houses: less queueing, merged task, no survey) and a slow, high-variance sub-process (apartments/old buildings: rework loops and permit discovery late). The median sits in the fast bulk; p90/p95 sit in the rework component. Two amplifiers make the tail explode:

- **Rework loops.** Failed visit  second visit (1319% and 1017% edge shares) adds a full extra visit-plus-rescheduling cycle, not a few hours.
- **Queueing near saturation.** At 93% coordinator and 92% second-visit utilisation, waiting time grows non-linearly; the observed increase in failed-visitsecond-visit delay in high-utilisation regions is the signature.

**Bias warning:** a six-week observation window truncates long, still-open cases more aggressively than the ten-week baseline. The pilot's true p95 is plausibly *worse* than 62 days. Use censoring-corrected (survival) estimates before comparing tails at all.

### 3.3 Fewer handovers, lower resilience
Handover count measures **transfers**, not **redundancy**. Consolidating ownership into one coordinator removed transfers *and* removed the second person who knew the case. The handover-of-work network simplified while the working-together network centralised — a structural single point of failure. When the coordinator is absent, no one else holds context, so cases stall. Additionally, the bottleneck **migrated**: field survey fell to 52% while second visits rose to 92%. Efficiency improved on the transfer metric; flow resilience and throughput capacity did not.

### 3.4 Lower visible cost, higher rework/downstream cost
Three distinct effects, often conflated:

1. **Boundary shift.** Cost after week 4 excludes complaint handling; the 30-day complaint window is immature for recent cohorts. Work moved outside the accounting frame.
2. **Actor shift.** Cost moved from the installation cost centre to second-visit teams, activation specialists and customer service — and partly to the customer (unpaid time, "installed but not working").
3. **Timing shift.** Saving EUR 23 today against a EUR 46 estimated downstream liability later is a cash-flow improvement, not necessarily a cost improvement.

The arithmetic is decisive about *what we need to know*: R is cheaper only if true downstream cost < EUR 37/case. That single number is currently an estimate, and it is the highest-value missing fact in the entire dossier.

### 3.5 Simple houses improve, apartments/old buildings deteriorate
The redesign's benefit mechanism is **removing waiting**; its cost mechanism is **removing information**. Where task uncertainty is low (simple houses), information has little value and waiting is pure waste — so removal is a clean win on every metric. Where task uncertainty is high (older apartments, permits, business activations), the survey and permit check are cheap options that prevent expensive failures; removing them raises variance, and the predictive rule was applied to a population (older apartment stock) over-represented in the pilot and plausibly under-represented in whatever data trained it. Post-billing activation adds a second, segment-independent failure mode: it converts a technical exception into a *customer-visible commercial* exception, which is why complaints changed character rather than only volume.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| | Verdict | Core logic |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | **Reject now** | Exports an unvalidated risk rule and a billing-control gap to the full order book; economics unresolved |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | **Reject** | Discards a well-evidenced 6-day/8-day/FTR gain for 34% of cases; restores a baseline that itself had 13% SLA breach and p95 of 45 days |
| **C. Coordinator only; suspend skip + post-billing activation** | **Strong fallback** | Removes the two most plausible harm mechanisms; but scales ownership into a 93%-utilisation constraint |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment** | **Necessary, insufficient alone** | Buys causal identification; delivers no customer benefit while running, and cannot ethically randomise the suspected-harmful arms |
| **E. Segment-gated deployment + measurement** | **Recommend, amended** | Matches intervention to segment uncertainty; keeps proven gains; contains harm; generates evidence |

**A — Scale nationally.**
*Advantages:* fastest realisation of the median and visible-cost gains; single national operating model; avoids change fatigue and dual-process complexity.
*Risks:* industrialises a 7pp second-visit rate and a billing-before-activation control gap; national mix may differ from the pilot in either direction; coordinator capacity requirements are unknown and 93% utilisation suggests they are large; generalisation 0.56 explicitly warns against extrapolating this model.
*Reasonable only if:* measured 60-day all-in cost beats EUR 198; per-segment p90/p95 and FTR are non-inferior to baseline; billing-before-activation is technically impossible; the risk rule is validated out-of-sample; coordinator capacity is funded to 85% utilisation. **None of these currently hold.**

**B — Full rollback.**
*Advantages:* immediately restores known controls, permit checks and pre-billing activation; eliminates the new failure modes; simplest to communicate.
*Risks:* throws away the strongest positive finding in the dataset (simple houses improve on *all* metrics, including complaints); re-imposes handovers that customers experienced as "waiting for appointment"; demoralises the pilot teams; and does not fix the baseline's own poor tail.
*Reasonable only if:* the billing/permit exposure proves to be a real, systemic compliance breach that cannot be contained by gating; or mature downstream cost shows R destroys value in *every* segment including simple houses.

**C — Coordinator ownership only.**
*Advantages:* retains the accountability gain (handovers 3412%) and the front-end speed for simple cases; removes the two interventions most plausibly causing rework and control violations; clean, testable hypothesis.
*Risks:* scaling single-owner accountability nationally without capacity or backup reproduces the absence-stall failure at scale; also removes the survey-skipping savings that produced much of the visible cost improvement, so the cost case must be rebuilt from scratch; the merged validate/schedule task is not explicitly covered by C and would need a decision.
*Reasonable if:* intake risk-classification is too unreliable to gate by segment (then gating is unenforceable and C is the safe default), **and** ownership is deployed with shared context and a utilisation cap.

**D — Controlled regional experiment only.**
*Advantages:* the only design that separates treatment from case mix, region and seasonality; supports stratified randomisation, difference-in-differences with matched control regions, and pre-registered guardrails.
*Risks:* six-to-twelve weeks of foregone benefit; randomising survey skipping on medium-risk addresses is not defensible when observational evidence already suggests harm (no equipoise); experiments confounded if staffing is changed simultaneously.
*Reasonable as:* the **learning engine attached to E**, not as a stand-alone answer. Restrict randomisation to strata where the recalibrated rule (M6) shows genuine uncertainty.

**E — Segment-gated deployment (recommended, amended).**
*Advantages:* aligns each component with the uncertainty of the segment it serves; banks the simple-house gain immediately; restores information and controls exactly where they pay; converts a binary accept/reject into a portfolio of separately governed components.
*Risks:* operational complexity of two paths; **gating is only as good as intake risk classification** — misclassification silently recreates the pilot's harm; coordinators may drift back into informal skipping under load; requires disciplined conformance monitoring.
*Amendment to the stated option:* extend **coordinator ownership with shared-context backup and an ~85% utilisation cap to all segments**, keep the **merged validate/schedule task for simple houses only**, and gate **survey skipping** and **pre-billing activation** by validated risk. This is E with C's ownership logic inside it.
*Reasonable if:* M6 shows the risk rule can classify reliably at intake (or a conservative rule-based gate can), and M7 can enforce the billing/permit gates automatically.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

**Ranking principle:** value = (probability the answer changes an action) × (magnitude of the consequence) ÷ (cost + delay). Measurements that confirm an already-robust action rank lower than measurements that could flip a decision.

**Zero-cost prerequisites (do these first, they are not in M1–M8):** re-standardise all aggregates to a common segment mix; re-estimate cycle-time tails with censoring correction; restrict complaint rates to cohorts with full 30-day maturity; re-run discovery on weeks 3–6 with consistent activity granularity; recompute the cost comparison on weeks 1–4 only. Several headline numbers may move before we spend anything.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty resolved | Decision it could change | Cost / latency | Why here |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **M4 — full 60-day downstream cost** | Whether all-in cost is < EUR 198; whether true downstream is above or below the EUR 37 break-even | Flips **A/E-scale vs B/C-retreat** on economics; determines whether the EUR 23 visible saving is real | Low–moderate; largely retrospective extraction from billing/CRM; ~2 weeks for mature cohorts | The single number on which the whole business case hinges, and it is currently an incomplete estimate |
| **2** | **M6 — validate risk rule vs survey outcomes** | Calibration and error rates of the skip rule by building-risk stratum; whether it is fixable or fundamentally mis-specified | Decides **suspend permanently vs recalibrate and re-enable**; also determines whether **E's gating is enforceable at all** (else fall back to C) | Low; retrospective comparison of skipped vs surveyed outcomes; ~2–3 weeks | Cheap, fast, and gates two decisions at once |
| **3** | **M3 — structured reason codes** (failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit) | Attribution of the rework increase: wrong skip rule vs missing permit vs coordinator overload vs customer reschedule vs activation defect | Determines **which component to fix**; without it, M1 and M2 results are uninterpretable; separates process failure from capacity failure | Low; taxonomy + mandatory field + training; ~3–4 weeks to usable volume | Highest enabling value per euro; converts move-on-log diagnostics into causes |
| **4** | **M7 — conformance monitoring** (billing-before-activation, permit absence) | Whether violations are real or timestamp artefacts; their volume, segment concentration and whether the new gates hold | Sizes **remediation/refund exposure**; is the **verification gate** for any rollout; could force B if pervasive and uncontrollable | Low; automated rules on the event stream; immediate | The *stop* decision is already robust, so its VoI is in sizing exposure and gating scale, not in the stop itself — hence 4, not 1 |
| **5** | **M2 — start timestamps** (visit, activation, manual activation) | Decomposition of cycle time into waiting vs touch time; whether the tail is queueing (capacity fix) or rework (process fix) | Decides **fund coordinators vs restore survey/permit**; makes utilisation figures trustworthy; prerequisite for M5 | Moderate instrumentation; ~4–6 weeks | Directly attacks the main blocker to scaling (the tail), but partially substitutable by M3 + utilisation proxies in the short run |
| **6** | **M1 — randomised survey-skipping trial by risk stratum** | Causal effect of skipping on FTR, second visits, tail and all-in cost, free of selection effects | Confirms or overturns the skip rule's value in the *equipoise* strata; strongest evidence for national policy | High; needs M6 strata, M3 codes, guardrails, ~1,000/arm for a 5pp FTR effect, ~2,700/arm for 3pp (80% power, =0.05); 8–12 weeks | Gold-standard identification, but slow, costly, and ethically constrained to strata where harm is not already indicated |
| **7** | **M5 — coordinator vs shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether single ownership's resilience loss is material and whether shared context recovers it | Shapes the **ownership design** (backup, pool size, utilisation cap) rather than the scale/no-scale decision | Moderate; can start as a natural experiment on absence events before randomising | Important, but the mitigations (backup, context sharing, 85% load) are worth doing regardless — robust actions have low VoI |
| **8** | **M8 — split CX measurement** (speed to appointment vs working service) | Whether NPS +21+17 is a net of two opposing effects; how customers weight speed against reliability | Informs **segment targeting, messaging and the acceptable speed/reliability trade-off**; unlikely to flip the operational decision alone | Low; survey redesign; piggybacks on existing NPS | Cheap and strategically useful, but the complaint-theme shift already tells us the direction; least likely to change the next 12 weeks of action |

**Sequencing logic.** M4 + M6 + M3 + M7 run in parallel from week 0 (all are largely retrospective or instrumentation-only). Their outputs define the strata, guardrails and outcome definitions for M2 and M1. M5 and M8 ride along.

**Explicit stopping rule for the experiment:** if any arm shows billing-before-activation, permit omission on a permit-sensitive address, or a segment-level FTR drop >5pp versus control, halt that arm immediately regardless of median or cost performance.

**What would make more measurement *not* worth it:** if M4 shows all-in cost above EUR 198 **and** M6 shows the skip rule is unfixable, then components 3 and 5 are dead and further experimentation on them is wasted — proceed directly to C/E with ownership and the merged task only.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: E, amended — unbundle Redesign R, deploy by segment, and run D as the learning engine. Do not scale nationally. Do not roll back.**

### Immediate action (weeks 0–2)
1. **Hard-block billing start until a successful activation test is logged.** System enforcement, not guidance. Quarantine and review any pilot cases billed before activation for customer remediation.
2. **Restore permit checks for all permit-sensitive addresses**, and verify by field audit whether the move-on-model finding reflects omission or unlogged work.
3. **Cap coordinator utilisation at ~85%** in the two saturated regions; mandate a named backup with shared case context; log absence-driven stalls.
4. **Redeploy released field-survey capacity** (52% utilisation) toward second visits (92%) and pre-installation checks in apartment/old-building stock.
5. **Run the zero-cost re-analyses** (mix standardisation, censoring-corrected tails, mature-cohort complaint rates, re-discovery on weeks 3–6).

### What to pause
- **Predictive survey skipping for medium-risk and old-building addresses** — until M6 validates the rule out-of-sample and M1 confirms causal safety in defined strata.
- **Activation checks after billing start** — indefinitely, pending M7 evidence that ordering was an artefact *and* a redesigned, controlled variant exists. Do not trade a conduct/revenue-assurance control for cycle time.
- **Any national rollout decision** — until the week-12 gate.

### What to scale now
- **Merged address-validation + appointment scheduling for simple low-risk houses** (34% of pilot; improves median, tail, FTR and complaints simultaneously).
- **Coordinator ownership for all segments**, but only in the amended form: shared case context, named backup, 85% utilisation target, and no authority to skip survey or permit steps.
- **Segment gating logic at intake**, with a conservative default: if risk cannot be classified confidently, treat as high-risk and run the full process.
