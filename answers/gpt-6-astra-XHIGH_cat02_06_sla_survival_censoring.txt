# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

The primary SLA measure should be **failure to achieve an internal `Resolve` by 72 hours**, accompanied by a separate measure of **remaining internal workload at 72 hours**. They answer different questions: a rejection can end workload without achieving operational success.

Set time zero to `Submit`, measure continuous elapsed hours, and count resolution at exactly 72 hours as on time. No business-hours adjustment or clock pause is specified. Let T be time to the first terminal internal outcome and J its type: R = Resolve, W = Withdraw, Jc = Reject, X = Transfer. Let C be age at the observation cutoff. For the primary internal analysis, all four outcomes are absorbing; `open` records are right-censored at C.

The following assumptions are needed and are not additional observations:

- Each supplied case is observed from submission until its recorded endpoint or cutoff, without selective omission of older cases or missing internal resolutions. Otherwise the sampling/observation process must be modeled.
- Terminal internal outcomes do not reopen within the same case. A transfer ends the internal episode; resolution in the external organization is a different, unobserved continuation.
- Priority can be used as a baseline characteristic only if the supplied value is the priority assigned at submission. If it is the latest priority, historical priority is needed.
- Survival point estimates below assume censoring at cutoff is independent of subsequent outcomes within the population or strata being analyzed. This assumption is unnecessary for the observed counts and finite-extract bounds, and is questionable for pooled cohort comparisons.

Define these estimands before choosing estimators:

| Estimand | Definition | Operational use |
|---|---|---|
| Internal non-resolution by 72 | B_R(72) = 1 − F_R(72), where F_R(t) = P(T ≤ t, J = R) | Success SLA, including unsuccessful terminal dispositions. It must be labeled “no internal Resolve,” since Withdraw, Reject and Transfer count as non-success. |
| Still internally active at 72 | A(72) = P(T > 72) | Backlog, staffing and overdue internal work. Any internal terminal disposition removes workload. |
| Late internal resolution by a specified horizon | L_R(72, τ) = P(72 < T ≤ τ, J = R) = F_R(τ) − F_R(72), for τ > 72 | Recovery of missed SLAs and planning how much overdue work will resolve within a recovery window. Use τ = 96 hours as an illustrative reporting horizon, not as a supplied policy. |
| End-to-end non-resolution by 72 | B_E(72) = P(T_R,external-inclusive > 72), allowing T_R = infinity for permanent non-resolution | Customer outcome and cross-organization accountability. External resolution after transfer requires additional follow-up or explicit assumptions. |

For the internal endpoints, B_R(72) = A(72) + F_W(72) + F_Jc(72) + F_X(72). Thus “unresolved” includes closed, unsuccessful cases as well as active cases. L_R is not its complement: cases that never resolve contribute to B_R but never to late-resolution incidence. Conditional recovery among cases active at 72 is L_R(72, τ)/A(72), when A(72) > 0; conditioning instead on all cases without Resolve gives a different denominator.

The requested examples contribute as follows. Entries describe actual endpoint information, not weights in an estimator.

| Case | Internal non-resolution by 72 | Internally active at 72 | Late internal Resolve in (72, 96] | End-to-end non-resolution by 72 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03, Resolve at 76 | Known yes: an observed missed success SLA | Known yes; remains at risk through 72 and resolves at 76 | Known event at 76 | Known yes |
| S09, open at 92 | Known yes; uncertainty about eventual resolution does not undo its observed breach | Known yes | No Resolve through 92; right-censored at 92, so resolution in (92, 96] is unknown | Known yes, assuming internal recording is complete |

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use one row per case for the terminal competing-risk analysis. In the primary encoding, the resolution indicator δ_R identifies Resolve events; the disposition indicator δ_D identifies any internal terminal outcome. A zero δ_R for a competing outcome does **not** mean that outcome should be discarded or automatically treated as ordinary censoring.

| Observed status | Recorded time | δ_R | δ_D | Primary competing-risk code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| resolved | Duration to Resolve | 1 | 1 | 1 | Observed event, including resolutions after 72 | Separate on-time and late windows; verify whether reopening can occur |
| withdrawn | Duration to Withdraw | 0 | 1 | 2 | Observed competing event, not missing follow-up | Report legitimate cancellations separately; count as success only for an explicitly defined disposition SLA; censor only for a clearly labeled net/hypothetical resolution analysis |
| rejected | Duration to Reject | 0 | 1 | 3 | Observed competing event | Same alternatives as withdrawal, with a separate rejection incidence and reason taxonomy |
| transferred | Duration to Transfer | 0 | 1 | 4 | Observed competing exit for the internal episode | External censoring, optimistic resolution proxy, and external outcome bounds described below |
| open | Age at cutoff | 0 | 0 | 0 | Right-censor at the supplied age; retain in every applicable risk set until then | Conditional censoring adjustment; delayed/missing-event sensitivity if the record is unreliable |

Three transfer analyses have distinct meanings:

1. **Absorbing internal exit, the primary analysis.** Transfer is code 4 and competes with internal resolution. No assumption about independence between transfer and resolution is needed to estimate the observed internal cause-specific incidence. S08 and S21 leave internal workload before 72; S32 transfers at 96 and is already known to have remained internally unresolved and active at 72.
2. **Loss to follow-up for an end-to-end endpoint.** Transfer is code 0 at its observed time. External resolution and subsequent external disposition are unobserved. Estimation requires transfer censoring to be independent of these subsequent outcomes, possibly conditional on pre-transfer history, with adequate overlap. This is a strong assumption: all three transfers are escalated P1 cases. Internal non-transferred cases may also be poor substitutes for outcomes under external care even after adjustment.
3. **Optimistic resolution proxy at transfer.** Recode Transfer as code 1 at its transfer time. This assumes immediate external success, or changes the outcome to Resolve-or-Transfer. It is a sensitivity scenario, never an observed Resolve. S08 and S21 become on-time proxy successes; S32 still misses 72 hours. Internal workload estimates remain unchanged because transfer already ended that workload.

A pessimistic external scenario assumes no transferred case resolves by 72. Together with the optimistic assignment it bounds transferred cases' binary 72-hour outcomes, but it does not identify their actual resolution times. The fitted AJ estimates under competing and optimistic encodings are not themselves assumption-free population bounds: they still rely on assumptions about open-case censoring.

For a 72-hour analysis, set observed time to min(recorded time, 72), preserving a terminal event code only when its time is ≤ 72. Retain the original time and status separately for known-breach labels, late-resolution analysis and auditability. Do not censor an open case at its last recorded activity merely because it has a long inactivity gap; the stated data say it remains open through cutoff. That rule changes only if a recording audit shows that observation actually stopped earlier.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Observed composition:** there are 32 cases: 13 resolved, 4 withdrawn, 3 rejected, 3 transferred and 9 open. Nine resolutions occurred within 72 hours. Four were late: S03 at 76, S13 at 110, S20 at 74 and S28 at 88.

Using only resolved cases produces 4/13 = 30.8% late resolutions. This estimates lateness conditional on having an observed Resolve in this extract. It is neither the probability of no Resolve by 72 among submissions nor the eventual late-resolution incidence. Selection favors cases that resolve quickly enough to appear before cutoff and excludes unsuccessful exits. Calling this survivor or completion-selection bias is more precise than assuming the bias must always have one sign.

Concrete omissions show the problem:

- S09, S11, S25 and S26 are open at 92, 81, 79 and 104 hours. Each already missed the success SLA and remains internal workload. A resolved-only analysis excludes all four.
- S10, S15, S24, S29 and S31 are open at 47, 35, 67, 51 and 23 hours. Their 72-hour success outcomes remain unknown; counting them as on time or as already breached is incorrect. S24 has only five hours of SLA time remaining.
- S06's withdrawal at 27 and S05's rejection at 9 are observed competing outcomes. Treating them as quick successful resolutions improves a closure metric while changing the operational-success endpoint. Censoring them as if randomly lost instead estimates a different quantity.
- S08 and S21 transfer before 72; their internal outcome is known but external success is unknown. S32 transfers after 72 and cannot erase its already-observed breach. All transferred cases are P1 and escalated, making random-loss assumptions particularly doubtful.
- S11, S25 and S26 have gaps of 55, 48 and 72 hours. They could be stalled cases, sparse activity recording, or unrecorded progress. The table cannot distinguish those explanations. None licenses silently replacing their cutoff ages with last-event times.

Cohort A has 15 cases and B has 17. A's resolved-only late proportion is 2/6 = 33.3%; B's is 2/7 = 28.6%. That apparent improvement is not a policy effect. B has more P1 cases proportionally: 5/17 versus 3/15; both cohorts have seven P2 and five P3 cases. B began later, so calendar conditions and follow-up opportunities may differ, and rollout compliance was incomplete. Its exact submission-date distribution is not supplied.

These are missing-outcome identification bounds for this finite extract, not confidence intervals for a future population. # 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Primary approach: nonparametric Aalen–Johansen (AJ) estimates of internal terminal outcomes**, with Resolve, Withdraw, Reject and Transfer as competing causes and open cases censored at their cutoff ages. Report all four cumulative incidences, internal non-resolution, internal event-free survival, numbers at risk and censoring counts. Start with pooled estimates and descriptive cohort and priority strata; do not fit a rich regression to these 32 cases.

At each distinct observed time u, let Y(u) be the number still under observation and without a prior terminal event immediately before u, and d_k(u) the number of events of cause k. Then

Ŝ(t) = ∏ over u ≤ t of [1 − Σ_k d_k(u)/Y(u)],

F̂_k(t) = Σ over u ≤ t of Ŝ(u−) d_k(u)/Y(u).

Censored cases enter Y until their censoring time and do not add an event. Independent competing causes are not required. Independent administrative censoring, suitable ascertainment and adequate follow-up support are required. If censoring is independent only after conditioning on cohort, priority or calendar time, use the corresponding stratification or adjustment rather than assume the pooled calculation is unbiased.

The supplied data yield these **illustrative estimates**, computed by the code below under the primary assumptions:

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pooled | 32 | 30.4% | 69.6% | 38.0% | 50.4 |
| Cohort A | 15 | 29.2% | 70.8% | 33.9% | 49.6 |
| Cohort B | 17 | 31.6% | 68.4% | 41.6% | 51.1 |

Pooled withdrawal, rejection and transfer incidence by 72 is approximately 14.0%, 10.1% and 7.5%, respectively. Together they explain why non-resolution risk is substantially higher than active workload risk. Nine cases remain observed and event-free at 72. The estimated late Resolve incidence in (72, 96] is 14.3%, or 37.8% conditional on being internally active at 72. Only three cases are at risk immediately before 96; these recovery estimates are fragile.

The choice among methods follows the endpoint and available information:

| Method | Role here | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Kaplan–Meier | Appropriate for time to **any internal disposition**, treating all four terminal outcomes as events. Its survival equals the event-free component of primary AJ. | Resolve-only KM with competing exits censored is a net cause-specific calculation, not actual-world Resolve incidence. Ordinary KM cannot distinguish how workload ends. |
| AJ / cumulative incidence | Primary estimator of actual internal outcome probabilities and late-resolution increments. It separates unsuccessful exit from continuing work. | Censoring assumptions remain; tiny strata and late risk sets create substantial uncertainty. |
| Cause-specific hazards | With more data, model the instantaneous rate of each outcome among cases still active; combine all cause-specific hazards to predict incidence. Useful for diagnosing stage-specific exits. | A higher Resolve hazard does not necessarily mean a higher Resolve CIF if other exit hazards also change. Competing cases leave the relevant risk set; this does not require independent latent competing failure times. |
| Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards | Possible later model for baseline associations with the 72-hour Resolve CIF. | Its weighted risk set includes competing-event cases, so its hazard is not a physical transition rate among active workers' cases. Covariates evolving after competing exits are problematic. The present sample cannot support a credible multivariable fit. |
| Restricted mean time | Secondary workload summary: RM_active(72) = ∫₀⁷² S(t)dt = E[min(T,72)]. It avoids extrapolating a mean or median into a poorly observed tail. | This is time internally active, not time to success. ∫₀⁷²[1 − F_R(t)]dt instead counts unresolved hours even after unsuccessful internal closure; label it separately. |
| Landmarking | Preferred future design for alerts: at a prespecified age such as 24 or 48 hours, include cases still active and observed, use only history available then, and predict through the original 72-hour deadline. | Current prefixes cannot generally be reconstructed at past landmarks without event timestamps. Each landmark has its own conditional population; censoring before the landmark and repeated predictions need appropriate handling. |
| Multi-state model | Future process-aware extension, with transitions among work stages and absorbing outcomes; can distinguish delay in review from post-escalation delay. | Activity order alone does not identify transition hazards or state residence times. A semi-Markov or expanded-state model may be needed when stage duration and rework history matter. |

**Secondary approach now:** report restricted mean internal active time, the finite-extract bounds, priority-standardized cohort summaries and transfer/exit sensitivity analyses. **Secondary approach after richer data collection:** landmark competing-risk prediction, followed by a parsimonious multi-state model if stage-level decisions justify it.

For descriptive standardization, weight each cohort's priority-specific AJ estimates by pooled priority shares: P1 = 8/32, P2 = 14/32 and P3 = 10/32. Estimated non-resolution becomes 68.8% for A and 67.5% for B, versus 70.8% and 68.4% unstandardized. For uncertainty reporting in a larger analysis, use competing-risk variance estimates or case-level bootstrap intervals, preserving relevant strata and explicitly identifying resamples lacking horizon support. Do not interpret unstable or zero-width results from tiny cells as certainty. These point estimates and sensitivity ranges do not establish a reliable cohort ranking. Avoid unrestricted mean resolution time, heavily parameterized Cox/Fine–Gray models, and claims about eventual resolution beyond the observed tail.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Prefixes give activity order and some control-flow structure, but not a timestamp for every activity. Features should be classified by when they become observable:

| Feature | What these records support | Correct temporal role and risks |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort and priority | Cohort membership; recorded priority | Baseline only if defined at submission. Cohort is a policy-era proxy, not randomized treatment; priority could itself change during processing. |
| Current recorded activity | Last activity in each open prefix: Screen, Review or Escalate | A landmark/current-time feature. An activity label is only a proxy for actual work state; “Escalate” may be a point action rather than a persistent queue. |
| Automated-check path | AutoCheck appears in 2/15 A prefixes and 13/17 B prefixes. B has both AutoCheck-before-Screen and Screen-before-AutoCheck paths. | Time-varying exposure after AutoCheck occurs; also a potential mediator of policy effects. Absence from an unfinished prefix is not proof that AutoCheck will never occur or that rollout was violated. |
| Repeated Review | S03, S07 and S13 each revisit Review | Running count available only after the repeat. This is distinct from explicit Rework: S03 repeats Review with rework_count = 0. |
| Explicit Rework | S07, S13 and S27 have a Rework event | Running rework count at a landmark. S27 resolves after Rework without another recorded Review, so rework and repeated review cannot be substituted for each other. |
| Escalation and its timing | The full prefix identifies whether escalation has already occurred by observation end; all three transferred cases were escalated | Switch exposure on only after escalation. Time since escalation requires its actual event time; full-case `escalated` is not a baseline predictor. |
| Inactivity gap | Open-case gaps range from 2 to 72 hours | A current-time, time-varying feature of recorded inactivity. It is not proven service time or abandonment. Terminal rows all have gap 0, making the end-of-record gap highly leakage-prone in a baseline model. |
| Path/variant and prefix length | Exact observed variants and their frequencies can be derived without timestamps | Descriptive control-flow features. Forecasting must use prefixes truncated at prediction time and variants learned on training data only; terminal labels and final path length reveal future outcomes. |
| Time in stage, age since last action, workload | Only current age and gap are provided; most historical stage times and workload are missing | Proposed time-varying predictors. Do not apportion total duration evenly over activities or assign synthetic dates to create them. |

For open cases, age minus gap legitimately gives the **elapsed time of the last recorded event**, not a fabricated calendar timestamp. For example, S10's last event, Escalate, occurred at elapsed hour 28, and S25's last Escalate at hour 31. This does not reconstruct every preceding event time or a complete historical risk-set dataset. Terminal gap 0 does not imply no waiting before completion.

Immortal-time bias arises if S13 is labeled escalated from submission: it had to remain unresolved until its escalation, yet all earlier hours would be attributed to exposure. Eventual rework has the same problem. Escalation also responds to difficulty and elapsed time, so an association with a long duration can reflect confounding by indication and reverse timing. Conversely, quicker escalated resolutions in B would not prove escalation helped. For prediction use X(t−), the history available immediately before the risk interval; for policy-effect estimation, distinguish baseline confounders from post-policy mediators and use an appropriate causal design.

A proposed representation, in text, is:

- Submit → Screen or AutoCheck.
- Screen → AutoCheck, Review, Escalated handling, Resolve, Withdraw or Reject.
- AutoCheck → Screen, Review, Escalated handling or Resolve.
- Review → Rework, Escalated handling, Resolve, Withdraw or Reject.
- Rework → Review or Resolve.
- Escalated handling → Review, Resolve or Transfer.
- Resolve, Withdraw and Reject are absorbing internal states. Transfer is absorbing internally; an external extension would continue Transfer → External pending → External Resolve or another external outcome, with unknown transition times here.

This diagram describes plausible observed routing and a proposed interpretation of escalation; it is not proof of a complete process specification. A stronger representation keeps work stage as one state component and an “escalation has occurred” flag as another, since S29 has returned to Review after escalation. Rework history or repeat count can likewise be retained without forcing a memoryless stage model.

With timestamps, form start–stop intervals and estimate transition-specific hazards by elapsed case age and time in current stage. Candidate questions are whether unresolved Review episodes have falling resolution rates as inactivity grows, whether rework raises return-to-review rates, and whether post-escalation delays differ by priority. These are hypotheses for analysis, not findings established by the prefix table. PM4Py is appropriate for ordered variants and loops here; duration-based process discovery or stage-performance attribution is unsupported.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

Use a small, explicit scenario matrix and separate changes in endpoint from changes in statistical assumptions.

| Analysis | Transfer treatment | Withdraw/Reject treatment | Censoring and interpretation | Illustrative pooled result at 72 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Internal primary | Competing exit | Separate competing exits | Administrative censoring assumed independent | No internal Resolve: 69.6%; still internally active: 38.0% |
| External follow-up extrapolation | Censor at transfer | Competing exits | Assumes transferred outcomes are exchangeable with followed cases, conditionally if adjusted | Estimated no end-to-end Resolve: 68.7%; no substantive disposition: 43.5% |
| Optimistic external proxy | Resolve at transfer | Competing exits | Immediate success is imposed as a sensitivity assumption | No Resolve-or-Transfer: 62.1%; internal active workload remains 38.0% |
| Net resolution calculation | Censor at transfer | Censor at their exit times | Removes all competing exits from the Resolve risk set | Net non-resolution: 63.9%; not actual-world success probability |
| Any-disposition SLA | Count as disposition | Count as disposition | Ordinary KM of any internal exit; different governance target | No disposition: 38.0% |

The net calculation is an estimable function of the observed cause-specific Resolve hazard, but interpreting it as a world where competing events are eliminated requires additional assumptions about how resolution would behave under that intervention. It must not be relabeled as the ordinary probability of success.

Several further sensitivities matter:

1. **Unknown outcomes among younger open cases.** Complete the five unknown 72-hour labels in favorable and unfavorable ways to obtain the bounds in Section 3. Cohort A's internal non-resolution fraction can be 60.0%–73.3%; B's can be 52.9%–70.6%. A favorable completion for A and unfavorable completion for B makes B worse, while the reverse makes B better. Thus even finite-extract cohort ordering is unidentified without assumptions about these outcomes. Add the two pre-72 transfers to the unknown set for the external endpoint.
2. **Informative transfer and censoring.** In a richer dataset, model censoring/transfer probabilities using history available before loss, including baseline priority, current stage, elapsed age, prior escalation, calendar time and workload. Compare unweighted AJ with inverse-probability-of-censoring weighted estimates, checking positivity, weight concentration and sensitivity to weight truncation. This sample cannot reliably estimate such a model, particularly with only three P1 transfers. As a separate tipping-point exercise, vary the assumed post-transfer Resolve hazard below and above that of comparable observed cases; specify the multipliers as analyst assumptions rather than fitted evidence.
3. **Long gaps and recording failure.** Under the supplied status definition, keep all open cases observed through cutoff. If audit evidence instead shows unreliable status during gaps, repeat the analysis with an explicit loss-of-observation or latent missing-event model. For example, a prespecified 48-hour gap flag would identify S11, S25 and S26; it should trigger review, not declare them censored. A deliberately pessimistic recording sensitivity could treat their last-event times as last confirmed follow-up, but that contradicts complete status ascertainment and must be labeled accordingly. Missing actual resolutions could lower apparent breach risk; true inactivity could raise future tail risk. Those mechanisms cannot be distinguished here.
4. **Policy-era effects.** Compare crude and priority-standardized cohort estimates, then calendar- and workload-adjusted estimates when those covariates become available. Analyze policy availability separately from actual execution of AutoCheck or escalation. A cohort contrast is not an intention-to-treat causal estimate without an assignment design; an as-executed comparison is also selected and time-dependent. An interrupted time series, phased-rollout comparison or randomized future rollout would require dates, sufficient pre/post observations and credible assumptions about concurrent changes.

The qualitative conclusion depends on the decision. B has lower estimated internal non-resolution but higher estimated active workload at 72; “B improved the SLA” is therefore ambiguous. Under the optimistic transfer proxy, cohort non-resolution is about 62.3% for A and 62.1% for B, nearly eliminating the primary apparent advantage. Priority standardization also reduces that advantage. Associations between AutoCheck, escalation and faster resolution could reverse after correcting timing, case mix and censoring. The observed facts that S03 resolved after 72 and S26 is recorded open beyond 72 do not reverse under alternative transfer handling.

# 7. Operational interpretation

For an active case of age a < 72 with recorded history H(a), an alert should use a conditional probability such as P(no Resolve by 72 | active at a, H(a)). Pair it with the probability of remaining internally active at 72 and probabilities of alternative dispositions. High non-resolution risk driven by likely legitimate rejection calls for a different action from high risk of a stalled review queue.

A dynamic model must retain the original deadline: a prediction made at age 48 has a 24-hour remaining horizon. Its score can prioritize review or capacity subject to priority and intervention costs, but it is not the probability that escalation will help. Estimating an action's benefit requires intervention data and causal assumptions, preferably a controlled rollout.

The supplied open inventory supports concrete actions without pretending to have a validated predictive model:

- S09, S11, S25 and S26 are already beyond 72. Place them in an overdue recovery queue and forecast recovery time or additional active hours, rather than issuing a probabilistic warning for an already-observed breach. S11 and S25 are P1; S26 has the longest open age and a 72-hour recording gap.
- S24 is open at 67 hours with a four-hour event gap. It merits immediate owner/capacity review because only five hours remain, even though its gap is short.
- S29 is P1 at 51 hours and has returned to Review after escalation; S10 is at 47 hours with a 19-hour gap since its last escalation event. Review their current blockers rather than assuming prior escalation completed the intervention.
- S15 and S31 are younger open cases at 35 and 23 hours. Keep them under monitoring; neither is a known breach.
- Follow up external outcomes for S08, S21 and S32 through a defined handoff process. Transfer should close an internal workload item without silently certifying customer resolution.

At a queue level, sum conditional active-at-horizon probabilities to estimate outstanding case counts, with uncertainty and future arrivals considered separately. Use restricted mean active time to describe expected case-hours remaining. Case-hours are not staff labor-hours: staffing decisions additionally require effort, service capacity and concurrency information.

Governance should publish a success SLA and a disposition/workload SLA side by side, with withdrawal, rejection and transfer incidences visible. Define cancellation eligibility and transfer accountability prospectively; avoid excluding unsuccessful exits after seeing their outcomes. Track both already-missed cases and unresolved younger cohorts so a later-starting cohort does not appear successful merely because follow-up is immature.

Before operational deployment, assess prediction calibration at 72 and at each landmark using later, temporally held-out cohorts. Compare predicted risks with censoring-adjusted observed incidence, use appropriate competing-risk Brier scores, and assess alert sensitivity, false-alert rates and positive predictive value at proposed workload thresholds. External-endpoint calibration needs external outcomes; a good internal calibration score cannot validate the transfer assumption.

Check calibration and error rates separately by baseline priority and cohort, together with transfer rates, follow-up completeness, alert burden and access to intervention. P1 can justifiably have a different action threshold under an explicit service policy, but different observed risks should not automatically reduce service to difficult cases. The present cells are too small for reliable fairness conclusions, and no demographic fairness analysis is possible from the supplied fields. Monitor feedback after deployment because alerts themselves can change escalation, censoring and subsequent outcomes.

# All three scenarios retain every case. Codes: 0 censor, 1 Resolve,
# 2 Withdraw, 3 Reject, 4 Transfer. Scenarios change endpoint meaning.
def encode(frame, transfer="competing", wr="competing"):
    x = frame.copy()
    codes = {"open": 0, "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2,
             "rejected": 3, "transferred": 4}
    codes["transferred"] = {"competing": 4, "censor": 0,
                              "optimistic": 1}[transfer]
    if wr == "censor":
        codes.update(withdrawn=0, rejected=0)
    x["time"] = x["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
    x["code"] = x["observed_status"].map(codes)
    x["resolve_event"] = x["code"].eq(1).astype(int)
    x["disposition_event"] = x["code"].gt(0).astype(int)
    x["time_72"] = x["time"].clip(upper=H)
    x["code_72"] = x["code"].where(x["time"].le(H), 0)
    return x

# Direct Aalen-Johansen product calculation; no survival-library dependency.
# With only code 1 events it is the ordinary Kaplan-Meier calculation.
# Assumes complete follow-up until each row's time, independent censoring
# for its estimand, no delayed entry, and absorbing terminal events.
def aj_table(x, horizon=H):
    if x.empty or x["time"].max() < horizon:
        raise ValueError("Insufficient follow-up support for requested horizon")
    S, cif, last, rm_active = 1.0, {k: 0.0 for k in range(1, 5)}, 0.0, 0.0
    rows = []
    for t in sorted(x.loc[x["time"].le(horizon), "time"].unique()):
        y = int(x["time"].ge(t).sum())  # immediately before t
        here = x.loc[x["time"].eq(t), "code"]
        d = {k: int(here.eq(k).sum()) for k in cif}
        rm_active += S * (t - last)
        before = S
        for k in cif:
            cif[k] += before * d[k] / y
        S = before * (1.0 - sum(d.values()) / y)
        rows.append({"time": t, "at_risk": y, "censored": int(here.eq(0).sum()),
                     **{f"d{k}": d[k] for k in cif}, "S": S,
                     **{f"F{k}": cif[k] for k in cif}})
        last = t
    rm_active += S * (horizon - last)
    tag = f"{horizon:g}"
    return pd.DataFrame(rows), {f"F_resolve_{tag}": cif[1],
        f"not_resolved_{tag}": 1.0 - cif[1], f"no_disposition_{tag}": S,
        f"F_withdraw_{tag}": cif[2], f"F_reject_{tag}": cif[3],
        f"F_transfer_{tag}": cif[4], f"RM_active_0_{tag}_h": rm_active}

def summary(frame, transfer="competing", wr="competing"):
    x = encode(frame, transfer, wr)
    if x["time"].max() < H:
        return {"n": len(x), "supported_at_72": False}
    _, result = aj_table(x)
    return {"n": len(x), "supported_at_72": True, **result}

def horizon_bounds(frame, external=False, active=False):
    st, t = frame["observed_status"], frame["duration_or_age_h"]
    unknown = st.eq("open") & t.lt(H)
    if active:
        known_yes = t.gt(H) | (st.eq("open") & t.ge(H))

            known_yes |= st.eq("transferred") & t.gt(H)
            unknown |= st.eq("transferred") & t.le(H)
        else:
            known_yes |= st.eq("transferred")
    return {"known_yes": int(known_yes.sum()), "unknown": int(unknown.sum()),
            "lower": known_yes.mean(), "upper": (known_yes | unknown).mean()}

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The strongest result is the distinction between known non-success, active workload and genuinely unobserved future outcomes. The data support transparent descriptive survival calculations and scenario comparisons; they do not support a dependable high-dimensional predictor, external-resolution estimate or causal policy evaluation.

Prioritize additional data as follows:

1. **Exact event timestamps and their semantics.** Obtain submission, activity occurrence and recording times; distinguish start/complete events and queue entry from instantaneous actions. Include escalation and rework timing, priority-change history and reopening events. These enable valid historical prefixes, start–stop covariates, stage residence times and landmark validation. Sequence order and final duration alone cannot supply them.
2. **Linked external outcomes after transfer.** Record receiving organization, reason for transfer, acceptance time, external Resolve or other final outcome, and last confirmed external follow-up. This most directly reduces uncertainty in the customer-success endpoint and tests the assumption that transferred P1 cases resemble internally followed cases.
3. **Submission dates, the exact cutoff and extract inclusion rules.** Confirm an inception cohort with complete submissions, full follow-up from time zero, no selective retention of recent completions and consistent time zones. Calendar dates are needed to distinguish maturity, seasonality, policy era and administrative censoring, and to identify any delayed entry or left truncation.
4. **Observation reliability.** Audit long-gap open records, ingestion delays, missing terminal events, status reconciliation and what `last_event_gap_h` measures for terminal rows. This determines whether gaps describe process inactivity or loss of information. Do not infer that distinction from gaps alone.
5. **Time-varying workload and capacity.** Collect queue lengths, arrivals, staffing, ownership, handoffs, work schedules, service effort and blockers aligned with event times. These are needed for useful stage-level predictions and to translate case-hours into staffing actions.
6. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance.** Obtain policy effective dates by team, eligibility, actual screening configuration, escalation triggers, overrides, compliance records and concurrent process changes. Add baseline complexity and case type to assess confounding and overlap. Actual execution timing is necessary for per-protocol questions; cohort labels alone are insufficient.
7. **More cases and longer, consistently defined follow-up.** There are only 13 Resolve events, three transfers and very small priority-by-cohort strata. Expand the sample for uncertainty estimation, temporal validation, calibration and subgroup checks, while avoiding extrapolation into unsupported tails.
8. **An agreed SLA contract and outcome taxonomy.** Specify whether legitimate withdrawal/rejection affects success accountability, whether transfer pauses or preserves end-to-end responsibility, how reopened cases are treated, and whether recorded or actual occurrence time determines compliance. These choices select the estimand; a statistical method cannot settle them.
