# 2. Integrated explanation

The work period is not one curve but a **moving mixture of case difficulty, load, human state, and measurement artifacts**, and each phase of the shift is dominated by a different mechanism.

**Early shift (positions 1–5):** the elevated service time (18.8 min) and error rate (3.4%) are primarily a **case-mix and selection story with a warm-up overlay**. The previous shift's backlog is complex (mean complexity 0.72, 48% high-complexity in the Early shift's first hour), and seniors deliberately pull complex carryover cases. Cold-start inefficiency adds a genuine warm-up component on top. This phase is *not* fatigue and *not* yet queue pressure.

**Mid shift (positions 6–30):** the apparent "learning curve" is real but overstated. Part of the improvement is genuine warm-up; part is that the **simplest cases of the day arrive here** — backlog cleared, new demand peak not yet arrived (complexity bottoms at 0.50, WIP at 31). The 2.5% error floor is the *combination* of warmed-up workers and an easy queue, not warm-up alone.

**Late shift (positions 51+):** service time keeps falling (11.4  10.8 min) but this is **not continued learning**. Four things compress it simultaneously: (a) the remaining queue is lower-complexity and lower-priority (19% high-priority), so rational effort allocation favors speed; (b) WIP explodes to 88 and workers truncate service times — the classic speed-quality tradeoff visible in the WIP table (9.4 min, 5.8% errors even for *low*-complexity cases); (c) batching by Junior_H/I mechanically deflates mean service time; (d) only a residual is true skill. Errors rise here because **pressure, accumulated fatigue, and end-of-shift QC detection bursts coincide**. Critically, the 76+ bin sits in the last ~20 minutes of the shift — exactly when delayed Quality Checks convert latent errors into *detected* errors, so part of the 7.9% is ascertainment, not just occurrence.

**Why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions:** the speed-quality tradeoff is **conditional on WIP, not a universal law of the shift**. On the Day shift, late hours combine peak arrivals (queue 38  75) with simple low-priority work  fastest service (10.9 min) *and* worst errors (7.5%). On the Night shift, low volume means no pressure: service is slow (15.2 min) and errors moderate (5.1%) — slowness there reflects circadian state and urgent-exception complexity, not care. On the Early shift, seniors absorb complex carryover at moderate speed with moderate errors. The same worker can be "fast and sloppy" at 16:00 under WIP 90 and "slow and careful" at 03:00 under WIP 20. Any single-mechanism story (e.g., "fatigue") fails precisely because the error minimum is mid-shift and the error maximum tracks WIP and QC timing more tightly than it tracks clock time.

**Automation compounds the late-shift problem:** Bot_Risk classifications are corrected by humans more often when queue > 70, meaning an extra hidden correction workload lands on humans at exactly the moment they are most loaded — and bot-originated errors surface in *human* error statistics.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias makes worker-level rankings misleading.** Assignment is not random: seniors take complex cases early (their raw early error rate looks worse than it is), juniors inherit the late low-priority high-WIP queue (their raw late error rate looks worse than it is). Aggregate comparisons can even produce Simpson's-paradox reversals across shifts.
- **Measurement error and ascertainment.** `error_flag` only records *detected* errors. Because Quality Check is delayed to shift end, late-shift events are scrutinized more — part of the 7.9% at position 76+ is detection timing, not behavior. Batching can hide errors entirely. Bot_Risk errors surface as *human* corrections, inflating human error counts.
- **Queue pressure is substantially management-created.** WIP trajectories are driven by staffing levels, arrival forecasting, and upstream intake policy. Blaming workers for errors that occur under WIP > 70 misattributes a system property to individual behavior.
- **Automation quality is a shared responsibility.** If bot classifications degrade or humans are forced to rubber-stamp under backlog, correcting workers are performing a *control function*, not making errors. Penalizing them suppresses exactly the checking behavior you need.
- **Small samples.** Night staff are two resources with few events; their rates are noisy and prone to regression-to-the-mean. Never act on a single bad night.
- **Punitive use of error rates triggers Goodhart effects.** Once late-shift error rates carry consequences, expect underreporting, avoidance of complex and late-shift work, batching to inflate throughput, and QC deferral — each of which corrupts the very log you depend on. Use error data for blameless learning, system redesign, and trend monitoring; reserve individual accountability for adjusted, multi-period, intent-based cases.
- **Fairness across shift types.** Night work carries a circadian burden; comparing Night staff to Day staff on raw speed or error rates without structural compensation is inequitable.
