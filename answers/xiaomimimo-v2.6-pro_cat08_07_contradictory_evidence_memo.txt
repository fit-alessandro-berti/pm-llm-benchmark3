# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### 3.1 High fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51)
Fitness measures how much of the observed log the model can replay. Precision measures how much of the behaviour the model *permits* is actually legitimate. Under Redesign R a large share of cases collapses onto one short coordinator path (merged validate/schedule  installation  activation  billing). A model that generates this path plus many optional branches will replay almost every observed trace  high fitness. But the same model also permits combinations the process should never produce — skip survey *and* bill before activation *and* trigger manual activation *and* raise a second visit, in almost any order. Those never/rarely observed permutations are exactly what precision penalises. The signature "high fitness + low precision" is textbook **over-generalisation**. Two aggravators: (a) the redesign deliberately deleted guardrail steps (survey, permit check), so the remaining ordering constraints are weak; (b) sparse start timestamps mean events are effectively modelled at completion granularity, collapsing concurrency and interleaving information and further reducing precision. Baseline was more constrained (more mandatory steps), hence precision 0.79. In short: the model fits what happened; it does not describe what is allowed.

### 3.2 Improved median but worse tail
These are different statistics of a mixture distribution, and the redesign changed the mixture in two opposite ways.

*Median effect (down):* for the modal, low-risk case the redesign removed pure waiting — merged validate/schedule, fewer handovers, no field survey. This is a location shift of the fast component, and it dominates the median. Simple houses alone show 6 days median.

*Tail effect (up):* the deleted guardrails used to catch problems *before* the customer felt them. Without them, failures surface as failed visits, second visits, manual activation and post-billing complaint escalation — all late, and all long. Billing start  complaint escalation is explicitly described as "a new long-tail relation."

*Queueing effect:* coordinator utilisation reached 93%. As utilisation 1, waiting time scales roughly as /(1), so the last few percentage points of load produce disproportionately large delays at the extreme right of the distribution while barely moving the median. The evidence is consistent: failed-visit  second-visit delay lengthened specifically in regions with high coordinator utilisation.

*Case-mix effect:* pilot regions over-represent apartments and older buildings, the segments with the worst p90/p95. Part of the tail deterioration is composition, not treatment — but since the redesign is intended for a mixed population, this is a real operational problem regardless of attribution.

### 3.3 Fewer handovers but lower resilience
Handovers are costly coordination points *and* redundant context-replication points. Removing them removes both. The handover-of-work network became simpler (34%12% of cases with 3+ handovers), which is the intended accountability gain. But the working-together network became *more* centralised on coordinators and activation specialists, i.e. the coordination work did not disappear — it concentrated. Each case is now single-threaded through one coordinator who is the sole holder of case context. That is efficient when the coordinator is present and loaded normally, and fragile when they are absent or at 93% utilisation. The evidence is direct: cases stalled when a coordinator was absent because context was not shared, and activation specialists report unclear ownership after billing start. Accountability improved; redundancy and bus-factor worsened.

### 3.4 Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost
The two cost figures use different accounting boundaries, and the redesign moved work across that boundary.

The €184€161 measure is "cost before complaint handling" — it captures scheduling, survey, install and activation labour but truncates at the point where rework typically begins. The redesign deliberately moved some activation checks *after* billing start, i.e. past the measurement boundary. Rework therefore shows up as second visits, manual activation, complaint handling and billing disputes — outside the €161 figure but inside the €207 estimate.

Second, the measurement window itself truncates: cost data excludes downstream complaint handling after week 4 of a 6-week pilot, while complaints can occur within 30 days of close. So the €207 figure is itself conservative; the true full cost is unknown but the direction of travel is unfavourable.

Third, capacity economics: field-survey utilisation fell 78%52% (freed, partly idle capacity) while second-visit utilisation rose 66%92% (new bottleneck). The organisation bought cheaper prevention and more expensive correction. Total resource consumption may have risen even though the visible line item fell.

### 3.5 Simple houses improve; apartments and old buildings deteriorate
This is treatment-effect heterogeneity on a heterogeneous population, not a contradiction.

* The redesign's interventions are **risk-classification-dependent**. Merging validate/schedule and skipping survey only help when the classification is right. For simple, low-risk houses it is right (median 6d, p90 8d, FTR +3pp, complaints 1pp). For medium-risk apartments the skip rule is described as "often wrong", so the survey that would have caught installation problems is removed  failed visits (13%19%), second visits, manual activation, complaints.
* For older, permit-sensitive buildings the harm comes from a different mechanism: permit checks not observed (move-on-model alignment)  +25 days p90, 11pp FTR, +7pp complaints. This is a missing control, not a mis-targeted one.
* Business customers are hit by a third mechanism: manual activation (7%15%) creates billing disputes because activation now trails billing.
* Rural cases lose the resilience benefit of distributed coordination when rescheduling is needed.

In short: Redesign R is a **one-size** process applied to a **varied-risk** population. Aggregating across segments produces a weighted average that hides a very large benefit in 34% of volume and a very large harm in 47%. Because pilot regions over-represent the harmed segments, the aggregate comparison is also composition-confounded in both directions — the simple-house benefit is understated and the harm is partly, though not wholly, compositional.

# 4. Decision alternatives

### A. Scale Redesign R nationally
**Advantages.** Fastest capture of the demonstrated median and handover gains; single process, simpler governance and training; maximum coordinator-network effect; visible cost line improves (€184€161); strong fit with the "fewer handovers = accountability" thesis.
**Risks.** Irreversibly extends a rework loop (FTR 6pp, second visit +7pp, manual activation +8pp) to national volume; extends the billing-before-activation exposure, which is a customer-harm and potentially regulatory issue; pushes p95 from 45 to 62 days to ~all regions; estimated full cost is already worse (€207 vs €198) on incomplete data; coordinator model is a single point of failure at 93% utilisation; discovered model precision 0.51 means we cannot even specify what the process is allowed to do, let alone govern it at scale.
**Reasonable only if.** The tail, rework and billing-before-activation findings are shown to be pilot artefacts (case mix + incomplete measurement), and the survey-skip and post-billing-activation mechanisms are fixed *before* scaling. Not defensible today.

### B. Roll back to baseline
**Advantages.** Restores guardrails (survey, permit check, activation-before-billing); restores model precision and conformance clarity; removes the new long-tail billingcomplaint relation; eliminates coordinator single-point-of-failure.
**Risks.** Forfeits a real, segment-verified benefit for the 34% simple-house share (median 6d, p90 8d, FTR +3pp, complaints 1pp) and the validated handover reduction; reinstates the "waiting for appointment" complaint driver; costs credibility with regions already reorganised; treats heterogeneous evidence as homogeneous.
**Reasonable only if.** The harm is shown to be diffuse across all segments (contradicted by segment data) or the compliance exposure cannot be mitigated any other way.

### C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation
**Advantages.** Keeps the component with the cleanest evidence (handovers 34%12%, accountability) while removing the two components with direct causal signatures in the alignment and DFG evidence (billing-before-activation; survey-skip misfires). Simple to explain and to govern. Directly addresses the compliance-like issues without discarding the redesign. Preserves merged validate/schedule benefit if scoped to simple cases.
**Risks.** Leaves apartments and old buildings on an unvalidated process (their harm may be permit-driven, which C addresses only if permit checks are reinstated explicitly — the alternative as stated does not say so); does not resolve the coordinator capacity/resilience problem (93% utilisation, absence stalls); does not generate the segment-level causal evidence needed to re-enable skipping later; may be over-conservative if the skip rule is actually accurate for some strata.
**Reasonable if.** Management wants one simple rule and can accept losing potential upside in medium-risk strata; or if the building-risk rule is shown (M6) to be uninformative.

### D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement
**Advantages.** Produces the causal, stratified evidence the current observational pilot cannot (M1-style randomisation removes the case-mix confound); improves the attribute base that is missing for weeks 1–2; supports later precision targeting; keeps options open.
**Risks.** Delays value capture by 2–3 months while simple-house gains are already proven; adds experimental complexity and governance load to an already-stretched coordinator organisation; experiment regions may themselves be harmed if controls are not applied concurrently; randomisation of a suspected-harmful treatment in the apartment/old-building strata raises customer-harm ethics unless a safety gate is applied.
**Reasonable if.** The measurement base is too poor to target confidently and management is willing to forgo near-term gains; must be paired with the safety controls regardless.

### E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses only; add controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale
**Advantages.** Captures the proven 34%-segment benefit immediately; confines the treatment to the only stratum with uniformly positive evidence; explicitly installs the missing guardrails (permit check, activation-before-billing) where the evidence shows they are load-bearing; keeps coordinator ownership (the unambiguous win) but with capacity caps; is compatible with — and in fact sequences — the measurement programme; is reversible and stratum-gated.
**Risks.** Two processes to run and govern (segmentation overhead); requires reliable pre-install risk classification, which is exactly what is missing in weeks 1–2 — so eligibility rules must default to "treat as high-risk" where attributes are absent; slower and less decisive than A or B; leaves 47% of volume on a slower path for now; risks internal political pressure to expand early.
**Reasonable if.** Management accepts segmented rollout and invests in classification quality. **This is the recommended option.**
