# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Board, Fiber Operations
**Subject:** Decision on Redesign R — recommend Option E (segment-gated hybrid), not national scaling or rollback

**Recommendation.** Deploy Redesign R now only for simple, low-risk houses; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation everywhere else; keep coordinator ownership but add capacity and backup coverage; and run a short, targeted measurement program (60–90 days) before deciding on wider scale. This is Option E, incorporating the safeguards of Option C and the measurement discipline of Option D.

**Why not scale nationally (A).** The pilot's headline win — median cycle time down from 14.2 to 10.8 days and 3+ handover cases down from 34% to 12% — is real but concentrated in one segment. Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume) improved on every metric. The other two-thirds of cases deteriorated: p95 cycle time rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. Two specific mechanisms drive the damage: (1) the survey-skip rule misfires for medium-risk apartments (p90 +12 days, first-time-right −8pp) and permit-sensitive old buildings (p90 +25 days, complaints +7pp), where alignment diagnostics show expected permit checks not observed; and (2) post-billing activation creates "billing before working service" — billing-start-before-activation-test is a frequent log deviation, manual activation doubled to 15%, and billing-to-complaint escalation grew from 3% to 8% of edge traffic. Scaling this nationally would export a compliance and customer-harm problem alongside the speed gain.

**Why not roll back (B).** Rollback destroys a large, verified benefit for a third of volume (−6 days median, −8 days p90, +3pp first-time-right, fewer complaints) and discards organizational learning. The evidence supports a targeting problem, not a failed concept.

**The trade-off we are accepting.** We trade some short-term average speed (median will rise modestly when surveys and pre-billing activation are reinstated for risky segments) for tail control, first-time-right quality, billing integrity, and complaint reduction. Given that estimated full cost including complaint handling already flipped from a saving (EUR 184 → 161 visible) to a loss (EUR 198 → 207 estimated, and that estimate is incomplete after week 4), the "fast but broken" version of R is likely value-destroying outside simple houses.

**Cautions on the evidence itself.** Three measurement gaps mean we should not over-read either direction: pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, so raw comparisons are biased against R; downstream complaint costs are missing after week 4, so true pilot cost is uncertain and probably worse than EUR 207; and the pilot process model's precision collapsed (0.79 → 0.51), meaning the process has become less controlled — the model permits many behaviors — so some "improvement" may be unmeasured variance rather than design.

**Immediate actions (next 2 weeks).**
1. Suspend survey skipping for apartments and old buildings; require permit check for permit-sensitive addresses (hard gate).
2. Suspend billing start before a passed activation test in all segments; billing integrity is a customer-trust and revenue-assurance issue.
3. Keep coordinator ownership, but cap utilization (~85%), add a paired-backup protocol and shared case notes so absences don't stall cases.
4. Continue R end-to-end for simple low-risk houses; publish its results as the reference case.

**Measurement before wider scale (60–90 days).** Prioritize: M6 (validate the risk rule against survey outcomes — it gates the biggest lever), M1 (randomized survey skipping by risk stratum — the only way to get causal evidence), M4 (full 60-day downstream cost), M7 (conformance monitoring on billing-before-activation and missing permit checks), and M3 (reason codes for failed visits and manual activation).

**Threshold for broader rollout.** Extend survey skipping to a segment only when a randomized test shows first-time-right within 2pp of baseline, 30-day complaints within 1pp, p90 no worse than baseline, and full 60-day cost per case at or below baseline for that segment, with conformance monitoring showing near-zero billing-before-activation and missing-permit events. Absent that, R remains a simple-house redesign — which is still worth roughly a third of national volume.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures whether the model can replay observed traces; precision measures whether the model forbids unobserved behavior. The pilot model (fitness 0.94, precision 0.51) replays almost everything because it is permissive: after the redesign, real cases combine skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits, and resequenced billing in many orders, so discovery produces a "flower-like" model that allows far more paths than were seen. High fitness here is not good news — paired with low precision it means the process has become behaviorally unconstrained. A well-controlled process yields models that are both fitting and precise, like the baseline (0.87/0.79).

**Improved median but worse tail.** These describe different parts of the case population. The median is dominated by the 34% simple-house segment plus other easy cases, which R genuinely accelerates (merged validation/scheduling, no survey, fewer handovers). The tail (p90/p95) is dominated by cases where R's shortcuts backfire: a wrongly skipped survey produces a failed visit, a second visit queued behind a 92%-utilized second-visit team, a missing permit discovered late, or a post-billing complaint escalation. Speeding up the easy 60% while adding rework loops to the hard 40% moves the median down and the tail up simultaneously — no contradiction, just a distribution that got faster in the middle and fatter on the right.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handover count measures how often work changes hands; resilience measures the system's tolerance to disruption. Concentrating each case in one coordinator eliminates queue delays and accountability gaps, but it also concentrates case context in one head. The social-network evidence shows the working-together network centralized around coordinators; when one is absent, no one else can act, so cases stall. The baseline's "wasteful" handovers were also implicit knowledge-sharing and redundancy. Efficiency and robustness traded off directly.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost.** The redesign cuts costs that are measured early and per-case (fewer surveys, fewer scheduling touches: EUR 184 → 161), while generating costs that appear later and elsewhere (second visits, manual activation effort, complaint handling, billing disputes: estimated EUR 198 → 207 — and the pilot estimate excludes complaint handling after week 4, so it is a floor, not a ceiling). Cost was displaced downstream and partly outside the measurement window, exactly where the accounting is weakest.

**Improvement for simple houses, deterioration for apartments/old buildings.** R's mechanisms assume low case complexity: merged validate/schedule assumes clean addresses, survey skipping assumes predictable sites, post-billing activation assumes signals rarely fail. Simple low-risk houses satisfy those assumptions and gain on every metric. Apartments violate the survey assumption (shared infrastructure, in-building wiring), and old buildings violate the permit assumption — the alignment diagnostics show exactly these two step-omissions as the dominant deviations. The same design is a fit for one segment and a mismatch for others; the pilot's apartment-heavy region mix amplified the visible damage.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
- *Advantages:* Captures median-cycle-time and handover gains immediately at full volume; organizational momentum; simple message.
- *Risks:* Exports worsened tails, −6pp first-time-right, +3.7pp complaints, billing-before-activation exposure, and missing permit checks to the whole country; coordinator model already at 93% utilization would likely break at scale; full cost is probably negative (EUR 207+, incomplete).
- *Reasonable when:* Only if segment analysis were wrong and aggregate deterioration were fully explained by region mix — the segment table shows it is not. Not defensible now; it also violates the constraint of addressing tail/rework/compliance issues before scaling.

**B. Roll back to baseline.**
- *Advantages:* Restores known first-time-right, complaint, and compliance performance; relieves the overloaded coordinator and second-visit teams; simple to execute.
- *Risks:* Destroys a large verified gain for 34% of volume (−6 days median, −8 days p90, +3pp FTR, −1pp complaints); wastes learning and demoralizes the pilot organization; ignores that harm is concentrated in identifiable, controllable mechanisms.
- *Reasonable when:* If targeted controls (permit gates, reinstated surveys, pre-billing activation) failed to stop the complaint/rework deterioration within one to two months, or if regulatory exposure from billing-before-activation demanded immediate cessation.

**C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
- *Advantages:* Keeps the least controversial element (handovers 34% → 12%) while removing the two mechanisms most clearly tied to harm; low compliance risk.
- *Risks:* Coordinator model itself is unproven at scale — 93% utilization, absence stalls, and increased failed-visit → second-visit delay in high-utilization regions suggest the ownership element is not risk-free; scaling it nationally before capacity and backup design is tested repeats the pilot's mistake in milder form; also forfeits the survey-skip upside for simple houses, where it demonstrably works.
- *Reasonable when:* If management needs one national standard process and cannot operate segment-differentiated flows; acceptable second choice after fixing coordinator capacity.

**D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
- *Advantages:* Strongest evidence quality: randomization removes the region-mix confound; fixes the attribute and cost-coverage gaps before committing capital.
- *Risks:* Delays capturing the simple-house benefit that is already well-evidenced; leaves current pilot regions running a partially harmful process during the study unless paired with immediate safeguards; costs time and analyst capacity.
- *Reasonable when:* If leadership judges the simple-house evidence too weak to act on (e.g., suspects novelty effects), or if the organization cannot implement segment gating operationally.

**E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale.**
- *Advantages:* Acts on the strongest evidence (simple-house gains) while stopping the identified harm mechanisms; keeps optionality — every element can still be scaled later with evidence; directly addresses tail, rework, and compliance issues; aligns measurement spend (M6, M1, M4, M7, M3) with the decisions it would change.
- *Risks:* Operational complexity of running two process variants (routing rules must be reliable — and the building-risk attribute itself is what misfired); segment gains may partly reflect pilot attention; requires disciplined governance to prevent "shortcut creep" back into risky segments.
- *Reasonable when:* Now — it is the best fit to the current evidence: benefits are segment-specific and mechanisms of harm are identified and controllable.

**Verdict:** E, with C's safeguards embedded and D's measurement rigor applied to the suspended components.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by decision value — how much each measurement could actually change the choice among A/B/C/E and the scaling threshold:

1. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** Resolves the single biggest uncertainty: whether survey skipping is salvageable. Cheap (retrospective: compare rule predictions with actual survey findings and failed-visit outcomes). If the rule has high false-negative rates for apartments, survey skipping is dead outside simple houses (locks in E/C); if it can be recalibrated by stratum, the largest cost lever revives. Directly changes which segments ever get the skip.
2. **M1 — Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.** The only causal evidence on the skip rule's net effect, free of region-mix confounding. Complements M6 (M6 says whether the rule *could* work; M1 says whether it *does* in the field). If randomized results show acceptable first-time-right and complaint deltas in a stratum, that stratum crosses the rollout threshold — this measurement literally operates the decision gate in Section 6.
3. **M4 — Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start.** The business case currently flips sign depending on incomplete data (EUR −23 visible vs +9 estimated). If true full cost of R is favorable even including complaints, tolerance for the quality dip rises and scaling accelerates; if strongly unfavorable, even the simple-house deployment needs re-checking. Changes the economic threshold for every other decision.
4. **M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit-check absence.** Converts the two compliance-like failures from retrospective findings into real-time controls. High value because these are the non-negotiable risks (revenue assurance, regulatory permits); monitoring verifies the suspension actually holds and provides the "near-zero deviations" evidence required for any broader rollout. Less a decision-changer than a decision-enabler.
5. **M3 — Reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, Second visit.** Explains *why* rework doubled: skipped survey vs. crew skill vs. equipment vs. scheduling. Determines whether fixes are process-design changes or execution/training changes — i.e., whether C-style suspension is sufficient or the coordinator flow itself needs redesign. Moderate cost, high diagnostic leverage.
6. **M2 — Instrument start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, Manual activation.** With completion-only timestamps we cannot separate waiting time from service time, so we cannot tell whether tail growth is queueing (capacity problem → hire/rebalance) or work complexity (design problem → change process). Valuable, but it refines *how* to fix the tail rather than *whether* to scale.
7. **M5 — Coordinator vs shared-team ownership under absence.** Addresses the resilience risk before national scaling of the ownership model. Important for the C/E long-run design (backup pairs vs pods), but the near-term decision already includes backup protocols regardless of outcome, so its marginal decision impact is medium.
8. **M8 — Separate CX measurement for "speed to appointment" vs "working service after installation".** Confirms the interpretation of the NPS drop and the complaint-mix shift, useful for communication and prioritization, but it is unlikely to change the decision: operational metrics (first-time-right, complaints, second visits) already capture the same signal more directly.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Adopt Option E — deploy Redesign R for simple low-risk houses only, suspend the harmful components elsewhere, harden the coordinator model, and gate any expansion on a 60–90 day measurement program.**

- **Immediate action (weeks 0–2):** Reinstate mandatory field surveys for apartments, old buildings, business, and rural segments; enforce a hard permit-check gate for permit-sensitive addresses; stop all billing starts prior to a passed activation test; cap coordinator utilization near 85% with a paired-backup and shared-notes protocol; rebalance capacity from the underused survey team (52% utilization) to clear the second-visit backlog (92% utilization).
- **What to pause:** Survey skipping outside simple low-risk houses; post-billing activation everywhere; any expansion of the pilot to new regions until the measurement program reports.
- **What to scale:** The full R flow (merged validate/schedule, coordinator ownership, survey skip) for simple low-risk houses across all regions, since this segment improved on median (−6.0 days), tail (p90 −8.0 days), first-time-right (+3pp), and complaints (−1pp); coordinator ownership more broadly, but only with the utilization cap and backup design.
- **What to measure next:** M6 immediately (rule validation, retrospective); M1 as a randomized stratified experiment; M4 (60-day full downstream cost); M7 (live conformance monitoring on billing-before-activation and permit absence); M3 (reason codes). M2, M5, M8 follow as second wave.
- **Threshold for broader rollout:** Extend any suspended component to a segment only when randomized evidence for that segment shows, over at least 8 weeks: first-time-right within 2pp of its baseline; 30-day complaints within 1pp; p90 cycle time no worse than baseline; full 60-day cost per case at or below baseline; and conformance monitoring showing near-zero billing-before-activation and missing-permit deviations. Additionally, the discovered model for the scaled process should recover precision toward baseline levels (≥ ~0.70), indicating the process is behaviorally under control, not merely fast on average.

This path keeps the verified value of the redesign, stops the identified harm mechanisms within two weeks, and buys exactly the information that would justify — or refute — a national rollout, rather than betting the network-wide customer experience on a six-week, region-confounded pilot.
