# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed “faster over the shift” pattern is not a single phenomenon. Several mechanisms operate simultaneously, and the aggregates show different mechanisms dominate at different times and for different worker groups.

Early in a shift, workers inherit a complex, high-priority backlog (“carryover,” seniors taking complex cases early). That increases mean service time mechanically (higher complexity and more exceptions) and can elevate early errors/rework because difficult cases are less routine. At the same time, there is a plausible warm-up component: from sequence bins 1–5 to 16–30, both service time and errors decline while the queue is falling. That combination (faster and fewer errors under easing pressure) is consistent with ramp-up, settling into tools/workflow, and reduced coordination friction once the shift stabilizes.

Mid-shift, the system appears closest to a steady regime: complexity and priority share continue to drop, queues are moderate, and errors are near their minimum. Service time reaches its best-quality region around bins 16–50, where work is easier and pressure is not extreme.

Late in the shift, the key change is that queue pressure rises sharply (queue length and WIP increase) while case complexity continues to trend downward. The late regime therefore mixes pressure-driven speed-up (shorter service time) with a speed–quality tradeoff (higher errors and rework) that is strongest for juniors and mid-level staff and for high-complexity cases processed under high WIP. Workarounds amplify the pattern: Document Review batching and end-of-shift Quality Check bursts compress timestamps and shift error detection toward the end of the shift. Automation adds another interaction: Bot_Risk corrections rise when queues are high, creating additional correction work and potentially inflating late error/rework counts.

This explains why service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions. Service time falls partly from genuine warm-up early, but late-shift speed is also plausibly a pressure response plus batching. Errors rise primarily when high WIP/queue and end-of-shift practices (delayed QC, batching, reliance on automation) coincide. Fatigue may contribute, but it is interacting with pressure and operational design rather than acting alone.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- Selection bias makes worker comparisons dangerous: seniors take complex cases early; night shift has urgent exceptions; late shift operates under higher WIP. Raw late-shift error rates can reflect assignment and pressure, not individual negligence.
- Queue pressure is partly managerial: staffing, routing, and demand-peaks create WIP > 70 episodes. Blaming workers for quality degradation under systemic overload is ethically and operationally flawed.
- Measurement and timing artifacts matter: delayed Quality Check shifts when errors are recorded; batching can compress timestamps and understate effort, creating misleading “efficiency.”
- Automation changes what “error” means: increased Bot_Risk corrections under high queue can reflect distribution shift or reduced oversight. Audit automation performance and oversight before attributing blame to humans.
- Punitive use of error rates is harmful: it encourages gaming (delayed logging, avoiding hard cases), suppresses reporting, and reduces psychological safety. Quality improves more reliably via capacity management, support, and feedback.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Create a dashboard that jointly tracks throughput, quality, pressure, mix, and fairness. Include at least these indicators (trendable over time and sliceable by `shift_id`, `activity_family`, and experience group):

1. Service time by activity and shift position: median/p90 of `service_time_min` by `concept:name` × `sequence_position_within_shift` bins, split by experience group.
2. Waiting time / queueing delay: case/event waiting time from `insert_case_service_waiting_time` and/or inter-event gaps to show whether faster service hides longer waits.
3. WIP and queue length trajectories: time series of `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start`, plus share of events with WIP > 70 and mean duration of high-WIP episodes.
4. Error rate (risk-adjusted): `error_flag` rate stratified by WIP band and complexity band; also report adjusted rates from a model controlling for complexity/priority/activity.
5. Rework indicators: `rework_flag` rate, rework within 7 days (if derived), and time-to-rework; show concentration by activity using `get_rework_cases_per_activity`.
6. Complexity and priority mix: distribution of `case_complexity_score` and high-priority share by hour since shift start and by shift_id to highlight selection effects.
7. Batching indicators: batch frequency/size from `discover_batches`, burstiness (run lengths of same activity per resource), and “end-of-shift compression” for QC.
8. Handover / queue ownership changes: distinct resources per case, handover counts (resource changes along a case), and shifts in which resources pick up queued work as WIP rises.
9. Worker-load fairness: per-resource workload volume and complexity-weighted workload; inequality metrics across resources and experience groups; share of complex cases handled by juniors vs seniors.
10. Automation interaction: Bot_Risk correction rate and time-to-correction stratified by WIP/queue and time-of-day.

Recommended visuals:
- Events distribution graph to detect bursts and shift boundaries.
- Dotted chart by resource/activity to reveal batching and end-of-shift QC bursts.
- Performance DFGs split by WIP regime (low vs high) to show regime-dependent bottlenecks and waiting-time shifts.
