# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## 2.1 How I read the log (evidence and two data caveats)

**Evidence used:**
- Queue inflation peak/normal: Registration ×4.0, Pre-check ×5.3, Accession correction ×4.7, Preparation ×4.4, Assay ×4.5, Technical validation ×4.6, **Medical validation ×11.0**, **Specialist review ×7.2**, Courier ×7.5. The inflation factor grows monotonically toward the tail of the process. This is not a uniform slowdown — it is a **bottleneck migrating downstream as each upstream station is relieved**.
- Utilizations at peak already exceed the stable operating envelope: Technical validation **101%** (a hard deficit — its queue grows linearly, not just queue-theoretically), Pre-check 96%, Assay 98%, Accession correction 91%, Prep 89%, Courier 88%, MV 93%, Specialist 87%.
- Long edges that create the p90 tails: Batch wait  Assay run **22 h**, Technical validation  MV **24 h**, Hold for specialist review  MV **48 h**, Request recollection  Receive sample **60 h**. Four edges carry most of the tail; only two of them are "queue at a station".
- Class tail spread (p90  median) roughly doubles at peak: Routine 30 h  58 h, Urgent 8 h  16 h, Complex 54 h  99 h, Repeat 62 h  95 h. Tail spread is the right objective function, not the median.

**Caveat 1 (capacity sizing is not reconcilable from the table alone):** MV arrivals rise +158%/wk but MV utilization rises only +60% (5893%); Technical validation arrivals rise +22% but utilization rises +33%; specialist holds rise ×5.7 but utilization only ×2.0. Either the roster was augmented in peak (overtime/temporary staff not logged) or per-case service time changed with mix. **Do not size capacity from the utilization column**; size from arrivals ÷ rostered hours and re-derive the roster.

**Caveat 2 (the peak extract has unbalanced edges):** Technical validation inflow 7,240 vs outflow 8,080 (+840, +11.6%), and assay visits 720/9,220 have no logged successor at peak vs 2.9% at normal. Also 10.7% (normal) / 13.3% (peak) of registered orders never reach ReleaseEND or CancelEND. Part of this is real accumulated WIP at the drum (useful!), part is likely top-N edge filtering or re-entry after repeat loops without a logged edge. **Fix the logging before trusting any capacity model**, and add explicit events for *batch id / batch wait* and *tube-level assay runs* (the log is flattened by order, so a Complex panel with 6 tubes is invisible as machine load — this underestimates assay occupancy exactly where it matters).

## 2.2 S1 (mix shift): the migration chain

**Chain:** Courier intake  Pre-analytical check  Accession correction  Preparation/Batch wait  Assay machines  Technical validation  Medical validation  Specialist review.

- The front is not the constraint in absolute terms (Registration 85%), but it is the **amplifier**: Pre-check at 96% with 9.5 h queue delays defect detection, so recollections start late (60 h loop) and 47% of them are cancelled. Each recollection also removes clerk capacity from intake, deepening the intake queue.
- **Relieving Pre-check (I6) does not reduce total work** — pre-check failures never reached preparation anyway. It converts a 9.5 h + 60 h delay into a 60 h delay. Its real value is (a) load-shedding at the 96% pre-check station and (b) starting the recollection clock earlier. Downstream, it *increases* the arrival rate at preparation/assay by pulling forward cases that would otherwise sit in the pre-check queue.
- **Relieving Assay (I3 or more machines) is the classic trap.** Technical validation is already at 101%. Assay  TV is 15 h at peak; every hour removed at the assay becomes an hour added to the TV queue and, because TV is over capacity, it becomes *permanent* backlog. The observed TV queue (3.5 h  16 h) is the visible part of a demand/capacity deficit.
- **Relieving Technical validation (I4) is worse still**: it moves two validators into accession correction while TV is at 101%, producing a bigger deficit at TV and accelerating arrivals into MV (93%). It is a net-negative under S1 unless TV time is backfilled.
- **Relieving MV (I5) moves the queue to specialist review.** Complex cases are 3.1× more likely to need MV and 2.4× more likely to hit Hold for specialist review; at peak, holds grow ×5.7 to 203/wk against a single specialist with an every-second-Friday absence. The Hold  MV edge (48 h) plus MV queue (22 h) is where p90 = 210–240 h is manufactured. **MV is only a bottleneck until you fix it; then specialist review is the bottleneck, and it has no second person.**
- Strict Urgent-first (I1) starves Complex and Routine: Routine p90 (already 120 h peak vs 72 h target) breaches systematically, Complex WIP swells (Little's law: Complex WIP  436 cases at peak), and WIP growth itself raises repeat probability and storage/traceability risk. Starvation also produces the "hidden" failure: Routine cases arrive at MV/TV late and in a lump, creating a second wave.

## 2.3 S2 (machine degradation): the repeat amplifier and the double wave

- The repeat loop is a **capacity multiplier**, not a rework line. With repeat rate r, required assay starts = 1/(1r): 1.18 at r = 15%, 1.28 at r = 25%. A 6 h M1 outage ( 25% of a day's M1 capacity) plus a repeat rate at 22% removes roughly 30% of effective assay capacity for the day.
- **I3 (hard split M1 = Urgent/Complex, M2 = Routine) is actively harmful here.** It concentrates *all* Routine volume on the higher-failure machine. Peak Routine = 1,386 cases/wk; at even a 12% repeat rate that is ~190 extra case-runs/wk consuming M2 twice, i.e. the "efficiency" of the split is paid back as rework. Meanwhile M1 receives 957 Urgent + 660 Complex per week, and Complex panels consume **multiple assay runs** (invisible in the flattened log)  M1 can be the *more* loaded machine despite fewer cases. The split is balanced by case count, not by run-length × failure risk. Result: M1 queue up, Urgent SLA worse, Routine rework up.
- **I2 (batch 12  24) is the second trap.** Batching improves setup efficiency, but (a) the logged Batch wait is 88–98% explained by Urgent cases that failed the bypass, so longer batch cycles directly convert Urgent cases into guaranteed 18 h breaches (Batch wait path costs 14 h + 22 h = 36 h median at peak); (b) a batch-level setup/reagent error invalidates 24 samples instead of 12 — **variance of loss scales with batch size**, exactly what you cannot afford when the failure rate is already ×2.2; (c) it delays the detection of a failing run, so the repeat wave is bigger and later.
- **Bottleneck migration:** machine  repeat queue  then a **double wave at TV/MV ~26–40 h later** (Repeat  Assay 26 h + Assay  TV 15 h). Because repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 usage, the wave is periodic and lands on the day shift, colliding with the normal arrival tide. Urgent cases that hit a repeat cannot meet 18 h at all (repeat path alone: 8 + 26 + 15 = 49 h).
- Observed evidence of the amplification: assay first-pass yield 93.3%  85.1%; repeats/wk 142  420; Repeat-after-failure class 184  297/wk; overall rework 7.5%  11.8% (superlinear to volume).

## 2.4 S3 (validator / specialist absence): serial tail queues compound

- MV arrivals are driven by Complex + flagged results + holds ( 530/wk  1,367/wk), i.e. MV share of cases 23%  41%. Removing one validator turns 93% into 120%+  backlog grows by ~0.3 × MV demand per day,  60–80 cases/day in S1-like mix.
- **Relieving upstream makes S3 worse.** Every hour saved at assay/TV simply places another case in front of a pool that cannot serve it. Worse, TV is at 101%, so the TV  MV edge (24 h) grows too: the case pays both queues serially.
- **Specialist review is the terminal single point of failure**: 36 h median hold + 48 h Hold  MV edge, one specialist, unavailable every second Friday, Complex 2.4× more likely to enter. A Complex case in S3 pays hold queue + MV queue serially  p90 210–240 h is structural, not exceptional.
- **I10 (outsourcing) is the only lever that removes work from this node**, but it trades a 36–48 h internal queue for a 12 h transport + handoff queue + traceability work. Net beneficial for Complex *only if* the analytes are stable over 12 h and the partner's result harmonization is validated. Under S4 (courier delay) the 12 h becomes 20 h+, so I10 and S4 are negatively correlated — an important design dependency.
- **I5 (evening MV shift) converts a queue into a burst.** If the evening shift starts when the day TV shift ends, it receives a lump of TV output (Assay  TV is 15 h at peak  TV output is itself lumpy) and pushes a release burst into 22:00–24:00, overloading report delivery/courier and leaving the next morning's MV queue untouched. MV coverage must be aligned to the *TV output arrival curve*, not to a calendar shift.

## 2.5 S4 (courier compression/delay): arrival shape becomes a priority failure

- Intake 1 h  7.5 h  12 h+ at the spike, idle afterwards. Pre-analytical detection is delayed by the intake queue, so recollection starts 7–13 h later; the recollection loop (60 h) plus late notification raises the recancellation rate 38%  47%+, which shows up later as lost cases and clinician complaints, not as a queue metric.
- **Compression converts into batching, which converts into SLA failure.** Lumpy arrivals feed preparation in lumps  batch wait (peak 1,180 cases)  Urgent bypass rate collapses 74%  58%  every non-bypassed Urgent breaches 18 h. - Late arrivals also create the worst sample-stability exposure: samples held in transit or at intake past stability  recollection or invalid result  the Repeat-after-failure class grows (184  297/wk already).

Under S5 the chain is: **arrival compression  intake/pre-check  (defect detection late)  correction/recollect loops  assay degraded (repeat ×2.2)  TV deficit (101%)  MV deficit  specialist SPOF**, with each relieved node converting its queue into the next node's queue.

# 3. Robust redesign package

## 3.1 Design envelope (targets are class- and tail-based, not average-based)

| Class | Target | Why this target |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent | **p95  16 h**, breach rate  3% | The contract is 18 h; today's peak *median* is 17 h — i.e. ~45–55% breach at peak. Design to a 2 h buffer, not to the mean |
| Routine | **p90  72 h**, median  48 h | 72 h target; today p90 = 120 h at peak |
| Complex-panel | p90  144 h, no case > 216 h; specialist-hold p50  12 h | Today 210–240 h |
| Repeat-after-failure | p90  96 h; inflow  5% of cases | Today 132–240 h |
| Rework (recollection + repeat) |  8% even in peak | 7.5% / 11.8% today |
| System | Total WIP  750 cases;  200 cases aged > 24 h;   0.88 at assay/TV/MV,  0.85 at specialist | Today ~1,300 WIP and  = 0.96–1.01 |

The honest implication: at  = 0.98 you cannot meet tail targets by tuning. The redesign must (a) hold  down by **admission control and demand shaping**, (b) **cut rework generation** (which multiplies load), and (c) put **elasticity only at the validators**. That is the alternative to "adding capacity everywhere".

## 3.2 R1 — Bounded, deadline-driven dispatch with anti-starvation (replaces I1 "strict Urgent-first")

**What:** Three virtual lanes with **reserved capacity windows, not exclusive machines**: STAT/Urgent (deadline 18 h, earliest-deadline-first with a reserved 35% of assay slots and 2 fast validation slots per shift), Complex/specialist lane (reserved 25% of MV + a protected daily specialist window), Routine (guaranteed  40% of slots + an **aging rule**: any Routine case with < 12 h of SLA slack outranks a new Urgent with > 10 h of slack). Priority is **bounded**: no class can take more than its reservation + a 15% burst allowance per shift.

**Why robust:** it works in S1, S2, S3, S5 because it protects the contractual class and the tail-most class simultaneously while preventing the starvation that makes Routine p90 (120 h) blow past 72 h. It converts "who wins" from an argument into a rule with an explicit ordering — recognizing that **all classes cannot be first**: Urgent is first on the clock, Complex is first on the tail, Routine is protected on the floor.
**Assumptions:** dispatch can be enforced at preparation and assay (lab-grade scheduling or a simple whiteboard rule with a shift lead); Urgent share stays below ~35% (the reservation ceiling); the aging rule is understood by clinicians.
**Fails if:** Urgent share exceeds the reservation for more than one shift (then bounded priority degrades to strict priority and Routine breaches) or if the deadline field in the LIS is wrong (garbage deadlines  garbage dispatch).

**Downstream load transferred to:** Technical validation and MV — the fast lane accelerates arrivals at the 101% and 93% nodes. Must be paired with R4/R5 (validator elasticity) and R10 (WIP control), otherwise R1 alone increases TV backlog and Routine p90.

## 3.3 R2 — Lane-specific batching and small-batch failure containment (revises I2)

**What:** Batch 24 **only for Routine, only when the 3-day rolling Urgent share < 22% and no machine-degradation trigger is active**. Urgent never batches (batch  4, direct load). Complex batches by panel family  8 with an early-read check. Batch closing rule: **"closes at cadence or at size, whichever first" with a 2 h maximum cadence in peak**. Add an explicit *batch id / batch-wait* event to the log (see §2.1).

**Why robust:** preserves the setup-efficiency gain of I2 where it is safe (Routine in low-Urgent periods) while capping (a) Urgent exposure — Batch wait is 36 h median at peak, which alone breaches 18 h — and (b) the blast radius of a batch-level setup/reagent error (12 vs 24 samples).
**Assumptions:** setup time is a meaningful share of prep time (otherwise the gain is small); batch-level failure risk is non-trivial.
**Fails if:** the failure is reagent-lot-wide (then small batches do not help) or if Urgent share is volatile within a shift (the ON/OFF rule hysteresis, §4, mitigates).

**Downstream load transferred to:** assay machine scheduling (more setups, more fragmentation) and TV (more, smaller lots arriving). Monitor assay setup count/shift.

## 3.4 R3 — Capacity reservation + machine-health rule (replaces I3's hard M1/M2 split)

**What:** Do **not** dedicate machines by case type. Instead: reserve 40% of M1 run-windows for Urgent + short Complex panels and 25% for Complex spill; route by **run-length × failure risk**, not by class; hard rules on M2 — (i) preventive maintenance and recalibration before winter and after every N failure alarms, (ii) **no Complex panels and no stacked night-shift runs on M2**, (iii) a second-person check for M2 night runs. Add an "M2 degraded" mode that pulls Complex and repeat-prone work off it.

**Why robust:** the log shows repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 usage; the fix is a shift/machine rule, not a class split. Reservation keeps Urgent protected in S2 and S5 while avoiding the mass-Routine-on-M2 rework explosion. Because M2 has the *faster* runtime but higher failure rate, routing by run-length × failure risk uses both machines' strengths.
**Assumptions:** failure cause is machine/shift-related (validate with root-cause tagging — it could be reagent lot or calibration); reservation software or a shift-lead rule is enforceable.
**Fails if:** M1's slower runtime cannot absorb Complex multi-assay run-length (the flattened log hides tube-level runs — measure them first) or if the reservation strands idle M1 slots in low-Urgent hours.

**Downstream load transferred to:** TV (Urgent+Complex on the fast machine arrive at TV sooner) and, if M2 degradation continues, the repeat queue and TV again in a double wave 26–40 h later.

## 3.5 R4 — Tiered accession correction + surge pod (revises I4)

**What:** Split corrections into **Tier 1** (metadata/identifier fixes, ~the majority of the 420/wk peak corrections) and **Tier 2** (specimen/assay-impacting). Tier 1 is done by trained pre-analytical staff against a work instruction and a two-field verification; the 2 specialists keep Tier 2 and become a **correction surge pod** that is activated only when the correction queue age > 8 h *and* TV utilization (4 h rolling) < 85%. Do **not** permanently move the two cross-trainable technical validators (I4 as proposed) — TV is at 101%.

**Why robust:** it addresses the S1/S4 correction queue (3 h  14 h) which blocks many cases, without subtracting capacity from the hardest deficit in the system. Decoupling the surge from the roster makes it scenario-conditional (see §4).
**Assumptions:** corrections are genuinely tierable (validate with the correction-reason codes — currently not in the log); pre-analytical staff have slack created by R6/R7.
**Fails if:** Tier 1/2 misclassification sends specimen-impacting corrections to generalists (raises recollection rate 12.3% further) or if TV never drops below 85% (then the surge pod never activates and the correction queue is unaddressed — a design dead-end).

**Downstream load transferred to:** test preparation and assay (corrections unblock cases, accelerating arrivals at the drum) and, if misused, TV.

## 3.6 R5 — Demand-triggered evening / flexible medical-validation capacity (I5, made conditional)

**What:** A standby MV shift (and a named per-diem backup) activated by trigger (§4), **aligned to the measured TV output arrival curve** (typically 14:00–22:00), not a fixed evening block. Rules within the shift: Urgent first (deadline), Complex second, then flagged Routine. Additionally, in S3 add a "low-risk Routine fast release at TV level with 10% audit sampling" policy (quality-governed relaxation) so scarce MV time is spent where it changes outcomes.

**Why robust:** MV is the fastest-growing load (530  1,367 arrivals/wk, +158%) and its queue inflates ×11 (2 h  22 h) — the biggest single tail driver for Complex p90 (210 h) and for the Technical validation  MV 24 h edge. Elastic rather than permanent capacity matches a seasonal demand curve and does not raise fixed cost.
**Assumptions:** qualified validators are obtainable on short notice; result-comparability of a fast-release audit policy is acceptable to the quality system; the roster reconciliation gap in §2.1 is resolved (you may already be paying for undocumented overtime).
**Fails if:** activation latency is > 12 h (the queue forms faster than the shift can be staffed) or if the release burst at 22:00 overloads report delivery.

**Downstream load transferred to:** specialist review (MV is only a queue *before* the specialist SPOF) and release/report delivery + courier at night. **This is why R5 must ship with R6/R11 and the outsourcing valve.**

## 3.7 R6 — Early pre-analytical defect triage  2 h (I6) + repeat root-cause control

**What:** (a) Gross-defect triage at courier receipt (hemolysis, wrong tube, underfill, temperature excursion) with a 2 h decision and immediate recollection request; (b) mandatory **root-cause tag on every assay repeat and recollection** (machine, shift, operator, reagent lot, courier route); (c) containment rule: **one repeat maximum, then escalate** to technical/medical validation or recollection — a third run is prohibited.

**Why robust:** it cuts the two most expensive loops. Recollection costs 42–60 h *plus* clerk time on physician calls (which starves intake); repeats cost double assay capacity and a 34 h loop. With peak repeat arrivals at ~420/wk, returning first-pass yield to 93% frees ~210 assay slots/wk ( 7% of machine capacity) — capacity created by *not doing work*, which is the only kind that does not move the bottleneck.
**Assumptions:** a meaningful share of repeats are sample-quality/shift/machine driven (the night-shift and M2 clustering suggests yes); triage staff can make a reliable gross judgment in 2 h.
**Fails if:** failures are analyte-internal or reagent-lot-wide (root-cause control fails) or if triage becomes a fourth queue at intake (it must be a 2 h *decision*, not a station).

**Downstream load transferred to:** nothing (this is the only intervention that reduces downstream arrivals). Its side effect is earlier recollection requests — front-loading clinician contact work.

## 3.8 R7 — Arrival smoothing with a protected STAT lane (I7)

**What:** Renegotiate courier pickup slots to spread routes across 07:00–19:00 in  3 waves (target: no 2 h window holds > 30% of daily volume), while keeping a **continuous STAT courier lane for Urgent** (Urgent SLA clock is 18 h and the population edge Register  Receive is already 5/8 h). Add a "route-delay alert" that fires when a route is > 60 min late so triage and clinician notification start early.

**Why robust:** it is demand *shaping* — it costs no capacity and helps S1, S2, S3, S4, S5 because every downstream station benefits from a smooth arrival process (queue is convex in arrival variability, Kingman: Wq  (ca² + cs²)). It directly attacks the observed 10:00/17:00 spikes and the batch-wait/bypass collapse they cause. Courier utilization 88% peak with a 7.5 h queue is a pure variability problem.
**Assumptions:** pickup slots are contractually renegotiable; Urgent samples do not depend on the very routes being shifted (hence the STAT lane).
**Fails if:** clinicians cannot adapt ordering times (the tide moves with physician rounds), or if smoothing merely shifts the peak (validate with an arrival-concentration index, §7).

**Downstream load transferred to:** a smoother *but earlier* arrival at pre-check and preparation in the morning — i.e., it front-loads the drum's day-shift load. Pair with R10 (release control) so a smooth arrival does not become a smooth overload.

## 3.9 R8 — Automated registration with a validation gate (I8), redeployed, not accelerated

**What:** Auto-register orders with complete digital metadata **behind an auto-validation gate** (patient/sample identifiers, test codes, deadline field, duplicate check). The freed clerk time is **ring-fenced and redeployed** to (a) early defect triage (R6), (b) recollection explanation calls, (c) intake surge at the 10:00/17:00 waves. Cap registrar shifts at 9 h (error rate rises beyond that) — no overtime-based surge.

**Why robust:** registration is the least-constrained front node (85% peak, 2.8 h queue) and its true value is the *redeployable labor* it releases during S1/S4, not the cycle-time saving. It helps S1 and S4 most and is neutral elsewhere.
**Assumptions:** digital metadata is high-quality (the 420/wk peak accession corrections suggest it is not always) and the redeployment is mandated — otherwise I8 is pure acceleration into a 96% pre-check.
**Fails if:** metadata errors pass the gate and are discovered at pre-check (increasing the correction rate and re-creating the correction bottleneck) or if automation hides a class of orders with no human touch (traceability risk).

**Downstream load transferred to:** pre-analytical check and assay (arrival pull-forward). This is precisely the "I8 exposes the next bottleneck" conflict — mitigated only by the redeployment clause and by R10.

## 3.10 R9 — Cadence-capped release consolidation (revises I9)

**What:** Consolidate Routine reporting into batched releases **only** with: (i) a  4 h cadence, (ii) a hard abort if projected Routine release would exceed 60 h, (iii) never in S2 (batch errors) or S4 (late arrivals). Keep Urgent and Complex on immediate release.

**Why robust:** it reduces per-release administrative work where Routine tolerates it (stated) but cannot push Routine past its 72 h target when Routine is already at median 62 h / p90 120 h in peak. Making it abort-on-slack-risk prevents it from becoming a hidden 5th tail edge.
**Assumptions:** release work is a real constraint (measure it — it is not visible as a station in the DFG); clinician tolerance for  4 h batched Routine reports.
**Fails if:** the 4 h cadence is not enforced and becomes a daily batch (this would add up to 24 h to an already-late class), or if an abnormal Routine result inside a batch is released late in a clinical sense.

**Downstream load transferred to:** nothing downstream (it is the end) — but it **accumulates WIP at the release step** and increases sample-storage time.

## 3.11 R10 — WIP/admission control ("the rope") at test-preparation and assay — **new**

**What:** Release gates at test-preparation and assay with a **work-content cap** (hours of work, not case counts): target total WIP  750 cases and assay/MV/TV utilization  0.88. When the cap is hit, new orders are **accepted and scheduled** ("admitted, promised for …"), not silently queued; Routine scheduling slips are disclosed to clinicians with a promised release window; Urgent is never refused (it draws on its reservation).

**Why robust:** this is the only intervention that directly controls . It is the difference between  = 0.90 (queue  9 t) and  = 0.98 (queue  49 t). It works in **all** scenarios including S5 because it is scenario-independent, and it converts an unbounded, invisible queue into a visible, bounded, communicated schedule. It also protects the tail classes from the "hidden" failure mode of every other intervention — acceleration into a saturated node.
**Assumptions:** management accepts that *some* demand is deferred (this is a governance decision, not a technical one); promised windows are tracked; clinicians accept transparent scheduling.
**Fails if:** the cap is set too low (idle capacity while Routine breaches 72 h) or too high (no effect), or if the LIS cannot hold an "admitted-not-released" state. **See the redesign-caused vulnerability in §6 (shadow queue and release surge).**

**Downstream load transferred to:** by design *nothing* — that is its purpose. Its cost is moved to the demand side (expectation management).

## 3.12 R11 — Bounded external relief valve for Complex overflow (revises I10)

**What:** Outsource **only Complex overflow above a threshold** (e.g., specialist-hold WIP > 12 cases or oldest hold age > 24 h), only for a **whitelist of analytes validated as stable over 12 h**, with mandatory chain-of-custody scanning at both handoffs and harmonization checks on the first 50 results. Never used in S4 (courier delay scenario) or for cold-chain-labile analytes.

**Why robust:** it is the only lever that removes work from the system's single point of failure (one specialist, 36 h hold queue + 48 h Hold  MV edge). In S3/S5 a 12 h transport beats a 48–96 h internal queue decisively. Bounded activation prevents permanent dependency and keeps internal capability alive.
**Assumptions:** sample stability across 12 h; partner assay harmonization; the partner's own peak behavior is understood (they face the same winter wave!).
**Fails if:** the partner saturates in the same winter wave (correlated failure), if transport is delayed (S4), or if outsourced results need internal medical validation anyway (then the queue is only moved).

**Downstream load transferred to:** courier/transport, registration and traceability work at handoff, and possibly internal MV for sign-out.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Always-on baseline = R1–R11 as configured above. The policies below are **modes** with explicit ON and OFF triggers, a 24 h minimum dwell time to prevent oscillation, and at most two concurrent modes.

## 4.1 Mode A — "STAT storm" (S1)

**ON when (any 2 of):** 3-day rolling Urgent share > 26%; Urgent arrivals > 850/wk; Complex arrivals > 750/wk; MV arrivals > 1,200/wk.
**Actions:** raise Urgent reservation to 45% of assay slots and 3 fast TV slots/shift; activate R5 MV standby aligned to TV output; freeze Routine batching (R2 goes to  12) regardless of Urgent-share rule; start pre-emptive Complex overflow screening (R11 armed, not yet fired); tighten R10 caps (WIP  700).
**OFF when:** Urgent share < 22% and Complex < 650/wk for 3 consecutive days **and** Urgent p90 < 15 h.

## 4.2 Mode B — "Machine degraded" (S2)

**ON when (any):** 12 h rolling first-pass assay yield < 90% on any machine; repeat rate > 10% over 24 h; any machine down > 2 h or in alarm state; two or more M2 night-shift failures in 7 days.
**Actions:** pull Complex and repeat-prone work off the failing machine (R3); **forbid batch-size increase** (R2 forced to  8 with early-read check); enforce one-repeat-then-escalate (R6c); schedule an immediate PM/calibration window; route by run-length × failure risk; move Routine work to the reliable machine and accept slower runtime; consider controlled deferral of Routine (R10) with promised windows.
**OFF when:** first-pass yield > 93% and repeat rate < 8% for 48 h after the machine is declared healthy.

## 4.3 Mode C — "Validator & specialist scarcity" (S3)

**ON when (any):** rostered MV hours < 85% of plan for the next 48 h; MV queue > 12 h; specialist-hold WIP > 12 cases **or** oldest hold age > 24 h; the every-second-Friday specialist absence falls within 48 h.
**Actions:** activate R5 immediately (do not wait for the queue to build); enable the low-risk Routine fast-release-at-TV policy with 10% audit; **fire R11 outsourcing** when oldest hold > 36 h; protect a daily 3 h specialist window for Complex holds; pair/reschedule the second-Friday absence (put a named backup reviewer on the roster — see §6); escalate to the quality lead if MV is unavailable at all.
**OFF when:** MV queue < 6 h and hold WIP < 6 for 48 h.

## 4.4 Mode D — "Arrival compression / route delay" (S4)

**ON when (any):** > 30% of projected daily volume arrives within a 2 h window; intake queue > 4 h; any route > 60 min late; temperature-excursion alert.
**Actions:** trigger the route-delay alert and start clinician notification + recollection calls immediately (before the pre-check queue clears); move redeployed clerks (R8) to intake/triage; enforce 2 h gross-defect triage (R6a); **suspend R9 batched releases** (late arrivals must not also wait for a report batch); if the route is > 2 h late, flag affected samples for stability review and pre-emptively request recollection for labile analytes.
**OFF when:** arrival-concentration index < 30% and intake queue < 2 h for 24 h.

## 4.5 Mode E — "Compound / tail stabilization" (S5)

**ON when:**  2 of Modes A–D active, **or** forecast 24 h backlog > 250 cases, **or** any Urgent case with < 4 h slack on the 18 h clock.
**Actions:** declare stabilization. (1) Urgent reservation to 50% — the contract is the hard constraint; (2) freeze Routine admission at R10's cap with disclosed promised windows (accept, schedule, communicate); (3) fire R11 outsourcing for Complex overflow; (4) **no registrar shift > 9 h** and no untrained surge staff at pre-check (the error loop is worse than the delay); (5) publish expected release windows to clinicians (demand-side expectation management is a legitimate capacity lever); (6) hold a 30 min shift-lead bottleneck review every 8 h and re-rank only against the class ordering (Urgent clock  Complex tail  Routine floor).
**OFF when:** fewer than 2 modes active **and** total WIP < 750 **and** no Urgent case with < 8 h slack, sustained 24 h.

## 4.6 Explicit non-policies (things we will not do)

- Never run "strict Urgent-first" without the Routine aging floor (I1 as proposed is rejected as an always-on rule).
- Never increase batch size in Modes B/D/E (I2 is a normal-period-only lever).
- Never dedicate machines by case type alone (I3 replaced by R3 reservation).
- Never move technical validators to corrections while TV is above 85% (I4 replaced by R4).
- Never prioritize all four classes at once — the ordering is contractual clock first, tail class second, volume class with a floor third.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Specialist review remains a single point of failure.** Nothing in the redesign creates a second qualified reviewer; R11 only diverts whitelisted Complex overflow. A 3-day absence with Complex at 21% still produces 48 h+ holds, and the Hold  MV edge (48 h) is serial behind it. Mitigation is partial: a named second reviewer must be trained (long lead time) — this is the deepest structural fragility.

2. **Redesign-caused: the shadow queue and the release surge.** R10's admission cap converts an invisible in-process queue into an "admitted-not-released" backlog. If promised windows are not actively tracked (or if the cap is released after a disruption clears), the accumulated demand hits the assay machines in a wave and re-creates exactly the batch/tide pattern the design was meant to remove. The backlog also becomes a *reporting* problem: cycle time measured from registration will look worse even as flow improves.

3. **Redesign-caused: reservation fragmentation.** R1's and R3's reserved windows for Urgent/Complex strand idle capacity on days with lumpy STAT arrivals — Routine p90 can worsen on an otherwise quiet day. The reservation ceiling (35–50%) is a bet on the Urgent share distribution; a sustained Urgent share above ~35% silently converts bounded priority back into strict priority.

4. **Redesign-caused: the priority-inversion risk of the aging rule.** In Mode E, a Routine case that ages past its slack threshold can displace an Urgent case with 10 h of contractual slack — occasionally correct, occasionally a penalty event. The rule needs a hard "Urgent slack < 4 h always wins" override, or it becomes a source of random SLA breaches.

5. **Redesign-caused: night-work expansion raises repeats.** Any elasticity that adds evening/night activity (R5's late release burst, R3's night runs when M1 is reserved for Urgent by day) runs straight into the observed clustering of repeats after night shifts. Gains from evening MV capacity can be paid back as assay rework if night assay running increases.

6. **Redesign-caused: surge overtime at registration/pre-check raises error rates** (>9 h shifts), feeding the accession-correction and recollection loops. Mode E forbids it — but under Mode E pressure the temptation to break this rule is highest.

7. **Outsourcing creates correlated and quality exposure (R11).** The partner lab faces the same winter wave (correlated saturation), 12 h transport is exactly the asset that fails in S4, and whitelisted stability is an assumption until validated analyte-by-analyte. Traceability now spans two organizations.

8. **Data/observability fragility.** The flattened-by-order log under-counts machine load for multi-tube Complex panels; the peak extract has unbalanced TV edges (+11.6%) and 10.7–13.3% of orders with no end event; Batch wait is essentially invisible for Routine (it is 88–98% explained by Urgent bypass failures); and MV utilization cannot be reconciled with MV arrivals without knowing the peak roster. **Any capacity model built on the current log will be wrong in the direction that matters** (it will under-size the drum and under-count rework).

9. **Demand-side fragility.** All four classes cannot be protected at once. If Urgent share rises above 35% (as S1 proposes at 33%) *and* Complex rises to 21%, the reserved-capacity arithmetic no longer closes without either Routine breach or Urgent breach. The design must have a stated, pre-agreed degradation order (Urgent contract  Complex tail  Routine floor) decided before the winter wave, not in the incident.
