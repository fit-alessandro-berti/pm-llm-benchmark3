For mediation, \(g_P\) denotes a mediator distribution generated by an explicit policy, not an arbitrary mediator value; the resulting interventional analogues avoid imagining that the same case simultaneously has two flag histories. Controlled direct effects are reserved for physically separable settings such as a randomized lane assignment.

Natural direct/indirect effects are not primary: they require a meaningful individual flag intervention, positivity, no interference, no exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding, and strong cross-world independence. Even the interventional analogues require sequential exchangeability or a valid queue model and are **not identified by these 20 cases**. The observed flag-1/flag-0 contrast is descriptive.

Interference arises because priority triage and additional reviews change arrivals to shared review and senior queues; one case’s flag can therefore alter another case’s waits, and protected capacity can benefit or displace unflagged cases. Case-level SUTVA is not credible. Manual review and escalation are post-flag mediators. Their outcomes depend on evolving queue state, reviewer judgment, workload, and resource assignment, which earlier flag handling can also affect: these are exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounders. Conditioning on review, escalation, post-arrival load, or reviewer can select cases, block pathways, or open collider paths. Treatment–confounder feedback arises as queue state affects waiting and human routing, while routing and completions update subsequent queue state. Baseline `risk_score`, `complexity`, and `arrival_load` cannot adjust away these time-varying problems.

## 6. Policy comparison

For P1, the table assumes **display-only suppression with automated routing held fixed**. Disabling flag-based automation would be a different policy bundle. P4 is separated conceptually into criteria and capacity variants.

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Fast flagged triage is observed | Long manual-start and senior-waiting intervals coexist with high review/escalation occurrence | Flagged mean is longer, but causal sign is unknown | Unknown; current review may be necessary, excessive, or both | Existing priority and shared queues may already displace other work | Strong descriptive timing evidence; no identified policy effect | Retain as comparator while improving instrumentation; do not infer that the flag itself is harmful |
| P1: display suppression | Approximately zero if lane automation is fixed; otherwise uncertain | Human-initiated review or escalation could change, but score visibility may preserve behavior | Uncertain and probably smaller than removing the full routing bundle | Uncertain anchoring or loss of useful salience | Probably limited unless staff substantially change routing | Very low; no display-only contrast | Useful as a human-behavior experiment, not as `do(flag=0)`; document automation first |
| P2: retain priority, remove automatic review | Little direct change expected | Review arrivals should fall if automation binds; manual and later senior queues may improve | Likely reduction for cases avoiding review, but all-case effect remains uncertain | Highest risk of missed review or compliance failure; possible reduction of unnecessary review | Reduced review load could benefit unflagged cases; priority triage displacement remains | Plausible occurrence mechanism, but effect size and quality are unidentified | Best direct test of occurrence mediation; pilot only in eligible strata with blinded quality audits |
| P3: protected manual-review capacity | Little direct change | Flagged manual waiting should fall if capacity is binding; unflagged waiting may rise if capacity is merely reserved | Flagged cycle time may fall, but improvement can be attenuated by downstream senior congestion | Relatively low routing-risk; rushed or less-skilled staffing could harm quality | Central issue: incremental capacity differs fundamentally from redeployment | Long waits support plausibility; capacity responsiveness is untested | Preferred initial pilot if capacity is genuinely incremental and reserve capacity is released when idle |
| P4c/P4k: escalation criteria or senior capacity | Little direct change | P4c changes senior arrivals; P4k reduces senior waiting if capacity binds. Manual queue is initially unchanged | Escalated-case cycle time may fall; all-case effect depends on criteria and displaced senior work | Criteria relaxation has potentially serious quality risk; capacity addition has lower but nonzero risk | Senior staff may be diverted from other essential work | Very low: escalation comparisons are strongly complexity-selected | Specify and evaluate variants separately; defer criteria relaxation without reliable quality evidence |

The objective is not simply the shortest flagged-case mean; quality and all-case tail performance matter.

**Pilot P3, as a capacity/waiting test—not a claim that P3 is optimal.** Use genuinely additional, equivalently skilled reviewer-hours; routing and escalation rules stay fixed.

- **Eligibility:** All new arrivals in pilot periods; flagged cases follow current review rules, and unflagged cases remain in the measurement frame for spillovers. Exclude only predeclared disaster or staffing-outage periods.
- **Assignment:** Randomize matched day-shift blocks to P3 or P0, stratified by weekday and forecast load, before arrivals. Record carry-over queues and analyze by assignment.
- **Queue guardrail:** Release idle protected capacity to unflagged work. Pause if unflagged review-ready waiting p90 exceeds its baseline safety limit on two shifts, the senior queue exceeds its safe limit, or review quality deteriorates.
- **Outcomes:** Primary: flagged `review_ready``manual_start` waiting. Secondary: all-case cycle-time mean/p90, triage completion, review occurrence/service, escalation and senior waits, unflagged waits, and blinded audit quality.
- **Minimum data:** Review-ready/referral timestamps; five-minute queue lengths and staffed capacity; reviewer/senior rosters and skills; all arrivals and resources; score, complexity, and policy exposure; timezone/DST-aware timestamps; audit outcomes.
- **Stopping conditions:** Any serious compliance event, guardrail breach, more than 5% missing critical timestamps, or failure to deliver incremental capacity. Do not stop early solely for a favorable flagged-case average.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** If independent audits reveal substantial residual severity that drives both review/escalation and delay, and a valid local or randomized policy evaluation finds little downstream policy effect, the flag-mediated delay interpretation should yield to case mix. P2 would also become less acceptable if that severity makes automatic review necessary.
2. **Queue interference:** If P3 reduces flagged waiting but unflagged waiting, senior congestion, or all-case cycle-time p90 worsens enough to offset the gain, the P3 recommendation reverses; capacity should then be redesigned at system level rather than protected for flagged cases.
3. **Decision quality:** If blinded audits show that automatic review prevents material false approvals or compliance failures, P2 should be rejected despite shorter cycles. Conversely, if reviews are largely redundant and quality is unchanged, the case for testing P2 strengthens.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** If triage-donemanual-start mainly represents scheduled batching, referral lag, or data-entry conventions rather than removable queue waiting—or clock/DST handling materially changes the intervals—the manual-queue mechanism and expected P3 benefit weaken.
5. **Positivity or overlap:** If broader data still contain no flagged low-complexity cases, no comparable lane/resource assignments, or too few cases near the assignment boundary, global flagged/unflagged causal claims and general rollout recommendations should be withdrawn in favor of narrowly eligible randomized evaluations.
6. **Reviewer behavior change:** If reviewers reinstate nearly all reviews under P2, infer hidden flags under P1, alter escalation thresholds, or change service effort when capacity is added, the estimated pathways are not stable structural mechanisms. In particular, P2 may yield no queue saving, while P3 may fail despite additional nominal capacity.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** In this extract, flagged cases reach triage completion in 0.84 hours on average, versus 3.43 hours for unflagged cases, yet their mean arrival-to-close cycle is 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All ten flagged cases receive manual review and six escalate; among unflagged cases, four receive review and two escalate. Conditional manual-start delay is longer for flagged cases, while manual service time is similar. These summaries use small, selected subgroups.

**Plausible mechanism.** The flag operates as a policy bundle: priority triage shortens its own wait, then faster and more frequent review referrals front-load work into shared queues. Review occurrence, escalation occurrence, and their waiting times can outweigh the triage gain. Complexity, residual risk, staffing, and reviewer assignment may generate part or all of the same pattern.

**Unidentified.** The data do not identify `do(flag=0)`, natural direct/indirect effects, or separate queue and behavioral effects. Post-flag queue states are confounders affected by earlier handling, other cases interfere, and approve/reject is not decision quality.

**Next evidence.** Randomize matched shifts to P0 or P3 with genuinely incremental review capacity, while holding routing and escalation rules fixed. Measure review-ready time, queue states, every arrival’s waits, and downstream senior congestion. A wait reduction without worse spillovers would support the waiting mechanism and the case for evaluating total-cycle benefit; no such reduction would redirect attention to batching, timestamps, or review occurrence. P2 should then be tested only with blinded quality audits and strict stopping rules.
