## 1. Stress scenarios

**Scenario A — infection-wave mix shift.** Daily demand reaches roughly the peak-period level (about 43% more cases per week than normal), with Urgent at 28–30% and Complex-panel at 18–22%, rather than the normal 18% and 13%. There is no single equipment failure, but the wave lasts long enough for queues and rework to compound.

**Scenario B — assay degradation or loss of one machine.** M2 develops its known higher failure rate, especially on night-shift work, or M1/M2 is unavailable for a substantial shift. Repeat assays rise and the remaining machine must carry both new work and repeats. This can occur during either normal or peak demand.

**Scenario C — medical-validation or specialist absence.** A medical validator is absent for a peak shift, or the specialist-review resource is unavailable on the recurring second Friday. Complex-panel work is disproportionately affected: it is 3.1 times as likely to need Medical validation and 2.4 times as likely to enter specialist review.

**Scenario D — courier compression or delay.** A route delivers a large fraction of the day's samples at 10:00 or 17:00, or a delayed route arrives several hours late. The total daily volume may be ordinary, but it arrives in a short window and consumes urgent SLA time before downstream work can begin.

**Scenario E — compound peak disruption.** The peak mix shift coincides with one assay machine being down and a specialist absence. This is a plausible winter-wave case, and it tests whether controls still work when several apparent spare-capacity pools disappear at once.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

**Scenario A — infection-wave mix shift**

The first constraint is likely Pre-analytical check and its correction loop. Peak utilization is already 96%, with a 9.5-hour median queue. The peak DFG sends only 8,530 of 9,730 received orders directly to Test preparation, while 780 go to recollection and 420 to manual accession correction. Recollection also consumes intake capacity: 520 cases return to Receive sample, and 370 eventually cancel. Thus, a small increase in defect rate does not merely add rework; it reduces the capacity available for new samples.

The next wave is Preparation and the machines. Preparation is at 89% utilization with an 11-hour queue, and Batch wait increases from 6 to 14 hours, followed by 22 hours from Batch wait to Assay run. The lower share of urgent work bypassing Batch wait (58% at peak versus 74% normally) shows that the nominal urgent exception is already losing effectiveness. If an Urgent-first rule is applied broadly, it will fragment batches and increase machine setup loss, while Complex-panel cases wait for their multiple assays.

Assay utilization reaches 98%, then Technical validation reaches 101% and Medical validation 93%. The DFG shows repeat assays rising to 1,260 from 1,140 despite the shorter peak observation period, and Technical validation's direct-to-release path falls to 4,050 while the Medical validation and specialist paths become relatively more prominent. The queue therefore migrates from intake to preparation, then to assay and technical validation, and finally to medical/specialist review. Adding an evening medical shift alone would leave the earlier machine and technical queues unchanged; freeing Pre-analytical check without controlling dispatch would instead create an assay and validation wave.

**Scenario B — assay degradation or loss of one machine**

The immediate bottleneck is the surviving machine. At peak, the two machines collectively run at 98% utilization and have an 18-hour median queue, so even a short outage removes the small remaining buffer. The preparation queue and Batch wait then rise because prepared work cannot be consumed. If the degraded machine is M2, routing all Routine work to it (as a permanent version of I3 would do) converts machine risk into repeats: Repeat-after-failure cases already consume assay capacity twice and cluster after night shifts and M2 usage. If M1 is down, the urgent/complex protected capacity disappears and Urgent work competes with Routine on M2.

An Urgent-first dispatch rule can protect the contractual class temporarily, but it cannot create machine hours. It will age Routine and Complex-panel work, and repeated urgent interruptions may also increase setup fragmentation. Complex panels are especially vulnerable because one completed assay does not complete the case; they can accumulate partially processed WIP while waiting for later assays. Once assays resume, the next bottleneck migrates to Technical validation, already at 101% in the peak, then Medical validation and specialist review as the backlog is released. Extra validation capacity taken from accession correction would shorten the final queue only by making the correction loop longer.

**Scenario C — medical-validation or specialist absence**

This scenario exposes the right-hand tail of the process. At peak, Medical validation has a 22-hour median queue and Specialist review a 36-hour queue. The Performance DFG gives 24 hours from Technical validation to Medical validation and 48 hours from Hold for specialist review to Medical validation. The peak DFG also has 610 cases entering Hold for specialist review, more than twice the normal count after adjusting for the shorter period, while 560 return to Medical validation.

When a medical validator is absent, Technical validation can initially appear to improve because fewer cases leave it, but its output accumulates in front of Medical validation. If technical validators are simultaneously moved to accession correction, the apparent upstream relief creates a Technical validation queue and delays cases that could have been released without medical review. A temporary evening medical shift helps only if qualified reviewers, not just more scheduled hours, are available; otherwise it creates handoff and quality risk.

When the specialist is absent, routing more Complex-panel work through ordinary Medical validation overloads the same pool. Bypassing specialist review by sending cases to a partner can reduce the 36-hour queue, but adds 12 hours of transport and traceability work and may move the bottleneck to courier intake, result reconciliation, or partner acceptance. A strict Urgent-first rule also cannot prioritize all Urgent and Complex-panel cases when both require scarce medical review; the cases should be ordered by SLA slack and clinical/contractual risk, with an explicit reservation for complex work.

**Scenario D — courier compression or delay**

With compressed arrivals, Courier intake and Registration are the first constraints: peak intake utilization is 88%, registration is 85%, and registration's queue grows from 0.7 to 2.8 hours. Smoothing the route with I7 can reduce that spike, but if it simply makes samples arrive earlier in a larger continuous stream, Pre-analytical check becomes the next bottleneck at 96% utilization and a 9.5-hour queue. The resulting wave reaches correction, Preparation, and the machines; this is why courier smoothing must be paired with an intake WIP cap and downstream release rate.

With a delay, the process receives a burst later in the day. Urgent cases have less of their 18-hour budget left, while preparation may either wait for a batch or break a batch to serve them. Breaking batches protects some Urgent cases but increases setup fragmentation and delays Routine and Complex-panel cases. If delayed samples are worked immediately without a defect triage, night-shift pressure increases the known M2 repeat pattern. If reception staff spend time explaining poor samples, the recollection loop further reduces intake capacity. The bottleneck can therefore move from courier/registration to Pre-analytical check, then to Batch wait and M2, and finally to validation.

**Scenario E — compound peak disruption**

The combined case has no safe single-lane answer. Arrival and pre-analytical queues create a wave; the machine outage prevents that wave from being absorbed; the specialist absence leaves Complex-panel output in Hold. Protecting only Urgent work sends the accumulated Complex-panel cases to Medical validation later, where the 22-hour peak queue and 48-hour specialist-return edge become materially worse. Outsourcing all complex work reduces specialist WIP but adds transport and reconciliation work exactly when courier and registration are stressed. Cross-training staff away from Technical validation or correction can expose another queue within hours.

The correct response is a bounded-control response: protect a small amount of assay and medical capacity for Urgent work and for the oldest/highest-risk Complex-panel cases, cap new WIP at each constrained station, stop low-value batch expansion, and activate selective partner overflow only for cases whose predicted in-house completion breaches the relevant target. Every intervention must be checked against the next queue rather than judged by the current queue alone.

## 3. Robust redesign package

**1. Install a class-aware dispatch and WIP-control layer.**

At registration, assign each case a class, due-time budget, required assays, medical-review requirement, and current age. Use four operational lanes: Urgent with an SLA clock, Complex-panel with a reserved multi-assay path, Routine, and Repeat-after-failure with an explicit quality hold. Within each lane, use oldest-due-first; across lanes, use a score based on remaining SLA slack, clinical risk, and the number of downstream scarce resources still required. Do not use a permanent strict Urgent-first rule.

Reserve a bounded fraction of preparation, machine, and medical-validation capacity for Urgent and Complex-panel work, but leave a movable share for the longest-waiting Routine cases. Set station-level WIP caps, with a lower cap downstream of a known constraint. When a cap is reached, upstream work is held in a controlled staging queue rather than released to create hidden WIP. This prevents a local queue reduction from simply filling the next queue.

The policy is robust because the mix, not just volume, changes. It depends on reliable classification and due-time data. It fails if the reserved fraction is fixed too high during a Routine-heavy period, or if all Complex-panel work is treated as equally urgent; both would starve Routine work. Review the reservation daily using SLA misses, queue age, and unused reserved slots.

**2. Create an early, quality-preserving front door.**

Use I8 for complete digital metadata, and add a two-hour pre-analytical defect triage based on I6. The triage should rapidly identify missing identifiers, inadequate volume, wrong tube, and other actionable defects; it should not indiscriminately reject samples. Send a structured recollection request immediately, preserve the case's original priority, and provide a standard physician explanation so clerks do not repeatedly absorb the same communication work.

Create a small correction fast lane and let trained technical validators cover accession correction only when the Technical validation queue is below a safe floor. I4 is useful because correction has a 14-hour peak queue and unblocks many cases, but moving both validators permanently would recreate a technical-validation bottleneck. Cross-train in advance, use one person at a time or time-boxed flex blocks, and stop the flex assignment when technical queue age or WIP exceeds its trigger.

This package reduces avoidable work before it consumes Preparation and assay capacity. Its failure mode is an overly aggressive early rejection that increases recollections, cancellations, and clinician dissatisfaction; the defect rate and recollection return time must therefore be monitored separately.

**3. Use dynamic machine protection and quality controls.**

Qualify both machines for each assay and maintain a live routing matrix that includes current failure rate, turnaround, and calibration state. Use M1 as the protected primary for Urgent and the most time-sensitive Complex-panel components, but do not dedicate it permanently. Route suitable Routine work to M2 only under a WIP and repeat-rate cap; retain some M1 reserve and a fallback sequence for a machine outage. Avoid sending all Routine work to M2 during high load.

Keep the normal Routine batch size of 12 as the default. Permit a batch of 24 only when the next urgent/complex reservation is intact, no urgent case is near its deadline, and machine queue age is below its warning band. An urgent exception should take a reserved slot, not repeatedly interrupt an already-started batch. For Repeat-after-failure cases, require a short failure classification and, where validated, route away from the suspected M2/assay condition before consuming another slot. This addresses the 1,140 normal and 1,260 peak repeat-assay transitions without assuming that more dispatch priority fixes a quality problem.

I2, I3, and urgent exceptions are therefore conditional parts of the package, not permanent global rules. They depend on assay qualification, a visible reserve, and a quality gate. They may fail if the outage is prolonged beyond the reserve or if the alternative machine lacks assay capability; then selective external overflow is required.

**4. Protect the validation tail.**

Publish a daily medical-validator roster with a backup and an evening peak shift using I5 only when trigger conditions are met. Pre-book medical slots for Urgent and Complex-panel cases, and use protocol-based technical release for cases that do not require medical validation. Give the medical queue a separate aging view so a low overall queue does not hide a Complex-panel tail. Schedule specialist coverage, including the known second-Friday absence, and prepare a qualified remote or partner review option for validated case types.

Use I10 selectively, not as a blanket routing rule. Send only Complex-panel overflow whose predicted in-house completion exceeds its target and whose assay/result package is complete enough for partner acceptance. Require barcoded chain-of-custody, electronic result reconciliation, and a return-time commitment. The 12-hour transport is acceptable only when it is smaller than the forecasted in-house specialist delay and when the Urgent contract or clinical risk permits it.

This protects the 22-hour Medical validation and 36-hour Specialist review peak queues. The residual risk is that temporary review capacity can be consumed by low-risk work unless the same due-time scoring and WIP cap are applied.

**5. Level the input rather than merely accelerating it.**

Implement I7 with courier appointment windows, a pre-alert containing case class and tube count, and a hard maximum for arrivals per short interval. Use a small controlled staging area when the downstream Pre-analytical queue is full. For a delay, pre-identify Urgent cases and process them against remaining SLA slack, but do not release more work than the next station can absorb. Keep the 10:00 and 17:00 route data visible to operations.

This reduces the first queue while avoiding the common failure in which a smoothed intake becomes an unbounded Pre-analytical queue. It depends on courier adherence and enough traceability to preserve order-to-tube identity; it fails when a route is too late to protect the Urgent SLA, in which case the delayed-arrival policy must be activated.

I9 should not be a general redesign element. Holding Routine releases for a batch report may reduce release transactions, but it deliberately adds waiting and can conceal aging. If used at all, it should apply only to a defined low-risk, already-complete cohort when release workload—not assay, validation, or medical review—is the measured constraint.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

**Mix-shift policy.** Turn on when Urgent is at least 27% or Complex-panel is at least 18% of rolling same-day receipts, or when either class's projected queue age breaches its warning band for two consecutive checks. Reduce Routine batch size to 12, open reserved urgent/complex slots, and activate the peak medical shift if the medical queue is rising. Turn off after both mix shares return below trigger values for one full operating day and reserved slots are not being consumed. Do not turn on strict Urgent-first dispatch solely because total volume is high.

**Machine-degradation policy.** Turn on when a machine is unavailable for more than 30 minutes, its quality-failure rate exceeds the validated baseline by a predefined margin, or repeat assays on that machine exceed 8% of runs in a rolling shift. Freeze large Routine batches, preserve the remaining machine's protected slots, route only qualified assays to it, and invoke the alternative-machine/partner decision tree. Stop using the affected machine for the assay until quality clearance. Turn off after quality is cleared, a short confirmation sample is accepted, and the surviving-machine queue is below its recovery threshold. Releasing the machine too early risks a second repeat wave.

**Medical/specialist absence policy.** Turn on as soon as a scheduled absence is confirmed, or when Medical validation queue age exceeds 12 hours or Specialist review exceeds 18 hours. Fill the qualified evening/backup roster, reserve slots for Urgent and oldest Complex-panel work, and divert only forecasted, eligible Complex-panel overflow to the partner. Turn off when qualified capacity is restored and the 90th-percentile age of the affected queue is below target for two checks. A partner handoff remains on until all in-flight cases are reconciled.

**Courier-compression policy.** Turn on when more than 25% of expected daily receipts are pre-advised for one 2-hour window, or when a route is more than 2 hours late. Pre-alert and segregate Urgent/Complex-panel samples, cap release into Pre-analytical check at its safe rate, and keep the correction/recollection lane separate. Use the urgent reserve without breaking every routine batch. Turn off when the arrival rate and Pre-analytical WIP are below their warning bands and no Urgent case has less than six hours of remaining SLA slack.

**Compound-disruption policy.** Turn on when two of the above conditions coexist, or when projected Urgent SLA breach exceeds 5% while a machine or specialist is unavailable. Use the smallest viable protected lanes, stop discretionary batch expansion and I9, divert only selected complete Complex-panel work, and run an hourly control-room review of WIP and queue ages. Turn off each component independently when its own recovery condition is met; do not declare the whole process recovered while a partner reconciliation or repeat-assay backlog remains.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

The process remains dependent on qualified medical and specialist reviewers. A simultaneous absence and Complex-panel surge can exceed both the internal roster and partner capacity; the design can bound the damage but cannot make a scarce clinical decision instantaneous.

One qualified assay machine may still be insufficient for a prolonged outage. The remaining machine reserve protects the most time-sensitive work at the cost of an expanding Routine queue, and a partner may not accept every assay or panel.

The redesigned priority system itself can create starvation and fragmentation. Reserved slots can sit unused while ordinary work ages, and frequent Urgent interruptions can reduce batch efficiency. Aging overrides and a daily adjustment of reservation size are therefore essential. A protected M1 lane can also concentrate risk in M1 if its qualification or calibration is not actively monitored.

Early defect triage can increase recollections and cancellations if its rules are too sensitive or if physicians cannot correct defects promptly. Courier staging can preserve downstream stability while making intake WIP look worse and can consume space or traceability effort.

Finally, an order-level log can hide tube-level synchronization and partial Complex-panel completion. A case may appear to have reached Assay run while one required tube or assay is still waiting. The redesigned controls require tube/assay lineage and timestamps; without that instrumentation, apparent cycle-time improvement may simply be delayed completion or an unobserved handoff.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

During peaks, review the following at least every two hours, with hourly review under compound-disruption policy.

**Leading indicators**

- **Demand and mix:** receipts per hour and per courier window; warning when a 2-hour window exceeds 25% of expected daily receipts; trigger when rolling same-day Urgent share reaches 27% or Complex-panel share reaches 18%.
- **SLA slack:** count and percentage of Urgent cases with less than 6 hours remaining before 18 hours, and Complex-panel/Routine cases projected beyond target; warning at 5% Urgent cases below six hours, trigger at 5% projected breach.
- **WIP and queue age by station:** Pre-analytical, correction, Preparation, each machine, Technical validation, Medical validation, and Specialist review. Warning at roughly 80% of the station's safe WIP or 12-hour queue age; red at the station's peak median plus a rising trend, or any queue age that consumes the remaining Urgent SLA.
- **Machine health:** uptime, run queue, failure rate, repeat rate by machine and shift, and M2 night-shift repeat concentration. Trigger machine-degradation policy after 30 minutes unavailable or repeat rate above 8% in a rolling shift.
- **Front-door quality:** pre-analytical defect percentage, accession-correction percentage, recollection requests, cancellation rate, and median time for a recollection to return. A rising defect/recollection band is an early warning even before assay utilization increases.
- **Capacity allocation:** utilization and available reserved slots by machine and medical reviewer; warning when reserved slots are consumed faster than forecast or remain unused while Routine age rises.
- **Flow integrity:** percentage bypassing Batch wait, batch size, setup interruptions, partial Complex-panel WIP, and cases waiting on one remaining assay. A falling urgent-bypass rate or rising partial-panel count warns of downstream fragmentation.
- **External flow:** partner acceptance time, transport age, chain-of-custody exceptions, and unreconciled results. Any exception is a red operational signal even if the internal specialist queue falls.

**Lagging outcome metrics**

- Urgent release within 18 hours, by case type and disruption scenario.
- Routine release within 72 hours, plus Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure p50/p90 cycle time.
- Percentage of cases with a queue over 12 hours and maximum queue age.
- Rework, repeat-assay, recollection, cancellation, and post-validation correction rates, split by machine, shift, case type, and origin.
- End-to-end completion, including partner cases and all required assays/tubes, rather than only the first released component.

The dashboard should display distributions and oldest cases by class, not just overall averages. A stable overall median can coexist with an Urgent SLA breach or a growing Complex-panel tail, which is precisely the failure mode this redesign is intended to prevent.
