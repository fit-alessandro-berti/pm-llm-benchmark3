## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1: Winter urgent/complex wave

The first bottleneck is **pre-analytical check**. It is the gateway before all downstream paths. In the peak DFG, 9,730 cases reach Receive sample, but only 8,530 reach Test preparation: 780 go to Request recollection and 420 go to Manual accession correction. Pre-analytical queue grows from 1.8h normal to 9.5h peak.

If pre-analytical is relieved, the bottleneck migrates to **Test preparation and Assay**. Urgent bypass of Batch wait drops from 74% normal to 58% peak, so urgent cases are no longer protected by routing. Assay queue grows from 4h to 18h. Increasing routine batch size would improve prep efficiency but would make urgent waits worse, especially if urgent cases cannot interrupt a 24-tube routine batch.

If assay is relieved by hard-dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex and M2 to Routine, the next bottleneck is **Technical validation**, already at 101% utilization with a 16h peak queue. After Technical validation, the next wave moves to **Medical validation** and **Specialist review**, with 22h and 36h peak queues. Strict urgent-first routing can therefore create a situation where urgent cases get to Medical validation faster but still miss the 18h target because Medical validation is now the congestion point.

### S2: Assay-machine degradation / outage

The initial bottleneck is **assay capacity**, but the migration is nonlinear because of the repeat loop. Peak DFG shows 1,260 repeat assays in three weeks; each repeat consumes assay capacity twice and then returns to Technical validation. M2’s higher failure rate means that routing Routine work to M2 to protect M1 can actually make the assay bottleneck worse, because repeat-after-failure work is added back into the queue.

If assay is temporarily fixed by overloading M1, the bottleneck moves to **Technical validation** after recovery. While M2 is down, downstream validators may be partly starved; when M2 returns, a wave of completed assays hits Technical validation at once. This is a classic “hidden queue” migration: the work is not gone, it is waiting inside the assay step.

### S3: Medical-validator / specialist-review absence

The initial bottleneck is **Medical validation**, needed for Urgent and Complex-panel cases. Peak Medical validation queue is 22h with 93% utilization. If an evening Medical validation shift is added without specialist planning, the bottleneck migrates to **Specialist review**: Technical validation  Hold for specialist review appears 610 times in peak DFG, and Hold for specialist review  Medical validation is 560 cases. Complex-panel cases are 2.4× more likely to enter that hold.

Specialist review is not only a utilization problem: one specialist is unavailable every second Friday, and specialist work is concentrated in complex-panel arrivals from assay batches. If specialist capacity is increased, the feedback loop returns to Medical validation because Hold for specialist review  Medical validation. Therefore Medical validation and Specialist review must be managed as one coupled system, not separately.

### S4: Courier arrival compression / delay

The initial bottleneck is **Receive sample / Registration**, because arrivals are compressed. Register  Receive is already 8h in peak DFG; under a compressed arrival window it becomes much worse. Courier intake utilization is 88% in peak with arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00.

If courier pickups are smoothed, the bottleneck migrates to **Pre-analytical check** and **Accession correction**. Peak pre-analytical queue is 9.5h; accession correction queue is 14h. Worse, recollection loops create a second arrival wave: Request recollection  Receive sample is 42h normal and 60h peak. If poor samples are rejected faster, that is good only if the resulting recollection wave is planned; otherwise the receive and pre-analytical steps see a second compressed spike 2–3 days later.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign is a control system, not a capacity build-out. I assume an explicit business priority: protect Urgent 18h deadlines first, protect Complex-panel medical review second, and protect Routine from starvation with a minimum-throughput floor.

### R1: Digital front door and 2-hour fast-fail pre-analytical triage

Refines I8 and I6.

- Automate registration only for orders with complete digital metadata.
- At Receive sample, run an objective pre-analytical adequacy check within 2 hours.
- Poor samples are returned to the physician with a structured reason, not left in the pre-analytical queue.
- Ambiguous samples go to Manual accession correction specialists.

Why robust: This reduces registration queues, manual accession corrections, and late recollection loops. It supports S1, S2, and S4 because it removes avoidable work before it reaches prep, assay, and validation.

Assumptions: objective rejection criteria exist; sample stability allows a 2-hour decision; the EMR can transmit structured recollect orders.

Failure mode: if rejection criteria are too aggressive, the recollection loop increases and cancels rise. This could shift delay from pre-analytical to the 42–60h recollection wait, so rejection rates need monitoring.

### R2: Due-date-slack scheduling and controlled batching at prep and assay

Refines I1 and I2; rejects strict urgent-first.

- At Test preparation and Assay, calculate a critical ratio: `(time to deadline) / (remaining standard processing time)`.
- Urgent cases should not interrupt every routine batch. Instead, group urgent cases into urgent mini-batches by assay method.
- Use 24-tube routine batches only when urgent WIP is low; otherwise keep routine batch size at 12.
- Reserve a share of assay slots for Complex-panel cases so they are not starved by Urgent work.

Why robust: This directly addresses the variant behavior that urgent bypass fragments machine schedules. It also prevents the I1 failure mode of starving Routine and Complex cases, and it prevents the I2 failure mode of creating long routine batches that block urgent work.

Assumptions: real-time WIP and due-date data are available; operators can flex batch sizes.

Failure mode: if the urgent slot cap is too high, Routine/Complex are starved; if too low, urgent cases miss the 18h deadline. The cap must be a tunable parameter, not a fixed policy.

### R3: Assay lane routing rules, not hard dedication

Refines I3.

- Do not hard-dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex and M2 to Routine.
- Prefer M1 for first-run of Urgent and Complex cases, and for repeat-after-failure cases, because M1 has lower failure rate.
- Use M2 for Routine and overflow work only when M2’s repeat rate is in control.
- If M2 degrades, switch to M1-protection mode and move routine overflow to off-peak or partner overflow.

Why robust: This reduces the repeat-loop amplification in S2 and protects critical cases in S1. It avoids the I3 trap of sending all Routine to the faster but more failure-prone M2.

Assumptions: both instruments can run most assays; failure rates can be tracked by instrument.

Failure mode: M1 becomes a single point of failure if M2 is down. In that case, urgent/complex/routine compete for one machine; no routing rule creates capacity.

### R4: Differentiated technical validation

This is process automation, not capacity expansion.

- Use rules-based auto-approval for low-risk Routine runs with in-control QC and no flags.
- Keep manual Technical validation for Urgent, Complex-panel, Repeat-after-failure, and all cases with QC exceptions.

Why robust: Technical validation is 101% utilized in peak and is the bottleneck after assay. Any upstream improvement will overload it. R4 reduces avoidable manual validation work without stealing validators.

Assumptions: regulatory acceptance, structured QC data, audit trail, and clear manual-override rules.

Failure mode: auto-approval could release a misread result if the rules are too broad. The manual-override rate and auto-approval error log must be monitored.

### R5: Medical-validation / specialist-review surge with WIP cap

Refines I5 and I10.

- Activate temporary evening Medical validation shifts only when Medical validation queue exceeds ~10h.
- Before each evening shift, set a cap on how many cases may be released into Specialist review, based on specialist capacity.
- Prepare specialist review packets while cases are still in Medical validation, so specialist time is not wasted.
- Outsource Complex-panel overflow to the partner lab only for non-urgent Complex cases and only when specialist queue exceeds ~24h.

Why robust: This addresses S1 and S3. It treats Medical validation and Specialist review as a coupled system, so relieving one does not blindly overload the other.

Assumptions: per-diem validators exist; specialist packets can be prepared; partner lab transport delay is acceptable for non-urgent complex cases.

Failure mode: evening Medical validation creates a night-time specialist backlog if the WIP cap is not enforced. Outsourcing adds 12h transport delay and traceability risk.

### R6: Courier smoothing with downstream WIP cap

Refines I7.

- Shift courier pickup slots to spread arrivals over at least 6 hours.
- If pre-analytical queue is high, hold stable Routine tubes in a controlled buffer within stability limits.
- Do not release all smoothed arrivals into pre-analytical at once.

Why robust: This helps S4 and also reduces peak pre-analytical load in S1. It uses Routine’s tolerance of batching as a buffer while protecting Urgent and time-sensitive samples.

Assumptions: sample stability permits buffering; physical storage exists; urgent/complex samples can be identified at receive.

Failure mode: holding Routine samples too long endangers the 72h target. Buffer age must be monitored.

### Explicit non-recommendations

- **I4 is not recommended.** Technical validators are already at 101% peak utilization. Moving two of them to accession correction would make Technical validation the critical path.
- **I9 is not recommended.** Holding Routine releases adds waiting time and does not fix the pre-analytical, assay, or validation bottlenecks. The DFG does not show release result as a high-queue step.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Absolute assay capacity shortfall remains.** If both M1 and M2 are degraded or if volume simply exceeds machine-hours, no scheduling rule creates capacity. The redesign still depends on contractual overflow or backup instruments.
- **Technical validation remains fragile.** R4 reduces routine validation work, but Urgent, Complex, and Repeat-after-failure cases still need manual Technical validation. If assay throughput improves, Technical validation is the next wall.
- **Specialist review is inherently human-dependent.** The redesign can cap WIP and prepare packets, but it cannot make a single unavailable specialist available. Complex-panel cases will still wait during specialist absence.
- **Partner-lab overflow creates a traceability exposure.** R5 limits outsourcing to non-urgent complex cases, but a 12h transport delay plus possible sample loss or quality failure remains.
- **Redesign-caused vulnerability: the “front-end empty, back-end jam” effect.** By automating registration, fast-failing poor samples, and smoothing arrivals, the redesigned front end will push valid work downstream faster. If Technical validation or Medical validation surge triggers are late, the bottleneck can migrate violently from assay to validation and cause an even more localized overload than the original process.
- **Another redesign-caused vulnerability: aggressive fast-fail can create a second recollection wave.** If the 2-hour triage is too strict, marginally acceptable samples are rejected, and the recollection loop re-enters Receive sample 42–60h later. The redesign may thus create a delayed secondary arrival peak that looks like a courier problem but is actually caused by the lab’s own rejection policy.
