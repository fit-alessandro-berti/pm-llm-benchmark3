## 1. Decision rule

Use a stage-gated, uncertainty-aware net-benefit policy rather than a breach-probability threshold.

### Eligibility

A case is eligible only if all of the following hold:

1. **Actionable stage:** it is in Triage or Diagnosis Started and is expected to remain before `Diagnosis Complete` long enough for a 45-minute swarm to act. Cases already at Diagnosis Complete are ineligible.
2. **Operational feasibility:** specialists can start soon enough to affect the deadline.
3. **Alert validity:** required fields are present, or any imputation has been manually checked.
4. **Alert persistence/evidence:** it is a first actionable alert, or there is a material new event. Repeated refreshes without new evidence are suppressed.
5. **Positive portfolio value:** plausible direct benefit exceeds the €450 cost, case burden, and marginal queue externality under at least the base scenario—and preferably remains non-negative under a conservative effect estimate.

### Calibration and effect treatment

I would use calibrated risk primarily to verify that there is material preventable risk, not as the intervention effect:

- **SME:** sensitivity-adjust risk upward by 0.04–0.09, capped at 1.
- **Strategic Enterprise:** sensitivity-adjust downward by 0.05–0.12.
- **Public Sector:** retain the estimate but widen uncertainty because of moderate sample size.
- **Non-Native-Language Support (NNLS):** do not apply a confident point correction; use a wide probability interval and discount the estimated effect in robust ranking.

These are scenario corrections, not mechanically “true” probabilities. The supplied risk-reduction ranges are also treated as uncertain observational/estimated effects, not established causal effects.

### Prioritization

For an eligible case \(i\), calculate:

\[
NB_i(e)=e_iV_i-450-B_i-P_i
\]

where:

- \(e_i\) is conservative, midpoint, or optimistic absolute effect;
- \(V_i\) is value of avoiding breach;
- \(B_i\) is false-alarm burden, weighted by the estimated probability that the swarm proves unnecessary;
- \(P_i\) is the case’s allocated share of portfolio spillover harm.

Because the table does not provide monetary values for the six exposed cases, spillover is kept as a separate breach-equivalent constraint rather than hidden behind an invented euro conversion. At base, each slot imposes approximately:

\[
6(0.01\text{–}0.03)=0.06\text{–}0.18
\]

expected spillover breaches. Selection uses the lower-bound, midpoint, and failure-case effects—not midpoint expected value alone.

Priority tiers:

- **Tier A:** positive conservative direct economics, credible evidence, urgent/actionable.
- **Tier B:** positive base economics but important effect, data, timing, burden, or calibration uncertainty.
- **Tier C:** wait for validation or more information.
- **Ineligible:** wrong stage or insufficient time for effect.

Legal, regulatory, safety, and contractual-criticality requirements may override monetary ranking, but the override and displaced case must be logged. One slot is normally reserved until 15:00 for the possible regulatory incident unless an existing case becomes materially more urgent.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

### Alert state and cool-down

- Create one alert episode per case rather than a new alert every 30 minutes.
- After an alert is reviewed, suppress identical alerts for **two hours or until a material event**, whichever comes first.
- After a swarm, suppress further swarm eligibility until a material new event; routine probability movement alone is insufficient.
- Normally allow only one swarm per case. A second requires supervisor approval and documentation of why the first swarm’s action space is no longer applicable.

### Material-change rule

A case re-enters if at least one occurs:

- new customer logs, diagnostics, or dependency information;
- stage transition or reopened diagnosis;
- deadline change or major remaining-time reduction;
- verified queue/congestion change;
- data-quality correction;
- a substantial calibrated-risk increase, provisionally at least **0.10 absolute**, sustained over two refreshes;
- documented failure of the current diagnostic path.

The 0.10 criterion is a persistence filter, not a universal treatment threshold. Smaller changes may qualify when accompanied by concrete new evidence or very short time to SLA.

### Escalation

Escalate to human review when:

- time to deadline is under two hours;
- data are missing or imputed;
- a sparse segment drives uncertainty;
- the case has repeated alerts but no intervention;
- expected specialist start time threatens to consume most of the remaining useful window.

A human review may lead to ordinary diagnostic action, data repair, language support, or downstream resolution escalation rather than a swarm.

### I-05 versus I-07

- **I-05:** The third alert reflects 30-minute refreshes with no new event. Its change from 0.55 to 0.57 is not new evidence and should remain in the same suppressed alert episode. SME underprediction warrants monitoring, but not duplicate slot demand. It may re-enter after a material event, sustained risk jump, stage/time deterioration, or completion of the cool-down review.
- **I-07:** The new customer diagnostic log is a material information event. It therefore creates valid re-entry even though \(p\) fell from 0.75 to 0.72. The intervention could extract value from the new log, and only 1.5 hours remain. This demonstrates why alert handling cannot depend solely on whether the model score increased.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

### Value-based prioritization

High-value cases such as I-04 and I-08 can dominate expected-euro ranking and systematically starve SMEs or customers with lower contractual valuations.

Safeguards:

- Use a **two-part score**: robust net benefit plus a non-monetary criticality/urgency score.
- Require a positive minimum clinical/operational effect case before value is used as a tie-breaker.
- Monitor intervention rates, wait times, breach rates, and spillover exposure by segment.
- Consider a rolling capacity floor for qualifying lower-value cases, subject to avoiding wasteful swarms.
- Permit documented public-interest, vulnerability, regulatory, and severe-service-impact overrides.
- Report results both in euros and breach-equivalent/customer-impact units.

### Non-Native-Language Support

I-09’s nominal economics are attractive, but sparse data could cause either under-treatment or inappropriate intervention. Blanket discounting would itself create inequity.

Safeguards:

- Route NNLS cases to bilingual or interpreter-assisted review where possible.
- Use wider uncertainty intervals, not an automatic adverse score adjustment.
- Require a rapid human evidence check before withholding or dispatching.
- Oversample this context in evaluation and effect studies.
- Compare false-positive, false-negative, intervention, and post-intervention breach rates with uncertainty intervals.
- Treat persistent under-service as a policy defect even when conventional significance thresholds are not met.

### Differential calibration

SME underprediction and Strategic overprediction can lead to systematically late SME intervention and excessive Strategic disruption.

Safeguards:

- Maintain segment-specific calibration models with shrinkage toward the portfolio estimate where samples are small.
- Publish calibration curves, Brier scores, and false-negative/false-positive rates by segment and stage.
- Do not let contractual value conceal calibration errors.
- Introduce review triggers if observed-minus-predicted breach differs by more than 0.05 for a sufficiently supported segment, or if intervals indicate operationally material harm.

### Selective intervention and contaminated labels

Successful swarms reduce observed breaches among alerted cases. Training on the resulting labels without treatment status can make treated risk profiles appear inherently safer, while untreated cases inherit spillover delays.

Safeguards:

- Log eligibility, score version, selection propensity, intervention timing, content, completion, and queue exposure.
- Distinguish factual post-policy outcomes from estimated no-intervention outcomes.
- Include policy version and treatment exposure in analysis datasets.
- Avoid naïvely retraining the baseline risk model on post-intervention labels.
- Use randomized or quasi-randomized evidence to estimate treatment effects.

### Unequal spillover exposure

The six untreated cases bearing delay may repeatedly be SMEs, lower-value customers, night-shift cases, or language-support cases.

Safeguards:

- Record which cases were in the affected queue during every swarm and estimated delay imposed.
- Audit cumulative spillover exposure by segment, language, value band, shift, and contractual class.
- Set exposure caps or rotate specialist coverage if one group absorbs disproportionate delay.
- Include protected/high-criticality untreated cases in the dispatch guardrail.
- Measure **net segment impact**: direct benefits received minus spillover harm imposed, rather than intervention receipt alone.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

### Operational monitoring

In real time, track:

- slot use, reserved-slot use, start delay, swarm duration, and completion;
- stage at dispatch and whether Diagnosis Complete occurred before useful action;
- specialist interruption time and ordinary-work displacement;
- queue length, aggregate delay, and delay per slot;
- repeated-alert suppression and override rates;
- data failures such as I-06’s missing queue field;
- customer burden, complaints, and regulatory overrides.

Pause dispatches when congestion exceeds the guardrail, data failure becomes systemic, or specialists cannot start within the useful window.

### Predictive monitoring

Weekly operational and monthly formal reviews should examine:

- calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope;
- Brier score and log loss;
- reliability curves by segment, stage, time-to-SLA, language support, and data-quality status;
- alert precision, missed-breach rate, and score-change distributions;
- SME and Strategic calibration offsets;
- performance on imputed versus complete records.

Recalibrate probabilities separately from treatment effects. Use hierarchical calibration for sparse groups and display uncertainty intervals.

### Causal-effect monitoring

The swarm effect cannot be estimated from treated-versus-untreated outcomes without adjustment because the policy selects cases.

Preferred design:

1. Among cases within a prespecified **equipoise band**—eligible, positive base value, but uncertain ordering—randomize immediate swarm versus delayed/usual-care assignment when ethically and operationally permissible.
2. Stratify by stage, segment, time remaining, and congestion.
3. Preserve mandatory intervention for legal/regulatory cases and cases with overwhelming expected benefit.
4. Estimate intention-to-treat and treatment-on-treated effects on breach, diagnosis duration, customer burden, downstream rework, and queue spillovers.
5. Use random slot scarcity or start-time variation only if its assumptions are documented; do not rely on unverified observational comparisons.

Report heterogeneous effects with partial pooling. For NNLS and other small groups, emphasize interval width and minimum-service safeguards rather than unstable point estimates.

### Capacity and spillover monitoring

Estimate:

- direct breaches avoided;
- spillover breaches induced;
- net portfolio breaches;
- marginal effect of the first, second, third, and fourth daily slot;
- delay imposed per slot under congestion bands;
- opportunity cost of holding a reserve slot;
- frequency and consequence of regulatory arrivals.

If the fourth slot repeatedly has negative marginal net impact, reduce routine capacity use or add staffing rather than filling all slots. If reserve slots frequently expire unused while eligible urgent cases breach, shorten the reservation window or use a recallable provisional allocation.

### Fairness monitoring

At least monthly, compare by segment, language support, value band, and stage:

- eligibility and intervention rates conditional on need;
- time from alert to review and swarm;
- suppression and override rates;
- false-positive and missed-breach rates;
- burden and customer complaints;
- direct benefit received;
- queue delay and spillover exposure;
- net breach impact.

Investigate material gaps using both absolute and relative measures, with confidence intervals. Small sample size should trigger cautious interpretation and targeted data collection—not an assumption of fairness.

### Policy-induced drift and change control

Maintain a shadow model trained/evaluated on carefully defined pre-intervention or untreated outcomes. Monitor changes in:

- case mix and stage duration;
- documentation behavior after teams learn the policy;
- selection propensities;
- treated and spillover-exposed outcome rates;
- reasons for alerts and reasons for swarm success or failure.

Change the allocation rule only through versioned review. Trigger review when:

- calibration error exceeds an operational tolerance such as 0.05;
- estimated lower-bound effect becomes non-positive for a segment;
- net spillover-adjusted breaches are non-improving;
- queue delay exceeds guardrails repeatedly;
- fairness disparities persist for two review periods;
- the reserve-slot policy causes repeated avoidable breaches.

Thresholds and tier boundaries should then be updated using out-of-sample simulation across optimistic, base, and adverse scenarios, followed by a limited pilot. The objective is not to maximize alert accuracy; it is to maximize defensible, spillover-adjusted portfolio benefit under uncertainty and service obligations.
