# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The apparent contradiction between Mira_Coordinator’s high handover/subcontracting centrality and her low working-together centrality is not really a contradiction. Handover and subcontracting networks describe how work is transferred between resources. The working-together network describes which resources are associated with the same case, often as co-workers on that case. A broker can be central in the first two views without being central in the third.

- **Handover-of-work network**  
  This directed network counts how often one resource directly hands work to another. Mira_Coordinator appears as a hub: high out-degree centrality (0.82), high in-degree centrality (0.51), and high betweenness (0.77). She receives many cases from Bot_Rules, sends cases to specialists and adjusters, and receives them back.  
  What this does **not** imply: that she is a deep contributor to the work, that she spends a lot of time on each case, or that she is necessarily a formal supervisor. Many of the edges may come from short “Assign Specialist”, “Reopen”, or “Clarification” events. The network shows frequency of handoffs, not the quality or depth of involvement.

- **Working-together network**  
  This undirected network shows how often two resources appear on the same case, regardless of whether they directly hand over work. Mira_Coordinator has low weighted degree (0.16) and low co-case rates with team leads and adjusters (4–8%). This means she is not a regular co-worker in the daily handling of cases.  
  What this does **not** imply: that she is unimportant or peripheral to outcomes. Mira touches only 22% of all claims, so her maximum possible pairwise co-occurrence rates are limited. Moreover, many of her events are short routing, assignment, reopening, and clarification events. She appears, makes a decision, and then disappears from the case. That is exactly the profile of a broker, not a teammate.

- **Subcontracting network**  
  This network captures patterns where resource A passes to resource B, then A appears again within a short window. Mira_Coordinator has strong subcontracting patterns with Omar_Medical and Priya_Legal, and high subcontracting betweenness. This means she frequently hands a case to a specialist and then takes the case back, acting as the controlling party in a delegation loop.  
  What this does **not** imply: that Mira is the legal or administrative “owner” of the claim, or that the pattern is necessarily good or bad. It does imply that she remains in the routing loop after specialist work is done, rather than simply passing cases on and forgetting them.

- **Performance/time statistics**  
  Mira’s median service time per event is only 4.2 minutes, much shorter than adjusters and specialists. Yet she has 960 events and 905 distinct cases, and 48% of her cases carry `exception_flag=True`. This suggests broad, shallow involvement: many cases, short touches, high exception focus. Her “average case cycle-time contribution” is high variance, which is consistent with a person whose decisions create waiting time, not with a person who spends a long time working on each case. The absence period is important: ordinary claims were unaffected, but exception cases queued up and the 90th percentile exception cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days.

- **Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity**  
  Mira’s activity profile is clearly different from leads, adjusters, specialists, and temporary staff. She performs Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Assign Specialist. A PM4Py organizational role discovery would likely identify her as a separate “exception coordinator / routing” role. Activity-based resource similarity would probably show that she is not similar to substantive workers because she does not perform their core activities. This supports the idea that her organizational function is routing, triage, and exception handling, not shared execution of case work.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1: Formal coordinator / exception desk

- **Mechanism:** Mira_Coordinator was placed in a formal or semi-formal coordinator role. Bot_Rules routes exceptions to her. She decides which specialist or adjuster should take the case, requests missing information, reopens claims, and escalates complaints.
- **Evidence supporting:** Bot_Rules  Mira_Coordinator is a strong handover edge (920). She is involved in 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Her event share has a high exception flag rate (0.48). She appears immediately after Bot_Rules on exception cases.
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting:** A process owner says she is “not a real team lead, just a queue cleaner.” She touches only 22% of claims, so she is not a general coordinator. She does not approve settlements or perform team-lead activities. Her short service time and low working-together centrality also make a formal “team lead” interpretation unlikely.
- **Observable predictions:** Future logs would show stable, documented routing rules. Her handover targets would be predictable from case attributes. If she is absent, another named person would likely take over, or management would have a visible escalation plan.

### Hypothesis 2: Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

- **Mechanism:** Mira_Coordinator is the only person who knows where difficult cases should go. Although her own events are short, her absence creates a queue because no one else can make the routing decision.
- **Evidence supporting:** She has the highest handover betweenness (0.77) and subcontracting betweenness (0.69). During her six-day absence, exception-flagged cases accumulated and the 90th percentile cycle time increased substantially. The operations manager argues that removing her would increase formal escalations and specialist overload.
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting:** Ordinary low-value claims were unaffected during her absence, so she is not a global bottleneck. Her own service time is short, so the queue may reflect a lack of backup rather than slow processing. Centrality alone does not prove that she is the only possible bottleneck; Bot_Rules or specialist availability may also matter.
- **Observable predictions:** Future absences would again show exception queue growth. The handover network would become less connected. Cases would wait longer between Bot_Rules and the first specialist or adjuster.

### Hypothesis 3: Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker

- **Mechanism:** Mira_Coordinator sits between automated routing and human expertise. She translates bot decisions into specialist assignments and moves cases across functional boundaries: medical, legal, adjustment, temporary staff, and vendors.
- **Evidence supporting:** Adjusters describe her as “the person who knows where hard cases should go.” She has high handover out-degree to all specialist and adjuster groups. Her subcontracting patterns with Omar_Medical and Priya_Legal show that she hands off to specialists and then takes the case back. Low working-together centrality is consistent with a boundary spanner who transfers cases rather than working jointly with every resource.
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting:** Boundary spanners usually have strong relational ties, but Mira’s working-together network is low. Her role may be rule-driven rather than knowledge-driven. She has no direct handover edges to team leads in the top edge list, so she may not be spanning team boundaries as much as functional boundaries.
- **Observable predictions:** Cases routed by Mira would be more likely to involve multiple specialist domains. Her handover target should correlate with case attributes such as medical, legal, complaint, or missing-information flags.

### Hypothesis 4: Quality gatekeeper / compliance chokepoint

- **Mechanism:** Mira_Coordinator detects problems in cases: missing information, closure exceptions, complaints, or unusual patterns. She reopens, escalates, or sends cases back for specialist review. This gives her a quality-control and control function.
- **Evidence supporting:** She is heavily involved in Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Request Missing Information. She receives handovers from Temp_1 and Temp_2 for missing-info and closure exceptions. Her exception-flag rate is high. The compliance reviewer’s concern is that she both detects problems and selects the next specialist.
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting:** Her service time is very short, so she may not perform deep quality review. She may simply be following routing rules written into the system. No direct evidence exists that she overrides decisions improperly or acts outside authorized discretion.
- **Observable predictions:** Future logs would show her interventions leading to rework loops: e.g., Request Missing Information  Specialist Review  Reopen. If she is a gatekeeper, reducing her role would increase downstream errors or formal escalations.

### Hypothesis 5: Data artifact / routing-event overlay

- **Mechanism:** Some of Mira’s events, such as “Assign Specialist”, may be workflow-system actions rather than substantive human work. The event log may record routing clicks as if they were organizational work, inflating her centrality.
- **Evidence supporting:** “Assign Specialist” is not listed among the main process activities. Mira’s service time is only 4.2 minutes. Her working-together co-occurrence is low even though her handover centrality is high. Many of her events are short routing, assignment, or clarification actions.
- **Evidence contradicting or limiting:** The absence period shows that Mira has a real operational effect, not just an artifact effect. Even workflow clicks represent human decisions. Removing or relabeling her events would not remove the underlying coordination function.
- **Observable predictions:** After filtering out “Assign Specialist” and similar routing events, Mira’s handover and subcontracting centrality would drop, but exception queue times and specialist assignment patterns would remain. If the role were entirely artificial, removing her would have no effect; the absence evidence suggests otherwise.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk:** Mira_Coordinator is a funnel for exception cases. Her high betweenness means many cases must pass through her. The absence evidence shows that exception cases queue when she is unavailable. However, this is a selective bottleneck, not a global one. The risk increases if exception volume grows and no backup exists.
- **Coordination risk:** Because her working-together centrality is low, her coordination function is not visible in shared case work. This may mean the organization does not see how much handoff coordination depends on her. A single person holding the routing map can lead to miscommunication when she is absent or overloaded.
- **Key-person risk:** The knowledge of “where hard cases should go” appears concentrated in Mira. The adjustment in 90th percentile exception cycle time during her absence is strong evidence of key-person dependency. This is a classic single-point-of-failure risk.
- **Compliance/control risk:** In a regulated claims process, combining problem detection and specialist selection creates discretion. The compliance reviewer is right to worry about uncontrolled discretion. This does not mean Mira is acting improperly; it means the process lacks an audit trail and objective criteria for her routing decisions.
- **Workload fairness risk:** Mira’s assignments may systematically overload some specialists. Carla_Adjuster and Diego_Adjuster have high workloads, but this could be due to team structure rather than Mira’s choices. If Mira is selecting specialists, her decisions should be monitored for consistency and fairness.
- **Data-quality risk:** The event log may mix system-generated routing events with substantive human work. Automated bots and vendor resources also create edges and waiting times that are not comparable to human resources. If lifecycle transitions and event types are not interpreted carefully, centrality metrics can mislead management.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

- **Handover-of-work network:**  
  Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)` to build directed handover graphs. Compute centrality with `networkx` for the full log and for filtered subsets, e.g., `exception_flag=True` vs `exception_flag=False`. Split the log by month or week to test whether Mira’s hub role is stable or growing. This directly addresses the key-person hypothesis.

- **Working-together network:**  
  Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)` and compare it with the handover network. Recompute after removing “Assign Specialist” and “Request Missing Information” events. If Mira’s working-together centrality rises after removing routing events, that supports the data-artifact hypothesis. Filter by `case_type` and `region` to see whether her low co-occurrence is due to case mix.

- **Subcontracting network:**  
  Use `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log, n=2)` and also `n=3`. Extract Mira’s A-B-A loops with specialists and adjusters. Test whether specialist returns to Mira are consistently followed by reassignment or reopening. Compare her subcontracting betweenness with other resources, especially after filtering out routing events.

- **Activity-based resource similarity and role discovery:**  
  Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)`. Determine whether Mira forms a distinct role or is grouped with team leads, temporary staff, or adjusters. This clarifies whether her role is formalized or merely emergent.

- **Filtering by exception_flag, activity, variants, and time ranges:**  
  Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "exception_flag", [True])` to analyse exception cases separately. Use `pm4py.filter_variants(log)` to focus on variants containing “Escalate Complaint” or “Reopen Claim”. Use `pm4py.filter_time_range` around Mira’s six-day absence to quantify the change in waiting times and cycle times.

- **Service and waiting-time analysis:**  
  Use `pm4py.get_service_time(log)` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)`. Separate service time from waiting time for each resource and for each handover edge. Compare elapsed time from Mira to specialists with elapsed time from other resources to specialists. This would show whether Mira creates waiting or whether specialists themselves are overloaded.

# 5. Recommendation

- **What should be changed immediately:**  
  Create a backup and cross-training plan for Mira_Coordinator. At least one other person should be trained to handle exception routing and specialist assignment. Add mandatory reason codes to Mira’s “Assign Specialist”, “Reopen”, and “Escalate” events so that her decisions are traceable. Set up a waiting-time dashboard for exception-flagged cases so that queue buildup is visible in real time.

- **What should be monitored:**  
  Monitor handover, working-together, and subcontracting networks monthly. Track exception case wait times before and after Mira’s involvement. Monitor specialist workloads to see whether Mira’s routing decisions create unbalanced load. Monitor any future absences to compare exception cycle times with the 6-day absence period. Watch compliance metrics such as the share of reopened claims and complaints that are handled without documented reason codes.

- **What should not be changed until more evidence is available:**  
  Do not remove Mira_Coordinator. If management wants to redesign the role, the lost coordination function must be replaced first: explicit triage rules, automated assignment suggestions, and a trained backup. Pilot any replacement on a subset of claims and compare exception cycle times, specialist overload, and formal escalations. Do not force more working-together activity simply to improve SNA metrics, and do not impose extra approval layers on Mira without testing whether that increases delays.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator is at the center of a puzzle: the event logs show her handing off many claims, especially unusual or difficult ones, but they also show her rarely partnering with other people on the same file. That pattern fits a router or triage broker more than a traditional team lead. She seems to decide where exceptions should go—specialists, adjusters, reopening, or escalation—and then step back. Her impact is real: when she was absent for six days, ordinary claims kept flowing, but complex exception cases backed up. The principal risk is not that she works too slowly; it is that too much routing knowledge and discretion are concentrated in one person. There is also a legitimate control question because she both flags issues and chooses the next specialist. I recommend documenting her routing rules, giving her a trained backup, and tracking exception waiting times. We should not remove her until a formal replacement for that coordination function has been designed and tested.
