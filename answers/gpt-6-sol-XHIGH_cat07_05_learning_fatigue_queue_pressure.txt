# 2. Integrated explanation

The first events inherit complex, high-priority work: mean complexity is 0.72 and high-priority share is 41% at positions 1–5, versus 0.43 and 19% at 76+. Senior selection into complex early cases adds another composition effect. Initial familiarization with the shift or activity may also improve speed, but the early decline in time and errors cannot be assigned to learning without comparing like cases within workers.

By positions 16–50, queues are lower and case mix is easier, so service time approaches 13 minutes and errors remain near 2.5%–2.7%. Later, demand and WIP rise while service time keeps falling. The high-WIP comparisons show the same faster-service/higher-error pairing within both broad complexity bands. A plausible explanation is pressure to clear work, aided by short Document Review batches and delayed Quality Checks. This can move effort into correction and rework: seven-day rework rises from 4.8% at positions 16–30 to 13.7% at 76+. Fast individual events therefore need not mean faster or better case resolution.

Fatigue could amplify late mistakes, and clock time could matter, particularly across Day, Late, and Night schedules. Neither is isolated here: late elapsed time, high load, demand peaks, assignment, and clock hour move together. Later low-WIP error rates are approximately flat for seniors, mid-level, and night staff, and lower for juniors than their first-20-event rate; under high WIP, later errors rise in every group. That pattern favors a substantial pressure and mix explanation, while leaving room for fatigue and circadian effects. The strongest risk zone is complex work under high WIP, where the observed error rate is 10.9%; it should not be read as a causal estimate for every complex case.

# 3. Proposed analyses

- **Define units and outcomes.** Compute human event service time from end minus start timestamps, excluding or separately reporting instantaneous Bot_Risk events. Distinguish event errors, subsequent seven-day rework, case lead time, and waiting time. Link corrections to the originating activity and case. Count only cases with a full seven-day follow-up window when comparing rework, or model censoring. Validate timestamp order, overlapping work, missing flags, and whether Quality Check records an existing error or creates a new error observation.
- **Compare within workers and shifts.** Plot service and error rates by minutes since shift start and sequence position for the same resource and activity, with complexity/priority standardization. Contrast first 20 events with later *low-WIP* events before attributing differences to practice. Use worker and activity effects, shift/date controls, and repeated-shift trajectories. Separate cumulative activity experience across days from within-shift warm-up; include breaks and overtime if available. Sequence and elapsed minutes are correlated, so report where they cannot be independently estimated.
- **Separate load, mix, and clock time.** Estimate flexible position and WIP/queue effects, including WIP-by-complexity and WIP-by-experience interactions, at comparable activity, case complexity score, priority, arrival rate, staffing, and backlog age. Compare low/high WIP at the same elapsed time and late/early events at similar WIP only where data overlap. Use clock-hour, shift-type, day-of-week, and calendar controls; compare overlapping clock hours across shifts. Fit service time (for example, log time) and error/rework (for example, logistic or survival) outcomes separately, with uncertainty clustered for repeated workers, cases, and shifts. Standardize predicted rates to a common case mix. An observational association remains vulnerable to unrecorded urgency and routing decisions.
- **Inspect process paths and batching.** Detect long same-resource/same-activity runs and shared timestamps, then compare Document Review batches with comparable unbatched work. Trace Bot_Risk → human correction and Quality Check → Rework Correction paths; measure the time and queue state between steps. Audit delayed Quality Checks and handovers. Assess both first-pass throughput and total work per resolved case.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Risk or limit | Monitor with event-log indicators |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May reduce errors if overtime and fatigue are material. | Regular shifts are already eight hours; a blanket cap may increase handovers and leave peak queues uncovered. | Overtime exposure, adjusted late error/rework, WIP at handover, case waiting time, and handover count. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour | Could reduce early overload while staff settle in. | Delays urgent carryover and may create a later complex-case pile; early complexity may reflect deliberate senior routing. | Age and SLA of complex cases, first-hour mix, adjusted error/rework, and end-to-end completion time. Pilot protected senior coverage for urgent cases. |
| 3. Alert when WIP > 70 | Prompts added capacity, redistribution, or protected review before risk escalates. | An alert alone may encourage faster, less careful work; 70 is not a validated causal boundary. | Time above threshold, response taken, adjusted service/error/rework, queue age, and downstream correction load. |
| 4. Force random case assignment | Among eligible workers, limited randomization can identify selection effects and distribute comparable load. | Unrestricted random assignment can put specialist or urgent work with unsuitable staff and damage continuity. | Assignment balance by complexity/priority, skill eligibility, handovers, waiting time, adjusted quality, and workload fairness. |
| 5. Mandatory micro-breaks in final two hours | May protect attention if fatigue contributes. | Without staffing coverage, breaks can raise WIP and shift pressure onto colleagues. | Break adherence, staffed capacity, queue/WIP, adjusted late error and seven-day rework, and workload redistributed to others. |
| 6. Defer low-priority quality checks to next shift | May smooth the last-45-minute Quality Check and correction burst. | Later detection, missed deadlines, extra handovers, and hidden unfinished work. Keep risk-based checks and a clear next-shift owner. | Check delay and completion, correction timing, seven-day rework, case lead time, aged unchecked cases, and handover failures. |
| 7. Add an extra senior reviewer at Day demand peaks | May improve complex-case decisions and bot oversight while reducing bottlenecks. | Could merely move the bottleneck or pull seniors from other shifts. | Adjusted Day peak errors/rework, bot correction outcomes, senior utilization, queue age, and waiting time across all shifts. |
| 8. Penalize workers with higher raw late-shift error rates | Raw penalties offer no reliable quality benefit; a supported case review might identify training or process needs. | Strong risk of unfair attribution, underreporting, avoiding hard cases, and rushed or skipped checks. Do not impose penalties from raw rates. | Reporting completeness, case mix and WIP by worker, adjusted outcomes with uncertainty, routing changes, and quality-check completion. |

Evaluate changes with a staged rollout where feasible and compare similar shifts before and after, allowing for demand, staffing, and case-mix changes. Track downstream quality and end-to-end time so a faster event cannot be mistaken for a better process.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Workers do not receive exchangeable cases. Seniors take complex carryover; night staff see urgent exceptions; juniors and others face different activity and WIP mixes. Raw worker rankings can therefore reverse after adjustment and may have wide uncertainty for small samples. Repeated events from one case are not independent. High WIP is partly created by demand, staffing, routing, and management targets, so attributing its consequences solely to individual effort is misleading.

Error flags can reflect when and by whom a problem is detected, not when it was caused. End-of-shift Quality Check bursts, incomplete seven-day follow-up, batched timestamps, overlapping activities, and inconsistent rework coding can distort both timing and worker attribution. Instant bot events lower pooled service time while later human corrections carry its cost. Audit automation inputs and outcomes, and make review ownership explicit before assigning responsibility.

Use adjusted comparisons, uncertainty intervals, case review, and staff input to improve staffing, routing, breaks, and checks. Avoid punitive use of raw late-shift errors: it can suppress reporting and quality checks precisely where they are needed. Preserve confidentiality and restrict individual-level views to legitimate coaching and investigation.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Show trends by shift, clock hour, activity, complexity band, priority, and bot/human path; allow drill-down from aggregates to de-identified cases. Display denominators, missing-data rates, and a consistent follow-up window for quality measures. Include:

| Indicator | Definition and useful breakdown |
|---|---|
| Service time | Median and 90th percentile human event end-minus-start minutes; by shift position, activity, WIP, and complexity. Report bot time separately. |
| Waiting and case lead time | Median and 90th percentile time before each activity and arrival-to-close duration; show queue age and overdue cases. |
| WIP and queue pressure | WIP and queue length at start, time above candidate thresholds, arrival/finish rates, and staffed capacity by clock hour. |
| Error rate | Error-flagged events per eligible reviewed events, by activity, position, WIP, and standardized case mix; show detection delay and missing flags. |
| Rework | Seven-day rework per fully observed eligible case, corrections per case, and time from original event to correction. |
| Complexity and priority mix | Mean/quantiles of complexity score and high-priority share by position, shift, worker group, and assignment route. |
| Batching | Count/share of same-resource activity runs (including 9–18-event Document Review runs), very short or identical timestamps, and batch-linked corrections. |
| Handover and queue ownership | Cases crossing shifts or resources, unowned/aged queue items, ownership changes, and wait after each handover. |
| Worker-load fairness | Per-worker and group distributions of assigned complexity, urgent cases, WIP exposure, active time, breaks, and overtime; pair outcomes with mix-adjusted uncertainty. |
| Automation and Quality Check | Bot_Risk classifications later corrected, correction rate by queue/complexity, time to human review, Quality Check delay, and end-of-shift check backlog. |

Flag changes in the joint pattern of speed, errors, rework, and waiting time. A drop in service time is favorable only if downstream quality and end-to-end resolution do not deteriorate.
