

# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let case index $c$ and branch index $i \in \{A, B, C\}$. Times are recorded in minutes from epoch.

*   **Upstream elapsed**: $U_c = \text{fork\_release}_c - \text{arrival}_c$.
*   **Branch queue wait**: $Q_{i,c} = \text{start}_{i,c} - \text{fork\_release}_c$.
*   **Branch service**: $S_{i,c} = \text{end}_{i,c} - \text{start}_{i,c}$.
*   **Branch completion**: $E_{i,c} = \text{start}_{i,c} + S_{i,c}$.
*   **Join ready**: $J_c = \max(A_{A,c}, A_{B,c}, A_{C,c})$.
*   **Branch sync wait**: $W_{i,c} = J_c - E_{i,c} \ge 0$.
*   **Downstream queue**: $D_c = \text{final\_start}_c - J_c \ge 0$.
*   **Final service**: $F_{service,c} = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{final\_start}_c$.
*   **Total flow**: $T_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{arrival}_c$.

Double counting arises because $J_c$ is a single point in time; summing synchronization waits $\sum_i (J_c - E_{i,c})$ treats each branch’s idle time as additive, but the total process time is bounded by $\max_i E_{i,c}$, not the sum of all ends.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

| branch | mean_service | mean_queue | critical_freq | mean_sync_wait |

- Critical branch counts: B (8), A (3), C (0).
- Influence ranking: B > A > C. B dominates both service time and queue, and frequently sets join_ready. C finishes early and causes synchronization waits but never controls completion.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

**1. Reduce A service by 20%**
- Direct benefit cases: F01, F04, F08 (where A is critical).
- Savings per case: 20% × A_service = 10 min per affected case  total reduction  30 min across 3 cases.
- Cases with zero benefit: B is critical (8 cases).
- Secondary effects: None; upstream/downstream unchanged.

**2. Reduce B queue by 50%**
- Direct benefit cases: All where B is critical (8 cases) and where B finish is within join_ready threshold (F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F11, F12).
- Savings per case: 0.5 × old_B_queue (e.g., F02 saves 10 min).
- Total savings: Approximately 100–110 min aggregate across cases.
- Secondary effects: Reduced downstream queue if B is critical and join_ready moves earlier.

**3. Reduce downstream queueing (increase final-station capacity)**
- Direct benefit cases: all 12 (current downstream queues vary 8–30).
- If downstream queue eliminated completely, total reductions equal sum(D_c)  203 min aggregate.
- Secondary effects: Final resource utilization increases, possibly leading to resource contention; sync waits unaffected.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

1. **Prioritize B queue reduction**: Implement dynamic scheduling to minimize B start delays (e.g., reserve resources at fork_release). Throughput improves when B is critical. Risk: resource over-provisioning may occur when B not critical. Pilot: measure B_queue distribution week 1.
2. **Add final-station capacity**: Introduce a third resource (F3) or implement pooling. Directly reduces downstream queues that persist even when branches are optimized. Risk: potential under-utilization during low volume; may require new coordination protocol. Pilot: simulate capacity addition on historical data and monitor queue tail.

# 7. Limits of inference

- No resource capacity data; we infer B queue waits but cannot confirm cause (e.g., single resource vs staffing shortage).
- Causality unobserved: we cannot confirm whether upstream arrival times affect fork_release policy or are random.
- No information about work preemption or interruptions during branches.
- Downstream waiting may reflect release policy, not actual queue congestion; F1 vs F2 load balance is opaque.
- Counterfactuals assume linear improvements; in practice, reducing queue may shift contention elsewhere.
