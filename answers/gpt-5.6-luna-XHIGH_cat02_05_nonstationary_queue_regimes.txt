# 1. Data audit and estimands

The observation window is 28 calendar days, each with 12 opening hours (07:00--19:00), for 336 open-hours. I use “per hour” below to mean per opening hour, not per elapsed clock hour including the overnight closure.

Let (A_d) be arrivals recorded during opening hours on day (d), (C_d) be completions recorded on that day, and (L_d) be the mean WIP from the 15-minute snapshots. For a window of (D) days, the directly estimable rates are

* arrival intensity \(\hat\lambda_A=\sum A_d/(12D)\) cases per open-hour;
* completion intensity [0m\(\hat\lambda_C=\sum C_d/(12D)\) completions per open-hour;
* mean WIP \(\bar L=\sum L_d/D\), in cases, assuming each daily mean is comparably constructed;
* daily net count \(A_d-C_d\), which is a flow imbalance, not necessarily the change in the queue at 07:00 or 19:00.

The service-time fields describe active handling time (S), in minutes, conditional on completed/observed service cases. They are a median and p90, not (E[S]). They therefore cannot be substituted for mean flow time (W), which would include queue waiting, active handling, and possibly overnight time. `staff_fte` is mean concurrent FTE; a rough rostered labor-minute denominator under a strong assumption of fully productive coverage is \(720\times\text{staff\_fte}\) minutes per day. It is not a measured service capacity unless productive fraction, skill mix, breaks, and availability by hour are known.

The percentage fields need weighting care. A simple mean of daily percentages is an equal-day summary. An arrival-weighted percentage is closer to the share of all arrivals, but is still approximate because each displayed percentage is rounded. The hourly vectors are counts in the 12 bins 07--08 through 18--19.

Important threats to naive inference are:

1. The backlog at 07:00 is missing. Cases can cross 19:00 and wait overnight, so daily arrivals minus completions does not identify the within-day queue change or the true starting population.
2. A completion need not have arrived on the same day. The 4/16, 4/17, and 4/29--5/1 completion surpluses are plausibly backlog clearance, not evidence that same-day service capacity exceeded same-day demand.
3. The 4/15 outage lasts about 180 minutes, but its exact start/end and the intervention start on 4/29 are not logged. A daily average mixes unaffected and disrupted intervals.
4. Daily aggregation hides the ordering of arrivals and completions. Two days with the same totals can have very different peak queues and waiting times; the representative 4/21 vector demonstrates this risk.
5. Median and p90 active service times do not provide the mean service time or its variance. Little's Law may estimate a residence-time-like quantity from WIP and throughput only under compatible boundary and stability conditions; it cannot turn a median service time into flow time.
6. Mean WIP from 15-minute snapshots is not a case-level flow-time sample and may include cases that arrived before the window. It is also not supplied at hourly resolution, so the hourly vectors cannot be paired with hourly WIP.
7. The rows are serially dependent and have a changing mix of weekdays, weekends, outage, recovery, and intervention. Treating 28 rows as independent replicates will understate uncertainty and can mistake a trend for overdispersion.
8. Complex-case percentage, p90 service, and peak share change together. The apparent case-mix change could be operationally real, a coding/variant change, or both; it is confounded with demand and staffing.
9. `staff_fte` is a daily concurrent mean, not an hourly schedule or effective capacity. It does not reveal whether extra FTE covered the burst, had the necessary skills, or was spent on recovery work.
10. The daily `peak_08_10_pct` values do not reconcile with the supplied representative vectors. For example, on 4/21 the vector gives \((3+8)/115=9.6\%\) in 08:00--10:00, while the daily field says 47%; 4/7 gives 16.8% versus 31%. This is much more than rounding. I use the two sources as separate evidence and do not combine them into one exact peak estimate.

# 2. Primary regime segmentation

I use a calendar-plus-event segmentation. Weekday/weekend is separated because the demand and staffing scales differ; the outage and recovery are separated because completions during recovery are not normal steady-state throughput; and the 4/20 mix/burst change is separated from the later intervention-containing dates. Estimates in the table are arithmetic means per day, with the parenthesized values being cases per open-hour. “Median/p90” are minutes. The two workload indices are only stress indicators: arrivals multiplied by the displayed median or p90, divided by (720\times) mean FTE. Neither is utilization, because the service-time mean is unknown.

The primary story is therefore not “capacity improved” in one undifferentiated step. It is: a near-balanced low-mix baseline; a one-day outage that adds a large backlog; explicit catch-up; a subsequent high-mix and bursty demand/service regime in which WIP grows; then a staffing/intervention-containing period with completion surplus and declining WIP, but with insufficient timing and case-level data for a causal estimate.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different alternative is a two-axis operational-state segmentation rather than a chronological segmentation. First classify demand by volume and burstiness, then classify service/capacity by mix and available FTE; retain outage as an overriding state:

| Alternative state | Rule suggested by the data | Operational story represented |
|---|---|---|
| Low-volume/low-capacity | Weekend-like counts, approximately 36--53 arrivals/day, 2.5--3.0 FTE | A separate service calendar with lower demand and staffing, not merely a low observation of the weekday process |
| Ordinary low-mix | No outage, complex roughly 18--33%, median service 24--33, and no sustained high-WIP signal | The baseline service process, including the possibility that a low-mix recovery day resembles it in demand but not in backlog state |
| High-mix/high-burst demand | Complex at least about 39% or peak field at least about 40%, with 4/21 as the clearest burst example | A changed demand and service requirement, regardless of whether it is caused by business demand or coding |
| Recovery/high-capacity | Completion minus arrivals materially negative and/or FTE at least 6.5, but with inherited WIP | A backlog-clearing mode; high completions should not be interpreted as a stable service rate |
| Disrupted | `outage_min` greater than zero | A capacity interruption with time-varying service, kept separate from every normal regime |

This alternative would allow a date to have more than one label: for example, 4/29 is simultaneously high-mix, high-capacity, and recovery. That is useful for asking whether additional FTE offsets a changed workload, whereas the primary segmentation is better for operational chronology and change management.

Its strength is causal discipline: it separates demand/mix from staffing and backlog state and avoids attributing every post-4/29 improvement to the intervention. Its weakness is that the thresholds are data-dependent, sample sizes become very small, and states can overlap. It also cannot identify a true latent regime without event-level observations; thresholds such as 39% complex are pragmatic labels, not discovered ground truth.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Across all dates there are 2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions, or 7.50 and 7.17 per open-hour. Mean daily WIP is 29.9, mean daily median service is 31.0 minutes, mean daily p90 is 67.1 minutes, mean daily FTE is 5.14, and the equal-day means of complex and peak percentages are 32.9% and 32.3%. An arrival-weighted calculation gives approximately 33.8% complex and 35.3% peak share, illustrating that even the definition of a pooled percentage matters.

| Quantity | Pooled summary | Segmented summaries that it conceals | Consequence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Arrivals and completions per open-hour | 7.50 / 7.17 | Baseline weekday 8.35 / 8.30; weekend 3.74 / 3.60; mix weekday 9.50 / 8.38; intervention weekday 9.58 / 10.08; outage day 8.75 / 4.83 | The pooled small deficit is a mixture of balanced ordinary days, low-volume weekends, a severe outage deficit, and recovery surpluses |
| WIP | 29.9 cases | Baseline weekday 20.3; baseline weekend 13.0; outage 47; mix weekday 44.3; intervention weekday 37.0 | A staffing decision calibrated to 29.9 cases is too high for quiet weekends but too low for high-mix weekdays and says nothing about the outage peak |
| Complex mix | 32.9% equal-day, approximately 33.8% arrival-weighted | Baseline weekday 20.9%; mix weekday 44.6%; intervention weekday 44.0% | Pooling turns a roughly doubled weekday complex share into an apparently moderate average and can make rising p90 service look like random noise |
| Active service tail | Mean of daily medians 31.0 and mean of daily p90s 67.1 | Baseline weekday median/p90 25/47; mix weekday 35/83; intervention weekday 32.7/74 | The pooled p90 is not the p90 of the pooled case population, and it hides the tail shift from 47 to 83 minutes |
| Staffing and pressure | Mean daily FTE 5.14 | Baseline and mix weekdays both average about 5.93 FTE; intervention weekdays 6.83; weekends 2.62; outage day 6.0 | The pooled FTE is dominated by the eight low-staffed weekends and does not represent the weekday operating policy |

Weekday/weekend composition is itself a Simpson-like issue. Weekdays are 20/28 = 71.4% of rows but contribute 2,160/2,519 = 85.7% of arrivals. The pooled arrival rate of 7.50/h is between a weekday 9.00/h mean over all weekdays and a weekend 3.74/h mean, but it is not the rate of either operating policy. Similarly, the pooled WIP is not a typical case’s WIP because it averages different calendars and states.

The outage gives a sharper reversal. On 4/15, arrivals are close to the ordinary weekday scale, but completions fall to 58 and net count is +47. On the next two weekdays, completions average 120.5 against 105.5 arrivals, so a pooled completion rate can look acceptable or even productive because it includes catch-up work. That is the wrong denominator for evaluating service under a normal, stable demand state.

There is also an intervention interpretation trap. After 4/29, weekday completions average 10.08/h versus 8.38/h in the preceding high-mix weekday segment and WIP averages 37 versus 44.3. This is consistent with useful additional capacity, but it is not a clean treatment effect: the intervention starts at an unknown time on 4/29, completion counts include inherited backlog, and the later weekend reduces demand. A pooled average can therefore make the intervention look uniformly successful, while a segmented view says it helped recovery but has not restored the low-mix service distribution (p90 is still 74 versus 47 minutes at baseline).

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

The representative arrival vectors support a strong intraday distinction, conditional on accepting them as representative:

| Representative day | Total arrivals | CV across 12 hourly bins | Fano/index of dispersion across bins | Maximum / hourly mean |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4/7 ordinary weekday | 101 | 0.62 | 3.23 | 2.38 |
| 4/15 outage day | 105 | 0.69 | 4.14 | 2.51 |
| 4/21 post-mix-change weekday | 115 | 1.03 | 10.12 | 3.55 |
| 4/25 weekend | 51 | 0.55 | 1.29 | 1.88 |

Here CV is sample standard deviation divided by the bin mean, and the Fano value is sample variance divided by the bin mean. These are dispersion indicators across the twelve positions in one day, not estimates of the event-count Fano factor from repeated realizations of the same hour. Even a stationary process with a deterministic diurnal profile can have a value above one when the bins are pooled. The very high 4/21 value nevertheless signals concentrated demand: the 10:00--11:00 bin has 34 arrivals and 13:00--14:00 has 24, separated by much quieter bins. This also creates transient queues even if the 12-hour total is similar to other weekdays.

The completion vectors add a mechanism. On the ordinary day, completions broadly track arrivals and the total is 99 versus 101. On the outage day, arrivals continue while completions are zero in approximately the 11:00--14:00 bins, followed by later completions; the daily total is only 58. On 4/21, the total is 101 completions against 115 arrivals, but the hourly pattern does not mean the queue grew smoothly at 14 cases. It grew sharply during the two arrival bursts and was partially worked down in other bins.

The daily sequence is also nonstationary. Calendar-week means move from approximately 81.6 arrivals/day in week 1 to 87.6, 93.3, and 97.4 in weeks 2--4. Mean WIP moves from 17.7 to 29.6, 36.3, and 35.9; mean complex share moves from 20.6% to 24.3%, 43.0%, and 43.6%. The 4/15 net +47 discontinuity and the sustained mix shift from 4/20 onward are change-point evidence, not just noisy daily variation.

For the daily arrival counts, the all-row sample CV is about 0.33 and the all-row Fano ratio is about 10.0. Within weekdays the CV is only about 0.08 and Fano about 0.66; within weekends the CV is about 0.13 and Fano about 0.73. The pooled overdispersion is therefore largely a regime-mixture and trend effect, not evidence by itself for a highly overdispersed arrival process within a fixed regime. A lag-1 autocorrelation of arrivals is about 0.41 over all ordered rows and about 0.54 on the weekday subset. Weekend-only lag correlation is about -0.43, but it is based on only eight observations and is not a reliable stochastic estimate. Lag-1 autocorrelation of daily net (A-C) is near 0.02 overall, which does not imply independence: completion response, weekday effects, and the large one-day shock can cancel in that particular statistic.

The daily peak-share field would imply much larger 08:00--10:00 concentration than the vectors. For example, the four vector-derived shares are about 16.8%, 18.1%, 9.6%, and 11.8%, whereas the corresponding daily fields are 31%, 32%, 47%, and 22%. Since the vectors sum exactly to the listed daily arrivals, this is a definition or data reconciliation problem, not a small rounding error. Burstiness conclusions based on the vectors should therefore be labeled conditional, and the peak field should not be used to validate those vectors until the source definitions are checked.

Four representative days cannot estimate a complete stochastic model: there are too few realizations to estimate tail probabilities, serial dependence at multiple lags, day-of-week effects, or the distribution of burst timing. The supplied summaries support regime hypotheses and stress indicators, not a fitted arrival or service distribution.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law is (L=\lambda W) for a compatible population over a sufficiently stable long-run window. Here the WIP is an open-hours snapshot mean, while arrival and completion counts are daily flows and cases can cross both the 07:00 and 19:00 boundaries. The following implied values are therefore diagnostics unless the stated conditions are met. I show both (L/\lambda_A) and (L/\lambda_C) where useful; the first uses arrivals as a throughput proxy and the second uses completions. The units are open-hours, not elapsed clock-hours.

| Candidate window/regime | Boundary stability | Throughput proxy | WIP comparability | Carry-over risk | Stationarity assessment | Implied flow-time calculation if defensible | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All 28 days | Poor: weekends, outage, mix shift, recovery, and intervention are combined | 7.50/h arrivals or 7.17/h completions | Mean WIP is aggregated over incompatible states | High; cumulative arrivals minus completions is +110 | Clearly nonstationary | No reported (L/\lambda) estimate; any pooled quotient would be a mixture average, not a queueing-law estimate | not defensible |
| Baseline weekdays, 4/6--4/14 | Reasonably consistent open-day boundaries, but 07:00 backlog is unknown | 8.35/h arrivals; 8.30/h completions | WIP 20.3 is at least aligned by day type | Moderate because overnight cases are possible, though net flow is near zero | Approximately stable over only 7 days | (20.29/8.35=2.43) open-hours using arrivals; (20.29/8.30=2.44) using completions | approximate only |
| 4/15 outage day | Unstable within the day; exact outage boundary unavailable | 8.75/h arrivals, 4.83/h completions | Daily WIP includes outage accumulation and does not resolve its timing | Very high | Not stationary | No valid quotient; the completion rate is suppressed by the outage | not defensible |
| Recovery weekdays, 4/16--4/17 | Open boundaries are consistent but the queue is deliberately being drained | 8.79/h arrivals; 10.04/h completions | WIP 43.5 includes inherited backlog | Very high | Nonstationary by design | Diagnostic only: (43.5/8.79=4.95) h and (43.5/10.04=4.33) h, with no steady-state interpretation | not defensible |
| High-mix weekdays, 4/20--4/28 | Same calendar boundaries, but WIP and mix are changing | 9.50/h arrivals; 8.38/h completions | WIP 44.3 is aligned by day type, not by a stable state | High; net +13.4/day indicates accumulation | Nonstationary or at best transient | Diagnostic only: (44.29/9.50=4.66) h and (44.29/8.38=5.29) h; the disagreement reflects accumulation and boundary effects | approximate only |
| Intervention-containing weekdays, 4/29--5/1 | Intervention starts at an unknown time and the interval is only 3 days | 9.58/h arrivals; 10.08/h completions | WIP 37 is comparable descriptively, but treatment exposure is mixed | High; completion surplus is clearance | Nonstationary/transient | Diagnostic only: (37/9.58=3.86) h and (37/10.08=3.67) h | not defensible |
| One representative hourly vector | The hourly count boundary is clear, but no hourly WIP is supplied | Hourly arrivals/completions are available | No compatible hourly (L) | Unknown | Cannot assess from one day | No (L/\lambda) calculation possible | not defensible |

The baseline weekday quotients are the most defensible numerical diagnostics, but even they should not be called measured mean flow time. A 2.4 open-hour residence estimate is much longer than the 25-minute median active handling time, which is qualitatively consistent with waiting and/or carry-over, but the difference cannot be quantified because mean active service and the opening/closing boundary populations are missing. An exact flow-time analysis needs case-level arrival and completion timestamps, including cases arriving before the window and completing after it.

# 7. Operational implications

Staffing and backlog recovery should be managed as separate policies. The outage should trigger a recorded disruption state, a recovery target, and temporary capacity or triage; the 4/16--4/17 completion surplus should be measured as backlog clearance, not rewarded as ordinary productivity. A useful operational dashboard would show arrivals, completions, starting backlog, ending backlog, WIP, and open-hour exposure for each state.

For the high-mix weekday state, staff planning should use the 9.5 arrivals/h and 35/83-minute median/p90 profile rather than the pooled 7.5 arrivals/h and 31/67-minute summary. Skills or routing for complex variants may matter as much as headcount. The intervention-containing weekdays show a plausible improvement in recovery, but the date and time of the first additional FTE must be logged before estimating a causal effect.

Peak smoothing is attractive, but it cannot be designed from the current fields until the daily peak percentage and hourly vector definitions are reconciled. Once corrected, staggered appointments, intake throttling, self-service for simple variants, or moving preparatory work before the dominant burst could reduce transient queues without changing the daily total. Evaluation should use hourly WIP and waiting-time percentiles, not only daily completions.

The most valuable data additions are: case ID; arrival timestamp; first service start; every activity start/end; completion timestamp; queue or channel; variant and complexity code version; resource/skill; hourly staffing; outage start/end; 07:00 backlog; 19:00 closing backlog; and explicit treatment of overnight cases. A case-level event log would allow flow-time, waiting-time, throughput, and variant analyses with consistent population boundaries.

A policy that looks beneficial under pooled averages but is risky under segmentation is to staff to the pooled mean of about 5.1 FTE and judge success by the pooled completion rate of 7.17/h. Low-volume weekends pull the staffing average down, the outage depresses completions, and recovery days inflate completions. That policy would miss the high-mix weekday demand of 9.5/h and its long tail, allowing WIP to grow, while making a later recovery shift look successful because it processes inherited backlog. A safer policy sets separate weekday/weekend baselines, adds burst coverage and complex-case capability, and treats outage recovery as a transient control mode.

# Two dispersion indicators: CV and Fano/index of dispersion.
def dispersion(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    return pd.Series({"mean": x.mean(), "cv": x.std(ddof=1) / x.mean(),
                      "fano_sample": x.var(ddof=1) / x.mean(),
                      "max_over_mean": x.max() / x.mean()})

hourly_dispersion = []
for row in hourly.itertuples(index=False):
    d = dispersion(row.arrivals)
    d["kind"] = row.kind
    d["total_arrivals"] = sum(row.arrivals)
    d["vector_08_10_pct"] = 100 * sum(row.arrivals[1:3]) / sum(row.arrivals)
    hourly_dispersion.append(d)
print("\nHOURLY DISPERSION (within representative vector)")
print(pd.DataFrame(hourly_dispersion).set_index("kind").round(2).to_string())

# Transparent, deliberately non-definitive segmentation heuristic:
# a three-day sustained complex share >= 39% marks a candidate mix change;
# an outage row overrides it; the known intervention date is only a marker,
# because its within-day implementation time is missing.
high_mix = daily["complex_pct"] >= 39
sustained = high_mix.rolling(3).sum().eq(3)
if sustained.any():
    first_run_end = sustained[sustained].index[0]
    mix_start = daily.loc[first_run_end - 2, "date"]
else:
    mix_start = pd.NaT

heuristic = np.select(
    [daily["outage_min"] > 0,
     (dates >= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-29")),
     (dates >= mix_start) if pd.notna(mix_start) else np.zeros(len(daily), dtype=bool),
     (dates > pd.Timestamp("2026-04-15")) & (dates < pd.Timestamp("2026-04-20"))],
    ["outage", "intervention-containing", "high-mix candidate", "recovery"],
    default="ordinary/calendar state")
daily["heuristic_regime"] = heuristic
print("\nHEURISTIC CHANGE-POINT")
print("first sustained high-mix run starts:", mix_start.date() if pd.notna(mix_start) else None)
print(daily[["date", "heuristic_regime"]].to_string(index=False))

# Little's-Law diagnostics are calculated only for explicitly chosen windows.
# They are not asserted to be steady-state flow times.
ll_windows = {"baseline_weekday": candidate_masks["baseline_weekday"],
              "mix_weekday": candidate_masks["mix_weekday"],
              "intervention_weekday": candidate_masks["intervention_weekday"]}
ll_rows = []
for name, mask in ll_windows.items():
    g = daily[mask]
    L = g["mean_wip"].mean()
    lam_a = g["arrivals"].sum() / (OPEN_HOURS * len(g))
    lam_c = g["completed"].sum() / (OPEN_HOURS * len(g))
    ll_rows.append({"window": name, "L": L, "lambda_arrival": lam_a,
                    "lambda_completion": lam_c,
                    "L_over_lambda_arrival_open_h": L / lam_a,
                    "L_over_lambda_completion_open_h": L / lam_c})

print(pd.DataFrame(ll_rows).set_index("window").round(2).to_string())
