The winter problem is a change in mix and variability. Weekly volume rises from about 2,300 cases to about 3,300 (+43%), while routine volume stays flat at about 1,400 cases per week. Urgent volume rises from about 410 to about 960 per week (2.3×) and complex-panel volume from about 300 to about 660 (2.2×). Repeat-after-failure rises about 1.6×. Urgent median cycle time at the peak is still 17h, just inside the 18h penalty line, while urgent p90 is 33h, routine p90 is 120h against a 72h target, and complex-panel p90 is 210h. A redesign aimed at the historical average, or at the urgent median, will miss the penalties and the long-tail queues.

Edge medians cannot be added up to a case cycle time. They are waits across all case types. Urgent cases survive only by bypassing those waits. That bypass is already eroding: urgent cases skip batch wait in 74% of normal cases and only 58% of peak cases. Because the log is one row per order, complex-panel multi-assay load is understated in the transition counts and shows up instead in machine utilization and in the complex cycle times.

## 1. Stress scenarios

Weekly baselines used below: normal routine ~1,400, urgent ~410, complex-panel ~300, repeat-after-failure ~180; peak routine ~1,390, urgent ~960, complex-panel ~660, repeat-after-failure ~300.

**S1 — Infection-wave mix shift (volume and mix, no extra asset failure).** The observed peak: +43% cases, routine flat, urgent 2.3×, complex-panel 2.2×, repeat-after-failure 1.6×. Urgent plus complex-panel becomes about half of arrivals (49% versus 31%). Rework rises from 7.5% to 11.8%. Cases with at least one queue over 12h rise from 22% to 54%.

**S2 — Assay degradation or outage, concentrated on repeat-prone work.** Two branches on top of S1. Branch A: M2 failure rate rises further on night shifts (the log already clusters repeat-after-failure and in-process repeats after night shifts and M2). Branch B: M1 is down for 24–48h, so the faster, less reliable machine is the only assay capacity. Repeat-after-failure orders already consume an assay slot twice; in-process repeat handoffs rise from 6.7% of assay outcomes (1,140 / 17,030) to 14.8% (1,260 / 8,500), and weekly repeat handoffs rise from about 140 to about 420.

**S3 — Validator absence during the same mix shift.** The specialist is already off every second Friday, and hold-for-specialist-review inflow rises from about 36 cases a week to about 200. This scenario extends that hole through Monday (illness) and removes one medical validator in the same week. Complex-panel cases are 2.4× more likely to enter specialist review and 3.1× more likely to need medical validation, so the absence hits complex-panel first and urgent cases second. Routine cases mostly skip both steps (normal technical validation sends 74% of cases straight to release).

**S4 — Courier compression or delay.** Arrivals already spike at 10:00 and 17:00. Branch A (compression): a slipped morning route lands on the 17:00 spike, so most of the day arrives in one window. Branch B (delay): weather holds samples on the road, so register-to-receive stretches past the peak median of 8h and the urgent 18h clock burns before the lab starts. Registration clerks are a related constraint: error rates rise after 9h shifts, and those errors feed the two accession specialists.

**S5 — Correlated winter day.** S1 mix, plus an M2-heavy evening, plus the uncovered specialist Friday, plus a compressed 17:00 dump. This is the realistic case. Policies that work on a single shock collide here.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Two measurements should frame every sequence. Technical validation is the only pool already over capacity at the peak (101%, median queue 3.5h → 16h). A station above 100% does not settle at a painful-but-stable queue; the queue trends up for as long as the overload lasts. Specialist review is the opposite pattern: utilization only goes from 43% to 87%, but the median queue goes from 5h to 36h and the hold-to-medical edge from 18h to 48h, because one person with a scheduled zero-capacity Friday cannot absorb a 5.6× inflow.

Referral intensity rose far beyond the mix shift. Applying the stated 3.1× medical and 2.4× specialist factors to the peak mix predicts roughly 27–29% of technical-validation outcomes going to medical validation and about 2% going to specialist review. The peak log shows 42% (3,420 / 8,080) and 7.6% (610 / 8,080). Direct release collapses from 74% to 50%. That excess is on the order of 400 extra medical referrals per week and about 150 extra specialist holds per week. Some of the gap may be urgent propensity that a one-factor model does not separate, but the gap is too large to treat as mix arithmetic. Peak behavior is escalating uncertain cases into the scarcest clinical steps. The release step itself barely moves (medical-to-release 2h → 5h). Holding routine releases for a batch report (I9) targets a step that is not the constraint.

**S1 sequence.** The mix hits medical validation and specialist review before it hits the front door. Medical inflow from technical validation rises from about 490 cases a week to about 1,140, while utilization moves from 58% to 93% and the median queue from 2h to 22h. Specialist inflow rises about 5.6× against a single reviewer. In parallel, complex-panel multi-assay work and a 1.6× repeat-after-failure load push assay machines from 71% to 98% and the median queue from 4h to 18h. Repeat handoffs then put the same cases back on the machine (repeat-to-assay 10h → 26h), so the machine bottleneck feeds itself. Technical validation crosses 100% as that output arrives. Pre-analytical check is the early quality bottleneck, not the first capacity bottleneck: direct pass-through falls from 93% (16,860 / 18,120) to 88% (8,530 / 9,730), recollection requests rise from 4.2% to 8.0% of checks, and accession corrections that leak into recollection rise from 14% (70 / 500) to 26% (110 / 420). Recollection return stretches from 42h to 60h and the cancel share of recollection requests rises from 35% to 42%.

Relieving one S1 station moves the pile. A strict urgent-first rule at preparation and assay (I1) pulls urgent p90 back toward 18h and immediately starves routine cases, whose p90 is already 120h, and complex-panel cases, whose median is already 111h. Starved cases stay in the lab, so work-in-process rises and the next urgent arrivals see a fuller system. Faster assay clearance dumps into technical validation at 101%, then into medical validation at a 22h queue. An evening medical shift (I5) that is not fed by technical validation sits idle in the evening and is swamped the next morning. Clearing medical validation without a specialist on duty only helps the cases that are not among the 610 holds. Larger routine batches (I2) make the already-falling urgent bypass worse: batch-wait entries rise from 760 to 1,180, in line with urgent cases that miss the bypass (roughly 0.26 × 3,300 ≈ 860 normal; 0.42 × 2,870 ≈ 1,210 peak), while routine weekly volume is flat. Bigger batches also deliver lumpy waves to assay and technical validation, which are already at 98% and 101%.

**S2 sequence.** The first bottleneck is the surviving or degraded machine. At 98% with both machines up, losing M1 or pushing more work onto a failing M2 removes the small slack that still exists. Repeats are a second bottleneck on the same resource: each failed run occupies the machine, waits a peak median of 26h, and runs again. Branch A (worse M2) hits routine cases if they have been forced onto M2, and hits repeat-after-failure cases because they already cluster there. Branch B (M1 down) hits urgent and complex-panel cases, because those are the cases a dedicated-M1 rule (I3) would have concentrated on the machine that is now gone. Technical validation is next: repeat and delayed first-pass results arrive as a lump, on a pool already at 101%, and uncertain results are escalated, which is how S2 inflates the S1 medical and specialist queues. Pre-analytical check is upstream and looks artificially calm while the machine is the dam.

Dedicating M1 to urgent and complex-panel work and sending all routine volume to M2 (I3) is the wrong standing answer to S2. Routine is still about 1,400 cases a week. Placing that load on the higher-failure machine during a high-load period raises the repeat rate, and those repeats return to assay and then to technical validation. I3 also fails outright in branch B: there is no M1 to dedicate. I1 on a single degraded machine lets urgent and complex-panel panels monopolize the surviving capacity and leaves routine and repeat-after-failure parked at preparation (peak median queue already 11h).

**S3 sequence.** Holds park immediately. The hold-to-medical edge is already 48h at the peak; a Friday-plus-Monday absence turns that into a multi-day wait and breaches complex-panel turnaround and any urgent case that happens to need review. While the specialist is gone, assay and technical validation can look healthier because finished complex-panel work is stuck in review rather than moving. That is false relief. The return-to-work day releases a bolus into medical validation (peak median queue 22h, 93% utilization). If a medical validator is also absent, the bolus has nowhere to go and urgent cases that need medical sign-off miss 18h even when their assay was on time. Release remains able to absorb a normal medical outflow (edge median only 5h at the peak). The overload on return is medical validation, not release.

Adding the evening medical shift on the days the specialist is gone, without limiting new complex-panel starts, keeps filling a review queue that cannot drain. Outsourcing complex-panel overflow (I10) removes local specialist load and adds a 12h transport leg plus a traceability handoff. Those partner results still need local medical validation, so I10 recreates the bolus. Urgent cases cannot spend 12h on the road inside an 18h limit. I4 (moving technical validators to accession correction) does not touch this scenario and makes S1’s 101% pool worse.

**S4 sequence.** The first bottleneck is outside the analyzers. Peak courier utilization is 88% and the median queue is 7.5h; register-to-receive is already 5h → 8h. Compression stacks that intake onto pre-analytical check (96%, median queue 1.8h → 9.5h). At that utilization a burst raises the defect rate: the peak already shows recollection requests almost doubling and accession-to-recollection leakage at 26%. Clerks then spend time explaining recollections, which removes registration capacity, and shifts past 9h raise error rates, which hit the two accession specialists (91%, median queue 14h). Good samples arrive at preparation and assay as a herd one shift later. Recollected samples return about 60h later and create a second herd. Delay (branch B) is harsher for urgent cases because the 18h clock loses the transport hours before any internal priority rule can act; each internal queue that an urgent case fails to skip (pre-analytical 9.5h, preparation 11h, assay 18h, technical validation 16h, medical 22h) is then large enough on its own to finish the breach.

Smoothing courier slots (I7) reduces the burst into pre-analytical check and, because queues are nonlinear near 96–101%, cuts the spike by more than it cuts average load. It does not reduce daily assay or technical-validation work, so those stations stay the next bottleneck on a smoothed day. Automating registration for clean orders (I8) during a compression day accelerates the herd into pre-analytical check. I1 during a 17:00 dump lets urgent tubes jump the herd and fragments the evening machine schedule, so the routine remainder collides with the next morning spike.

**S5 sequence.** The shocks stack in time rather than averaging out. The 17:00 dump fills pre-analytical check and creates tomorrow’s accession and recollection load. The same evening, M2 and the night shift generate repeats that occupy the next day’s machines (26h rerun waits). Complex-panel work started that afternoon reaches specialist review on the uncovered Friday and sits until Monday, then hits medical validation together with the partner-lab returns and the urgent cases that missed their bypass. Technical validation stays over 100% the whole time because repeats and the herd both pass through it. The dangerous management response is to chase whichever queue is longest this morning: pulling people out of technical validation to accession correction (I4), enlarging routine batches (I2), or holding routine releases (I9). Each of those feeds the next day’s bottleneck.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The standing design has six parts. It assumes winter is declared early enough to use the four-week cross-training window, that urgent and complex-panel work cannot both be first, and that a minimum share of machine time must remain with routine cases whenever both machines are up. It will fail if M1 is fully down, if metadata quality is too poor for gated auto-registration, or if the same infection wave disables the partner lab.

**1. Reorder pre-analytical check so obvious defects exit within 2 hours (I6), as a replacement for the front of the current check.** Peak detection waits behind a 9.5h queue, then a recollection costs 60h and often a cancel. About 12% of peak checks already leave the straight path (780 recollections + 420 corrections out of 9,730). Pulling doomed tubes off preparation and assay protects the 98% machines and the 101% technical-validation pool. This is robust across S1, S4, and S5 because bad samples appear in mix shifts and in arrival herds. It depends on the screen being the start of the existing check. An extra touch for every tube at 96% utilization lengthens the queue the change is meant to shorten. Downstream, recollections hit couriers and registration clerks; script the physician contact so clerks do not absorb it as unscheduled calls. A second arrival wave follows about 60h later and must have intake slots waiting. If the screen is sensitive and non-specific, cancel rate rises from an already elevated 5.5% of peak cases (540 / 9,900).

**2. Split the courier plan (I7).** Spread routine pickups across the day. Keep a separate urgent pickup; never hold an urgent sample at a clinic to flatten the lab. Publish route ETAs so preparation does not hold a machine batch open for a late van. This is robust in S1 and S4 because pre-analytical, preparation, and assay queues are all in the range where arrival variance dominates. It depends on clinics accepting a routine window change. Downstream, assay and technical validation still receive the day’s full viable volume, so smoothing without the slot rules below only shortens spikes. A region-wide road closure collapses the smooth plan back into S4; staff the triage screen to the delayed ETA.

**3. Replace I1, I2, and I3 with a three-lane machine schedule and a routine floor.** Keep batch size at 12 during the day. Use a larger overnight routine batch only when that week’s M2 repeat rate is under 8% and no urgent work is projected to miss 18h. Reserve M1 time in this order: urgent cases whose age projects an 18h miss; complex-panel cases that have a medical validator and a specialist on duty within 24h of the expected result; repeat-after-failure cases in a fixed daytime M1 block, off M2 and off the night shift; routine cases in the remaining time, at least 30% of daily slots if both machines are up. Run ordinary routine work on M2. Do not run urgent, complex-panel, or repeat-after-failure work on M2 at night. This is the intervention that attacks the repeat spiral (6.7% → 14.8%, rerun wait 10h → 26h) without giving every class priority. It is robust in S1 and in S2 branch A. It depends on planners enforcing the floor; a “priority” culture will spend the floor on the loudest urgent case and re-create the work-in-process spiral I1 causes. Downstream, protected M1 output arrives sooner at technical validation and medical validation, so this schedule is unsafe unless parts 4 and 5 are active. Fragmented M1 runs also lower effective throughput; that cost lands on routine p90.

**4. Point the four-week cross-training at technical validation, and use auto-registration to free the donors (I4 reversed, plus gated I8).** Technical validation is the only pool at 101%, and registration’s peak median queue is only 2.8h. Before winter, qualify registration staff to do technical validation. At the same time, automate registration only for orders with complete digital metadata, and cap remaining clerk shifts at 9h so the error rate does not rise. Incomplete orders stay with clerks. One technical validator can be qualified as backup for the two accession specialists, and may move only when the accession queue exceeds 10h, at least one urgent case is blocked there, and the technical-validation queue is under 8h. Training technical validators into accession correction as a peak tactic (literal I4) moves labor from a 16h, all-case queue to a 14h, low-volume queue (about 140 corrections a week at the peak) and is not the standing rule. Downstream of auto-registration is pre-analytical check at 96%, which is why auto-registration waits until the 2h defect screen is in place. Downstream of moving clerks is registration accuracy after 9h and then accession correction. Downstream of a careless swing onto accession work is a longer technical-validation queue and more defensive medical referrals.

**5. Run a triggered evening medical-validation shift only when technical validation actually feeds it (I5).** Medical validation’s normal utilization is 58%, so a permanent extra shift is idle outside the wave. Turn the evening shift on when urgent plus complex-panel share exceeds 40% (normal is 31%, peak is 49%) and the medical queue median exceeds 6h, or when specialist hold WIP will be released the next day. Turn it off after three days with medical median queue under 3h and urgent p90 projected under 16h. Staff a short late-day technical-validation clearance of urgent and complex-panel cases so the evening shift receives work. Freeze peak escalation rules: clean routine cases that would have gone straight to release in the normal period still go straight to release. The peak’s jump from 25% to 42% medical routing is the main reason a 58% station becomes a 22h queue. Downstream of a working evening shift is release, whose edge only moved from 2h to 5h. If the technical-validation clearance is skipped, the evening shift shows little benefit and will be cancelled just before the specialist-return bolus.

**6. Close the known specialist Friday during declared peak weeks, and use the partner lab only as a complex-panel overflow valve (I10 narrowed).** A scheduled absence is visible in advance; about 200 holds a week cannot sit with one person who is gone on Friday (queue 36h, sojourn 48h). Keep a backup reviewer on that Friday for peak weeks. Send complex-panel overflow to the partner only when local hold age exceeds 24h and the partner can start promptly. Never send urgent cases: 12h transport consumes most of the 18h limit. While the specialist backlog is above one day’s capacity, stop starting new complex-panel runs at preparation. Otherwise assay keeps manufacturing work the reviewer cannot see. Downstream of Friday backup is a medical-validation bolus, which is why the evening medical shift is pre-scheduled on that Friday and on the return day. Downstream of outsourcing is transport, traceability, and the same medical-validation bolus when results return. The partner valve fails if the partner is in the same regional wave.

Standing rejections, because they fail more than one scenario: enlarge routine batches from 12 to 24 (I2) while urgent bypass is falling and batch-to-assay is already 9h → 22h; hold routine releases for a batch report (I9) while release is not the constraint and routine p90 is 120h; hard-dedicate M1 and dump routine onto M2 (I3); strict urgent-first preemption with no routine floor (I1).

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

**S1 policy — escalation freeze and complex-panel cap.** On when, for two consecutive days, urgent plus complex-panel share exceeds 40% or weekly urgent arrivals exceed 1.5× the normal ~410. Off when share stays under 35% and medical queue median stays under 3h for three days. While on: apply the three-lane schedule, the routine 30% floor, the evening medical shift if its own trigger is met, and a daily complex-panel start cap equal to specialist slots in the next 24h plus any confirmed partner slots. The cap waits at preparation. Preparation’s queue (peak median 11h) is the downstream cost; sample-stability timeouts must jump the cap ahead of new routine batches.

**S2 policy — protect the reliable machine.** On when the trailing 24h repeat rate exceeds 12% overall or 8% on M2, or when either machine is declared down. Off when both machines are up and the 24h repeat rate is under 8% for two days. While on: move routine work off M2, cancel the overnight large batch, keep repeat-after-failure on daytime M1 only, and shrink M1 batching so a failure does not spoil 24 tubes. If M1 is down, switch to urgent-only on M2, pause new routine and complex-panel starts, and move complex-panel overflow to the partner if the partner is healthy. Downstream, technical validation receives a delayed lump of urgent reruns and needs the late-day clearance; routine and complex-panel work-in-process builds at preparation and must be drained by restoring the 30% floor the morning both machines are back, ahead of new routine arrivals. Strict urgent-first with no recovery slot will otherwise leave the deferred routine in the lab into the next wave.

**S3 policy — absence calendar.** On when the specialist or a medical validator is absent today or tomorrow, or hold WIP exceeds 20 cases (a normal week starts only about 36 holds). Off the morning after the backup shift if hold WIP is under 10 and the medical queue median is under 6h. While on: run the Friday backup, pre-schedule the evening medical shift for the absence day and the return day, stop new complex-panel machine starts above what the backup can review, and open I10 for complex-panel holds older than 24h. Do not move medical validators into technical validation during this window; urgent cases need both steps. Downstream of the return-day clearance is medical validation, then release. If partner results and local holds land the same evening, keep the medical shift and defer routine medical questions to the next daytime block.

**S4 policy — herd and echo.** On when one clock hour exceeds about 35% of the day’s receipts, when any urgent sample’s collect-to-receive age exceeds 6h, or when a route ETA slips by more than 2h. Off when no hour exceeds 25% of receipts and no urgent sample is older than 4h on the road. While on: call urgent samples in on the express pickup, leave routine samples on the revised timetable, extend the 2h defect screen to cover the dump, and refuse clerk shifts past 9h even if the dump is still being booked (auto-registration covers complete orders; incomplete orders wait until morning rather than generating accession errors). Pre-clear a same-evening M1 urgent block sized to the express pickup. Book recollection returns from this dump into intake about 60h later instead of letting them join the next 10:00 or 17:00 spike. Downstream of a successful screen is preparation and assay one shift later; downstream of an overscreeen is a larger recollection wave, clerk workload, and cancels.

**S5 policy — intersection, with a published deferral.** When two or more of the S2, S3, and S4 triggers are on during an S1 week, apply the strictest limit from each: urgent-only on the reliable machine, complex-panel starts capped to confirmed review or partner slots, routine explicitly deferred, defect screen on, clerk shifts capped at 9h, evening medical shift on. The deferral needs a recovery rule or work-in-process grows without a plan: the next day both machines are up and the specialist backlog is under one day, routine receives its 30% floor before any new complex-panel start beyond that day’s review slots. This policy fails if it is left on after the asset and the reviewer have recovered; routine p90 is the casualty.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

M1 being fully down is still a broken urgent promise. Slot reservation cannot reserve a machine that is off. Urgent-only operation on M2 accepts a higher repeat rate, and the 18h limit will be missed for a large share of urgent cases until M1 returns. That fragility is in the asset base.

The redesign itself creates three exposures. First, M1 lane rules and urgent preemption fragment runs. The log already shows that urgent bypass fragments machine schedules. Effective capacity falls, and the loss is paid by routine cases, whose peak p90 is 120h against 72h, and by any complex-panel case that loses the M1 race. The 30% floor limits starvation and does not remove it. Second, the complex-panel start cap, which is what keeps the specialist queue from returning to 36–48h, deliberately delays complex-panel start. Tuned too tightly, it makes complex-panel median cycle time worse than a push system and piles stable samples at preparation. Third, a 2h defect screen that is too aggressive manufactures the recollection loop the screen was meant to shrink: clerk explanation load, courier load, a 60h echo, and a higher cancel rate than the peak’s already elevated 5.5%.

Other remaining exposures: the partner lab sits in the same region and can be saturated by the same infection wave, in which case 12h transport plus a partner queue is slower than a bad local hold and the traceability work lands on local medical validation; cross-trained registration staff will miss technical calls in the first season and can feed the specialist queue the Friday cover is meant to empty; courier smoothing assumes clinics and roads cooperate, and a lab staffed for a flat day is more brittle when a storm recompresses arrivals; watching urgent median cycle time will look acceptable near 17h while urgent p90, routine p90, and the specialist queue are the actual failure.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Review the leading set every morning and again at 15:00 during a declared peak, in time to set the evening medical shift, the express courier, and the next day’s complex-panel cap. Review lagging outcomes once a day. They confirm harm after the queues have already formed.

**Leading indicators**

- **Arrival mix and compression.** Urgent plus complex-panel share of today’s receipts: normal band near 31%, watch above 40%, peak band near 49%. Busiest clock-hour share: watch above 35%. Urgent collect-to-receive age: watch above 4h, act above 6h. These switch on S1 and S4 before internal queues fully reflect the dump.
- **Urgent age in lab.** Count of open urgent cases older than 10h, and the age of the oldest. Act when a case cannot plausibly release inside the remaining time to 18h. This is the preemption trigger. Urgent median cycle time is the wrong trigger: it was still 17h when p90 was 33h.
- **Station queues, median of cases waiting now.** Watch / act bands, set from the normal and peak medians: pre-analytical 5h / 8h (normal 1.8h, peak 9.5h); preparation 6h / 9h (2.5h, 11h); assay 8h / 14h (4h, 18h); technical validation 8h / 12h (3.5h, 16h) — treat utilization at or above 100% as act regardless of queue; medical 6h / 12h (2h, 22h); specialist 12h / 24h (5h, 36h); accession 6h / 10h (3h, 14h). A falling assay queue with a rising specialist queue is migration, not recovery.
- **24h repeat rate by machine and by night versus day.** Watch above 8%, act above 12% (normal outcome rate 6.7%, peak 14.8%). This is the S2 switch and the permission bit for any overnight routine batch.
- **Urgent batch-bypass rate.** Watch below 70%, act below 60% (normal 74%, peak 58%). Falling bypass with flat routine volume means the batch-wait activity is filling with urgent cases.
- **Quality and referral pressure.** Recollection requests as a share of receipts: watch above 6%, act above 8% (normal about 4.6% of checks, peak about 9%). Medical-routing share minus the mix-based expectation (about 27% at peak mix): watch a gap above 5 points, act above 10 (observed peak gap was about 15 points, 42% actual). Specialist on-duty flag for the next 48h plus hold WIP: act if the reviewer is absent or hold WIP exceeds 20.
- **Routine floor.** Routine share of today’s assay starts: act below 30% unless the S2 or S5 deferral is explicitly on. This catches starvation while it is still a schedule choice.

**Lagging outcome metrics**

- Median and p90 cycle time by case type, against 18h urgent and 72h routine. Report p90 beside the median every time. Complex-panel and repeat-after-failure have no contractual line in this brief; track them against their normal p90 (118h and 132h) so a “good” urgent median cannot hide a 210h or 240h tail.
- Urgent penalty count and routine breaches of 72h, counted as cases, not as a blended average.
- Rework rate (normal 7.5%, peak 11.8%) and in-process repeat rate.
- Cancel rate (normal 3.1%, peak 5.5%) and the cancel share of recollection requests (normal 35%, peak 42%).
- Share of cases with at least one queue over 12h (normal 22%, peak 54%). This is the system-level confirmation that pain is no longer confined to a tail.
