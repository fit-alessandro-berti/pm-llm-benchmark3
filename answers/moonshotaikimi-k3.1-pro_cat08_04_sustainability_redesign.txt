## 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Accounting unit and quantified boundary.** Use the order as the current accounting unit because the log is flattened, while recognizing that an order can contain several line items, production batches, and shipments. The quantified boundary runs from **Receive order** through **Close** or cancellation and includes:

- Stock picking, partial shipments, backorders, and delay cancellations.
- Planned and emergency production, CNC and heat-treatment batching, inspection, and production-rework loops.
- Standard/reinforced packaging and packaging rework.
- All outbound shipments associated with an order, using `route_mode` and `transport_kgco2e`.

Waiting is not assumed to consume energy directly; its environmental relevance is through subsequent expediting, cancellation, additional shipments, and obsolescence.

**Environmental measures.** Report `energy_kwh`, `transport_kgco2e`, `material_scrap_kg`, and `package_material_kg` separately. Energy cannot be converted into COe without electricity emission factors, and material kilograms cannot be converted into lifecycle impacts without material and treatment data. Report both absolute totals and intensities per fulfilled order and, once allocation is possible, per good delivered line item. Include energy and materials used on cancelled, returned, or replacement orders rather than removing them from the numerator.

**Outside the current quantified boundary** are upstream production of stocked items and raw materials, capital equipment, product use and end-of-life, most customer-downtime consequences, and potentially inbound transport, return transport, and dedicated-van empty backhaul. These are not assumed to be zero: returns, replacements, unsold batch output, and customer-arranged emergency logistics must be checked for burden shifting. The 14-day non-shipment measure is an obsolescence-risk proxy, not proof that an item was ultimately discarded.

Important uncertainties are multi-line/batch energy allocation, transport load factors and backhaul coverage, route reliability at the decision time, part mix differences between CNC_A and CNC_B, and returns after Close. Low model precision during combined emergency, disruption, and partial-shipment paths also means that raw variant averages are not causal estimates.

As a screening calculation only, weighting the supplied averages by segment shares suggests that Made-to-order represents 34% of orders but approximately **87% of logged energy and 85% of scrap**. Critical-downtime represents 11% of orders but approximately **41% of transport emissions**. Production/rework and segment-specific transport therefore both need attention.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching energy savings can reappear as transport emissions and delay.** Moving CNC_A toward larger batches can reduce energy per item while increasing waiting from hours to days. Heat-treatment batching also carries higher late risk. The resulting order may then require emergency production, air, or a dedicated van, so machine kWh improves while total order impact and p90 service worsen.

2. **Efficient batches can increase obsolescence and total production.** When forecasts are wrong, 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days. A low kWh-per-produced-item metric can therefore conceal high energy and scrap per item actually delivered. Longer negotiated promises or earlier customer ordering can intensify this if production is moved ahead of firm demand.

3. **Lower-emission transport can trigger more emission-intensive exceptions.** A missed consolidation cutoff or an over-optimistic ground recommendation can create a partial shipment, a second parcel, or a late air/dedicated upgrade. For Critical-downtime, forcing the stock/ground pattern can also worsen downtime service; any resulting customer-arranged emergency transport may fall outside the log.

4. **Packaging minimization can shift impact into returns.** Reinforced packaging doubles package material but reduces damage-related returns by 40% for heavy MTO parts. Removing it to improve a packaging KPI can cause Q1 violations, replacement CNC production, additional scrap, and return/replacement transport. Conversely, indiscriminate reinforcement adds material where inspection shows low damage risk.

5. **Rework reduction can become a queue or quality displacement.** Shifting work to CNC_A and adding inspection gates can overload CNC_A or inspection_cell. Waiting may simply move to another segment, while pressure to preserve throughput can cause rushed inspections or unofficial bypasses. Defects then reappear later as packaging rework or customer returns, outside the machine-level analysis.

6. **Partial-shipment reduction can delay the most important line.** A wait-for-complete rule may save a parcel but leave a customer without an available critical line, causing cancellation, downtime, or a later express shipment. If availability problems are instead “fixed” through forecast stock building, the burden moves into inventory and obsolescence.

7. **Priority rules can disadvantage non-target orders.** Priority picking for Standard-stock Service-contract orders, preferential CNC_A capacity, and inspection prioritization can push Regular or other MTO orders into longer waits and more express upgrades. Monitoring only the intervention cohort would incorrectly claim a system improvement.

8. **Customer behavior can offset promise and routing improvements.** Customers may reject negotiated dates, pad requested lead times, order earlier or in larger quantities, split orders, overstate downtime, or demand air to preserve perceived service. Merely changing `promised_lead_time_hours` can improve reported lateness without improving environmental impact or actual customer service.

9. **Critical classification and shared lanes can weaken resilience.** Misclassifying true downtime as precautionary can produce a much worse emergency later. A shared lane can also become a common-capacity failure during disruption, while an apparently successful reduction in dedicated-van events may only reflect missing SP2 documentation or transport moving outside the boundary.

10. **Boundary and case-mix effects can create false savings.** Totals may fall because demand declined, cancellations increased, production moved to an unmeasured supplier, or returns occurred after Close. Conversely, an intensity metric can improve while total impact rises. Both absolute and case-mix-adjusted measures are therefore necessary.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a **service-gated waste-and-flow redesign**, not a blanket air ban or maximum-batch policy.

1. **Establish guardrails and exception evidence first.** Deploy S12 and run S1 in shadow mode before automating route changes. Preserve Q1, SP2, and R1 as constraints. Define segment-specific non-inferiority limits for p90 cycle time, lateness, cancellation, quality, and disruption service before pilots; Critical-downtime requires the strictest protection.

2. **Remove avoidable quality and packaging waste before pursuing marginal batch savings.** Combine S7 with adequate inspection capacity, S6 with Q1-compatible packaging rules, and S3 with explicit CNC_A queue and due-date gates. Keep CNC_B qualified for urgent work and disruption recovery. This attacks the large MTO energy/scrap burden while potentially improving, rather than trading away, service and quality.

3. **Use dynamic batching only where slack is real.** Apply S2 after the quality/routing changes so that efficient batches do not conceal a rework problem. Waiting caps should reflect remaining promise slack and route reliability, with emergency bypasses and an obsolescence stop rule. Use S10 only as an optional way to create genuine slack for willing Regular MTO customers.

4. **Apply transport measures by feasibility, not by a uniform hierarchy.** S4 is a focused first transport use case because of the 22% SP1-violation finding. Add S5 only for non-urgent stock orders with a ground fallback. S1 then generalizes the decision using reliability-adjusted slack. S11 should remove avoidable partials while retaining partial shipment as a service and resilience option.

5. **Treat Critical-downtime separately.** Use S9 to identify precautionary urgency, but do not force true downtime onto the lower-emission stock/ground path. Pilot S8 only on lanes where response time, surge capacity, and an independent fallback have been demonstrated. A dedicated van remains justified when it prevents unacceptable downtime and SP2 evidence is recorded.

This package prioritizes win–win rework and compatibility improvements, then captures batching and transport savings only inside service, quality, and resilience constraints. It also avoids claiming success from a pooled average while a non-target segment deteriorates.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

**1. Strengthen the event-log design.** Link order, line item, production batch, shipment, return, and replacement identifiers so that partial shipments and batch energy are neither omitted nor double-counted. Add decision-time snapshots for remaining slack, route reliability, recommended and selected alternatives, batch-cap release reason, inspection queue/result, part weight/geometry, original and negotiated customer dates, exception reason, and alternative-route availability. Follow 14-day non-shipment cases to final disposition.

**2. Use staged, counterfactual validation.**  
- Run S1 in shadow mode and test whether its above-85% on-time predictions are calibrated by carrier, route, and disruption condition.
- Use matched or stepped pilots for S2–S6 and S11, controlling for segment, part, weight, machine, batch size, promise slack, and demand mix.
- Compare CNC_A/CNC_B on comparable parts rather than treating the 16- versus 32-kWh path averages as a machine experiment.
- Do not randomize critical customers to unsafe service. Use simulation, historical replay, and tightly monitored shared-lane pilots with immediate dedicated fallback.
- Re-run path and conformance analysis after implementation to detect new loops such as Batch wait  late  air, consolidation  missed cutoff  partial shipment, or inspection gate  queue  quality escape. Do not rely solely on the low-precision discovered DFG.

**3. Monitor service by segment.** Compare against each segment’s own baseline median/p90/late-delivery profile: Standard-stock Regular **31/62 h and 7%**, Standard-stock Service-contract **18/41 h and 5%**, MTO Regular **94/180 h and 18%**, MTO Service-contract **78/156 h and 13%**, and Critical-downtime **15/30 h and 4%**. Track their respective rework baselines as well. For the emergency/dedicated critical cohort, also compare with its stronger **14-hour/3%-late** path performance. Include cancellations, time to first/final delivery, and effects on non-target segments. S10 must additionally report service to the original request so that renegotiation cannot manufacture an on-time improvement.

**4. Automate policy conformance tests.**

| Policy | Event-log test and review |
|---|---|
| **SP1** | Reconstruct eligibility using information available at the shipping decision: whether ground or consolidated truck could meet the promise with above-85% probability. Report violations and overrides by segment, starting with the 22% Standard-stock Service-contract finding, and audit whether compliant recommendations nevertheless produce excess lateness. |
| **SP2** | Join every Dedicated van event to an explicit Critical-downtime justification and outcome. Reduce the 31% missing-justification rate without delaying emergencies; missing documentation remains a conformance failure even when service was successful. |
| **Q1** | For every MTO line over 8 kg, verify reinforced packaging or an inspection-certified low-damage-risk exception. Correlate violations with packaging rework, damage returns, replacements, scrap, energy, and transport. No carbon-only waiver is permitted. |
| **R1** | Verify that Service-contract customers have an actually available alternative route during carrier disruption, not merely a second route name using the same failed capacity. Test fallback activation and monitor disruption-period p90, lateness, cancellation, and expediting. |

**5. Govern exceptions and stop rules.** A cross-functional operations, service, quality, sustainability, and resilience group should review recurring exception reasons and outcomes. Justified critical exceptions should not be penalized; repeated overrides should trigger correction of unreliable route predictions, unrealistic batching rules, capacity shortages, or poor master data.

Pause or roll back an intervention if its pre-agreed guardrails fail: route or consolidation changes that increase lateness/cancellation or later express upgrades; batching that causes cap breaches or rising obsolescence; CNC/inspection changes that create damaging queues; packaging changes that increase damage returns; or shared emergency lanes that miss critical response or lose R1 coverage.

Scale only when absolute and case-mix-adjusted environmental indicators improve **without** increases in rework, returns, obsolescence, or unmeasured replacement activity, and while segment service, Q1 quality, and disruption-resilience guardrails hold. Report remaining allocation and lifecycle uncertainties rather than converting the result into an unsupported single COe savings figure.
