## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Throughout, treat the **flag as an information-and-routing policy bundle** triggered by (imperfect) `risk_score`, not as a manipulable “risk itself.” Let \(c\) denote the operational cutoff that maps `risk_score` to `flag` in this dataset; when policies suppress the flag, define the “flag-eligible” population by observed `risk_score ≥ c` (not by cross-world “would be flagged” strata). Let `cycle_time := close - arrival` and `triage_completion := triage_done - arrival`.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P0 vs P1 (current bundle vs suppress flag display; keep `risk_score` computed) | case; `risk_score ≥ c` arrivals in comparable calendar/loads | `triage_completion` | total policy effect (information + routing) | Yes, if “suppressed” implies no priority lane and no automatic routing | behavior adapts when flag is hidden; time-varying congestion; interference (priority displaces others) |
| E2 | P0 vs P1 | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | total policy effect | Yes (policy-level) | unobserved `U_underlying_risk`; downstream routing changes are part of P1; shared-queue spillovers |
| E3 | P0 vs P2 (keep priority triage; remove mandatory/manual-default routing) | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | total policy effect focused on removing the “mandatory review” component | Mostly (needs a precise replacement rule for “no automatic mandatory review”) | selection into manual review under P2 (humans); exposure-induced confounding for downstream escalation |
| E4 | P0 vs P2 | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | **interventional indirect effect via `manual_review` occurrence**: shift \(M\) to its P0 distribution while holding the rest at P2 | Partially (stochastic intervention on a post-triage decision) | mediator-outcome confounding created by triage info; cross-case interference through the review queue |
| E5 | P0 vs P3 (add protected manual-review capacity for flagged/eligible) | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | **interventional indirect effect via `manual_queue_wait`** (reduce waiting while keeping routing) | Partially (queue wait is system-generated, not individually settable) | interference/SUTVA violations; need queue-state measurement or cluster-randomized capacity shocks |
| E6 | P0 vs P4 (retain review; change escalation criteria and/or capacity) | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | **interventional indirect effect via `escalation` (and `escalation_queue_wait`)** | Partially (depends on how criteria/capacity is operationalized) | unobserved review findings \(U\_review\_findings\); scarce-capacity spillovers |
| E7 | P0 vs P3 | case; `risk_score ≥ c` | `cycle_time` | total policy effect of capacity protection | Yes, if “protected capacity” is specified (extra vs reallocated) | if capacity is reallocated, spillovers confound; if added, secular trend and learning effects |
| E8 | P0 vs P3 | case; `risk_score < c` (unflagged-eligible) | `cycle_time` | spillover (indirect) effect under interference | Yes (system-level) | interference is the estimand, but requires cluster/time-block designs to identify |
| E9 | P0 vs P2 (or P0 vs P1) | case; all arrivals | `decision_outcome` / decision quality proxy (audit error rate, appeals, downstream harm) | total policy effect on quality (not just speed) | Conceptually yes; operationally often missing ground truth | observed `outcome` is not validated “quality”; differential auditing; hidden risk affects both routing and correctness |

**Framework choice**: I avoid **natural direct/indirect effects** because they rely on cross-world counterfactuals and “no interference,” both implausible with shared queues and exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding. I use **separable policy decomposition** (information display vs routing vs capacity) and **interventional/stochastic mediation analogues** (distributions of `manual_review`, `manual_queue_wait`, `escalation`) that correspond to implementable levers and can be targeted by cluster/time-block experiments.

```text
            triage_done

                   └─ manual_service ← complexity + review_resource + U_reviewer_skill
                        manual_done
                          └─ escalation? ← U_review_findings + policy/capacity + complexity
                               ├─ escalation_queue_wait ← scarce senior team + interference
                               └─ senior_service
                                    escalation_done
                                      └─ residual_to_close (admin/coordination) → close

cycle_time = triage_wait + triage_service + [manual_queue_wait + manual_service] + [escalation_queue_wait + senior_service] + residual_to_close
decision_outcome influenced by U_underlying_risk, U_review_findings, reviewer decisions, and escalation.
```

Interference arises because `manual_queue_wait` and `escalation_queue_wait` are functions of **other cases’ routing and service times**; priority triage also displaces non-priority work. Exposure-induced confounding arises because `flag` changes who enters `manual_review`, and `manual_review` generates new information (`U_review_findings`) that drives both `escalation` and `decision_outcome`, breaking simple mediator ignorability. Treatment-confounder feedback can occur if triage outputs (not explicitly modeled) affect subsequent routing and are themselves affected by priority/resourcing.

The timestamps show a clear **front-end acceleration**: median `triage_completion` is about **0.8h** for `flag=1` vs **3.5h** for `flag=0`, driven by much shorter `arrival-to-triage-start` (priority lane). Yet `flag=1` has much longer `cycle_time` (16.45h vs 5.75h), consistent with **downstream delays**: (i) universal manual review for flagged (100% vs 40% unflagged), (ii) much longer `triage-done-to-manual-start` (6.75h vs 1.75h among unflagged manual cases), and (iii) more frequent escalation (60% vs 20%) with large escalation waiting. These contrasts are not automatically causal because flagged cases differ in `complexity` and unobserved risk, and waiting times are system-level outcomes affected by other cases (interference) and staffing/shift patterns.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| flag → priority_lane → triage_wait | Priority routing reduces queueing before triage (direct speedup) | `flag=1` median arrival→triage_start 0.20h vs 2.50h | Comparable arrivals/loads; triage_start semantics consistent | Triage timestamps for flagged recorded earlier (measurement) | Keep priority triage in all policies (P2/P3/P4) or A/B priority rule | Displaces unflagged triage (spillover) unless capacity added |
| flag → triage_resource → triage_service | Specialized triage staff/service standard for flagged | Slightly lower triage service medians (0.60h vs 0.95h) | Resource assignment not confounded by unobserved complexity | Flagged cases triaged “superficially,” deferring work downstream | Standardize triage protocols across lanes; audit service-time quality | May increase triage time but reduce downstream rework |
| flag → manual_review (occurrence) → cycle_time | Mandatory review adds an extra stage for most flagged | Manual review rate: 100% flagged vs 40% unflagged | Manual review itself (not hidden risk) drives added time | Flagged are inherently higher risk/complex so would be reviewed anyway under P1/P2 | P2: remove automatic mandatory review; use risk-score banding/triage notes to trigger review | Faster cycles but potential quality/compliance degradation |
| flag → manual_queue_wait → cycle_time | Routing many flagged into shared review queue increases waiting | `triage_done→manual_start` median 6.75h flagged; higher under high load (7.0h) | Queue state measured/controlled; no unmeasured confounding of queue wait | Review is scheduled/batched (shift-driven), not purely queue congestion | P3: protected or added manual-review capacity for flagged | If capacity reallocated, unflagged/manual waits may worsen |
| manual_review → escalation (occurrence) → cycle_time | Review findings trigger escalation to scarce senior team | Escalation rate 60% flagged vs 20% unflagged; flagged+escalation median total 21.9h | Escalation decision comparable across groups given findings | Escalation used for complex admin, not risk | P4: refine escalation criteria; introduce “fast-track senior consult” alternatives | Risk of under-escalation; or senior overload if criteria broadened |
| escalation_queue_wait / senior_service | Scarce senior capacity creates long waits; dominates tail times | Manual_done→escalation_start medians ~7–8h; senior_service ~2h | Queue discipline stable; timestamps reflect actual start | Escalation timestamps reflect “acceptance” not actual work start | P4: add senior capacity or reserve slots for escalations | May cannibalize other senior duties; shifts bottleneck upstream |

| shared-queue interference (cross-case) | One case’s routing affects others’ waits; “effects” are not individual-only | Priority triage and shared review/escalation queues described; load effects within flagged | Partial interference (e.g., by shift/team) or explicit queue-state modeling | Apparent effects due to time-of-day or staffing rather than interference | Cluster/time-block experiments; queue guardrails; simulation with measured arrivals/service | Policies may shift delays from flagged to unflagged (equity/SLA tradeoff) |
| reviewer/resource assignment & fatigue | Different reviewers/seniors change service and escalation propensity | Multiple review resources (M1/M2/M3) and long waits suggest batching | Reviewer assignment exogenous given observed covariates | High-risk cases routed to stricter reviewers (selection) | Balance workloads; rotate reviewers; decision support for escalation | Could reduce heterogeneity but may reduce autonomy/buy-in |
| decision-quality tradeoff | Longer cycles may buy better decisions (fewer false approves/rejects) | Flagged routed to more checks; outcomes alone not quality | Need ground-truth error/harm measures; stable auditing | Same reject rate masks offsetting errors (false rejects vs false approves) | Couple any speed policy with audit sampling + appeal tracking | Faster routing may increase harm/compliance incidents if checks removed |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does priority routing cause faster triage completion? | Randomize **priority lane assignment** for `risk_score ≥ c` by time blocks (e.g., alternating hours) while keeping downstream routing constant | `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, time-of-day/day-of-week | Time-block randomization creates exchangeability; minimal spillovers across blocks | Check balance of covariates across blocks; verify unflagged triage not harmed beyond guardrail | If triage speedup disappears under randomization, observed speedup is mostly case-mix/measurement |
| What is the causal effect of **flag display** (P0 vs P1) rather than score availability? | Randomized **flag-suppression** (hide binary flag; optionally keep numeric score visible) | `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, calendar time | Humans do not infer hidden flag perfectly; compliance with UI condition | Placebo: compare effects on stages that should not change (e.g., residual-to-close) | If hiding flag leaves routing unchanged, “flag” is not the operative lever (humans use the score/other cues) |
| Does removing mandatory review (P2) reduce cycle time without harming quality? | Randomized encouragement: for mid-band `risk_score` (near cutoff), randomize “auto-send to review” vs “review only by human judgment” | `risk_score` bands, `complexity`, arrival time; optionally triage notes length as proxy | Encouragement affects review probability; exclusion restriction approximately holds | Compare not only cycle time but audit error/appeals; check escalation rates for substitution | If error/harm increases meaningfully, then the delay is a deliberate control worth keeping |
| Is the delay mainly **manual queue waiting** (vs manual service)? | P3 pilot: add protected/manual capacity for flagged in randomized shifts; measure change in `triage_done→manual_start` holding routing fixed | Shift/team fixed effects, `arrival_load`, `risk_score`, `complexity` | Capacity shock changes waiting times; no concurrent policy changes | Falsify with unchanged manual wait despite added capacity (suggest batching/semantics) | If manual waits do not drop but cycle time drops, timestamps are not representing the intended queue interval |
| How much is mediated by escalation vs review? | P4 stepped-wedge: add senior slots or change escalation threshold on alternating days; track `manual_done→escalation_start` and escalation rate | `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, review_resource, time | No other changes coincide with wedge steps; stable demand within steps | If escalation waiting drops but total cycle doesn’t, bottleneck is elsewhere (e.g., manual queue) | If cycle time remains dominated by manual waits, focus on P3 over P4 |
| Can we identify a near-threshold causal effect of flag-triggered routing? | **Local-threshold/RD** at cutoff \(c\): compare cases just above vs just below \(c\) | Restrict to \(|risk\_score-c|<\epsilon\); adjust `complexity`, arrival time | Continuity of unobservables at cutoff; no manipulation of score | Density test around \(c\); covariate continuity; placebo cutoffs | If covariates jump at \(c\) or score bunching occurs, RD is not credible |
| Can we decompose occurrence vs waiting mediation without cross-world claims? | **Interventional (stochastic) mediation** via g-computation: decompose P0→P2 into components through `manual_review` and `manual_queue_wait` | Pre-treatment: `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, calendar time; queue state measures if available | Sequential exchangeability for chosen mediators; correct model specification | Compare predicted vs observed stage-time distributions; sensitivity to unobserved risk | If small changes in sensitivity parameters flip mediation estimates, treat decomposition as descriptive only |
| Are differences an artifact of timestamp semantics? (negative control) | Negative-control outcome: `residual-to-close` and/or `triage_service` after start; should be less sensitive to routing | Stratify by `complexity` and resource; control for time-of-day | Negative control not affected by the intervention, only by confounding/measurement | If negative-control outcome shifts strongly with flag, suspect hidden complexity or logging differences | A large flag effect on residual-to-close would weaken “queue-wait mediation” and point to unmeasured work after last stage |
| How large are spillovers under shared queues? | Interference-aware design: cluster randomize by reviewer team/shift; estimate both flagged and unflagged outcomes (E7/E8) | Cluster fixed effects; `arrival_load`; baseline queue lengths | Partial interference across clusters; stable routing within clusters | Check whether unflagged wait times rise when flagged get protection | If protecting flagged substantially worsens unflagged SLAs, recommendation shifts toward adding capacity or rebalancing priority rules |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 (current) | Faster for flagged via priority lane | Higher review/escalation load; long waits in shared queues | Longer total for flagged; potential tail from escalation | Lower risk (more checks) but may be inefficient | Priority and review consume shared capacity | Moderate (consistent timestamp pattern) | Baseline only |
| P1 (suppress flag display) | Likely slower triage for high-risk (priority removed) | Less automatic review; queues may shorten but routing becomes discretionary | Could reduce cycle time for some, increase for truly high-risk | Potentially higher errors if checks were critical; accountability risk | May relieve queues but shift burden to humans | Low–moderate (behavior adapts) | Not first choice |
| P2 (keep priority triage; remove mandatory review) | Keeps triage speedup | Review queue load falls; but discretionary review may concentrate on hardest cases | Likely shorter average cycle time for flagged-eligible; fewer escalations | Highest risk if review prevented bad decisions; needs audit | Positive spillover if review queue relieved | Low without experiment | Consider only for narrow risk bands with strong guardrails |
| P3 (keep routing; add protected manual-review capacity for flagged) | Same triage benefit | **Reduces manual queue wait** for flagged; escalation waits may also drop via earlier handoff | Likely shorter flagged cycle time without changing checks | Low (keeps review/escalation logic) | If capacity is added: minimal; if reallocated: may harm unflagged | Moderate (mechanism aligns with long waits) | **Preferred pilot** |
| P4 (keep review; change escalation criteria or capacity) | Same triage | Reduces escalation queue waits and/or escalation frequency | Shortens cycles mainly for escalated cases; may not fix manual queue | Medium: under-escalation or over-escalation risk | Adding senior capacity may shift bottleneck | Low–moderate | Secondary pilot if escalation dominates after P3 |

Preferred limited pilot (P3), ≤250 words:
- **Eligibility**: all `risk_score ≥ c` cases (flag-eligible) during pilot period; exclude cases with legally mandated senior escalation.
- **Assignment rule**: cluster randomize by **shift/day** (e.g., alternating shifts) to “protected capacity ON” vs “OFF.” ON means one additional reviewer dedicated to flagged-eligible cases (or reserved review slots) without pulling capacity from the unflagged queue.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail**: monitor unflagged `triage_wait` and (if unflagged manual review exists) unflagged `manual_queue_wait`; stop/adjust if either increases by >20% relative to baseline for two consecutive shifts.
- **Primary outcomes**: flagged-eligible `cycle_time`; flagged-eligible `triage_done→manual_start` (manual queue waiting); escalation waiting (`manual_done→escalation_start`) among escalated.
- **Secondary outcomes**: unflagged cycle time (spillover), escalation rate, rework/appeals, SLA breaches, and an audited **decision quality** metric (sampled).
- **Minimum process data needed**: all stage start/done timestamps; resource IDs; explicit queue-state snapshots (queue length or WIP) at triage/manual/escalation starts; `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`; outcome plus audit label for a subsample.
- **Stopping conditions**: audited error/harm rate increases beyond pre-set margin; sustained guardrail violation; evidence of gaming (e.g., reviewers delaying “start” timestamps).

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. If a strong **hidden `U_underlying_risk`** (not captured by `risk_score`/`complexity`) both increases review/escalation *and* directly increases service times, then the observed delays could be mostly case-mix rather than policy-mediated, shifting recommendations toward better risk measurement over capacity changes.
2. If **queue interference** is severe such that protected capacity for flagged (P3) measurably worsens unflagged SLAs (net harm), then the recommendation reverses toward added total capacity or reducing priority strength.
3. If validated **decision quality** (audit error/harm) improves substantially under P0 and degrades under P2/P1 beyond an acceptable margin, then speed-focused changes (especially removing review) should be avoided even if cycle time falls.
4. If **timestamp semantics differ by lane** (e.g., `triage_start` logged at “assigned” for flagged but at “work begins” for unflagged), the apparent triage acceleration is a measurement artifact, and the mechanism shifts from priority routing to logging/measurement correction.
5. If there is **no overlap/positivity** near the flag cutoff (or strong score manipulation), local-threshold identification fails; conclusions must rely on experiments rather than quasi-experimental comparisons.
6. If reviewers change behavior when capacity or visibility changes (e.g., **escalate more to avoid blame** when the flag is hidden or when protected capacity exists), then the mechanism is dominated by behavior adaptation, and policy choice must prioritize governance/accountability design.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed**: Flagged cases reach triage much sooner (median arrival→triage_done about 0.8h vs 3.5h), but have much longer end-to-end duration (median cycle time 16.45h vs 5.75h). Flagged cases undergo manual review in all instances here, and are escalated more often; delays concentrate in triage_done→manual_start and (when escalated) manual_done→escalation_start, while residual-to-close is similar across groups.

**Plausible mechanism**: The flag triggers a priority lane that accelerates the front end, but simultaneously routes many cases into shared manual-review and senior-escalation queues. Because those queues are capacity constrained and shared, waiting-time mediation (not service time) dominates the added cycle time, and escalation creates a long-tail subgroup. High `arrival_load` amplifies manual waiting even when triage is protected.

**What remains unidentified**: How much of the difference is causal (vs hidden risk/complexity), how large spillovers are on unflagged cases under shared queues, and whether the additional delay buys meaningful decision quality (the observed approve/reject labels are not validated “quality”).

**Most useful next evidence**: A cluster/time-block pilot of **P3 (protected added manual-review capacity for flag-eligible cases)** with guardrails and quality auditing isolates waiting-time mechanisms while keeping checks intact, generating both performance gains and clean evidence about queue-mediated delay under interference.
