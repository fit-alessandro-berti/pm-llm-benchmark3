# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The process is optimizing the visible front end while allowing cost, quality, and compliance failures to emerge downstream. The high-volume route begins with 3,600 auto eligibility screens; 2,550 proceed directly to Fast approval, and 2,300 Fast approvals flow to Dispatch replacement. That route is demonstrably quick: Intake to Auto eligibility averages 0.8 hours, Auto eligibility to Fast approval 1.0 hour, and Fast approval to Dispatch 3.0 hours. It is also 31% cheaper before rework. But the complaint/reopen rate is 14.1% for Fast approval cases, versus 3.3% for cases following Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device. After complaints, reshipments, and service credits, the fast route is only 9% cheaper. The frequent route is therefore a good speed benchmark, not a safe universal default.

The downstream pattern helps explain the apparent contradiction. The DFG records 140 transitions from Fast approval to Customer complaint/reopen and 260 from Dispatch replacement to complaint/reopen. Dispatch to complaint has a 96-hour mean, 70-hour median, and 180-hour p90; the temporal findings link this long tail to incorrect replacement-part selection. These figures do not prove every complaint was caused by the fast decision, but they show why counting only approval and dispatch time overstates the benefit. Complaints also loop back to Manual eligibility review (310 observed transitions) or go to an Escalation board (120), adding substantial elapsed time and scarce human work.

The process has serious evidence and queue delays. Manual eligibility review averages 7.5 hours after intake, versus 0.8 hours to the auto screen. When evidence is requested, the return to Manual eligibility review averages 34 hours, with an 88-hour p90. A request occurs at least twice in 26% of cases that enter that activity; 22% of cases are in the unclear-defect/missing-evidence segment, whose median cycle time is 140 hours and abandonment is highest. The DFG records 320 transitions from Request missing evidence to Claim abandoned. This is both a customer friction problem and a poor use of reviewer capacity: reviewers may be waiting for information that could have been collected in one complete, comprehensible request.

The quality trade-off is segment-specific. Fast-path complaint/reopen is 22% for Premium high-value devices and 18% for Standard high-value devices; reviewed/repair outcomes are 5% and 6%, respectively. Yet the reviewed/repair medians are 96 and 120 hours, so sending every device through the slow path would impose a large service penalty. Premium low-risk accessories have an 8-hour fast median and high service-credit exposure above 24 hours, while Standard low-risk customers tolerate up to 72 hours if status is clear. Repair is not risk-free either: repaired high-value devices have 12.5% rework, compared with 4.0% for low-risk devices. A suitable policy must distinguish value, defect confidence, segment, and fulfillment risk.

Conformance evidence warns against treating the auto route as a policy-compliant straight-through process. R1 requires Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review before Fast approval above EUR 400. R2 prohibits replacement dispatch before evidence completeness except for Premium low-risk accessories. R3 requires Manual eligibility review after any reopen before final close or denial. The alignment diagnostics identify Fast approval before Evidence completeness, complaints after close, and high-value dispatch without risk review. Token replay reports log fitness of 0.91, but only 68% fitting traces; high fitness does not mean every trace follows policy. Low precision in the discovered model means it admits many low-frequency combinations that policy does not allow. Together these are reasons to enforce explicit controls, not to infer that the frequent path is compliant. The summary does not establish how many cases breach each rule, so the breaches need case-level measurement.

Finally, capacity limits make broad routing changes risky. Seven manual reviewers provide 252 productive hours per week in total, and queue growth steepens above 82% utilization. Updating auto rules could raise manual inflow by 15–35%. The two risk specialists are already a Monday and post-campaign bottleneck; three technicians lose time waiting for part-price confirmation. The two quality inspectors are underused except after repair campaigns, and the once-daily 45-minute escalation board is not scalable. The DFG contains loops and the listed edges are selected directly-follows counts, not disjoint end-to-end case totals. They should be read with the segment and performance evidence, not added as if every case followed exactly one path.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-tiered, policy-gated fast lanes

**Redesigned flow.** At intake, capture customer segment, device value, defect type/confidence, and evidence status. Run an updated auto eligibility screen that returns an explicit decision and reason code. Keep a compliant straight-through route for eligible, complete-evidence, low-risk claims. For claims above EUR 400, require Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review before Fast approval. For all non-exempt claims, confirm evidence completeness before dispatch. Apply R3 as a hard reopen control: every reopened claim goes through Manual eligibility review before final close or denial. Route unclear defects and uncertain eligibility to a single, reason-coded review queue rather than letting them alternate among auto, risk, and repair paths. Premium low-risk accessories may use the R2 exception under an auditable whitelist; do not extend that exception to high-value devices.

**Targets.** This redesign primarily targets high-value devices, uncertain eligibility, and the two low-risk accessory segments. It protects the low-risk speed advantage while directing high-value claims away from the fast route associated with 18–22% complaints. Premium low-risk cases can preserve their 8-hour median and avoid service credits; Standard low-risk cases can retain a fast option with visible status.

**Why it should help.** It changes the auto screen from a general accelerator into a segment-aware router with policy gates. High-value decisions get the control R1 requires; evidence is checked at the point where it prevents noncompliant dispatch; reopened cases cannot bypass review. Low-risk cases avoid reviewer waits when their evidence and eligibility are clear. Reason codes and explicit routing also make future process-mining traces more interpretable. The capacity guardrail is to phase the rule change and cap its added reviewer inflow, since the forecast 15–35% increase could push reviewers past the 82% utilization threshold.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** Some high-value claims will take longer than the 20–28-hour fast medians, and this design does not itself remove the 96–120-hour reviewed/repair delays. More cases may queue for reviewers or risk specialists. The Premium low-risk exception still needs monitoring for evidence and customer outcomes. This is a safer routing policy, not a guarantee of a particular cost saving or a cure for repeated evidence requests or wrong-part selection.

# 3. Redesign B: One-touch evidence completion with an assisted channel

**Redesigned flow.** Move evidence clarification to the front of the process. After intake, generate a defect-specific checklist that states exactly which photos, serial numbers, proof of purchase, or diagnostic information are needed. Offer upload validation, examples, and a single consolidated request with reminders and status updates. When evidence is incomplete, pause the decision queue with a clear next action instead of sending repeated partial requests. Offer phone or agent-assisted submission for customers who cannot use the portal easily; an agent can capture the same checklist fields on the customer's behalf. Once complete, route the claim to the existing eligibility, risk, technical decision, and fulfillment controls, including R1–R3 and R2's dispatch rule.

**Targets.** Focus first on the 22% unclear-defect/missing-evidence segment and cases entering Request missing evidence, especially those that otherwise return repeatedly to Manual eligibility review. Premium customers receive proactive assistance and status updates. Standard customers can use self-service with transparent progress, consistent with their stated tolerance for up to 72 hours when visibility is good.

**Why it should help.** The evidence loop is a major source of elapsed time: Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review averages 34 hours and has an 88-hour p90, and 26% of cases entering the activity encounter it at least twice. A complete first request and assisted collection can reduce avoidable return trips and abandonment. The portal's estimated 30–45% reduction in repeated requests is a potential, not a promised result. Front-loading evidence also makes downstream auto decisions and policy checks more reliable without requiring every complete low-risk claim to wait for manual review.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** This adds design, integration, and assistance work before benefits are known. A portal can increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy, so digital-only intake is not acceptable. More complete submissions may increase reviewer or risk-specialist inflow in the short term. Evidence completeness will not correct a wrong replacement selection, shorten technician price confirmation by itself, or decide an ambiguous defect; those still need sound downstream routing.

# 4. Redesign C: Verified fulfillment and targeted repair quality

**Redesigned flow.** After a technically approved claim, select repair or replacement using a device/defect compatibility check and a current approved-parts reference. Before dispatch, add a lightweight check of the selected part against device model, defect, and claim record. Make the check mandatory for high-value devices and for low-confidence or changed-part selections; use a narrower exception or sampling rule for routine low-risk accessory replacements. Keep R1, R2, and R3 as upstream invariants: the fulfillment check cannot substitute for required review or evidence. For repairs, focus Quality check on high-value devices and known high-rework defect/repair combinations; record failure reason and feed it back into the compatibility and repair guidance. Where price confirmation is delaying Repair quote, use a maintained price reference for common parts and escalate only exceptions.

**Targets.** This is a downstream accuracy redesign, aimed at the long-tail Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen pattern, high-value wrong-replacement exposure (EUR 410 expected cost per error), and the 12.5% rework rate on repaired high-value devices. It preserves repair as an option where the evidence and technical assessment support it, recognizing its lower 3.3% complaint/reopen outcome in the cited path, while adding more quality attention to high-value repairs than to low-risk accessories.

**Why it should help.** It prevents an avoidable fulfillment error from converting a quick approval into a reshipment, complaint, or credit. A pre-dispatch gate is estimated to add 6–18 hours depending on design, which is a deliberate exchange of some speed for fewer costly errors. Targeting the check by value and uncertainty avoids applying the full delay indiscriminately. A price reference can also address the stated cause of technician quote waits, while targeted repair checks focus limited inspector time where observed rework is highest.

**Deliberate sacrifice or unresolved issue.** The gate adds time and handling cost, and an overly broad rule could erase the fast path's advantage. Two inspectors may become a bottleneck if every replacement or repair is sent to them, especially after campaigns. A compatibility reference can be stale or incomplete; a check only works if the source data and exception ownership are reliable. This redesign does not reduce evidence-request loops or fix the upstream high-value review delays.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A**

- Updated rules may send 15–35% more cases into manual review and push the seven reviewers above 82% utilization. The result could be longer waits for both high-value and low-risk exceptions.
- Risk specialists may become the new queue, especially on Mondays and after marketing campaigns; adding a risk gate without arrival control could replace compliance deviations with SLA failures.
- Incorrect segment, value, or evidence data can put a high-value claim into the low-risk fast lane. Exception rules and reason codes need audit and correction paths.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** work shifts from the auto screen to Manual eligibility review/Fraud-risk review. Track queue age and utilization, not just auto decision time.

**Redesign B**

- Customers with low digital literacy may abandon the portal or provide unusable images, worsening the already-high abandonment of unclear-defect claims. Assisted intake must remain available and visible.
- A checklist that requests too much evidence can add friction without improving decisions; a checklist that is too broad or poorly tailored can preserve repeat requests.
- More complete cases can arrive at reviewers and risk specialists in a tighter wave, increasing queues even while evidence handling improves.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** delay moves from evidence chasing to reviewer/risk queues or to the customer-assistance channel. Measure end-to-end completion and queue age after evidence arrives.

**Redesign C**

- The part reference may be wrong or stale, making the gate a false assurance and allowing errors through; an overly cautious checker may also reject valid substitutions.
- Applying the gate to every low-risk dispatch can add 6–18 hours where the EUR 95 error exposure does not justify that delay. If exceptions are too permissive, expensive high-value errors remain.
- Targeted high-value repair checks could exceed inspector capacity (two inspectors), particularly following campaigns; rushed checks could increase errors.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** dispatch waits move to the pre-dispatch gate or Quality check, while price exceptions may accumulate with the three technicians. Track gate wait separately from gate work time.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

1. **Establish a case-level baseline and pilot the policy gates first.** Reconstruct a recent baseline by segment, value, defect, and path, with mature complaint/reopen and cost outcomes. Then pilot Redesign A in a limited queue or region: retain a fast lane for complete low-risk claims, route above-EUR-400 claims through the required manual or risk review, and enforce evidence-before-dispatch and reopen review. Use a concurrent matched or randomized holdout within the same segments where operationally feasible. Start with a capped share so added manual inflow cannot silently exceed available capacity. A short shadow run should confirm that the rules classify cases correctly before live routing.

2. **Monitor outcomes that expose shifted work.** At minimum track end-to-end median and p90 cycle time from intake to final close, time in each queue, reviewer utilization and risk queue age, policy breaches by R1/R2/R3, fast-path complaints/reopens, repeat evidence requests, abandonment, wrong-part dispatches, repair rework, reshipments, credits, labor time, and fully loaded cost per resolved case. Break results out by Premium/Standard, low-risk/high-value, and unclear-evidence segments. Give cases a fixed follow-up window long enough to capture complaints and reopen activity; compare cohorts only after that window matures, and report unresolved/censored cases separately.

3. **Use explicit proposed guardrails, then tune them with baseline variation.** Any confirmed R1/R2/R3 breach in the pilot should trigger a pause and rule correction. For low-risk segments, require complaint/reopen not to rise by more than about 1 percentage point and avoid worsening p90 cycle time by more than about 10% without a clear offsetting quality gain. For high-value claims, look for a material decrease from the observed 18–22% fast-path complaint rates toward the 5–6% reviewed/repair outcomes, while keeping reviewer utilization below the stated 82% queue-growth threshold. These are pilot decision thresholds, not predicted effects; set final thresholds against measured baseline variation and sample size before launch.

4. **Pilot Redesign B on the evidence-heavy segment after A's routing is stable.** Randomize or phase the portal plus assisted option for unclear-defect and repeat-request cases, keeping the current evidence process as a control. A go signal would be a clear reduction in repeated requests (for example, at least 25–30% relative in the pilot, within the portal's stated 30–45% potential), with no material increase in abandonment among low-digital-literacy customers and no unacceptable reviewer queue growth. Compare both self-service and assisted users; a portal-only success among digitally confident customers is not sufficient.

5. **Pilot Redesign C narrowly on high-value and uncertain-part fulfillment.** First measure wrong-part frequency and cost by device/defect type. Compare a targeted compatibility gate with the existing selection process, including the added 6–18-hour delay and inspector/technician workload. Expand only if errors, complaint/reopen, or repair rework fall enough to justify that delay and total cost improves or customer outcomes materially improve without breaching service commitments. Avoid a blanket gate until segment-level results show it is worthwhile.

6. **Decide on portfolio, not a single average.** Keep, combine, or withdraw each change based on segment-level net outcomes. A may be the routing foundation, B a targeted evidence intervention, and C a high-value fulfillment control; they need not be deployed to every claim together. To confirm an improvement is real rather than displaced downstream, compare the full path through mature close, include labor and rework/credits/reshipments, check all three control points, inspect queue migration, and verify that reduced repeats or complaints do not reappear as abandonment, unresolved claims, or a new backlog.
