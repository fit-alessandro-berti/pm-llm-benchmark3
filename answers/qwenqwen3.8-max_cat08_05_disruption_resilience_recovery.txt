## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this incident, **resilience** means preserving the most important service objective—protecting hospital-critical supply—while maintaining, substituting, or explicitly recovering the controls required for patient safety, traceability, and regulatory compliance. Resilience is not simply “keeping goods moving.” It is adaptive capacity with three properties:

1. **Service protection**: critical orders continue to flow.
2. **Control preservation or documented substitution**: required controls are either maintained, replaced by an equivalent manual control, or formally risk-accepted.
3. **Recoverability**: missing evidence and hidden workload are surfaced quickly, not buried in after-the-fact cleanup.

**Concealed noncompliance** occurs when throughput is preserved by removing, weakening, or delaying required controls, while the loss of control is not visible in operational metrics. The manual phase shows this clearly: hospital-critical fill rate remained high, but traceability exceptions rose from 0.8% to 9.5%, cold-chain temperature documentation missing rose from 0.4% to 4.8%, and controlled-substance dual-control missing rose from 0.2% to 2.9%. The operation looked resilient on service, but the control environment had degraded.

### True resilience

The following adaptations were genuinely resilient when they preserved both service and control:

- **B. Manual authorization check**: preserved authorization control under outage conditions.
- **F. Temporary release control**, especially where it provided 100% manual release for controlled-substance orders.
- **A. Hospital-critical fast lane**, to the extent that it prioritized critical patients without bypassing mandatory product controls.
- **I. Defer noncritical order**, when used as explicit, time-boxed triage to protect critical capacity.
- **J. Backlog prioritization**, when based on transparent risk rules.
- **K. Reconcile manual reservation**, as a recovery control to restore inventory and order integrity.
- **L. Audit remediation**, as a catch-up mechanism, provided it is not treated as equivalent to real-time compliance.

### Risky shortcuts and concealed noncompliance

The following adaptations were primarily control-eroding shortcuts:

- **C. Skip system authorization check**: preserved ambient throughput but created an authorization gap. This is concealed noncompliance unless replaced by a pre-approved standing authorization list and post-audit.
- **G. Dispatch without scan**: accelerated dispatch but created the largest traceability gap. This is not resilience for high-risk products because it destroys custody and recall traceability.
- **H. Phone proof of delivery** when used as the sole evidence for controlled-substance, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive orders.
- **D. Manual inventory snapshot check** after the snapshot became stale, especially for batch/lot-sensitive or recall-sensitive products.
- **E. Manual pick list** when scans were reconstructed without clear flags, evidence, or error checks.
- **L. Audit remediation** if used to justify closing cases before high-risk evidence gaps were resolved.

### Ambiguous adaptations depending on risk appetite

Several adaptations are neither purely resilient nor purely noncompliant. Their classification depends on product risk, customer criticality, documentation quality, and time window:

- **A. Hospital-critical fast lane**: resilient if it accelerates work; noncompliant if it bypasses authorization or product controls.
- **D. Manual inventory snapshot check**: acceptable for the first few hours; increasingly risky as inventory becomes stale.
- **E. Manual pick list**: acceptable if batch/lot/expiry and picker identity are captured; risky if evidence is reconstructed later without audit trail.
- **F. Temporary release control**: resilient for controlled-substance orders if 100% checked; inadequate for cold-chain orders if temperature documentation is missed.
- **H. Phone proof of delivery**: acceptable for low-risk ambient orders with corroborating courier evidence; unacceptable as sole proof for controlled or cold-chain products.
- **I. Defer noncritical order**: legitimate triage if transparent; unfair or commercially damaging if informal and indefinite.
- **J. Backlog prioritization**: resilient if rules are explicit; risky if informal and unfair.
- **L. Audit remediation**: necessary recovery work, but not equivalent to having had the control at the time of dispatch.

The conformance findings support this distinction. The manual workaround phase had fitness 0.71 and only 44% fitting traces. Move-on-log deviations included `Skip system authorization check`, `Dispatch without scan`, `Phone proof of delivery`, and `Spreadsheet invoice hold`. Move-on-model deviations included missing quality/release check, missing scan, missing proof of delivery, and missing temperature-control record. These are not harmless variants; they represent missing normative controls.

## 4. Recovery process model

I recommend a **Minimum-Viable Compliant Degraded Operation** model. The purpose is to replace ad hoc improvisation with a pre-approved degraded process that protects hospital-critical supply while preserving the minimum controls required for pharmaceutical wholesale compliance.

**Notation used below:**  
- `` means sequence.  
- `XOR` means an exclusive decision path.  
- `||` means parallel activities.  
- `[Gate]` means a mandatory control gate that must be passed or formally risk-accepted.

### 4.1 Explicit trigger conditions and phase gates

| Phase | Entry trigger | Operating rule | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manual Mode** | WMS unavailable >30 minutes; scanning unavailable >30 minutes; inventory synchronization unavailable >30 minutes; cyber incident declared affecting order-to-delivery; any suspected loss of controlled-substance ledger integrity. | Only approved degraded-mode variants may be used. High-risk products require mandatory manual controls. No dispatch without at least manual chain-of-custody. | Systems available for high-risk processing; manual evidence capture complete; no uncontrolled dispatches remain open. |
| **Partial Recovery Mode** | Order intake restored, but inventory synchronization error >2%, scan reliability <95%, or backlog age exceeds service threshold. | Hybrid operation. System-assisted order intake, but manual controls remain for high-risk products. Reconciliation occurs at least once per shift. | Inventory synchronization 99%; scan capture 98%; manual records imported; high-risk exceptions identified. |
| **Stabilization Mode** | Systems mostly restored; backlog remains; reconciliation and audit remediation required. | Normal processing plus explicit backlog prioritization, reconciliation, and audit remediation. Operational recovery and compliance recovery are tracked separately. | High-risk audit remediation closed; reconciliation complete; fitness to approved recovery model 0.95; fitting traces 90%; exception rates within agreed thresholds. |
| **Incident Closure** | Operational and compliance recovery complete. | Close incident only after hidden workload is resolved or formally risk-accepted. | No open high-risk traceability, temperature, controlled-substance, authorization, or recall exposure. |

### 4.2 Triage rules

Use a priority lane model rather than informal fast lanes.

| Priority lane | Definition | Service target | Deferral rule | Control rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1: Hospital-critical high-risk** | Hospital-critical orders containing cold-chain, controlled-substance, recall-sensitive, or clinically urgent items. | Ship within 8–12 hours where physically possible. | No deferral without medical/pharmacist escalation. | Full degraded-mode controls mandatory. No skip authorization, no dispatch without custody, no missing temperature or dual control. |
| **P2: Hospital-critical standard and Pharmacy high-risk** | Hospital-critical ambient orders; Pharmacy-standard cold-chain, controlled-substance, or recall-sensitive orders. | Ship within 12–24 hours. | No silent cancellation. | Manual authorization and release controls required. High-risk evidence mandatory. |
| **P3: Pharmacy-standard and essential Wholesale-bulk** | Pharmacy-standard ambient orders and Wholesale-bulk orders needed for continuity. | Ship within 24–72 hours. | May be deferred with customer notice. | Simplified controls allowed for low-risk ambient products, but traceability and proof of delivery still required. |
| **P4: Nonessential Wholesale-bulk** | Bulk orders without immediate clinical or contractual urgency. | Ship after P1–P3 or by renegotiated date. | May be deferred up to a defined limit, for example 72–120 hours, unless customer agrees otherwise. | Deferral must be visible, communicated, and reviewed. No indefinite silent deferral. |

**Authorization rule:**  
- Use pre-approved offline authorization lists for known customers and products.  
- If a case is not on the list, perform manual authorization by delegated compliance staff.  
- For controlled-substance, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive products, authorization must be positively confirmed.  
- For low-risk ambient products, standing authorization may be used only if the customer/product pair is pre-approved and the case is flagged for post-incident audit.

### 4.3 Minimum controls by product/customer risk

| Risk class | Mandatory degraded-mode controls | Allowed manual substitute | Prohibited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Controlled-substance, all customers** | Customer authorization, product authorization, dual control, pharmacist/release approval, batch/lot record, security seal, courier custody, signed proof of delivery. | Paper dual-signature form, pharmacist signature, manual custody manifest, seal ID, courier signature, scan at first available point. | Skip authorization; dispatch without custody; phone-only proof of delivery; sample-only release. |
| **Cold-chain, all customers** | Validated packaging, pre-dispatch temperature reading, temperature indicator/logger ID, dispatch time, delivery confirmation, temperature evidence at receipt where required. | Paper temperature log, indicator photo, manual dispatch record, recipient temperature confirmation. | Dispatch without temperature evidence; phone-only proof of delivery where temperature status is not confirmed. |
| **Recall-sensitive items** | Batch/lot/expiry capture, recall hold check, destination traceability, positive proof of delivery. | Paper batch manifest, signed delivery record, manual recall lookup. | Dispatch without batch/lot; delivery without positive receipt evidence. |
| **Hospital-critical ambient** | Customer identity, product authorization, pick/pack record, dispatch record, proof of delivery. | Offline authorization list, paper pick list, courier manifest, phone or electronic proof of delivery. | Unauthorized customer shipment; missing proof of delivery. |
| **Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk ambient** | Customer identity, product authorization, pick/pack record, dispatch record, proof of delivery. | Standing authorization whitelist, simplified paper pick list, courier manifest, corroborated phone proof of delivery. | Indefinite deferral; missing invoice evidence; dispatch without custody record. |

### 4.4 Manual evidence capture

Every manual record must be distinguishable from a normal system record and must not be backdated.

Minimum evidence set:

- Incident ID and manually controlled form number.
- Order ID, customer ID, customer priority lane.
- Product ID, batch/lot, expiry date, quantity.
- Picker and packer identity.
- Authorization method: system, offline list, manual compliance check, or standing authorization.
- Release approver identity and time.
- Temperature readings or indicator ID for cold-chain.
- Dual-control signatures for controlled-substance.
- Courier name, vehicle ID, manifest ID, seal ID, dispatch time.
- Proof of delivery: recipient name, signature or electronic confirmation, delivery time, and temperature/security confirmation where required.
- Flag indicating whether a scan or event is **original manual evidence** or **reconstructed after recovery**.

Capture method:

- Use sequentially numbered paper forms or offline mobile/barcode capture.
- Photograph or scan forms as soon as connectivity allows.
- Store records in an immutable incident evidence repository.
- Reconstructed system events must be marked as reconstructed and linked to the original manual evidence.

### 4.5 Backlog prioritization

Backlog prioritization must be explicit, auditable, and fair.

Rules:

1. Process P1 first, then P2, P3, and P4.
2. Within each priority lane, process oldest order first.
3. Add exceptions for expiry risk, recall exposure, contractual commitments, and clinical urgency.
4. Impose aging caps: for example, P1 should not age beyond 12 hours without escalation; P2 beyond 24 hours; P3 beyond 72 hours; P4 beyond the agreed deferral window.
5. Publish daily backlog age by segment and priority.
6. Cancellations must be deliberate and documented, not a side effect of informal deferral.
7. Customer communications must be logged for deferred Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk orders.

### 4.6 Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

Reconciliation and audit remediation must be treated as a formal recovery process, not as background cleanup.

**BPMN-style flow:**

1. **System recovery detected**  **Freeze manual records**.
2. **Import manual records** || **Import system records** || **Import courier/temperature evidence**.
3. **Three-way match**: order record  inventory movement  custody/delivery evidence.
4. **Exception gateway XOR**:
   - Missing authorization  Compliance authorization remediation.
   - Missing scan or custody  Traceability remediation.
   - Missing temperature record  Cold-chain quality review.
   - Missing controlled-substance dual control  Controlled-substance investigation.
   - Missing proof of delivery  Proof-of-delivery remediation.
   - Inventory mismatch  Cycle count and reservation correction.
5. **[Quality gate]**: High-risk cases cannot be invoice-closed until evidence is complete or a formal risk acceptance is signed by Quality/Compliance.
6. **Audit remediation**  root-cause classification  regulatory/customer notification if required  closure.
7. **Incident closure gate**: close only when high-risk remediation is complete and hidden workload is no longer growing.

This flow recognizes that **after-the-fact audit remediation is necessary but not equivalent to real-time compliance**. A reconstructed temperature record or manually entered proof of delivery may reduce audit exposure, but it does not recreate the preventive control that should have existed at dispatch.

|---|---|---|---|
| Throughput | Median and p90 cycle time by segment | Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, Wholesale-bulk cycle times. | Hospital should recover first; prolonged nonhospital delays indicate triage excess or backlog mismanagement. |

## 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P0** | Create a formal degraded-mode playbook with explicit trigger conditions, priority lanes, mandatory controls, and stop-ship rules for high-risk products. | Medium | Prevents ad hoc improvisation. Makes resilience repeatable and auditable. | Human error under stress; playbook must be rehearsed. |
| **P0** | Pre-position manual chain-of-custody kits: numbered forms, courier manifests, seal logs, temperature logs, dual-control sheets, and pharmacist release forms. | Low to Medium | Directly addresses dispatch-without-scan, missing temperature documentation, and missing controlled-substance dual control. | Paper records can be lost or poorly completed if training is weak. |
| **P0** | Build offline authorization packs and standing-authorization whitelists for known customers/products, with strict update governance. | Medium | Replaces “skip system authorization check” with a controlled, auditable alternative. | Lists can become stale if ownership and refresh frequency are not enforced. |
| **P1** | Deploy offline barcode/mobile capture with store-and-forward capability so manual picks and dispatches can be captured locally and synchronized later. | High | Greatly reduces reconstructed scans and traceability gaps. Improves evidence quality. | Device security, battery life, network sync conflicts, and cyber containment must be managed. |
| **P1** | Define inventory snapshot freshness rules and manual cycle counts for high-risk items during outages. | Medium | Reduces overselling, stockouts, batch errors, and reconciliation burden. | Inventory drift can still occur during high-volume manual operations. |
| **P1** | Implement explicit backlog prioritization with aging caps, fairness dashboard, and customer communication requirements. | Medium | Protects hospitals while reducing unfair deferral of Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk orders. | Capacity constraints may still force difficult trade-offs. |
| **P1** | Establish a reconciliation and audit-remediation control tower with separate SLAs for high-risk cases. | Medium | Makes hidden workload visible. Prevents false declaration of recovery based only on cycle time. | Remediation backlog can grow if Quality/Compliance staffing is insufficient. |
| **P2** | Integrate couriers into manual proof-of-delivery and temperature/security confirmation processes. | Medium to High | Strengthens custody and delivery evidence, especially for cold-chain and controlled-substance products. | Courier compliance may vary; contractual and technical integration required. |
| **P2** | Improve cyber resilience for WMS and scanning: segmented backups, read-only inventory replica, secure offline exports, and tested restoration runbooks. | High | Reduces duration and severity of future outages. | Sophisticated cyber incidents may still impair systems; manual mode remains necessary. |
| **P2** | Run quarterly simulation exercises using the degraded-mode playbook, including controlled-substance and cold-chain scenarios. | Low to Medium | Builds muscle memory and reveals control gaps before a real incident. | Simulation may not fully replicate stress and volume of a real cyber incident. |

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept temporary, explicitly authorized degraded operations that protect hospital-critical supply, even if Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk cycle times worsen and rework increases, provided the degraded controls are documented, time-boxed, and evidence-producing. Management should not accept silent skipping of authorization, dispatch without chain-of-custody, missing cold-chain temperature evidence, missing controlled-substance dual control, phone-only proof of delivery for high-risk products, reconstructed records presented as real-time evidence, or operational closure while high-risk audit remediation remains open. The recommendation would change only if evidence showed that manual substitutes were genuinely equivalent: near-complete manual evidence capture, zero unresolved high-risk control gaps, regulator-approved temporary controls, reliable courier evidence, and a clear, documented hospital stockout threat that justified a narrowly scoped risk acceptance.
