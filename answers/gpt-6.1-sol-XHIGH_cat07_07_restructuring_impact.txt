# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring exchanges local access and regional knowledge for pooled capacity and common decision rules. Its effect depends on effective capacity by specialty, the additional work sent to the shared service, and whether each case retains a clear owner. Pooling could help East, whose technical specialist is already at 82% utilization, while imposing additional coordination costs on North and South.

The supplied estimates do not demonstrate a reduction in total handovers. If the listed categories are disjoint and measured consistently, they total **343 handovers per 100 cases before and 359 afterward**, an increase of about 4.7%. Technical outbound/return transitions increase from 67 to 77; approval transitions increase from 81 to 92. Ad hoc cross-team transfers fall from 6 to 3. Management may therefore reduce one kind of transfer while increasing transitions through formal queues. Moreover, a formerly local transfer becomes an organizational boundary crossing when the same specialist moves into a central team.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

These are conditional forecasts, not numerical predictions of post-change cycle times. The log summary lacks arrival variability, active service durations, specialist schedules, and sufficient detail on routing overlap to support a calibrated queueing forecast.

**Optimistic scenario: pooling is supported by adequate skill-specific capacity, consistent case notes, and explicit ownership.**

- **Handovers:** Ad hoc transfers and avoidable clarification loops decline. Formal technical referrals can still increase as management predicts, but advice returns to the original owner and specialists share information without duplicated requests. Actual handlers and repeat referrals per case fall even if formal queue crossings do not.
- **Queue/waiting times:** East gains access to spare technical capacity. Skill-aware routing, protected legal/risk capacity, and explicit priority rules prevent head-of-line blocking. Shorter approval processing and fewer clarifications offset some additional queue-entry delay. Backlogs remain stable through ordinary demand peaks.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East's median and p90 improve most. North/South remain stable or improve modestly as standardized decisions reduce downstream work. Improvements extend to complex cases and the upper tail, rather than being confined to simple cases.
- **Rework and quality:** Shared templates and consistent specialist advice reduce avoidable rework and improve outcomes. Regional context and accommodations remain available through the local case owner.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Every request names the specialist decision maker, and every case retains a local accountable owner. The shared approval queue records who approved, which rule applied, and who resolves exceptions.
- **Fairness:** East's service disadvantage narrows without materially worsening North/South or accommodation-sensitive segments. Priority-sensitive service remains consistent across locations.
- **Early warning signs:** Technical or legal/risk backlog starts growing despite good aggregate results; regional clarification requests rise; completed advice remains unclaimed; ordinary-priority requests age while urgent work dominates. These would challenge the optimistic assumptions before final outcomes deteriorate.

**Central scenario: technical pooling partly succeeds, but increased demand and coordination consume much of the benefit.**

- **Handovers:** Ad hoc cross-team transfers fall, while technical and approval transitions rise approximately as estimated. Some advice returns to a different caseworker, and extra clarification offsets the disappearance of local loops. Total recorded handovers are flat or higher.
- **Queue/waiting times:** Technical waits increase modestly, with peaks around difficult cases and Quality Check. Legal/Risk remain the dominant constraint. Approval waits increase as more cases require approval and one supervisor position is removed. Queue aging becomes more uneven across specialties.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East's typical case improves; North/South are flat or somewhat slower. An aggregate median or mean can improve if simple work speeds up, while high-complexity p90/p95 and specialist-dependent SLA misses worsen. The overall p90 could remain flat while particular segments deteriorate.
- **Rework and quality:** Standardization reduces some inconsistent decisions, but context loss creates more clarification and local exceptions. Complex North/South cases show higher rework; East can improve where earlier overload caused quality problems.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Formal roles become more uniform, but ownership at the advice-return and approval boundaries is uneven. Some cases wait for someone to accept responsibility rather than for technical work itself.
- **Fairness:** East gains, but benefits are distributed unevenly. Customers needing regional or language accommodation may make more contacts or receive generic advice. Low-priority complex cases risk being repeatedly deferred.
- **Early warning signs:** Specialist arrivals consistently exceed completions; local referral rates rise beyond the intended routing change; owner changes, clarification variants, approval aging, and unclaimed advice increase. Local staff report less opportunity to solve difficult cases.

**Adverse scenario: extra demand overwhelms scarce skills, and ownership and local expertise deteriorate.**

- **Handovers:** A graph aggregated to a single “Shared Service” node looks simpler, but individual-resource transfers, repeated specialist reviews, queue bouncing, and reassignments increase. Informal workarounds and exceptions become a parallel process.
- **Queue/waiting times:** Technical triage and difficult Quality Check add unplanned workload. Legal/risk bottlenecks propagate into the technical queue; approval becomes another constraint. High utilization leaves little room for variability or absence, producing persistent backlog growth and long waits.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** p90/p95, open-case age, and SLA misses increase substantially, especially for complex and specialist-dependent cases. Early averages among completed cases can look favorable because delayed cases have not closed. A central absence or operational failure can affect every location simultaneously.
- **Rework and quality:** Generic advice misses local conventions, hurried decisions increase errors, and cases return from Quality Check to review or approval. Satisfaction declines and repeat contacts rise. East can also lose its expected benefit if its prior local bottleneck becomes a larger central one.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases move between queues without a responsible owner; approvals are delayed, duplicated, or contested. Local workers lack both decision authority and access to timely specialist advice.
- **Fairness:** Scarce skills, priority overrides, and unequal ability to escalate produce large team and segment differences. Customers needing accommodations bear extra delays; less assertive customers or teams can be disadvantaged even at the same legitimate priority.
- **Early warning signs:** Repeated weekly backlog increases, rising age of unstarted requests, stalled handovers, growing exception paths and reassignment counts, overloaded specialist schedules, and staff reports that urgent escalation has become the normal route.

Queueing is nonlinear. Pooling can reduce variability, but pushing effective utilization close to capacity sharply increases waiting when arrivals or service times vary. A shared queue also cannot solve a shortage in a particular qualified skill merely by pooling unrelated staff.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** Many simple cases can accelerate while a smaller number wait much longer. Monitor p90/p95, overdue open work, and the oldest queued requests. An average over closed cases can improve specifically because difficult cases remain unfinished.
2. **High-complexity cases:** Mandatory central routing concentrates long and variable tasks. Their waits and repeated reviews can increase even as the larger simple-case population improves. Compare distributions within complexity and specialty, including cases requiring multiple specialties.
3. **Local knowledge loss:** A generic answer may be delivered quickly but miss a regional convention or needed accommodation. Subsequent clarification, rework, complaints, or reopened cases reveal the loss later. Track early local advice and context-sensitive outcomes.
4. **Fairness:** An aggregate improvement can conceal deterioration in North/South, particular customer segments, or ordinary-priority cases. East may benefit disproportionately, which can be desirable, but does not excuse avoidable harm elsewhere. Report group-level results and investigate accommodations that customer_segment does not identify.
5. **Learning:** Routing every difficult case away can reduce local staff's exposure and competence. Initial cycle times may benefit while future referral dependence and specialist demand grow. Monitor mentoring, joint reviews, and competence, rather than assuming a narrow resource activity profile means efficiency.
6. **Accountability:** Standard approval output can hide cases without a responsible owner, unclear authority, or decisions that nobody will explain to the customer. Measure orphaned work, owner changes, contested approvals, and time spent accepting returned advice.
7. **Exception handling:** Rare but legitimate cases may bounce between technical, legal/risk, approval and QC queues. Their small volume has little effect on the mean. Track exception variants, repeated requests, escalation reasons, and time to resolution.
8. **Loss of resilience:** A shared pool can cover routine absence yet become a common failure point. A scarce-skill absence or demand surge may create a long recovery backlog. Monitor qualified backups and performance during disruptions, not just utilization during normal weeks.
9. **Hidden labor and measurement changes:** Shorter recorded cycles can coexist with more informal calls, unpaid overtime, or work outside the event log. Renaming activities, collapsing resources, or changing Close semantics can create artificial improvement. Keep logging definitions stable and triangulate workload with staff feedback.

The present data do not directly record accommodation needs, approved value bands, accountable ownership, staff availability, or customer satisfaction. Add appropriate operational measurements; do not infer sensitive characteristics from names or locations, or claim that the current columns establish these effects.

# 5. Pilot design

**Scope and sequencing.** Start with a limited, stratified share of eligible cases from North, South and East—for example, 20–30% where volume and capacity permit. Keep a named local case owner and access to local technical consultation. Establish a technical shared-service route first, with consistent case notes, advice-return responsibility, and qualified backup cover. Give legal/risk/security and difficult QC explicit skill routing and protected capacity; a common intake interface need not mean one undifferentiated work queue.

Do not remove the supervisor position in the first stage. Test shared approval separately after technical routing is stable, then evaluate the proposed staffing reduction as an additional change. This identifies whether effects arise from pooling, expanded referral rules, approval changes, or capacity removal. Specify what happens when a case meets both the high-complexity rule and a legal/risk/security rule: create the necessary coordinated requests without accidental duplication or an unqualified substitution.

**Comparison group.** Where feasible, assign eligible cases randomly within each team and relevant complexity/specialty/segment stratum to shared-service or existing local routing. Reserve corresponding staff capacity for both groups and record actual staffing. Otherwise use matched concurrent local-routing cohorts and a pre/post comparison, reporting the limits of causal inference. East must have its own treatment/comparison analysis; North or South alone is not an adequate control for East.

Shared specialists can create spillovers into the comparison group. Record cross-cover and shared legal/risk exposure, maintain practical capacity boundaries, and report contamination. Interpret the primary effect by assigned routing, including cases rescued or rerouted, rather than removing unsuccessful pilot cases. A low-volume pilot with generous spare capacity is not proof that the full system can sustain the projected load.

**Observation window.** Use about 8–12 weeks of comparable baseline data and an 8–12-week pilot, with weekly leading-indicator reviews. Continue follow-up at least 4–6 weeks after the final pilot intake and longer if necessary to observe slow cases. Current p90 values reach 22.8 days, so a short completed-case snapshot is inadequate. Continue learning and resilience monitoring for several months. Record calendar effects, staffing, absences, outages, and changes in case mix. Assign cohorts by intake/assignment date, not Close date; separately identify transition cases exposed to both structures.

**Segmentation.** Retain originating local team even when event team changes to Shared Service. Capture initial complexity, specialty needs, priority, and customer segment before routing; also record subsequent changes. Evaluate North, South and East separately; low/medium/high complexity; technical/legal/risk/security and multiple-specialty demand; and relevant customer segments. Include accommodation needs where appropriately recorded. Approval value/rule metadata must be added because priority does not establish case value.

**Guardrails.** Predefine operational thresholds using baseline variability, actual SLAs and acceptable harm. Illustrative adjustment triggers are:

- Persistent queue growth over two weekly reviews, rising p90 waiting, or sustained high utilization with insufficient qualified cover: cap admissions, add capacity, or separate congested specialty queues. An 85–90% utilization alert can be a starting assumption, not a universal safe threshold.
- A team or material segment's p90 cycle time rises more than 20%, or its SLA miss/rework rate rises more than 2 percentage points versus the concurrent comparison and baseline: investigate and pause expansion once enough observations support a sustained signal. Review counts and uncertainty so small samples do not produce false reassurance or spurious conclusions.
- An overdue urgent case, serious quality failure, lost ownership, or denied necessary accommodation: immediate case rescue and review, without waiting for a statistically significant group trend.
- Growing disparities within comparable priority/complexity/specialty groups, declining local technical learning, or repeated regional-context errors: revise routing, retain local consultation, and introduce joint reviews or mentoring.

Compare capacity and waiting at expected full-rollout demand using observed arrivals, service-time distributions, skill requirements and absence scenarios. Test the projected increases in technical, specialist and approval demand, not merely today's referral volume. If adequate legal/risk/security capacity or approval cover cannot be demonstrated, leave those elements out of expansion until they are resolved.

**Staff feedback.** Collect a short weekly survey across local workers, specialists, supervisors and all three teams on ownership, useful advice, missing context, workload, and learning. Hold structured reviews of a sample of ordinary cases, complex cases and exceptions, including both successful and delayed cases. Connect themes to handover_reason, variants and recorded outcomes. Include customer feedback on repeated contacts and accommodations.

**Rollback and adjustment.** Retain the ability to route new cases locally, restore named approvals, or slow admissions. Give every already-open case an owner and a documented transfer plan before changing routes; otherwise rollback can create another backlog. Expand only when workload is sustainable, segment-level guardrails hold, quality is stable or better, and staff can explain who owns each decision. If measures fail, adjust capacity, queue design or scope and repeat the affected phase before proceeding.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with modifications to the proposed queue and staffing design.** Technical pooling is a plausible remedy for East's overload and inconsistent absence cover. North/South's successful local expertise and stable loops are assets worth retaining through local consultation and case ownership. Legal/Risk's existing utilization, additional difficult-QC work, and increased approval demand make immediate full rollout insufficiently supported.

Use skill-aware queues, explicit original-case ownership, consistent notes, protected scarce-specialty capacity, and local mentoring. Defer supervisor removal until shared approval demonstrates adequate capacity and accountability. Expand conditionally on stable queue age and backlog, sustainable specialty utilization, acceptable p90/SLA/rework results in each team and relevant segment, and preserved exception handling and learning.

Management should not infer from an early improvement in the average that handovers have decreased, complex cases are faster, quality has improved, fairness has been protected, or the system will withstand absence. Nor does higher specialist utilization establish success. Early averages are affected by case mix, transition work, and unfinished slow cases. Decisions should combine mature outcome cohorts with open-work indicators, segment-level comparisons, staff evidence, and a realistic full-load capacity assessment.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The following Python-style sketch separates measures supported by the supplied columns from measures requiring additional instrumentation. It is an analysis design rather than a claim that the log already contains service intervals, queue arrivals or SLA definitions. Use the installed PM4Py version's documented signatures and keep parameters consistent across periods.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

C = 'case:concept:name'
A = 'concept:name'
T = 'time:timestamp'
R = 'org:resource'

# Inputs:
# raw_events: the event dataframe described in the question.
# case_registry: one validated row per case, established at intake/assignment:
#   origin_team, initial_complexity, initial_specialty,
#   initial_customer_segment, initial_priority, intake_at,
#   assigned_route, cohort_period, and actual SLA deadline/policy.
# snapshot_at: the timezone-aware extraction cutoff.
# selected_customer_segments: actual segment labels for the example filter.
# Use cohort_period based on entry/assignment, NOT completion date.
# Derive origin_team from the first genuine local intake if reliable;
# do not use the specialist's later event team as the case's origin.
# Keep multi-specialty needs as explicit case memberships as well.

raw_events[T] = pd.to_datetime(raw_events[T], utc=True)
events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw_events.copy(), case_id=C, activity_key=A, timestamp_key=T
)

# Preserve a reliable source sequence for equal timestamps. If none exists,
# mark tied-event order as ambiguous and test network/DFG sensitivity.
# 'event_sequence' is an added source field, not part of the supplied schema.
order = [C, T] + (['event_sequence'] if 'event_sequence' in events else [])
events = events.sort_values(order, kind='stable')
events = events.merge(case_registry, on=C, how='left', validate='many_to_one')

# Keep complete traces for assigned cohorts, including events after the
# intake window. Mark transition/co-intervention cases separately.
before = events[events['cohort_period'].eq('before')].copy()
pilot = events[events['cohort_period'].eq('pilot')].copy()
after = pilot[pilot['assigned_route'].eq('shared')].copy()
control = pilot[pilot['assigned_route'].eq('local')].copy()


def segment(log, teams=None, complexities=None,
            specialties=None, customer_segments=None):
    # Constant case attributes make these whole-case filters.
    # Filtering event 'team' directly would drop shared-service events
    # and create artificially shortened traces.
    selections = {
        'origin_team': teams,
        'initial_complexity': complexities,
        'initial_specialty': specialties,
        'initial_customer_segment': customer_segments,
    }
    for attribute, values in selections.items():
        if values is not None:
            log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
                log, attribute, values, level='case', retain=True
            )
    return log


def transitions(log):
    d = log.sort_values(order, kind='stable').copy()
    grouped = d.groupby(C, sort=False)
    d['previous_resource'] = grouped[R].shift()
    d['previous_activity'] = grouped[A].shift()
    d['previous_time'] = grouped[T].shift()
    d['previous_team'] = grouped['team'].shift()
    d['inter_event_gap_h'] = (
        d[T] - d['previous_time']
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    # These gaps include processing, off-hours and other delays.
    # They are NOT measured queue waits or active service times.
    return d[d['previous_time'].notna()].copy()


def completed_traces(log):
    # Assumes Close is a terminal event. Validate against operational
    # case status; a provisional Close or reopened case needs more logic.
    last = log.sort_values(order, kind='stable').groupby(C).tail(1)
    closed_ids = last.loc[last[A].eq('Close'), C]
    return log[log[C].isin(closed_ids)].copy()


def structural_summary(log):
    if log.empty:
        return {'n_cases': 0}  # Do not interpret empty cells as good outcomes.

    # These functions return different organizational views.
    how = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
    together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
    subcontracting = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log)
    similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)

    # DFGs retain the full process; open cases remain useful for finding
    # emerging delays. Compare edge definitions and time units consistently.
    dfg = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
    performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    edge_gaps = transitions(log).groupby(['previous_activity', A])[
        'inter_event_gap_h'
    ].agg(n='size', p50='median', p90=lambda x: x.quantile(0.90))

    closed = completed_traces(log)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(closed) if not closed.empty else {}
    # A prespecified common variant can be examined with:
    # focus = pm4py.filter_variants(closed, selected_variants, retain=True)
    # Preserve exception variants and compare shares against all cases.

    pairs = transitions(log)
    known = pairs[R].notna() & pairs['previous_resource'].notna()
    handovers = pairs[known & pairs[R].ne(pairs['previous_resource'])]
    n_cases = log[C].nunique()
    handover_rates = (
        handovers.groupby(['previous_resource', R]).size()
        .mul(100 / n_cases).rename('transfers_per_100_cases')
    )
    boundary_crossings = handovers[
        handovers['team'].notna()
        & handovers['previous_team'].notna()
        & handovers['team'].ne(handovers['previous_team'])
    ]

    # Activity repetition is a signal, not a definitive defect label.
    repeated_activity_cases = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
    temporal_features = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)

    return {
        'n_cases': n_cases,
        'handover_network': how,
        'working_together_network': together,
        'subcontracting_network': subcontracting,
        'activity_similarity': similarity,
        'roles': roles,
        'dfg': dfg,
        'performance_dfg': performance_dfg,
        'edge_gap_percentiles': edge_gaps,
        'variants_completed': variants,
        'handover_rates': handover_rates,
        'total_handovers_per_100': 100 * len(handovers) / n_cases,
        'boundary_crossings_per_100': 100 * len(boundary_crossings) / n_cases,
        'repeat_activity_cases': repeated_activity_cases,
        'temporal_features': temporal_features,
    }


def case_outcomes(log, snapshot_at):
    # snapshot_at is the extraction cutoff, with compatible timezone.
    d = log.sort_values(order, kind='stable').copy()
    last = d.groupby(C).tail(1).set_index(C)
    intake = d.groupby(C)['intake_at'].first()
    closed = last[A].eq('Close')
    result = pd.DataFrame(index=last.index)
    result['closed'] = closed
    result['cycle_h'] = ((last[T] - intake).dt.total_seconds() / 3600).where(closed)
    result['open_age_h'] = (
        (snapshot_at - intake).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    ).where(~closed)

    # Explicitly normalize flag encodings; bool('False') would be wrong.
    flag = d['rework_flag'].isin([True, 1, '1', 'true', 'True'])
    observed_rework = flag | d[A].eq('Rework')
    result['observed_rework'] = observed_rework.groupby(d[C]).any()
    result['outcome'] = last['outcome']  # Validate final-outcome semantics.

    # sla_due_at must come from the actual SLA rule and working calendar.
    # Never invent a uniform threshold from priority or the current median.
    if 'sla_due_at' in d.columns:
        due = d.groupby(C)['sla_due_at'].first()
        assessment_time = last[T].where(closed, snapshot_at)
        known_due = due.notna()
        result['sla_miss_observed'] = assessment_time.gt(due).where(known_due)
        # Timely open cases remain unresolved for their final SLA outcome;
        # count overdue open cases now and report the unresolved remainder.
        result['sla_outcome_resolved'] = (
            closed | assessment_time.gt(due)
        ).where(known_due)
    return result


def duration_summary(log):
    # Requires reliable paired activity start/completion intervals.
    if 'start_timestamp' not in log.columns:
        return {'service': None, 'case_times': None}
    timed = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(

# True request waiting requires an additional table with request_id,
# case ID, queue/skill, queued_at, started_at, completed_at and status.
# Request identities prevent repeated reviews from being paired incorrectly.
def request_times(requests, snapshot_at):
    q = requests.copy()
    q['waiting_h'] = (
        q['started_at'] - q['queued_at']
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    q['service_h'] = (
        q['completed_at'] - q['started_at']
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    q['unstarted_age_h'] = (
        (snapshot_at - q['queued_at']).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    ).where(q['status'].eq('queued'))
    # Service intervals must handle pauses/off-hours according to policy.
    # Report starts/completions AND open age; started-only waits censor the tail.
    return q
```
