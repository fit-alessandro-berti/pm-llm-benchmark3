# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

**Automated intake and entitlement precheck.**  
A1 and A2 are core for Nora because they are the only observed activities and occur at very high volume. Although human front-office resources also execute these activities, the bot’s work is a boundary service: it initiates cases and performs prechecks rather than owning resolution. Its low median complexity supports that distinction.

**Permanent front-office resolution.**  
For Amira and Ben, validation, standard responses, and closure are core because they dominate both counts and routine variants. Registration is also routine, although much intake is automated. Diagnosis, pricing, negotiation, configuration, and implementation are peripheral extensions. Those extensions are not equivalent across both agents: Amira’s higher case complexity suggests greater discretion or experience, while Ben is more concentrated in high-volume routine work.

**Temporary routine-service support.**  
Registration, validation, standard response, and closure are core for Kai and Lina by frequency, but only within a narrow low-complexity scope. Their few diagnostic or negotiation events are peripheral and do not demonstrate specialist competence. The reopen evidence indicates that activity completion counts should be assessed alongside downstream quality and rework.

**Technical diagnosis and implementation.**  
Diagnosis, configuration, and implementation form the technical core for Chen and Dina. Security reviews, quality checks, closures, and KB updates are peripheral or collaborative activities. Despite similar activity profiles, Chen’s higher critical-case exposure and greater security adjacency indicate a senior or advanced technical tier. Similarity therefore supports a shared occupational family, not complete interchangeability.

**Security risk specialist.**  
Security review is Erik’s unmistakable core despite totaling only 96 events. Diagnosis, configuration, implementation, and post-incident knowledge work are peripheral because they support security decisions. His high complexity and critical-case concentration matter more than raw volume. Mateo’s filtering reduced Erik’s expected A6 count, showing that upstream triage can alter specialist frequency without reducing the role’s importance.

**Commercial exception specialist.**  
Pricing review and customer negotiation are Fatima’s core activities. Approval, validation, closure, and implementation are peripheral contributions around commercial decisions. Her A9 events do not imply supervisory authority; they may represent preparation, recording, or delegated workflow actions.

**Senior front-office/commercial bridge.**  
Giorgio has two durable activity centers: front-office resolution and commercial exception work. His pricing activity cannot be dismissed as purely temporary because his ten-week totals are substantial and he holds a pricing certification. Nevertheless, the concentrated absence episode inflated the commercial counts and coincided with pricing duration increasing from 38 to 74 minutes. His permanent role is therefore best represented as a senior front-office role with shadow commercial capacity, pending evidence about authorization and performance outside substitution periods.

**Supervisory decision authority.**  
A9 is Hana’s core by frequency and, more importantly, by formal authority. Negotiation, reopen review, and quality activity are peripheral escalation interventions. Mateo’s 21 preliminary approvals do not make him equivalent: 13 later required Hana’s recheck. The distinction is between workflow participation and final accountable authority.

**Quality assurance and knowledge stewardship.**  
Quality checks and KB updates are core for Ivan and Julia. Implementation, configuration, closure, and diagnosis are peripheral because quality analysts may reproduce, inspect, or finalize technical outcomes. Their near-identical profiles and complexity distributions make this one of the strongest clean role assignments.

**Cross-functional escalation coordination.**  
Mateo’s core cannot be inferred from a single largest count. Reopen coordination, routing, escalation triage, and orchestration across functions define the role. A9, A6, A7, and A8 are normally peripheral touches made while coordinating domain owners. Temporary preliminary approvals and security filtering expand his operational footprint but do not necessarily transfer final accountability.

**Conditional escalation cell.**  
This overlapping role exists only for relevant cases. Its core is collaboration among coordination, domain expertise, and final authority—not a common activity vector. The participating specialist activity is case-dependent: security review for Erik, pricing for Fatima or Giorgio, advanced diagnosis for Chen, routing for Mateo, and authorization for Hana.

Across all roles, “core” should therefore combine four signals: sustained frequency, concentration relative to the resource’s workload, case context, and recognized authority. A temporary spike or isolated workflow event is insufficient.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

### R07_Giorgio: commercial shadow and senior front-office bridge

Giorgio is not merely a front-office agent who occasionally touches pricing. His 72 pricing reviews, 88 negotiations, pricing certification, and complexity profile support meaningful commercial capability. He also absorbed a large portion of Fatima’s work during her absence.

This shadow role benefits the organization by providing continuity, translating between customer-facing and commercial concerns, and reducing single-person dependency. The risks are slower work—pricing duration rose during the substitution—and unclear delegation limits. Management should document which exception bands Giorgio may decide independently and which require Fatima or Hana.

### R13_Mateo: cross-functional escalation broker

Mateo’s role is encoded in sequence position and network centrality more than in frequency. He appears after reopens, between security review and implementation, and across commercial, technical, quality, and approval work. His high critical-case exposure reinforces that he is an escalation broker rather than an undifferentiated generalist.

His filtering of low-risk security referrals shows useful gatekeeping, while the two critical cases waiting over two days show the danger of a coordination bottleneck or imperfect triage. His preliminary approvals during Hana’s absence also reveal continuity capacity, but subsequent rechecks indicate constrained authority.

### R05_Erik: rare but critical specialist

Erik has relatively few total events, but a uniquely concentrated A6 profile and the highest median complexity. His organizational importance would be underestimated by methods that reward volume or broad similarity.

The role provides expert risk control and prevents generalists from making unsafe decisions. Its risks are scarce capacity, delayed critical cases, and possible overreliance on Mateo’s referral filter. Low event volume should not be interpreted as excess capacity without examining arrival rates, waiting times, and risk avoidance.

### R14_Nora: automation boundary role

Nora bridges customers and human resolution teams through registration and entitlement precheck. Treating the bot as a normal resource would distort role discovery and handover statistics. Management should model it separately while monitoring where automated validation is overridden and whether its routing affects rework or segment-level outcomes.

### Temporary workforce as a capacity bridge

Kai and Lina bridge seasonal demand rather than functional domains. They provide substantial routine capacity, but their low complexity and reopen pattern show that apparent activity equivalence does not establish full substitutability with Amira or Ben. Better note-quality controls and entitlement training could make this bridge safer.

### Quality gatekeeping bridge

Ivan and Julia connect implementation to closure and feed recurring findings into the knowledge base. Their occasional technical activity likely reflects verification rather than technical ownership. This role is valuable because it turns execution defects into organizational learning, but it can be weakened if quality staff are treated simply as spare implementers.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format the event log
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
raw = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution.csv")

raw["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(
    raw["time:timestamp"], utc=True, errors="raise"
)

# Preserve business attributes while telling PM4Py which columns carry
# case, activity, timestamp, and resource semantics.
log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

log = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])

# Keep automation visible for process analysis, but also create a human-only
# view so Nora's very high A1/A2 volume does not dominate human-role mining.
human_log = log[log["org:resource"] != "R14_Nora"].copy()

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Baseline organizational-role discovery
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# Exact signatures can vary somewhat by PM4Py release. In common versions,
# the dataframe must expose concept:name and org:resource.
roles_all = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource",
)

roles_human = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    human_log,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource",
)

print("Roles including automation:")
print(roles_all)
print("\nHuman-only candidate roles:")
print(roles_human)

# Treat these as candidate behavioral groupings, not formal titles or
# authorization groups.

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Activity-based resource similarity
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    human_log,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource",
)

print(similarity)

# Depending on the installed release, the result may be a matrix, dict,
# or graph-like structure. Convert it to an edge table as appropriate.
def similarity_to_edges(sim):
    if isinstance(sim, pd.DataFrame):
        if sim.index.equals(sim.columns):
            rows = []
            for i, r1 in enumerate(sim.index):
                for j, r2 in enumerate(sim.columns):
                    if j > i:
                        rows.append((r1, r2, float(sim.loc[r1, r2])))
            return pd.DataFrame(
                rows, columns=["resource_1", "resource_2", "similarity"]
            )
        return sim.copy()

    rows = []
    for key, value in sim.items():
        if isinstance(key, tuple) and len(key) == 2:
            rows.append((key[0], key[1], float(value)))
    return pd.DataFrame(
        rows, columns=["resource_1", "resource_2", "similarity"]
    )

similarity_edges = similarity_to_edges(similarity)

# High similarity is evidence of similar executed work, not equal authority.
high_similarity = similarity_edges.query("similarity >= 0.90")
print(high_similarity)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Handover-of-work and working-together networks
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    human_log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    human_log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)

print("Handover network:", handover)
print("Working-together network:", working_together)

# Optional: subcontracting can reveal A -> B -> A patterns, useful for
# identifying specialists temporarily inserted into another resource's case.
subcontracting = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    human_log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

print("Subcontracting network:", subcontracting)

# Inspect escalation-only collaboration rather than relying on the full log.
escalation_cases = log.loc[
    (log["escalation_level"].fillna(0) > 0)
    | log["concept:name"].isin([
        "A6 Security Review",
        "A7 Pricing Exception Review",
        "A9 Supervisor Approval",
        "A13 Reopen Request",
    ]),
    "case:concept:name",
].unique()

escalation_log = human_log[
    human_log["case:concept:name"].isin(escalation_cases)
].copy()

escalation_handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    escalation_log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

escalation_collaboration = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    escalation_log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Complexity and substitution profiles
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# Ensure an explicit Boolean/0-1 indicator.
human_log["temporary_substitution"] = (
    human_log["temporary_substitution"]
    .fillna(False)
    .astype(bool)
)

resource_context = (
    human_log.groupby("org:resource", as_index=False)
    .agg(
        event_count=("concept:name", "size"),
        cases=("case:concept:name", "nunique"),
        median_case_complexity=("case_complexity", "median"),
        mean_case_complexity=("case_complexity", "mean"),
        substitution_events=("temporary_substitution", "sum"),
        substitution_share=("temporary_substitution", "mean"),
        max_escalation=("escalation_level", "max"),
    )
)

complexity_mix = (
    human_log.assign(
        complexity_band=pd.cut(
            human_log["case_complexity"],
            bins=[-float("inf"), 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, float("inf")],
            labels=["low", "medium", "high", "critical"],
        )
    )
    .groupby(["org:resource", "complexity_band"], observed=True)
    ["case:concept:name"]
    .nunique()
    .unstack(fill_value=0)
    .reset_index()
)

resource_context = resource_context.merge(
    complexity_mix, on="org:resource", how="left"
)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Normalize role-discovery output into resource-role candidates
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# PM4Py releases may expose role objects differently. This adapter is
# illustrative and should be adjusted after inspecting `roles_human`.
role_rows = []

for role_id, role in enumerate(roles_human):
    if isinstance(role, dict):
        resources = (
            role.get("resources")
            or role.get("originators")
            or role.get("members")
            or []
        )
        activities = role.get("activities") or []
    elif isinstance(role, tuple) and len(role) >= 2:
        activities, resources = role[0], role[1]
    else:
        resources = getattr(role, "resources", [])
        activities = getattr(role, "activities", [])

    for resource in resources:
        role_rows.append({
            "org:resource": resource,
            "candidate_role_id": role_id,
            "discovered_activities": sorted(list(activities)),
        })

role_membership = pd.DataFrame(role_rows)

enriched_roles = role_membership.merge(
    resource_context, on="org:resource", how="left"
)

print(enriched_roles)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 7. Compare ordinary and substitution periods
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
activity_period_profile = (
    human_log.groupby(
        ["org:resource", "temporary_substitution", "concept:name"],
        as_index=False
    )
    .size()
    .rename(columns={"size": "events"})
)

# Giorgio's commercial work inside vs. outside substitution episodes.
giorgio_pricing = activity_period_profile[
    (activity_period_profile["org:resource"] == "R07_Giorgio")
    & activity_period_profile["concept:name"].isin([
        "A7 Pricing Exception Review",
        "A8 Customer Negotiation",
    ])
]

# Mateo's A9 execution must be examined alongside recheck/authority data.
mateo_approval = human_log[
    (human_log["org:resource"] == "R13_Mateo")
    & (human_log["concept:name"] == "A9 Supervisor Approval")
][[
    "case:concept:name",
    "time:timestamp",
    "temporary_substitution",
    "case_complexity",
    "escalation_level",
]]

print(giorgio_pricing)
print(mateo_approval)

# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 8. Complexity-adjusted activity profiles
# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# Compare resources within complexity bands so low-complexity volume does
# not make temps appear fully equivalent to permanent agents.
adjusted_profile = (
    human_log.assign(
        complexity_band=pd.cut(
            human_log["case_complexity"],
            bins=[-float("inf"), 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, float("inf")],
            labels=["low", "medium", "high", "critical"],
        )
    )
    .groupby(
        ["org:resource", "complexity_band", "concept:name"],
        observed=True
    )
    .size()
    .rename("events")
    .reset_index()
)

# Also inspect variants and service time for substitution effects.
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(log)

# Final interpretation should combine:
#   1) discovered activity roles,
#   2) complexity-adjusted profiles,
#   3) substitution vs. normal periods,
#   4) handover/collaboration centrality,
#   5) formal title, certification, and authorization data.
#
# Never translate a candidate cluster directly into an approval permission
# or permanent staffing substitution without governance validation.
```

Where a PM4Py version uses slightly different network parameter names or return structures, the same analytical sequence should be retained: discover candidate behavioral roles, inspect activity similarity, study case-level collaboration, and then enrich the result with complexity and substitution context.

# 6. Operational interpretation

**Staffing.**  
Management should staff routine intake and closure separately from scarce specialist capacity. Nora, the temps, and permanent front-office agents can absorb volume, but they are not interchangeable. Security demand should be planned using risk-weighted arrivals and waiting time rather than Erik’s event count alone. Commercial resilience should formally include Giorgio as bounded deputy capacity, while preserving Fatima’s ownership of higher-risk exceptions.

The technical team should be capacity-planned as one occupational family with at least two capability tiers. Chen’s critical and security-adjacent workload suggests that equal event counts with Dina do not imply equal staffing coverage. Quality should remain a distinct gate rather than being treated as spare technical capacity.

**Training.**  
Kai and Lina need targeted entitlement-note and closure training, with checklists or mandatory fields before closure. Amira may be a suitable coach for higher-complexity front-office work. Giorgio should receive explicit commercial delegation training and periodic calibration with Fatima. Mateo needs triage training and documented thresholds for security referral and preliminary approval. Cross-training should include simulations of Fatima, Hana, Erik, and Chen being unavailable.

**Risk control.**  
Final approval authority should be represented separately from behavioral role mining. Mateo may prepare or preliminarily execute approval steps, but Hana’s rechecks indicate that accountable sign-off remains different. Security triage needs service-level controls so that filtering reduces false escalations without delaying critical cases. Monitoring should include:

- critical-case waiting time by referral path;
- reopen rate by resolver and complexity;
- pricing duration and reversal rate during substitutions;
- approvals later rechecked or overturned;
- bot validation overrides;
- quality defects by implementation resource and product line.

**Resilience.**  
Shadow roles are valuable when made explicit and bounded. Giorgio offers commercial continuity; Mateo provides cross-functional coordination; Chen may provide security-adjacent technical depth. Management should maintain a capability matrix containing normal scope, emergency scope, required review, and maximum delegation threshold. This recognizes shadow work without incorrectly converting it into unrestricted authority.

Two important cautions are:

1. **Do not change headcount or replace specialists based only on mined event volume.** Rare security and approval interventions may carry disproportionate legal, financial, or operational risk. Low counts can also result from effective upstream filtering.
2. **Do not infer interchangeability from activity similarity.** Chen and Dina, permanent agents and temps, or Hana and Mateo differ in complexity exposure, competence, accountability, and authorization despite overlapping event profiles.

Further cautions are that substitution spikes should not be treated as proof of sustainable competence, and automation throughput should not be compared directly with human workload. Before implementing staffing changes, management should validate mined roles against job design, certification records, delegation policies, outcome quality, waiting time, and employee interviews.
