# Procedural Fairness Assessment of Public-Benefits Case-Management Process

## 1. Fairness dimensions

### Outcome parity
- **Raw approval rates differ substantially** (Group A: 78% vs. Group D: 58%), but these reflect legitimate case-mix differences (risk scores, documentation completeness, etc.).
- **Adjusted models show smaller but persistent disparities**: Group B (-5.5pp), Group C (-2.2pp), and Group D (-3.1pp) approval probabilities remain lower than Group A after controlling for documented complexity.
- **Interpretation**: Some outcome disparity persists even after accounting for measured case attributes, suggesting potential unfairness in decision-making or process routing.

### Adjusted outcome disparity
- The adjusted model reveals that even after controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, and other factors, certain groups face lower approval probabilities.
- This suggests that either:
  - Unmeasured case complexity exists (e.g., nuanced eligibility nuances not captured in data)
  - Decision-makers apply different standards across groups
  - Process routing introduces delays that indirectly affect outcomes (e.g., longer cycles  applicant disengagement)

### Procedural burden
- **Handovers and complexity**: Groups B and C experience more handovers (e.g., general  bilingual  general for Group B; general  accommodation specialist  supervisor for Group C).
- **Specialized queue delays**: Language support (Group B) and accommodation review (Group C) have median waits of 3.8d and 4.7d respectively, far exceeding other steps.
- **Documentation burden**: Group B faces higher "Request Missing Documents" probability (+9.5pp vs. Group A), likely due to language barriers in initial submissions.
- **Appeal/rework burden**: Group D has the highest appeal rate (16%) and reopen rate (20%), suggesting procedural complexity or stricter initial decisions.

### Timeliness
- **Median cycle time differences**: Group C (15.9d) and Group B (13.7d) face substantially longer median times than Group A (8.1d), even after adjustment.
- **90th percentile disparities**: Group C (38.8d) and Group B (34.2d) experience much longer tails of delay than Group A (21.4d).
- **Step-level delays**: Language support (Group B) and accommodation review (Group C) are key bottlenecks, with waits far exceeding service-level targets (if they exist).
- **Supervisor review delays**: Group B (+1.9d wait) and Group C (+1.6d wait) face longer waits for supervisor review than Group A.

### Consistency of routing
- **Variant distribution differences**:
  - Group A is more likely to follow the "standard" path (V1: 42%), while Group B is overrepresented in paths requiring language support (V4: 24%) or rework (V2: 34%).
  - Group D is disproportionately routed to fraud review (V3: 28%), reflecting higher risk_score_band but also creating longer cycle times (18.4d).
- **Handovers**:
  - Group B has more handovers between general and bilingual caseworkers, increasing process complexity.
  - Group C has more handovers involving accommodation specialists and supervisors.
- **Legitimate vs. concerning routing**: Some routing differences (e.g., fraud review for high-risk cases) are policy-compliant, but others (e.g., language support delays) may reflect resource constraints rather than applicant needs.

### Quality/accessibility of support
- **Language support**: Group B has 57% needing language support but only 7 bilingual caseworkers, creating delays and potential miscommunication.
- **Accommodation review**: Group C (62% requesting accommodation) relies on a small team (5 specialists), with 4.7d median waits.
- **Policy compliance**: The process includes rights-preserving steps (language support, accommodation review), but these are not held to separate service-level targets, creating inequities in timeliness.

### Appeal/rework burden
- **Appeal rates**: Group D (16%) and Group B (13%) have higher appeal rates than Group A (6%), even after adjustment.
- **Reopen rates**: Group C (24%) and Group B (22%) have high rework rates, suggesting initial decisions may be less robust or processes more error-prone for these groups.
- **Cycle time for appeals**: Variant V6 (appeal path) has a median cycle time of 30.7d, with Group D overrepresented (13% share), indicating appeals are slow and burdensome.

### Resource-allocation fairness
- **Caseworker distribution**:
  - Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers (low concentration).
  - Group B cases are concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers.
  - Group C cases are concentrated among 5 accommodation specialists (2 of whom also do supervisor review).
- **Workload imbalance**: The small teams handling specialized support (language, accommodation) are likely overwhelmed, leading to delays and reduced service quality.
- **Regional disparities**: Two regions show backlogs in supervisor review, affecting Groups B and C disproportionately.

## 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty, the following analysis strategy is proposed:

### Matching or stratification variables
- **Primary stratification**: applicant_group (A, B, C, D)
- **Case-mix controls**:
  - risk_score_band (high/medium/low)
  - documentation_completeness (complete/incomplete)
  - channel (online/in-person)
  - household_size_band
  - prior_case_count (high/low)
  - region
  - month (to account for seasonal effects)
- **Process complexity controls**:
  - variant_id (to compare similar process paths)
  - number of handovers
  - time spent in specialized steps (language support, accommodation review)

### Path/variant comparison
- **Stratified variant analysis**: Compare approval rates, cycle times, and appeal rates *within each variant* across applicant groups.
  - Example: Within V2 (path requiring "Request Missing Documents"), does Group B still have lower approval rates than Group A after controlling for documentation_completeness?
- **Path deviation analysis**: Identify where groups deviate from the "standard" path (V1) and whether these deviations correlate with worse outcomes.
- **Log-skeleton/Declarative checks**: Use `pm4py.discover_log_skeleton` or `pm4py.discover_declare` to identify whether certain groups are systematically routed through non-compliant paths (e.g., skipping rights-preserving steps).

### Resource-assignment comparison
- **Handover network analysis**: Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` to compare:
  - Number of handovers per case
  - Types of handovers (e.g., general  bilingual vs. general  supervisor)
  - Resource workload distribution (are certain caseworkers overloaded for specific groups?)
- **Activity-based resource similarity**: Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` to check if certain resources are systematically assigned to specific groups.
- **Organizational role analysis**: Use `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` to see if roles (e.g., bilingual caseworker) are concentrated in ways that disadvantage certain groups.

### Waiting-time decomposition
- **Service vs. waiting time**: Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to decompose cycle time into:
  - Active processing time (service time)
  - Waiting time (queues, handovers, resource unavailability)
- **Step-level waiting time**: Compare median waits at each step (e.g., language support contact  eligibility assessment) across groups, controlling for variant and resource availability.
- **Queue length analysis**: Use daily resource workload data (if available) to correlate waiting times with resource utilization.

### Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates
- **Risk_score_band bias**: Test whether risk_score_band itself encodes historical disparities by:
  - Comparing approval rates for high-risk cases across groups (are high-risk Group B cases treated differently than high-risk Group A cases?).
  - Using alternative risk measures (e.g., excluding historical case counts) to see if disparities persist.
- **Documentation_completeness bias**: Test whether documentation gaps are more likely to be attributed to certain groups due to language barriers or other unobserved factors.
- **Channel bias**: Compare online vs. in-person applicants *within groups* to isolate the effect of channel from group identity.

### Subgroup checks by region and channel
- **Regional disparities**: Compare adjusted disparities across regions to identify if certain regions systematically disadvantage specific groups.
- **Channel disparities**: Compare online vs. in-person applicants within each group to isolate the effect of channel from group identity.
- **Interaction effects**: Test whether the effect of applicant_group on outcomes varies by region or channel (e.g., is Group B’s disadvantage worse in regions with fewer bilingual caseworkers?).

## 4. Plausible mechanisms

### Legitimate mechanisms
1. **Risk-based routing**: Higher risk_score_band (Group D) triggers additional reviews (fraud review, supervisor review), legitimately increasing cycle time and reducing approval rates.
2. **Rights-preserving support**: Language support (Group B) and accommodation review (Group C) are policy-mandated but under-resourced, leading to delays. These are not unfair in intent but create procedural burden.
3. **Documentation complexity**: Group B’s higher "Request Missing Documents" rate may reflect genuine language barriers in initial submissions, requiring additional follow-up.
4. **Specialized team constraints**: Small teams for language support (7 caseworkers) and accommodation review (5 specialists) are overwhelmed by demand, leading to queues.

### Concerning mechanisms
1. **Resource-driven inequity**: The concentration of Group B cases among 7 bilingual caseworkers and Group C cases among 5 accommodation specialists creates bottlenecks that disadvantage these groups. This is not a case-mix issue but a resource-allocation issue.
2. **Unmeasured case complexity**: The adjusted model shows persistent approval disparities even after controlling for documented complexity. This could reflect:
   - Unmeasured nuances in eligibility (e.g., cultural context in documentation)
   - Decision-maker bias (e.g., unconscious stereotypes affecting risk assessments)
   - Process complexity (e.g., longer cycles  applicant disengagement  lower approval rates)
3. **Language barrier amplification**: Documentation_completeness may be a flawed measure for Group B, as language barriers could lead to incomplete submissions *even when applicants are eligible*. This could unfairly penalize Group B in initial reviews.
4. **Appeal process burden**: Group D’s high appeal rate and long appeal cycle times may reflect:
   - Stricter initial decisions (legitimate but harsh)
   - Process complexity that discourages applicants from appealing (unfair if initial decisions are wrong)
   - Lack of clear explanations for denials (reducing appeal success)

## 5. Limitations of available attributes

### Measurement bias
- **risk_score_band**: Likely encodes historical disparities (e.g., Group B may have been scored higher due to past systemic issues). Adjusting for risk_score_band may "control away" legitimate disparities.
- **documentation_completeness**: May be biased against Group B due to language barriers. A "complete" submission in Group A may not be equivalent to a "complete" submission in Group B.
- **case_outcome**: Does not capture quality of explanation, dignity of treatment, or applicant satisfaction. Two "approved" cases may differ greatly in the applicant’s experience.

### Missing applicant needs
- **Unmeasured complexity**: Nuances in eligibility (e.g., cultural context, nuanced disability needs) may not be captured in the data.
- **Applicant intent**: Some applicants may withdraw or abandon applications due to process burdens, but this is not captured in the data.
- **Quality of support**: The data does not measure whether language support or accommodation review was effective or respectful.

### Unobserved resource workload
- **Daily granularity**: Resource workload is observed only at daily levels, masking intra-day bottlenecks (e.g., queues forming at specific times).
- **Caseworker capacity**: The data does not capture caseworker workload beyond assignment counts (e.g., caseworker A may be handling 50 Group B cases while caseworker B handles 10 Group A cases).
- **Specialized team capacity**: The small teams for language support and accommodation review are likely overwhelmed, but the data does not quantify their true capacity or burnout.

### Appeal quality
- **Appeal outcomes**: The data does not include appeal outcomes (e.g., were appeals successful? Were explanations provided?). This limits interpretation of appeal rates.
- **Applicant experience**: The data does not capture applicant frustration, dignity violations, or trust in the process.

### Historical bias
- **risk_score_band**: May reflect past systemic discrimination (e.g., Group B historically scored higher due to language barriers or other factors).
- **prior_case_count**: May encode historical disparities (e.g., Group D’s higher prior-case count may reflect past systemic issues rather than current needs).

## 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation option | Description | Potential side effects/trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add capacity for language support** | Hire more bilingual caseworkers or use translation tools to reduce language support wait times. | Cost; risk of over-reliance on translation tools (may miss cultural nuances). |
| **Add capacity for accommodation review** | Hire more accommodation specialists or train general caseworkers to handle simpler accommodations. | Cost; risk of diluting expertise in specialized accommodation review. |
| **Service-level targets for support steps** | Set separate SLAs for language support (e.g., 2d wait) and accommodation review (e.g., 3d wait). | May require reallocating resources from other steps, creating new bottlenecks. |
| **Routing redesign to reduce handovers** | Assign Group B cases to dedicated bilingual caseworkers from intake to reduce handovers. | May reduce flexibility in resource allocation; could increase wait times for other groups if resources are diverted. |
| **Decision-explanation improvements** | Provide clear, jargon-free explanations for denials and required next steps. | Cost of training and documentation; may increase workload for caseworkers. |
| **Monitor adjusted disparities** | Track approval rates, cycle times, and appeal rates *after adjusting for case mix* on an ongoing basis. | Requires maintaining a robust adjustment model; may miss unmeasured disparities. |
| **Audit risk_score_band** | Review risk_score_band for bias (e.g., does it penalize Group B disproportionately?). Consider alternative risk measures. | May reduce the predictive power of risk_score_band for fraud detection. |
| **Audit documentation_completeness** | Review whether documentation gaps for Group B reflect genuine incompleteness or language barriers. Consider alternative measures (e.g., applicant self-report). | May require policy changes to accept alternative documentation. |
| **Regional resource reallocation** | Redistribute supervisor review capacity to regions with backlogs (affecting Groups B and C). | May face resistance from regional offices; could disrupt local processes. |
| **Appeal process simplification** | Reduce steps in the appeal process or provide free legal aid for appeals. | Cost of legal aid; may increase appeal rates if applicants see appeals as more viable. |

## 7. PM4Py analysis plan

Below is pseudocode for using PM4Py to analyze procedural fairness. This assumes the event log is loaded as a Pandas DataFrame (`df`).

```python
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.log.util import dataframe_utils
from pm4py.objects.conversion.log import converter as log_converter

# 1. Preprocess the log
# Convert to PM4Py event log format
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df,
                           case_id='case:concept:name',
                           activity_key='concept:name',
                           timestamp_key='time:timestamp')
log = log_converter.apply(df)

# 2. Variant analysis
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
print("Top variants by group:")
for group in ['Group A', 'Group B', 'Group C', 'Group D']:
    filtered_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', [group])
    variant_counts = pm4py.get_variants(filtered_log)
    print(f"\n{group} top variants:")
    for variant in sorted(variant_counts.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)[:5]:
        print(f"  {variant[0]}: {variant[1]} cases")

# 3. Performance DFG (cycle time by variant)
perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
pm4py.view_performance_dfg(perf_dfg)

# 4. Handover network analysis
handover_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
pm4py.view_handover_of_work_network(handover_net)

# Compare handovers across groups
for group in ['Group A', 'Group B', 'Group C', 'Group D']:
    filtered_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', [group])
    handover_net_group = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(filtered_log)
    print(f"\nHandover network for {group}:")
    print(handover_net_group)

# 5. Waiting-time decomposition
# Insert service and waiting times
log_with_times = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
service_times = pm4py.get_service_time(log_with_times)
waiting_times = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(log_with_times) - service_times

# Compare waiting times by group and step
for group in ['Group A', 'Group B', 'Group C', 'Group D']:
    filtered_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', [group])
    waiting_times_group = pm4py.filter_case_performance(filtered_log, waiting_times)
    print(f"\nWaiting times for {group} (median): {np.median(waiting_times_group)} days")

# Step-level waiting time analysis
# Example: Language support wait time for Group B
language_support_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', ['Group B'])
language_support_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(language_support_log, 'concept:name', ['Language Support Contact'])

waiting_times_language = [waiting_times[case] for case in language_support_cases if case in waiting_times]
print(f"\nGroup B language support wait time (median): {np.median(waiting_times_language)} days")

# 6. Declarative/log-skeleton checks for procedural constraints
# Example: Check if all cases requiring language support go through the language support step
from pm4py.algo.discovery.log_skeleton import algorithm as log_skeleton_discovery
skeleton = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log)
print("Log skeleton constraints:")
for constraint in skeleton['constraints']:
    print(f"  {constraint}")

# Check for violations (e.g., cases needing language support but not going through the step)
language_needed_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'language_preference', ['non-majority'])
language_needed_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(language_needed_cases, 'concept:name', ['Submit Application'])
cases_without_language_support = [case for case in language_needed_cases if not any(event['concept:name'] == 'Language Support Contact' for event in case)]
print(f"\nCases needing language support but not receiving it: {len(cases_without_language_support)}")

# 7. Resource assignment analysis
# Activity-based resource similarity
resource_sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
print("Resource similarity by activity:")
for activity, resources in resource_sim.items():
    print(f"  {activity}: {resources}")

# Organizational roles
org_roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
print("Organizational roles:")
for role, resources in org_roles.items():
    print(f"  {role}: {resources}")

# 8. Adjusted comparison: Stratified analysis by variant
# Example: Approval rate by group within variant V2
v2_log = pm4py.filter_variants(log, ['Submit Application', 'Identity Verification', 'Completeness Check', 'Request Missing Documents', 'Eligibility Assessment', 'Decision Draft', 'Applicant Notification'])
for group in ['Group A', 'Group B', 'Group C', 'Group D']:
    filtered_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(v2_log, 'applicant_group', [group])
    approval_rate = sum(1 for trace in filtered_log if trace[-1]['concept:name'] == 'Benefit Activation') / len(filtered_log)
    print(f"\nApproval rate in V2 for {group}: {approval_rate:.2%}")
```

## 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process exhibits **systemic procedural unfairness** for Groups B, C, and D, even after controlling for documented case complexity. While some disparities are justified by legitimate routing (e.g., fraud review for high-risk cases, accommodation review for disability requests), others reflect **resource constraints and policy gaps** that disproportionately burden these groups. Key concerns include:

1. **Rights-preserving steps without service-level targets**: Language support and accommodation review are critical for equity but lack timeliness guarantees, creating delays that are not justified by case complexity.
2. **Resource concentration**: Small teams for specialized support (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists) are overwhelmed, leading to bottlenecks that disadvantage Groups B and C.
3. **Persistent adjusted disparities**: Even after controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, and other factors, Groups B, C, and D face lower approval probabilities, longer cycle times, and higher appeal rates. This suggests either unmeasured case complexity or unfair decision-making/routing.
4. **Handovers and process complexity**: Groups B and C experience more handovers and complex routing, increasing the risk of error and applicant burden.

The evidence does not support a conclusion of intentional discrimination, but it does indicate **structural inequities** in process design and resource allocation. The process is **not procedurally fair** for Groups B, C, and D, as these groups face greater procedural burden, longer timeliness, and more complex routing—even when their cases are similar in documented complexity to Group A cases.

**Recommendation**: Prioritize mitigations that address resource constraints (e.g., hiring more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists) and policy gaps (e.g., service-level targets for rights-preserving steps). Monitor adjusted disparities closely to ensure improvements are realized. Avoid simplistic solutions that remove rights-preserving steps, as these are essential for equity.
