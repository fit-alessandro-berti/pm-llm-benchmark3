## 1. Interaction-graph description

The appropriate representation is a **typed temporal multigraph with event hyperedges**. Object nodes are Jobs, Components, ComponentLots, Machines, Tools, Batches, and Shipments. An event such as `Batch Cure` at 12:05 is one hyperedge connecting B1, OVEN-1, RACK-7, three Jobs, and three Components; reducing it to independent pairs loses the synchronization semantics.

**Direct observations** are the supplied O2O relations—`requires`, `contains`, `batches`, `consolidates`—and recorded E2O event relations. **Inferred relations** include `competes_for`, `waits_for`, `synchronized_with`, and `delay-propagates-to`. They require counterfactual assumptions and must not be treated as logged facts.

Important higher-order motifs are:

1. **Batch-resource join:** J1/J2/J3–C1/C2/C3–B1–OVEN-1–RACK-7. The latest-ready member or any missing resource can gate one shared cure.
2. **Tool-machine contention:** J3–FIX-A–CNC-2–J4–C4. A tool held by one job can leave the machine needed by another job idle.
3. **Lot-policy split:** L7–C2/C5–J2/J5–B1/B2. One lot state couples jobs in different batches, while J2’s override makes the policy effect asymmetric.
4. **Test-maintenance-consolidation:** J5/J6–TEST-1–CAL-3–S3. Calibration can determine the last-ready shipment member.

```text
[D-O2O] Jk -requires-> Ck
[D-O2O] L7 -contains-> {C2,C5}; L8 -contains-> {C1,C3}
[D-O2O] B1 -batches-> {J1,J2,J3}; B2 -batches-> {J4,J5}
[D-O2O] S1 -consolidates-> {J1,J4}; S2->{J2,J3}; S3->{J5,J6}

[D-E2O] BatchCure@12:05 ->
          {B1,OVEN-1,RACK-7,J1,J2,J3,C1,C2,C3}
[D-E2O] MachineProcess@11:45 -> {J4,C4,CNC-2,FIX-A}
[D-E2O] Quarantine/ReleaseLot -> {L7,C2,C5,J2,J5}

[I] {J1,J2} -synchronized_with-> J3 through B1
[I] J4 -possibly_waits_for-> FIX-A
[I] J1 -possibly_waits_for-> J4 through S1
```

The inferred graph is therefore not merely a resource-sharing graph. It contains **joins, policy gates, maintenance states, and downstream partner dependencies**.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

Each calculation is conditional on its stated counterfactual and is not causal proof.

1. **J1 in B1:**
   (12{:}05-08{:}10=\mathbf{235}) minutes, or **3 h 55 min**, ready-to-start waiting. Under fixed B1 membership, 08:10–11:55 is **225 minutes waiting for the latest member**, followed by 10 minutes from J3 readiness to batch start. Under the stronger counterfactual that J1 could be cured individually at 08:10 without extra setup or capacity costs, all 235 minutes are potentially avoidable.

2. **J2 in B1:**
   (12{:}05-08{:}25=\mathbf{220}) minutes, or **3 h 40 min**. This decomposes into **210 minutes of partner synchronization** and the same 10-minute launch interval, assuming readiness timestamps represent true operational readiness.

3. **B1 synchronization burden:**
   Relative to the latest-ready member, the three jobs incur (225+210+0=\mathbf{435}) job-minutes, averaging **145 minutes per member**. This calculation deliberately excludes batching’s possible setup, energy, or quality benefits.

4. **J4 and FIX-A:**
   J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and starts at 11:45, so observed ready-to-start time is **115 minutes**. FIX-A remains unavailable until 11:40, creating **110 minutes of temporal exposure** to fixture unavailability. Only 10:05–10:47, or **42 minutes**, is explicitly confirmed as simultaneous CNC idleness and FIX-A unavailability. Under a duplicate-fixture counterfactual, the candidate tool effect lies somewhere within this exposure, but could be smaller—including zero—if another hidden prerequisite also bound J4.

5. **B2 and RACK-7:**
   J4 waits 13:10–14:50 = **100 minutes**. The latest known prerequisite is
   (\max(14{:}40\text{ J5 ready},14{:}35\text{ rack available})=14{:}40).
   Thus only **10 minutes** remain after all known prerequisites are available. The 115-minute OVEN-1 idle interval therefore does not establish an oven-capacity problem.

6. **J1 and S1:**
   J1 is packed at 13:30 and S1 dispatches at 19:25: **355 minutes**. S1 departs 15 minutes after J4 is packed. If J1 could have used a separate shipment with the same 15-minute handling interval, it would depart at 13:45. The candidate consolidation penalty is therefore (355-15=\mathbf{340}) minutes.

7. **J6 and CAL-3:**
   J6 is ready at 16:05 and starts at 17:25: **80 minutes**. Keeping J5’s test first but removing recalibration gives an earliest J6 start of 16:35. The candidate CAL-3-associated delay is therefore **50 minutes**: 45 minutes of recalibration plus five minutes of handoff.

8. **L7 quarantine:**
   10:20–13:50 = **210 minutes**. This is an upper bound for J5 only if J5 was otherwise ready at 10:20. J2 enters B1 at 12:05, **105 minutes before release**, demonstrating that the restriction is policy-contingent rather than a physical impossibility.

## 4. What flattening misses

| Viewpoint    | Makes visible                                                | Hides or duplicates                                                                                                         | Misleading conclusion                                                             |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Job          | Per-job order, rework, and apparent transition durations     | Shared events may be copied into several jobs; L7 events disappear under direct-only extraction; waiting reasons are absent | Normal medians and no statistical outlier imply independent, healthy cases        |
| Machine      | Utilization, activity order, and explicit idle gaps          | Ready queues, tools, lot status, batch membership, and shipment effects                                                     | Low utilization means waiting jobs could immediately use the machine              |
| Batch        | Load/cure/unload and batch duration                          | Dispersion in member readiness and early-member waiting                                                                     | B1 is efficient because it starts only 10 minutes after its final member is ready |
| Tool         | Holds, cleaning, quarantine, recalibration, and repeated use | Whether a job was otherwise feasible and whether a hold was necessary                                                       | Moderate average tool utilization rules out tool bottlenecks                      |
| ComponentLot | Quarantine/release and component membership                  | Separate-system overrides and inconsistently propagated Job links                                                           | J2 and J5 were affected equally by L7                                             |
| Shipment     | Create/load/dispatch execution                               | Pack-to-dispatch waiting of each member and causes of late partners                                                         | Load→Dispatch around 0.3 h means shipping creates little delay                    |

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because utilization measures **executed work**, not feasible demand. A job can be nominally ready but unable to start without FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3, batch partners, lot permission, or a qualified operator. The job waits off-machine while the machine is starved or blocked. Conversely, forcing higher utilization could worsen delays by processing low-priority work that consumes a tool needed by a more critical connected job.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

**Narrative A — two consecutive joins for J1.**
**Temporal precedence:** J1/J2 are ready at 08:10/08:25, J3 at 11:55, B1 cures at 12:05, J1 packs at 13:30, J4 packs at 19:10, and S1 dispatches at 19:25.
**Resource feasibility:** B1 jointly needs OVEN-1, RACK-7, three Jobs, and three Components.
**Policy constraints:** fixed B1 membership and S1 consolidation create separate joins.
**Causal speculation:** J3 plausibly gates J1 at the batch and J4 plausibly gates J1 at shipment, but planned oven slots or carrier cutoffs could explain part of either interval.

**Narrative B — possible FIX-A cascade through J4.**
**Temporal precedence:** J3 holds FIX-A from 09:00–11:40; J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and begins with C4, CNC-2, and FIX-A at 11:45; J4 subsequently belongs to B2 and is packed at 19:10; S1 leaves at 19:25.
**Resource feasibility:** J4 needs both CNC-2 and FIX-A; B2 then needs OVEN-1, RACK-7, and J5.
**Policy constraints:** S1 requires both J1 and J4.
**Causal speculation:** FIX-A may contribute to a cascade ending in J1’s shipment wait, but missing intermediate durations prevent assigning the full 340 minutes to the fixture.

**Narrative C — L7 through batching, testing, and S3.**
**Temporal precedence:** L7 is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50; J5 is B2-ready at 14:40; B2 starts at 14:50; J5 tests at 16:10; J6 then encounters CAL-3 recalibration before its 17:25 test.
**Resource feasibility:** C5 must be usable, B2 needs rack and oven, and testing requires TEST-1 plus CAL-3.
**Policy constraints:** lot release, calibration, and S3 consolidation are distinct gates.
**Causal speculation:** L7 may shift J5 into the later tool and shipment interaction window, but the 50-minute release-to-readiness gap and absent S3 packing times prevent proof.

Evaluation must include untreated neighbors in the connected object graph. Splitting B1 can consume an additional oven and rack slot; freeing TEST-1 can move congestion to packing; splitting S1 can overload the dock. These spillovers are why purchasing another primary machine is not the first intervention justified by the evidence.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline

PM4Py’s current top-level API treats the selected object type as the case during flattening, produces ordered object-pair rows in the interaction summary, and supports OC-DFG comparison by event couples, unique objects, and total object occurrences. OTG and ET-OT preserve complementary type-level structure. ([GitHub][1])

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Assumption: `ocel` is a valid PM4Py OCEL instance.
EID = ocel.event_id_column
ACT = ocel.event_activity
TS = ocel.event_timestamp
OID = ocel.object_id_column
OT = ocel.object_type_column

rel = ocel.relations.copy()
rel[TS] = pd.to_datetime(rel[TS], utc=True)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Interaction evidence and typed graphs
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
interactions = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

# The interaction summary contains ordered pairs, so A->B and B->A
# are both present for a co-occurring object pair.
interaction_type_counts = (
    interactions.groupby([OT, f"{OT}_2"], dropna=False)
    .size()
    .rename("ordered_object_pair_occurrences")
    .reset_index()
)

object_interaction_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(
    ocel, graph_type="object_interaction"
)

otg_object_types, otg_weights = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)

(
    etot_activities,
    etot_object_types,
    etot_edges,
    etot_weights,
) = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. OC-DFG and comparison of different frequency semantics
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(
    ocel,
    compute_edges_performance=True,
    business_hours=False,
)

edge_rows = []
for metric in ("event_couples", "unique_objects", "total_objects"):
    for object_type, edge_map in ocdfg["edges"][metric].items():
        for (source, target), evidence_set in edge_map.items():
            edge_rows.append(
                {
                    "object_type": object_type,
                    "source": source,
                    "target": target,
                    "metric": metric,
                    "frequency": len(evidence_set),
                }
            )

edge_frequency_comparison = (
    pd.DataFrame(edge_rows)
    .pivot_table(
        index=["object_type", "source", "target"],
        columns="metric",
        values="frequency",
        aggfunc="sum",
        fill_value=0,
    )
    .reset_index()
)

# event_couples counts transitions between events; unique_objects counts
# the distinct objects traversing the edge. Their divergence identifies
# repeated transitions or multiplicity that a single frequency hides.
edge_frequency_comparison["event_couples_per_unique_object"] = (
    edge_frequency_comparison["event_couples"]
    / edge_frequency_comparison["unique_objects"].replace(0, pd.NA)
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Compare traditional views without treating any one as ground truth
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
available_types = set(pm4py.ocel_get_object_types(ocel))
flattened_views = {}
performance_dfgs = {}

for object_type in [
    "Job",
    "Machine",
    "Batch",
    "Tool",
    "ComponentLot",
    "Shipment",
]:
    if object_type not in available_types:
        continue

    flat = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, object_type)
    flat = flat.sort_values(
        ["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"],
        kind="stable",
    )

    performance_dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
        flat,
        business_hours=False,
    )

    flattened_views[object_type] = flat
    performance_dfgs[object_type] = {
        "dfg": performance_dfg,
        "start_activities": starts,
        "end_activities": ends,
    }

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Object-level features for comparison and modelling
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
job_features = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(
    ocel,
    obj_type="Job",
    include_obj_id=True,
    enable_object_lifecycle_paths=True,
    enable_object_work_in_progress=True,
)

# Optional heterogeneous graph for connected-component and centrality work.
nx_graph = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(
    ocel,
    variant="ocel_to_nx",
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Audit inconsistent ComponentLot-to-Job relations
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
lot_activities = {"Quarantine Lot", "Release Lot"}

lot_event_type_sets = (
    rel[rel[ACT].isin(lot_activities)]
    .groupby(EID)[OT]
    .agg(set)
    .rename("related_object_types")
    .reset_index()
)

lot_event_type_sets["has_component_lot"] = lot_event_type_sets[
    "related_object_types"
].map(lambda types: "ComponentLot" in types)

lot_event_type_sets["has_job"] = lot_event_type_sets[
    "related_object_types"
].map(lambda types: "Job" in types)

lot_event_type_sets["job_link_missing"] = (
    lot_event_type_sets["has_component_lot"]
    & ~lot_event_type_sets["has_job"]
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Candidate waiting intervals from explicit semantic milestones
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# A machine-event gap alone is never classified as job waiting.
# A candidate interval requires:
#   (a) an explicit readiness/request milestone;
#   (b) a later start event;
#   (c) relations to the relevant job and required shared objects.
#
# Adapt the readiness activity labels to the vocabulary in the OCEL.

def common_event_time(
    activity: str,
    required_objects: list[str],
) -> pd.Timestamp:
    """Earliest event time having the activity and all required objects."""
    subset = rel[rel[ACT].eq(activity)]
    if subset.empty:
        return pd.NaT

    common_ids = None
    for object_id in required_objects:
        object_event_ids = set(
            subset.loc[subset[OID].eq(object_id), EID]
        )
        common_ids = (
            object_event_ids
            if common_ids is None
            else common_ids.intersection(object_event_ids)
        )

    if not common_ids:
        return pd.NaT

    return subset.loc[subset[EID].isin(common_ids), TS].min()


wait_specs = [
    (
        "J1",
        "batch_sync",
        "Ready for Batch",
        ["J1"],
        "Batch Cure",
        ["J1", "B1", "OVEN-1", "RACK-7"],
    ),
    (
        "J2",
        "batch_sync",
        "Ready for Batch",
        ["J2"],
        "Batch Cure",
        ["J2", "B1", "OVEN-1", "RACK-7"],
    ),
    (
        "J4",
        "fixture_exposure",
        "Reach Machine",
        ["J4", "CNC-2"],
        "Machine Process",
        ["J4", "CNC-2", "FIX-A"],
    ),
    (
        "J6",
        "test_queue",
        "Ready for Final Test",
        ["J6"],
        "Final Test",
        ["J6", "TEST-1", "CAL-3"],
    ),
    (
        "J1",
        "shipment_hold",
        "Pack",
        ["J1"],
        "Dispatch",
        ["S1"],
    ),
]

wait_rows = []
for (
    job,
    mechanism,
    ready_activity,
    ready_objects,
    start_activity,
    start_objects,
) in wait_specs:
    ready_at = common_event_time(ready_activity, ready_objects)
    start_at = common_event_time(start_activity, start_objects)

    if (
        pd.notna(ready_at)
        and pd.notna(start_at)
        and start_at >= ready_at
    ):
        wait_rows.append(
            {
                "job": job,
                "mechanism": mechanism,
                "ready_at": ready_at,
                "start_at": start_at,
                "elapsed_minutes": (
                    start_at - ready_at
                ).total_seconds() / 60.0,
                "causal_status": (
                    "candidate interval; requires counterfactual"
                ),
            }
        )

candidate_waits = pd.DataFrame(wait_rows)

print(
    interaction_type_counts.sort_values(
        "ordered_object_pair_occurrences",
        ascending=False,
    )
)
print(
    edge_frequency_comparison.sort_values(
        "event_couples",
        ascending=False,
    ).head(30)
)
print(candidate_waits)
print(lot_event_type_sets)
```

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken batch synchronization as the top explanation include:

1. The “ready” timestamps precede true material, operator, or quality readiness by hours.
2. OVEN-1, RACK-7, or a qualified operator was independently unavailable until approximately 12:05.
3. Batch starts follow fixed production windows and do not advance when the final member becomes ready earlier.
4. Removing J3 from B1 would not have advanced the start.
5. Across historical batches, readiness dispersion is unrelated to early-member waiting after controlling for product and shift.
6. Split-batch pilots do not reduce end-to-end job or shipment time after accounting for extra setups.
7. Comparable non-batched jobs exhibit the same waiting pattern.
8. Clock skew or retrospective entry materially changes the ordering of readiness and start events.

Additional discriminating fields or event types should include:

1. Explicit `Job Ready`, including readiness criterion and source system.
2. `Resource Requested`, queue entry, reservation, acquire, release, and resource location.
3. Reason-coded machine states: processing, setup, starved, blocked, and maintenance.
4. Batch creation, composition decision, minimum size, planned start, hold reason, and split decision.
5. Tool cleaning, quarantine, and calibration start/end, including planned versus unplanned status.
6. Lot quarantine reason, component-level disposition, downstream propagation, and override approval with risk basis.
7. Shipment-member ready time, carrier cutoff, assigned slot, consolidation rule, and split decision.
8. Operator shift and skill availability, setup cost, energy, quantity, quality outcome, and rework.

These additions would distinguish a true synchronization barrier from scheduled release, hidden human-resource scarcity, quality policy, tooling constraints, and extraction artifacts.
