## Section 1: What is observed

The panel contains five branches observed over eight months (January–August 2026). Three branches (Alpha, Beta, Gamma) transitioned from `automation=0` to `automation=1` during the observation window, while Delta and Epsilon remained untreated throughout. Several key distinctions must be drawn before any causal reasoning begins.

**Availability vs. actual use.** The binary `automation` flag records system availability, not utilization. In treated branches, the `automated_fast_path_pct` starts at 4% (a baseline level) and rises to 25–50% over subsequent months, reflecting gradual adoption. Even in untreated months, the 4% floor appears universal, suggesting it measures some residual or default categorization rather than zero automation.

**Endogenous branch selection.** Alpha and Beta were chosen explicitly because they had high volumes and historically poor cycle times—a classic case of targeting interventions to the worst-performing units. Gamma followed after a readiness pilot. This selection implies that adopters differ systematically from non-adopters on both observed and unobserved dimensions.

**Contemporaneous operational changes.** A centralized fraud desk launched in March for all branches. A routing campaign redirected approximately 10% more incoming volume to Alpha and Beta starting April, and 5% more to Gamma starting July. Staffing changed in four of the five branches during the window. A May product change increased the proportion of high-complexity cases across branches. These changes are entangled with the automation rollout.

**Outcome measurement limitations.** CSAT is a monthly survey mean with 20–45% response rates and no branch-month response counts, making it vulnerable to selection bias and low precision. Median and p90 cycle times aggregate all cases within a branch-month, obscuring within-cell heterogeneity and variant-specific effects. Rework and escalation definitions appear stable, but the fraud desk introduction may have partially reclassified what counts as "specialist review."

**Primary strategy explanation.**

The staggered DiD is preferable because it uses both cross-sectional and temporal variation: the never-treated branches (Delta, Epsilon) provide a counterfactual trend against which to measure the adopters' post-adoption departures. With month fixed effects, the design absorbs any common temporal shocks (the May product change, the March fraud desk, seasonal patterns), leaving the identification to rest on whether adopters and non-adopters were on parallel trajectories before treatment.

The event-study specification (relative time indicators) makes pre-trend testing transparent: if the pre-adoption coefficients are jointly zero, the parallel-trends assumption is more credible. This is critical given that Alpha and Beta were selected for poor performance—pre-trends test whether this selection generated diverging or converging trajectories.

However, the design has severe limitations. With only five branches and three treated, cluster-robust inference is unreliable. The routing campaign violates SUTVA by reallocating cases between treated and untreated branches, and the month fixed effects cannot absorb branch-specific time-varying confounders (Alpha's FTE loss, Beta's FTE gain). The synthetic control method would allow more flexible counterfactual construction but lacks sufficient donor branches. The ITS approach is transparent per-branch but cannot separate automation from the many concurrent changes. In sum, the staggered DiD is the least-bad option, not a definitive one, and every estimate should be interpreted as a bounding exercise rather than a point estimate.

## Section 8: Rollout recommendation

**Recommendation: expand with an embedded evaluation.**

I recommend expanding automation to Delta and Epsilon, but with an embedded evaluation design that generates both operational benefit and causal evidence. The specific plan:

**Branch sequencing.** Roll out to Delta first (month 1, targeting September 2026), then to Epsilon three months later (targeting December 2026). Delta is the better initial candidate because it has lower baseline complexity (18–23% vs. Epsilon's 30–38%), a recent staffing increase (+1 FTE in June), and lower current cycle times—making it a cleaner environment in which to observe automation effects without the confounding of high-complexity workloads. Epsilon serves as a concurrent control during Delta's initial rollout, and then receives the intervention itself, enabling a clean before-after/control-impact comparison.

**Treatment assignment mechanism.** Within Delta, implement a week-level randomized phase-in: during the first four weeks, automate triage on alternating weeks (e.g., weeks 1 and 3 automated, weeks 2 and 4 manual). This within-branch randomization provides a local randomized estimate of the automation effect, uncontaminated by branch-level confounders. After week 4, switch to full automation. ## 1. What is observed

The panel covers five branches over eight months (January–August 2026). Three branches—Alpha, Beta, and Gamma—switched from `automation=0` to `automation=1` at known dates; Delta and Epsilon remained at `automation=0` throughout. Several distinctions are essential before reasoning causally.

**Availability vs. actual use.** The binary `automation` flag records that the system was live at a branch, not that every case was triaged by it. Actual utilization, captured by `automated_fast_path_pct`, starts at a baseline of roughly 4% everywhere and rises only gradually—to 25–50% in treated branches over months. The intervention is therefore better described as a ramp than a switch.

**Endogenous branch selection.** Alpha and Beta were selected explicitly for high volume and historically poor cycle times; Gamma followed after a readiness pilot. This means adopters differ systematically from non-adopters on baseline performance, volume, and managerial attention—precluding naïve between-branch comparisons.

**Contemporaneous operational changes.** A centralized fraud desk launched organization-wide in March, and automated triage routed a somewhat larger fraction of borderline cases to it. A routing campaign redirected 10% more incoming volume to Alpha/Beta from April and 5% more to Gamma from July. Staffing changed in four of five branches during the window. A May product change raised high-complexity caseloads by 5–8 percentage points everywhere. Each of these confounders moved outcomes in the same period as automation.

**Outcome measurement limitations.** CSAT is a monthly survey mean with response rates varying between 20% and 45%; branch-month response counts are unavailable, making the measure vulnerable to differential nonresponse. Median and p90 cycle times are branch-month aggregates that mask within-cell heterogeneity across case types, specialists, and days.

**Why the primary strategy is preferable.**

The staggered event-study DiD is the most defensible option because it simultaneously exploits cross-sectional and temporal variation: never-treated branches provide a counterfactual trend, and the relative-time specification makes pre-trend testing transparent. With month fixed effects, common shocks (the March fraud desk, the May product change, any seasonal patterns) are differenced out. The event-study coefficients at t = 3 and t = 2 directly test the parallel-trends assumption—if they are near zero, we have greater confidence in the post-treatment estimates.

However, this strategy has severe limitations with only five branches. The cluster-robust standard errors are unreliable with fewer than 50 clusters, and conventional inference may be either too liberal or too conservative. The routing campaign creates a SUTVA violation that no fixed-effects structure can address: adopters' outcomes are affected by the cases redirected to them, and non-adopters' outcomes are affected by the cases redirected away. Furthermore, Alpha's FTE loss, Beta's FTE gain, and Gamma's FTE loss are branch-specific time-varying confounders that the month fixed effects cannot absorb. In sum, the staggered DiD is the least-bad framework for these data, not a definitive one, and all estimates should be read as descriptive bounding exercises, not causal point estimates.

Pre-trends are flat or weakly sloping in all branches. The level gap is a persistent structural difference (volume, complexity), not a transient overshoot.

**Regression to the mean around the May–June complexity spike.** All branches experienced a spike in cycle times in May–June (product change) followed by partial recovery in July–August. Gamma's recovery was most complete: median returned to near its pre-spike baseline despite complexity staying elevated at 27%. By contrast, Delta and Epsilon recovered partially but remained above their pre-spike baselines. The question is whether Gamma's more complete recovery reflects automation (absorbing the complexity shock) or simply faster mean-reversion in a smaller, lower-volume branch. This cannot be resolved from branch-month aggregates alone.

### 5.5 Sensitivity analysis

**Sensitivity to control group.** The median DiD ranges from 2.6 h (Beta vs. Epsilon) to 6.4 h (Alpha vs. Delta). Using the average of both controls: Alpha  5.6 h, Beta  3.4 h, Gamma  3.8 h. The range is driven by Epsilon's own substantial cycle-time increase (which reduces the contrast).

**Sensitivity to pre-period window for Gamma.** Using Jan–Mar as the pre-period (pre-fraud-desk): DiD against Delta  5.8 h. Using Jan–Jun (including product-change months): DiD against Delta  9.4 h. Using Jan–Apr: DiD against Delta  4.6 h. The range for the Gamma median DiD is therefore approximately **3 to 9 hours**, reflecting sensitivity to how the product-change confounder is handled.

**Bounding the median cycle-time effect.** The most conservative DiD across all adopter-control pairs is 2.6 h (Beta vs. Epsilon). The routing campaign added roughly 10% more volume to Alpha/Beta—a workload increase that, in a queueing system, would typically increase cycle time. If the routing effect alone would have added +5 h to Alpha's median, then the automation effect may be as large as the observed DiD *plus* the offsetting routing penalty, potentially 8 to 12 h. Allowing for the possibility that all observed differences are driven by confounders (lower bound: 0 h), the **plausible range for the median cycle-time reduction attributable to automation is approximately 0 to 9 hours** (0% to 20% of baseline). For p90, the range is wider: **0 to 10 hours**, with the additional caveat that p90 worsened in absolute terms in 2 of 3 adopters.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**Recommendation: `expand with an embedded evaluation`.**

Leadership should expand automation to Delta and Epsilon, but structured as a controlled learning exercise rather than a blanket deployment. The specific plan follows.

**Branch sequencing.** Roll out to Delta first (target: September 2026), then to Epsilon three months later (target: December 2026). Delta is the better initial candidate for three reasons: (1) it has lower baseline complexity (18–23% vs. Epsilon's 30–38%), providing a cleaner environment to observe automation effects without the high-complexity confound; (2) it recently gained an FTE (June), reducing the staffing-pressure confounder that plagued Alpha; and (3) its moderate volume (690–770 arrivals/month) is more interpretable than Epsilon's larger and more volatile demand. Epsilon serves as a concurrent control during Delta's initial rollout, enabling a clean before-after/control-impact comparison.

**Treatment-assignment mechanism.** Within Delta, implement a week-level randomized phase-in during the first four weeks: automate triage on alternating weeks (e.g., weeks 1 and 3 automated, weeks 2 and 4 manual). This within-branch randomization provides a local experimental estimate of the automation effect, free of branch-level confounders. After week 4, switch to full automation. The alternating design also provides a direct estimate of the effect on actually-automated cases (the LATE), not just the ITT.

**Minimum measurement windows.** Collect at least three full months of pre-rollout data (June–August 2026) for Delta, including case-level event logs, specialist queue depths at daily resolution, and CSAT with weekly sampling and tracked response counts. Post-rollout measurement should extend at least four months through December 2026, capturing learning dynamics and any interaction with future product or routing changes.

**Process-mining data to collect.** Beyond branch-month aggregates: (a) case-level event logs with timestamps for each activity; (b) specialist queue depth and waiting time at daily or sub-daily resolution; (c) automation routing decisions with triage confidence scores; (d) case-level resolution outcome (first-pass resolution vs. rework). These enable variant-specific cycle-time analysis, direct measurement of fast-path substitution, and specialist-queue congestion tracking.

**Spillover monitoring.** Track Epsilon's specialist queue times and fraud desk utilization during Delta's rollout. If Delta's automation increases fraud desk referrals, Epsilon's specialist wait times should be monitored for negative spillovers. Also monitor whether the routing campaign adjusts volume allocation to Delta, which would confound the evaluation.

**Guardrails for customer harm.** Three automatic pause triggers: (1) if Delta's p90 cycle time exceeds 120% of its three-month pre-rollout average for two consecutive weeks, pause and investigate; (2) if rework_pct exceeds 18% (roughly the 90th percentile across all historical branch-months), escalate for review; (3) if weekly sampled CSAT drops below 3.7 (roughly the historical 10th percentile), pause automation for that week.

**Decision rule for scale-up.** After four months of Delta's full automation and two months of Epsilon's: (a) if Delta's median cycle time decreased by  2 hours relative to a synthetic counterfactual constructed from Epsilon's pre-adoption trajectory, and (b) if the within-branch randomized weeks show a median reduction of  1 hour (p < 0.10, acknowledging low power), proceed to full organization-wide rollout. If neither condition is met but no harm is detected, extend evaluation by three months with additional case-level process mining before deciding. If harm triggers activate, pause and redesign the triage rules before continuing.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- **Observed:** Escalation rates increased by 2–3 percentage points in every branch that adopted automated triage, with approximately 1 percentage point of that increase attributable to automation above the background fraud-desk-driven increase observed in all branches. This is a mechanical consequence of the triage system routing more cases to specialist review and is consistent across all six adopter–control comparisons.

- **Suggestive:** Gamma branch—lowest volume, no concurrent staffing loss, smallest routing increase—showed a median cycle-time reduction of approximately 3–5 hours relative to non-adopter trends and a near-complete reversal of the product-change-driven tail-performance spike, while both non-adopters saw persistent cycle-time degradation. Beta and Gamma showed rework reductions of 0.5–2.5 percentage points relative to non-adopters, consistent with improved initial routing reducing downstream correction loops.

- **Conditionally causal:** If one assumes parallel pre-trends (supported but not confirmed by three months of pre-data), absorbs common calendar-time shocks through month fixed effects, and accepts that the routing campaign biases the DiD toward zero (because it added workload to adopters), then automation availability is associated with a median cycle-time reduction of roughly 3–7 hours across adopter branches. However, this estimate is based on only 3 treated clusters, confounded by branch-specific staffing and routing changes, and may not generalize to branches with different baseline characteristics.

- **Not supported:** A single, precise statement such as "automation reduces cycle time by X%" is not defensible from these data. The effect is heterogeneous across branches (Gamma showed strong improvement; Alpha showed negligible median change despite buffering), entangled with at least four concurrent operational changes, measured on only 3 treated clusters, and sensitive to control-group and pre-period window choices. Such a statement would misrepresent the uncertainty and mask the important finding that automation's benefit appears conditional on staffing stability, volume management, and case-complexity context.
