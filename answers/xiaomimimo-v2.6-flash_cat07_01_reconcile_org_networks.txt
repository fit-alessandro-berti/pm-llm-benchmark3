# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

- It does not tell us whether her handovers are fast or slow, nor whether the work she dispatches is well-dispatched. The betweenness score is a topological property of the directed edge-frequency graph, not a measure of decision quality.

# 3. Risk assessment

## Bottleneck risk

**Calibrated claim:** There is moderate-to-strong evidence of a bottleneck specifically in exception handling. The absence experiment showed a ~65% increase in p90 cycle time for exception cases during a 6-day absence. However, this does not establish that she is a *chronic* bottleneck in normal operations — her median service time (4.2 min) is low, and routine flow was unaffected by her absence. The risk is more accurately characterized as a **contingency bottleneck** that manifests under absence or surge conditions rather than a continuous throughput constraint.

## Coordination risk

**Calibrated claim:** Mira_Coordinator performs a coordination function that appears to span teams (both Team North and Team South handovers), specialists, vendors, and automation. If her coordination logic is tacit and undocumented — which is suggested by the qualitative comment about knowing "where hard cases should go" — then coordination quality is unverified. We observe that she routes cases, but we do not have evidence about whether her routing is consistently appropriate, timely, or equitable. The low rework rate visible in the data (no obvious Mira  different specialist loops in the handover table) is mildly reassuring but not conclusive.

## Key-person risk

**Calibrated claim:** The absence experiment provides single-instance but operationally significant evidence of key-person dependency for exception cases. The risk is real: a longer or unplanned absence (illness, resignation) could cause substantial backlog in exception queues. However, we should note that n=1 absence, and it is possible that an informed backup would have been arranged during a planned absence. The key-person risk is highest for unplanned absence scenarios where no substitute has been pre-designated. This is the most actionable risk in this analysis.

## Compliance / control risk

**Calibrated claim:** The compliance reviewer raises a legitimate concern: Mira_Coordinator appears to both detect problems (via interaction with Bot_Rules' fraud screen output and exception flags) and select the next specialist who will work on the case. In regulated claims handling, combining detection and assignment roles without independent review can create a segregation-of-duties concern. However, there is **no direct evidence** that she has misused this discretion — no evidence of self-serving routing, of systematically avoiding certain specialists, or of suppressing complaints. The risk is structural (absence of independent verification), not evidential (no observed misconduct). This distinction should be preserved in any report.

## Workload fairness risk

**Calibrated claim:** Mira_Coordinator's discretion in assigning specialists and reassigning adjusters could create workload imbalance. We observe she sends work to all four adjusters (Carla, Diego, Elise, Farid) with somewhat different frequencies (350, 330, 290, 280), and both specialists receive cases (210, 180). The distribution appears broadly balanced at first glance, but we do not know whether the *complexity* of cases is balanced — she may route harder cases to senior adjusters and easier cases to juniors, or vice versa. The apparent balance in raw counts does not rule out fairness concerns about case difficulty allocation. No direct evidence of unfair allocation is present.

## Data-quality risk

**Calibrated claim:** Several observed patterns could be influenced by logging practices rather than organizational reality. For example: (a) "Assign Specialist" may only be logged for Mira, inflating her centrality; (b) vendor events (Vendor_Pay with 2850 events on 2840 cases) are nearly one-per-case, which is expected for payment but means vendor centrality is driven by volume not coordination; (c) Bot_Rules events on 4090 distinct cases with 4750 events means it performs ~1.16 events per case — if some Bot_Rules events are co-located with human events but logged with sub-second timing, handover attribution could be affected. The working-together metric's known sensitivity to case length (longer cases accumulate more co-occurrences) also means short specialist cases (Omar: 330 events/316 cases) will naturally show lower co-case rates. These data-quality caveats should temper any strong conclusion drawn from a single network metric.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

1. **Designate and begin training a backup for exception-desk functions.** The absence experiment demonstrates a concrete, measurable key-person risk. A second person (e.g., Alice_TeamLead or a new coordinator) should be trained on Mira_Coordinator's core activities — Reopen Claim, Assign Specialist, Escalate Complaint — and granted the necessary system permissions. This does not require removing Mira; it requires ensuring the process does not depend on a single individual. Target: eliminate the observed p90 cycle-time spike during future absences.

2. **Document the exception-handling decision rules.** The qualitative evidence suggests Mira_Coordinator's routing logic is tacit ("knows where hard cases should go"). Her decision criteria for selecting medical vs. legal review, or for choosing between Carla, Diego, Elise, and Farid, should be elicited (through structured interview or think-aloud protocol) and written down — not as a rigid algorithm, but as documented guidance. This reduces key-person risk and creates an audit trail for the compliance concern.

3. **Add an independent review step for specialist assignment decisions.** The compliance reviewer's concern about combining detection and assignment is valid as a structural observation. Introduce a lightweight check — e.g., a team lead signs off on specialist assignments exceeding a threshold (all legal reviews, or all cases above a certain amount band). This addresses the segregation-of-duties concern without requiring evidence of actual misuse, and it is proportionate to the risk level.

## What should be monitored

4. **Track Mira_Coordinator's handover centrality over time** (monthly or quarterly recomputation of handover out-degree and betweenness). A steadily increasing value would indicate growing dependency and should trigger the backup-training plan. A stable value suggests a sustainable role.

5. **Monitor exception case queue depth and p90 cycle time** as leading indicators of coordination failure. The absence experiment showed p90 rose from 8.4  13.9 days; this metric should be on a dashboard with an alert threshold (e.g., p90 > 11 days triggers review).

6. **Measure specialist re-routing rate.** Track how often a case assigned by Mira to Omar or Priya is subsequently reassigned to a different specialist within the next N events. A high re-routing rate would suggest her routing accuracy needs improvement; a low rate supports her brokerage competence.

7. **Monitor workload distribution by case complexity, not just case count.** The raw handover counts to adjusters look balanced, but fairness should be assessed by amount_band and priority distribution across resources she assigns to.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

8. **Do not remove or reduce Mira_Coordinator's role.** Doing so without a replacement would eliminate the coordination function that keeps exception cases moving — the absence experiment already shows what happens when she is unavailable. Any restructuring must be paired with a proven substitute.

9. **Do not automate her specialist-assignment decisions yet.** The Bot_Rules  Mira handover volume and the "Fraud Screen  manual exception  Fraud Screen update" pattern show that automation already interacts with her role, but there is no evidence that a rules engine could replicate her tacit routing knowledge. Automation should be preceded by documentation of her decision logic (see item 2) and evaluation of rule-based routing accuracy on historical data.

10. **Do not restructure the team boundaries (North/South) based on current SNA evidence.** The working-together network's low Mira-Alice/Bruno co-case rates might suggest she is disconnected from teams, but this could be a metric artifact (short events, sparse participation). Role-discovery and exception-filtered analyses should be run first.

11. **Do not draw conclusions about the quality of her decisions from network metrics alone.** Centrality measures position, not competence. A comprehensive evaluation requires process conformance checking (does her routing follow expected patterns?), rework analysis (do her assignments lead to reversals?), and outcome analysis (do cases she handles close successfully?).

She is the most connected resource in handover and subcontracting networks — routing work between automation, specialists, adjusters, and vendors — yet she appears peripheral in working-together networks because her contributions are brief, discrete interventions rather than sustained case involvement. This pattern is consistent with an exception-desk and triage-broker role, but multiple explanations compete: she could be a formal coordinator, an informal boundary spanner, an automation validator, or a key-person bottleneck whose centrality reflects accumulated informal responsibility rather than designed structure.

The strongest evidence for concern comes from her 6-day absence: routine cases were unaffected, but exception-case tail cycle time increased by roughly 65%, with no backup apparent. This signals a contingency bottleneck and key-person dependency — not necessarily a current performance problem.

No evidence of misuse of discretion exists, but the structural combination of problem detection and specialist selection warrants an independent review step. The immediate priority is designing and training a backup for her functions, documenting her routing logic, and monitoring queue metrics — not removing or restructuring the role.
