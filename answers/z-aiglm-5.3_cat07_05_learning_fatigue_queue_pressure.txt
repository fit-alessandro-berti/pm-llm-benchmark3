**Key structural problem:** position-in-shift, clock time, WIP, and case mix are *mutually correlated by design* (fixed shift windows). Bin 76+ is simultaneously "late," "high WIP," "simple mix," and (on Day) "early evening." Any single-mechanism story is therefore unidentifiable from the aggregate tables alone.

# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern — service time falls monotonically while errors fall then rise — is a **superposition of at least five mechanisms whose timing happens to align**, plus two measurement artifacts.

**Phase 1 (positions 1–30): the "slow start" is mostly the work, not the worker.** The first hour inherits backlog with 48–56% high-complexity share, and seniors deliberately take complex cases while fresh. A back-of-envelope consistency check: bin 1–5 (complexity 0.72, queue 46) has 18.8 min service — almost exactly what the WIP×complexity table predicts for that mix (~17–19 min). Its error rate (3.4%) is actually *below* the low-WIP/high-complexity reference cell (4.2%). Fresh workers handle complexity well; the early "slowness" is composition and is not a quality problem.

**Phase 2 (positions 16–50): genuine warm-up riding on simplifying mix.** At matched moderate WIP and complexity, mid-shift events run ~15% faster than mix-predicted time — this residual is the real warm-up/skill component. The juniors' error decline under low WIP (5.1%4.4%) confirms real learning. This is the best-performing window: warmed-up workers, drained queue, simple cases.

**Phase 3 (positions 51+): pressure-driven speed–accuracy tradeoff, not "more learning."** Service time continues falling — bin 76+ at 10.8 min is *faster than its own complexity mix predicts* (~12.6 min at high WIP) — while errors and 7-day rework spike. Critically, the bin-76+ error rate (7.9%) is almost exactly what the WIP×complexity interaction table predicts (~8.0%), and bin 51–75's 4.6% matches its predicted ~4.2–4.6%. **Once WIP and complexity are accounted for, there is almost no residual "position" effect.** The late error spike is produced by the *system state* (WIP 6288), not by elapsed hours per se. Where does the extra speed come from? Effort truncation — skipping verification steps — plus junior batching runs. The medium-WIP cell (11.6 min, 3.0%) shows moderate load actually speeds work with little quality cost, suggesting an inverted-U: moderate pressure is efficient, overload buys speed with errors.

**Why errors rise only in some conditions:** error propensity is an **interaction**, not a main effect — pressure × complexity (10.9% at high WIP/high complexity vs 5.8% low complexity) and pressure × inexperience (Juniors 12.4% vs Seniors 4.9% at high WIP). Seniors' quality barely moves under pressure; they buffer. Errors rise only where truncation's quality cost exceeds the quality gains from simpler mix and warm-up: high WIP, high complexity, juniors, and Night's urgent exceptions.

**The modest, real contributions of fatigue and circadian rhythm:** at low WIP, late-shift errors barely move (2.2–2.9%), so pure fatigue appears small; more likely, fatigue *raises error-proneness at a given speed*, amplifying the pressure effect rather than driving it. Circadian effects are plausible for Night staff's elevated low-WIP error and complexity-adjusted slowness, but with two resources, 56% first-hour complexity, and exception-heavy intake, they cannot yet be separated from mix and staffing.

**A self-reinforcing loop:** Day builds queue (3875)  Late inherits it and rushes (6.8% error)  rework plus unfinished cases land on Night/Early first hours  complex backlog carryover  the cycle repeats. Batching and deferred QC distort the measurement at both ends: junior batch runs make late Day look even faster than it is, and end-of-shift QC bursts attribute errors to the shift that *detected* them, not the shift that *committed* them, while feeding tomorrow's complex backlog.

**Bottom line:** early slowness = case mix; mid-shift speed = warm-up on simple mix; late speed = pressure truncation plus batching; late errors = WIP × complexity × inexperience; fatigue and circadian = small amplifiers; and the apparent "learning curve" and "end-of-shift collapse" are both partly artifacts of composition and detection timing.

Analysis plan mapped to the required dimensions:

- **Within-resource comparisons:** resource fixed effects above; additionally match each worker's own events across strata (same activity, complexity band, WIP band) early vs late; compare position-1 performance against `resource_experience_months` to split within-shift warm-up from durable skill.
- **Within-shift position effects:** spline on `minutes_since_shift_start` (fatigue dose) vs `sequence_position_within_shift` (practice count); check whether the position coefficient survives WIP/mix controls — the aggregate tables suggest it largely will not for errors.
- **WIP/queue stratification:** test nonlinearity (threshold near 70; inverted-U at 30–70); use `queue_length_at_start` and case-level arrival rate (`insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`) as demand-load covariates.
- **Complexity adjustment:** direct standardization — reweight each bin to a common complexity/priority/activity distribution and recompute the position curve; report "actual vs expected" ratios per cell.
- **Shift and time-of-day controls:** the cross-shift identification design — the *same position bin* maps to different clock hours across Early/Day/Late/Night (e.g., 378 min since start  12:18, 15:18, 20:18, 04:20), so position-matched, WIP-controlled cross-shift comparisons separate time-on-task from circadian phase; the shared 09:00–14:00 window of Early and Day gives the mirror comparison.
- **Batching detection:** `discover_batches`; flag events in runs 5 of the same resource/activity; recompute all headline statistics with batched runs excluded; measure QC deferral (share of Quality Check in last 45 min of shift).
- **Resource-experience interactions:** the `experience_months × WIP` term (Seniors 4.9% vs Juniors 12.4% at high WIP is the key contrast); also `experience × complexity`.
- **Rework/error downstream analysis:** join `rework_flag` events within 7 days back to the originating event's `minutes_since_shift_start`, `wip_at_start`, and shift; compute **detection latency** (commission  QC  Rework Correction) to correct misattribution from deferred QC; `get_rework_cases_per_activity` for hotspots; `discover_performance_dfg` on the errorcorrection path.
- **Mediation check:** add service time to the error model — if the WIP coefficient shrinks materially, WIP damages quality *through* effort truncation (speed), which is the operational lever.
- **Exploratory segmentation:** `pm4py.cluster_log(df)` and dotted charts to surface behavior profiles (batchers vs steady workers, complex-case specialists).

# 4. Intervention options

1. **Strictly cap shift length.** *Benefit:* limits exposure to the 76+ bin (7.9% error, 13.7% rework) if fatigue contributes. *Risk:* misdiagnosed — the 76+ error rate is nearly fully explained by WIP×complexity; capping hours without adding capacity just transfers the queue to the next shift's first hour, which is already the most complex window, potentially *raising* total errors; overtime is often worked by the most experienced staff. *Monitor:* queue at shift start/end (backlog transfer), time-above-WIP-70, first-hour complexity share, position-binned error curve before/after (it should flatten at 51+ only if fatigue was causal).

2. **Move complex cases away from the first hour.** *Benefit:* modest — would reduce early service times. *Risk:* likely backfires. First-hour workers are fresh and currently handle complexity *at or better than* baseline (3.4% at complexity 0.72 vs 4.2% reference); deferral pushes complex cases into the 51+/high-WIP zone where high-WIP×high-complexity error is 10.9%. *Monitor:* error on high-complexity cases by WIP band and hour; if implemented, watch the high-WIP/high-complexity cell and Day last-2h error closely.

3. **Queue-pressure alert at WIP > 70.** *Benefit:* targets the dominant risk factor; cheap; enables pacing, escalation to seniors, temporary bot routing, break rescheduling. *Risk:* alert fatigue; the threshold is not magic (errors already climb around 60+ and complexity modulates risk); alerts without authority or capacity to act just add stress. *Monitor:* time-above-70 per shift, error/rework rates inside >70 windows, alert-to-action rate, alert volume (gaming check).

4. **Force random assignment.** *Benefit:* removes selection bias from performance attribution; equalizes exposure. *Risk:* destroys *beneficial* skill-based routing — seniors-on-complex-early is working (senior high-WIP error 4.9% vs juniors 12.4%); random routing of complex cases to juniors under pressure would raise errors. Better: keep routing, adjust the *metrics* for mix. *Monitor:* complexity mix per resource group, error by complexity×group, cycle time for complex cases, senior-on-complex share.

5. **Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours.** *Benefit:* if residual fatigue matters, breaks reduce error at a given speed; low cost. *Risk:* capacity loss exactly at the demand peak can raise WIP and worsen pressure errors unless coverage is staffed; note that at low WIP, late errors barely move (2.2–2.9%), so the expected effect is modest — this is a supplement to intervention 3/7, not a substitute. *Monitor:* error rate in final two hours *by WIP band*, WIP trajectory in the final two hours, service-time distribution (a slight *rise* toward complexity-adjusted expected time is a success signal, not a failure), 7-day rework.

6. **Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift.** *Benefit:* smooths end-of-shift bursts; checks occur with fresher eyes. *Risk:* significant. QC delay is already implicated in the burst corrections and the backlog-complexity cycle; further deferral increases detection latency, raises rework cost (wrong eligibility decisions may already have reached customers via callbacks), and loads the next shift's first hour — improving *measured* throughput by moving cost downstream. This must not become a backdoor for removing checks. *Monitor:* commission-to-detection latency, 7-day rework rate, queue at shift start, first-hour complexity share, redo callbacks/complaints.

7. **Add an extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks.** *Benefit:* attacks the best-evidenced mechanism at its source — Day queue 3875 with 7.5% last-2h error; seniors hold quality under pressure; extra peak capacity lowers WIP for *everyone*. Likely the highest-leverage option. *Risk:* cost; senior scarcity; bottleneck may shift downstream (callbacks, QC) — pair with capacity checks there. *Monitor:* Day WIP curve and time-above-70, Day last-2h error and rework, waiting time on the Eligibility Decision path, arrival vs finish rate (`insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`).

8. **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates.** *Recommend against.* Late-shift errors are driven mainly by queue state (a staffing/rostering outcome), case mix, deferred-QC detection timing, and batching artifacts — raw rates conflate worker behavior with system design, and would disproportionately punish juniors and Night staff (n = 2, unstable individual estimates). Expected side effects: under-reporting, avoiding hard cases, inflating service times, hiding errors (Goodhart), and loss of the psychological safety needed for error reporting. *Monitor (if any individual-level feedback is used):* risk-adjusted actual-vs-expected error ratios with confidence intervals, near-miss reporting rates (these should *rise* in a healthy system), and gaming indicators (complexity mix of assigned cases, service-time inflation, error under-detection at QC).

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias makes raw worker comparisons unfair.** Seniors receive complex early cases (and look appropriately slow); juniors receive batched document reviews (and look artificially fast); Night staff receive urgent exceptions. Comparing unadjusted rates punishes people for their assignment stream, not their behavior.
- **Measurement error is systematic, not random.** Errors are recorded at *detection*, and detection is deferred to end-of-shift QC — errors get attributed to the shift that found them, not the shift that caused them. Batching makes per-event service time nearly meaningless (setup cost amortized across a run), and workers who check *more* will *find* more errors, looking worse than colleagues who check less.
- **Queue pressure is substantially management-created.** WIP is an outcome of staffing, rostering, break policy, and demand forecasting. Penalizing workers for pressure-induced errors while leaving the staffing decisions unchanged is scapegoating; the fair unit of accountability for the high-WIP error zone is the *system*, not the individual.
- **Automation quality shifts hidden work onto humans.** Bot_Risk looks instant and efficient while humans absorb its correction rework — and those corrections may originate from *upstream* hurried triage, not the bot or the corrector. Grading humans on "bot correction rate" or crediting the bot for speed without charging it its downstream corrections double-counts unfairly in both directions.
- **Punitive error metrics corrupt the data they measure.** Small samples (Night staff) make individual rates statistically meaningless; unadjusted public ranking invites gaming and legal/HR exposure; error rates require risk adjustment, confidence intervals, and worker voice in how the model is built. Report at team/shift level by default; individual drill-down should be governed, transparent, and developmental, never purely punitive.
