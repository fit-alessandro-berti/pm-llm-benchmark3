# 1. Fairness dimensions
Procedural fairness here is multi-dimensional, so equal approval rates are neither necessary nor sufficient.

- Outcome parity: Raw outcomes are materially unequal. Group A has the highest approval rate and shortest cycle time, while Groups B, C, and D have lower approval, longer cycle times, and higher appeal/rework. That is evidence of unequal experience, but not by itself evidence of discrimination because case mix differs.
- Adjusted outcome disparity: After controlling for risk score, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior case count, region, and month, residual disparities remain. Relative to Group A, Group B still has about 5.5 percentage points lower approval, 2.6 more days of cycle time, 8.0 points more supervisor review, 9.5 points more missing-document requests, and 4.4 points more appeals. Group C still has 4.1 more days of cycle time and elevated supervisor review and document requests. Group D still has lower approval and much higher appeals. This means observed complexity does not fully explain the gaps.
- Procedural burden: Groups B and C bear more steps, more rework, and more burdensome paths. Higher use of V2, V4, and V5, plus higher adjusted rates of missing-document requests and supervisor review, indicate that some groups must do more to reach a decision even after controls.
- Timeliness: Timeliness is part of fairness, not just efficiency. The largest delays are concentrated around support and review queues: Group B waits 3.8 days between Language Support Contact and Eligibility Assessment, and Group C waits 4.7 days between Accommodation Review and Eligibility Assessment. Those are rights-preserving steps, but delay attached to those rights is itself a fairness issue.
- Consistency of routing: Some routing differences are legitimate, such as Fraud Review for higher-risk cases, Language Support Contact for minority-language applicants, and Accommodation Review for applicants requesting accommodation. But consistency requires that similarly situated cases follow similar rules and service levels. The residual excess of supervisor review and document requests for Groups B and C suggests routing may still be inconsistent within comparable cases.
- Quality and accessibility of support: The existence of support steps is favorable in principle, but procedural fairness depends on whether support is timely, understandable, and non-punitive. If support access causes long queues, extra handoffs, or lower-quality explanations, then the process may remain unfair even while formally offering support.
- Appeal and rework burden: Appeals and reopen/rework are procedural harms. They signal extra effort, delay, and possible confusion or correction of earlier decisions. Group B and Group D have especially elevated adjusted appeal rates, and Groups B and C have much higher raw reopen/rework rates than Group A.
- Resource-allocation fairness: Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers, while Group B is concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded officers, and Group C among 5 accommodation specialists. Specialization may be necessary, but staffing certain groups into thinner, more congested queues creates unequal process quality even if formal rules are the same.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy
The analysis should separate four things: legitimate complexity, procedural disparity, possible discrimination, and uncertainty from imperfect covariates.

1. Build a case-level comparison table from the event log with one row per case and features for applicant group, channel, language preference, accommodation request, risk score band, documentation completeness, household size band, prior case count, region, month, outcome, appeal, cycle time, rework indicators, number of handovers, assigned role mix, and path/variant.
2. Use matching or stratification on variables that plausibly reflect complexity or operating context: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. Compare Groups A, B, C, and D within the same strata rather than only in aggregate.
3. Run two adjustment views rather than one. First, a broad adjusted comparison using the listed controls to estimate residual disparity. Second, a more fairness-sensitive view that treats risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as potentially biased or partly downstream of barriers, so they are removed, coarsened, or sensitivity-tested rather than accepted as unquestionably neutral.
4. Do not “adjust away” the support process when assessing fairness of support. For language support and accommodation, compare cases within support-need strata and ask whether those cases receive timely, consistent, low-handoff treatment. Otherwise the analysis can hide unfair burden created by the support mechanism itself.
5. Compare path and variant distributions within matched strata. If Group B and Group C still disproportionately enter longer variants, accumulate extra loops, or trigger supervisor review more often than similarly situated Group A cases, that is evidence of procedural disparity beyond case complexity.
6. Compare resource assignment within matched strata. Measure whether certain groups are disproportionately routed to overloaded officers, smaller specialist pools, or extra handoff structures after controlling for legitimate need. Distinguish “needs specialist capability” from “is forced through avoidable queue fragmentation.”
7. Decompose cycle time into service time and waiting time by segment. For each group and matched stratum, estimate how much delay arises from active work versus queueing between key steps such as Submit to Verification, Completeness to Request Missing Documents, Language Support to Eligibility, Accommodation Review to Eligibility, Risk Screening to Fraud Review, and Supervisor Review to Decision Draft.
8. Use subgroup checks by region and channel. Because in-person cases wait longer and two regions have supervisor backlogs and extra handovers, estimate disparities separately for online versus in-person cases and separately within each region, especially the two problematic regions.
9. Interpret findings by category. If disparities disappear within matched strata and are concentrated in steps explicitly required by policy, they are more likely legitimate. If disparities persist within matched strata and are tied to staffing bottlenecks, extra document requests, or inconsistent review escalation, they indicate procedural unfairness. If disparities persist even after strong controls but the controls themselves may be biased, the result is concerning but not definitive proof of discrimination.
10. Perform sensitivity analysis. Re-estimate disparities with and without risk_score_band and documentation_completeness, with coarser bins, and after excluding obvious post-intake mediators. Examine whether the sign and size of residual group effects are stable. Large swings would show that conclusions depend heavily on potentially biased covariates.

# 3. Path and resource disparities
The strongest procedural disparities are concentrated in Groups B and C, with a more mixed pattern for Group D.

- Group B shows the clearest support-linked burden. V4, the language-support path, accounts for 24% of Group B cases versus 1% for Group A. That difference is largely legitimate because Group B has much higher language-support need. But the associated waiting structure is concerning: 1.6 days from Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents, then 3.8 days from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, plus higher adjusted supervisor review and document requests. The concentration of Group B cases among only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and the extra general-to-bilingual-to-general handovers in two regions, suggest that under-capacity and fragmented routing are adding burden beyond the legitimate need for language access.
- Group C shows a similar pattern around accommodation. V5, the accommodation path, accounts for 31% of Group C cases versus 2% for Group A, which is expected given the much higher accommodation-request rate. But Group C still has the largest adjusted cycle-time penalty at +4.1 days, a 4.7-day median wait from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment, and elevated supervisor review. Because only 5 accommodation specialists handle many of these cases and two also perform supervisor review, the specialized support pathway appears to function as a bottleneck. The support step itself is justified; the queue and handoff burden attached to it is not clearly justified.
- Group D’s routing is more plausibly tied to legitimate complexity. V3, involving Risk Screening, Fraud Review, and Supervisor Review, is 28% of Group D versus 9% of Group A, consistent with Group D’s much higher measured risk. The 2.8-day Risk Screening to Fraud Review wait is also consistent with a heavier review path. However, two concerns remain. First, risk_score_band may encode historical bias, so “consistent with risk” does not settle the fairness question. Second, Group D still has a large adjusted appeal difference (+5.7 points), which suggests possible over-screening, weaker explanations, or more contested decisions even after the heavier route is taken into account.
- Group A benefits from the shortest, cleanest path structure. V1 accounts for 42% of Group A cases but far less of the other groups, and Group A cases are distributed across many more caseworkers. That does not prove favoritism by itself, but it shows the process is operationally optimized for the majority-language, lower-support, mostly online population.
- Some disparities appear more justified than others. Fraud-related routing for Group D is more justifiable on the current evidence than the long support queues for Groups B and C. Language support and accommodation review are also justifiable steps, but the data indicate that the way those steps are staffed and connected to the broader workflow creates extra burden that remains concerning after complexity controls.
- The location of delay matters. Differences from Decision Draft to Applicant Notification are small, so the unfairness signal is not mainly in the final communication stage. It is concentrated earlier, in verification, document collection, support queues, supervisor review, and handoff-heavy routing.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms
Several mechanisms could generate the observed patterns, and they are not all normatively equivalent.

- Legitimate risk-based escalation: Higher measured risk can legitimately trigger Fraud Review and more supervisory attention, especially for Group D.
- Legitimate rights-preserving support routing: Minority-language cases may require Language Support Contact, and accommodation requests may require Accommodation Review. Those steps can improve substantive fairness even if they lengthen the path.
- Capacity shortages in specialized support pools: Too few bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists can turn a justified support step into a systematic delay for Groups B and C.
- Fragmented ownership and extra handovers: Routing cases from a general caseworker to a specialist and back can create queueing, repetition, and inconsistency, especially in the two regions with known handoff patterns.
- Documentation barriers: Lower measured documentation completeness may partly reflect language barriers, inaccessible instructions, or channel effects rather than purely applicant behavior. That can drive additional missing-document requests for Group B and Group C.
- Differential scrutiny or escalation thresholds: If similarly situated Group B or Group C cases are more likely to receive supervisor review or extra document requests even within comparable strata, a plausible mechanism is inconsistent discretionary scrutiny.
- Explanation and communication quality: Elevated appeals may arise because notices, evidence requests, or rationales are less understandable or less accessible for some groups, even when the underlying decision is substantively correct.
- Historical bias in upstream risk scoring: If risk_score_band is itself shaped by prior unequal enforcement or measurement error, then apparently legitimate downstream routing can reproduce older disparities.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes
The current attributes support a strong procedural audit, but they are not sufficient to identify every source of unfairness or to prove discrimination.

- Measurement bias in risk_score_band: Risk scores may reflect historical enforcement patterns, proxy variables, or label bias. Treating them as neutral controls can mask unfairness rather than remove it.
- Measurement bias in documentation_completeness: Documentation completeness may partly measure the accessibility of forms, instructions, translation, and contact processes. If language barriers or disability barriers reduce completeness, then adjusting for it can subtract away part of the unfair process itself.
- Missing applicant needs: The log does not appear to capture literacy, digital access, cognitive load, severity or type of accommodation need, need for an interpreter beyond language preference, legal assistance, or urgency of benefits. Those omitted factors can affect both routing and fairness.
- Unobserved workload and queue dynamics: Resource workload is only observed at daily granularity. That is too coarse to know intra-day queue length, appointment availability, absences, batching practices, or whether the same nominal team experienced very different operational pressure at the moment a case arrived.
- Appeal quality is not observed: An appeal flag tells us that a case was appealed, not whether the initial decision was wrong, poorly explained, inaccessible, or simply contested by a more persistent applicant.
- Outcome quality and dignity are missing: case_outcome does not capture whether the explanation was understandable, whether the applicant felt respected, whether support was effective, or whether the process imposed stress or repeated paperwork.
- Hidden off-system work may be missing: Calls, translation efforts, failed contact attempts, or informal clarifications may not be fully logged, which can distort the apparent burden and service-time estimates.
- Coarse geography and channel effects: Region may be too broad to capture office-level staffing and channel-specific appointment constraints. In-person versus online is useful, but it may not fully capture access frictions.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects
The goal should be to reduce unfair burden while preserving legitimate support and risk controls.

- Add capacity for language support and accommodation review. Increase bilingual staffing, cross-train more caseworkers, add backup specialist coverage, or create surge capacity in overloaded regions. Side effects: higher cost, slower short-run implementation, and possible dilution of specialist expertise if cross-training is shallow.
- Add explicit service-level targets for support steps. Track separate timeliness targets for Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment and Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment, not only overall cycle time. Side effects: staff may rush support interactions or game timestamps unless quality and applicant understanding are also monitored.
- Redesign routing to reduce handovers. Use end-to-end ownership where possible, specialist-enabled pods, or stable case pairing so that support does not require repeated transfer back and forth. Side effects: lower staffing flexibility, possible underutilization in low-demand periods, and more complex scheduling.
- Improve decision explanations and evidence requests. Provide multilingual, plain-language, and accessible notices; standardize document-request explanations; explain why supervisor or fraud review occurred when appropriate; and make appeal instructions clearer. Side effects: longer drafting time, more template governance, and potential legal review overhead.
- Monitor adjusted disparities continuously. Create dashboards for matched or stratified differences in cycle time, supervisor review, missing-document requests, appeals, rework, and handovers by group, region, and channel. Side effects: small-sample noise can create false alarms, and punitive use of metrics can encourage staff gaming rather than better process design.
- Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness. Evaluate calibration, differential error, stability by group, and whether those variables are acting as biased proxies or downstream mediators of access barriers. Side effects: changing these controls can temporarily reduce operational predictability or fraud-detection sensitivity if redesign is crude.
- Standardize escalation and review criteria. Use clearer rules or checklists for when supervisor review and missing-document requests are appropriate, especially in high-variance regions. Side effects: overly rigid rules can reduce discretion needed for unusual cases or nuanced accommodations.
- Improve front-end access rather than removing support steps. Better translated instructions, accessible forms, pre-submission guidance, and early document-check tools can reduce later rework without eliminating language or accommodation protections. Side effects: new intake tooling can be expensive and may shift burden onto applicants if not designed carefully.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment
The process does not look fully procedurally fair. The raw disparities are not enough on their own, and some of the routing differences are clearly legitimate: Group D plausibly receives more fraud review because of higher measured risk, Group B legitimately receives more language-support routing, and Group C legitimately receives more accommodation review. But the evidence also shows that complexity does not fully explain the experience gap.

The strongest concern is not simply lower approval rates; it is that Groups B and C appear to pay a procedural penalty for using rights-preserving support steps. After adjustment, they still face more document requests, more supervisor review, materially longer cycle times, and queue-heavy, handoff-heavy paths concentrated in under-resourced specialist pools. That is credible evidence of procedural unfairness in burden, timeliness, and resource allocation. For Group D, the fairness picture is more mixed: some disparity may be legitimate, but residual appeal and approval gaps remain concerning because the neutrality of risk_score_band cannot be assumed. The evidence therefore supports a calibrated conclusion: the process appears procedurally uneven and at least partly unfair, while the available data are not sufficient to prove that all residual disparities are caused by discrimination rather than a combination of biased covariates, staffing design, and inconsistent routing.
