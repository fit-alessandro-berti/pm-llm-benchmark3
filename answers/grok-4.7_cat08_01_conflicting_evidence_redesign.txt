## 1. Current-process diagnosis

The log’s dominant behavior is a short replacement path. Of 5,000 claims, 3,600 go from Intake claim to Auto eligibility screen and 2,550 of those go straight to Fast approval. Fast approval then sends 2,300 cases to Dispatch replacement, and Dispatch replacement reaches Quality/compliance close on 2,320 transitions. A second, smaller fast-approval inflow of 240 comes from Fraud/risk review. Summing the median times on the four happy-path edges (0.5 + 0.4 + 1.8 + 3.2 hours) gives about 6 hours. That sketch matches the segment medians on the fast path: 8 hours for Premium low-risk accessories and 13 hours for Standard low-risk accessories. High-value fast-path medians are still only 20 hours (Premium) and 28 hours (Standard). Fast-approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework and 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits.

That cost and speed picture is an average over a path with a second, much worse life after first close. Customer complaint/reopen occurs in 9.2% of all cases, in 14.1% of cases that use Fast approval, and in 3.3% of cases that travel Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair device. The performance graph puts the failure far outside the close metric: Fast approval → Customer complaint/reopen has median 50 hours and p90 150 hours; Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen has median 70 hours and p90 180 hours. The temporal profile ties that dispatch tail to incorrect replacement part selection. A wrong replacement has expected cost EUR 95 on low-risk accessories and EUR 410 on high-value devices. The 31-to-9 percent collapse is the financial version of the same fact: first-close cost stops before the episode is over. The remaining 9% is an all-segment average. It mixes a 3–4 percentage-point complaint gap on low-risk accessories with a 12–17 point gap on high-value devices, and it mixes a EUR 95 error with a EUR 410 error. The average still favors speed; the high-value slice is where that conclusion is weakest, and the data are not fine enough to say the 9% has already reversed there.

Compliance failures sit on the same popular path. Token-based replay against the normative net shows log fitness 0.91 and only 68% fitting traces: most events can be explained, and about one trace in three still contains a deviation. Alignment diagnostics name the frequent move-on-log patterns: Fast approval before evidence completeness, Customer complaint/reopen after close, and Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices. Those are direct conflicts with the rules that govern this process.

- R1: a claim above EUR 400 needs Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review before Fast approval.
- R2: Dispatch replacement must wait for confirmed evidence completeness, with a stated exception only for Premium low-risk accessories.
- R3: a reopened claim needs Manual eligibility review before final close or denial.

The auto-screen rules are outdated and not segment-aware, at EUR 0.20 per case and with effectively unlimited capacity. They are a cheap way to produce exactly the R1 and R2 escapes. Precision of the discovered model is low because low-frequency paths also combine auto approval, risk review, and repair in ways policy does not allow. The control problem is both a high-volume illegal highway and a long tail of illegal combinations.

The slow path is a different operating regime, and it is better only in some segments. Reviewed or repaired high-value claims have median cycle times of 96 hours (Premium) and 120 hours (Standard), against fast-path complaint rates of 22% and 18% and reviewed/repair complaint rates of 5% and 6%. That quality gap is large. The elapsed-time cost of buying it is also large, and it is already worse than Premium customers can carry: Manual eligibility review → Repair quote sits at median 15 hours, mean 22, p90 60, and the temporal profile flags this edge as often too slow for Premium. Repair quote → Customer approval adds median 21 hours, mean 30, p90 80, with the wait caused mostly by part-price confirmation rather than by the three technicians’ touch time. Rework repair hits 12.5% of repaired high-value devices and 4.0% of repaired low-risk devices, so “send it to repair” also imports a second quality loop (Quality check → Rework repair, 45 transitions, almost all returning via Rework repair → Repair device).

Low-risk accessories do not justify that trade. Premium low-risk complaints move only from 6% on the fast path to 3% on the manual path, while the manual median is 46 hours against service-credit exposure once Premium cycle time exceeds 24 hours. R2 already permits their fast dispatch. Standard low-risk complaints move from 8% to 4%, and those customers tolerate completion within 72 hours when status visibility is good. Their manual median of 52 hours can fit that window; an invisible queue will not. Standard low-risk is inside the R2 rule, so a fast dispatch before evidence completeness is a compliance miss even though the economics are mild.

The third population is the evidence loop, about 22% of cases, median cycle time 140 hours, with the highest abandonment and the repeated requests. Request missing evidence returns to Manual eligibility review on 890 transitions at median 26 hours, mean 34, p90 88. It is requested at least twice in 26% of cases that enter it. Of 390 recorded abandonments, 320 leave from Request missing evidence and 70 leave from Customer approval. Claim denial is the smaller exit: 220 cases, almost all from Fraud/risk review. The process loses more claims by stalling and by shipping the wrong outcome than by deciding “no.”

Two measurement limits should bound every redesign below. Performance-graph hours are elapsed times between activities, so they mix queue, customer response, and touch time; they are not labor standards. The frequency graph is also not a closed case balance: recorded terminals are 3,015 quality/compliance closes, 220 denials, and 390 abandonments, while listed inflows to Manual eligibility review (intake 1,400, evidence return 890, complaint 310) exceed the listed manual outflows. Unlisted edges and loops are real. Shares below use the counts that are given; they are not a full throughput model.

Capacity is narrow in specific places, not everywhere. Seven reviewers provide 252 productive hours a week at EUR 34 per hour, and the queue grows sharply once utilization passes 82%. Current utilization is not stated, so the spare hours below that cliff are unknown; what is known is that a rule update can raise manual inflow 15–35%, which is a large shock relative to the 18-point band between the cliff and full load. Fraud/risk review is two specialists at EUR 52 per hour, already bottlenecked on Mondays and after marketing campaigns. Quality check is two inspectors at EUR 38 per hour, underused except after repair campaigns. The escalation board is one 45-minute slot a day: Customer complaint/reopen reaches it on 120 transitions and reaches Manual eligibility review on 310, at medians of 42 and 31 hours. The board has a strong satisfaction effect and almost no scale. Any redesign that buys quality by pouring volume into manual review, fraud review, or the board will move the complaint elsewhere.

Mid-risk devices are in scope operationally and absent from the segment outcome table. They should be tagged in any pilot and not treated as either low-risk or high-value by assumption.

## 2. Redesign A: Tiered authorization before Fast approval

**Flow.** Intake claim records segment, claim value, defect-type clarity, and whether the evidence checklist is already complete. The auto-screen remains the router, with rules rebuilt around R1 and R2.

- Premium low-risk accessories stay on Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval → Dispatch replacement. R2 allows that exception. The only added control is an automated SKU compatibility check with no human queue.
- Standard low-risk accessories with complete evidence may still be fast-approved and dispatched. If evidence is incomplete, they go to evidence completion and cannot be fast-approved first.
- Any claim above EUR 400, and any high-value device, is barred from Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval. Clear-defect cases go to Fraud/risk review. Ambiguous cases go to Manual eligibility review.
- Fraud/risk review keeps the three exits it already has: Fast approval when replacement is justified (today 240 cases, median 12 hours after review), Repair quote when the device should be repaired (310 cases), and Claim denied when the claim fails (210 cases).
- Premium high-value work is steered to the fraud-then-fast-approval route whenever the technical question is “is this replacement legitimate,” because Manual eligibility review → Repair quote is already too slow for Premium. Full repair is reserved for a failed part match or a defect that replacement will not solve.
- A reopened claim goes to Manual eligibility review before any further close, denial, replacement, or repair. The escalation board can prepare the case; it cannot be the activity that finally dispatches or denies.

**Targets.** High-value and above-EUR-400 claims that now reach Dispatch replacement without Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review. Secondarily, Standard claims that are fast-approved before evidence completeness. Premium low-risk accessories are explicitly left on the fast path.

**Mechanism.** The cheap, unlimited auto-screen is the activity that creates the illegal highway: 2,550 fast approvals from a screen whose rules are not segment-aware. Putting the R1 token in front of Fast approval uses a path the log already contains, Fraud/risk review → Fast approval, instead of forcing high-value claims onto the 96–120 hour repair path. Complaint rates on the reviewed/repair side are 5–6% against 18–22% on the high-value fast path. The design tries to capture that quality gap with a short specialist review, then still dispatch when replacement is the right remedy. Standard low-risk customers can absorb a completeness wait inside their 72-hour tolerance if status is visible. Premium low-risk customers cannot absorb a move from an 8-hour median to a 46-hour manual median, and their complaint gap is only 3 points, so slowing them destroys the service-credit position for a small quality gain.

**Accepts and leaves unresolved.** Premium high-value cycle time rises above today’s 20-hour fast median by the fraud-path waits (entry median 8 hours from the auto-screen, 13 hours from manual review, then median 12 hours to Fast approval, with p90s of 28–44 hours into review). Part-selection error on claims that are legitimately fast-approved stays in place, including the 6% Premium low-risk complaint rate. The evidence cohort’s 140-hour median and the 26% repeat-request rate stay in place. Escalation-board scale stays as it is. Mid-risk thresholds are a judgment, not a measured policy. Whether specialist time at EUR 52 and EUR 34 per hour removes the residual 9% cost gap, or reverses it, cannot be calculated from this extract. The deployment fact that matters most is also a risk: new rules can be in place in about two weeks and may increase manual-review inflow by 15–35%.

## 3. Redesign B: Single-pass evidence lane with an assisted fallback

**Flow.** Evidence completion becomes its own lane, with a single return into eligibility.

- At Intake claim, a defect-type checklist decides completeness before any approval. Complete cases continue into eligibility under whatever authorization rules are then in force. This redesign does not rebuild the auto-screen.
- Incomplete cases, other than the R2 exception, enter Evidence completion. They do not go to Fast approval and they do not drop into the general manual-review queue merely to have a reviewer write a free-text request.
- The digital portal asks for the specific missing items once. Its design target is the stated 30–45% reduction in repeated requests. That figure is a hypothesis to be piloted.
- Customers with low digital literacy use an assisted capture channel, phone or store, against the same checklist. The portal is one door, not the only door.
- Status is visible as soon as the request is open. That visibility is the condition under which Standard customers accept up to 72 hours.
- The packet returns to eligibility once. Manual eligibility review may not issue a second generic Request missing evidence. If the file is still thin after one assisted attempt, the case gets an explicit decision: approve within policy, send to Fraud/risk review, or deny, and the record states that the decision used a thin file.
- Premium low-risk accessories keep the R2 exception and may be dispatched without the completeness token. Every other segment needs the token before Dispatch replacement.

**Targets.** The unclear-defect / missing-evidence cohort, 22% of cases, median 140 hours. The activities in scope are Request missing evidence, the return edge into Manual eligibility review (890 transitions, median 26 hours, p90 88 hours), and the 320 abandonments that leave from that activity. The conformance target is the frequent deviation “Fast approval before evidence completeness.”

**Mechanism.** A large share of cycle time and abandonment is a ping-pong, not a decision problem. Cases that enter the evidence activity repeat it at least once 26% of the time, and the return into manual review is one of the slowest ordinary edges in the performance graph. Those elapsed hours mix customer response with internal requeue, so the redesign can only remove the repeat lap and the unstructured ask; it cannot assume the whole 34-hour mean is internal waste. A defect-specific ask, one pass, and a human fallback attack the repeat rate the portal is supposed to cut, while the assisted channel is there because the same proposal is expected to raise abandonment among customers with low digital literacy. Keeping incomplete non-exempt claims off Fast approval removes a known illegal move without sending the whole population down the repair path.

**Accepts and leaves unresolved.** High-value claims that already have complete evidence, and that are dispatched under a weak auto-approval, keep today’s part-selection and R1 exposure. Some customers who would have answered a second or third request will now be decided on a thin file or will abandon. The 70 abandonments at Customer approval, which sit after a slow quote, are outside this lane. Assisted capture consumes reviewer hours that sit under an 82% utilization cliff. Cycle time for incomplete Standard and high-value claims can lengthen first, because they lose an illegal fast approval before the repeat-loop savings show up. The 30–45% repeat reduction is not a demonstrated result in this log.

## 4. Redesign C: Parts-master release gate on high-value dispatch

**Flow.** Eligibility timing stays as it is. A new release step stands between the replacement decision and Dispatch replacement, backed by parts master data that also feeds repair prices.

- The master data answers two questions the log currently answers slowly or wrongly: which SKU matches this defect, and what the part price is. The first is the stated driver of the dispatch-to-complaint tail. The second is the stated driver of Repair quote delay.
- Premium low-risk accessories get an automated master-data check only, measured in minutes, and then dispatch. They do not enter a human gate. The design keeps their 8-hour median on the safe side of the 24-hour credit line and accepts their residual 6% versus 3% complaint gap.
- Standard low-risk accessories get the automated check plus an evidence-completeness token before release, consistent with R2.
- High-value devices, and any replacement above EUR 400, pass a human part-confirmation staffed from the two quality inspectors. The work content is a checklist against master data, aimed at the 6-hour end of the stated 6–18 hour range. Eighteen hours is the point at which the gate has failed its own design, not the planned duration.
- A SKU mismatch does not ship a near-match. The case either waits for the correct SKU or moves to Repair quote.
- A missing R1 token on a claim above EUR 400 quarantines the case to Fraud/risk review or Manual eligibility review before shipment. The gate records the escape; it does not silently perform a full eligibility review.
- A missing evidence token, outside the Premium low-risk exception, sends the case to evidence completion rather than to dispatch.
- Repair quote uses the same price feed, so Customer approval is not waiting on a manual price chase.

**Targets.** Dispatch replacement after Fast approval, especially Standard and Premium high-value devices, where wrong-replacement cost is EUR 410 and fast-path complaint rates are 18% and 22%. The activity being added is a release control in front of the 2,300 fast-approval dispatches. Quality inspectors are the intended resource because they are underused outside repair campaigns.

**Mechanism.** Eligibility review and correct part selection are different failures. R1 can be satisfied and the wrong SKU can still ship; a careful repair path can show a 3.3% complaint rate for reasons that have nothing to do with how the auto-screen behaves. The long tail after dispatch is specifically tied to part selection, and its clock starts after the process has already told itself the claim is closed: median 70 hours, p90 180 hours to the complaint. Catching the mismatch before shipment changes the episode the customer actually experiences. Selective gating is what makes the economics plausible: a human delay of 6–18 hours can be justified against a EUR 410 error on high-value devices far more readily than against a EUR 95 error on a Premium accessory whose service credit triggers at 24 hours. Using idle inspector time, at EUR 38 per hour, avoids an immediate new load on the seven reviewers and the two fraud specialists. Master data also shortens the repair-quote wait for the cases the gate diverts, which is the edge that currently adds median 21 hours and is already too slow for Premium when reached from manual review.

**Accepts and leaves unresolved.** Gated high-value replacements slow from today’s 20–28 hour fast medians by 6–18 hours. They should still land well short of the 96–120 hour reviewed/repair medians if the gate stays near 6 hours. Cases diverted into repair inherit high-value repair rework of 12.5% and depend on three repair-quote technicians. The 22% evidence cohort is not redesigned; if the gate kicks incomplete Standard claims back into today’s evidence loop, those cases can collect both the evidence return (median 26 hours) and a gate delay. If a large share of high-value dispatches lack an R1 token, the quarantine becomes a second eligibility department located after the customer was promised speed. Exact payoff against the residual 9% cost advantage is not knowable from the given costs and rates. The escalation board remains a single daily slot.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A.** First, the rule update raises manual inflow toward the stated 15–35% and utilization crosses 82%. Elapsed time on Manual eligibility review, already median 5 hours and p90 18 hours from intake, stretches for every segment sharing the queue, including Premium low-risk cases that were supposed to stay near 8 hours and under the 24-hour credit line. Second, defect-type scoring remains weak because today’s rules are already outdated. Clear high-value claims are dumped into manual review, or ambiguous claims are still auto-approved, and the complaint rate stays near the fast-path 14.1% while the extra labor is already being paid. Bottleneck migration: the visible pain leaves Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen and reappears as a Fraud/risk review backlog. That team is two specialists, already late on Mondays and after campaigns, with entry p90s of 28 hours from the auto-screen and 44 hours from manual review. A standing multi-day fraud queue will also raise denials above today’s 220, and unsuccessful denials return through Customer complaint/reopen into the same manual pool R3 requires.

**Redesign B.** First, the portal cuts repeats and raises abandonment among customers with low digital literacy by more than the assisted channel can absorb. Today 320 of 390 abandonments already leave from evidence request; a higher abandonment rate makes cycle time and cost look better because difficult cases exit. Second, the one-pass rule denies or thinly approves claims that the second request would have completed. Those customers re-enter through Customer complaint/reopen. The escalation board has one 45-minute slot and already sees 120 transitions; overflow lands on Manual eligibility review 310 times today at median 31 hours. Bottleneck migration: customer wait on the 34-hour mean return edge shifts into assisted-capture staffing. If that staffing is taken from the seven reviewers, the Premium repair-quote path, already flagged as too slow, slows further. A further migration is from “repeat evidence request” to “deny, then reopen,” which consumes the escalation slot the design cannot scale.

**Redesign C.** First, repair campaigns coincide with the gate. Inspectors are underused only outside those campaigns, so the gate drifts from about 6 hours to the 18-hour end, and high-value customers complain about the queue that was built to prevent complaints. Premium high-value, with a 20-hour fast median and strict expectations, feels that drift immediately. Second, most high-value fast approvals lack an R1 token. The quarantine is then a large new queue at the worst moment in the episode, and fraud or manual review becomes the critical path after a fast-approval promise has already been made. Third, mismatches routed to repair replace a dispatch complaint with repair rework, which is already 12.5% on high-value devices, and with quote waits at median 21 hours if the price feed is late or wrong. Bottleneck migration: the long tail after dispatch moves to the inspector release queue and, for mismatches, to the three repair-quote technicians. If master data is incomplete, both the gate and the quote wait fail together, because they were given the same source.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

Run the pilots as a mechanism test. The log currently flatters Fast approval because Quality/compliance close happens before the complaint edges, and because a reopen can look like a new, slow case. A pilot that reports only time-to-first-close will reproduce the contradiction.

**First pilot.** Redesign C, restricted to Standard high-value replacements whose evidence is already complete. Randomize at case level inside that segment. Leave Premium low-risk, the evidence cohort, and the auto-screen rules on the current process. Standard high-value is the right first cohort because the complaint gap is wide (18% fast versus 6% reviewed/repair), the error cost is EUR 410, customers are less credit-sensitive than Premium, and the pilot does not add the 15–35% manual inflow. Staff the gate from quality-inspector time and cap it: if added wait reaches the high end of 6–18 hours, stop adding cases rather than letting the queue become the new path.

Monitor, on the pilot and its holdout, for a follow-up window at least as long as the dispatch-to-complaint p90 of 180 hours:

- Complaint/reopen rate, reshipment, service credit, and escalation entry, counted on the whole episode.
- Share of gated cases with an actual SKU mismatch, and share quarantined for a missing R1 token. This split is the learning goal.
- Added hours at the gate, median and p90, against the 6–18 hour design range.
- Repair rework among diverted cases, against the known 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate.
- Premium low-risk median cycle time and credit exposure, as a spillover canary that should not move.
- Inspector queue in campaign weeks and non-campaign weeks separately.

Go if the Standard high-value episode complaint rate moves from about 18% toward about 6% by a material share of that 12-point gap, added median wait stays nearer 6–12 hours than 18, and reshipment, escalation, and repair rework do not rise by a comparable amount. Stop or narrow the gate if wait sits at about 18 hours, the complaint rate stays in the high teens, or complaints fall only because cases pile up in quarantine or in repair. A gate that finds many SKU mismatches and few R1 escapes means part selection is the main fast-path failure, and a broad Redesign A reroute is a larger intervention than the mechanism requires. A gate that finds few SKU errors and many missing R1 tokens means authorization is the main failure, and Redesign A is the next substantive pilot.

**Second pilot.** Redesign B, on unclear / missing-evidence cases that are not in the Stage 1 high-value dispatch pilot. Overlap cases stay in one pilot only. Run this before any broad rule change, because a real drop in repeat requests frees Manual eligibility review hours, and Redesign A may need those hours. Keep the assisted channel in the pilot from the first week; a portal-only test would reenact the known abandonment risk and call it a result.

Go if, among cases that enter evidence request, the share with two or more requests falls by something in the stated 30–45% relative band, the unclear-cohort median moves down from 140 hours, abandonment does not rise above the current evidence-linked level (320 of 390 recorded abandonments), and manual hours per evidence case fall. Stop if abandonment rises, especially in the low-literacy slice, or if one-pass denials and escalation entries replace the second request one for one. Also stop the “success” reading if portal wait simply replaces the median 26-hour return edge with the same customer delay and the same eventual manual review.

**Third pilot.** Redesign A, only after Stage 1 has shown what share of the complaint gap a part gate already removes, and only up to the manual capacity Stage 2 actually freed. Start with above-EUR-400 claims on ordinary, non-campaign weeks. Route clear defects to Fraud/risk review → Fast approval, not by default to Repair quote. Put a hard stop at 82% manual utilization and a fraud-queue stop: on ordinary weeks, wait to enter fraud review should not settle at today’s p90s of 28–44 hours. Campaign weeks and Mondays are reported separately, because those peaks already exist.

Go if R1 escapes on in-scope claims fall sharply, high-value episode complaint rates improve beyond what the Stage 1 gate achieved alone, Premium low-risk median cycle time stays well below 24 hours (baseline 8 hours), and manual utilization stays at or below 82%. No-go if manual inflow lands in the 15–35% band and the cliff is crossed, if Premium credit exposure rises, or if the complaint improvement is entirely the result of pushing Premium high-value claims onto the 96-hour repair path.

**How to tell a real improvement from a shift.** Score every pilot cohort on episode time and episode cost through a window of at least 180 hours after first close, including reshipment, repair rework, service credit, and a second close. Keep the segment cut: Premium versus Standard, low-risk versus high-value, evidence complete versus not. An average gain can be Premium low-risk getting slower while high-value gets better. Report the three alignment deviations separately, and report both fitness and the percentage of fitting traces (baselines 0.91 and 68%); fitness alone can stay flattering while a third of traces still deviate. Watch the receiving resources at the same time as the customer metrics: manual utilization against 82%, fraud queue by weekday and campaign, inspector queue, escalation slots filled, abandonment, and repair-quote wait. A complaint rate that falls while one of those queues rises by a similar case volume is displacement. Hold out a randomized slice of the same segment so a marketing campaign, which already bottlenecks fraud, is not credited to the redesign. Mid-risk devices should be counted on their own row until their outcomes exist; until then they are not evidence for either lane.
