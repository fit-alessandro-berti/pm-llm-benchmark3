# 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Functional unit:** one fulfilled spare-part order, with results also reported by order segment and by line-item/weight where available. Because the log is flattened at order level, multi-line orders and split shipments should be flagged rather than treated as identical to single-line orders.

**Inside the environmental boundary**

- **Order fulfillment operations**
  - Availability check, partial-shipment and backorder paths.
  - Production planning, batch waiting, CNC_A/CNC_B production, heat treatment, inspection, emergency production, and rework loops.
- **Direct manufacturing resource use**
  - Recorded `energy_kwh`.
  - Recorded `material_scrap_kg`.
  - Rework-production energy and material use, including repeated CNC and inspection events.
- **Packaging**
  - Recorded `package_material_kg`.
  - Packaging rework and the associated material/handling burden.
  - Damage/return effects where linked to Q1 or packaging-route incompatibility.
- **Transport**
  - Recorded `transport_kgco2e` by route mode: parcel ground, parcel air, consolidated truck, dedicated van.
  - Additional shipment legs created by partial shipment/backorder paths.
  - Empty-return exposure for dedicated vans, where observable or estimated through route records.
- **Inventory-related waste proxy**
  - Batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days, treated as an obsolescence/material-risk indicator, not necessarily as confirmed waste.

**Outside the boundary or only partially represented**

- Upstream embodied emissions of purchased raw materials, supplier production, and capital equipment manufacture.
- Warehouse building energy, unless it can be allocated from facility metering.
- Customer-site impacts, including downtime avoided by urgent delivery, except as a service/resilience benefit rather than a quantified carbon credit.
- End-of-life treatment of packaging and parts.
- Full vehicle utilization, return loads, distance, and carrier-specific fuel mix, unless linked transport data are added.
- Customer return events that occur outside the current order lifecycle.

**Key assumptions and uncertainties**

1. Recorded order-level energy, transport emissions, scrap, and packaging material are sufficiently consistent to compare routes and variants, but should not be interpreted as a complete product life-cycle assessment.
2. Lower energy per item from larger batches is not automatically a net environmental gain because waiting, late delivery, emergency production, obsolescence, and unsold inventory may offset it.
3. Air and dedicated-van routes are not categorically inappropriate. They can be justified for true Critical-downtime cases or where remaining lead-time slack and route reliability make lower-carbon modes unsafe.
4. Observed associations are not necessarily causal. For example, CNC_B may serve more difficult or urgent parts, and air may be selected for already high-risk orders. Changes should therefore be piloted with matched comparisons by part, segment, promised lead time, and disruption state.
5. Low process-model precision indicates that disruption and emergency behavior has many variants. Governance should preserve controlled exceptions rather than attempt to eliminate all exceptions.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

A concise dashboard should report indicators by `case_type`, `customer_type`, route, machine group, batch-size band, promised lead time, and disruption condition.

| Energy | Rework energy share | Energy on `Rework production -> CNC production` loops divided by total production energy |
| Transport | Transport kgCO2e per fulfilled order | `transport_kgco2e`, `route_mode`; report separately for ground, air, truck, van |
| Transport | Avoidable expedited-mode rate | Share of air/van shipments where ground or consolidated truck had modeled on-time probability above 85% under SP1 |
| Transport | Multi-leg shipment burden | Number of shipment legs and transport kgCO2e for partial shipment/backorder cases versus complete shipments |
| Materials | Scrap kg per order and per first-pass production | `material_scrap_kg`; distinguish normal scrap from rework-associated scrap |
| Materials | Packaging material kg per order | `package_material_kg`, package type, route mode, part weight/geometry where available |
| Materials | Obsolescence exposure | Share of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days; segment by batch size and forecast quality |

| Resilience | Emergency-path dependence | Emergency production and dedicated-van rate by true downtime versus precautionary urgency |
| Governance | Exception completeness | SP1/SP2/Q1 override events with reason, approver, remaining slack, and outcome |

# 3. Intervention options

## S1. Green shipping recommender using lead-time slack, route reliability, and segment

- **Target path or segment:** Primarily `Stock -> standard pack -> air`, especially Standard-stock Service-contract cases; also non-critical MTO shipments where ground remains viable.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Select parcel ground or consolidated truck when the modeled probability of meeting the promise exceeds 85%, consistent with SP1. The current air path has much higher transport impact than ground: 18.5 versus 3.6 kgCO2e per order in the selected stock paths.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Service can remain protected if the recommendation considers promised lead time, current carrier reliability, priority picking capacity, destination, and disruption status. It should not switch a shipment merely because ground has a lower average footprint. R1 requires keeping a viable alternative route for Service-contract customers.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Route choice versus remaining promise slack; predicted and actual on-time probability; SP1 conformance; route-mode emissions; late-delivery rate after mode substitution; carrier-disruption indicators; customer segment and override reasons.

## S2. Dynamic batching for CNC and heat treatment with segment-specific maximum waiting caps

- **Target path or segment:** `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production`, especially MTO Regular and MTO Service-contract paths; heat-treatment batch waits.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Build batches where they materially reduce energy per item. CNC_A drops from 2.8 kWh per item at batch size 1–3 to 1.3 kWh at batch size 12–20, and heat treatment is more energy-efficient above batch size 10.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Unconstrained batching is unacceptable: batch wait rises from 4h to 38h, and heat-treatment waiting has 1.7 times higher late-delivery risk. Use maximum waiting caps that differ by segment and remaining due-date slack. Critical-downtime should normally bypass batching; Service-contract MTO needs tighter caps than Regular MTO.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Batch-size distribution, batch waiting time, promised-lead-time slack at batch release, late-delivery rate by batch-size band, emergency-production rate caused by batch delay, energy per item, unshipped-after-14-days exposure, and cancellations.

## S3. Shift eligible MTO work from CNC_B to CNC_A

- **Target path or segment:** MTO cases assigned to CNC_B, particularly the `MTO -> batch wait -> CNC_B -> inspection -> rework -> ground` path.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** CNC_B has an 18% rework rate versus 9% on CNC_A. Moving eligible work to CNC_A reduces repeated machining, scrap, inspection load, packaging delay, and potentially emergency transport triggered by production delay.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** CNC_A may have queue or capacity constraints, so shifting all work could worsen lead times and create a single-machine dependency. Use CNC_A only when due-date slack, queue length, part capability, and maintenance status allow. Retain CNC_B as a resilient alternative for surge and disruption conditions, with enhanced quality controls.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Machine assignment, queue/wait time, rework loops, scrap, energy, part family, rush status, due-date performance, CNC_A utilization, CNC_B fallback usage, and machine-down disruption periods.

## S4. Replace avoidable air for Standard-stock Service-contract orders with priority pick plus ground

- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Service-contract orders currently following `Pick from stock -> Package standard -> Ship air`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** This directly addresses the 22% SP1 violation rate in the segment. Priority picking and earlier package release can recover much of the 24-hour transport-time disadvantage of ground without the 4–6 times emissions intensity of air.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** The main risk is treating all Service-contract orders as non-urgent. The switch should occur only when ground plus priority pick retains sufficient slack and a reliable alternative route exists. Air remains a valid contingency during carrier disruption, verified risk of lateness, or contractually critical cases.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Time from availability check to pick, pick-to-package delay, route-mode choice, promise slack, ground reliability by lane, late delivery, SP1 override rate, and customer escalation/complaint signals.

## S5. Consolidated truck departures twice daily for non-urgent stock orders

- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Regular and eligible Service-contract orders currently shipped as individual ground parcels; especially orders with sufficient lead-time slack.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Consolidated truck transport reduces transport emissions by approximately 35–55% versus individual ground parcels.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Consolidation introduces a 12–24-hour wait and therefore should not be used for orders near their promise limit, partial-shipment backorders, or disruption-sensitive Service-contract commitments. Twice-daily departures are preferable to a single daily or less frequent dispatch because they constrain wait accumulation.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Consolidation wait, truck fill rate, mode-switch rate, transport kgCO2e, promised slack at dispatch decision, late-delivery rate, missed-cutoff events, and availability of ground/air alternatives under disruption.

## S6. Packaging-route compatibility checks before shipment

- **Target path or segment:** `Package standard` and `Package reinforced` flows that enter `Rework packaging`; MTO heavy parts and route/geometry-sensitive parts.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Preventing packaging rework avoids duplicate packaging material, handling, delay, and potential damage. The DFG shows 620 packaging-rework events, including returns to both standard and reinforced packaging.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** A digital or physical compatibility check adds a small pre-shipment control but should reduce delay and damage downstream. It must enforce Q1 for MTO parts over 8 kg unless a valid low-damage-risk inspection certification exists.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Packaging choice, part weight and geometry, route mode, `rework_flag`, packaging rework loop frequency, package material kg, Q1 conformance, return/damage rate, and time added by the check.

## S7. Add a risk-based inspection quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations

- **Target path or segment:** MTO production, especially CNC_B and rushed production that leads from `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production`.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Better first-pass detection reduces rework energy, scrap, emergency production, and secondary packaging/transport. This is important because the CNC_B rework-ground path averages 32 kWh and 1.8 kg scrap per order, with rework dominating its impact.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Extra inspection can lengthen cycle time if applied indiscriminately. Use risk scoring based on machine group, part family, batch size, rush status, historical defects, and prior rework. Capacity planning for `inspection_cell` is necessary because queues already contribute to rushed inspections and missed defects.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Inspection queue time, inspection duration, defect detection point, rework rate, scrap, return rate, late delivery, emergency shipment after rework, and first-pass yield by machine/part/rush combination.

## S8. Replace some dedicated vans with shared emergency courier lanes

- **Target path or segment:** Critical-downtime Service-contract orders on `Emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van`, particularly where the urgency is not true production-stopping downtime.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Shared emergency lanes can reduce empty-return exposure and improve vehicle utilization relative to dedicated vans, while preserving faster-than-ground service.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** This must not degrade genuine downtime response. A shared lane is appropriate only if pickup, transit-time reliability, chain-of-custody, and backup capacity meet the required recovery time. Dedicated vans should remain available for verified critical failures, remote destinations, or disrupted carrier networks.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** `expedite_flag`, downtime justification, actual urgency category, transport mode, pickup and delivery times, van return utilization where available, service failures, SP2 justification completeness, and alternative-route availability.

## S9. Split Critical-downtime orders into true downtime and precautionary urgent categories

- **Target path or segment:** The current Critical-downtime Service-contract population, including both dedicated-van and stock/ground exception paths.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Better triage prevents precautionary urgency from defaulting to high-emission emergency production and dedicated vans. It also protects capacity for true downtime events, where high-emission response may be justified by operational resilience.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Misclassification is the central risk. The category must be set with service operations and customer evidence, such as asset status, available on-site spares, downtime cost class, and latest acceptable delivery time. A rapid escalation path is needed if precautionary orders become true downtime.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Category assignment time, category changes, asset/downtime justification events, mode and production path selected, actual delivery outcome, escalation frequency, dedicated-van use, SP2 conformance, and customer/service outcomes.

## S10. Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular MTO customers

- **Target path or segment:** MTO Regular cases, especially immediate CNC plus air paths and cases that otherwise bypass efficient batches.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Where customers can accept a slightly longer promise date, the distributor can use planned CNC_A/heat-treatment batches and ground transport rather than energy-intensive immediate production and air. This targets the contrast between the 70-hour MTO immediate/air path and the 118-hour batch/ground path.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** This is unsuitable for Critical-downtime and should not be imposed on contracted service commitments without agreement. It can improve predictability if presented as a customer choice with transparent delivery and price/service options rather than as a unilateral delay.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Offered versus accepted promise dates, segment, quote-to-order conversion, requested expedite changes, actual lateness against agreed promise, route/machine/batch choices, cancellations, and customer behavior after offer changes.

## S11. Reduce partial shipments through availability-check and allocation rules

- **Target path or segment:** `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait` and partial shipment followed by second shipment.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** Fewer split orders reduce duplicate packaging, transport legs, customer-contact workload, and the high-impact backorder path, which has 7.2 kgCO2e and 0.08 kg scrap per order versus 3.6 kgCO2e and 0.04 kg scrap for stock-ground.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Holding an order for complete shipment can harm service if a customer needs an available line urgently. Use line-level criticality, customer contract rules, expected replenishment time, and marginal emissions to decide between complete shipment, partial shipment, and substitute allocation. The flattened log should be improved with line-item events before broad rollout.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Partial-shipment rate, number of shipment legs, backorder wait, cancellation due to delay, customer/line criticality, fill rate, transport emissions, package material, and late delivery at both order and line level.

## S12. Create a sustainability exception log for SP1, SP2, and Q1 overrides

- **Target path or segment:** All exception paths: air, dedicated van, nonstandard packaging, emergency production, and policy overrides.
- **Mechanism of environmental improvement:** The log does not directly reduce emissions, but it makes avoidable exceptions visible and enables learning. It addresses missing SP2 justifications in 31% of dedicated-van cases and supports root-cause analysis of SP1 and Q1 deviations.
- **Likely service/quality/resilience effect:** Properly designed exception governance preserves the ability to act quickly during real disruptions. It should capture justification with minimal operational friction and allow retrospective review rather than requiring lengthy approval during an active downtime event.
- **Required process-mining evidence to monitor:** Override event, policy identifier, reason code, approver or automated-rule version, remaining lead-time slack, route reliability, customer and case segment, part weight, outcome, and repeat-exception patterns.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching energy savings can create late delivery and emergency recovery.**  
   Larger CNC_A and heat-treatment batches reduce energy per item, but longer batch wait can increase late delivery. A delayed MTO order may then move to immediate production, air, or dedicated transport, offsetting the original energy saving.

2. **Efficient batches can increase obsolescence and embedded material waste.**  
   The evidence already shows that 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong. Larger batches may lower kWh per item while producing unused material, scrap, storage burden, or eventual disposal.

3. **Replacing air with ground can displace emissions into missed-service costs or later expedites.**  
   Ground is lower carbon only when it can reliably meet the promise. If it causes a missed Service-contract commitment, the customer may request a recovery shipment, hold more local inventory, or move demand to a competitor with less transparent impacts.

4. **Truck consolidation can turn lower transport emissions into higher delay and split-order emissions.**  
   A 12–24-hour consolidation wait may cause partial shipments, missed promised dates, or air escalation for a missing line item. Consolidation should therefore be based on remaining slack and complete-order availability, not only truck fill.

5. **Reducing reinforced packaging can lower package material but increase damage and returns.**  
   Reinforced packaging doubles package material, but it reduces damage-related returns by 40% for heavy MTO parts. Relaxing Q1 to save packaging could increase return transport, replacement production, scrap, and customer downtime.

6. **Overusing reinforced packaging can create unnecessary material use and packaging rework.**  
   Applying reinforced packaging to all MTO or all high-risk routes may increase packaging material without further damage benefit and may worsen compatibility problems if route mode or geometry is not checked.

7. **Shifting from CNC_B to CNC_A can create a new queue and concentration risk.**  
   Lower rework on CNC_A may be offset if its queue grows, causing late delivery, emergency production, or loss of resilience during CNC_A maintenance or failure. CNC_B should be improved and retained as controlled surge capacity.

8. **More inspection can reduce scrap but create an inspection bottleneck.**  
   A universal extra inspection gate could extend queue times at the inspection cell, causing rushed releases later. The result could be more expedites or even more defects if rushed inspections recur. Inspection must be risk-based and capacity-managed.

9. **Reducing partial shipments can worsen customer outcomes for truly urgent line items.**  
   Holding the entire order until complete can avoid a second shipment, but may cause equipment downtime if one available critical part is withheld. Line-level criticality is essential; flattened order-level data are insufficient for a blanket rule.

10. **Customer-facing carbon options can shift demand toward more urgency.**  
    If green promise dates are perceived as slow or unreliable, customers may choose premium/express options more often, place duplicate orders, or increase precautionary stock. The program should offer dependable alternatives, not simply longer waits.

11. **Restricting dedicated vans may reduce near-term emissions but reduce recovery capacity.**  
    Shared emergency lanes can be lower impact, but during a carrier disruption or true plant downtime, a dedicated van may be the resilient option. Eliminating the capability rather than reducing inappropriate use creates operational risk.

12. **Exception controls can cause informal workarounds.**  
    If SP1/SP2/Q1 approval is slow or punitive, teams may misclassify urgency, omit events, or use unlogged workarounds. Exceptions need rapid capture, clear reasons, and retrospective learning rather than bureaucratic delay.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a **segmented, slack-aware fulfillment redesign** rather than a blanket “lowest-carbon route” policy.

## Core design

1. **Make route and production decisions from one decision rule**
   - At availability check and production planning, calculate remaining promised-lead-time slack, route reliability, part criticality, customer segment, production queue, batch opportunity, and packaging requirement.
   - Use this to choose among stock/ground, consolidated truck, air, planned production, immediate production, shared emergency courier, and dedicated van.
   - The rule should explicitly preserve R1: Service-contract customers must retain at least one alternative route during disruptions.

2. **Prioritize the largest avoidable transport opportunity first**
   - Deploy S1 and S4 for Standard-stock Service-contract orders. These orders have relatively low production energy but elevated transport emissions, and SP1 is violated in 22% of the segment.
   - Replace air with priority pick plus ground only where the model indicates that ground can meet the commitment with at least 85% probability.
   - Keep air as an approved exception for inadequate slack, lane unreliability, carrier disruption, or explicit service-critical need.

3. **Use consolidation only for genuinely non-urgent stock flow**
   - Implement S5 with twice-daily consolidated truck departures for eligible Regular stock orders and selected Service-contract orders with ample slack.
   - Do not send near-due, partial, or disruption-sensitive orders into a consolidation queue merely to improve aggregate transport metrics.

4. **Reduce hidden production impact by addressing rework before chasing marginal energy gains**
   - Implement S3 and S7 together: move eligible MTO work toward CNC_A when its queue permits, while applying a risk-based inspection gate to high-rework CNC_B/rushed combinations.
   - The high-impact CNC_B rework path has 32 kWh and 1.8 kg scrap per order. Avoiding rework is likely to improve energy, material, quality, and downstream transport simultaneously.
   - Do not eliminate CNC_B; maintain it as surge and contingency capacity, and use the evidence to identify which machine/part/rush combinations require process improvement.

5. **Use dynamic batching with hard service and obsolescence guardrails**
   - Implement S2 for MTO Regular first, then carefully extend to Service-contract MTO.
   - Set maximum batch-wait caps by segment and remaining slack. Release production before a cap breach rather than waiting indefinitely for an “optimal” energy batch.
   - Add an obsolescence control: batch size should be limited by forecast confidence, firm demand, and the observed 14-day unshipped rate. Energy per item should not be optimized in isolation.

6. **Make packaging a quality and circularity control, not merely a material-reduction target**
   - Implement S6 and enforce Q1. Use reinforced packaging for MTO parts over 8 kg unless low-damage-risk inspection certification is present.
   - Focus on eliminating packaging rework and incompatible package-route combinations. This reduces material use without sacrificing the damage protection that prevents returns and replacement production.

7. **Protect true downtime while reducing precautionary urgency**
   - Combine S8 and S9. Split Critical-downtime cases into verified downtime and precautionary urgent categories.
   - Keep emergency production and dedicated vans for verified downtime, remote or unreliable lanes, and major disruptions.
   - For precautionary urgency, use stock allocation, shared emergency courier lanes, or priority ground where service evidence supports it. This preserves emergency capacity for events where it has the greatest resilience value.

8. **Reduce split shipments with line-level decision logic**
   - Implement S11 after enriching the log with line-item criticality and shipment-leg data.
   - Avoid partial shipment where the missing line is expected soon and the customer gains little from a first delivery. Preserve partial shipment where an available line is operationally critical.

## Why this package is balanced

The package addresses the main impact sources in their process context:

- **Transport:** avoidable air in Standard-stock Service-contract orders and unnecessary dedicated-van use are reduced, without denying urgent-response capability.
- **Energy:** efficient batching is used selectively, but not at the expense of due-date performance or inventory obsolescence.
- **Materials:** packaging rework, production rework, and avoidable split shipments are reduced; reinforced packaging is preserved where it prevents more damaging downstream waste.
- **Quality:** quality gates and Q1 conformance reduce the repeated production and scrap visible in rework loops.
- **Service:** promised lead time, p90 delay, and cancellation risk act as guardrails rather than being traded away for carbon metrics.
- **Resilience:** alternate routes, CNC_B fallback, air contingency, and dedicated-van capability remain available for verified exceptions and carrier disruption.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

## A. Establish a baseline and decision cohorts

Before rollout, create a baseline by:

- `case_type` and `customer_type`;
- promised-lead-time band and remaining slack at each decision point;
- route mode and transport lane;
- machine group, part family, and batch-size band;
- package type, part weight, and geometry;
- normal versus disruption periods;
- first-pass versus rework loops;
- complete versus partial shipment cases.

Use matched cohorts rather than comparing all ground orders with all air orders. For example, compare air and ground candidates with similar customer segment, destination/lane, promised lead time, stock availability, and disruption status.

## B. Pilot design and phased rollout

1. **Pilot S1/S4 in Standard-stock Service-contract orders**
   - Start with lanes where ground reliability is well measured.
   - Apply the 85% on-time rule, with priority picking as part of the intervention rather than merely changing the transport label.
   - Maintain air as a documented fallback.

2. **Pilot S5 in non-urgent Standard-stock Regular orders**
   - Use twice-daily truck consolidation.
   - Exclude cases with low slack, partial-shipment risk, or uncertain carrier capacity.

3. **Pilot S3/S7/S2 in selected MTO part families**
   - Compare CNC_A assignment and risk-based inspection against matched CNC_B/current-state cases.
   - Introduce batch-wait caps before raising target batch size.
   - Monitor unshipped-after-14-days exposure before expanding batch policies.

4. **Pilot S8/S9 with service operations**
   - Require a lightweight urgency classification and justification event.
   - Test shared emergency courier lanes only where their actual pickup and delivery performance can be demonstrated.

## C. Post-implementation event-log validation

For each pilot and rollout wave, compare pre/post and treatment/control cohorts on:

- **Environmental outcomes**
  - `energy_kwh` per fulfilled order and first-pass item;
  - `transport_kgco2e` per fulfilled order and per shipment leg;
  - `material_scrap_kg`;
  - `package_material_kg`;
  - rework energy and scrap share;
  - unshipped-after-14-days batch exposure.

- **Process outcomes**
  - Median and p90 cycle time;
  - time in batch wait, inspection queue, packaging, and consolidation wait;
  - frequency of emergency production, air, dedicated van, partial shipment, and packaging rework;
  - number of shipment legs per order.

- **Service and quality guardrails**
  - Late delivery, cancellation due to delay, and customer escalation rate;
  - first-pass yield, production rework loops, packaging rework, damage/return events;
  - Q1 compliance and outcomes for parts over 8 kg;
  - service performance by Regular, Service-contract, MTO, Standard-stock, and Critical-downtime segments.

- **Resilience guardrails**
  - R1 alternate-route availability for every Service-contract shipment;
  - route performance during carrier disruptions;
  - dependency on any single machine, carrier, consolidation departure, or emergency courier lane;
  - time to recover from disruption and use of controlled fallback routes.

Use statistical process-control charts and segmented trend analysis, with special review of p90 service performance because median improvement can hide deterioration for urgent orders.

## D. Conformance monitoring for SP1, SP2, and Q1

| Policy | Automated check | Required evidence | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| SP1: ground/truck when on-time probability exceeds 85% | At shipment decision, compare selected route with modeled feasible lower-carbon modes | Remaining slack, predicted on-time probabilities, carrier reliability, selected route, override reason | Review repeated avoidable air use by lane, planner, customer segment, and disruption status |
| SP2: dedicated van requires Critical-downtime justification | Require a justification event before or immediately after dispatch in emergency conditions | Downtime category, asset status, latest acceptable delivery time, alternative-route assessment, approver/automatic rule | Investigate missing justification events, especially the current 31% gap; distinguish documentation failure from inappropriate use |
| Q1: MTO parts over 8 kg require reinforced packaging unless certified low risk | Validate package type against weight and inspection certification | Part weight, packaging type, inspection low-damage certification, route mode, geometry constraints | Block non-compliant release where possible; review any override linked to damage, return, or rework |

The conformance dashboard should show not only violation counts, but also **outcomes after exceptions**: whether the air shipment prevented lateness, whether the van addressed true downtime, and whether the Q1 override caused a return or packaging rework loop.

## E. Exception governance

Create the S12 exception record with structured fields:

- policy overridden: SP1, SP2, Q1, or another rule;
- case and line-item identifier;
- `case_type`, `customer_type`, customer contract status;
- promised lead time, remaining slack, and actual decision timestamp;
- route reliability/disruption state;
- machine queue and batch status where production is involved;
- part weight, geometry, and package selection;
- reason code: true downtime, contractual commitment, carrier disruption, quality risk, lack of capacity, customer instruction, or other;
- selected fallback and alternative routes considered;
- approver, automated-rule version, and later outcome.

Governance should be **fast at execution and rigorous in review**:

- Allow rapid emergency override for true downtime.
- Require completion of the justification event shortly after dispatch if immediate entry is impractical.
- Review recurrent exceptions weekly by operations, service, quality, and sustainability leads.
- Trigger root-cause analysis when a lane, machine, package type, or customer segment repeatedly creates SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides.
- Treat repeated exceptions as redesign signals: for example, insufficient priority-pick capacity, poor ground-carrier reliability, recurring CNC_B quality issues, or packaging master-data gaps.

The redesign should be considered successful only if environmental indicators improve **while** late delivery, cancellations, rework/returns, Q1 compliance, and Service-contract route resilience remain within agreed guardrails.
