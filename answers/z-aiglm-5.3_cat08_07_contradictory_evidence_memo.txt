## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### High fitness but low precision
Fitness asks whether the model can replay observed cases; precision asks whether it forbids behavior not observed. The pilot model replays nearly everything (0.94) because much of the volume follows the new simplified coordinator path (Address validation  Combined validate/schedule at 38%). But R legalized optionality — survey skipped or not, activation before or after billing, standard or manual activation, zero or more second visits — and each option multiplies paths, so the discovered model becomes permissive and precision collapses (0.51). Falling generalization (0.56) adds that many variants rest on thin case support. Together: a highly replayable but weakly standardized process. Practical consequence: the discovered model can no longer tell coordinators what *should* happen, so targeted conformance rules (M7) must replace model-based control.

### Improved median but worse tail
The median is set by the bulk of cases; p90/p95 are set by the failure-and-recovery sub-process. R improved the bulk (merged steps, fewer handovers, skipped surveys on the happy path) while degrading failure handling on both margins: more cases enter failure (FTR 6pp; failed-visit edge +6pp) and recovery got slower (second visits +7pp; failed  second-visit delay up under coordinator load; billing  complaint as a new long-tail relation). Prevention is cheap, early, and planned; remediation is expensive, late, and unplanned. The duration distribution became more skewed — a faster typical case and a much worse unlucky case. This is also why SLA breach rose overall while falling for simple houses.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience
Handovers are interfaces: each is a chance to drop context (defect) *and* a chance to duplicate knowledge (redundancy). R removed interfaces — the handover network simplified and 3+ handover cases fell by two-thirds — cutting handoff defects and speeding the median. But the working-together network centralized on coordinators and activation specialists, so context now lives in one head. At 93% utilization there is no slack for absence or surges; cases stall when a coordinator is out, and a new ownership gap emerged after billing start. Efficiency and resilience traded off exactly as the network structure predicts. The ownership model scales only if resilience is deliberately rebuilt (load caps, trained backups, shared case notes).

### Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost
Prevention costs are early, certain, and inside the measurement window (survey team utilization 78%  52%). Failure costs are late, probabilistic, and partly outside it (second visits, manual activation, complaint handling — excluded after pilot week 4). By dropping FTR to 76%, R raised the expected failure bill: the fuller estimate is €207 vs €198, and it is still incomplete, so true full cost is likely worse. The €23 visible saving is real cash avoided on the happy path, but much of the cost was not eliminated — it was deferred and relocated into rework, disputes, and complaints. The utilization shift (survey down, second-visit team up to 92%) is the physical signature of this relocation.

### Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings
R's mechanisms embed assumptions that hold selectively. Merged validation/scheduling assumes a clean address — true for low-risk houses. The skip rule assumes an accurate risk classifier — it fails on medium-risk apartments (FTR 8pp, p90 +12 days; "installation repeated without preceding survey" in the alignments). Permit-sensitive buildings require checks that were omitted (FTR 11pp, p90 +25 days). Business customers are sensitive to billing-before-activation; rural installs need flexible rescheduling, which fewer handovers hinder. R effectively transferred value from complex segments to the simple segment; aggregates — already blurred by the apartment-heavy pilot mix — hide this. The correct lens is segment-stratified, and it says: deploy by segment, not by average.

## 4. Decision alternatives

### A. Scale Redesign R nationally — **Reject now**
- **Advantages:** locks in median and handover gains at full scale; one operating model; momentum; simple houses benefit broadly.
- **Risks:** nationalizes the tail regression (p95 +17 days), rework (second visits +7pp, manual activation +8pp), complaints (+3.7pp), and two compliance exposures (billing before activation; missing permits); coordinator saturation (93% in two regions) would worsen at scale; full cost likely above baseline; brand damage from "installed but not working."
- **Reasonable only if:** later evidence shows the regressions were artifacts of mix or measurement (not supported today), or if "national" means the eligible simple-house segment only, with gates reinstated — which is Alternative E.

### B. Roll back to baseline — **Reject as a blanket option**
- **Advantages:** immediately restores FTR, tail, complaints, and compliance posture; removes coordinator overload.
- **Risks:** forfeits demonstrated gains (simple houses: 6 median days, +3pp FTR, 1pp complaints); reinstates known baseline pain (waiting complaints; 34% of cases with 3+ handovers); wastes pilot learning; credibility cost.
- **Reasonable if:** a regulator classifies billing-before-activation or missing permits as intolerable and gating cannot be implemented quickly; containment fails to stabilize metrics by the pre-committed checkpoint; or national mix analysis shows the beneficiary segment is negligible.

### C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skip rule and post-billing activation — **Partially adopt**
- **Advantages:** directly neutralizes the two mechanisms indicted by the alignment diagnostics; retains the accountability gain; fast.
- **Risks:** the saturation/resilience problem (93% utilization; stalls on absence; slower recovery under load) belongs to the ownership model itself, not to the two rules; scaling ownership to all segments before fixing load would replicate it; ownership benefits for complex segments are unproven once the rules are suspended.
- **Reasonable if:** coordinator capacity is expanded and backup ownership built first (utilization  85%), with staged, monitored extension. The two suspensions are adopted in our recommendation; broad ownership scaling is deferred.

### D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement — **Adopt as a component, not a substitute**
- **Advantages:** clean causal estimates per component per segment; resolves mix and seasonality confounds (apartment-heavy regions; attributes incomplete before pilot week 3); produces the evidence national scaling actually needs.
- **Risks:** 6–10+ weeks of delay; running known-harmful variants for experimental cleanliness is costly and unfair to customers; only four regions, limiting power; requires instrumentation (timestamps, reason codes) that doesn't exist yet.
- **Reasonable when:** paired with immediate containment — experiment on the safe variants (ownership models, a recalibrated skip rule), not on configurations already shown to harm customers.

### E. Hybrid — segment-gated deployment with controls and measurement — **Recommended**
- **Advantages:** aligns deployment with the strongest signal in the data (heterogeneous segment effects); captures the proven win now; contains compliance and rework exposure immediately; preserves learning and momentum; lowest-regret path under current uncertainty.
- **Risks:** dual operating model adds complexity and routing-error risk (an apartment misrouted as simple inherits skip-rule harm — mitigated by M6-validated routing, M7 guardrails, survey fallback); national segment mix is unknown; does not by itself resolve whether the ownership model scales; coordinator capacity must absorb added simple-house volume.
- **Reasonable if:** the routing rule is validated, gates are reinstated, caseload caps are live, and rollout thresholds are pre-committed — as specified in Section 6.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

**Principle:** information value = probability the result changes a decision × size of the consequence, minus cost and delay. Decisions already forced by current evidence — stopping billing-before-activation and missing permit checks — have near-zero VoI: act, don't measure. The live decisions are (1) routing eligibility for the simple-house rollout, (2) whether the skip rule can ever return, (3) whether R's economics survive full-cost accounting, (4) how to fix the tail, and (5) whether the ownership model scales.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty resolved | How it could change the decision | Why this rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M6** — validate building-risk rule vs. survey outcomes | Accuracy of the risk classifier on medium-risk addresses; reliability of the intake routing rule E depends on | If unfixable  skip rule permanently retired, surveys standard for medium risk; if recalibrable  M1 can test safe reintroduction, recovering a core efficiency lever | Two live decisions hinge on it; retrospective, cheap, fast; gates M1 |
| 2 | **M3** — reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit | Actual causes of rework (currently inferred only from alignments/DFG) | Reprioritizes fixes; could reveal causes unrelated to R (e.g., network issues behind manual activation), altering containment; turns hypotheses into monitored KPIs | Lowest cost, immediate, multiplies the value of every other measurement |
| 3 | **M4** — full downstream cost, 60 days post billing | True cost per case; whether the €23 visible saving survives complaint/revisit costs | Economics-based go/no-go even for the simple-house rollout; verdict on whether deferred activation is ever admissible; calibrates the €198 threshold | Management's decision currency; 60-day lag means the clock must start now |
| 4 | **M1** — randomized skip trial by risk stratum | Causal, confound-free segment effects of a recalibrated skip rule | Whether skipping returns, for which strata, with what guardrails; quantifies the safe boundary | Definitive, but must follow M6; needs 6–8 weeks |
| 5 | **M2** — instrument start timestamps | Where tail time accrues (queue vs. execution); validity of temporal findings currently resting on sparse starts | Designs the tail fix; tests whether deferred activation saved real time once waiting time is visible | Cheap to instrument, but signal accumulates over weeks; near-tie with M1 |
| 6 | **M7** — conformance monitoring (billing-before-activation; permit absence) | Little new information (frequency already known); verifies containment holds | Triggers re-suspension if violations persist; supplies compliance evidence for rollout gates | Ranked mid as *information* — but deploy immediately as a *control* |
| 7 | **M5** — coordinator vs. shared-team ownership under absence | Resilience of the single-owner model at scale | Shapes the national operating model (deputies vs. team ownership) | Important before broad scaling, but load caps and backups are prudent regardless; absence is hard to randomize — use a natural experiment |
| 8 | **M8** — split CX: speed-to-appointment vs. working service | How customers weigh the two outcomes R trades off | Refines SLAs, targets, and where to invest next | Complaint themes already indicate direction; fold into the next survey wave; limited near-term decision leverage |

**Sequencing:** Weeks 0–2: M6 (retrospective), M3 and M7 live, M4 clock starts, M2 instrumented. Weeks 4–8: M1 trial with the recalibrated rule; M5 as a backup-rotation natural experiment; M8 in the next survey. Checkpoints at ~week 6 (routing hardening), ~week 10 (M1 interim, M4 30-day read), ~week 14 (go/no-go on extension).

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E, implemented as contain  scale  measure  re-test.**

**Immediate action (weeks 0–2):**
- Suspend the medium-risk survey-skip rule in all regions; medium-risk addresses return to standard surveys.
- Reinstate the activation-before-billing gate for all cases; audit pilot-period old-building cases for missing permits and remediate (compliance exposure).
- Cap coordinator caseloads (target  85% utilization); name and train backups; require shared case notes so cases survive coordinator absence.
- Rebalance field capacity: survey team (52%) absorbs reinstated surveys; temporarily augment the second-visit team (92%) to clear the backlog driving the tail.
- Profile the national order book by the same segmentation to size the eligible simple-house population before scaling.

**What to pause:** national scaling; extension of R to apartments, old buildings, business, and rural segments; survey skipping; post-billing activation.

**What to scale now:** coordinator ownership plus merged validation/scheduling for simple low-risk houses only, launched region by region under M7 conformance guardrails, with a routing fallback — any case misrouted and failing reverts to survey plus standard activation.

**What to measure next:** immediately M6, M3, M7, M4, M2; from ~week 4, the M1 randomized skip trial; M5 and M8 in parallel.

**Thresholds/evidence justifying broader rollout (pre-committed):** extend R to a segment only after a controlled 8-week extension wave shows: first-time-right  82%; complaints  6.1%; second visits  11%; manual activation  7%; p90  31 days; zero billing-before-activation; zero missing permit checks; coordinator utilization  85%; 60-day full cost per case  €198 mix-adjusted; NPS  +21. National rollout requires two consecutive compliant waves, including at least one apartment/old-building-heavy region. Re-extension of survey skipping additionally requires M6 to show the recalibrated rule is safe and M1 to show non-inferiority (e.g., incremental failed visits < 2pp; segment p90 within +2 days of control). If the simple-house rollout itself regresses versus baseline on first-time-right or complaints, revert that element — targeted rollback, not blanket.
