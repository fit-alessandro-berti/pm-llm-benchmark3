## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee
**Re:** Redesign R (pilot): next-step decision
**Decision requested:** Scale, roll back, experiment, or invest in measurement

**Recommendation:** Adopt Alternative E (hybrid). Do not scale Redesign R nationally, and do not roll it back wholesale. Keep R only where the evidence shows benefit, suspend the mechanisms that drove the harm, and run a 90-day controlled test before any wider rollout.

**Why not scale now.** Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, and 3+ handover cases fell from 34% to 12%. But the tail worsened: p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45 to 62 days. First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation rose from 7% to 15%, and second visits rose from 11% to 18%. The full-cost estimate rose from EUR 198 to EUR 207, while visible cost fell. The harms are concentrated in apartments and permit-sensitive old buildings (together 47% of the pilot), and they trace to three specific mechanisms: the survey-skip rule, missing permit checks, and activation after billing start.

**Why not roll back.** Simple, low-risk houses (34% of pilot cases) improved on median (-6.0 days), p90 (-8.0 days), first-time-right (+3pp), and complaints (-1pp). Coordinator ownership also reduced handovers. Rolling back would discard these gains and would not address the underlying design faults.

**Immediate actions (next 2 weeks)**
1. Suspend survey skipping for apartments and old buildings. Restore field survey for all non-simple cases.
2. Block billing start until a successful activation test is recorded.
3. Enforce permit checks for old buildings.
4. Cap coordinator load at about 85% and require shared case notes plus a named backup owner, to prevent stalls during absence.
5. Limit R to simple, low-risk houses. Keep business and rural cases on baseline until they are evaluated separately.

**Measurement (30–90 days).** Run a randomized survey-skip test stratified by building risk. Instrument start timestamps and reason codes. Measure full 60-day downstream cost after billing start. Validate the building-risk rule against survey outcomes. Test coordinator ownership against shared-team ownership under absence.

**Trade-off.** We accept a slower national benefit and some continued coordinator effort in exchange for avoiding a tail, rework, and complaint expansion whose full cost is currently under-measured. If the gates in Section 6 are not met, we roll back the affected segments. We will not expand on the basis of median gains alone.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness, low precision.** Fitness measures whether logged behavior can be replayed by the model. Pilot fitness is high (0.94) because the discovered model is flexible enough to reproduce many paths: skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start in varying orders. Precision fell (0.79  0.51) because the model also permits many combinations that the pilot did not observe, and generalization is low (0.56). The high fitness therefore reflects model flexibility, not process quality. It indicates that R created multiple unstable paths, which is consistent with the move-on-log alignments (billing before activation, repeated visits without survey).

**Median improved, tail worsened.** The median reflects the bulk of cases, and simple houses are the largest beneficiary and the fastest group. The p90 and p95 reflect the slowest cases, which are concentrated in apartments and old buildings, where survey skipping and missing permit checks generate second visits and failed-visit delays. Coordinator utilization at 93% likely adds queuing delay on top of these process failures. Improving the middle of the distribution while lengthening the right tail is consistent with a mixture of segments with opposite effects, not a single uniform change.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** Handovers fell because one coordinator owns each case. This simplifies the handover-of-work network but centralizes the working-together network around coordinators and activation specialists. When a coordinator is absent, context is not shared, so cases stall. Fewer handovers improve accountability in normal conditions but create a single point of failure. Resilience is a separate dimension from efficiency and is not captured in the headline metrics.

**Lower visible cost, higher full cost.** Visible cost falls because survey visits are skipped, and activation is moved after billing start. These costs do not disappear; they reappear as second visits (+7pp), manual activations (+8pp), and complaint handling, which the cost data excludes after week 4. The full-cost estimate (EUR 207 vs EUR 198) is incomplete, so the true gap is uncertain and may be larger.

**Simple-house improvement, apartment/old-building deterioration.** Segment effects are opposite in sign and differ in mechanism. Simple houses do not require the survey or permit steps that R removed, so skipping those steps costs little there. Apartments and old buildings do require them, so removing them creates rework. A single national rule therefore cannot capture both effects. The pilot's regional mix, with more complex buildings than the baseline, further inflates the apparent aggregate harm and confounds the comparison.

## 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
- *Advantages:* Captures the median and handover gains; simplest operationally; consistent with the strongest segment (simple houses).
- *Risks:* Extends the p95 deterioration (62 days) and the 30-day complaint increase to all regions; repeats the survey and permit errors at larger scale; coordinator overload may worsen; the cost trend is adverse once hidden costs are included.
- *Reasonable only if:* Segment-level tail and rework effects are shown to be negligible under controlled conditions, full cost is shown to be lower, and coordinator capacity is secured. None of these conditions is currently met.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process.**
- *Advantages:* Restores known tail performance and first-time-right; removes compliance-like risks (billing before activation, missing permits); no transition risk.
- *Risks:* Forfeits the simple-house gains (median -6 days, p90 -8 days); keeps 34% handover cases; the baseline has its own capacity constraints.
- *Reasonable only if:* Segment-specific fixes cannot be designed and tested within a reasonable period, or if the controlled test shows the coordinator model is unsafe even for simple houses.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
- *Advantages:* Keeps the handover reduction, which is the least risky component; removes the two mechanisms most directly linked to rework and billing disputes.
- *Risks:* Coordinator utilization (93%) and resilience problems remain; without survey skipping, some efficiency gains are lost; the coordinator-absence failure mode is not yet tested.
- *Reasonable if:* Coordinator load is capped and backup ownership is confirmed, and the absence test shows shared-team ownership is no better.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better measurement only.**
- *Advantages:* Produces causal evidence; resolves the largest uncertainties; avoids committing to a direction on incomplete data.
- *Risks:* Leaves known harms running during the test, unless the harmful mechanisms are paused; slower decision; may be read as inaction.
- *Reasonable if:* Harms are contained during the experiment, which requires pausing the survey-skip and post-billing mechanisms anyway.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and measure before wider scale.**
- *Advantages:* Captures benefits where they are demonstrated; limits harm where it is documented; generates causal evidence through a randomized test; uses explicit gates for expansion.
- *Risks:* More operational complexity (segment routing); benefits accrue slowly; requires sustained governance; risk classification errors may leak harm into the wrong segment.
- *Reasonable when:* Segment effects are heterogeneous, as they are here, and the key uncertainties (rule accuracy, true cost, causal survey effect) can be resolved within 90 days. This is the case.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranking reflects how much each measurement could change the decision (A–E) and how quickly it resolves uncertainty.

**1. M1 – Randomized survey-skip test by building-risk stratum.** *Highest value.* Resolves whether skipping the survey causes rework, separately from selection effects in the pilot. If skipping is harmless for medium-risk apartments, the decision moves toward a restricted E or even C with a broader skip rule. If it causes rework across strata, the skip rule is removed permanently and the decision moves toward C or B for those segments.

**2. M4 – Full 60-day downstream cost after billing start.** *Very high value.* Resolves whether the visible savings are real. The current EUR 9 gap is an incomplete estimate. If full cost is clearly lower than baseline, the economic case for R strengthens and A becomes more plausible once quality gates pass. If full cost is materially higher, B or C becomes the default.

**3. M6 – Validate the building-risk predictive rule against survey outcomes.** *High value.* Resolves whether the rule correctly identifies which cases can skip the survey. If the rule is accurate, skipping can be limited to genuinely low-risk cases and the apartment harm may shrink. If inaccurate, the rule must be retired and the apartment and old-building problems are structural.

**4. M2 – Instrument start timestamps for installation visit, activation test, and manual activation.** *High value for diagnosis.* Current logs have sparse start times, which prevents separating queue waiting from work time. This determines whether the tail is caused by coordinator queuing (favoring capacity fixes and C) or by process steps (favoring design changes and E). It is also needed to test the temporal-profile findings.

**5. M3 – Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint escalation, and second visit.** *High value, low cost.* Converts the root-cause question from speculation into counts. It tests whether permit absence and billing-before-activation explain most rework. It also makes M1 and M6 interpretable.

**6. M7 – Conformance monitoring for billing before activation test and permit-check absence.** *Medium decision value, but immediate and cheap.* It is primarily a guardrail. It does not change the strategic choice, but it verifies compliance with the suspensions in Section 6 and provides early warning.

**7. M5 – Coordinator ownership vs shared-team ownership under coordinator absence.** *Medium value.* Resolves whether the resilience concern is material and whether the handover benefit can be kept without a single point of failure. It determines whether C can be scaled safely, or whether the ownership model should be modified before any expansion.

**8. M8 – Separate customer experience measures for "speed to appointment" and "working service after installation."** *Lower decision value.* Helps interpret NPS (+21  +17) and complaint shifts, and clarifies whether customers are reacting to speed or to service quality. It refines messaging and prioritization but is unlikely to change the choice among A–E on its own.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E (hybrid), executed as a 90-day controlled program. No national scale-up now. No full rollback.**

**Immediate actions (next 2 weeks)**
- Suspend survey skipping for apartments and old buildings.
- Suspend billing start before a successful activation test (no billing before activation).
- Enforce permit checks for old buildings.
- Cap coordinator utilization at about 85%; assign named backup owners and shared case notes.
- Restrict R to simple, low-risk houses. Return business and rural cases to baseline pending separate evaluation.
- Start conformance monitoring (M7) and reason-code capture (M3) immediately.

**What to pause**
- National rollout planning for Redesign R.
- Survey-skip rule in any segment except as part of the randomized test (M1).
- Post-billing activation checks.
- Coordinator model expansion into apartments, old buildings, business, or rural cases.

**What to scale**
- Coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple, low-risk houses, provided the coordinator load cap is respected. This is the segment with demonstrated benefit (median -6 days, p90 -8 days, FTR +3pp).

**What to measure next (in priority order)**
1. M1: randomized survey-skip test stratified by building risk.
2. M4: full 60-day downstream cost after billing start.
3. M6: validation of the building-risk rule.
4. M2: start-timestamp instrumentation.
5. M3: structured reason codes.
6. M5: absence test, coordinator vs shared ownership.
7. M7 (continuous) and M8 (ongoing customer-experience split).

**Thresholds for broader rollout** (proposed; to be pre-registered with management)
A segment may move from controlled to broader rollout only if, over the 90-day test, all of the following hold relative to baseline in that segment:
- p90 cycle time is no more than 2 days worse, and p95 is not worse by more than 5 days.
- First-time-right is no more than 2pp lower.
- 30-day complaint rate is no more than 1pp higher.
- Manual activation is at or below 8%, and billing before activation test is effectively zero.
- Second-visit rate is at or below baseline (11%).
- Full 60-day cost per case, including complaint and revisit cost, is lower than baseline.
- Coordinator utilization stays below 85%, and a coordinator-absence test shows no stall beyond the baseline tolerance.

**Rollback trigger.** If any segment fails two or more thresholds at day 90, roll that segment back to baseline. If the simple-house segment also fails on full cost or coordinator resilience, the decision reverts to B for that segment.

**Acknowledged trade-off.** This path delays national benefit and sustains some coordinator and measurement effort. In return, it avoids expanding a tail, rework, and complaint pattern whose full cost is not yet measured, and it keeps the segment gains that the pilot has demonstrated.
