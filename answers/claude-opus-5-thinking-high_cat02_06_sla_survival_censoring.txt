# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

## 1.1 Time definitions

**Time origin (t = 0).** The `Submit` event of each case. Every prefix in the extract begins with `Submit`, so the origin is uniform and no left truncation is present at the case level. `duration_or_age_h` is therefore measured on a common clock and I treat it as elapsed calendar hours, not working hours (Assumption A1, below).

**Time scale.** Elapsed hours since `Submit`. The SLA is an *elapsed-time* SLA, so no calendar/business-hour deflation is applied.

**Horizon.** τ = 72 h. This is a fixed, pre-specified horizon, which matters: at a fixed horizon the only cases whose status is genuinely unknown are those censored *strictly before* τ. In this extract that is only five cases — S31 (23 h), S15 (35 h), S10 (47 h), S29 (51 h), S24 (67 h). The four heavily stalled cases (S09 92 h, S11 81 h, S25 79 h, S26 104 h) are censored *after* τ, so their status *at 72 h* is fully observed (undisposed). This single observation does most of the work in bounding the answer and is why the 72 h estimands are far more robust than any estimand defined at, say, 168 h.

**Observation cutoff.** One administrative freeze date, unknown in absolute terms. `open` rows are administratively censored at their age; terminal rows are fully observed.

**Assumptions (labelled, with reasons):**

- **A1 — elapsed-calendar clock.** No shift calendars are supplied, so I cannot convert to working hours. Needed because otherwise durations are uninterpretable. Consequence: apparent "stalls" may be nights/weekends.
- **A2 — single common cutoff.** Stated in the prompt. Needed so that age is a valid censoring time. Consequence: staggered entry cannot be modelled; a case with age 104 h entered earlier than one with age 23 h, so the age distribution of open cases is confounded with entry cohort.
- **A3 — the 32 cases are the complete frozen extract**, i.e. no length-biased or truncated sampling beyond the cutoff. Needed for the risk sets to be valid. This is the least verifiable assumption: if the extract selected only cases *submitted* in a window, cases submitted late in the window cannot be old, which biases long-duration estimates downward.
- **A4 — prefixes are complete and ordered** for the events they list, but **intermediate event times are unavailable**. Needed to justify prefix-derived state features while forbidding any duration-based intra-case feature.
- **A5 — internal-resolution focus.** The organisation's SLA is defined on internal `Resolve`. Whatever happens to a case after `Transfer` is outside the measured system. This assumption is what makes `transferred` genuinely ambiguous, and I therefore vary it rather than fix it.

## 1.2 The estimands

"Probability of breaching the SLA" resolves into at least four distinct quantities. All four are computed at τ = 72 h from the same risk sets; they differ only in what counts as the event and what counts as a competing outcome.

**E1 — Non-resolution by 72 h (cause-specific cumulative incidence complement).**
E1 = 1 − F_resolve(72), where F_resolve is the Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence of `Resolve` with `Withdraw`, `Reject` and (optionally) `Transfer` as competing terminal events.
*Interpretation:* "What fraction of submitted cases will have received an internal resolution within the promised window?" A rejected case counts as a non-resolution, because the customer did not get a resolution.
*Estimate (transfer = competing event): F_resolve(72) = 0.304, so **E1 ≈ 0.696** (Greenwood-type SE on the survival factor ≈ 0.09; a log-log 95 % interval is roughly 0.50–0.85).*

**E2 — No terminal disposition at 72 h (all-cause survival in the initial/active state).**
E2 = S_any(72) = P(the case is still live and undecided at 72 h), with `open` rows as the only censoring.
*Interpretation:* "What fraction of cases will still be sitting in my queue, consuming capacity and exposed to escalation, when the clock runs out?" A rejection at 9 h is a *success* for this estimand — the case is off the books.
*Estimate: **E2 ≈ 0.380** (SE 0.093). Assumption-free bounds: 9 cases are known to be undisposed at 72 h and 5 are unknown, giving [9/32, 14/32] = [0.281, 0.438]; the Kaplan–Meier value sits inside that interval, as it must.*

**E3 — Late-resolution incidence (tail mass of the resolution CIF).**
E3 = F_resolve(∞) − F_resolve(72) = probability that a case resolves, but only after the SLA has already been breached.
*Interpretation:* "How much of my eventual good outcome arrives late?" This is the estimand for remediation-capacity planning and for the "recovered breach" KPI.
*Estimate: with transfer as a competing event, F_resolve(max observed 110 h) = 0.605 and F_resolve(72) = 0.304, so **E3 ≈ 0.30** — roughly **half of all eventual resolutions arrive after the SLA**. With transfer treated as censoring the split is 0.748 vs 0.313, i.e. E3 ≈ 0.44. E3 is much more assumption-dependent than E1 or E2 because it lives entirely in the censored region, and here it is largely an artefact of one case: the largest observation (S13 at 110 h) is an event, so S is driven to zero and the whole residual mass S(72) is redistributed to whichever causes are observed after 72 h — and almost all of them are resolutions. The redistribution is exact where every post-72 event is a resolution (cohort A: E3 = 0.339 = S_A(72); transfer-as-censoring: E3 = 0.435 = S(72)); overall, 0.301 of the 0.380 residual goes to resolution and the rest to S32's transfer at 96 h. Treat E3 as an optimistic extrapolation, not an estimate.*

**E4 — Cause-specific risk decomposition at 72 h.**
The full competing-risks partition at τ (transfer = competing event):

| state at 72 h | estimate |
|---|---:|
| resolved | 0.304 |
| withdrawn | 0.140 |
| rejected | 0.101 |
| transferred (external, unobserved) | 0.075 |
| still undisposed | 0.380 |
| **total** | **1.000** |

*Interpretation:* This is the only estimand that supports "why" questions and portfolio-level accountability, because it shows that the 0.696 in E1 is not one failure mode but four with different owners.

**E0 — Already-observed breaches (a descriptive count, not an estimand).**
Nine cases have provably passed 72 h without an internal resolution: S03 (76 h), S13 (110 h), S20 (74 h), S28 (88 h) resolved late; S09, S11, S25, S26 open beyond 72 h; S32 transferred at 96 h. This is a *reporting figure* for cases already in hand (9/32 = 0.281) and is a lower bound on E2, not an estimate of risk for a newly arriving case. Conflating E0 with E1/E2 is the most common governance error: E0 shrinks automatically if the extract is refreshed with young cases.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

## 2.1 Primary encoding table

`cause` is the competing-risk code (0 = censored). "At risk for resolution" means the case still contributes to the denominator of the resolution hazard.

| observed_status | n | E1 event indicator (resolve) | E2 event indicator (any disposition) | competing-risk code | censoring rule | primary sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | 13 | 1 at t, counted for F_resolve(72) only if t ≤ 72 | 1 at t | 1 = resolve | none (fully observed) | split into "resolved after rework" vs clean-path resolution; treat `Resolve` after `Transfer`-like handoffs separately (not observed here) |
| `withdrawn` | 4 | 0 (never resolves); removed from resolution risk set at t | 1 at t | 2 = withdraw | **not** censoring — treating it as censoring asserts a counterfactual resolution that cannot happen | (a) competing event (primary); (b) censoring, i.e. "would have resolved like similar live cases" — only defensible if withdrawal is customer-driven and independent of internal delay; (c) merge with reject into "negative disposal"; (d) treat customer-initiated withdrawal after long waits as an *outcome caused by* delay, i.e. an SLA failure to be modelled jointly |
| `rejected` | 3 | 0; removed from resolution risk set at t | 1 at t | 3 = reject | not censoring (an authoritative terminal decision) | (a) competing event (primary); (b) reclassify as a legitimate "closure" success for E2-style queue metrics; (c) if some rejections are appealable, treat as an intermediate state with a return transition |
| `open` | 9 | 0, censored at age | 0, censored at age | 0 | administrative right censoring at the cutoff | (a) independent censoring (primary); (b) dormancy reclassification: open with `last_event_gap_h` ≥ 24 h recoded as de-facto withdrawal at the last event time; (c) IPCW with priority/stage weights; (d) worst/best-case bounding at τ |
| `transferred` | 3 | see §2.2 | see §2.2 | 4 (or 0) | see §2.2 | see §2.2 |

## 2.2 `transferred` under three (plus two) plausible assumptions

All three transfers are S08 (A, P1, 68 h), S21 (B, P1, 26 h), S32 (B, P1, 96 h). All three are P1 and all three are `escalated`, and every one of them has `Escalate` immediately before `Transfer`. This concentration is the single most important structural fact in the dataset for censoring validity.

**T1 — Transfer as right censoring ("we stopped watching; the case might still resolve").**
Encoding: `cause = 0` at t. Rationale: the prompt says the eventual resolution is *unknown*, which is literally the definition of censoring.
Result: F_resolve(72) = 0.313, E1 = 0.687, E2 = 0.435.
Problem: KM/AJ requires censoring to be independent of the residual resolution hazard *given the covariates in the model*. Transfer is 100 % concentrated in P1 and 100 % in escalated cases, and P1 has the *worst* resolution incidence (0.250) of the three priorities. Marginally, T1 is dependent censoring almost by construction. It is only usable inside a P1-stratified or escalation-conditional model, and even then it silently imports the external organisation's (unmeasured) speed.

**T2 — Transfer as a competing terminal event ("internal resolution is now impossible").**
Encoding: `cause = 4` at t. Rationale: under A5 the estimand is *internal* resolution; once transferred, internal resolution can never occur, so this is not censoring but a realised alternative outcome.
Result: F_resolve(72) = 0.304, E1 = 0.696, E2 = 0.380, F_transfer(72) = 0.075 (and 0.281 within P1).
This is my primary encoding, because it makes the estimand well defined without any untestable claim about the external organisation.

**T3 — Transfer as a satisfied disposal at transfer time ("handoff completes our obligation").**
Encoding: `cause = 1` (relabelled as resolution) at t. Rationale: some SLA regimes stop the clock on legitimate external referral.
Result: F_resolve(72) = 0.379, E1 = 0.621, E2 unchanged at 0.380 (relabelling a cause cannot change all-cause survival — a useful internal consistency check).

**T4 — Transfer as a definitional breach at t (accountability view).** Transfer is coded as an *event of interest* in a "breach" analysis: the internal system failed to deliver, and the fact that a P1 case was pushed out is itself the failure. Numerically identical to T2 for E1, but it reverses the *sign of the managerial reading*: under T3 transfers are good news, under T4 they are the headline problem. The choice is a policy decision, not a statistical one, and it must be made before the analysis.

**T5 — Bounding.** T2 and T3 are the extremes for the resolution CIF, so **E1 ∈ [0.621, 0.696]** with T1 (0.687) in between. The whole transfer controversy is worth about **7.5 percentage points** on E1. That is a genuinely useful finding: transfers are contentious but not decisive for the headline number, whereas they dominate any P1-specific statement (0.281 transfer incidence in P1).

## 2.3 What must never be encoded as a covariate

`last_event_gap_h` is **0 for every one of the 23 terminal rows and > 0 for every one of the 9 open rows**. It is a deterministic function of the outcome at the freeze date. Any model that uses it as a static feature will separate perfectly and will be worthless in production. It is admissible only as a *time-varying* quantity evaluated at the prediction time (see §5). Likewise `duration_or_age_h` and `observed_status` are outcome definitions, not features.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**The naive number and why it is not merely imprecise but provably wrong.** Restricting to the 13 `resolved` cases gives 4 late resolutions (S03 76 h, S13 110 h, S20 74 h, S28 88 h) out of 13, i.e. an apparent breach rate of **0.308**. But the assumption-free bounds on E1 are [0.562, 0.719]: 18 of 32 cases are *known* not to have resolved by 72 h (4 late resolutions + 4 withdrawals + 3 rejections + 3 transfers + 4 open cases already older than 72 h), and only 5 cases (S31, S15, S10, S29, S24) have genuinely unknown 72 h status. **0.308 lies outside the interval that logically must contain the truth.** No amount of sampling noise explains this; it is a definitional error. The process-aware estimate, 0.696, sits inside the interval near its upper end.

**Survivor / conditioning-on-the-future bias.** The set "cases with `observed_status = resolved`" is defined by an event that occurs after t = 0. Conditioning on it discards precisely the cases that are failing: the four open cases that have already blown through the SLA (S09, S11, S25, S26), the three transferred P1 cases (S08, S21, S32), and all seven withdrawn/rejected cases. Within the surviving set, the slowest members are also systematically missing, because a case can only be *observed* resolving at 110 h (S13) if the extract is old enough to contain it; a case submitted 30 h before the freeze can only ever appear as a fast resolution or as `open`. Length/left-truncation bias therefore compresses the observed resolution-time distribution downward as well.

**Competing risks are not censoring.** A naive Kaplan–Meier that keeps all 32 cases but censors withdrawals, rejections and transfers gives 1 − S(72) = **0.361** for "resolved by 72 h", versus the Aalen–Johansen **0.304**. The 5.7-point inflation is exactly the KM artefact: censoring S06 at 27 h asserts that it remains eligible to resolve after 27 h, which is false — `Withdraw` is terminal. Every competing event redistributes its probability mass to the resolution cause, and 1 − S_KM is not a probability of anything real when competing risks exist. The same logic applies to S05 (rejected at 9 h) and S12 (withdrawn at 15 h): early terminal disposals are the biggest offenders because they inflate the risk-set weight of the remaining cases for the longest time.

**Informative censoring.** Two distinct mechanisms:

- *Transfer-driven.* S08, S21 and S32 are all P1 and all escalated. Censoring at transfer removes the highest-difficulty stratum from the risk set precisely when it is about to fail. Since P1's resolution incidence (0.250) is well below P2's (0.431), marginal T1 censoring is upward-biased for resolution. Conditioning on priority is the minimum fix.
- *Dormancy-driven.* Four open cases have large inactivity gaps: S11 (gap 55 h at age 81 h, last event at t = 26), S25 (48 h at 79 h, last event t = 31), S26 (72 h at 104 h, last event t = 32) and S09 (31 h at 92 h, last event t = 61). Three of the four stalled before t = 35 and have been untouched since. Two readings compete and cannot be separated with these data: (i) these cases are *abandoned* — de-facto withdrawals that were never recorded, so treating them as "still at risk" overstates future resolution; (ii) they are *queue-starved but live* — genuine backlog whose hazard will resume, so censoring is fine and the operational message is a staffing problem. Reading (i) is closer to informative censoring; reading (ii) is not. Note the useful asymmetry: because all four are older than 72 h, this ambiguity **does not touch E1 at τ = 72 h** (the dormancy sensitivity in §6 moves F_resolve(72) only from 0.304 to 0.308) but it moves **E2 from 0.380 to 0.229**. The choice matters enormously for the queue estimand and hardly at all for the resolution estimand.

**Cohort imbalance and non-comparability.** Cohort B is larger (17 vs 15) and has a different priority mix (P1: 5/17 = 29 % vs 3/15 = 20 %), which matters because P1 is where all transfers happen. Rollout compliance is partial and observable through the prefixes: `AutoCheck` occurs *before* `Screen` in 12 of 17 B cases but in **0 of 15** A cases; the five non-compliant B cases (S17, S20, S23, S26, S30) look procedurally like cohort A. So an intention-to-treat contrast (A vs B) dilutes the effect by ~29 %, while an as-treated contrast (`AutoCheck`-first vs not) is confounded by whatever caused non-compliance. Neither is a causal effect, since assignment was not randomised.

**Active open cases below versus above 72 h are different objects.** This distinction is what a completed-case analysis destroys.

- *Below τ (S31 23 h/gap 3, S15 35 h/gap 2, S10 47 h/gap 19, S29 51 h/gap 6, S24 67 h/gap 4).* Their 72 h status is **unknown**; they are the only true censored contributions to E1/E2 at the horizon; they are the only cases where a *preventive* alert can still change the outcome; and four of the five are demonstrably active (gap ≤ 6 h). S10 (gap 19 h, sitting in `Escalate`) is the borderline case.
- *Above τ (S09 92 h, S11 81 h, S25 79 h, S26 104 h).* Their 72 h status is **observed** (all breached); no imputation is needed; and they are not a prevention target but a remediation and root-cause target. Three of the four have gaps of 48–72 h, i.e. they are the dormancy problem.

Pooling these ten open cases into one "open" bucket, or dropping them all, therefore mixes fully-observed breaches with the only genuinely uncertain observations, and simultaneously deletes the entire preventable population.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## 4.1 Method-by-method assessment against *these* data

| method | what it estimates here | verdict for this extract |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan–Meier for `Resolve`** (all else censored) | 1 − S_KM(72) = 0.361 | **Reject as a primary estimator.** Competing events are 10 of 32 cases (31 %), so the "would eventually resolve" extrapolation it makes is counterfactually impossible for withdrawn/rejected cases. Retain KM only for the *all-cause* endpoint, where it is exactly the right tool. |
| **Kaplan–Meier for all-cause disposition** (only `open` censored) | E2 = S(72) = 0.380, SE 0.093 | **Accept.** Here there is only one event type ("left the active state"), so no competing-risk problem exists by construction, and the estimate is nonparametrically bounded by [0.281, 0.438]. This is the cleanest, least assumption-laden statistic in the whole analysis. |
| **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence** | F_resolve(72) = 0.304 plus the full E4 partition summing to 1 | **Accept as primary.** It is the only estimator that gives probabilities that are simultaneously interpretable and additive, and it makes the transfer assumption explicit rather than hidden. |
| **Cause-specific hazards (Cox)** | multiplicative effects on the instantaneous rate of each cause | **Accept as secondary, heavily constrained.** With 32 cases and 13 resolutions, the events-per-variable budget is about 1–2 covariates (13/10 ≈ 1). Fit at most `priority` + `cohort`, and only as stratified/descriptive. This is the right frame for *mechanism* questions ("does escalation change the rate at which cases resolve?"), and it is where time-varying escalation belongs. |
| **Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray)** | direct covariate effects on F_resolve | **Defer.** It answers the prediction question directly, but coefficients are notoriously hard to interpret when several causes move at once (here withdrawal, rejection and transfer all differ across strata), and 13 events cannot support it. Also its risk-set convention (keeping competing-event cases at risk with decreasing weight) interacts badly with the transfer ambiguity. |
| **Restricted mean time (RMST / RMTL)** | restricted mean time spent undisposed in (0, 72]: **50.4 h overall** (A 49.6, B 51.1, P1 52.8, P2 52.1, P3 45.7) | **Accept as a co-primary summary.** It needs no proportional-hazards assumption, is defined even with heavy censoring inside the window, and converts directly into capacity language: each case occupies the active queue for ~70 % of its SLA window on average. It is also the one summary that management can multiply by arrival rate to get WIP. |
| **Landmarking** | P(resolve by 72 h │ still active at L, history up to L) | **Adopt as the design principle, but currently infeasible in full.** Landmarking is the correct cure for the escalation/immortal-time problem. It requires knowing each case's state *at* L, which needs intermediate timestamps that the extract does not supply (A4). The one thing recoverable is the entry time into the current state for open cases (`duration_or_age_h` − `last_event_gap_h`: S11 at 26 h, S10 at 28 h, S25 at 31 h, S26 at 32 h, S15 at 33 h, S29 at 45 h, S09 at 61 h, S24 at 63 h, S31 at 20 h). So a *partial* landmark analysis on the open subset is possible; a full one is a data request. |
| **Multi-state model** (Markov or semi-Markov, e.g. `Screened → UnderReview → Escalated → absorbing`) | transition intensities and state-occupancy probabilities | **Recommend as the target design; not identifiable today.** The prefixes give the *sequence* of transitions but not their *times*, so transition intensities cannot be estimated. What is estimable now is the crude transition-count matrix and the state occupancy of open cases at cutoff. This is the highest-value data request in §9. |

## 4.2 Recommendation

**Primary: Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence of `Resolve` at τ = 72 h with the full four-cause partition, stratified by priority, transfer coded as a competing event (T2), reported alongside the T1/T3 bounds and the assumption-free [0.562, 0.719] interval for E1.**
Justification specific to these data: (i) 10 of 32 cases end in a state from which resolution is impossible, so competing risks are the dominant feature, not a technicality; (ii) transfer is 100 % concentrated in P1, so priority stratification is the minimal defence against dependent censoring rather than a refinement; (iii) at a fixed 72 h horizon only 5 cases are censored before τ, so the estimator is close to a bounded, almost design-free quantity, and I can *show* that with the bounds; (iv) the E4 partition is what makes the result actionable, because it separates "slow" (0.380 undisposed) from "disposed without resolving" (0.316).

**Co-primary: all-cause Kaplan–Meier for E2 plus restricted mean active time in (0, 72].** These require no competing-risk assumptions at all and cover the queue/capacity decision.

**Secondary: priority-stratified cause-specific hazard description, with escalation entered strictly as a time-varying covariate once timestamps exist, and landmark models at L ∈ {12, 24, 48} h for the operational scoring pipeline.**

**Explicitly not recommended:** Fine–Gray as a headline (uninterpretable with four moving causes at n = 32), any parametric AFT/Weibull with covariates (13 events), and any single-number "SLA breach probability" that does not name its estimand.

## 4.3 Inference and power

Two-sample tests between cohorts are uninformative here, and it is better to say so quantitatively than to report a plausible-looking p-value. Log-rank statistics (transfer = competing): all-cause disposition O_A = 11.0 vs E_A = 10.99, z = +0.01, χ² = 0.00; cause-specific resolution O_A = 6.0 vs E_A = 6.51, z = −0.30, χ² = 0.09 (p ≈ 0.76); withdrawal/rejection z = +0.60, χ² = 0.36. The per-cohort SEs on S(72) are 0.133 (A) and 0.130 (B), so the SE of any cohort difference is ≈ 0.19 — an order of magnitude larger than the 0.024 difference in F_resolve(72). **This extract can detect nothing smaller than roughly a 35–40 point difference in 72 h incidence.** All cohort statements below are hypothesis-generating.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

## 5.1 Feature derivation and risk classification

`Escalate`, `Rework` and repeated `Review` all occur *after* t = 0, so their status must be declared. The taxonomy below is the analytical core of the section.

| feature | definition from the supplied fields | class | why, and how to use it safely |
|---|---|---|---|
| `priority` (P1/P2/P3) | given | **baseline** | Known at `Submit`. Safe as a stratifier and as the fairness axis. |
| `cohort` (A/B) | given | **baseline (assignment)** | Fixed by submission date. Safe for ITT; not a causal effect (non-randomised, incomplete rollout). |
| `autocheck_first` = `AutoCheck` precedes `Screen` | `prefix.startswith("Submit>AutoCheck")` — true for 12/17 B, 0/15 A | **post-treatment / adherence proxy** | This is the *realised* policy exposure, and it is the cleanest available compliance measure (≈ 71 % in B). But it is a consequence of routing decisions, so an as-treated contrast is confounded. Use as a per-protocol *sensitivity* arm and to report rollout fidelity, never as the primary contrast. |
| `current_stage` = last activity of the prefix | `Screen` (S31); `Review` (S09, S15, S24, S26, S29); `Escalate` (S10, S11, S25) | **time-varying state** | The correct multi-state covariate. Legitimate only inside a model where the case is evaluated *while in* that stage (landmark or counting-process form). Using "ever reached `Escalate`" as a baseline flag is the immortal-time error. |
| `time_in_current_stage` | equals `last_event_gap_h` for open rows (0 by construction for terminal rows) | **time-varying; catastrophic leakage if static** | Deterministically 0 for all 23 terminal rows and > 0 for all 9 open rows, so as a static feature it perfectly predicts the outcome. Admissible only as "hours since last event *as of the scoring instant*". |
| `stage_entry_time` | `duration_or_age_h − last_event_gap_h` (open rows only) | **time-varying, recoverable** | Gives left-truncation-aware entry into the current state: S11 at 26 h, S10 at 28 h, S25 at 31 h, S26 at 32 h, S09 at 61 h, S24 at 63 h. This is the only intra-case timing information the extract contains and it is what makes a partial semi-Markov sojourn analysis possible. |
| `escalated_by_L` | `Escalate` ∈ prefix, restricted to events before landmark L | **time-varying / post-treatment** | The `escalated` column is a whole-trajectory summary and is the primary immortal-time trap (see §5.2). |
| `rework_count` | given; 1 for S07, S13, S27, else 0 | **time-varying, post-treatment** | Requires two `Review`s, hence survival to at least the second review. As a baseline covariate it is immortal-time contaminated; as a time-varying covariate it is a legitimate marker of quality failure. |
| `n_review` (repeated review) | count of `Review` in prefix; 2 for S03, S07, S13 | **time-varying** | Same treatment as rework. Note S07 and S13 are the two rework cases in A and both are slow (54 h, 110 h). |
| `prefix_length` | number of activities | **leakage** | Grows monotonically with elapsed time; correlates with the outcome mechanically. Only usable at a fixed landmark. |
| `path_signature` (variant) | full prefix | **mixed; too sparse** | 32 traces yield **27 distinct variants**, with only 5 variants appearing twice (`Submit>Screen>Review>Resolve`, `Submit>Screen>Reject`, `Submit>Screen>Review>Withdraw`, `Submit>Screen>Review`, `Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Escalate>Transfer`). One-hot variants are hopeless at n = 32; abstraction into stage + flags is mandatory. Also, variants of *open* cases are prefixes, not complete paths, so variant frequencies mix two different objects and must be reported separately. |
| `early_path` (2-prefix) | first activity after `Submit` | **near-baseline** | Coarsening the variant space with `get_prefixes_from_log(log, length=2)` yields exactly two classes (`Submit>AutoCheck`, `Submit>Screen`) and recovers the policy-exposure feature with far less sparsity. The 3-prefix view yields 7 classes and already leaks `Escalate` for S04, S11, S18, S29 — a concrete illustration of how prefix length trades information against leakage. |
| `bypass_screen` | `Screen` absent | `S18` (`Submit>AutoCheck>Escalate>Resolve`), `S29` | **post-treatment** | Rare fast-path pattern: S18 resolved in 12 h, the fastest P1 in the data. Worth a hypothesis, not a coefficient. |

## 5.2 The immortal-time and leakage arguments, concretely

Twelve cases are `escalated`. Of these, only S04 (29 h) and S18 (12 h) resolved within 72 h — 2/12 — versus 7/20 for non-escalated cases. Reading this as "escalation makes cases 3× less likely to meet the SLA" is wrong twice over:

1. **Immortal time / reverse causation.** To be classified as escalated, a case must survive un-resolved until its escalation event. S13 escalates only after `Submit>Screen>Review>Rework>Review`, so it *cannot* have been a fast case — escalation is downstream of difficulty. The escalated group is guaranteed to contain no quick resolutions except those escalated almost immediately (S18, escalated straight from `AutoCheck` and resolved at 12 h — the exception that proves the mechanism).
2. **Conditioning on a mediator of the outcome.** Escalation is also plausibly the *response* to impending breach, so its coefficient mixes selection with treatment effect. The correct handling is a time-varying indicator in a counting-process Cox model (a case contributes escalated = 0 person-time before its escalation and escalated = 1 after), or a landmark analysis at L that uses only `escalated_by_L`. Both need the escalation timestamp, which is missing.

The same argument applies to `rework_count` and, most starkly, to `last_event_gap_h`, whose structural zero on terminal rows would make any naive classifier appear near-perfect in cross-validation and useless in deployment.

## 5.3 Proposed multi-state representation

States are process stages; absorbing states are the four terminal outcomes. `Transferred` is drawn as absorbing-and-unobserved to make the censoring/competing-risk decision visible in the model rather than buried in the code.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

All numbers below come from the same code path with only the encoding switched.

**S1 — Transfer treatment (T1 censoring / T2 competing / T3 disposal).**

| scenario | F_resolve(72) | E1 = P(not resolved by 72) | E2 = S(72) | E3 late resolution |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| T1 transfer censored | 0.313 | 0.687 | 0.435 | 0.435 |
| T2 transfer competing (primary) | 0.304 | 0.696 | 0.380 | 0.301 |
| T3 transfer = disposal at t | 0.379 | 0.621 | 0.380 | 0.380 |

*Reading:* E1 moves by 7.5 points, E2 by 5.5 points, and E3 by 13 points. **The tail estimand E3 is the fragile one**; the horizon estimands are comparatively stable. Note also the consistency check that T2 and T3 give identical E2 (relabelling a cause cannot change all-cause survival), which is a cheap validation of the implementation.

**S2 — Dormancy / informative censoring (open cases with `last_event_gap_h` ≥ 24 h — S09, S11, S25, S26 — recoded as de-facto withdrawals at their last event time).**

| quantity | primary | dormancy-recoded |
|---|---:|---:|
| E2 = S(72) | 0.380 | **0.229** |
| F_resolve(72) | 0.304 | 0.308 |
| E4: withdrawal incidence at 72 | 0.140 | **0.282** |
| RMT active in (0,72] | 50.4 h | 45.7 h |
| cohort A / B on E2 | 0.339 / 0.416 | 0.175 / 0.277 |

*Reading:* this is the most consequential sensitivity for the queue estimand and almost irrelevant for the resolution estimand — precisely because all four dormant cases are older than 72 h, so their 72 h status is already observed. The cohort ordering on E2 survives (B still has more undisposed cases). A governance implication: if dormancy really is silent abandonment, then roughly half the apparent backlog is fiction and the real failure mode is unrecorded case death, which no amount of survival modelling will fix.

**S3 — Withdrawal/rejection treatment.** Under the primary coding, withdrawal and rejection are competing events (E1 = 0.696). If they are instead treated as censoring (the "they would have resolved like comparable live cases" assumption), the resolution estimate rises to 1 − S_KM(72) = 0.361, i.e. E1 falls to 0.639. If instead they are counted as *legitimate closures* (the E2 view), the 7 cases become successes and the SLA story improves sharply. The three codings disagree by roughly 6 points on E1 and reverse the sign of the contribution of 7 of 32 cases. Especially important for P3: its 72 h partition is 0.200 resolved, 0.300 rejected, 0.200 withdrawn, 0.300 undisposed — half of all P3 cases are disposed *without* resolution inside the window. Whether P3 is the best-performing or worst-performing priority depends entirely on this coding choice.

**S4 — Cohort/policy effect under all three transfer codings.**

| estimand | cohort A | cohort B | direction |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| F_resolve(72), T2 | 0.292 | 0.316 | B slightly better |
| F_resolve(72), T1 | 0.292 | 0.332 | B better |
| F_resolve(72), T3 | 0.377 | 0.379 | indistinguishable |
| **E2 = S(72)**, T2 | **0.339** | **0.416** | **B worse** |
| RMT active (0,72] | 49.6 h | 51.1 h | B worse |
| early disposal (withdraw+reject+transfer) by 72 | 0.369 | 0.269 | A disposes more, earlier |
| log-rank on resolution | — | — | z = −0.30, χ² = 0.09, p ≈ 0.76 |

The per-protocol (as-treated) contrast is larger than the ITT contrast, exactly as dilution predicts: `AutoCheck`-first cases (n = 12, all in B) give E1 = 0.630 and E2 = 0.444, versus E1 = 0.736 and E2 = 0.357 for `Screen`-first cases (n = 20). So the apparent policy benefit on resolution grows from 2.4 points (ITT) to 10.6 points (per protocol) — while remaining far inside the noise and now additionally confounded by whatever drove the 29 % non-adherence. Note that the E1/E2 divergence persists in the per-protocol arm too.

*Reading:* the sign of the "cohort B effect" **depends on the estimand, not on the transfer assumption**. On resolution incidence B is (trivially) ahead; on "still clogging the queue at 72 h" B is 7.7 points worse. A coherent mechanistic hypothesis: earlier automated screening reduces early rejection (rejection incidence 0.151 in A vs 0.059 in B) and therefore keeps more cases alive, raising the undisposed fraction while barely changing the resolution rate. Whether that is good news depends on whether A's early rejections were correct or were disposal-by-convenience. This is a hypothesis, not a finding: every difference here is a fraction of its own standard error (~0.19), the priority mix differs (P1 29 % vs 20 %), rollout adherence is ~71 %, and assignment was not randomised.

**Which qualitative conclusions could reverse.**

- *Would reverse:* the direction of the cohort-B effect (estimand-dependent, S4); whether P3 is the best or worst-served priority (S3); whether transfers are a success or the headline failure (T3 vs T4); whether the backlog is real or abandoned (S2); whether "about half of eventual resolutions are late" is true (E3 ranges 0.30–0.44).
- *Would not reverse under any coding examined:* E1 is large — between 0.62 and 0.70 under every transfer assumption, and bounded below by 0.562 with no assumptions at all, so the naive 0.308 is wrong in every scenario; only 9 of 32 cases resolved within 72 h; all transfers are P1 and escalated, so P1 censoring is dependent; roughly 40 % of the SLA window is not enough for the current process, since the restricted mean active time is ~50 of 72 h; and no cohort or priority difference in this extract is statistically distinguishable from zero.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Match the estimand to the decision.** This is the whole point of §1 and it should be stated on the dashboard itself.

| decision | estimand | why |
|---|---|---|
| "Should I expedite *this* case now?" | E1-type conditional risk from a landmark model: P(no `Resolve` by 72 h │ active at now, history to now) | Decision is about delivering a resolution; conditioning on "still active" is what makes it actionable. |
| "How many cases will still be open tomorrow / what staffing do I need?" | E2 and RMT active | Capacity is consumed by undisposed cases regardless of how they eventually end; a rejection frees capacity. |
| "How big must the late-remediation team be?" | E3 | Sizes the population that resolves after breach (~0.30–0.44 of cases). |
| "Where do I invest to fix the process?" | E4 partition by priority | Distinguishes slow (P1: 0.469 undisposed, 0.281 transferred) from disposed-without-resolution (P3: 0.500 rejected/withdrawn by 72 h). |
| "Did the policy work?" | E1 *and* E2 side by side, ITT and per-protocol | Because the sign flips between them (S4), reporting one alone is misleading. |

**Alerting.** Score only cases that are *still active*, at fixed decision points (e.g. every 6 h, and mandatorily at 12/24/48 h landmarks), using only information available at the scoring instant: priority, cohort, current stage, hours elapsed, hours since last event, review/rework counts so far, escalation-so-far. Trigger on the *conditional* risk and on the remaining slack (72 − elapsed), not on the marginal risk — S31 (23 h, active 3 h ago) and S09 (92 h, idle 31 h) need entirely different playbooks and the second is not an alert at all but a remediation ticket. A pragmatic and defensible first rule needs no model: **idle-time alerts**. Every one of the four cases with a gap ≥ 24 h has already breached, and all four stalled early (last events at 26–61 h). A "no event in 12 h while active and inside the window" trigger would have flagged S10 (gap 19 h at age 47 h) while 25 h of slack remained. Escalation-queue occupancy deserves its own watch: the three open `Escalated` cases have a mean idle time of 40.7 h.

**Workload actions.** Convert survival output into capacity, not just into red flags: expected active-hours per case within the window is ~50.4 h, so expected concurrent WIP ≈ arrival rate × 50.4 h. Use E3 to size the after-breach remediation queue. Treat the P1 transfer incidence of 0.281 by 72 h as a *routing* question — if handoff is the intended design, transfers should be measured as a distinct planned pathway with its own SLA and its own external follow-up feed; if it is not intended, it is capacity leakage and 3 of 8 P1 cases leaked.

**SLA governance.** Publish the estimand definition, horizon, and transfer/withdrawal coding next to every number, plus the assumption-free bounds (E1 ∈ [0.562, 0.719] here) so that reviewers can see how much of the number is data and how much is coding choice. Never report the completed-case rate (0.308): it is outside the logically possible range and it improves whenever the backlog worsens, because slow cases are excluded until they finish. Guard the perverse incentive that E2 creates: "no case undisposed at 72 h" can be satisfied by rejecting or withdrawing cases, and cohort A's rejection incidence (0.151 vs B's 0.059) shows the pathway exists — so pair E2 with E1 and monitor the disposal mix as a compensating control.

**Nothing here is a causal effect.** Escalation is associated with worse on-time resolution (2/12 vs 7/20), but escalation is chosen *because* cases are hard and *after* they have already survived un-resolved (§5.2). A risk score that ranks escalated cases as high-risk is doing its job as a predictor and says nothing about whether escalating helps. The same applies to cohort B: non-randomised assignment plus ~71 % adherence means the A/B contrast is a policy-period comparison, not a treatment effect. Present model output as "cases like this one currently tend to breach", and reserve causal language for a design that supports it (staged rollout with recorded assignment, or an interrupted time series with concurrent controls).

**Calibration checks.** (i) Time-dependent calibration at the landmarks: bin predicted P(breach) into deciles and compare with Aalen–Johansen-estimated observed incidence within each bin, which handles censoring correctly — a naive observed rate would be biased. (ii) Report time-dependent AUC/Brier with inverse-probability-of-censoring weights. (iii) Verify the model's implied marginal incidence against the nonparametric bounds; a model predicting a mean breach probability below 0.562 for a cohort like this one is refuted without further testing. (iv) Recalibrate per cohort, since the policy change shifts the baseline hazard.

**Fairness checks by priority and cohort.** The data already show why this matters: the resolution incidence at 72 h is non-monotone in priority — P2 0.431, P1 0.250, P3 0.200 — so the highest-priority class does *not* get the best outcome, largely because P1 cases are transferred out (incidence 0.281) rather than resolved. Concretely: (a) report the whole E4 partition per priority, not just the breach rate, so that "disposed by rejection" cannot masquerade as performance; (b) check calibration *within* each priority and each cohort separately, since a model calibrated on the pooled data can be systematically optimistic for P1 (where competing transfer risk dominates) and pessimistic for P2; (c) monitor equalised-odds-style gaps in alert rates across priority and cohort, and treat a rising P3 rejection incidence as a fairness alarm rather than an efficiency gain; (d) audit whether escalation and transfer decisions themselves are being driven by the score, which would create a feedback loop in which the model's predictions become self-fulfilling.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

Executable Python. Requires `pandas`, `numpy`, `pm4py` (all present in a standard PM4Py environment). **`lifelines` is optional**: if importable, it is used to cross-check the all-cause Kaplan–Meier curve; if not, the script's own Kaplan–Meier / Aalen–Johansen / Greenwood / log-rank implementations are used throughout (the fallback is the default path, so the script never depends on a survival library). PM4Py's `parse_event_log_string` synthesises 1-second-apart 1970 timestamps; these are quarantined and used **only** for activity ordering — every duration in the analysis comes from the supplied `duration_or_age_h` and `last_event_gap_h`.

```python
"""
Process-aware SLA survival analysis of a frozen case extract (72 h elapsed-time SLA from Submit).

Estimands implemented
  E1  P(not resolved by 72 h)          = 1 - Aalen-Johansen CIF_resolve(72)
  E2  P(no terminal disposition at 72) = all-cause Kaplan-Meier S(72)   ('open' = only censoring)
  E3  late-resolution incidence        = CIF_resolve(max) - CIF_resolve(72)
  E4  full cause-specific partition at 72 h (resolve/withdraw/reject/transfer/undisposed)
  plus RMT: restricted mean time undisposed in (0, 72]

Transfer scenarios
  T1 'censor'      transfer = right censoring   (external resolution possible, unobserved)
  T2 'competing'   transfer = competing event   (internal resolution impossible)  [PRIMARY]
  T3 'as_resolved' transfer = satisfied disposal at transfer time (best case for resolution)

Dependencies: pandas, numpy, pm4py.  lifelines OPTIONAL (cross-check only; see LIFELINES below).
No event timestamps are fabricated: PM4Py's synthetic timestamps are used for ORDER ONLY.
"""

import io
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

H = 72.0  # SLA horizon, hours from Submit

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. The supplied case table, verbatim (no invented rows, no invented timestamps)
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------

df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(RAW), sep="|")
df["duration_or_age_h"] = df["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
df["last_event_gap_h"] = df["last_event_gap_h"].astype(float)

# Derived, timestamp-free process features -----------------------------------------
acts = df["prefix"].str.split(">")
df["current_activity"] = acts.str[-1]                      # time-varying state at cutoff
df["n_events"] = acts.str.len()                            # LEAKAGE if used as baseline
df["n_review"] = acts.apply(lambda a: a.count("Review"))   # time-varying
df["autocheck_first"] = df["prefix"].str.startswith("Submit>AutoCheck")  # adherence proxy
# Entry time into the CURRENT state, recoverable only for open rows (gap == 0 by
# construction on terminal rows, which is exactly why gap must never be a static feature).
df["stage_entry_h"] = np.where(df["observed_status"] == "open",
                               df["duration_or_age_h"] - df["last_event_gap_h"], np.nan)

STATUS2CAUSE = {"resolved": "resolve", "withdrawn": "withdraw",
                "rejected": "reject", "transferred": "transfer", "open": "cens"}

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Encoding: estimand-relevant (time, cause) under a chosen transfer scenario
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
def encode(d, transfer="competing", dormant_gap=None):
    """transfer in {'competing','censor','as_resolved'}; 'cens' == right censoring.
    dormant_gap: if set, open rows with gap >= dormant_gap are recoded as de-facto
    withdrawals at their LAST OBSERVED EVENT time (informative-censoring sensitivity)."""
    out = d.copy()
    cause = out["observed_status"].map(STATUS2CAUSE)
    t = out["duration_or_age_h"].copy()
    if transfer == "censor":
        cause = cause.where(cause != "transfer", "cens")
    elif transfer == "as_resolved":
        cause = cause.where(cause != "transfer", "resolve")
    elif transfer != "competing":
        raise ValueError(transfer)
    if dormant_gap is not None:
        m = (out["observed_status"] == "open") & (out["last_event_gap_h"] >= dormant_gap)
        cause = cause.mask(m, "withdraw")
        t = t.mask(m, out["duration_or_age_h"] - out["last_event_gap_h"])
    out["t"], out["cause"] = t, cause
    return out

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Risk-set / KM / Aalen-Johansen machinery -- open cases are NEVER dropped
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
def risk_table(enc):
    """KM/AJ-ready risk table: one row per distinct event time, all 32 cases retained
    (censored rows leave the risk set without contributing an event)."""
    rows = []
    for tt in np.sort(enc.loc[enc.cause != "cens", "t"].unique()):
        at_risk = int((enc["t"] >= tt).sum())
        ev = enc[(enc["t"] == tt) & (enc["cause"] != "cens")]
        cens = int(((enc["t"] == tt) & (enc["cause"] == "cens")).sum())
        rows.append(dict(t=tt, n_at_risk=at_risk, d_total=len(ev),
                         cause=",".join(sorted(ev["cause"])), n_censored_at_t=cens,
                         cases=",".join(sorted(ev["case"]))))
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def km_aj(enc, horizon=H):
    """All-cause Kaplan-Meier S(t) + Aalen-Johansen cause-specific CIFs, Greenwood SE,
    and restricted mean time undisposed over (0, horizon]. Pure numpy fallback -- no
    survival library required."""
    causes = sorted(set(enc["cause"]) - {"cens"})
    S, cif, gvar, rmt, prev = 1.0, {c: 0.0 for c in causes}, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    S_at_h = 1.0
    for tt in np.sort(enc.loc[enc.cause != "cens", "t"].unique()):
        n = int((enc["t"] >= tt).sum())
        ev = enc[(enc["t"] == tt) & (enc["cause"] != "cens")]
        rmt += S * (min(tt, horizon) - min(prev, horizon))   # area under S up to horizon
        prev = tt
        if tt <= horizon:
            for c in causes:                                  # AJ: dF_c = S(t-) * d_c/n
                k = int((ev["cause"] == c).sum())
                if k:
                    cif[c] += S * k / n
            if n > len(ev):
                gvar += len(ev) / (n * (n - len(ev)))          # Greenwood
        S *= 1.0 - len(ev) / n
        if tt <= horizon:
            S_at_h = S
    rmt += S * max(0.0, horizon - min(prev, horizon))
    return dict(n=len(enc), S=S_at_h, se_S=S_at_h * np.sqrt(gvar), cif=cif, rmt=rmt)

def estimands(d, transfer="competing", dormant_gap=None):
    enc = encode(d, transfer, dormant_gap)
    at_h = km_aj(enc, H)
    at_inf = km_aj(enc, float(enc["t"].max()))   # 'max observed', not infinity
    f_res = at_h["cif"].get("resolve", 0.0)
    return dict(n=at_h["n"], E1=1.0 - f_res, CIF_res=f_res, E2=at_h["S"], se_E2=at_h["se_S"],
                E3=at_inf["cif"].get("resolve", 0.0) - f_res, RMT=at_h["rmt"],
                E4={**at_h["cif"], "undisposed": at_h["S"]})

def line(label, e):
    other = " ".join(f"{k[:4]}={v:.3f}" for k, v in sorted(e["E4"].items())
                     if k not in ("resolve", "undisposed"))
    return (f"{label:34s} n={e['n']:2d} E1={e['E1']:.3f} CIF_res(72)={e['CIF_res']:.3f} "
            f"E2={e['E2']:.3f}(se {e['se_E2']:.3f}) E3={e['E3']:.3f} RMT={e['RMT']:.1f}h  {other}")

def logrank(enc, event_causes, group="cohort"):
    """Two-sample (stratum-1 vs rest) log-rank; event_causes selects all-cause or
    cause-specific comparisons. Non-listed causes act as censoring."""
    e = enc.copy()
    e["ev"] = e["cause"].isin(event_causes)
    g0 = sorted(e[group].unique())[0]
    O = E = V = 0.0
    for tt in np.sort(e.loc[e.ev, "t"].unique()):
        n = int((e["t"] >= tt).sum()); n0 = int(((e["t"] >= tt) & (e[group] == g0)).sum())
        dt = int(((e["t"] == tt) & e["ev"]).sum())
        d0 = int(((e["t"] == tt) & e["ev"] & (e[group] == g0)).sum())
        O += d0; E += dt * n0 / n
        if n > 1:
            V += dt * (n0 / n) * (1 - n0 / n) * (n - dt) / (n - 1)
    z = (O - E) / np.sqrt(V) if V > 0 else np.nan
    return O, E, z, z * z
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Structural limitations of this extract.** n = 32 with 13 resolutions supports roughly one covariate; every stratified number above is a description, not an inference (log-rank χ² ≤ 0.36 for all cohort contrasts, and the SE on a cohort difference is ~0.19). Only one cutoff is available, so nothing about trend, seasonality or staggered entry is identifiable. Intermediate event times are absent, so no transition intensity, no hazard-based multi-state model and no proper landmark analysis is possible today; the "process-aware" part of the analysis is currently limited to state labels and counts. Assumption A3 (complete extract, no length-biased sampling) is untestable and could shift the long-duration estimates materially.

**Data requests, in priority order.**

1. **Exact event timestamps for every activity in every trace.** Highest value by a wide margin. Unlocks: time-varying escalation (removing the immortal-time bias that currently makes escalation uninterpretable), landmark models at 12/24/48 h, semi-Markov sojourn distributions per stage, and the identification of *where* the ~50.4 h of active time is spent. Without this, the escalation association (2/12 vs 7/20) cannot be turned into anything usable.
2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`, with dates.** Collapses the T1/T2/T3 ambiguity that currently spans 7.5 points on E1 and 13 points on E3, and converts P1's 0.281 transfer incidence from a blind spot into a measured pathway. Requires a data-sharing feed or at least a periodic reconciliation file from the external organisation.
3. **The cutoff date and each case's submission date.** Needed to test A3, to detect staggered entry (age is currently confounded with entry time), to build a proper time-origin-aligned cohort, and to run an interrupted-time-series or difference-in-differences design instead of a raw A/B comparison.
4. **Ground truth on dormancy: case status audits, work-item assignment logs, and closure conventions.** Distinguishes "abandoned but never closed" from "queued and live" for S09, S11, S25, S26 — the assumption that swings E2 from 0.380 to 0.229 and reframes the entire backlog conversation. Cheap to obtain (ask the team about four cases).
5. **Time-varying workload and capacity:** queue depth, staffing per shift, handler identity and concurrent caseload, plus the shift calendar. Needed to relax A1 (elapsed vs working hours), to explain the early-stall pattern (three of four dormant cases stalled before t = 35 h), and to distinguish a capacity problem from a routing problem.
6. **The assignment mechanism for cohorts and the escalation/transfer decision rules.** Needed for any causal claim: who decided cohort membership, on what information, and what triggers `Escalate` and `Transfer`. If escalation is triggered by an SLA-countdown rule, then escalation and the outcome share a common cause and no adjustment on observed covariates will fix it.
7. **Documented rollout compliance,** independent of the prefixes. The prefix-derived adherence figure (12/17 in B; 0/15 in A) is an inference, not a record; a true per-case exposure log would separate non-compliance from case-mix routing and support a per-protocol or instrumental-variable analysis.
8. **Case-mix and complexity covariates measurable at submission** (channel, product, requester type, text complexity, prior related cases). These are the only legitimate baseline predictors beyond priority, and without them the priority strata remain internally heterogeneous — which is the most likely explanation for the non-monotone incidence pattern (P2 0.431 > P1 0.250 > P3 0.200).
9. **A larger extract, ideally several hundred cases per cohort with the above fields.** With 13 resolution events, even a genuinely large policy effect would be undetectable; the current data can support definitions, encodings and bounds, but not effect estimation.
