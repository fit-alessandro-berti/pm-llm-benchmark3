# 1. Current-process diagnosis

**Bottom line:** The evidence supports a **segment- and risk-dependent Pareto frontier**, not one universally optimal path. The dominant straight-through route is genuinely fast and inexpensive, but its apparent advantage largely disappears after downstream failure costs are included.

- **The DFG is dominated by the automatic/fast route.** Of 5,000 starts, 3,600, or 72%, enter auto eligibility. Auto eligibility produces 2,550 fast approvals; another 240 occur after fraud/risk review, for 2,790 fast-approval transitions. Of these, 2,300 move directly to replacement dispatch—the dominant productive path.
- **That path is genuinely fast at the activity level.** The selected happy-path edges—IntakeAuto screen, Auto screenFast approval, Fast approvalDispatch, and DispatchClose—have means of 0.8, 1.0, 3.0, and 5.0 hours. If traversed consecutively, those edge means total about 9.8 hours, although this is not an end-to-end case-time estimate. Reported end-to-end fast-path medians are more realistic at 8 hours for Premium low-risk and 13 hours for Standard low-risk, rising to 20–28 hours for high-value cases.
- **The tail is much worse than the fast route’s headline suggests.** Fast approvalComplaint/reopen has a mean of 72 hours and p90 of 150 hours; Dispatch replacementComplaint/reopen has a mean of 96 hours and p90 of 180 hours. Thus a fast initial decision can still create a very slow recovery process. The temporal profile links this tail strongly to incorrect replacement-part selection.
- **Missing evidence is the largest cycle-time problem.** Request missing evidenceManual eligibility review takes 34 hours on average, 26 hours at the median, and 88 hours at p90. Evidence is requested at least twice in 26% of cases entering that activity. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort has a 140-hour median and the highest abandonment rate.
- **The reviewed route is slow for identifiable reasons beyond reviewer productivity.** Manual eligibilityRepair quote takes 22/15/60 hours, Repair quoteCustomer approval takes 30/21/80 hours, and Customer approvalRepair device takes 20/15/52 hours for mean/median/p90. Repair-quote delay is attributed primarily to waiting for part-price confirmation. This directly explains why Premium reviewed cases are slow.
- **Fast is cheaper before failure, but much less attractive after failure.** Fast-approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits are included. Complaint/reopen occurs in 9.2% of all cases, 14.1% of fast-approval cases, and only 3.3% of the Repair quoteCustomer approvalRepair device route. The latter comparison is not a randomized causal estimate—reviewed cases may be more complex—but it shows that the aggregate fast lane is not risk-adjusted.
- **Quality failure is concentrated in high-value repairs.** Rework repair occurs in 12.5% of repaired high-value devices versus 4.0% of repaired low-risk devices. A wrong replacement has an expected cost of EUR 95 for a low-risk accessory but EUR 410 for a high-value device, so the same failure rate has very different financial consequences.
- **Compliance exceptions are visible and material.** Alignment diagnostics identify Fast approval before evidence completeness, complaint/reopen after close, and dispatch without risk review on high-value devices. These correspond to R1, R2, and R3. The visible Escalation boardClaim denied path of 10 transitions is also incompatible with R3 unless an unrepresented manual review occurred.
- **The discovered model is descriptive, not normative.** Token fitness of 0.91 indicates that aggregate movements can mostly be replayed, but only 68% of traces fit the model and precision is low because many observed combinations are prohibited by policy. Low-frequency paths should not be treated as valid templates. Explicit rule checks are needed in any redesign.
- **Customer outcomes contradict a single global routing rule.**
  - Premium low-risk cases need the fast route: fast median is 8 hours versus 46 manually, but manual complaint/reopen is 3% versus 6% fast. Because service-credit exposure rises after 24 hours, routing every Premium case through manual review would also be poor.
  - Standard low-risk customers tolerate up to 72 hours with good status visibility; manual review takes 52 hours and reduces complaint/reopen from 8% to 4%. Selective review is therefore plausible here.
  - Premium high-value cases show 22% complaint/reopen on the fast route versus 5% on the reviewed/repair route; Standard high-value cases show 18% versus 6%.
  - Mid-risk devices are mentioned in the process context, but no segment outcome distribution is supplied. They should not be assumed to qualify for the low-risk lane.
- **Resources constrain the alternatives.** Manual eligibility has seven reviewers, sharp queue growth above 82% utilization, and EUR 34/hour labor. Auto-screen rules can be updated in two weeks, but the change may raise manual inflow 15–35%. Fraud/risk has only two specialists at EUR 52/hour and is already a Monday/campaign bottleneck. Repair quote has three technicians but is primarily waiting on pricing. Quality inspection has two inspectors and is underused except after campaigns, making earlier quality control feasible. The daily 45-minute escalation board improves satisfaction but cannot scale to default handling.
- **The DFG should not be read as a unique-case waterfall.** Its listed terminal counts total 3,625 rather than 5,000, and activity occurrences can repeat through loops such as evidencemanual review and repairrework. Consequently, edge counts are workload signals, while the supplied segment-level outcome rates are the better baselines for redesign and ROI hypotheses.

The redesign implication is therefore not “automate more” or “review everything.” It is to use straight-through processing only where risk and customer economics support it, reserve independent assurance for high-value work, and redesign the evidence bottleneck rather than repeatedly sending incomplete cases through the same queue.

# 2. Redesign A: Segment-calibrated accessory lane with a part-fit gate

**Redesigned flow**

1. **Segment-aware intake:** At Intake, capture customer tier, item value, defect code, serial/accessory identifier, evidence status, promised completion time, and risk flags in one transaction.
2. **Explicit routing rules:**
   - Any claim above EUR 400 leaves the fast lane for a recorded Manual eligibility or Fraud/risk review before approval.
   - Premium low-risk accessory claims with known defects take the express lane, with the narrow R2 evidence exception used only when the item has been positively classified.
   - Standard low-risk claims with complete, known-defect evidence take straight-through processing.
   - Standard low-risk claims that are complete but technically ambiguous may use manual review with proactive status updates; the 52-hour manual median remains within their stated 72-hour tolerance.
   - Missing-evidence or unclear-defect cases leave A’s primary lane for C’s evidence workflow.
   - Mid-risk device claims are not admitted to A until segment-specific outcomes demonstrate that the error and complaint rates are acceptable.
3. **Decision and remedy:** Auto eligibility and technical decision are combined for known accessory defects. The system selects the remedy and part before creating the fulfillment token.
4. **Two-tier pre-dispatch gate:**  
   - A first-pass automated check validates entitlement, evidence state, serial/model compatibility, defect code, and proposed part.  
   - Flagged cases receive inspector review. This avoids placing every claim behind the full 6–18-hour gate while exploiting currently underused quality-inspection capacity.
5. **Fulfillment and close:** Compliant replacements are dispatched; low-risk cases receive quality sampling and a final R1/R2/R3 checklist before closure.
6. **Recovery:** Every complaint/reopen enters Manual eligibility review before final close or denial. Routine recovery is handled by the case owner; the escalation board remains reserved for severe cases.

**Targets**

Premium and Standard low-risk accessory claims—the two reported accessory segments representing 43% of the stated mix. Premium claims receive strict clock management; Standard claims can use selective review because they accept up to 72 hours with good visibility.

**Mechanism of improvement**

- **Speed:** Retains the current 8–13-hour fast medians for eligible claims rather than routing all Premium low-risk work through a 46-hour manual path.
- **Cost:** Preserves the EUR 0.20 auto screen and confines costly human review and inspection to uncertain or higher-risk cases.
- **Quality:** Moves part selection upstream and checks compatibility before dispatch, targeting the wrong-part pattern behind long complaint tails.
- **Compliance:** Makes value, evidence, customer tier, and risk attributes explicit routing inputs rather than allowing fast approval to bypass them.
- **Customer experience:** Keeps Premium low-risk completion inside the 24-hour service-credit horizon while giving Standard customers visibility when selective review is used.

**Deliberate sacrifices or unresolved issues**

- A remains a fast process, so its complaint/reopen rate may not reach the 3–4% reviewed-path baseline for low-risk claims.
- A serial inspector gate could consume 6–18 hours and cause Premium cases to miss 24 hours. The automated-first design must therefore be measured separately from human review time.
- The forecast 15–35% increase in manual inflow could push reviewer utilization above 82%; deployment must be capacity-controlled.
- A does not solve the 140-hour unclear-evidence cohort or the EUR 410 high-value replacement error; those are addressed by C and B respectively.

# 3. Redesign B: High-value one-owner assurance pod

**Redesigned flow**

1. **Priority assignment:** All high-value device claims enter a dedicated assurance pod at intake, before the automatic/manual eligibility split. Premium cases receive priority clock management; Standard cases use the same control environment without consuming Premium capacity.
2. **One accountable case owner:** A cross-trained coordinator owns the complete case, its SLA, customer communication, and all handoffs. This removes repeated case ownership after evidence, quote, approval, repair, and complaint transitions.
3. **Parallel preparation rather than serial review:** The owner starts three workstreams concurrently:
   - a qualified Manual eligibility or Fraud/risk decision sufficient to satisfy R1;
   - serial/IMEI-based defect isolation and evidence-sufficiency checking;
   - parts availability, certified alternatives, and price confirmation using a controlled price-authorization catalog.
   Final approval still waits for required evidence; only independent preparatory work is parallelized.
4. **Technical remedy decision:** The case owner combines eligibility/risk, diagnostic, and remedy information into one decision package. A claim that has passed fraud/risk must not automatically receive “fast approval” merely because a risk review occurred; the 240 Fraud/riskFast approval transitions show that review and remedy appropriateness are different questions.
5. **Customer choice:** The owner presents available replacement and repair options, expected timing, and service credits in one interaction. Pre-authorized price rules remove much of the Repair quoteCustomer approval delay, while proactive contact reduces the abandonment risk visible on Customer approvalClaim abandoned.
6. **Independent pre-dispatch control:** For replacement, a party other than the case owner verifies evidence completeness, serial-part compatibility, and remedy authorization. For repair, an independent quality check occurs before final closure.
7. **Closed-loop recovery:** Any complaint/reopen returns to the same pod but must pass Manual eligibility review before close or denial. The single daily escalation-board slot is used only for genuine policy exceptions or severe cases.

**Targets**

All high-value device claims—Premium and Standard—because R1/R2 exposure and the EUR 410 wrong-replacement cost make generic fast approval inappropriate. Mid-risk devices are placed in this assurance lane provisionally until their outcome distribution is measured; they can graduate only after demonstrated control performance.

**Mechanism of improvement**

- **Speed:** Parallel eligibility, diagnostic preparation, and price confirmation attack the serial waits behind the 96-hour Premium and 120-hour Standard reviewed-path medians.
- **Cost:** Higher direct labor is accepted in exchange for fewer EUR 410 replacement errors, less rework, and fewer credits and reshipments. The net result cannot be calculated from the supplied data.
- **Quality:** Retains the reviewed path’s materially better high-value complaint outcome—5% Premium and 6% Standard versus 22% and 18% fast—and adds independent part verification.
- **Compliance:** R1 is explicit, R2 requires complete evidence before high-value dispatch, and R3 is built into complaint handling.
- **Customer experience:** Premium high-value cases receive proactive status and fewer repeated handoffs; Standard cases retain slower service but receive materially stronger assurance.

**Deliberate sacrifices or unresolved issues**

- B has the highest direct labor cost of the three designs and is slower than A by design.
- Fraud/risk capacity remains two specialists, so the pod can become a high-cost queue during campaigns or on Mondays unless work is scheduled and cross-trained.
- A common price catalog may be unavailable for unusual parts; exceptions must not be fast-tracked merely to protect the SLA.
- Parallel work can increase work in process and make total elapsed time worse unless the pod owner limits concurrent starts.
- The two-person quality-inspection pool may become the new bottleneck once inspection moves upstream.
- Premium prioritization can starve Standard high-value cases unless capacity and queue rules are separated.

# 4. Redesign C: One-evidence-pack omnichannel resolution

**Redesigned flow**

1. **Evidence-problem detection:** Intake immediately identifies unclear defects or missing evidence rather than sending the claim through Request missing evidenceManual eligibility review before discovering what is actually required.
2. **One structured case file:** A defect-specific evidence matrix defines the complete required package—for example, product identity, symptom description, diagnostic output, images/video, and prior repair information. The customer or agent sees one consolidated request, not a sequence of partial requests.
3. **Choice of channel with one underlying record:**
   - The portal provides guided uploads and validation.
   - Telephone, chat, or in-person assistance uses the same checklist and writes into the same case file.
   - Digital assistance is never the only route, specifically to protect customers with low digital literacy.
4. **Completeness and quality validation:** Automated checks determine whether required fields are present and internally consistent. A human evidence concierge intervenes only when the request is ambiguous or the customer needs assistance.
5. **Concurrent case preparation:** While the customer responds, the system pre-checks entitlement, serial/warranty history, risk flags, known defect patterns, parts availability, and price. Nondependent work therefore does not wait for the evidence response.
6. **One atomic decision:** Once the evidence package is sufficient, eligibility and technical remedy are decided together. Low-risk complete cases can return directly to A’s decision rules; higher-risk cases receive specialist review. Claims above EUR 400 still require Manual eligibility or Fraud/risk review before approval.
7. **Safe fulfillment and feedback:** Fulfillment follows the normal dispatch or repair steps with evidence and part checks. Complaint/reopen cases pass Manual eligibility review. Evidence-request and complaint reasons are fed back to the defect-specific matrix so the process learns rather than repeatedly asking the same question.

**Targets**

The unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort—22% of the stated mix—as well as evidence-heavy cases from any segment. This design changes where and how evidence is produced; it is not another eligibility queue.

**Mechanism of improvement**

- **Speed:** It directly attacks the 34-hour mean/26-hour median/88-hour p90 evidence-to-manual edge and the cohort’s 140-hour median. The portal is expected to reduce repeated requests by 30–45%.
- **Cost:** Fewer repeated requests and manual re-review cycles reduce handling cost, although portal and assisted-channel costs are not quantified.
- **Quality:** A single validated package should reduce incomplete claims, repeated evidence, and the rework caused by contradictory information. It does not directly prove a reduction in wrong-part selection.
- **Compliance:** Evidence completeness becomes an explicit system state before approval and dispatch, reducing R2 leakage while preserving R1 review gates.
- **Customer experience:** Customers make one structured submission and receive visible status, rather than discovering a new requirement after each delay.

**Deliberate sacrifices or unresolved issues**

- The portal may increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy; the assisted route is therefore a required part of the design, not an optional fallback.
- Customer response time may become the critical path, moving rather than eliminating delay.
- “Complete” evidence can still be incorrect, so technical rework may persist.
- CRM, serial, diagnostic, and parts-data inconsistencies can create downstream rework.
- C does not by itself solve high-value part selection; high-value cases must exit into B’s assurance controls.
- A broad rule change combined with more complete evidence could add to the forecast 15–35% manual-review inflow unless demand is staged.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A**

- **Failure mode 1 — Automation leakage:** Stale value, defect, or risk attributes misclassify a high-value or ambiguous claim into the fast lane, recreating R1/R2 violations and wrong-part dispatch.
- **Failure mode 2 — Reviewer overload:** The predicted 15–35% manual inflow pushes utilization above 82%, turning a fast lane into a queue and lengthening Standard cases.
- **Failure mode 3 — Gate latency:** A universal or human-heavy 6–18-hour gate pushes Premium cases beyond 24 hours and raises service-credit exposure.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** From auto eligibility to manual reviewers and the two-person quality-inspection pool, particularly if exception rates are high.

**Redesign B**

- **Failure mode 1 — Fraud capacity saturation:** The pod queues behind the two fraud/risk specialists during Monday peaks or campaigns, turning “one owner” into a waiting list.
- **Failure mode 2 — Hero-owner dependency:** Absence of the trained case owner or too much concurrent work creates hidden queues and repeated handoffs.
- **Failure mode 3 — Premature parallelization:** Parts or pricing are prepared before diagnosis is stable, causing restarts, substitutions, or rework.
- **Failure mode 4 — Control independence failure:** If the pod owner also performs the pre-dispatch check, errors can pass because no independent challenge exists.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** To fraud specialists, the parts-price catalog, technicians, and quality inspectors, with pod-level work in process becoming the leading indicator.

**Redesign C**

- **Failure mode 1 — Digital exclusion:** Customers with low digital literacy abandon more often, so the portal reduces repeat requests but worsens total completion and equity outcomes.
- **Failure mode 2 — Customer-response bottleneck:** Evidence collection becomes dominated by customer response and reminders rather than internal processing.
- **Failure mode 3 — False completeness:** The package is structurally complete but contains incorrect or unusable evidence, increasing technical rework.
- **Failure mode 4 — Data inconsistency:** Serial, CRM, diagnostic, and parts records disagree, causing evidence packages to be rejected or remedies to be changed.
- **Likely bottleneck migration:** From manual eligibility to the evidence concierge, customer-contact channels, digital support, and specialist review of ambiguous evidence.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0 — Repair measurement quality before redesign testing**

- Enrich each claim with customer tier, claim value, defect type, evidence-completeness timestamp, risk-review type, selected part, remedy, complaint/reopen date, reshipment, service credit, and full downstream labor.
- Recompute path and outcome KPIs on claim cohorts rather than transition occurrences. The 3,625 terminal-edge total versus 5,000 starts must be reconciled or explicitly treated as censoring/extraction loss.
- Replay claims against the policy net and classify each violation. Do not use low-precision discovered paths as permissible templates.
- Establish an immediate, non-experimental compliance guardrail: claims above EUR 400 receive Manual or Fraud/risk review, incomplete evidence blocks dispatch except the narrow Premium low-risk exception, and reopened claims cannot close or be denied without Manual review. Capacity and cost effects from this guardrail should not be credited to a redesign.
- Define a common outcome window of at least 60 days, and longer for high-value repair cases, so delayed complaints, reshipments, credits, and rework are observed.

**Stage 1 — First pilot: Redesign A in Standard low-risk accessory claims**

- **Why first:** This cohort has 25% of the stated mix, no high-value compliance downside, and a 72-hour customer tolerance that is less fragile than the Premium 24-hour service-credit horizon. It also allows the two-week auto-rule update to be tested in shadow mode before full release.
- **Design:** Randomize eligible claims into current process, segment-aware routing only, and segment-aware routing plus targeted part-fit gate. If volume is insufficient for three arms, use a matched stepped-wedge comparison.
- **Metrics:** End-to-end median and p90 by Premium/Standard and defect type; evidence wait; complaint/reopen at 30 and 60 days; wrong replacement, reship, credit, and rework; R1/R2/R3 exceptions; abandonment; customer effort/status satisfaction; all-in cost per started and completed claim; manual inflow and utilization; inspector and fraud queue.
- **Proposed go thresholds:**
  - No pilot-attributable compliance exception.
  - Premium low-risk: at least 90% completed within 24 hours and median below 24 hours.
  - Standard low-risk: p90 at or below 72 hours.
  - Complaint/reopen not materially above the current 6% Premium/8% Standard baselines; use a 2-percentage-point adverse-change signal only if the study has adequate power.
  - Wrong-replacement, reship, credit, and rework rates do not increase.
  - All-in 60-day cost per completed claim no more than 5% above current and trending downward if the redesign is to claim a cost benefit.
  - Manual utilization remains below 82%; otherwise cap release and add capacity.
- **Stop/rollback signals:** Any compliance breach caused by routing, sustained reviewer utilization above 82%, Premium 24-hour breach rate materially above baseline, a complaint or wrong-part deterioration, or downstream cost that erases the apparent fast-path saving.

Only after the Standard cell passes should Premium low-risk be added as a separate experimental stratum. Premium should not be pooled with Standard because its 24-hour threshold and R2 exception create different behavior.

**Stage 2 — Redesign C: Separate the evidence redesign from the portal technology**

- **Pilot structure:** Test three cohorts: current evidence process; one-pack assisted workflow without the portal; and one-pack portal with an assisted fallback. This distinguishes the effect of the redesigned evidence request from the effect of digital self-service.
- **Metrics:** Requests per claim, customer response time, abandonment before and after the first request, time from evidence receipt to decision, manual review and fraud inflow, evidence accuracy, downstream technical rework, complaint/reopen, support contacts, customer effort, and all-in cost.
- **Proposed go thresholds:**
  - At least a 30% relative reduction in repeated evidence requests, near the portal’s stated 30–45% potential.
  - Abandonment no more than 2 percentage points above a matched control for the portal arm, with no materially worse outcome for customers requiring assisted service.
  - Clear-case median at least 15–20% below the 140-hour baseline, approximately 112–119 hours.
  - No sustained growth in specialist or fraud queues and no increase in downstream complaints.
- **Stop/rollback signals:** Material abandonment growth, worsening outcomes in low-digital-literacy cohorts, evidence-desk backlog growth, or superficially complete but frequently rejected evidence.

**Stage 3 — Redesign B: Shadow first, then bounded high-value pilots**

- Begin pod decision shadowing during Stages 1–2, comparing pod recommendations with existing Manual/Fraud decisions and identifying R1/R2 gaps without exposing customers to a new fulfillment path.
- Then run separate Premium and Standard high-value live pilots. Compare each against its current reviewed/repair path, not against the policy-inappropriate fast path.
- **Metrics:** Time to the recorded R1 decision; time waiting for parts and prices; customer approval abandonment; repair rework; complaint/reopen; wrong-part incidence and actual cost; service credits; all-in labor and replacement cost; pod, fraud, technician, and inspector queues; customer contacts and satisfaction.
- **Proposed go thresholds:**
  - Zero pilot-attributable R1/R2/R3 violations.
  - Complaint/reopen no worse than the reviewed baselines—5% Premium and 6% Standard—and trending downward.
  - Repaired high-value rework no worse than 12.5%, with an explicit downward target.
  - Actual wrong-replacement cost below current, not merely fewer complaints.
  - Median cycle time approximately 15–20% below the current reviewed medians—about 77–82 hours Premium and 96–102 hours Standard—without quality deterioration.
  - All-in cost, including labor, reship, credits, and errors, no worse than current.
- **Stop/rollback signals:** Quality converges toward fast-path complaint levels, fraud or parts-price queues absorb the theoretical parallelization gain, Standard cases are starved, or total cost rises without offsetting error reduction.

**Sequencing logic**

A goes first because it is the fastest reversible test, has a two-week rule-update window, and can reveal whether predicted manual inflow threatens the 82% capacity threshold. C follows because the 140-hour evidence cohort is the largest cycle-time burden and a validated one-pack record provides cleaner inputs to both other designs. B’s shadow work begins early because high-value compliance risk cannot wait, but live rollout follows after evidence and parts-data foundations are stable. If a high-value compliance incident occurs, B’s bounded pilot should be accelerated rather than waiting for C.

**Learning whether gains are real rather than displaced**

- Use intent-to-treat cohorts containing every started claim, including abandoned and still-open cases; do not evaluate only successfully closed claims.
- Compare treatment with concurrent control or matched historical cases, stratified by customer tier, value, defect, evidence state, and prior complaint history.
- Track total elapsed time to final resolution, not just time to first approval or first closure.
- Include all downstream labor, complaints, reshipments, credits, wrong-part costs, rework, and repeated contacts in cost per started and completed claim.
- Monitor the next activity’s queue and utilization to reveal bottleneck migration.
- Use 60- to 90-day follow-up for complaints and service recovery, and survival/time-to-event analysis for unresolved claims so long-running cases are not silently dropped.
- Have compliance and quality outcomes audited independently of the process owner.

This sequence can establish whether each redesign occupies a genuinely better local Pareto position; the supplied artifacts alone cannot establish exact ROI or global Pareto dominance.
