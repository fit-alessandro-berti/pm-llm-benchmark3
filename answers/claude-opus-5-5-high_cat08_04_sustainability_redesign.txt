# 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Environmental scope (what the redesign is judged on).** The boundary is the operational order-to-delivery footprint visible in the enriched event log. It covers everything from *Receive order* to *Close* or *Cancel due to delay*, including every loop and repeated activity:

| Inside the boundary | Attribute / path evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Production energy for CNC_A, CNC_B, heat_treat and inspection_cell, including emergency production and rework production | `energy_kwh`, `machine_group`, `batch_id`, `batch_size`, `rework_flag` | Measured |
| Outbound transport for each shipment, including second shipments from partial orders and the empty return legs of dedicated vans | `transport_kgco2e`, `route_mode`, *Partial shipment*, *Backorder wait* | Mostly measured. Van empty returns are only known qualitatively and are probably not attributed to orders |
| Production scrap | `material_scrap_kg` | Measured (mass only, not embodied CO2e) |
| Packaging material, including material discarded or added during packaging rework | `package_material_kg`, *Rework packaging* loops | Measured per order. Repack discards are only partly visible |
| Wasted output: items produced but not shipped within 14 days (obsolescence), and orders cancelled after production has started | Batching evidence (6%), *Cancel due to delay* (530 END events) | Partly observed. Obsolete items need linkage to inventory data |
| Damage-related returns and re-deliveries | Evidence mentions customer returns and that reinforced packaging cuts damage returns by 40%. There is no *Return* activity in the DFG | Not directly observed, so it is a key data gap |

**Outside the boundary**, but noted qualitatively where they could change decisions:
- Upstream raw-material and semi-finished production. Only scrap mass is logged, not the embodied footprint of the steel or of the packaging material.
- Inbound logistics, warehouse building energy, and the IT energy of recommenders.
- The use phase and end of life of the parts and the packaging (recyclability, reuse).
- **Customer-side consequences.** A late Critical-downtime part can stop a customer's plant. That can cause idle energy, emergency workarounds and the customer's own expediting. These impacts are outside the boundary, but they are the reason Critical-downtime service must not be traded away for transport savings.

**Functional unit.** The unit is one delivered, conforming order: closed, not returned, not reworked after delivery. It is normalised per line item or per kg shipped where the attributes allow. Batching decisions are also assessed per item because the batching evidence is expressed per item. Cancelled orders are counted as failures that still carry whatever energy, material and transport they consumed.

**Key assumptions**
- `energy_kwh` for a batch is allocated to the orders in it in a consistent way (for example pro rata by item). Per-order energy in the segment table therefore mixes batch-size effects with order size.
- `transport_kgco2e` comes from one consistent factor method across route modes. The prompt only supports the ratio "air ≈ 4–6x ground" and "consolidated truck ≈ 35–55% below individual ground parcels".
- kWh is **not** converted to CO2e because no grid emission factor is given. Material is kept in kg. The indicator set is therefore a **vector** (kWh, kgCO2e from transport, kg scrap, kg packaging, obsolete items), not a single score. Any weighting between these dimensions needs an agreed factor set and stakeholder sign-off.
- Service requirements are expressed relative to `promised_lead_time_hours`. Critical-downtime Service-contract orders have near-zero tolerance for extra delay.

**Remaining uncertainty that must be stated up front**
1. **The DFG is not flow-balanced.** CNC production has about 4,780 incoming arcs but about 3,530 outgoing. Confirm delivery has 9,410 incoming arcs, yet there are 11,200 Close events. Cancellation has 470 incoming arcs but 530 END events. The likely causes are the order-level flattening of multi-line orders, multiple shipments per order, and missing or merged events. Path-level averages are indicative, not exact.
2. The selected path variants cover only about 89% of orders. The rest are the combinatorial disruption variants that reduce model precision.
3. **Associations are not causal.** For example, the "CNC_B → rework" path may mix a machine effect with a part-family effect. The low late rate of air may reflect which orders get air, not what air does. Emergency paths look good on service partly because they receive priority everywhere.
4. The 6% obsolescence figure is conditional on forecast errors. How often forecasts are wrong is unknown.
5. Returns, van empty legs and customer downtime costs are not in the log.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Rough baseline shares below come from the prompt's segment and path averages, used as a hotspot map rather than exact accounting:

- **Transport.** The weighted average is about 10 kgCO2e per order. Critical-downtime orders (11% of orders) produce roughly 40% of it, almost all on the emergency-production → dedicated-van path (8% of orders × 52 kg). Air and van paths together are about 19% of orders and roughly 60% of transport emissions.
- **Energy and scrap.** Made-to-order produces roughly 85–90% of energy and scrap. The CNC_B → rework path alone is only about 5% of orders but accounts for roughly 20–25% of energy and about 25% of scrap.

# 3. Intervention options

All twelve candidates are analysed below. They are grouped by the hotspot they target and cross-referenced where they depend on each other.

### S1. Green shipping recommender (promised lead time, route reliability, segment)
- **Target path or segment:** Every *Package → Ship* decision. It matters most at *Package standard → Ship air* (620), *Package reinforced → Ship air* (800) and *Package reinforced → Dedicated van* (620).
- **Mechanism:** At the ship decision, compute the remaining slack from `promised_lead_time_hours` and the elapsed time. Estimate the on-time probability of each route_mode from historical transit times by lane. Pick the lowest-emission mode whose probability is ≥ 85% (the SP1 rule), in the order consolidated_truck → parcel_ground → parcel_air → dedicated_van. This makes SP1 operational instead of relying on discretion.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Service is neutral if the model is well calibrated. It degrades if transit-time estimates are optimistic (for example during carrier disruption or peak periods). Packaging compatibility must be checked together with the recommended route (see S6), or packaging rework rises. Resilience is neutral or positive if the recommender keeps at least two feasible modes visible per lane (R1), and negative if it collapses volume onto one carrier.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Logged recommendation events with the predicted probability; the override rate; calibration (predicted vs realised on-time by lane and mode); T1, T2, and the late rate by segment. Also check whether overridden cases outperform the recommendations, since that would show the model is missing information.

### S2. Dynamic batching for CNC and heat treatment with waiting-time caps by segment
- **Target path or segment:** *Plan production → Batch wait → CNC production* (3,020 cases) and heat-treatment queues (1,820 cases). This is mainly the MTO Regular batch path (13% share, 118h median, 21% late).
- **Mechanism:** Release a batch when the target size is reached (≥ 12 on CNC_A, > 10 for heat treatment, where energy per item is lowest) **or** when the cap for the least-slack order in the queue is reached, whichever comes first. The cap depends on segment and slack. Critical-downtime orders never wait. Service-contract MTO orders get a short cap. Regular MTO orders with large slack get the longest cap. Batch composition must come from confirmed orders. Speculative extra items are capped to limit obsolescence.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** For tight-slack orders, waits are shorter than today's uncapped 38h, so their late risk falls, including the 1.7x heat-treatment risk. For large-slack orders, waits stay long but within the promise. Average batch size may fall slightly for urgent mixes, which gives up some energy savings. Quality effect is neutral. Resilience improves because queue lengths are bounded, which also reduces the pressure that drives air rescue later.
- **Evidence to monitor:** B1 and B2; E1 per batch-size band; late rate of batch-wait cases split by whether heat treatment was involved; M3 obsolescence per batch; the share of batch-path orders that end up on air, which is the displacement signal.

### S3. Shift Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A when queue and due-date slack allow
- **Target path or segment:** The *batch wait → CNC_B → inspection → rework → ground* variant (5% share, 160h, 32% late, 32 kWh, 1.8 kg scrap).
- **Mechanism:** Rework on CNC_A is about half that on CNC_B for MTO (9% vs 18%). Each avoided rework loop saves repeated CNC energy, scrap, re-inspection time and often an air rescue. Routing is conditional: move a job to CNC_A only if CNC_A's queue plus the job's processing time still fits the slack. Otherwise keep it on CNC_B with an inspection gate (see S7).
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Quality is positive. The loss of CNC_B's speed advantage is compensated by fewer loops, since the CNC_B path is currently the slowest. **Resilience risk:** CNC_A can become a single point of failure and its queue can grow. CNC_B must remain qualified and in use as a fallback.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Rework rate by machine × part family, to check whether the CNC_B effect is really machine-driven or driven by part mix; CNC_A utilisation and queue (RS2); energy and scrap per good item (E1, M1); late rate for shifted jobs.

### S4. Priority pick plus ground instead of air for Standard-stock Service-contract orders, unless slack is insufficient
- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Service-contract orders (16% of orders, 7.8 kgCO2e/order vs 4.2 for Regular). SP1 is violated in 22% of these cases, which is about 420 orders in the log.
- **Mechanism:** Priority picking gains back part of the roughly 24h transit advantage of air. The order then ships by ground where the SP1 probability test passes. Each shifted order avoids most of its transport emissions, because air is 4–6x ground.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Late risk rises slightly only if slack estimates are wrong. Median cycle time rises for shifted orders, but only within the promise. Quality is neutral. Resilience is neutral as long as the air contract stays active for cases with real need and as the R1 alternative.
- **Evidence to monitor:** SP1 conformance; the late rate of shifted cases compared with similar-slack cases that were air-shipped before; pick-to-package time under priority picking; any congestion that priority picking causes for Regular stock orders.

### S5. Consolidated truck departures twice daily for non-urgent stock orders
- **Target path or segment:** Standard-stock Regular orders (39%, median 31h, p90 62h, 7% late) currently on *Package standard → Ship ground* (5,100). Only 850 go by consolidated truck today.
- **Mechanism:** A consolidated truck cuts kgCO2e by about 35–55% versus individual parcels. Two fixed departures a day bound the consolidation wait to roughly half a day instead of 12–24h, and make the wait predictable enough for the S1 recommender to use.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Cycle time for eligible orders rises by up to about 12h. The late rate stays neutral only if eligibility is restricted to orders with enough slack. Fewer parcel touches may reduce damage, but that needs evidence. Resilience improves because truck departures are a second route alongside parcel carriers, which helps R1, provided parcel ground stays available.
- **Evidence to monitor:** T4 (fill, wait); the late rate of consolidated vs ground among similar-slack orders; missed cut-offs that end up on air; damage and returns by mode.

### S6. Packaging–route compatibility checks before shipment
- **Target path or segment:** *Rework packaging* loops: 260 from standard and 360 from reinforced packaging, returning as 420 to reinforced and 130 to standard. Also Q1 violations.
- **Mechanism:** Decide the route (S1) before packaging, or at least in the same step. Then validate package type against route_mode, weight (Q1 applies above 8 kg) and part geometry. This avoids double packing and the discarded material that comes with it. It also avoids under-packing that causes damage returns, and over-packing (130 reinforced → standard reversals suggest some over-packing).
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Cycle time falls, because fewer repacking loops happen right before the shipping cut-off. Quality improves because Q1 conformance rises and damage returns fall. Resilience is neutral.
- **Evidence to monitor:** R2q by package × route; M2; Q1 conformance; returns once a *Return received* event exists.

### S7. Inspection quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations
- **Target path or segment:** *Inspection → Rework production* (470) and *Rework production → CNC* (390), concentrated on CNC_B and certain part families. Also the finding that rushed inspections miss defects, which later show up as packaging rework or customer returns.
- **Mechanism:** Apply risk-based inspection depth. High-risk machine/part combinations get a mandatory full inspection with protected capacity. Low-risk ones get a lighter check. The gate can also issue the "low damage risk" certificate that Q1 allows, so certified heavy parts can use standard packaging and save material. Catching defects early avoids downstream waste: return transport, re-production and extra scrap.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Inspection time per high-risk case increases, which adds queue risk at inspection_cell. The net cycle time should fall because rework loops are the slowest path. Quality is strongly positive. Resilience depends on inspection capacity, and the gate must not become a new bottleneck.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Inspection queue time and utilisation; first-pass yield; defects found after inspection (packaging rework, returns); the share of heavy MTO parts certified low-risk and their subsequent damage rate.

### S8. Replace some dedicated vans with shared emergency courier lanes
- **Target path or segment:** *Package reinforced → Dedicated van* (620, 52 kgCO2e/order on the critical path). Vans often return empty.
- **Mechanism:** Pool urgent shipments on scheduled or shared express lanes (multi-drop or regional shuttles). This spreads each trip across several orders and removes empty legs. Dedicated vans remain for true downtime cases where the shared lane cannot meet the downtime window.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Transit time is somewhat less deterministic than a dedicated van. That is acceptable for precautionary urgent orders, but not for true downtime without a proven lane on-time rate. A shared lane with a courier partner can add a route option (a resilience gain), or create dependence on one partner (a resilience risk).
- **Evidence to monitor:** T5 (van utilisation, empty legs, which requires trip data); lane on-time rate for critical orders; the Critical-downtime late rate by urgency class; SP2 conformance.

### S9. Split Critical-downtime into true downtime and precautionary urgent
- **Target path or segment:** All Critical-downtime orders (1,320), especially *Availability check → Emergency production* (760) and the van path. The existing "Critical → stock/ground exception" path (3%, 23h, 9% late) shows that some critical orders tolerate ground, while others clearly do not.
- **Mechanism:** Classify urgency at order receipt: machine down now vs spare or preventive stocking. Precautionary orders go through standard or priority paths with S1 routing. They are served from stock or small-batch production with ground or shared lanes instead of emergency production plus van. True downtime keeps the full emergency treatment.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Service for true downtime is protected, or even improved, because emergency capacity is less congested. Emergency production has 8% rework, and less rush may reduce it. The risk is misclassification: a true downtime order treated as precautionary could be very costly for the customer.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Classification event and reason; late rate and customer-downtime complaints by class; reclassification rate (precautionary → true after receipt); emergency production share; the share of true-downtime orders on van.

### S10. Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular made-to-order customers
- **Target path or segment:** MTO Regular orders (22%, 94h median, 180h p90, 18% late). Promises that are too tight force immediate production and air, as on the MTO → immediate CNC → air path (6%: 24 kWh, 23 kgCO2e).
- **Mechanism:** At order entry, offer a slightly longer promise date that is reliably met, with a green option that allows full batches and ground or consolidated shipping. Service-contract and critical orders are excluded. A more realistic promise also cuts late deliveries, because many "late" orders are late against unrealistic promises.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Promised lead time gets longer, but reliability improves and the late rate should fall. There is a commercial risk that customers take their business elsewhere, or that they ask for Critical classification to get speed (see Section 5).
- **Evidence to monitor:** Acceptance rate of green promises; late rate against the promise; orders that switch category; batch sizes achieved; cancellation due to delay; customer churn and reorder patterns.

### S11. Reduce partial shipments by changing availability-check rules
- **Target path or segment:** *Pick from stock → Partial shipment* (1,010) → *Backorder wait* (220) → *Cancel* (60). This path is 7% of orders, 79h, 24% late, 7.2 kgCO2e (two shipments).
- **Mechanism:** At the availability check, predict the time to replenish missing lines. If the full order can still ship within the promise, hold and ship complete (one shipment, one package). Ship partial only when the available lines are what the customer needs urgently, for example for Service-contract customers or when the line is critical to a repair. Also route missing lines to consolidated replenishment.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Delivery of the first lines is delayed for held orders. The late rate may fall, because backorder paths are the slow ones, as long as the replenishment predictions are accurate. Cancellations may fall if customers value complete delivery, or rise if they value speed. There is a small resilience risk if holding stock hides shortages.
- **Evidence to monitor:** T3; the late rate and cancellations of held vs partial orders; replenishment prediction accuracy; customer contacts.

### S12. Sustainability exception log for SP1/SP2/Q1 overrides
- **Target path or segment:** All air and van decisions, and Q1 packaging exceptions. SP2 justification is missing in 31% of van cases, and SP1 is violated in 22% of Standard-stock Service-contract cases.
- **Mechanism:** Every override becomes an explicit event with a reason code, approver and expected slack. This makes premium transport visible and reviewable, supplies training data for S1, and removes "silent defaults" to air or van.
- **Service/quality/resilience effect:** Neutral if the log is lightweight. Legitimate emergencies must never wait for approval: justification can be recorded immediately after dispatch for true downtime. If it is burdensome, staff will pick a generic reason code, and the data loses value.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Share of overrides with a valid reason; override outcomes (were they needed?); exception rates by team and customer; SP1/SP2/Q1 conformance over time.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching → waiting → air rescue (energy traded for transport).** Larger batches cut energy per item from about 2.8 to 1.3 kWh on CNC_A. But the median batch wait rises from 4h to 38h, and heat-treatment waits carry 1.7x late risk. Late orders are then often rescued by air. That swaps a few kWh per item for an air shipment that is 4–6x more carbon-intensive than ground. Without a grid factor this cannot be netted exactly, but the per-item kWh savings are small compared with an avoided air shipment. *Mitigation:* slack-based caps (S2), and monitoring the share of batch-path orders that end on air.

2. **Batching → obsolescence (embodied waste).** 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong. An obsolete part carries all its production energy, scrap and material with no benefit. That wipes out the per-item energy gain for the batch. *Mitigation:* fill batches only with confirmed demand, cap speculative extras, and track obsolescence per batch (M3).

3. **Transport consolidation → service erosion → premium rescue.** Consolidated trucks need a 12–24h consolidation wait. If eligibility ignores slack, missed cut-offs are shipped by air, which is worse than the parcel ground that was replaced. Concentrating on one truck lane also weakens R1 redundancy. *Mitigation:* fixed departures (S5), a slack gate in S1, and keeping parcel ground active.

4. **Packaging reduction → damage → returns (material traded for transport and production).** Removing reinforced packaging halves package material but loses the 40% reduction in damage returns. Each return needs return transport, replacement production (energy and scrap) and a second outbound shipment, often by air because the customer is waiting. The opposite over-correction, reinforced packaging for everything, doubles material where it is not needed. *Mitigation:* S6 compatibility rules, and S7 certification for Q1 exemptions based on inspection evidence, not convenience.

5. **Rework control → inspection congestion → rushed inspection.** A stricter gate (S7) adds work at inspection_cell. The evidence already shows that queues at inspection lead to rushed inspections, missed defects and rework loops, so the gate can recreate the problem it targets. *Mitigation:* risk-based gating (full inspection only for high-risk combinations) and protected inspection capacity. Monitor inspection queue time next to first-pass yield.

6. **Machine shift → bottleneck and single point of failure.** Moving MTO jobs from CNC_B to CNC_A lowers rework. But it can overload CNC_A, extend queues, trigger emergency production and air, and leave the plant exposed if CNC_A fails. *Mitigation:* conditional routing (S3), a CNC_A utilisation ceiling, and CNC_B improvement with S7 gating instead of abandoning CNC_B.

7. **Emergency production disrupting batches.** 760 emergency jobs go straight to CNC production. Each one can break a forming batch or force an early release, raising energy per item for the other orders in that batch. *Mitigation:* S9 lowers the number of emergency jobs. Reserve capacity or time slots for true downtime.

8. **Customer behaviour: reclassification and gaming.** If green defaults (S4, S10) make standard service feel slower, customers may declare orders Critical-downtime or true downtime to regain speed (S9 gaming). They may also split orders into many small urgent ones, which undoes consolidation and batching. *Mitigation:* classification criteria linked to the service contract, audited reclassification rates, and reliable green promises so customers do not need to escalate.

9. **Customer behaviour: displacement outside the boundary.** Longer promises or fewer partial shipments may lead customers to hold more safety stock of spare parts (inventory and obsolescence moved to the customer), to place duplicate orders and cancel one (cancellations are already 530), or to move urgent business to competitors who expedite by air. The distributor's footprint then looks better while the system footprint does not improve. For true downtime, a slower delivery can mean customer plant downtime, whose impacts are likely much larger than the transport saved. *Mitigation:* keep the true-downtime service level, monitor duplicate orders, cancellations and churn, and treat customer downtime as a hard constraint.

10. **Partial-shipment reduction → longer waits → cancellation.** Holding orders to ship complete saves a second shipment. But if replenishment predictions are wrong, waits grow, and the *Backorder wait → Cancel* pattern (60 cases) can grow. Cancelled orders waste the picking, packaging and possibly production already done. *Mitigation:* hold only when the predicted completion fits the promise, and escalate automatically.

11. **Shared emergency lanes → hidden variability and dependence.** Removing empty van legs saves emissions. But shared lanes can miss downtime windows and create dependence on one courier partner, which weakens R1. *Mitigation:* use shared lanes for precautionary orders first, keep a dedicated-van fallback, and measure lane reliability.

12. **Metric gaming and paper conformance.** Exception logs with generic reason codes improve SP2 "conformance" without changing behaviour. Allocating energy per batch can make large batches look efficient even when the items become obsolete. *Mitigation:* reason-code audits, per-good-item indicators, and outcome checks on overrides.

---

# 6. Balanced recommendation

**Redesign package: "Right first time, right urgency, right mode, and batch only with the slack that is left".**

The core logic is that **promise slack is a shared budget**. Rework, partial shipments and packaging loops *consume* slack. Air and dedicated vans are then used to *buy it back* at a high transport cost. Batching and consolidation also *consume* slack, in return for energy or transport savings. The package therefore frees slack first by fixing quality and flow. It then spends slack on the options that give the most environmental benefit per hour of delay, while keeping hard limits on service and resilience.

**Stage 1: Stop wasting slack (quality and flow first; win–win measures).**
- **S7 + S3 together:** risk-based inspection gating on high-rework CNC_B/part combinations, and conditional routing to CNC_A under a CNC_A utilisation ceiling, with CNC_B kept qualified. This targets the path with the highest energy, scrap and lateness per order (32 kWh, 1.8 kg, 32% late). It also reduces the air rescues that follow rework.
- **S6:** decide the route before packaging and validate the package against route_mode, weight (Q1) and geometry. This removes most of the 620 packaging-rework loops and enforces Q1, which is associated with fewer return and rework loops. Q1 exemptions are allowed only with a certificate from the inspection gate.
- **S11:** ship complete when the predicted replenishment fits the promise, and ship partial when the customer needs the available lines.

**Stage 2: Spend slack where the environmental return is highest (transport).**
- **S1 + S4:** the SP1-based recommender applied first to Standard-stock Service-contract orders, the clearest avoidable air (22% SP1 violations). It uses priority picking to create the slack for ground. Air remains available and contracted, both for insufficient slack and as the R1 alternative.
- **S5:** twice-daily consolidated trucks for Standard-stock Regular orders whose slack covers the next departure. S1 decides eligibility, so orders that would miss the promise stay on parcel ground.

**Stage 3: Spend what slack remains on production energy (batching), within strict caps.**
- **S2:** dynamic batching with caps based on slack and segment. Critical orders bypass it. Service-contract orders get short caps. Regular MTO orders get the longest caps. Batches are filled only with confirmed demand, which keeps the obsolescence risk low. This comes after transport because the per-item energy savings are likely smaller in CO2e than the avoided premium transport, and must never cause air rescues.
- **S10:** offer green promise dates to Regular MTO customers on an opt-in basis. This gives batching and ground shipping enough slack by agreement instead of creating it by making orders late.

**Stage 4: Right urgency for critical orders (the largest transport hotspot, handled carefully).**
- **S9 before S8:** first classify true downtime vs precautionary urgent at order receipt, with auditable criteria linked to the service contract. Only precautionary orders move to shared emergency lanes or priority ground, and only after the lanes prove their reliability. True downtime keeps emergency production and dedicated vans where needed, with justification recorded immediately afterwards (SP2) so dispatch is never delayed.
- **S12:** a lightweight exception log with structured reason codes across SP1, SP2 and Q1. It supports governance and feeds back into the recommender.

**Guardrails that make the package acceptable.** The numeric thresholds below are illustrative and must be set by management.
- **Service.** Late delivery per segment must not rise above baseline plus a small tolerance: for example +1–2 percentage points for Regular segments, no increase for Service-contract, and no increase for true downtime. The Critical-downtime late rate (baseline 4%) is a hard limit.
- **Quality.** Q1 conformance must rise. First-pass yield and the damage/return rate must not worsen.
- **Resilience.** R1 is maintained: every Service-contract lane keeps at least one alternative active route. CNC_A stays below a utilisation ceiling, and CNC_B stays qualified. Inspection queue time stays within a limit.
- **Obsolescence.** Items not shipped within 14 days stay at or below baseline.

**Why this package is balanced**
- **Environmental coverage.** It targets the measured hotspots: air and van (about 60% of transport emissions from about 19% of orders), MTO production (about 85–90% of energy and scrap), and the CNC_B rework path (about 20–25% of energy and scrap from about 5% of orders). It also accounts for obsolescence, returns and repacking, not just direct emissions.
- **Service.** Fixing quality first creates slack before any greener option spends it. Every transport or batching change is gated on slack and on the SP1 probability test, and true downtime is protected.
- **Quality.** Rework is treated as both an environmental and a service problem. Packaging choices are decided on damage risk, not on minimising packaging mass alone.
- **Resilience.** Air, parcel ground, consolidated truck and dedicated vans all remain in the network, with their use calibrated by segment and slack. Machine and inspection capacity limits prevent new single points of failure.
- **Not everything premium is treated as waste.** Air for tight-slack Service-contract orders and dedicated vans for true downtime remain legitimate choices. Only avoidable premium transport is targeted.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

**7.1 Instrument the log before the change.** New events and attributes:
- *Urgency classified* (true downtime / precautionary, with reason).
- *Route recommended* (recommended mode, predicted on-time probability for each mode, slack at decision time).
- *Route override* (reason code, approver).
- *Van justification*.
- *Packaging compatibility check* (result).
- *Inspection certificate* (low damage risk).
- *Batch released* (trigger: target size reached or cap reached; queue slack).
- *Return received* and *Re-shipment*.
- *Item obsolete/written off*, linked to batch_id.
- Carrier disruption flag by period and lane.
- Van trip legs, including empty returns.

Decision-time attributes are essential. They allow SP1 to be judged on what was known at decision time, not with hindsight.

**7.2 Baseline and study design**
- Freeze a pre-change baseline of all Section 2 indicators, stratified by case_type × customer_type × weight class × disruption regime.
- **Roll out in stages:** by warehouse or shipping cell, by week, or by customer cohort. For Regular customers, S1 can use randomised or alternating assignment. Service-contract and Critical customers are *not* randomised onto riskier options; they follow the slack gate only.
- Estimate effects with difference-in-differences between treated and not-yet-treated units, or with interrupted time series. Control for case mix, seasonality and demand shocks, and compare cases with similar slack. Report environmental effects as a vector (kWh, kgCO2e from transport, kg scrap, kg packaging, obsolete items) with uncertainty intervals, not as a single score.
- **Re-discover process models per stage** and compare DFGs and variants. Check that loops (*Inspection → Rework → CNC*, *Rework packaging*, *Partial shipment → Backorder*) shrink and that no new loops appear, such as consolidation-miss → air.

**7.3 Conformance monitoring.** Use declarative, data-aware rule checking, because the discovered model's precision is low in disruption variants.

| Policy | Rule checked on the log | Target direction |
|---|---|---|
| SP1 | If the recorded on-time probability for ground or consolidated is ≥ 85% at *Route recommended*, then the shipment mode ∈ {ground, consolidated}, unless a *Route override* with a valid reason precedes the shipment | Violations in Standard-stock Service-contract fall from 22% towards a low single-digit share. Also audit the reverse case: ground chosen where the probability was < 85% and the order was late |
| SP2 | *Dedicated van* must be preceded by *Urgency classified = true downtime* and followed by *Van justification* within a short window. Precedence and response constraints | Missing justification falls from 31% towards zero. The van share among precautionary orders falls |
| Q1 | For MTO orders with weight > 8 kg: *Package reinforced* **or** a preceding *Inspection certificate* | Violations fall. Check that the link between Q1 violations and returns/rework weakens |
| R1 | For each Service-contract lane, ≥ 1 alternative route mode was active (contracted and used or tested) in the monitoring window | 100% of lanes |

Monitor conformance separately for normal and disruption periods. During disruptions, expect and allow more exceptions, but they must still be logged.

**7.4 Segment-level service, quality and resilience monitoring**
- **Weekly control charts per segment:** median and p90 cycle time, late rate against `promised_lead_time_hours`, and cancellation due to delay. Critical true-downtime is monitored daily.
- **Calibration of the S1 recommender:** predicted vs realised on-time rate by lane and mode (a calibration curve), with automatic tightening of the threshold if realised on-time falls below 85% for recommended ground or consolidated shipments.
- **Batching:** batch wait vs cap, realised batch size, energy per good item, 14-day obsolescence per batch, and the share of batch-path orders ending on air.
- **Quality:** first-pass yield by machine × part family, packaging rework rate, damage and return rate by route × package type, and the inspection queue time. This queue is the leading indicator for rushed inspections.
- **Resilience:** CNC_A utilisation vs ceiling, emergency production share, R1 lane coverage, and performance in flagged disruption episodes compared with baseline disruption episodes.
- **Displacement signals:** reclassification rate into true downtime, order splitting, duplicate orders, cancellations, and churn among Regular MTO customers.

**7.5 Exception governance**
- The exception log (S12) records: case, policy (SP1/SP2/Q1), reason code from a controlled list, approver, slack at decision time, and outcome (on time? returned?).
- **True downtime is never delayed by approval.** Justification can be completed after dispatch within a fixed window, and a missing justification is escalated.
- **Monthly review** by a cross-functional board (logistics, production, quality, customer service, sustainability). It reviews exception rates by team, customer and reason. It also reviews whether overrides were justified in hindsight, feeding back into recommender features and classification criteria, and it decides whether caps and thresholds should change.
- **Audit sampling** of reason codes, to prevent a generic "customer request" code from becoming a loophole. Frequent-exception customers are handled in contract discussions, for example Service-contract terms defining true downtime.
- **Stop and rollback rules:** if any guardrail in Section 6 is breached for two consecutive review periods in a segment, the related intervention reverts for that segment until root-cause analysis is done. Examples: the late rate rises above tolerance, the CNC_A ceiling is exceeded, inspection queues grow, obsolescence rises, or R1 coverage drops.
- **Annual re-baselining** of emission and energy factors, once grid factors and material footprints are available. At that point the kWh and kg indicators can be converted to CO2e, and the slack-spending priority (transport first, batching second) can be re-checked.
