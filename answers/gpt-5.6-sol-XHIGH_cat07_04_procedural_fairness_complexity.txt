# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ substantially: 78% for Group A, 63% for B, 69% for C, and 58% for D. These are important screening indicators, but they are not evidence of discrimination by themselves. The groups differ in measured risk, documentation completeness, channel, prior cases, and support needs. Approval rates also cannot establish decision accuracy without underlying statutory eligibility information.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity.** The preliminary model still estimates lower approval probabilities for B (-5.5 percentage points), C (-2.2 points), and D (-3.1 points) after the listed controls. These residuals warrant investigation, especially B’s. They are not necessarily causal: confidence intervals, model specification, common support, and key eligibility variables are unavailable. Moreover, `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may themselves reflect earlier inequities.

- **Procedural burden.** Fairness concerns include the number of reviews, document requests, handovers, repeated contacts, and rework—not merely the final decision. B and C are much less likely to use the short V1 path and much more likely to enter document, language-support, or accommodation paths. Those steps may be necessary and rights-preserving, but avoidable handovers and queues create an additional burden associated with exercising those rights.

- **Timeliness.** B and C have longer medians and much longer 90th-percentile cycle times. Adjusted cycle-time differences remain +2.6 days for B and +4.1 days for C, suggesting that measured case mix does not explain all delay. The most visible bottlenecks are the 3.8-day interval after Language Support Contact and the 4.7-day interval after Accommodation Review. Fairness should be evaluated for both median and tail delays and separately for time to initial decision and post-decision appeal time.

- **Consistency of routing.** Different routing is fair when based consistently on valid policy criteria—for example, high risk requiring Fraud Review, an accommodation request requiring Accommodation Review, or a language request triggering Language Support Contact. The relevant test is whether applicants with the same policy-relevant circumstances receive the same routing, not whether every group has the same path distribution. The adjusted excesses in Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents for B and C indicate possible inconsistent scrutiny, although coarse or omitted complexity could also explain them.

- **Quality and accessibility of support.** The presence of a Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review event does not establish effective support. Relevant dimensions include timeliness, interpreter or specialist qualification, accessible format, successful contact, whether deadlines are extended appropriately, and whether the applicant can understand and participate in the process. These qualities are not measured.

- **Appeal and rework burden.** B and D have adjusted appeal differences of +4.4 and +5.7 percentage points, respectively. Raw rework is also elevated for B, C, and D. A high appeal rate could reflect poor initial decisions, inadequate explanations, or greater disagreement, but it could also reflect effective access to appeal rights. Appeal reversal, abandonment, issue type, representation, and resolution quality are needed before interpreting it. Rework is more directly suggestive of avoidable burden, but no adjusted rework analysis is provided.

- **Resource-allocation fairness.** Equal staff counts are not the proper benchmark; effective capacity should reflect legitimate need. Concentrating B cases among bilingual staff and C cases among accommodation specialists is partly justified by skill requirements. However, assigning these cases to small, overloaded pools without demand-adjusted capacity makes slower service predictable. Extra handovers in two regions further suggest that organizational design, rather than inherent case complexity alone, contributes to burden.

Overall, procedural fairness requires consistent rules, effective support, proportionate scrutiny, understandable decisions, and comparable avoidable waiting—not identical paths or identical approval rates.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

**1. Define distinct estimands.**

At least three comparisons are needed:

1. **Total group disparity:** How much do outcomes and burdens differ after standardizing on baseline case characteristics, while retaining language and accommodation pathways as possible mechanisms?
2. **Complexity-adjusted disparity:** Among applicants with comparable legitimate complexity, do group differences remain?
3. **Route-conditional implementation disparity:** Among cases that legitimately need the same support or review, are waiting, handovers, service quality, and decisions comparable?

Controlling for Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review in the total-disparity model would remove part of the burden being evaluated. They should instead be treated as mediators and then used as conditioning variables for route-specific diagnostics.

**2. Construct a case-level analysis table.**

Use one row per case, fixing attributes at their appropriate time:

- Baseline or pre-routing variables: household size, prior-case count, region, submission month, channel, language preference, accommodation request.
- Potential complexity variables: risk band and documentation completeness, but only after confirming when and how they are generated.
- Process variables: variant, number of activities, repeat activities, document requests, reviews, handovers, assigned resources, and route-specific waiting.
- Outcomes: approval, time to Decision Draft, time to Applicant Notification, total time to closure, appeal, appeal duration, and rework.

Primary-decision time should be separated from appeal-inclusive cycle time because V6 mechanically has a longer endpoint.

**3. Match or stratify comparable cases.**

Use exact or coarsened-exact matching, propensity weighting, or entropy balancing on:

- `region` and submission month;
- `channel`;
- `household_size_band`;
- `prior_case_count`;
- risk and documentation bands in one specification;
- language preference and accommodation request in route-conditional specifications.

For example:

- Compare B and A applicants within the same region, month, channel, risk band, and documentation band.
- Compare language-support cases across regions, workers, and channels.
- Compare accommodation-request cases handled by different specialists or regional arrangements.
- Compare Fraud Review referrals within the same risk band rather than comparing all D cases with all A cases.

Report covariate balance and overlap. If few A applicants have minority-language or accommodation needs, do not extrapolate a matched estimate beyond common support.

**4. Estimate adjusted process and outcome differences.**

Use standardized risks or hierarchical regression for:

- approval;
- Request Missing Documents;
- Supervisor Review and Fraud Review;
- appeal and rework;
- number of events, repeated activities, and handovers.

Use survival or quantile methods for time outcomes, including censoring and p50/p90 effects. Include group-by-region and group-by-channel interactions, with uncertainty clustered or modeled at resource and region levels.

A residual group coefficient is evidence of an unexplained disparity, not automatically discrimination. A discrimination inference would require stronger evidence that legally equivalent applicants were treated differently because of group membership or a closely associated proxy, after addressing omitted variables and measurement bias.

**5. Compare paths and variants.**

Within matched strata:

- Compare variant probabilities and transition frequencies.
- Compare timing within the same variant or same route segment.
- Count loops, repeated document requests, reversals, and skipped or out-of-order required steps.
- Assess whether support and review steps occur when required and whether unnecessary referrals occur among ineligible cases.

Conditioning on an entire variant is useful for diagnosing implementation, but the path is a mediator and possibly a collider. Thus, within-variant comparisons should not replace the total-disparity analysis.

**6. Compare resource assignment.**

For applicants eligible for the same type of worker:

- Model the probability of assignment to each resource, specialist pool, or overloaded office.
- Compare workload at assignment, queue age, caseload, handovers, and completion time.
- Distinguish skill-based assignment from assignment to systematically slower resources.
- Compare outcomes within worker, across workers, and under workload-adjusted models.
- Examine whether regional office practices explain B’s additional Supervisor Reviews or document requests.

Resource fixed effects can identify worker-level variation but should be interpreted carefully: assignment and workload may themselves be part of the unfair mechanism.

**7. Decompose waiting time.**

Decompose cycle-time differences into:

1. route frequency—how often each group enters slower paths;
2. within-route elapsed time;
3. active service versus queue waiting, where interval data permit;
4. handover delay;
5. rework and repeated-step delay;
6. post-decision appeal time.

A standardized decomposition can hold route frequencies fixed and vary within-route times, then reverse the exercise. Because decomposition order can affect attribution, use a Shapley or g-computation approach and bootstrap whole cases. Means are additive; medians and 90th percentiles require distributional simulation rather than summing segment medians.

**8. Conduct sensitivity and subgroup analyses.**

Estimate at least these specifications:

- baseline variables only;
- baseline plus risk and documentation;
- excluding or replacing `risk_score_band`;
- treating documentation completeness as a possible mediator rather than a neutral control;
- route-conditional models including language and accommodation need;
- models with and without appeal-inclusive time.

Test omitted-variable sensitivity and alternative risk thresholds. Audit missing data and trimming from lack of overlap. Repeat comparisons by region, online versus in-person channel, month, and intersections such as minority language × in-person and accommodation request × region. The two regions with extra handovers should be analyzed separately.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

| Pattern | Potentially justified component | Remaining concern |
|---|---|---|
| V1 is used by 42% of A but only 19% of B, 22% of C, and 18% of D | A has fewer incomplete, high-risk, language-support, and accommodation cases | The short route is much less accessible to other groups, and adjusted cycle-time differences remain after measured controls |
| B uses V2 at 34% versus 24% for A | B has substantially more incomplete documentation | B still has an adjusted +9.5-point probability of Request Missing Documents, suggesting either unmeasured document complexity, coarse completeness measurement, or inconsistent standards |
| B uses V4 at 24%, with a 19.2-day median | Language support is a legitimate and rights-preserving route | The 3.8-day post-support interval, reliance on seven bilingual workers, overloaded officers, and additional handovers indicate an avoidable capacity burden |
| C uses V5 at 31%, with a 21.6-day median | Accommodation Review is required for accommodation requests | The 4.7-day specialist queue, five-person specialist pool, role overlap with Supervisor Review, and extra handovers plausibly contribute to the adjusted +4.1-day cycle difference |
| D uses V3 at 28% versus 9% for A | D has 51% high-risk cases versus 18% for A, so more Fraud Review is expected | Risk-score validity is unresolved; within-band referral and treatment consistency must be tested |
| V6 is more common for B and especially D | Different eligibility facts or greater disagreement may legitimately produce more appeals | Adjusted appeal differences remain +4.4 points for B and +5.7 points for D; explanation quality, initial-decision accuracy, and appeal outcomes are unknown |

Several effects compound for B. Submit-to-verification wait is 1.4 days versus 0.8 for A, partly associated with in-person submission. Completeness-to-document-request wait is 1.6 days versus 0.4, and Supervisor Review-to-Decision Draft is 1.9 days versus 1.0. B also has adjusted increases in both document requests and supervisor review, so it appears more likely to enter slower stages and then wait longer once there.

C’s principal disparity is procedural delay rather than a large adjusted approval difference. The accommodation pathway is legitimate, but a 4.7-day interval before Eligibility Assessment and concentration among five specialists make accommodation use costly in time. The additional specialist-to-supervisor handoffs may also require applicants to repeat information or increase the risk of communication loss.

D’s pattern is more consistent with measured risk-based routing: the excess cycle time falls to +0.9 days after adjustment, and its fraud-review path is common. That is evidence that case mix explains a substantial portion of D’s raw difference, but not that the process is necessarily fair. The lower adjusted approval probability and particularly high adjusted appeal probability remain unexplained, while the risk score itself may reflect historical disparities.

The resource evidence is concerning but not conclusive. Specialist assignment is appropriate when specialized skill is needed, and the number of workers alone does not reveal comparable caseloads or productivity. Nevertheless, known overload, small specialist pools, and regional handover chains are strong operational mechanisms for unequal waiting. A fair implementation would preserve support steps while providing enough capacity that exercising a right does not predictably create excessive delay.

The variant medians should not be interpreted as causal effects of individual activities. V1, V4, V5, and V6 represent different populations and sometimes different endpoints. The full difference between 5.8 days for V1 and 19.2 or 21.6 days for support variants cannot be attributed solely to support activity.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate need- and risk-based routing.** Higher measured risk can lead to Fraud Review, language needs to Language Support Contact, and accommodation requests to Accommodation Review. This mechanism explains part of the path distribution without implying unfair treatment.

2. **Documentation and channel complexity.** In-person submissions and incomplete or differently formatted documents may require more verification and follow-up. B’s case mix makes some extra requests and waiting plausible, although it does not fully explain the adjusted excess.

3. **Specialist capacity bottlenecks.** Seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists serve concentrated demand. Queueing and role overlap can produce long waits even if assignment rules are neutral.

4. **Fragmented routing and handovers.** General worker  specialist  general worker or supervisor chains can add scheduling delay, duplicate review, communication loss, and repeated requests. The concentration in two regions points to organizational implementation rather than applicant complexity alone.

5. **Differential scrutiny or inconsistent local practice.** The adjusted excesses in document requests and Supervisor Review for B and C could arise if workers or regions apply completeness and escalation standards differently. The present evidence does not distinguish this from omitted complexity.

6. **Biased or poorly calibrated inputs.** Historical enforcement or case outcomes may influence `risk_score_band`, causing similar current applicants to receive different risk classifications. Documentation completeness may also encode language accessibility, form design, or whether prior assistance was available.

7. **Decision-explanation deficiencies.** Unclear, untranslated, or inaccessible explanations could increase appeals and rework, even where the underlying eligibility decision is correct.

8. **Measurement and endpoint effects.** If cycle time includes Appeal Review for some cases but ends at notification or activation for others, group differences partly reflect different endpoint definitions. Timestamps may also combine queue and service time.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Missing substantive eligibility facts.** Risk, documentation, household size, and prior cases are not substitutes for the legal facts determining eligibility. Income, assets, benefit type, residency details, program-specific criteria, urgency, and exact document requirements are absent. Adjusted approval differences therefore cannot establish differential decision accuracy.

- **Historical bias in risk scores.** A risk score may inherit unequal investigation, labeling, or confirmed-fraud practices. Controlling for it can suppress a disparity produced by the score itself. The score’s calibration, source variables, error rates, and ground-truth labels are unknown.

- **Measurement bias in documentation completeness.** “Incomplete” may reflect inaccessible forms, untranslated instructions, unavailable records, or inconsistent worker judgment. If completeness is assessed after interaction with the process, it may be a mediator rather than a baseline confounder.

- **Missing applicant needs.** `language_preference` does not capture proficiency, literacy, dialect, interpreter preference, or whether support was actually accepted and effective. An accommodation flag does not identify modality, urgency, complexity, accessibility format, or whether the requested accommodation was fulfilled.

- **Unobserved workload and capacity.** Daily workload granularity does not reveal queue state at assignment time, shift coverage, leave, concurrent complex cases, interruptions, or appointment availability. This limits conclusions about whether a particular worker caused delay.

- **Support and interaction quality.** The log does not capture explanation clarity, respect, dignity, applicant understanding, repeat storytelling, missed contacts, interpreter quality, or accessible document format.

- **Appeal quality.** `appeal_flag` does not show reversal, partial relief, issue type, representation, abandonment, time to hearing, explanation quality, or whether applicants knew of their appeal rights. Both high and low appeal rates can be problematic for different reasons.

- **Timestamp semantics.** With only `time:timestamp`, it may be unclear whether events represent start, completion, or data-entry time. Inter-event elapsed time cannot automatically be labeled queue waiting. Regional time zones, weekends, batching, and backdated entries may also affect results.

- **Broad group categories and intersectionality.** Applicant groups may hide important differences within language, disability, region, channel, and socioeconomic circumstances. Small intersectional subgroups may also produce imprecise estimates.

- **No uncertainty information.** The preliminary adjusted differences lack confidence intervals, diagnostics, missing-data analysis, overlap assessment, and specification checks. Statistical and practical uncertainty therefore remain substantial.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation | Intended benefit | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** through qualified hiring, cross-training, regional pooling, backup coverage, and demand forecasting | Reduces specialist queues and makes support rights effective | Hiring is costly; poorly designed cross-training may reduce expertise; pooled or remote support may be less culturally appropriate; unused reserve capacity may appear inefficient |
| **Create service-level targets for support steps** covering time to first contact, completion, and p90 delay, while continuing to report end-to-end time | Prevents support stages from being excluded from timeliness accountability | Targets may encourage superficial contacts, premature completion, queue shifting, or prioritization of easy cases; quality guardrails are necessary |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** using a primary case owner, integrated specialist pods, warm handoffs, or parallel specialist consultation | Reduces delay, repeated explanations, and information loss | Parallel work can cause rework; broader case-owner roles may overload staff; fewer handoffs can reduce independent review if not designed carefully |
| **Improve decision and document-request explanations** with specific reasons, plain language, qualified translation, accessible formats, and clear appeal instructions | May reduce repeat document requests, avoidable appeals, and uncertainty | Drafting and translation can increase service time and cost; appeal rates may initially rise because applicants better understand their rights, which should not be treated as failure |
| **Monitor adjusted disparities continuously** for approval, route entry, waiting, p90 delay, handovers, repeated requests, rework, appeal, and resource assignment | Detects persistent or regional implementation differences | Models can reproduce biased assumptions; small-cell reporting creates privacy and instability risks; metric targets may be gamed; monitoring should not become automated individual decision-making |
| **Audit `risk_score_band`** for calibration, false-positive rates, source-feature bias, threshold effects, and regional consistency | Tests whether risk-based routing is valid rather than assumed neutral | Better fairness may involve predictive-performance trade-offs; historical outcome labels may themselves be biased; abrupt score removal could create inconsistent manual review |
| **Audit `documentation_completeness`** through sampled dual review, translated-form testing, worker-consistency analysis, and applicant-centered usability testing | Distinguishes genuine missing evidence from accessibility or judgment problems | More detailed review costs time; standardized checklists may be too rigid for unusual cases |
| **Use workload-aware, skill-constrained assignment** and escalation when specialist queues exceed thresholds | Reduces assignment to predictably overloaded workers while preserving expertise | May move work across regions, disrupt continuity, or overburden the most efficient staff; assignment logic itself requires fairness auditing |
| **Conduct appeal and rework root-cause review** using reversal reasons and repeated-request patterns | Identifies unclear decisions and preventable first-stage errors | Requires additional review resources and must preserve the independence of the appeal function |

Mitigation should not remove Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review merely to improve measured cycle time. The goal is to preserve those protections while reducing avoidable delay and burden.

df = pd.read_csv("benefits_event_log.csv")

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACT,
    timestamp_key=TS

# Verify that purported case attributes are stable within a case.
case_attrs = [
    "applicant_group", "channel", "language_preference",
    "risk_score_band", "documentation_completeness",
    "household_size_band", "disability_accommodation_requested",
    "prior_case_count", "region", "case_outcome", "appeal_flag"

stability = log.groupby(CASE)[case_attrs].nunique(dropna=False)
assert not (stability > 1).any().any(), "Reconcile changing case attributes first"

# Case-level table for matching/modeling.
first = log.groupby(CASE)[case_attrs].first()
times = log.groupby(CASE)[TS].agg(start="min", end="max")
cases = first.join(times)

    cases["end"] - cases["start"]

Conditional checks can also be calculated directly:

```python
seqs = log.groupby(CASE)[ACT].apply(list)
attrs = log.groupby(CASE)[case_attrs].first()

def before(seq, required, later):
    if required not in seq:
        return False
    if later not in seq:
        return True
    return seq.index(required) < seq.index(later)

checks = []
for case_id, seq in seqs.items():
    a = attrs.loc[case_id]

    if a["disability_accommodation_requested"]:
        checks.append({
            "case": case_id,
            "rule": "accommodation_before_assessment",
            "ok": before(seq, "Accommodation Review",
                         "Eligibility Assessment")
        })

    if a["risk_score_band"] == "High":
        checks.append({
            "case": case_id,
            "rule": "high_risk_has_fraud_review",
            "ok": "Fraud Review" in seq
        })

    # Define this predicate from the actual language-support policy.
    if a["language_preference"] != "majority_language":
        checks.append({
            "case": case_id,
            "rule": "language_support_before_assessment",
            "ok": before(seq, "Language Support Contact",
                         "Eligibility Assessment")
        })

violations = pd.DataFrame(checks).merge(
    attrs[["applicant_group", "region", "channel"]],
    left_on="case", right_index=True
)

violation_rates = (
    violations.groupby(
        ["rule", "applicant_group", "region"]
    )["ok"].apply(lambda x: 1 - x.mean())
)
```

The policy owner should define exceptions—for example, cases closed before eligibility assessment. Compare violation and unnecessary-referral rates within matched strata and investigate worker- or region-level concentrations.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **not demonstrably fair and appears procedurally inequitable in important respects**, particularly for Groups B and C. Legitimate differences in documentation, risk, language needs, and accommodation requests explain a meaningful share of the different paths, so the raw approval and timing gaps should not be labeled discrimination.

However, rights-preserving support steps are implemented through scarce specialist queues, overloaded resources, and extra handovers. B and C retain adjusted delays and elevated review or document-request probabilities, while B and D retain notable adjusted appeal disparities. These patterns support a material procedural-fairness concern, not a definitive finding of intentional or unlawful discrimination. Stronger conclusions require better eligibility data, route-conditional comparisons, uncertainty estimates, risk and documentation audits, and direct measures of support and decision quality.
