## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

A flag value alone is not a sufficiently precise intervention. I define policies as reproducible changes to information, routing rules, queue discipline, and capacity applied to an arrival cohort or shift:

- **P0:** current policy bundle.
- **P1:** flag is not displayed and no automatic flag-triggered priority/manual-review rule runs; the raw `risk_score`, `complexity`, and other human information remain visible. A UI-only hiding policy that leaves backend routing unchanged would be a different policy, \(P1^{UI}\).
- **P2:** retain flagged priority triage, remove automatic/manual-review requirement, retain discretionary human review based on score and case information.
- **P3:** retain P0 and add explicitly additional protected manual-review slots for flagged cases. Reserving existing shared slots instead would be a different intervention.
- **P4a:** retain review, replace escalation with a pre-specified audited escalation rule.
- **P4b:** retain review and escalation criteria, add senior-team capacity. “P4” without choosing a rule or capacity version is underdefined.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | \(P1-P0\) | Case sampled from all arrivals in a policy-period cohort; policy applied to the whole cohort | arrival-to-close cycle time | Total policy effect of removing flag-triggered handling | Yes, under the operational P1 definition above | Hidden risk, score-based selection, and system-wide queue interference |
| E2 | \(P0-P1\) | Arrival cohort, including cases whose queues are affected by preceding arrivals | arrival-to-triage-done time | Total component-policy effect on an early-stage outcome; not a natural direct effect | Yes | Priority and staffing may co-vary by time, resource, and unobserved severity |
| E3 | \(P2-P0\) | Flagged eligible arrivals not subject to legal mandatory review | cycle time and review occurrence | Total policy effect of removing automatic review while retaining priority | Yes, if discretionary-review rules and eligibility are fixed | Review discretion may respond to treatment assignment and queue state |
| E4 | \(P0 - P0[M_{\text{review occurrence}}\leftarrow g_{P2}]\) | Entire arrival cohort, with queues jointly re-evolved | cycle time | Interventional indirect-effect analogue through manual-review occurrence | Only if the stochastic P2 review-routing rule is specified jointly for all arrivals | Mediator-outcome confounding by review findings; policy-induced queue changes |
| E5 | \(P0 - P0[\text{manual capacity/discipline}\leftarrow P3]\) | Whole cohort, including unflagged spillover cases | manual queue wait and cycle time | Controlled queue-component effect; an interventional analogue for delay through manual waiting | Yes if added slots, hours, eligibility, and priority discipline are fixed | Cannot set an individual’s waiting time independently of other cases |
| E6 | \(P0 - P0[\text{escalation rule}\leftarrow P4a]\) | Reviewed cases and all cases sharing senior capacity | escalation occurrence, escalation wait, cycle time | Interventional analogue through escalation routing | Yes if P4a’s rule uses only specified available information | Review findings and staffing can be exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounders |
| E7 | \(P3-P0\) | Whole arrival cohort | arrival-to-close cycle time | Total policy effect of added protected manual-review capacity | Yes only when “protected” means additional, not reallocated, capacity | Capacity may accelerate reviewed cases but create an escalation bottleneck |
| E8 | \(P4a-P0\) or \(P4b-P0\) | Reviewed or escalation-eligible arrivals | final `decision_outcome` distribution | Total policy effect on decisions, not yet decision quality | Yes for the recorded decision distribution | Approve/reject is not a validated correctness or compliance measure |
| E9 | \(P2-P0\) | Flagged eligible arrivals with later audit labels or appeal/rework records | audited correctness, compliance, reversal, or rework | Total quality effect; potentially a safety estimand for a pilot | Only if a valid post-decision quality label is collected | No ground-truth quality outcome is in the supplied data |

I would **not** estimate conventional natural direct or natural indirect effects. They require cross-world counterfactuals such as a case having P0’s review occurrence but P2’s downstream queue state; that is particularly implausible when queues are shared. The preferred framework is total policy effects, controlled policy-component effects, and explicitly specified **interventional mediation analogues** at the cohort/queue-system level.

Queue interference arises because one case’s priority, review, or escalation consumes shared capacity and changes later cases’ waits. `arrival_load` is only a coarse proxy for the actual pre-arrival queue state. Exposure-induced confounding arises when flag handling changes reviewer assignment, review findings, or queue state, and those variables then affect escalation and closure. For example, conditioning on review findings while estimating the total flag-policy effect would block part of the policy’s pathway and may induce bias. Treatment-confounder feedback also occurs over time: prior flagged cases affect current queue state, while current policy affects queue state for subsequent cases. Thus, the relevant unit is often an arrival block, shift, or queue cohort rather than an isolated case.

The timestamp arithmetic is consistent with faster initial handling for flagged cases: their mean triage wait is 0.2 hours versus 2.5 hours for unflagged cases, and triage service is also somewhat shorter. Downstream, all flagged cases receive manual review versus 4 of 10 unflagged cases; flagged reviewed cases wait 6.5 hours before review versus 1.9 hours among reviewed unflagged cases. Escalation is also more frequent for flagged cases, 6 of 10 versus 2 of 10. These patterns can explain the 16.5 versus 8.2 hour mean cycle-time contrast without requiring longer service times. They are not automatically causal: flagged cases have no low-complexity support, have different triage resources, may contain omitted risk, and affect one another’s queues. High-load flagged cases also have longer downstream waits, but high load is coarse and composition differs.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does crossing into the flagged regime cause a priority/manual-routing bundle effect? | Use a local-threshold or within-risk-score design around the operational score cutoff, over many dates and shifts. Estimate discontinuities in triage wait, review occurrence, and cycle time. | Running score, local score polynomial/bandwidth, complexity, calendar time, arrival hour, pre-arrival workload snapshot | No precise score manipulation; cases just above/below cutoff are exchangeable; no other rule changes at threshold | Check density bunching, covariate continuity, and discontinuities in outcomes that should precede flag generation | No triage or review discontinuity at threshold, or discontinuities explained by another threshold rule, weakens the flag-policy mechanism |
| Are long review waits caused by queue state rather than case severity alone? | Construct exact pre-arrival queue features: WIP by review/senior team, oldest waiting case, available staff, class-specific backlog, and staffing schedule. Model conditional waits or use queue simulation. | Pre-arrival queue state, staffing, arrival time, score, complexity, case type | The measured state precedes the current case’s routing; unmeasured queue determinants are limited | Compare cases arriving into the same measured queue state; inspect whether residual wait gaps remain | If the flagged manual-wait gap disappears within tightly matched queue states, queue composition rather than flag-specific treatment becomes more likely |
| What is the effect of removing mandatory review while retaining priority? | Run a randomized, block-level P2 encouragement or policy trial among eligible flagged arrivals. Analyze all arrivals in treated blocks, not only assigned cases. | Randomization blocks; baseline queue state and shift covariates for precision only | Randomized blocks affect outcomes through the policy; implementation fidelity is measured | Check balance, staff adherence, discretionary-review override rates, and effects on unflagged waits | No reduction in review inflow/wait, or a quality decline beyond the guardrail, rejects P2 as a useful change |
| How much of the policy effect plausibly operates through review occurrence, review waiting, and escalation? | Fit a longitudinal interventional-mediation analysis or queue-aware discrete-event simulation. Replace whole-cohort routing rules with the empirically observed P2/P3/P4 rules and re-evolve queues. | Baseline score, complexity, intake facts, calendar/staffing, pre-arrival queue state; sequential models for review and escalation | Correct mediator models, positivity, measured mediator-outcome confounding, and valid system simulation | Compare simulated queue lengths, transition frequencies, and wait distributions with held-out periods | Poor reproduction of actual queue and stage distributions means the proposed mediated decomposition is not credible |
| Are apparent effects artifacts of data quality or generic time confounding? | Add a negative-control outcome measured before flag visibility, such as intake-completeness-to-score-computation time; use a shadow flag randomly generated but never displayed or routed. | Calendar time, source system, score-computation version, intake channel | Negative controls are truly unaffected by real flag handling | Test for a “flag effect” on the pre-flag outcome or shadow-flag effect on downstream waits | A strong negative-control association suggests timestamp, workload, or analytic confounding rather than a flag mechanism |
| Could unobserved risk explain the observed manual-review and escalation associations? | Collect audited case features and conduct quantitative bias analysis over plausible prevalence/effect sizes of omitted severity. Report bounds rather than a single adjusted estimate. | Score, complexity, observed intake fields, audited latent-risk proxies | Chosen sensitivity parameters cover plausible hidden-risk relationships | Compare bounds with expert elicitation and external audit samples | If modest, credible hidden-risk imbalance eliminates the estimated policy contrast, do not attribute the contrast to flag handling |
| Does the policy create harmful spillovers? | Randomize policy by arrival block, queue-day, or team-day; estimate both eligible-case effects and effects on unflagged/shared-queue cases. Use cluster-level estimands. | Block randomization, staffing, baseline queue state, arrival volume; avoid conditioning on post-policy queues for total effects | Limited cross-block contamination; enough clusters; queue state is observed | Plot waits and WIP before/after block transitions; test unflagged and senior-team effects | Shorter flagged cycles accompanied by longer unflagged or senior waits would reverse a narrowly case-level conclusion |
| Is resource assignment the actual cause of faster triage or slower downstream handling? | Compare eligible cases randomized or balanced within qualified triage/reviewer resources; estimate resource-by-policy interactions. | Score, complexity, shift, staffing, baseline queue state, team fixed effects | No remaining unmeasured specialization within resource strata | Examine whether the flag effect remains when the same resource, shift, and queue state are compared | If effects vanish within resource strata, lane/team allocation rather than flag information is the principal mechanism |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Fast flagged triage; slower unflagged triage in this sample | High flagged review inflow and substantial manual/senior waits | Current flagged mean is longer despite faster triage | Current safeguards retained, but true quality is unmeasured | Existing queue burden remains shared | Descriptive only; n=20 | Maintain as control condition during evaluation |
| P1: suppress flag and disable flag-triggered routing | Formerly flagged cases likely lose priority, so triage may slow | Automatic review inflow may fall, but humans can still use score | Ambiguous: less review may offset slower triage | Potentially high if flag carries useful information not recoverable from score display | May relieve review/senior queues, but may move work to later remediation | Weak; depends heavily on human response to visible score | Do not deploy broadly before testing P2 or collecting quality evidence |
| P2: keep priority, remove mandatory review | Should preserve current triage advantage | Lower automatic review inflow; manual queue likely shortens if discretionary review does not fully replace it | Likely shorter for lower-need flagged cases; uncertain for genuinely high-risk cases | Main risk is missed review, inconsistent documentation, or delayed escalation | Likely positive spillover to all manual-review users | Moderate policy logic, weak empirical evidence | Preferred limited pilot with audit and queue safeguards |
| P3: add protected manual-review capacity | No material direct triage change | Manual waits should fall if capacity is genuinely additional; escalation load may rise sooner | Likely lower for reviewed flagged cases, possibly limited by senior queue | Review safeguard preserved; fairness concern if nonflagged access worsens | Positive if added capacity; negative if existing capacity is reserved | Weak-to-moderate; mechanism consistent with timestamps | Consider only after measuring whether manual review, rather than senior escalation, is the active bottleneck |
| P4: change escalation criteria or add senior capacity | No direct triage change | P4a may reduce escalations and senior waits; P4b reduces senior waits but not escalation volume | P4a may shorten cycles at safety cost; P4b may shorten escalated cases | P4a can create false negatives; P4b raises cost and may increase marginal escalation | P4a relieves senior queue; P4b may improve shared senior access | Weak; P4 is not one intervention until criterion/capacity choice is fixed | Specify and audit P4a or P4b separately; do not infer from aggregate escalation rates |

**Limited pilot: P2.**  
Eligibility: newly flagged cases above the operational threshold that are not legally, clinically, or contractually required to receive mandatory review; exclude predefined urgent/high-harm categories. Randomize eligible arrivals 1:1 by short arrival blocks, stratified by shift and pre-arrival manual-review WIP, to P0 or P2. Block assignment is preferable to isolated case randomization because queue spillovers are part of the estimand.

Guardrail: retain baseline staffing, do not redeploy reviewers during the pilot, and suspend randomization in blocks with manual or senior WIP above pre-specified safety thresholds. Staff may initiate discretionary review under P2, but must record the reason.

Primary outcomes: cohort-level eligible-case cycle time and audited decision/compliance noninferiority. Secondary outcomes: triage wait, review occurrence, manual and senior queue waits, escalation, appeal/rework, reviewer overrides, unflagged waiting times, and staff workload.

Minimum data: exact stage timestamps and semantics, score/flag version, review/escalation reasons, resource/staffing schedules, detailed queue state at arrival, audit labels, and all concurrent arrivals.

Stop if audited quality/compliance breaches its margin, senior or unflagged waits materially worsen, override behavior defeats implementation, or timestamp/queue telemetry is incomplete.

    """Return elapsed hours only when both timestamps are observed."""
    if pd.isna(start) or pd.isna(end):

cases["escalation_observed"] = (
    cases["escalation_start"].notna() & cases["escalation_done"].notna()
)

# Warn about malformed partial optional stages rather than silently inventing events.
partial_manual = cases["manual_start"].notna() ^ cases["manual_done"].notna()
partial_escalation = (
    cases["escalation_start"].notna() ^ cases["escalation_done"].notna()
)
if partial_manual.any() or partial_escalation.any():
    warnings.warn(
        "Some optional stages have only a start or completion timestamp. "
        "They are excluded from completed-stage event construction."
    )

# 3. Calculate waiting, service, residual, and total cycle times.
# Missing optional stages remain NaN, never zero.
cases["triage_wait_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["arrival"], r["triage_start"]), axis=1
)
cases["triage_service_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["triage_start"], r["triage_done"]), axis=1
)
cases["manual_queue_wait_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["triage_done"], r["manual_start"])
    if r["manual_observed"] else np.nan,
    axis=1,
)
cases["manual_service_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["manual_start"], r["manual_done"])
    if r["manual_observed"] else np.nan,
    axis=1,
)
cases["escalation_queue_wait_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["manual_done"], r["escalation_start"])
    if r["escalation_observed"] else np.nan,
    axis=1,
)
cases["senior_service_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["escalation_start"], r["escalation_done"])
    if r["escalation_observed"] else np.nan,
    axis=1,
)

def final_operational_completion(row):
    if row["escalation_observed"]:
        return row["escalation_done"]
    if row["manual_observed"]:
        return row["manual_done"]
    return row["triage_done"]

cases["last_operational_done"] = cases.apply(final_operational_completion, axis=1)
cases["residual_to_close_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["last_operational_done"], r["close"]), axis=1
)
cases["total_cycle_h"] = cases.apply(
    lambda r: hours_between(r["arrival"], r["close"]), axis=1
)

# 4. Reshape to observed event-level data compatible with PM4Py.
# Completion timestamps are used as event timestamps; start timestamps preserve
# the service intervals for Triage, Manual Review, and Escalation.
event_rows = []

def append_event(case_row, activity, completed_at, started_at, resource=pd.NA):
    if pd.isna(completed_at):
        return
    event_rows.append({
        "case:concept:name": case_row["case"],
        "concept:name": activity,
        "time:timestamp": completed_at,
        "start_timestamp": started_at,
        "org:resource": resource,
        "flag": case_row["flag"],
        "risk_score": case_row["risk_score"],
        "complexity": case_row["complexity"],
        "arrival_load": case_row["arrival_load"],
        "outcome": case_row["outcome"],
    })

for _, row in cases.iterrows():
    # Register and Close are observed instantaneous milestones in this input.
    append_event(row, "Register", row["arrival"], row["arrival"])

    # Add only completed observed stage events.
    if pd.notna(row["triage_start"]) and pd.notna(row["triage_done"]):
        append_event(
            row, "Triage", row["triage_done"], row["triage_start"],
            row["triage_resource"]
        )

    if row["manual_observed"]:
        append_event(
            row, "Manual Review", row["manual_done"], row["manual_start"],
            row["review_resource"]
        )

    if row["escalation_observed"]:
        # No escalation resource was supplied, so org:resource remains missing.
        append_event(
            row, "Escalation", row["escalation_done"], row["escalation_start"]
        )

    append_event(row, "Close", row["close"], row["close"])

events = pd.DataFrame(event_rows).sort_values(
    ["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp", "concept:name"]

# 5. Format for PM4Py. start_timestamp_key is explicitly supplied.
formatted_events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",

print("\nObserved event-level dataframe:")
print(formatted_events.head(12).to_string(index=False))

# 6. Discover frequency and performance DFGs separately by observed flag.
# DFG performance based on completion timestamps measures elapsed time between

for flag_value, label in [(0, "unflagged"), (1, "flagged")]:
    sublog = formatted_events.loc[formatted_events["flag"] == flag_value].copy()

    print(f"\n=== {label.upper()} FREQUENCY DFG ===")

    print(f"\n=== {label.upper()} PERFORMANCE DFG ===")

metrics = [
    ("arrival-to-triage-start", "triage_wait_h"),

def print_stage_summary(label, data):
    n_cases = len(data)
    print(f"\n--- {label}: n={n_cases} ---")

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk dominates:** audited intake features show that flagged cases have materially greater omitted risk, documentation difficulty, or legal sensitivity than score and complexity capture, and that their longer reviews improve verified outcomes; then reducing mandatory review under P2 would be inappropriate.

2. **Queue interference is harmful:** a block-level test finds that P2 shortens flagged cycles only by shifting manual, senior-team, or unflagged waiting times upward; then the apparent benefit is not a system-level improvement.

3. **Decision quality declines:** audited correctness, compliance, appeal, rework, complaint, or adverse-event rates exceed a pre-specified noninferiority margin under P2; then preserving review or adding capacity becomes preferable.

4. **Timestamp semantics are misleading:** manual or escalation “start” timestamps are batch-entry, overnight-hold, or administrative timestamps rather than actual queue entry/service start; then the inferred waiting-time bottleneck may be false.

5. **Positivity fails:** near-threshold or eligible flagged cases do not overlap with comparable unflagged/discretionary-review cases in complexity, staffing, queue state, or risk; then observational comparisons and mediated analogues are too extrapolative.

6. **Reviewer behavior adapts:** staff compensate for P2 by informally recreating mandatory review, altering documentation, or escalating more readily; then the operational intervention differs from its specification and P2’s estimated mechanism no longer applies.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** In these 20 cases, flagged cases reach triage far faster: mean arrival-to-triage-start is 0.2 hours versus 2.5 hours for unflagged cases. They nevertheless have longer mean total cycles, 16.5 versus 8.2 hours. Flagged cases are observed in manual review more often, wait longer before manual review, and escalate more often. Their service times are not uniformly longer, which makes routing and waiting more visually prominent than raw work duration.

**Plausible mechanism.** The flag appears to operate as a policy bundle: priority triage accelerates the front of the process, while routine manual review and more frequent escalation place flagged cases into scarce shared queues. Long manual and senior-team waits can outweigh the initial triage gain. Added review capacity or reduced automatic review could change both treated cases and other cases sharing the queues.

**Unidentified.** The data do not establish that the flag itself causes the downstream delay. The score is imperfect, complexity differs sharply across groups, triage resources are segregated by flag, actual queue state is unobserved, and cases interfere through shared capacity. A natural direct/indirect decomposition is not defensible here.

**Most useful next evidence.** A guarded, block-randomized P2 pilot—retain priority while removing automatic review for eligible flagged cases—would test a concrete policy while measuring discretionary overrides, queue spillovers, escalation, and audited quality. It is more informative than simply “setting the flag to zero,” because it isolates an actionable routing component while retaining risk information and triage prioritization.
