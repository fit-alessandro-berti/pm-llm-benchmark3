# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

I interpret the board’s decision as spanning four main use cases:

- **Exploration / discovery**: understand typical variants, outliers, and improvement opportunities. Here high fitness and generalization are valuable, and some over-permissiveness can be tolerated as long as it is clearly sign‑posted and never reused for enforcement or KPI calculation.
- **Conformance enforcement**: detect and prevent violations of P1–P7 (and similar policies) on individual cases. Here **precision, explicit semantics, and auditability** dominate; missed novel but valid variants are less acceptable than in exploration, but still important.
- **Simulation / what‑if analysis**: generate realistic process executions for capacity planning and risk analysis. This needs a model that captures typical branching and repetition without encoding every observed trace literally; overfitting to training variants is problematic.
- **Stakeholder communication and ownership**: give managers, auditors, and IT architects a shared, understandable artifact. Here structural simplicity, stable naming, and a clean mapping from activities to owners are key.

Key assumptions about the submitted models:

- **Model S semantics**: `->` is sequence; `X(a, b)` is exclusive choice; `X(label, tau)` encodes an optional activity; `+` is a parallel/partial‑order composition where each child can execute at most once. Thus Model S:
  - forces `Register Dossier` first and `Release Order` last;
  - allows each of `Credit Check`, `Technical Review`, `Supplier Review`, and `Expedite Review` to appear at most once, in any order, and each can be skipped;
  - enforces exactly one of `Manager Approval` and `Auto Approval` before release.
- **Model C semantics**: `X(...)` is an exclusive choice over eight fully enumerated sequences; each listed trace is accepted exactly, with no additional concurrency or looping beyond what is syntactically present.
- **Policy stance**: P1–P7 and the B1–B8 probes are treated as the reference intent; the event log may contain both compliant and non‑compliant behavior. Metrics are **evidence, not ground truth**.

Throughout, I separate models for **exploration** (where Model S has strengths) from models for **compliance and simulation** (where Model C has some strengths but serious weaknesses).

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

## Strongest case for Model S

- **Simplicity and communicability**: Its structure (Register → configurable review block → approval → Release) closely matches how business users narrate the process. This is reflected in high simplicity and comprehension scores.
- **Robustness to logging imperfections**: Optionalization of `Credit Check`, `Technical Review`, `Supplier Review`, and `Expedite Review` makes S tolerant to missing or mislogged events, contributing to high training and holdout fitness and good generalization.
- **Operational tractability**: Fast alignment and a small, duplicate‑free transition set make S attractive for dashboards, quick what‑if replays, and embedding into other tooling where model size is constrained.

## Strongest case against Model S

- **Violates key policy constraints by construction**: By encoding all review steps as optional and unordered, S contradicts P2 and P4 and cannot distinguish compliant from non‑compliant variants.
- **Conflates “missing in log” with “optional in policy”**: To handle missing events, the model treats mandatory checks as skippable behavior, rewarding exactly the pathology we wish to detect.
- **No explicit handling of correction loops**: S has no `Correct Dossier` activity or any repetition of checks, so it cannot represent P6 or flag P7‑type suspicious behavior.
- **Over‑generalization risk**: The combination of parallel composition and optional branches yields very high generalization but extremely low precision, which is not acceptable for compliance or simulation.
- **Ambiguous treatment of external vs internal and expedite logic**: S lacks any mechanism to distinguish external from internal orders, or to enforce the requirement that Expedite Review follows both mandatory checks.

## Undesirable or ambiguous behaviors admitted by Model S

Under the assumed semantics of `+` (each child optional and executed at most once in any order), Model S admits at least the following problematic traces (not exhaustive):

1. **Skipping all checks (violates P2)**  
   `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   or with auto approval:  
   `Register Dossier, Auto Approval, Release Order`.

2. **Expedite before checks (violates P4)**  
   `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`.

3. **Expedite without any checks (violates P2 and P4)**  
   `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`.

4. **Supplier Review without mandatory checks (violates P2, P3)**  
   `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`.

5. **External‑style path with Auto Approval but no Manager involvement (policy‑ambiguous)**  
   `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`.  
   Depending on governance expectations, external orders with Supplier involvement may be required to go through Manager Approval; S cannot express or enforce that distinction.

6. **Permutations of checks that may hide sequencing expectations**  
   For example, if the business implicitly expects `Credit Check` before `Technical Review` for risk reasons, S nonetheless admits both orders:  
   `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order`.

These behaviors arise from two design choices:

- **Intentional noise tolerance**: `X(activity, tau)` was likely chosen to cope with missing events.
- **Accidental semantic flaw**: wrapping *all* checks and reviews inside a single permissive `+` block removes ordering and mandatory‑ness that the policy clearly requires.

For exploration, S can still be useful as long as stakeholders are continually reminded that it is **not a normative process model** and that any KPI computed against S (e.g., “fraction of dossiers without missing checks”) will be misleading.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

## Strongest case for Model C

- **Enforces core sequencing constraints**: Every enumerated branch begins with `Register Dossier`, includes both `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` before approval, and ends with `Release Order`. No branch allows approvals without both checks, aligning with P1 and P2.
- **Better precision on known behavior**: High alignment precision and explicit encoding of `Correct Dossier` plus repeated checks support F1, F4, and F6’s desire for audit‑ready traces that closely match observed compliant variants.
- **More faithful treatment of Expedite Review**: Expedite appears, when present, only *after* both mandatory checks and before `Manager Approval`, satisfying P4 for those variants.
- **Support for corrections (partially)**: One branch includes `Correct Dossier` followed by repeated `Credit Check` and `Technical Review`, capturing at least the “both checks repeated after correction” pattern.

## Strongest case against Model C

- **Branch enumeration and overfitting**: By listing individual frequent traces, C behaves like an over‑fitted classifier. New but policy‑compliant combinations (e.g., order of checks, combinations of Supplier Review, Auto Approval, and Expedite Review) are rejected, explaining the low holdout fitness and poor generalization.
- **Duplicate labels and governance complexity**: 33 duplicate labels (F3) make it hard to answer “who owns Credit Check?” or “what is the impact of changing Supplier Review?” Individual branches embed ownership and semantics that should be factored out.
- **Inflexible correction handling**: Only one correction pattern is encoded: after `Correct Dossier`, both checks repeat. P6’s more nuanced requirement—repeat only the affected checks—cannot be represented without further branch explosion.
- **Fragmented policy encoding**: External vs internal, Supplier Review conditions, and exactly‑one approval are all encoded implicitly in which branches exist rather than as explicit rules, making maintenance brittle.
- **Holdout degradation undermines audit role**: With holdout fitness only 0.744, C will misclassify many real cases. F2 and F4 correctly worry that legitimate but new combinations of known activities will be flagged as noncompliant, creating alert fatigue.

In sum, Model C is closer to a **whitelist of historically common patterns** than to a robust, maintainable policy model. It is safer than S for strict enforcement on the subset of behavior it knows, but too brittle and opaque to be the board’s long‑term single source of truth.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Considering the reported metrics, feedback F1–F8, and the model structures, the following pathologies are likely and relevant:

1. **Train/holdout leakage or split sensitivity**  
   Without seeds or split descriptions, differences in alignment fitness and precision may be partly due to how cases were partitioned. If, for example, long or complex traces were disproportionately placed in the holdout, C’s low holdout fitness could be exaggerated. However, given the qualitative evidence (F2, F4) and C’s enumerated nature, overfitting seems intrinsic rather than purely split‑driven.

2. **Missing-event bias**  
   To cope with logs where mandatory activities are occasionally unlogged, Model S treats them as truly optional (`X(label, tau)`), boosting fitness and generalization. This biases the learning process towards **under‑enforcement** of mandatory behavior, directly conflicting with P2. For C, missing Supplier Review events (F4) might have caused the discovery algorithm to under‑represent Supplier‑related variants.

3. **Positive-log-only precision**  
   Precision is computed only against observed positive traces (F5). Model C’s high precision may simply indicate that it rarely produces traces not seen in training, not that it avoids undesirable behavior. Conversely, Model S’s low precision may partly reflect that its broader language includes both valid but unseen variants (desirable for exploration) and truly undesirable ones.

4. **Duplicated-label effects**  
   Model C’s 33 duplicate labels inflate model size, reduce simplicity, and complicate alignments: misalignment to the “wrong” branch‑local `Credit Check` can create misleading diagnostics and cost profiles. This also increases median alignment runtime and makes impact analysis hard (F3).

5. **Silent-transition (tau) effects**  
   Model S’s use of tau in `X(activity, tau)` changes alignment behavior: aligning a missing event to tau is cheap, so fitness can remain high even when mandatory behavior is skipped. This masks violations of P2 and P4 and artificially increases both fitness and generalization scores for S.

6. **Alignment-cost sensitivity**  
   Without specifying insertion/deletion/substitution costs and tau penalties, we cannot tell how robust the reported fitness and precision are. For example, slightly penalizing skipping `Credit Check` more heavily than reordering events would reduce S’s apparent fitness and precision and might narrow the gap to C.

7. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior**  
   S achieves high fitness on both train and holdout primarily by making checks optional and removing ordering constraints. This is a classic pathology: the metric rewards models that “explain” violations instead of highlighting them.

8. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness**  
   Token‑based generalization favors S because its simple structure admits many permutations and subsets of activities. Some of these are policy‑compliant, but many (e.g., B3 and B4) are not. C’s low generalization partly reflects useful conservatism but is also driven by over‑enumeration.

9. **Stakeholder-score methodology**  
   The comprehension scores strongly favor S, but we lack information on the evaluation protocol. If stakeholders were only asked to interpret common “happy‑path” traces, structural errors (like missing correction logic) would go unnoticed. Complexity aversion may bias scores against necessary but intricate models.

10. **Runtime confounded by model size**  
    The ~25× runtime difference largely reflects model size and duplicate labels, not necessarily algorithmic inefficiency. However, runtime is still a practical governance concern: very slow alignment can discourage rigorous conformance checking.

### Ranking by likely decision impact

From a governance perspective, the most critical pathologies are:

1. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior (7)** — leads directly to underestimating compliance risk if S is used beyond exploration.
2. **Missing-event bias (2)** and **silent-transition effects (5)** — together, they make skipped mandatory checks appear normal.
3. **Positive-log-only precision (3)** — obscures whether high precision for C reflects true policy safety or mere memorization.
4. **Duplicated-label effects (4)** — drive complexity, runtime, and maintainability concerns for C, affecting long‑term governance.
5. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness (8)** — particularly dangerous if generalization is used to justify S for simulation or enforcement.
6. **Runtime confounding (10)** — operationally important but secondary to semantic correctness.
7. **Train/holdout split sensitivity (1)** and **stakeholder-score methodology (9)** — impact interpretation but are less fundamental than the modeling choices themselves.

# 7. Third Alternative

I propose **model_third** as a **block-structured procedural backbone plus declarative constraints**, aiming to:

- enforce P1–P5 explicitly in the backbone;
- represent correction/recheck behavior of P6 and P7 via a loop and external constraints;
- retain enough flexibility to generalize beyond enumerated variants (unlike C);
- remain structurally simple and semantically transparent (improving on C, safer than S).

## 7.1 Compact formal description

At a high level, model_third is:

```text
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  loop(
    BODY,          # review cycle for current dossier version
    RECHECK,       # correction + selective recheck
    tau            # exit after last successful cycle
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

Where:

- `BODY` is a partial-order block enforcing both mandatory checks before any approval-related activity:

  - Activities: `Credit Check (CC)`, `Technical Review (TR)`, optional `Supplier Review (SR)`, optional `Expedite Review (ER)`.
  - Precedence constraints (as a strict partial order):

    - `Register Dossier` ≺ {CC, TR}.  
    - {CC, TR} ≺ {SR, ER}.  
    - ER ≺ {Manager Approval, Auto Approval}.  
    - SR has no fixed order relative to ER (may be before or after), but SR must still follow both mandatory checks.

  - Each of CC and TR must occur at least once per loop iteration.

- `RECHECK` is:

  ```text
  ->(
    'Correct Dossier',
    PAR_RECHECK
  )
  ```

  where `PAR_RECHECK` is a parallel composition over **optional** rechecks of CC and TR, with an additional **external constraint** that at least one of them must occur after each `Correct Dossier`.

This structure ensures:

- P1: Register is first; Release is last.
- P2: In every BODY iteration, both CC and TR are required before any approval.
- P3: SR remains optional in the backbone; external constraints tie SR to an `order_type="external"` attribute.
- P4: ER is permitted only after both CC and TR and before approval, possibly interleaving with SR.
- P5: Exactly one of Manager or Auto occurs (exclusive choice).
- P6/P7: `Correct Dossier` introduces a new review cycle; post‑hoc constraints ensure that repeated checks without a preceding correction, and corrections without any subsequent recheck, are flagged.

## 7.2 PM4Py code skeleton assigning `model_third`

Below is a schematic construction using POWL for the review backbone and a process tree wrapper for the loop. It is intentionally high‑level and can be refined during implementation.

```python
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.powl.obj import Transition, SilentTransition, OperatorPOWL, StrictPartialOrder

# Transitions
t_reg = Transition("Register Dossier")
t_cc = Transition("Credit Check")
t_tr = Transition("Technical Review")
t_sr = Transition("Supplier Review")
t_er = Transition("Expedite Review")
t_corr = Transition("Correct Dossier")
t_ma = Transition("Manager Approval")
t_aa = Transition("Auto Approval")
t_rel = Transition("Release Order")
t_tau = SilentTransition("tau_exit")

# BODY: mandatory CC and TR, optional SR and ER with ordering constraints
body_nodes = [t_cc, t_tr, t_sr, t_er]
body_order = StrictPartialOrder()
# Register precedes all review activities
for n in body_nodes:
    body_order.add_edge(t_reg, n)
# Mandatory checks precede SR and ER
body_order.add_edge(t_cc, t_sr)
body_order.add_edge(t_tr, t_sr)
body_order.add_edge(t_cc, t_er)
body_order.add_edge(t_tr, t_er)
# No edge between SR and ER: either order is allowed

body_block = OperatorPOWL("and", body_nodes, body_order)  # partial-order block

# RECHECK: Correct Dossier followed by optional CC/TR rechecks
recheck_nodes = [t_cc, t_tr]
recheck_order = StrictPartialOrder()
recheck_block = OperatorPOWL("and", recheck_nodes, recheck_order)

# Wrap BODY and RECHECK into a loop-like structure using a process tree string
model_third_str = """
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  loop(
    +('Credit Check', 'Technical Review', X('Supplier Review', tau), X('Expedite Review', tau)),
    ->('Correct Dossier', +(
        X('Credit Check', tau),
        X('Technical Review', tau)
      )),
    tau
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
"""

# In practice, you would either:
# (a) generate a Petri net directly from the POWL object, or
# (b) parse an equivalent process tree string.
model_third = pm4py.parse_process_tree(model_third_str)
```

This code sketch isolates the main modeling idea:

- BODY enforces both checks and the relative ordering of SR and ER.
- RECHECK is restricted to repeating CC and/or TR after `Correct Dossier`.
- Exactly one approval occurs after the loop.

## 7.3 Declarative dictionaries and external checks

On top of `model_third`, I propose explicit declarative constraints implemented as Python functions or a simple rule DSL. Examples:

```python
# Example configuration dictionaries

MANDATORY_CHECKS = {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}

def is_external_case(trace):
    return trace.attributes.get("order_source") == "external"

def approvals_in_trace(trace):
    return [e for e in trace if e["concept:name"] in {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"}]

def corrections(trace):
    return [i for i, e in enumerate(trace) if e["concept:name"] == "Correct Dossier"]

def affected_checks_for_correction(trace, corr_index):
    # When data becomes available, use e.g. e["affected_checks"] as a set
    return trace[corr_index].attributes.get("affected_checks", None)
```

Declarative policies (checked after alignment) include:

- **Supplier rule**: `Supplier Review` must occur **iff** `is_external_case(trace)` is true.
- **Expedite rule**: `Expedite Review` is allowed only after all mandatory checks for the current cycle and before the first approval.
- **Correction rule**: after each `Correct Dossier`, at least one check in `MANDATORY_CHECKS` must occur before the next approval; if an `affected_checks` attribute exists, only that subset needs to repeat.
- **Suspicious repeat rule (P7)**: any repeat of a mandatory check without an intervening `Correct Dossier` is flagged as suspicious.

These rules can be evaluated on replayed traces aligned to `model_third`.

## 7.4 Explicit semantics

- **Mandatory checks**: Every iteration of BODY must contain at least one `Credit Check` and one `Technical Review` before any approval. Missing either is a conformance violation.
- **Optional Supplier Review**: `Supplier Review` is optional in the control‑flow model but must be present for cases labeled as external, and absent for internal cases. Violations are classified as data‑policy breaches (external/internal misclassification or missing SR event).
- **Expedite Review ordering**: If `Expedite Review` occurs, it must follow completion of both mandatory checks in the corresponding BODY iteration and precede the first approval. It may appear before or after `Supplier Review`.
- **Exactly-one approval**: Exactly one of `Manager Approval` or `Auto Approval` appears per case, after the final review cycle and before `Release Order`. Multiple approvals or missing approvals are treated as serious violations.
- **Correction and recheck behavior**:
  - `Correct Dossier` starts a new review cycle.
  - After each correction, at least one mandatory check must repeat before approval.
  - If an `affected_checks` attribute is available, only those checks must repeat; repeating unaffected checks is permitted but flagged as potential inefficiency.
  - Any repetition of a mandatory check without an intervening `Correct Dossier` is labeled suspicious (P7).

## 7.5 Limitations due to missing attributes

- **External/internal attribute**: Without an `order_source` attribute, the model cannot definitively enforce P3; we can only *infer* likely external orders from SR/ER usage, which risks both false positives and false negatives. The third model therefore classifies any use of `Supplier Review` without `order_source="external"` as “policy‑ambiguous” rather than automatically invalid.
- **Affected-check attribute**: Without a field such as `affected_checks`, we cannot tell whether repeating only CC (or only TR) after `Correct Dossier` is correct. The model_third backbone supports both behaviors, but conformance diagnostics must mark them as “conditional” and invite manual review.
- **Historical metrics**: Recalculating fitness/precision/generalization for model_third will still be affected by historical logging quality. We cannot retroactively repair missing events or misclassified external/internal cases.

Despite these limitations, `model_third` more faithfully encodes P1–P7 than S, is less brittle and more compact than C, and cleanly separates **control‑flow structure** from **data‑ and attribute‑driven policies**.

Expedite optional, after both checks, before approval, before/after Supplier** | Violates: ER can precede checks and approvals inside `+` (B4). | Satisfies: ER only appears after both checks and before `Manager Approval`; ordering with Supplier is enumerated in specific branches. | Satisfies: ER constrained to occur between completion of CC/TR and the first approval; may interleave with SR. | For expedite policy, C and the third model are aligned; S is unsafe. |
| **P5. Exactly one of Manager or Auto** | Satisfies via `X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval')`. | Satisfies: each branch has exactly one approval. | Satisfies: approvals modeled as XOR. | All models agree structurally; enforcement quality then depends on how approvals are used with attributes (e.g., Auto vs external). |
| **P6. Correction may repeat only affected checks** | Violates: no `Correct Dossier` or repeat of checks; cannot express policy. | Partially satisfies: only pattern is `Correct Dossier` then repeating **both** checks. | Satisfies at the modeling level via RECHECK and affected-check constraints; operationally limited by missing `affected_checks` attribute. | Third model best matches the intent; S ignores corrections, C over-constrains them. |
| **P7. Repeated mandatory check without correction is suspicious** | Cannot represent: repeats of CC/TR are not allowed at all. | Cannot represent suspicious repeats: repeats only occur with `Correct Dossier` in one branch. | Explicitly flags any repeat of CC/TR without an intervening `Correct Dossier` as suspicious. | Third model provides explicit anomaly detection; S and C structurally miss this pattern. |
| **B1** | Accepts. | Accepts. | Accepts as compliant. | All agree; baseline happy path. |
| **B2** | Accepts. | Rejects (order and Auto combination not enumerated). | Accepts as compliant. | Third model sides with S here: order of checks before Auto is not semantically important, so C is overly strict. |
| **B3** | Accepts (violating P2). | Rejects. | Rejects as serious violation. | Confirms that S cannot be used for enforcement; C and third model behave correctly. |
| **B4** | Accepts (violating P4). | Rejects. | Rejects as violation of expedite policy. | Again, S admits forbidden behavior; C and third model concur with policy. |
| **B5** | Accepts. | Rejects. | Accepts as valid for external urgent orders, with a note that Auto+SR should be reviewed by policy owners. | Third model preserves S’s desirable flexibility for plausible new external/urgent combinations while making the Auto/SR combination explicit in diagnostics; C is needlessly rigid. |
| **B6** | Rejects (no correction behavior at all). | Rejects (requires both checks to repeat). | Classifies as “conditionally valid”: accepted if a future `affected_checks={"Technical Review"}` attribute supports it; otherwise flagged as ambiguous. | Third model exposes the dependency on missing data; S and C fail to support P6’s nuance. |
| **B7** | Accepts. | Accepts. | Accepts for external orders, rejects for internal ones. | Only third model distinguishes internal vs external semantics; S and C treat SR as just another activity. |
| **B8** | Rejects due to lack of repeats. | Rejects due to missing `Correct Dossier`. | Rejects and flags as P7-type suspicious pattern if ever observed. | Third model maintains rejection but provides a clearer rationale and detection rule. |
| **A1: Register, Credit, Technical, Supplier, Auto, Release** | Accepts. | Rejects (no branch with Supplier + Auto). | Accepts but flags as “requires explicit policy decision” for external orders approved automatically. | Distinguishes third model from both: it neither blindly rejects (C) nor silently accepts (S) but surfaces the combination as a governance choice. |
| **A2: Register, Credit, Technical, Correct, Credit, Manager, Release** | Rejects (no correction/repeat structure). | Rejects (only branch repeats both checks). | Accepts as compliant if `affected_checks={"Credit Check"}`; otherwise marks as ambiguous. | Demonstrates third model’s selective recheck semantics: behaviors consistent with P6 but unseen in training can be admitted with proper data, unlike S or C. |
| **A3: Register, Credit, Technical, Correct, Credit, Technical, Auto, Release** | Rejects. | Rejects (no correction + Auto branch). | Accepts as compliant (both checks repeated after correction, Auto allowed) assuming P5 only requires exactly-one approval. | Shows that third model generalizes correction plus Auto Approval consistently, while both S and C are too restrictive given the stated policies. |

These audits show that **model_third**:

- matches C’s strictness where policy is clear (P2, P4, P5);
- preserves S’s desirable flexibility for new combinations (B2, B5, A3);
- adds explicit treatment of corrections and suspicious repeats (P6, P7, B6, B8, A2);
- exposes dependencies on missing attributes as “conditional” rather than silently absorbing them.

# 9. Validation Experiments

To govern adoption of model_third (and to reassess S and C), I recommend the following evaluation program.

## 9.1 Reproducible case splits and preprocessing

- Define a canonical preprocessing pipeline (case ID definition, timestamp sorting, noise filtering, event attribute normalization).
- Use fixed random seeds (e.g., 42, 1729, 2026) and document them.
- Apply **stratified splits** to ensure similar distributions of case length, presence of Supplier/Expedite, and corrections in train/validation/holdout sets.

Skeleton:

```python
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.log.importer.xes import factory as xes_importer
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedShuffleSplit

log = xes_importer.apply("orders.xes")

def case_stratum(trace):
    has_sr = any(e["concept:name"] == "Supplier Review" for e in trace)
    has_er = any(e["concept:name"] == "Expedite Review" for e in trace)
    has_corr = any(e["concept:name"] == "Correct Dossier" for e in trace)
    return f"sr={has_sr}|er={has_er}|corr={has_corr}"

strata = [case_stratum(t) for t in log]

splitter = StratifiedShuffleSplit(
    n_splits=1, test_size=0.2, random_state=42
)
(train_idx, holdout_idx), = splitter.split(range(len(log)), strata)

train_log = pm4py.objects.log.util.split_log.split_log_by_indices(log, train_idx)
holdout_log = pm4py.objects.log.util.split_log.split_log_by_indices(log, holdout_idx)
```

## 9.2 Attribute-stratified holdout tests

- Once `order_source` (external/internal) and `affected_checks` attributes are added, create separate holdout subsets:
  - external vs internal;
  - with vs without correction;
  - with vs without Expedite.
- Evaluate fitness and precision per stratum for S, C, and model_third.

```python
def filter_traces(log, predicate):
    from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog
    return EventLog([t for t in log if predicate(t)])

external_holdout = filter_traces(holdout_log, lambda t: t.attributes.get("order_source") == "external")
internal_holdout = filter_traces(holdout_log, lambda t: t.attributes.get("order_source") == "internal")
```

## 9.3 Curated negative trace suite

- Manually construct a small `EventLog` of policy‑forbidden behaviors (e.g., B3, B4, B8‑style and other clear violations).
- Measure how often each model **accepts** such negatives under reasonable alignment‑cost settings.

```python
from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event

def make_trace(labels):
    tr = Trace()
    for i, l in enumerate(labels):
        tr.append(Event({"concept:name": l, "time:timestamp": i}))
    return tr

negative_log = EventLog([
    make_trace(["Register Dossier", "Manager Approval", "Release Order"]),  # B3
    make_trace(["Register Dossier", "Expedite Review", "Credit Check", "Technical Review", "Manager Approval", "Release Order"]),  # B4
    make_trace(["Register Dossier", "Credit Check", "Technical Review", "Credit Check", "Manager Approval", "Release Order"]),  # B8-style
])

# Example: fitness / diagnostics for model_third
net3, im3, fm3 = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model_third)
neg_fit_3 = pm4py.fitness_alignments(negative_log, net3, im3, fm3)
```

- Evaluate how each model’s alignment fitness and diagnostics change under these perturbations.

```python
from copy import deepcopy

def drop_events(trace, names):
    t = deepcopy(trace)
    t[:] = [e for e in trace if e["concept:name"] not in names]
    return t

no_cc_log = EventLog([drop_events(t, {"Credit Check"}) for t in holdout_log])
```

- Check that model_third accepts them with good fitness, while S and C behave as expected (S over‑accepting, C over‑rejecting).

## 9.8 Separate reporting of fitness, precision, generalization, simplicity

- For all three models, recompute:

  - alignment fitness on train/holdout;
  - alignment precision on train;
  - token-based generalization;
  - arc-degree simplicity.

- Report per stratum and overall, without aggregating into a single score.

```python
def evaluate_model(log, model):
    net, im, fm = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model)
    return {
        "fitness": pm4py.fitness_alignments(log, net, im, fm),
        "precision": pm4py.precision_alignments(log, net, im, fm),
        "generalization": pm4py.generalization_tbr(log, net, im, fm),
        "simplicity": pm4py.simplicity_petri_net(net, im, fm, variant="arc_degree"),
```

## 9.9 Stakeholder comprehension protocol

- Define a documented protocol: tasks (e.g., “find where corrections are modeled”), participant roles, time limits, scoring rubric.
- Compare S, C, and model_third diagrams under this controlled protocol, emphasizing understanding of **edge cases** like correction and external orders.

## 9.10 Decision rule

Adopt a governance rule such as:

- A model is **rejected for enforcement** if:

  - it admits any curated negative trace with high fitness; or
  - it misclassifies more than a specified fraction of holdout cases in key strata (e.g., external with corrections); or
  - it fails soundness checks.

- A model may still be used for **exploration** if:

  - it is clearly labeled as non‑normative; and
  - its weaknesses (e.g., optionalized checks) are documented alongside any dashboards or reports built on it.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

- **Exploration / discovery**: Model S may continue to be used **only** as an exploratory lens, clearly labeled as permissive and non‑normative. Its diagrams and fast runtime are valuable for workshops and high‑level communication, but no compliance decisions or KPIs should be based on it.
- **Compliance / conformance checking**: Neither S nor C, in their current forms, should serve as the primary enforcement model. Model C is closer to a normative model but is too brittle, over‑enumerated, and opaque. The board should invest in implementing and validating **model_third** as the main compliance artifact, with explicit attribute‑based constraints and correction semantics.
- **Simulation / what‑if analysis**: Model_third, once validated, should replace both S and C for simulation, as it balances generalization and policy fidelity. C’s low holdout fitness makes it unsuitable for scenario generation; S’s over‑permissiveness makes simulated paths misleadingly optimistic.
- **Retirement plan**: Model C should be retired for general use and retained only as a historical reference of training variants. Model S should be retained as a teaching and exploration tool under clear governance notes. Model_third should only be deployed once it passes the validation experiments in Section 9 and has undergone stakeholder comprehension testing.

# 11. Dissenting View

A reasonable dissenting perspective is that the board is overcomplicating the modeling landscape and underestimating the practical value of Model C. From this angle, the primary business risk is not over‑enforcement but under‑enforcement: orders slipping through without the mandated checks. Model C’s high precision on the training log, its strict enforcement of P2 and P4, and its explicit inclusion of correction cycles all indicate a model that **takes compliance seriously**. The low holdout fitness may simply reflect a backlog of undocumented, potentially non‑compliant behavior that ought to be confronted, not normalized. Moreover, branch enumeration and duplicate labels, while inelegant, make every allowed path explicit, which many auditors prefer to the more abstract partial orders and declarative rules proposed for model_third. Investing in a sophisticated hybrid model with new attributes and constraints could delay much‑needed enforcement, whereas deploying Model C now—with clear instructions that “unseen = noncompliant until approved” (F8)—might immediately improve control.

What evidence would change this dissenting position is, first, a **carefully curated negative test suite** showing that Model C still admits clearly forbidden behaviors (such as B3/B4‑style traces) under realistic alignment‑cost settings. Second, **attribute‑stratified holdout evaluations** demonstrating that many of the holdout “rejections” by C are actually policy‑compliant, especially in high‑value external urgent orders, would weaken the case for strictness‑at‑all‑costs. Third, a **controlled stakeholder comprehension study** in which auditors and process owners can reliably explain model_third’s treatment of corrections, Supplier Review, and Auto Approval—while failing to do so for C—would support replacing C with a more structured hybrid. Finally, if runtime and maintenance costs for C continue to grow disproportionately with process changes, that operational burden would further justify transitioning to model_third despite the comfort some stakeholders feel with explicit branch lists.
