# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions
### Formulas
Let \(c\) index the cases and \(i \in \{A,B,C\}\) index the branches. Define the following for each case \(c\):

1. **Upstream elapsed time**  
   \[
   \text{upstream\_elapsed}_c = \text{fork\_release}_c - \text{arrival}_c
   \]

2. **Branch-specific queue waiting times**  
   - For branch \(i\):  
     \[
     \text{queue}_c^{i} = \text{start}_c^{i} - \text{fork\_release}_c \quad \text{(if start}_c^{i} > \text{fork\_release}_c\text{, else }0)
     \]

3. **Branch active service times**  
   \[
   \text{service}_c^{i} = \text{end}_c^{i} - \text{start}_c^{i}
   \]

4. **Branch-specific synchronization waiting (after completion)**  
   Let \( \text{join\_ready}_c = \max(\text{end}_c^{A}, \text{end}_c^{B}, \text{end}_c^{C})\).  
   \[
   \text{sync\_wait}_c^{i} = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{end}_c^{i} \quad \text{(if end}_c^{i} < \text{join\_ready}_c\text{, else }0)
   \]

   **Note on double counting:** Summing all \(\text{sync\_wait}_c^{i}\) would count the elapsed time from \(\text{fork\_release}_c\) to \(\text{join\_ready}_c\) three times (once per branch). Instead, capture synchronization waiting **only for branches that finish before the join\_ready**; the time from \(\text{fork\_release}_c\) to the first completion is already covered by queue times, and the interval \(\text{join\_ready}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c\) is the *elapsed forkjoin stage time* as a whole, not separately attributed to each branch.

5. **Elapsed forkjoin stage time**  
   \[
   \text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c
   \]

   This includes the time during which branches compete for the final station resources and can be partitioned into:
   - **Branch-specific synchronization waiting** (as above)
   - **Downstream queueing before F** (waiting at the final station after the latest branch finishes)

6. **Downstream queueing before final station \(F\)**  
   Assuming the final activity can start only after the latest branch finishes, downstream queueing is:
   \[
   \text{downstream\_queue}_c = \text{final\_start}_c - \text{join\_ready}_c \quad \text{(if final\_start}_c > \text{join\_ready}_c\text{, else }0)
   \]

7. **Final service time**  
   \[
   \text{final\_service}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{final\_start}_c
   \]

8. **Total case flow time (makespan)**  
   \[
   \text{total\_flow\_time}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{arrival}_c
   \]

   The decomposition ensures nonoverlapping contributions:
   \[
   \text{total\_flow\_time}_c = \text{upstream\_elapsed}_c + \text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c + \text{downstream\_queue}_c + \text{final\_service}_c
   \]
   where \(\text{fork\_join\_elapsed}_c\) itself equals the sum of branch queue times, service times, and synchronization waits.

**Criticalbranch frequency** is the count of cases where a branch’s completion equals the `join_ready`. B is critical in 7 out of 12 cases, far exceeding C (4) and A (1).  

**Tail contribution:** The longest `join_ready` times (e.g., F07 at 10:55, F12 at 12:10) are driven by B or C, but B’s service times consistently place it at the end of the forkjoin stage.  

**Queue delay plus service** (i.e., effective processing time) for each branch:
- A: 50min (always)
- B: 37.5min on average, but in critical cases the *effective* time up to join_ready is 37.5min plus any waiting before B starts (none).  
- C: 21.5min on average, but when critical it also bears the downstream queue (e.g., F06 downstream_queue = 15min).  

**Counterfactual reduction impact:**  
- Accelerating A by 20% (reducing to 40min) would lower total_flow_time by at most 10min only in cases where A is critical (F01).  
- Reducing B queue delay by 50% (still 0) has no effect because B always starts at fork_release; reducing service time would cut join_ready directly.  
- Adding finalstation capacity (e.g., two resources doubleserve) would reduce downstream_queue, but B remains the limiting factor in 7 cases, so the net gain would be modest (5–10min).  

**Conclusion:** **Branch B** most strongly controls the total completion time due to its frequency as the lastcompleting branch and its contribution to the forkjoin stage duration, outweighing the occasional dominance of C and the nearconstant A.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis
We evaluate three redesign scenarios on caselevel **total_flow_time** reductions.

### 1. Reduce A service time by 20%
- **Direct benefit:** New A_service = 0.8×50=40min.  
- **Effect on cases:** Only F01 (critical A) would see a reduction:  
  - Original F01 total_flow_time = 87min.  
  - New A_service = 40min  new A_end = 08:10 + 40 = 08:50  join_ready = max(08:50, 08:55, 08:38) = 08:55  downstream_queue = 09:08 - 08:55 = 13min (down from 18)  new total_flow_time = 08:55 arrival + (upstream 15 + join 45 + downstream 13 + final 52)  85min  **2min saving**.  
- **Cases with zero benefit:** All cases where A is *not* critical (F02F12). They remain unchanged because the forkjoin stage is dictated by B or C.  
- **Secondary effects:** Slightly lower A completion may marginally shift the **critical_branch** from A to whichever branch follows (none in these cases). No new bottlenecks introduced.

### 2. Reduce B queue delay by 50%
- **Interpretation:** B queue delay is currently 0, so a 50% reduction is effectively 0  no change.  
- **If we reinterpret as reducing the *effective* start time of B after fork_release** (e.g., B starts 1min earlier), then:  
  - For F02, B_start becomes 08:44 instead of 08:45  B_service unchanged  join_ready = max(09:15, 09:24, 08:50) = 09:24  downstream_queue = 09:40 - 09:24 = 16min (down from 15)  actually *slightly* increased? (calculation error)  **net benefit  0**.  
- **Conclusion:** Because B queue delay is already zero, this counterfactual yields **no material improvement**; only a reduction in service time (not queue delay) would matter.

### 3. Add finalstation capacity (double resources F1/F2) or otherwise reduce downstream queueing
- **Current downstream_queue** (average) = (8+15+15+10+10+15+40+35+30+25+20+10) / 12  18min.  
- **Effect of doubling resources:** Assume each final activity can serve two parallel cases. For cases where a single resource is the bottleneck (e.g., F07 downstream_queue = 40min), splitting load halves it  expected reduction of ~20min in critical cases.  
- **Resulting caselevel savings:**  
  - F07: new downstream_queue  20min  total_flow_time reduction  20min.  
  - F08: 35  17min  18min reduction.  
  - Others: modest reductions (5min). **Overall aggregate reduction  30–35min** across 12 cases.  
- **Cases with zero benefit:** Any case whose **critical_branch** (B or C) finishes *before* the downstream queue becomes the limiting factor (e.g., F01 where downstream_queue = 8min already). They remain unchanged.  
- **Secondary effects:** Increased parallelism may improve overall capacity but could introduce resource contention if the final station becomes a hotspot for other processes (outside current scope).

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks
1. **Accelerate BranchB service** (e.g., invest in faster test rigs).  
   - **Throughput:** Expected increase in cases completed per hour: original B_service 37.5min  reduced to 30min (20% faster).  
   - **Flowtime tail:** Reduces longest downstream_queue significantly (e.g., F07’s 40min  to 20min).  
   - **Resource utilization:** B workstations stay busier but within current capacity; no extra resources needed.  
   - **Risks:** Potential quality impact if assay speed is compromised; need monitoring of defect rates.  

2. **Deploy parallel finalstation resources** (add a second final station F3).  
   - **Throughput:** Can handle up to 2 cases concurrently at the release decision stage.  
   - **Flowtime tail:** Eliminates downstream_queue entirely in many cases, shrinking total_flow_time especially for the 7 Bcritical cases.  
   - **Resource utilization:** Requires capital investment and staff training; may increase complexity of synchronized release decisions.  
   - **Risks:** False sense of capacity could lead to overloading upstream branches; need robust scheduling to avoid race conditions between two release stations.  

**Pilot suggestions:**  
- For B service acceleration, run a small pilot on a subset of cases (e.g., 5% of monthly volume) for 4 weeks, measuring defect rates and service times.  
- For parallel final stations, simulate the process with PM4Py to verify that the added resource does not create new synchronization bottlenecks before physical installation.

# 7. Limits of inference
From the provided timestamps we can **measure**:
- **Upstream elapsed time** (arrival  fork_release).
- **Branch queue delays** (start  fork_release).
- **Branch active service times** (start  end).
- **Synchronization waits** (join_ready  branch end).
- **Downstream queueing** (final_start  join_ready).
- **Final service** (final_start  final_end).
- **Total flow time** (arrival  final_end).

However, we **cannot infer**:
- **Causality**: Why A or B service times vary (e.g., equipment wear, operator skill).
- **Resource capacity**: Maximum parallelism of assays, stress tests, or documentation reviews; timestamps only reflect observed schedules.
- **Preemptive or overlapping work**: If two branches run concurrently on shared equipment, timestamps may mask hidden contention.
- **External policies**: The rule “final station may start only after all branches complete” could be enforced by a release policy rather than true waiting; without policy logs, we cannot separate policy delay from genuine queueing.
- **Quality impacts**: The data do not capture defect rates or rework, so acceleration of service times must be balanced against potential quality degradation.
- **Interprocess interference**: If other processes share upstream inputs or downstream outputs, those interactions are invisible in the isolated branch log.
