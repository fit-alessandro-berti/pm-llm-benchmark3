# 1. Data audit and estimands

I treat each date as 12 scheduled open hours (07:00–19:00). The daily intensity estimates below are cases per open day and, in parentheses, cases per scheduled open hour. They are not rates per clock hour across the whole week. On 2026-04-15, the outage occupied approximately three of those hours; keeping the scheduled 12-hour denominator makes that day's lost production visible rather than recasting it as a shorter normal day.

Let A be arrivals during an open day, C be completions during an open day, and L be the time-average number of cases in the system. The table reports mean_wip as a 15-minute-snapshot average, but the exact snapshot coverage and whether it is an open-hours-only average are not stated. staff_fte is concurrent available capacity averaged over the day, not a measured productive service rate. median_service_min and p90_service_min describe active handling time in minutes; they exclude waiting and are neither mean service time nor flow time. complex_pct is a daily percentage, and a simple mean of these percentages is not exactly the case-weighted fraction because daily arrival totals differ and the percentages may be rounded.

At least these threats limit inference:

1. **Unobserved boundaries and carry-over.** The 07:00 backlog is not known for each day. Cases can remain overnight, and the completed count on a date need not belong to that date's arrivals. Daily A−C is therefore not a same-day queue growth or cohort service measure. Across all 28 days there are 2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions, a net observed difference of 110, but the initial and final system contents are unknown.
2. **Non-independent dates.** Calendar day, repeated weekdays, changing case mix, backlog, and intervention are serially structured. Treating 28 rows as 28 independent replications would understate uncertainty.
3. **Unequal operating conditions.** Weekends have much lower staffing and arrivals; one day has a three-hour outage; the intervention starts at an unknown time on April 29. A common 12-hour denominator is useful for scheduled exposure, but does not make the conditions equivalent.
4. **Changing or recoded case mix.** Complex share rises from about 21% in April 6–14 to about 44% in April 20–28. The prompt explicitly leaves open whether this is operational or a coding change, so service-time change cannot be attributed to workload complexity alone.
5. **Percentiles cannot be pooled as if they were means.** Daily medians and p90s do not identify case-level service-time distributions, and the prompt supplies no case counts by service-time distribution or means. A median of daily medians is only a robust summary of the daily statistics.
6. **WIP sampling and scope.** A snapshot average has no supplied variance, start/end boundary values, or precise time coverage. It is unclear whether WIP includes both waiting and active cases at all snapshots. The queueing quantity must cover the same system scope as throughput.
7. **Intervention confounding.** Staffing, weekday/weekend mix, backlog, and possibly work mix all change across the intervention boundary. There is no untreated comparison group and only three post-boundary weekdays, including the uncertain start date.
8. **Intraday evidence is sparse and internally inconsistent.** Only four representative hourly vectors are given. Interpreting their bins as labeled, their 08:00–10:00 shares are 16.8%, 18.1%, 9.6%, and 11.8% for April 7, 15, 21, and 25 respectively; the corresponding daily-table values are 31%, 32%, 47%, and 22%. This mismatch must be reconciled before using either source to set an exact 08:00–10:00 staffing target.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different and useful operational view is to segment by **observed queue state**, regardless of calendar date or presumed cause. For example, define a high-WIP state as mean_wip ≥ 35, a low-WIP state as <25, and retain the middle as a transition band; separately flag days when completed > arrivals as possible drawdown days. These are descriptive cut points, not validated service thresholds. The high-WIP state includes the outage and recovery dates, all seven high-mix pre-intervention weekdays, and April 29–30. The low-WIP state includes early routine days and the later high-mix weekends. The latter is informative: high case complexity does not automatically imply high WIP when arrival volume is lower.

This view represents the controller's question, “How much inventory is present, and is it rising or falling?” It helps identify when to trigger backlog triage or relief capacity without first deciding whether mix, arrival bursts, or service capacity caused the state. Its weaknesses are that the threshold is arbitrary, it groups the outage with case-mix stress, and it can call a high-completion day “recovery” even when completions simply include older cases. It also obscures weekday/weekend demand and the possible coding change. The primary phase segmentation is better for diagnosing changing operating conditions; the queue-state alternative is better for describing current pressure. They should be reported as complementary views rather than forced into one “true” partition.

A simpler reporting alternative is four calendar weeks (Apr 6–12, Apr 13–19, Apr 20–26, Apr 27–May 3). It offers complete seven-day blocks with weekday/weekend composition controlled at the week level, and can suit routine weekly reporting. But it puts the outage and recovery in one week, combines mix change with high-mix operation in the next, and combines high-mix days with the staffing transition in the last. It is therefore weaker for attributing operational mechanisms than the primary segmentation.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

The representative vectors show different hourly dispersion. Calculating population variance and standard deviation across the 12 hourly arrival counts for each representative day gives:

| Apr 25 weekend | 51 | 4.25 | 1.18 | 0.53 | 1.88 | 29.4% |

The Apr 21 vector has 34 arrivals in one hour and another 24 in a later hour, unlike a smoothly varying day. Its hourly Fano index and CV are high relative to these three other examples. The outage vector also has zero completions for three consecutive midday bins while arrivals continue, so a daily completion average erases when capacity was unavailable. These Fano values are only **within-vector hourly heterogeneity**. Hours have a diurnal pattern and are not repeated independent observations from a stationary count process; an Fano index above one here is not, on its own, an estimate of a Poisson alternative or an overdispersion parameter. Four selected vectors cannot estimate the distribution of hourly counts, day-to-day burst probability, or arrival-completion dependence.

For the 28 daily rows, simple lag-1 Pearson correlations are approximately 0.41 for arrivals, 0.30 for completions, 0.61 for WIP, and 0.93 for complex share. They are descriptive only: a weekday/weekend cycle and the strong time trend in complex share can induce serial correlation. Still, the sustained run of high complex share is evidence against treating the sequence as exchangeable. A plausible change point occurs between Apr 19 and Apr 20 (complex share +18 percentage points; median service 29 to 34 minutes; p90 56 to 78), with a separate documented-but-imprecise staffing boundary at Apr 29. The outage and immediate recovery are another transient episode, not a stable regime. A transparent threshold heuristic can flag the Apr 20 mix jump and Apr 29 staffing change, but this is a prompt for investigation, not a statistical change-point test or ground truth.

The reported peak_08_10_pct suggests a strong weekday concentration: its mean is 37.5% on weekdays and 19.3% on weekends. However, when bins are interpreted as labeled, the four representative vectors imply only 9.6–18.1% during 08:00–10:00, conflicting with the daily table. Resolve the definitions, time-bin alignment, or extraction before using these as precise peak shares. Even with reconciliation, four illustrative days do not establish a stable intraday arrival profile or staffing distribution.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law relates long-run average system population L (cases) to throughput λ (cases per unit time) and average time in the same system W (time): L = λW. Here a defensible calculation needs matching system boundaries, WIP time coverage, and a sufficiently stable window. For the two illustrative calculations below, I assume the WIP snapshots summarize the same 07:00–19:00 windows as the count rates. I use completions as the throughput proxy because in a stable window completions include cases carried over from earlier arrivals; the proxy is only credible when starts and ends are approximately balanced. Results are **scheduled open-hour equivalents** for mean system time, not calendar elapsed time, waiting-only time, or median flow time. Overnight closure pauses service but does not erase elapsed calendar time.

# 7. Operational implications

- **Staff by day type and intraday load.** Maintain separate weekday and weekend baselines: observed weekday demand averages 108 arrivals/day versus 45 on weekends, with correspondingly different mean FTE. Within weekdays, use reconciled interval arrivals, service starts, and staff presence to place relief around peaks. Until the peak-share mismatch is resolved, treat the daily peak percentages as a signal to investigate rather than a precise schedule.
- **Protect recovery capacity.** Keep outage recovery visibly labeled as backlog drawdown. Track WIP at opening and closing, oldest-case age, and completions by arrival cohort; do not use Apr 16–17's 120.5 completions/day as a normal target. Trigger temporary triage or flex capacity from sustained WIP and backlog age, not from a single high completion count.
- **Evaluate the staffing change with a clean comparison.** Log the exact implementation time and assignment of added FTE, then compare like weekdays with comparable mix and starting WIP. The post-boundary weekday data are encouraging descriptively: complex share is similar, staffing is higher, WIP falls, and completions exceed arrivals. Three weekdays cannot establish a causal or sustainable effect.
- **Smooth peaks only after validating the time series.** If arrival timestamps confirm a concentrated interval, appointment spacing, callbacks, or moving deferrable work away from the peak may reduce short-lived overload without changing daily demand. Preserve a no-show and urgent-work buffer and assess queue age and completion times by cohort so smoothing does not simply delay complex cases.
- **Collect event-level evidence.** Retain case ID, arrival, service start/stop, completion, variant/complex flag, resource/FTE roster, WIP snapshots, outage start/end, and coding-version timestamps. This permits matched flow-time analysis, queue-versus-system WIP separation, and a credible estimate of service-time means and arrival variability.

A risky pooled-average policy would be to remove the extra weekday staffing after seeing 92.2 completions/day against 88.6 arrivals/day in Apr 29–May 3 and a lower pooled WIP than the high-mix phase. Segmented data show the preceding high-mix weekdays were short by 13.4 completions/day at about 5.93 FTE, while the three transition weekdays averaged 6.83 FTE and still had mean WIP 37. The observed improvement may depend on that extra capacity and backlog drawdown; withdrawing it based on the five-day pooled comparison could restart accumulation.
