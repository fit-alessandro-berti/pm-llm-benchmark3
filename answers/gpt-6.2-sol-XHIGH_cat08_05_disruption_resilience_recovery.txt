# 1. Disruption timeline

| Phase | Operational state, dominant variants, and risk posture |
|---|---|
| **T0 · Day −30 to −1** | The baseline covers 42,000 orders over 21 business days. The dominant path ran from electronic authorization and reservation through picking, release, dispatch, proof of delivery, and invoice close. Compliance holds, backorders, and repack/recheck loops handled exceptions. Baseline traceability exceptions were 0.8%. |
| **T1 · Day 0 morning** | The WMS outage interrupted electronic stock verification and the ordinary pick, scan, and handoff workflow. In-flight orders could already have been reserved, picked, packed, or dispatched when visibility was lost. Establishing the last trustworthy state of each order and stock movement became a prerequisite for safely resuming fulfillment. T1 has no separate quantitative summary. |
| **T2 · Day 0 afternoon to Day 2** | The manual phase covered 5,600 orders. The log shows 5,100 receipt-to-triage transitions. Triage led to manual authorization 2,500 times, an explicit system-check skip 1,600 times, and the hospital fast lane 1,000 times. Snapshot checks, paper and spreadsheet picking, and selective manual release sustained shipping. The displayed edges into dispatch without scan include 1,800 directly from manual pack and 1,450 from temporary release control. Hospital-critical fill was 91% and pharmacy-standard fill was 61%. This is consistent with teams redirecting capacity to hospitals while deferrals and cancellations accumulated elsewhere. The morning stock snapshot became less reliable as movements accumulated. |
| **T3 · Days 3–6** | Intake returned for a phase covering 11,400 orders, but inventory synchronization and scanning remained unreliable. A plausible hybrid variant was electronic intake feeding manual stock and scan controls, with exposure to conflicting reservations, duplicate processing, and late event entry. Service and conformance improved relative to T2, while bulk tail delays and evidence gaps remained substantial. Order-level evidence is needed to establish how work moved between manual and restored systems. |
| **T4 · Days 7–21** | Across 35,000 orders, the normal electronic path again dominated, alongside backlog prioritization, reservation reconciliation, delivery-proof repair, and post-invoice audit work. The DFG shows 1,400 reservation-to-reconciliation transitions, 1,200 dispatch-to-proof-remediation transitions, and 2,300 invoice-close-to-audit-remediation transitions. The hospital median returned to baseline, while pharmacy and bulk tails, rework, and evidence exceptions stayed elevated. The audit queue persisted after operational recovery. |

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience preserved critical supply and the purpose of the controls under outage conditions. B is a strong example when its evidence establishes eligibility at the time of release. J is resilient when explicit allocation rules make room for newly urgent orders. K restores the stock and order state needed for safe fulfillment decisions. A and I can protect service when urgency changes queue position and when deferral has a review deadline. The hospital and pharmacy results suggest a redistribution of service. Estimating the contribution of the fast lane requires comparing similarly urgent orders, including open and canceled cases and differences in stock availability.

C and bare G are shortcuts when eligibility or custody cannot be established. D, E, F, and H depend on the quality of the substitute. A snapshot can support allocation while it is fresh and backed by an accountable movement ledger. Manual picking can preserve lot lineage if staff record the actual pick at the time. Pharmacist sampling leaves some orders without a documented release and cannot cover mandatory per-order checks. Phone confirmation has different evidentiary value for routine ambient delivery and controlled-product custody. Management can vary tolerances for delay, staffing cost, and how long a verified record waits for system entry. Required release controls have to be satisfied at the point of decision. L has value for containment, record repair, and learning, with a distinct outcome for any control failure it uncovers.

The T2 alignment shows both added actions, such as phone proof and the spreadsheet invoice hold, and missing normative events, such as release, scan, delivery proof, and temperature records. An added action may implement the same control through a different medium. A missing event may reflect delayed upload, alternative evidence, or a control failure. Test those explanations by linking orders across phases and checking signed release forms, time-stamped temperature data, courier manifests, and authorization records against actual movement times. Distinguish a check supported by contemporaneous evidence, a check whose performance remains uncertain, and a confirmed omission. Sample by route, product risk, site, and shift to compare competing explanations, and review every known high-risk gap for containment. Follow the long backlog-to-pick, handoff-to-phone-proof, and invoice-to-audit intervals as distinct queues with different owners.

# 4. Recovery process model

1. **Activate degraded operations and establish the cutover.** The incident lead activates the approved manual procedure for a site or workflow as soon as the WMS is unavailable or the integrity of a required control cannot be established. Examples include inconsistent reservations, untrusted authorization data, or failed verification of scan capture. Suspend the affected release path immediately. Record the cutover time, affected systems, and the last trusted data versions. Identify orders already picked, packed, or handed over, and hold uncertain stock and in-flight orders until their status is established. Operations and Quality approve the scope that can run with verified manual controls.
2. **Triage and reserve.** Give each order and shipment a unique ID. Record the verified clinical deadline, shortage or patient impact, customer segment, product risk flags, and stock and courier availability. Give imminent clinical needs access to the fast lane. Sequence other eligible orders by promised delivery time and age, and recheck urgency periodically. Product risk determines who can handle, check, and release the order. Offer partial fills or approved reroutes when they meet the clinical need and every control still applies to the shipped lines. Maintain one controlled ledger of lot-level reservations and stock movements. If the ledger is out of balance, its opening data is beyond the approved freshness limit, or lot status is uncertain, require confirmed physical availability and trusted status before allocation.
3. **Apply the release gate.** Every shipment requires verified customer and product eligibility, valid stock and lot status, an item and quantity match, a documented release decision, and a traceable handoff to an approved carrier or recipient. Product controls accumulate when flags overlap. Hospital urgency accelerates access to qualified reviewers and transport. Pharmacy and bulk orders follow the same minimum control rules for their products.

   | Product flag | Additional controls in degraded operations |
   |---|---|
   | **Ambient** | Verify current authorization, lot and expiry as applicable, pick and pack accuracy, and handoff. A preverified, current customer record and a documented manual release can expedite routine orders. |
   | **Cold-chain** | Use approved packaging and transport and a working calibrated logger. Link the logger, shipment, and lot before release. Check available readings and handling times, monitor throughout transport, and review the delivered record for gaps and excursions. Hold before release or contain after handoff when required evidence is missing or limits are exceeded, pending Quality disposition. |
   | **Controlled-substance** | Verify specific customer and product authority. Capture named, independent dual-control attestations at the required steps. Use secure storage and transport, and verify and record custody transfers and the authorized recipient's receipt. |
   | **Recall-sensitive** | Check current recall and quarantine status by lot before release, preserve lot-to-recipient linkage, and block affected or uncertain lots. If a concern emerges after dispatch, locate shipments and escalate immediately for containment. |

4. **Capture evidence at the point of work.** Use a secured offline register or numbered forms under controlled custody. Link the order line, lot, shipment, package and, where applicable, logger or seal ID. Record who performed and independently verified each applicable step, the source and version of decision data, the actual time, the decision or reading, and signatures or attestations. Carrier and warehouse staff sign and time the handoff. Track delivery proof as due, received, or overdue, with recipient authentication appropriate to product risk. Keep originals and preserve revisions. Assign overdue proof to an owner, and hold invoice closure as needed until shipment and delivery status can be supported.
5. **Restart in scoped waves and reconcile before releasing affected backlog.** IT establishes integrity of the restored services. Operations and Quality test representative orders, comparing system records and physical stock through reservation, scan, release, and delivery evidence. Retain a single authoritative reservation path for each stock scope during cutover. Replay manual records using unique transaction IDs and duplicate checks. Match reservations, physical balances by lot and status, pending picks, actual dispatches, deliveries, cancellations, and invoices. Quarantine discrepancies and prevent duplicate picks, dispatches, and invoices. Revalidate stock, order demand, and authorization before releasing backlog work. Within each eligible queue, prioritize verified clinical need and promised time, then age. Publish reason codes, log overrides, and use age thresholds and protected capacity for eligible deferred orders. A failed cutover check returns that scope to verified manual processing or hold.
6. **Close service recovery and compliance recovery separately.** Service recovery requires sustained operation of the restored controls in the affected scope and backlog and service levels within agreed limits. At discovery, route missing evidence and suspected breaches to a Quality-owned queue with an owner, risk tier, deadline, and disposition, even for an already invoiced shipment. For late entry, attach the original record and retain both the actual and posting times. Where evidence cannot establish that a mandatory step occurred, record the unresolved gap or confirmed breach and assess customer contact, containment, recall, or other escalation as appropriate. Quality closes compliance recovery after each high-risk exception has a documented disposition and remaining work is resolved or formally transferred to corrective action with an owner and deadline. Track the transferred work through closure.

# 5. Resilience indicators

| Area | Leading indicators and intervention | Lagging indicators and definitions |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughput and service protection** | Compare incoming and eligible ready-to-release orders with staffed release and courier capacity by shift. Count clinical deadlines at risk and escalate those with no verified fulfillment route. | Report unique shipped orders or lines per shift alongside the count meeting the release controls. Measure on-time fill as units delivered by the agreed deadline divided by units ordered in the due cohort. Report p50 and p90 cycle times by segment, together with open-order age and cancellations. |
| **Traceability and control completeness** | Monitor offline data age and unreconciled movements. Measure the share of shipments presented for release with complete authorization, lot linkage, release evidence, and all applicable pre-dispatch checks. Any missing mandatory control blocks the affected release. Monitor overdue delivery proof and missing in-transit readings. | Measure traceability exceptions among dispatched orders, successful lot-to-recipient trace tests, and missing temperature and dual-control records among orders to which each control applies. Show completeness at release and after reconciliation separately, with late verifiable entry and confirmed omissions counted separately. |
| **Backlog and hidden workload** | Track queue size, oldest age, p90 age, and counts above the age limit by segment, urgency, and product risk. Measure age from original receipt for deferred orders and from discovery for remediation items. Watch inflow versus staffed resolution capacity across both queues. | Measure time to bring each queue below its agreed threshold, staff hours for reconciliation and audit repair, and time from invoice close to signed compliance disposition. Report unresolved cases alongside completed durations. |
| **Rework and data integrity** | Monitor unmatched reservations, duplicate IDs, inventory discrepancies, and mismatches in restart tests. Stop the affected restart wave for discrepancies that could cause an unsafe or duplicate shipment. | Measure the share of orders requiring correction, repack, inventory adjustment, repeat verification, or evidence repair, counting each order once in the overall rework rate. Track staff hours and confirmed duplicate shipments or invoices separately. |
| **Fairness across customer groups** | Track the shares of eligible orders deferred past their review deadline and receiving priority overrides. Break these out by customer group within comparable urgency and product-risk tiers. Investigate persistent gaps and review documented reasons. | Compare on-time fill, p90 delay, and cancellations across hospital, pharmacy, and bulk groups and within comparable tiers. Use all accepted orders for cohort reporting, and retain counts of deferred, open, and canceled orders so that shipment-only measures cannot hide displaced demand. |

Use a shift-level dashboard during disruption and continue reporting reconciliation and remediation through compliance closure. Set warning thresholds before an incident using service commitments, baseline distributions, and tested manual capacity. Review conformance against both the normal process and the approved degraded process, including the mandatory controls of each.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Put Quality-owned release rules and a stop/hold mechanism into the outage playbook. Replace history-based authorization and unrecorded handoff with approved manual equivalents. Train backup reviewers and prearrange verified couriers. | Medium | Protects eligibility, custody, and high-risk release during the first hours of an outage. | Verified approvals or couriers may be scarce. Safe holds can delay urgent orders. |
| **2** | Prepare and exercise a secure offline evidence kit and a single stock movement ledger. Maintain trusted authorization and recall data, serialized labels and forms, and independently usable temperature loggers. | Medium to high | Preserves lot lineage, reduces double allocations, and reduces dependence on evidence reconstruction. | Offline sources can age and records can contain entry errors, especially as outages lengthen. |
| **3** | Adopt explicit allocation and deferral rules with clinical escalation, promised reassessment times, and group-level service monitoring. Give staff a controlled route for partial fulfillment or rerouting. | Low to medium | Preserves scarce capacity for urgent orders while reducing unchecked delay and cancellation among deferred customers. | A severe shortage can still force uneven service, and urgency information can be incomplete. |
| **4** | Build and rehearse phased cutover, duplicate-safe replay, reconciliation, and separately staffed audit closeout. Exercise a scenario with restored intake, stale inventory, intermittent scanning, and missing delivery proof. | High | Avoids secondary stock, shipping, and invoicing errors and exposes the staff time required for compliance recovery. | Some original evidence may be irrecoverable, leaving cases that require formal disposition or containment. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Accept temporarily slower service for deferrable orders, the cost of manual verification, and delayed electronic posting of evidence captured at the time. Protect hospital supply through priority access to staff, stock, and transport while requiring verified authorization, release, custody, and all applicable cold-chain and dual-control checks. Rule out shipment based on customer familiarity, unrecorded handoffs, and using post-close paperwork to clear an unperformed control. Expand manual fulfillment if drills and incident records demonstrate reliable controls at the proposed workload, timely lot-to-recipient tracing, and manageable delays across customer groups. Narrow its scope or use verified alternate fulfillment if evidence gaps, inconsistent stock, or persistent delays in lower-priority groups undermine those conditions.
