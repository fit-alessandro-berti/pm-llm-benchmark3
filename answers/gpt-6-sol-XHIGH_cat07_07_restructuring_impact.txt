# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic

- **Handover pattern:** Standard routing replaces ambiguous referrals; repeat local-specialist loops and ad hoc cross-team cover fall. Central request/return edges remain visible, but case-level transfers and reassignments fall after adjustment for case mix.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** The technical pool balances East's previous overload; reserved skill capacity and clear priority rules hold technical, legal/risk, and approval queue tails steady or lower. Utilization increases without a persistent backlog.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** East's median and p90 improve; North and South remain stable or improve. High-complexity p90 and SLA misses also improve, rather than being concealed by simple cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Standard decisions and better specialist matching reduce rework or at least preserve outcomes, including for cases needing local knowledge.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Every case retains a named local owner, each queue has a responsible assignee and escalation path, and approval reversals and reassignment_count decrease.
- **Fairness implications:** East closes part of its baseline gap without worsening North/South or customer segments after case-mix adjustment; accommodation needs are routed correctly.
- **Early warning signs to watch even here:** Growing queue-age p90, more repeat referrals, or improvement confined to East or simple cases would weaken this scenario.

## Central

- **Handover pattern:** Named local technical handovers and cross-team cover decline, but formal requests/returns through Technical Shared Service and shared approval offset them. Total touch count is flat or slightly higher.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** East's technical wait improves while North/South lose quick local access. Technical and approval median waits track management's higher estimates; legal/risk and difficult quality checks have intermittent tail spikes.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Overall median stays similar or improves slightly through standard cases; p90 is flat or worse for high-complexity, legal/risk, and North/South cases. East's p90 may improve from its 22.8-day baseline.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Routine decisions become more consistent, but complex regional cases show more referrals or rework; the aggregate rate may conceal that mix.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Formal roles are clearer, but ownership between local caseworker, shared specialist, and shared approver requires active case-owner rules; some approval loops grow.
- **Fairness implications:** East may gain while North/South or customers needing regional/language accommodation lose. Report adjusted gaps, not only the organization-wide mean.
- **Early warning signs:** Oldest-open-case age, approval ping-pong, rising handover_reason = clarification, legal/risk backlog, and localized SLA or rework deterioration.

## Adverse

- **Handover pattern:** Formal referrals and reassignments proliferate as local staff lose informal technical support; returns for missing context, repeated reviews, and approval reversals create more touches despite fewer ad hoc cross-team handovers.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Technical triage, difficult quality checks, legal/risk, and approvals contend for shared capacity. High utilization turns arrival variability or absence into persistent backlog and long waits, particularly for lower-priority cases.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Simple cases keep the average stable or lower, but high-complexity and scarce-specialty p90/p95, old-case age, and SLA misses rise sharply. East's benefit may vanish if the shared queue becomes saturated.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Lost local context and poorer notes cause incorrect referrals, repeated assessments, failed quality checks, and worse outcomes; local technical learning declines.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Cases sit unowned between queues, are reassigned repeatedly, or receive conflicting specialist and approval decisions.
- **Fairness implications:** Some teams, locations, customer segments, and accommodation cases bear disproportionate waits and rework, even when raw aggregate outcomes look good.
- **Early warning signs:** Backlog grows for consecutive weeks, oldest cases age faster than completions, high-complexity p90 rises, legal/risk utilization stays near saturation, and ownership or exception escalations rise.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Use the intake team's identity as **origin team**, since the event's `team` may change as a case moves. Review all indicators by origin team, complexity, specialty, priority, location, and customer segment, including meaningful intersections; standardize comparisons to a common case mix.

|---|---|---|
| Leading: days/weeks | Requests, returns, and total distinct-resource handovers per case; weighted handover-of-work edges; `handover_reason` | Tests the nominal reduction and detects duplicated or clarification handoffs |
| Leading: days/weeks | Queue arrivals, completions, work in progress, age of open work, p50/p90/p95 assignment wait by queue and specialty | Detects bottlenecks before completed-case cycle times react; add queue-entry/assignment events if absent |
| Leading: days/weeks | Specialist utilization and staffed capacity by skill, plus absence-day throughput | Tests whether pooling preserves reserve capacity; utilization requires roster/work-time data, not event counts alone |

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency:** A larger pool may speed ordinary cases while a few shared-queue cases wait weeks. Track p90/p95, oldest-open-case age, and censored open cases.
2. **High-complexity harm:** Mandatory technical routing may delay hard cases while automation speeds simple cases. Compare complexity-specific distributions and SLA misses.
3. **Local knowledge loss:** Removing quick regional advice can raise inappropriate referrals or rework later, even when the first review is faster. Track local_expertise_flag, referral reasons, and subsequent quality outcomes.
4. **Fairness gaps:** An overall gain can coexist with worse North/South service, language or regional accommodation, or a particular customer segment. Compare adjusted outcomes and intersectional groups; small groups need uncertainty intervals.
5. **Learning decay:** Local caseworkers may lose technical judgment gradually, so early cycle times can look good. Follow local participation, triage accuracy, and skill assessments over months.
6. **Accountability gaps:** Shared queues can leave cases without a clear owner or produce conflicting decisions without immediately changing completed-case averages. Measure unowned time, reversals, reassignment_count, and escalation reasons.
7. **Exception handling:** Unusual legal/risk/security and difficult quality cases can be stranded by standard routing, especially when notes are incomplete. Track exceptions, manual bypasses, repeated reviews, and their tails.
8. **Resilience loss:** Higher utilization can eliminate the slack that handles absences or surges. Compare backlog recovery time, service under absence, and concentration of work in a few specialists.
9. **Selection and censoring:** A post-change average over only closed cases excludes the still-open backlog; changes in priority or case mix can also make the average misleading. Use intake cohorts, common follow-up, and case-mix adjustment.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Scope and comparison:** Pilot a dedicated shared technical lane for a stratified subset of eligible cases from *each* team, including East, plus the proposed shared approval and specialist/QC routing for that subset. Keep a contemporaneous local-route comparison within each team where operationally feasible, assigning eligible cases by a predeclared random or rotating rule and reserving comparable staff capacity. Ring-fence scarce legal/risk capacity or measure spillover into controls. If parallel routing is impractical, stagger team adoption and compare each team with its own pre-period and not-yet-changed teams; do not treat East alone as representative.
- **Windows:** Collect at least 12 weeks of pre-change data and run the pilot for about 12 weeks, followed by at least four weeks to mature open cases. Include weekday and seasonal demand patterns; report intake cohorts and censored open cases. Size the pilot to estimate p90 and segment effects credibly before interpreting small-group gaps.
- **Segmentation:** Predefine team, location, case_complexity, required_specialty (including legal, risk, security), priority, and customer_segment, with intersections for high-risk or accommodation groups. Adjust for changes in case mix and compare East's benefit with North/South's possible losses.
- **Guardrails:** Monitor daily queue age, backlog, unowned cases, and absence coverage; weekly compare p90/p95 cycle and queue waits, SLA misses, rework, outcomes, and adjusted team/segment gaps. Use baselines (North/South/East p90 of 16.4/18.2/22.8 days; SLA miss rates 6.5%/7.2%/9.8%) and matched controls, not a single pooled target. Pre-register practical triggers, for example sustained p90 deterioration above 10%, SLA misses or rework above control by 2 percentage points, or a persistent queue-age rise; require sufficient sample or repeated evidence for noisy small groups.
- **Staff feedback:** Weekly short interviews and case audits with local caseworkers, specialists, supervisors, and quality staff; record missing context, ownership confusion, skill-development opportunities, and accommodation failures, then link these to case IDs and handover_reason.
- **Adjustment/rollback:** Immediately pause routing for unowned or materially misrouted high-risk cases. If backlog grows for two consecutive weeks, legal/risk or approval tails breach guardrails, or a team/segment repeatedly deteriorates, add capacity, change triage/priority rules, restore local consultation or approval authority, and re-evaluate. Roll back the affected lane if these corrections fail; expand only after the full mature-cohort and fairness review passes.

# 6. Recommendation

**Pilot first, with modifications:** preserve a named local case owner and a channel for early local technical consultation; publish queue ownership, escalation, and priority rules; add capacity or protected slots before increasing referrals to Legal/Risk. East's overloaded embedded specialist gives pooling a plausible benefit, while North/South risk losing effective local knowledge. The listed waits, increased formal requests, and already high Legal/Risk utilization make a full rollout unjustified without evidence.

Expand only if the pilot shows a genuine reduction in case-level friction or a justified quality gain, controlled queue tails, no material deterioration in p90/p95 or SLA performance for complex and scarce-specialty cases, stable or improved rework/outcomes, clear ownership, preserved absence coverage and local learning, and no unacceptable adjusted team or customer-segment gap. Management should **not** infer success from early mean cycle time, a lower cross-team handover count, or higher utilization alone: these can coexist with open-case backlog, displaced waiting, weaker quality, and unfair tail delays.

        {False: "before", True: "after"}
    )
    return df
