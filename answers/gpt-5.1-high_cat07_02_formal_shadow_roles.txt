# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

**Front-office generalist service agents (R01, R02)**  
Core activities are the high-volume A1/A2/A4/A12 tasks plus low/medium-complexity A3 diagnose work, which together form the standard “intake → entitlement → simple resolution → close” path in fragments 1 and 6. Peripheral activities (A5, A7, A8, A10, A11, A13, A14) occur less frequently and usually when a case crosses boundaries: minor configurations for simple products, supporting negotiations initiated by commercial staff, or reworking reopened cases after temp-agent errors. Case-complexity metrics show that Amira routinely takes on more medium/high and some critical cases, indicating that her peripheral activities are linked to higher-importance cases and tacit authority, even though her activity profile is similar to Ben’s.

**Temporary low-complexity front-office assistants (R11, R12)**  
For Kai and Lina, the same A1/A2/A4/A12 pattern is core but tightly restricted to low-complexity cases, with almost no A3+ specialist or escalation work and no approvals. Their peripheral A3 diagnose events are rare and low-risk; they serve more as clarifications than deep troubleshooting. The substitution episode in weeks 8–10, where their closures lead to 17 later reopens due to weak entitlement notes, shows that even high-frequency core tasks remain constrained in authority and complexity, and that they rely on R01/R02/R13 for rework.

**Technical resolution analysts (R03, R04)**  
Chen and Dina’s core consists of A3 diagnose, A5 configure, and A10 implement, predominantly on medium/high and critical cases as shown in complexity statistics and fragments 2 and 3. Their peripheral activities—A1/A2/A4 for special cases, A11 quality checks, A14 updates, and occasional A6 for Chen—arise when complex incidents require end-to-end ownership or when they shadow security and QA. Chen’s higher share of critical cases and A6 suggests a more senior or specialized profile within the same technical role, but both analysts fundamentally provide deep technical resolution rather than routine front-office work.

**Security risk specialist (R05)**

The week 7–8 incident spike, where Mateo filters low-risk referrals so Erik focuses on the genuinely critical ones (though two critical cases are delayed), underscores that his role is defined more by risk level and decision authority than by volume, with peripheral activities orbiting the security decision.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**R07_Giorgio – Shadow commercial reviewer and hybrid front-office/pricing role**

This shadow role is beneficial because it increases responsiveness and resilience when commercial demand spikes or Fatima is absent, but it carries risks of inconsistent pricing decisions, blurred accountability, and potential audit issues if his authority is not clearly defined in policy.

**R13_Mateo – Cross-functional escalation broker**  
Mateo’s activity profile and complexity mix show that he is a structural broker across technical, commercial, security, and supervisory roles: he appears in fragments 3, 5, and 6 between specialists and final approvals. Temporary substitution episodes (preliminary A9 approvals when Hana is at an audit workshop, security referral filtering under high incident load) illustrate that he not only routes cases but also exercises bounded decision-making authority. As a shadow or bridging role, he helps absorb volatility and smooth handovers of work, but he can become a hidden single point of coordination whose overload or absence severely impacts flow time and the visibility of who “owns” escalated incidents.

**R05_Erik – Rare but critical specialist**  
Erik is a classic rare specialist: he handles relatively few cases overall but a disproportionate share of high and critical ones, especially where A6 Security Review is present. He also performs some A3/A5/A10/A11, indicating that he sometimes carries cases through diagnosis and implementation when security dominates the root cause. His shadow nature comes from the fact that most role-mining algorithms will see him as either an outlier or a small technical cluster, while in reality he defines a crucial risk-control function whose absence (as seen when Mateo filters low-risk referrals) can delay critical cases and create security exposure. The benefit is concentrated expertise and clear accountability for security; the risk is over-reliance on a single individual and difficulty in scaling or substituting his competence.

**Automation boundary role – R14_Nora as digital front-office**  
Nora acts as a boundary role between automation and human front-office agents: she performs A1 and A2 at scale, handing off only exceptions or passed prechecks to humans. From a process perspective, she is a shadow participant because organizational charts may not count her as “staff,” yet event logs attribute substantial workload to her resource ID. The benefit is high-volume, low-complexity work removal from human queues; the risk is that mining results could underestimate human intake effort or mislead staffing decisions if analysts forget that much of the “front-office” role is automated.

**Quality and knowledge shadow role – R09_Ivan and R10_Julia**  
Ivan and Julia form a shadow knowledge-engineering role in addition to formal QA: they not only perform A11 Quality Check but also A14 KB Update in high volumes, effectively curating the knowledge base that front-office and technical staff rely on. Their authority is not primarily in approvals of individual cases but in defining what “good” looks like and encoding reusable solutions, which standard role-discovery might collapse into a generic back-office cluster. This shadow gatekeeping is beneficial for consistency and learning but risky if their bandwidth is constrained or if they over-centralize knowledge changes without enough frontline feedback.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# 1) Load and format the dataframe
df = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution_log.csv")

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

# 2) Discover organizational roles (activity-based clustering of resources)
roles_result = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name"
)

# roles_result is typically a list of clusters; build a DataFrame mapping resources to role IDs
role_records = []
for role_id, cluster in enumerate(roles_result):
    for res in cluster:
        role_records.append({"org:resource": res, "role_id": role_id})

roles_df = pd.DataFrame(role_records)

# 3) Discover activity-based resource similarity (e.g., cosine over activity profiles)
similarity_matrix = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name"
)

# similarity_matrix can be inspected to find pairs like (R03, R04) or (R06, R07)

# 4) Handover-of-work and working-together networks
handover_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    log,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource"
)

working_together_net = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource"
)

subcontracting_net = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    log,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource"
)

# Use these networks to identify brokers like R13 (high betweenness) or shadow roles

# 5) Join role-discovery output with case_complexity and temporary_substitution
case_cols = ["case:concept:name", "case_complexity", "customer_segment",
             "product_line", "escalation_level"]
case_df = df[case_cols].drop_duplicates()

events_with_roles = df.merge(roles_df, on="org:resource", how="left")

# Aggregate complexity and substitution indicators per role
def share_true(s):
    return (s == True).mean()

role_case_stats = (
    events_with_roles
    .merge(case_df, on="case:concept:name", how="left")
    .groupby("role_id")
    .agg(
        median_case_complexity=("case_complexity", "median"),
        pct_critical=("escalation_level",
                      lambda s: (s == "critical").mean()),
        substitution_events=("temporary_substitution",
                             lambda s: (s == True).sum()),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

# 6) Guardrails against over-interpreting activity-frequency clusters

# Example: inspect, not assume, that each role_id is a formal role
role_activity_mix = (
    events_with_roles
    .groupby(["role_id", "concept:name"])
    .size()
    .reset_index(name="count")
)

# Combine with role_case_stats and manual labels for interpretation
roles_summary = (
    role_activity_mix
    .merge(role_case_stats, on="role_id", how="left")
)

# At this point, an analyst should:
# - Compare roles_summary to organizational charts and job titles
# - Check whether clusters are dominated by temporary_substitution periods
# - Verify that high-activity clusters are not automatically labeled as "senior"
#   or "authoritative" without looking at escalation_level and approvals
```

In practice, you might also:
- Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to segment the log by product_line or customer_segment before role discovery (e.g., separate Enterprise Security from standard products).  
- Use `pm4py.get_service_time` or `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to analyze how different roles impact lead times and see whether shadow roles (e.g., Giorgio or Mateo) reduce or increase waiting time during substitutions.  
- Run `pm4py.get_variants` to compare typical paths for different roles (e.g., front-office only vs. front-office plus pricing) before renaming clusters into interpretable roles.


# 6. Operational interpretation

From a management perspective, the discovered structure suggests a layered organization: high-volume front-office generalists and temps supported by specialized technical, security, commercial, quality, and coordination roles, plus an automation boundary at registration/entitlement. Staffing decisions should ensure robust coverage for specialist bottlenecks (pricing, security, supervision, coordination, QA/KB) and plan explicit back-up capacity where today shadow roles (Giorgio, Mateo, Chen) absorb volatility. Training plans should focus on strengthening temp agents’ entitlement documentation to reduce reopens, formalizing cross-training and decision thresholds for hybrid/shadow roles, and leveraging QA/KB roles to turn complex incidents into better standard responses and automation opportunities.  
For risk control and resilience, management should explicitly recognize rare specialists and brokers, define substitution rules for high-risk functions, and monitor handover networks so that critical paths do not depend excessively on single individuals. At the same time, they should be cautious: mined roles reflect observed behavior over a limited period, not formal RACI or off-system work, and temporary spikes (e.g., absences or incidents) can distort clusters. As a result, organizational changes should not be based solely on activity-frequency clusters or similarity matrices; they must be validated with supervisors and staff, cross-checked against case-complexity and escalation patterns, and stress-tested for compliance, auditability, and long-term workload trends.
