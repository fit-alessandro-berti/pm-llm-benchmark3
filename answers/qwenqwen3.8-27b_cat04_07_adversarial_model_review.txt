```
def stratified_case_split(log, case_key='case:conceptual', strata=None, test_frac=0.2, seed=42):
    df = pm4py.to_dataframe(log)
    case_df = df.drop_duplicates(case_key)
    if strata:
        for col in strata:
            if col not in case_df.columns:
                case_df[col] = 'missing'
    else:
        strata = []
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    test_cases = []
    group_cols = [case_key] + strata
    for _, group in case_df.groupby(strata, dropna=False):
        cases = group[case_key].values
        rng.shuffle(cases)
        n_test = max(1, int(len(cases) * test_frac)) if len(cases) > 1 else 0
        test_cases.extend(cases[:n_test])
    test_cases = set(test_cases)
    train_df = df[~df[case_key].isin(test_cases)]
    test_df = df[df[case_key].isin(test_cases)]
    return pm4py.from_dataframe(train_df), pm4py.from_dataframe(test_df)
```

```
def perturb_missing_or_duplicate(log, case_key='case:conceptual', activity_key='conceptual:activity', time_key='time:timestamp', drop_prob=0.1, duplicate_prob=0.1, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    df = pm4py.to_dataframe(log)
    rows = []
    for case_id, group in df.groupby(case_key):
        group = group.sort_values(time_key)
        for _, row in group.iterrows():
            r = row.to_dict()
            if rng.random() >= drop_prob:
                rows.append(r)
            if rng.random() < duplicate_prob:
                dup = r.copy()
                dup[time_key] = pd.Timestamp(dup[time_key]) + pd.Timedelta(milliseconds=1)
                rows.append(dup)
    return pm4py.from_dataframe(pd.DataFrame(rows))
```

# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

This review treats the reported metrics as evidence about two submitted models, not as proof of business correctness. The central question is not “which model has the better aggregate score?” but “which model is fit for which use, and what can we safely infer from the metrics?”

I distinguish four primary use cases:

1. **Exploration / discovery**  
   Purpose: help analysts understand what happened, find variants, and generate hypotheses.  
   Acceptable trade-off: higher fitness and generalization are valuable; false positives are tolerable if the model is clearly labeled as non-enforcing. Precision is less critical, but the model must not be mistaken for policy.

2. **Conformance enforcement / compliance**  
   Purpose: decide whether a case is compliant, flag exceptions, or block release.  
   Acceptable trade-off: precision and policy fidelity dominate. A model that admits forbidden behavior is unsafe even if fitness is high. A curated negative suite, soundness checks, and explicit handling of missing attributes are required.

3. **Simulation / what-if analysis**  
   Purpose: generate synthetic behavior or estimate process performance.  
   Acceptable trade-off: the model must be sound, reachable, and parameterizable. High fitness alone is insufficient if the model admits deadlocks, impossible approval states, or policy-ambiguous loops.

4. **Stakeholder communication / governance**  
   Purpose: make the model understandable to process owners, compliance, operations, and IT.  
   Acceptable trade-off: simplicity and comprehension matter, but not at the cost of hiding mandatory constraints. A model that is easy to read but semantically wrong is worse than a slightly more complex model with explicit uncertainty.

Key assumptions for this review:

- The activity labels in the models and probes are the only reliable control-flow evidence.
- The `external/internal` attribute and the `affected_check` attribute are missing from the discovery log.
- Policy items P1–P7 are the stakeholder-agreed target semantics, but some details remain unresolved.
- PM4Py alignment metrics are assumed to be computed over the same log, but random seeds, preprocessing, alignment costs, and exact held-out cases are not supplied.
- Process-tree semantics are interpreted strictly: `X` is exclusive choice, `+` is parallel composition, `->` is sequence, and `tau` is a skip/silent option.
- The behavior probes B1–B8 are policy test cases, not an exhaustive ground truth.
- For the third alternative, I assume Supplier Review is a pre-approval review that normally occurs after the two mandatory checks. This assumption is explicit and must be validated; if Supplier Review can legitimately occur before mandatory checks, the third model should be adjusted rather than silently made permissive.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

## Strongest case for Model S

Model S is a compact, easy-to-read model. Its high training and holdout fitness, high token generalization, high simplicity, low runtime, and high stakeholder comprehension make it attractive for exploratory analysis. If the goal is to create a lightweight “what did the log mostly look like?” model, S has several advantages:

- It is robust to missing or noisy events because many activities are optional.
- It is fast to align.
- It is easy for non-technical stakeholders to inspect.
- It avoids the combinatorial explosion of Model C.
- It can serve as a discovery scaffold for later refinement.

The advocate’s argument F7 is reasonable in an exploratory context: low precision may be acceptable if the model is explicitly labeled as non-enforcing.

## Strongest case against Model S

For compliance, Model S is unsafe. It does not encode the core policy. It makes mandatory checks optional, allows Expedite Review before mandatory checks, does not condition Supplier Review on external sourcing, and does not represent Correct Dossier or correction-driven rechecks.

The high fitness and generalization are therefore not evidence of quality; they are evidence of permissiveness.

## Concrete undesirable or policy-ambiguous behaviors admitted by Model S

| # | Admitted behavior | Trace | Policy issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Skips both mandatory checks | `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Violates P2. This is probe B3 and is policy-forbidden. |
| 2 | Skips Technical Review | `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Violates P2. Technical Review is mandatory before approval. |
| 3 | Skips Credit Check | `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Violates P2. Credit Check is mandatory before approval. |
| 4 | Places Expedite Review before mandatory checks | `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Violates P4. This is probe B4 and is policy-forbidden. |
| 5 | Allows Expedite Review without any mandatory checks | `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Violates P2 and P4. Expedite Review must follow both mandatory checks. |
| 6 | Allows Supplier Review without external/internal condition | `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` | Policy-ambiguous without the external attribute. If the order is internal, Supplier Review may be forbidden; if external, it may be required. The model cannot distinguish these cases. |
| 7 | Allows an external order to omit Supplier Review | `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order` with `external = True` | Violates P3 if the order is externally sourced. The model cannot enforce this because the attribute is missing. |

## Intentional noise tolerance versus accidental semantic flaws

Some permissiveness may be intentional: optional branches can tolerate missing events and support exploration. However, the following are not reasonable noise tolerance; they are semantic flaws:

- Making Credit Check and Technical Review optional violates P2.
- Allowing Expedite Review before mandatory checks violates P4.
- Not representing Correct Dossier at all violates P6 and P7.
- Not conditioning Supplier Review on external sourcing leaves P3 unenforced.

Model S is therefore not merely a noisy exploratory model; it is a model with the wrong hard constraints.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

## Strongest case for Model C

Model C is much closer to a conservative audit whitelist. It enforces Register Dossier first and Release Order last. It requires both mandatory checks before approval in every branch. It rejects B3, B4, and B8. It represents one correction path. Its high training precision and low permissiveness make it attractive for a high-risk setting where unseen behavior should be treated as noncompliant until approved.

The advocate’s argument F8 is defensible if the governance objective is “unknown means blocked.”

## Strongest case against Model C

Model C is brittle, overfit to observed variants, and difficult to maintain. It enumerates specific sequences instead of expressing policy. This creates several problems:

### Branch enumeration

Model C lists eight explicit sequences. It does not express the policy as constraints. As a result, it misses many valid combinations:

- Alternate check order with Auto Approval: B2 is rejected.
- Alternate check order with Supplier Review and Expedite Review: B5 is rejected.
- Supplier Review with Auto Approval: rejected.
- Expedite Review with Auto Approval: rejected.
- Supplier Review with Expedite Review and Auto Approval: rejected.
- Correction followed by only Technical Review: B6 is rejected.
- Correction followed by only Credit Check: rejected.
- Multiple corrections: rejected.

The model is not generalizing; it is memorizing a small set of frequent variants.

### Duplicate labels

The reported duplicate-label count is 33, but the definition is undocumented. Regardless of the exact count, Model C repeats the same activity labels across many branches. This creates alignment ambiguity, complicates impact analysis, and makes ownership harder for IT architecture.

### Unseen combinations

The low holdout fitness and low token generalization are consistent with a model that cannot combine known elements in new ways. F4 explicitly notes that holdout cases combine Supplier Review, Auto Approval, and alternate check order in ways absent from training. Model C cannot handle these without adding new branches.

### Correction handling

Model C includes only one correction branch:

`Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Correct Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`

This does not represent:

- Correction affecting Credit Check only.
- Correction affecting Technical Review only.
- Multiple corrections.
- Correction followed by Supplier Review or Expedite Review.
- Correction followed by Auto Approval.

F6 is therefore not satisfied.

### Holdout degradation

The drop from training fitness 0.973 to holdout fitness 0.744 is severe. It suggests that the model does not capture the underlying policy structure. It captures surface variants.

### Maintainability

Every new valid combination requires a new branch. The number of branches grows combinatorially with:

- check order,
- supplier presence,
- expedite presence,
- approval type,
- correction presence,
- affected checks.

This is not a sustainable governance model.

### High precision may be an artifact

C’s high training precision is likely an artifact of positive-log-only evaluation. Because the model admits only a small set of observed-like traces, precision looks high. But precision against observed positives does not prove that the model correctly rejects forbidden behavior or accepts valid unseen behavior.

# 7. Third Alternative

I propose a **procedural Petri-net backbone with explicit correction loop and external attribute checks**. This is safer than Model S because it enforces mandatory checks, expedite ordering, exactly-one approval, and correction-before-repeat. It is more generalizable and maintainable than Model C because it expresses policy as structure rather than enumerating variants.

## Compact formal description

The third model has the following hard semantics:

1. **Register Dossier is first.**
2. **Release Order is last.**
3. After Register Dossier, both **Credit Check** and **Technical Review** must complete before any approval, Supplier Review, or Expedite Review.
4. **Supplier Review** is optional in the core net, but an external check enforces:
   - required if `external = True`,
   - forbidden if `external = False`,
   - uncertain if the attribute is missing.
5. **Expedite Review** is optional, must occur after both mandatory checks, must occur before approval, and may occur before or after Supplier Review.
6. Exactly one of **Manager Approval** and **Auto Approval** occurs before Release Order.
7. **Correct Dossier** may occur zero or more times after the initial mandatory checks and before approval.
8. Each Correct Dossier must be followed by at least one of Credit Check or Technical Review before returning to the pre-approval state. If the `affected_check` attribute exists, only the affected checks should be repeated; if it is missing, the model accepts any nonempty subset and flags the case as uncertain.
9. A repeated mandatory check without a preceding Correct Dossier is forbidden by the net.

## PM4Py code

The following code assigns the main third model to `model_third` as a Petri-net triple `(net, im, fm)`.

```python
import pm4py

def build_model_third():
    try:
        from pm4py.PetriNet import PetriNet
    except Exception:
        try:
            PetriNet = pm4py.PetriNet.PetriNet
        except Exception:
            PetriNet = pm4py.PetriNet

    net = PetriNet()

    places = [
        "p_start",
        "p_registered",
        "p_cc_pending",
        "p_tr_pending",
        "p_cc_done",
        "p_tr_done",
        "p_both_done",
        "p_corrected",
        "p_recheck_credit",
        "p_recheck_technical",
        "p_supplier_done",
        "p_expedite_done",
        "p_approved",
        "p_end",
    ]

    for p in places:
        net.add_place(p)

    def add_transition(name, label):
        try:
            net.add_transition(name, label)
        except Exception:
            net.add_transition(name)
            try:
                net.transitions[name].label = label
            except Exception:
                pass

    transitions = {
        "t_register": "Register Dossier",
        "t_split": None,
        "t_credit": "Credit Check",
        "t_technical": "Technical Review",
        "t_sync_mandatory": None,
        "t_correct": "Correct Dossier",
        "t_credit_recheck": "Credit Check",
        "t_technical_recheck": "Technical Review",
        "t_technical_after_credit": "Technical Review",
        "t_credit_after_technical": "Credit Check",
        "t_exit_credit": None,
        "t_exit_technical": None,
        "t_supplier": "Supplier Review",
        "t_supplier_exit": None,
        "t_expedite": "Expedite Review",
        "t_expedite_exit": None,
        "t_manager": "Manager Approval",
        "t_auto": "Auto Approval",
        "t_release": "Release Order",
    }

    for name, label in transitions.items():
        add_transition(name, label)

    arcs = [
        # Register and split into mandatory checks
        ("p_start", "t_register"),
        ("t_register", "p_registered"),
        ("p_registered", "t_split"),
        ("t_split", "p_cc_pending"),
        ("t_split", "p_tr_pending"),

        # Mandatory checks in either order
        ("p_cc_pending", "t_credit"),
        ("t_credit", "p_cc_done"),
        ("p_tr_pending", "t_technical"),
        ("t_technical", "p_tr_done"),

        # Synchronize after both mandatory checks
        ("p_cc_done", "t_sync_mandatory"),
        ("p_tr_done", "t_sync_mandatory"),
        ("t_sync_mandatory", "p_both_done"),

        # Correction loop
        ("p_both_done", "t_correct"),
        ("t_correct", "p_corrected"),

        # Recheck credit only
        ("p_corrected", "t_credit_recheck"),
        ("t_credit_recheck", "p_recheck_credit"),
        ("p_recheck_credit", "t_exit_credit"),
        ("t_exit_credit", "p_both_done"),

        # Recheck credit then technical
        ("p_recheck_credit", "t_technical_after_credit"),
        ("t_technical_after_credit", "p_both_done"),

        # Recheck technical only
        ("p_corrected", "t_technical_recheck"),
        ("t_technical_recheck", "p_recheck_technical"),
        ("p_recheck_technical", "t_exit_technical"),
        ("t_exit_technical", "p_both_done"),

        # Recheck technical then credit
        ("p_recheck_technical", "t_credit_after_technical"),
        ("t_credit_after_technical", "p_both_done"),

        # Optional Supplier Review, at most once in core net
        ("p_both_done", "t_supplier"),
        ("t_supplier", "p_supplier_done"),
        ("p_supplier_done", "t_supplier_exit"),
        ("t_supplier_exit", "p_both_done"),

        # Optional Expedite Review, at most once in core net
        ("p_both_done", "t_expedite"),
        ("t_expedite", "p_expedite_done"),
        ("p_expedite_done", "t_expedite_exit"),
        ("t_expedite_exit", "p_both_done"),

        # Exactly one approval
        ("p_both_done", "t_manager"),
        ("t_manager", "p_approved"),
        ("p_both_done", "t_auto"),
        ("t_auto", "p_approved"),

        # Release last
        ("p_approved", "t_release"),
        ("t_release", "p_end"),
    ]

    for src, dst in arcs:
        net.add_arc(src, dst)

    im = {"p_start": 1}
    fm = {"p_end": 1}

    return (net, im, fm)

model_third = build_model_third()
```

```python
def external_policy_checks(activities, case_attributes):
    issues = []
    acts = list(activities)

    external = case_attributes.get("external")
    if external is True and "Supplier Review" not in acts:
        issues.append("missing_supplier_for_external_order")
    if external is False and "Supplier Review" in acts:
        issues.append("supplier_present_for_internal_order")
    if external is None:
        issues.append("external_attribute_missing")

    i = 0
    while i < len(acts):
        if acts[i] == "Correct Dossier":
            j = i + 1
            rechecked = set()
            while j < len(acts) and acts[j] not in {
                "Correct Dossier",
                "Manager Approval",
                "Auto Approval",
            }:
                if acts[j] in {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}:
                    rechecked.add(acts[j])
                j += 1

            affected = case_attributes.get("affected_check")
            if affected is None:
                if not rechecked:
                    issues.append("correction_without_mandatory_recheck")
                else:
                    issues.append("affected_check_missing")
            else:
                required = set(affected.split(","))
                if not required.issubset(rechecked):
                    issues.append("missing_required_recheck_after_correction")
                if rechecked - required:
                    issues.append("unrequired_recheck_after_correction")

            i = j
        else:
            i += 1

    return issues
```

## Explicit semantics

- **Mandatory checks:** Credit Check and Technical Review are both required before approval. The net enforces this through a synchronization place.
- **Optional Supplier Review:** The net makes Supplier Review optional. The external check enforces the external/internal condition.
- **Expedite Review ordering:** Expedite Review is enabled only after both mandatory checks and before approval. It may occur before or after Supplier Review.
- **Exactly-one approval:** The net has two alternative transitions from the same pre-approval place to a single approved place. This enforces exactly one approval.
- **Correction/recheck behavior:** Correct Dossier can repeat. Each correction must be followed by at least one mandatory recheck. The affected-check attribute, if available, should determine which recheck is required.

## Limitations caused by missing attributes and assumptions

- The missing `external/internal` attribute means Supplier Review cannot be fully enforced by the net. Compliance status is uncertain when the attribute is absent.
- The missing `affected_check` attribute means the net cannot enforce “only affected checks repeat.” It accepts any nonempty subset of mandatory rechecks and relies on external checks to flag uncertainty.
- The model assumes Supplier Review occurs after the mandatory checks. If business confirms that Supplier Review can legitimately occur before mandatory checks, the net should be extended rather than silently made permissive.
- The core net allows Supplier Review and Expedite Review at most once. If corrections can affect supplier or expedite status, repeated Supplier Review or Expedite Review may be needed; this is currently outside the core net.
- The net uses duplicate activity labels for initial and recheck transitions. This is semantically necessary but must be managed through transition IDs, alignment-cost sensitivity, and impact analysis.
- The net uses silent transitions for split, synchronization, and exits. Silent-transition semantics must be included in validation.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

def stratified_case_split(
    log,
    case_key="case:conceptual",
    strata=None,
    test_frac=0.2,
    seed=42,
):
    df = pm4py.to_dataframe(log)
    case_df = df.drop_duplicates(case_key)

    if strata:
        for col in strata:
            if col not in case_df.columns:
                case_df[col] = "missing"

        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        test_cases = []

        for _, group in case_df.groupby(strata, dropna=False):
            cases = group[case_key].values
            rng.shuffle(cases)
            n_test = max(1, int(len(cases) * test_frac)) if len(cases) > 1 else 0
            test_cases.extend(cases[:n_test])

        test_cases = set(test_cases)
    else:
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        cases = case_df[case_key].values
        rng.shuffle(cases)
        n_test = max(1, int(len(cases) * test_frac)) if len(cases) > 1 else 0
        test_cases = set(cases[:n_test])

    train_df = df[~df[case_key].isin(test_cases)]
    test_df = df[df[case_key].isin(test_cases)]

    return pm4py.from_dataframe(train_df), pm4py.from_dataframe(test_df)
```

## 4. Missing-event and duplicate-event perturbation tests

Test how each model behaves when events are missing or duplicated.

```python
def perturb_missing_or_duplicate(
    log,
    case_key="case:conceptual",
    activity_key="conceptual:activity",
    time_key="time:timestamp",
    drop_prob=0.1,
    duplicate_prob=0.1,
    seed=42,
):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    df = pm4py.to_dataframe(log)
    rows = []

    for case_id, group in df.groupby(case_key):
        group = group.sort_values(time_key)
        for _, row in group.iterrows():
            r = row.to_dict()

            if rng.random() >= drop_prob:
                rows.append(r)

            if rng.random() < duplicate_prob:
                dup = r.copy()
                dup[time_key] = pd.Timestamp(dup[time_key]) + pd.Timedelta(milliseconds=1)
                rows.append(dup)

    return pm4py.from_dataframe(pd.DataFrame(rows))
```

Report:

- fitness change,
- precision change,
- accepted negative fraction,
- runtime change,
- diagnostic categories.

```python
def get_net_im_fm(model):
    if isinstance(model, tuple) and len(model) == 3:

    return pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model)

def report_metrics(model, log, cost=1):
    net, im, fm = get_net_im_fm(model)

    # Use the cost parameter supported by your PM4Py version.
    return {
        "fitness": pm4py.fitness_alignments(log, net, im, fm, cost=cost),
        "precision": pm4py.precision_alignments(log, net, im, fm, cost=cost),
        "generalization": pm4py.generalization_tbr(log, net, im, fm),

def alignment_cost_sensitivity(model, log, costs=(1, 2, 5, 10)):
    return {cost: report_metrics(model, log, cost=cost) for cost in costs}
```

## 9. Stakeholder comprehension measured with a documented protocol

Use a structured protocol instead of an informal 1–5 score.

Recommended protocol:

1. Recruit 5–10 stakeholders per role: process owner, compliance, operations, IT, data analyst.
2. Give each stakeholder the same model and the same task set.
3. Tasks:
   - Identify mandatory activities.
   - Identify optional activities.
   - Identify the approval rule.
   - Identify whether a given trace is valid, forbidden, or uncertain.
   - Explain what happens after a correction.
   - Explain how Supplier Review is conditioned on external sourcing.
4. Score each task 0–5 with a documented rubric.
5. Record time-on-task and think-aloud comments.
6. Compare models using paired tasks, not global impressions.

## 10. Decision rule for rejection despite good aggregate metrics

A model should be rejected for compliance if any of the following occur, even if aggregate fitness or generalization is good:

1. It accepts any hard-forbidden probe, such as B3 or B4.
2. It accepts a curated negative trace labeled forbidden.
3. It rejects a policy-valid trace without marking it as uncertain due to missing attributes.
4. It fails soundness or reachability checks.
5. Its precision is high only on the positive training log and not supported by a negative suite.
6. Its metrics are unstable across documented alignment-cost settings.
7. It cannot explain how missing `external/internal` or `affected_check` attributes are handled.
8. It has unmanaged duplicate-label ambiguity that prevents reliable impact analysis.
9. Stakeholder comprehension fails the documented protocol for the intended use case.
10. Runtime exceeds the operational service-level agreement for conformance checking.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

## Model S

**Do not use Model S for compliance, enforcement, audit, or simulation.**

It may be used only for **exploration and discovery**, provided that it is clearly labeled as a non-enforcing, permissive discovery model. It should not be presented to stakeholders as the process policy. Its high fitness and generalization are insufficient because it admits forbidden behavior and omits correction semantics.

## Model C

**Do not use Model C as the operational compliance model.**

It may be retained temporarily as an **audit snapshot of known variants** or as a conservative whitelist for a narrowly scoped, high-risk interim control. However, it should be retired as the primary model because:

- it overrestricts valid unseen combinations,
- it is hard to maintain,
- it has duplicate-label and impact-analysis problems,
- its high precision is likely a positive-log artifact,
- it does not transparently represent correction-driven rechecks.

## Third alternative

**Adopt the third alternative as the compliance candidate**, subject to the following conditions:

1. It passes soundness and reachability checks.
2. It rejects all hard-forbidden probes and curated forbidden traces.
3. It accepts valid probes B1, B2, B5, and B7.
4. It handles B6 as policy-dependent and flags uncertainty when `affected_check` is missing.
5. It documents how missing `external/internal` attributes are treated.
6. It passes attribute-stratified holdout tests.
7. It passes missing-event and duplicate-event perturbation tests.
8. Its metrics are stable across documented alignment-cost settings.
9. It passes a documented stakeholder comprehension protocol.
10. Its runtime meets the operational service-level agreement.
11. IT architecture confirms that duplicate transition labels and silent transitions are manageable for impact analysis and change control.

If the business confirms that Supplier Review may occur before mandatory checks, or that corrections may affect only supplier/expedite status, the third model should be explicitly extended. It should not be made permissive by silently optionalizing mandatory behavior.

# 11. Dissenting View

The strongest case against my recommendation is that the third model may trade one failure mode for another. It is more complex than Model S, uses duplicate transition labels and silent transitions, and still relies on missing attributes. If Supplier Review can legitimately occur before the mandatory checks, or if a correction often affects supplier data or metadata rather than Credit or Technical Review, the net will reject valid cases and create operational friction. Model S, despite poor precision, is easy to understand and has high holdout fitness; for an exploratory dashboard it may be the only model that keeps operations engaged. Model C, although brittle, provides a conservative whitelist: in a high-risk environment, treating unseen behavior as noncompliant until approved may be preferable to a generalizable model that admits policy-ambiguous traces. The board may also worry that the third model’s apparent precision is still only as good as the curated negative suite and the assumptions about correction semantics.

I would change my decision if a documented negative suite shows the third model rejects a material fraction of policy-valid correction or supplier cases; if attribute logs prove Supplier Review is often pre-mandatory or corrections frequently require no mandatory recheck; if the stakeholder protocol shows poor comprehension; if alignment-cost/runtime results make it operationally unusable; or if a simpler bounded model with post-hoc checks achieves equivalent safety.
