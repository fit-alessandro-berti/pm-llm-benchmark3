## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

**Jan-Mar baseline.** Observed AUROC 0.81-0.82, Brier about 0.13, ECE 0.022-0.025, mean predicted risk equal to observed breach, unknown activity below 0.3%, missing queue feature 1%, variant mix near 45% V1, 35% V2 and 20% V3. Inference: a stable reference regime. Part time creeps from 28h to 35h without a breach increase.

**Apr schema and pipeline incident.** Observed AUROC falls to 0.67, Brier rises to 0.184, ECE rises to 0.110, mean predicted risk rises to 0.28 while observed breach remains 0.19. Unknown activity, missing queue feature and unmapped variants spike. Inference: pipeline failure is primary. The direction of miscalibration is overprediction. Variant-specific breach rates remain close to baseline, part time is normal at 34h, and there is no documented business change in April, contradicting a genuine concept or process explanation.

**May partial repair.** The 6 May mapping patch improves most metrics, but AUROC is still only 0.78, ECE 0.050, unknown activity 2.0% and missing queue feature 3%. Inference: residual contamination from backfilled records and cached features. May should be audited by sub-period around 6 May rather than treated as wholly clean or wholly failed.

**Jun intentional routing and covariate-process mix change.** V2 rises to 52%, V1 falls to 30%, V3 remains 18%. Observed breach falls to 0.15. Conditional breach by variant is essentially unchanged from May. Inference: a real process-mix and covariate shift, but not strong evidence of concept drift. The small ECE rise to 0.060 and mean prediction of 0.20 versus observed 0.15 leave open a secondary selection effect within remote routing that needs stratified testing.

**Jul early supplier effect.** Part time reaches 65h and mature V3 breach rises to 0.46, while V1 and V2 remain stable. AUROC falls to 0.76. Inference: the start of V3-specific performance and concept drift. Because prediction occurs at diagnosis, before part ordering, the future supplier shock is not available as a legitimate feature.

**Aug severe supplier and composition effects.** V3 share reaches 35%, part time 130h, overall observed breach 0.28 versus mean predicted 0.19, AUROC 0.68 and ECE 0.120. Crucially, V3 breach reaches 0.64. Inference: composition alone cannot explain the deterioration because the outcome rate changes within V3. This is genuine concept drift interacting with process and covariate drift. The calibration sign is now opposite to April: systematic underprediction.

**Sep overlapping supplier, population and censoring effects.** Volume jumps to 5,100, V3 reaches 42%, part time 148h, observed breach 0.31 versus predicted 0.21, AUROC 0.66, ECE 0.140 and maturity 82%. Partner Z accounts for 24% and is more V3-heavy, more rural and older-device. Inference: supplier concept drift persists, Partner Z adds population and process composition, and censoring begins to hide the true magnitude. Non-Partner-Z analysis is essential because August already proves deterioration before Partner Z.

**Oct dominant label-delay confounding.** Maturity is only 55%, median label delay 52d, observed breach 0.16 and variant breach rates are not stable. Yet V3 share is 41% and part time is 155h. Inference: October labels are heavily right-censored, especially for long V3 paths. AUROC, Brier and ECE computed on the mature subset are selection-biased. Process evidence suggests the Aug-Sep high-risk regime probably persists, but October labels cannot quantify it and must not be read as recovery.

**Why this is not one homogeneous failure.** April has bad data quality, overprediction and stable conditional outcomes. June has clean data, a deliberate variant-mix change and stable conditional outcomes. August-September have clean-ish data, severe process-time change, within-V3 outcome change and underprediction. October has a low observed breach rate precisely because long cases are missing labels. Pooling these mechanisms into one retraining decision would mix a pipeline bug, a policy mix shift, a real supplier shock, a new population and immature labels.

Falsification logic is as follows. Pipeline-only April is falsified if cleaned April features still give AUROC near 0.67. Concept drift in June is falsified if within-variant conditional calibration remains stable. Composition-only August is already contradicted by the within-V3 increase. Partner-Z-only September is falsified if non-Partner-Z September cases show the same miscalibration and reweighting does not restore performance. An October recovery is falsified if, at a fixed 90-day label horizon, breach and miscalibration remain high.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment.** Freeze automatic retraining and any recalibration fitted on October. Quarantine original April features and all October labels for training. Keep the prior model visible for continuity, but flag V3 predictions from August onward as likely underpredicted. Do not treat October dashboards as evidence of improvement. Operational escalation should focus on part supply and remote-resolution capacity, not only on model scores.

**Feature repair.** Deploy a versioned pipeline that normalizes the diagnosis alias before feature extraction, recomputes the cached event-count feature, applies a documented duplicate rule and preserves UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY for audit rather than silently recoding it. Regenerate April and transitional May prefixes. Rescore all months with the same frozen model to create a clean comparison vintage. Only after this repair should drift magnitude be judged.

**Short-term recalibration.** Recalibration is appropriate only on mature, repaired data. Fit an intercept and slope overall and a V3-specific intercept on mature Aug-Sep data, with time-based validation. If within-variant AUROC is preserved, recalibration can correct ECE and mean risk. If within-V3 AUROC remains around the pooled 0.66-0.68 level, recalibration will not repair ranking and retraining with new predictors is required.

**Full retraining.** Retrain only after label maturity and feature repair pass gates. Candidate predictors must be available at diagnosis: a supplier lead-time forecast, parts-stock or part-risk indicator, variant, rural travel distance and device age, subject to business and fairness review. Actual future Part Received time must not be used as a predictor at diagnosis because it is leakage. Use temporal validation, for example training through July and validating on mature Aug-Sep, followed by champion-challenger deployment.

**Month safety for training.**

| Month | Training status | Validation status | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan-Mar | Safe | Safe gold baseline | 100% maturity, clean data, stable process |
| Apr | Unsafe in original form; secondary use only after full recomputation | Unsafe until rescored | Severe pipeline and cache defects |
| May | Transitional; exclude from primary training until post-patch audit passes | Conditional | Patch improves data but residual defects remain |
| Jun | Safe | Safe | Mature and clean; useful for routing-mix robustness |
| Jul | Safe | Safe and useful for early supplier-regime validation | 98% maturity; supplier effect is emerging |
| Aug | Conditional; include only after fixed-horizon maturity and censoring sensitivity | Suitable for validation and recalibration sensitivity | 93% maturity and real regime change |
| Sep | Unsafe until labels mature | Provisional only | 82% maturity, Partner Z shift and nonrandom delay |
| Oct | Unsafe | Unsafe for performance; process and data-quality monitoring only | 55% maturity and severe selection bias |

**Segment-specific modelling.** Start with a pooled model containing variant interactions and variant-specific recalibration. A separate V3 model is a plausible next step because V3 has a different causal path, longer labels and supplier-specific risk drivers, and it has enough volume in mature months. A separate Partner Z model is not yet justified: mature sample size is limited, labels are delayed, and the gap may be explained by V3 mix and missing rural or device features. Prefer hierarchical or pooled modelling with source interactions, then consider a segment model only if mature stratified data show persistent different calibration slopes after feature repair.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

| Domain | Core signals | Cadence and cohort | Example trigger | Gated action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predictive | Prediction volume, mean risk, score distribution, PSI, risk by variant and source | Daily ingestion; monthly mature evaluation | Large score-distribution shift within a clean, mature cohort | Investigate; do not auto-retrain |
| Calibration | ECE, Brier and its decomposition, calibration intercept and slope, reliability by decile | Monthly on fixed-horizon mature labels | Persistent within-V3 intercept shift for two mature periods | Trigger recalibration review |
| Label maturity | Percentage labelled, median and p90 label delay, maturity by variant and source, API backlog | Daily or weekly | Maturity below 90% or delay above 1.5 times baseline | Mark metrics provisional; suppress retraining |
| Data quality | Unknown activity, unmapped variant, duplicate rate, missing features, schema and label-dictionary changes, prefix failure rate | Every ingestion | Unknown activity above 1%, missing feature above 5%, or schema change | Quarantine affected partition; block drift interpretation |
| Process variant | V1, V2, V3 and unmapped shares, trace conformance, route changes | Weekly case-start cohort | V3 share or unmapped share moves beyond baseline control limits | Operations and process review |
| Performance DFG | Median and p90 elapsed time for Order Part to Part Received, diagnosis to Remote Fix and diagnosis to Dispatch | Weekly case-start cohort | Part edge above baseline p90 or rising for consecutive periods | Supplier operations alert; contextualize model metrics |
| Population | Source share, rural distance, device age, case complexity, segment volume and representation | Monthly mature cohort | New source above 5% or major non-model feature shift | Stratified evaluation and transport check |
| Intervention and deployment | Model and feature versions, thresholds, overrides, expedited parts, remote attempts and downstream outcomes | Continuous logging; monthly review | Intervention rate changes sharply or overrides concentrate in one segment | Review feedback loops before retraining |

**Alert dependencies.** First, data-quality and schema alerts quarantine the affected data and block predictive conclusions. Second, label-maturity alerts gate calibration, discrimination and retraining alerts; if maturity is low, performance metrics are labelled provisional. Third, process, variant and population alerts explain context but do not automatically trigger retraining. Fourth, recalibration review requires clean data, adequate maturity and a persistent within-stratum calibration shift. Fifth, full retraining review additionally requires loss of discrimination or a new legitimately available predictor. This ordering prevents an October label backlog from causing an unnecessary model rebuild.

The following sketch extracts process evidence without fitting a model. It distinguishes an event-calendar cohort, which attributes events occurring in a month, from a case-start cohort, which attributes complete traces to the month in which the case began and therefore needs maturity before outcome interpretation.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

DIAGNOSIS_ALIAS = {
    "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": "Initial Diagnosis Completed"
}
TARGET_EDGE = ("Order Part", "Part Received")


def monthly_process_evidence(path="field_service_events.csv"):
    # Expected columns: case_id, activity, timestamp.
    df = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=["timestamp"])

    # Normalize the known alias before feature or prefix extraction.
    # Keep UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY unchanged so pipeline defects remain auditable.
    df["activity"] = df["activity"].replace(DIAGNOSIS_ALIAS)

    # Duplicate strategy: drop exact duplicates, then duplicates on
    # case + timestamp + activity, keeping the first. Do not dedupe on
    # case + activity alone because repeated activities can be legitimate.
    n_raw = len(df)
    df = df.drop_duplicates()
    df = df.sort_values(["case_id", "timestamp", "activity"])
    df = df.drop_duplicates(
        subset=["case_id", "timestamp", "activity"], keep="first"
    )
    duplicates_removed = n_raw - len(df)

    log_all = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case_id",
        activity_key="activity",
        timestamp_key="timestamp",
    )
    case_start = df.groupby("case_id")["timestamp"].min()
    rows = []

    for month_start in pd.date_range("2026-01-01", periods=10, freq="MS"):
        month_end = month_start + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)

        # Event-calendar cohort: events occurring in this month.
        calendar_log = pm4py.filter_time_range(
            log_all, month_start, month_end
        )

        # Case-start cohort: complete traces for cases starting this month.
        starting_cases = case_start[
            (case_start >= month_start) & (case_start < month_end)
        ].index
        case_df = df[df["case_id"].isin(starting_cases)]
        case_log = (
            pm4py.format_dataframe(
                case_df,
                case_id="case_id",
                activity_key="activity",
                timestamp_key="timestamp",
            )
            if not case_df.empty
            else []
        )

        for cohort, log in [
            ("event_calendar", calendar_log),
            ("case_start", case_log),
        ]:
            if len(log) == 0:
                continue

            variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
            n_traces = sum(len(traces) for traces in variants.values())
            dfg = pm4py.discover_dfg(log)
            perf = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
            temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)
            edge = perf.get(TARGET_EDGE, {}) if isinstance(perf, dict) else {}

            rows.append(
                {
                    "month_start": month_start.date().isoformat(),
                    "cohort": cohort,
                    "n_events": len(log),
                    "n_traces": n_traces,
                    "duplicates_removed": duplicates_removed,
                    "n_variant_types": len(variants),
                    "top_variant_share": max(
                        (len(t) / n_traces for t in variants.values()),
                        default=0,
                    ),
                    "dfg_size": len(dfg) if isinstance(dfg, dict) else None,
                    "order_part_to_received_median_h": (
                        edge.get("median") if isinstance(edge, dict) else None
                    ),
                    "n_temporal_features": (
                        temporal.shape[1]
                        if hasattr(temporal, "shape")
                        else None
                    ),
                }
            )

    return pd.DataFrame(rows)
```

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

| Failure mode | How it happens | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retraining on immature Oct or Sep labels | Recent long-path breaches are still missing, so the model learns an artificially low breach rate and underestimates V3 risk. | Worse underprediction, unsafe thresholds and false confidence in recovery. | Gate training on fixed-horizon maturity; use maturity curves, censoring sensitivity and quarantine Oct until labels complete. |
| Calibrating around the April pipeline bug | A recalibrator fitted to wrong prefixes, missing queue features and inflated scores learns to reproduce the bug. | The bug is baked into production and later clean data appear miscalibrated. | Repair and version features first; rescore the frozen model; only then estimate calibration on repaired data. |
| Ignoring genuine supplier-driven concept drift | All deterioration is attributed to April pipeline defects or variant composition. | V3 cases remain systematically underpredicted and promised dates continue to be breached. | Monitor within-V3 calibration on mature data; add at-diagnosis supplier forecasts; trigger recalibration or retraining when the V3 interaction persists. |
| Attributing Partner Z shift to unfairness without stratified evidence | A raw 0.45 versus 0.27 breach gap is interpreted as model bias despite V3 mix, rurality, device age and slower maturity. | Unjustified model changes, source-based penalties or failure to address real travel and parts constraints. | Stratify by variant, source and maturity; adjust for legitimate pre-diagnosis features; audit interventions and outcomes before making a fairness claim. |
| Creating separate V3 or Partner Z models prematurely | Segment models are fitted on immature, small or leakage-contaminated samples. | Unstable scores, poor transportability and possible encoding of structural disadvantage as inherent risk. | Begin with pooled modelling, interactions and segment recalibration; require mature sample sizes, temporal validation and no post-diagnosis leakage. |
| Treating June routing change as concept drift | A deliberate increase in low-risk remote cases triggers an aggressive retrain on pooled data. | The model becomes unstable and may undo useful baseline ranking for V1 and V3. | Use stratified evaluation and reweighting; retrain only if conditional calibration or discrimination changes on mature clean data. |
| Ignoring intervention feedback | High-risk cases receive expedited parts or remote attempts, changing outcomes without logging the intervention. | Later evaluation confuses intervention effects with model drift or improvement. | Log model version, score, threshold, action and override for every case; include intervention status in monitoring and evaluation. |

Overall, April is primarily a pipeline incident, June is a real routing and covariate-process mix change without clear concept drift, August-September combine supplier process and concept drift with composition effects and an emerging Partner Z population shift, and October is dominated by label censoring. The proportionate response is therefore repair and quarantine first, mature-label recalibration second, and full retraining only after clean features, adequate label maturity and evidence that discrimination or conditional outcome relationships have actually changed.
