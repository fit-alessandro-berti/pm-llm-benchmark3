# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

To analyze the 72-hour SLA tail risk rigorously, we define:
- **Time origin ($T=0$)**: The exact moment the `Submit` activity is recorded.
- **Time horizon ($T=72$)**: 72 hours of elapsed time from submission.
- **Censoring**: Administrative right-censoring occurs at the observation cutoff for `open` cases.

We define two distinct estimands corresponding to different operational questions:

**Estimand 1: Cumulative Incidence of Resolution (CIF) at 72 hours**
This answers: *In the presence of competing terminal events (withdrawal, rejection, transfer), what is the marginal probability that a case successfully resolves before the SLA breaches?* 
This treats competing events as terminal states that prevent resolution.

**Estimand 2: Probability of Failing to Resolve by 72 hours (Strict SLA Breach)**
This answers: *What proportion of cases fail to achieve a `resolved` status within the SLA window, either because they exceed 72 hours, remain open, or hit an unsuccessful terminal state?*
This models the cause-specific hazard of general failure, treating resolution as the competing censoring mechanism or analyzing binary failure at the 72-hour mark.

**How specific cases contribute:**
- **S03 (resolved at 76h)**: Under Estimand 1 (CIF of resolve), it contributes to the risk set for the first 72 hours without an event. At $T=72$, it is un-censored and unresolved. It experiences the event at $T=76$. Under Estimand 2, it is a recorded SLA failure at 72 hours.
- **S09 (open at 92h)**: Right-censored at $T=92$. It remains in the risk set and unresolved at the 72-hour mark for both estimands, confirming a known SLA breach.
- **S06 (withdrawn at 27h)**: Under Estimand 1, it experiences a competing event at $T=27$ and is removed from the risk pool for resolution. Under Estimand 2, withdrawal is mapped as an SLA failure (it failed to resolve).
- **S08 (transferred at 68h)**: Under Estimand 1, if treated as a competing event, it drops from the resolution risk pool at $T=68$. Under Estimand 2, it is evaluated as an SLA failure at $T=68$.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

This table maps the `observed_status` to numerical encodings for modeling Resolution as the event of interest.

| observed_status | Event (for CIF) | Competing Risk Code | Censoring Rule | Transfer Sensitivity Alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `resolved` | 1 (Event) | 1 | None | 1 (Event) |
| `open` | 0 | 0 | Right-censored at `age_h` | Right-censored at `age_h` |
| `withdrawn` | 0 | 2 | Competing Event | Competing Event |
| `rejected` | 0 | 3 | Competing Event | Competing Event |
| `transferred` | 0 | 4 | **Assumption A:** Competing Event | **Assumption B:** Right-Censored<br>**Assumption C:** SLA Failure (Event=0 for Success, Event=1 for Failure model) |

**Three Plausible Assumptions for `transferred`:**
1. **Transfer as Competing Risk (A)**: The case is definitively handed off and cannot physically resolve internally. It is removed from the internal resolution risk set.
2. **Transfer as Independent Right-Censoring (B)**: Assumes transferred cases would have resolved with the exact same probability/hazard as internally retained cases. This is likely a false assumption but serves as an optimistic baseline.
3. **Transfer as SLA Failure (C)**: Treating transfer identically to failing the SLA (a strict "did we resolve it ourselves?" test).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis analyzing only completed `resolved` cases fundamentally distorts reality:
- **Survivor bias**: Dropping long-running `open` cases like **S09** (92h) and **S26** (104h) artificially removes the tail of the distribution, inflating the apparent speed of the process. 
- **Active open cases below vs above 72h**: **S15** (open, 35h) is actively in the risk set and hasn't had time to breach. **S11** (open, 81h) is actively open *above* 72 hours, meaning it is a *known* SLA breach. Discarding S11 throws away known SLA failures.
- **Informative censoring**: If we treat transfers as right-censored, we drop cases like **S08** (transferred at 68h) and **S32** (transferred at 96h). Transfers often happen for complex, escalated cases that are about to breach. Removing them censors them informatively, hiding systemic delays.
- **Competing risks**: Dropping **S06** (withdrawn at 27h) or **S05** (rejected at 9h) assumes these cases would have behaved like the resolved population, misrepresenting operational throughput.
- **Cohort imbalance**: Cohort B had a policy change. If Cohort B pushes cases to faster `AutoCheck` resolutions (e.g., **S16**, 16h) but leaves complex cases hanging in `open` state (e.g., **S25**, 79h), a completed-case analysis will wrongly conclude the policy was a pure success.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

We evaluate standard survival methods for this data context:
- **Kaplan-Meier (KM)**: Inappropriate for the primary estimate of resolution because KM treats competing risks (Withdraw/Reject) as independent censoring, overestimating the true probability of resolution (1-KM > actual CIF).
- **Aalen-Johansen / Cumulative Incidence (CIF)**: **Recommended Primary Approach**. This natively estimates the actual fraction of cases that reach `resolved` by 72 hours in the presence of competing states, perfectly answering the operational capacity question.
- **Cause-Specific Hazards (CSH)**: Good for isolating the specific rate of resolution per hour among cases still active, useful for process mining diagnostics.
- **Subdistribution Hazards (Fine-Gray)**: Useful for regressing covariates directly on the CIF of resolution, but complex to interpret causally.
- **Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST)**: Less relevant because the operational target is an absolute 72h SLA, not the mean time.
- **Landmarking**: **Recommended Secondary Approach**. Because activities like `Escalate` happen mid-process, they are immortal-time covariates. Landmarking (e.g., fitting a model on cases still active at 24h) allows us to safely include time-varying covariates like "has escalated by 24h".
- **Multi-state model**: Ideal for modeling the exact graph (Submit $\to$ Screen $\to$ Review $\to$ Resolve), but heavily limited here due to missing exact intermediate timestamps for each transition.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

From the `prefix` sequence and gap timings, we extract features.

**Feature Extraction & Risk Types:**
1. **Initial Path (`AutoCheck` first vs `Screen` first)**: **Baseline** covariate. Determined at or immediately after Submit. Safe for day-0 survival models.
2. **Current Stage (last activity in prefix)**: **Time-varying** / Post-treatment.
3. **`rework_count` > 0**: **Time-varying**. Cannot be used as a baseline predictor.
4. **Escalation status (`escalated`)**: **Time-varying**. Using this as a baseline feature introduces severe immortal time bias / leakage (a case must survive long enough to be escalated).
5. **`inactivity_gap` (`last_event_gap_h`)**: **Time-varying** snapshot of idleness.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

To ensure our conclusions regarding Cohort B's policy change are robust, we must run three sensitivity comparisons:
1. **Transfer Treatment Variations**: Compare CIF of resolution under Transfer-as-Competing-Risk vs. Transfer-as-Right-Censored vs. Transfer-as-SLA-Failure. *Reversal Risk*: If Cohort B relies heavily on late-stage transfers (e.g., S32 at 96h) to clear its queue, analyzing transfers as "optimistic censoring" will falsely validate the policy change.
2. **Informative Censoring of Stale Open Cases**: Assume any `open` case with `last_event_gap_h` > 48 hours is effectively "informatively withdrawn" (abandoned) and enforce a competing event at `duration_or_age_h - last_event_gap_h`.
3. **Cohort-Policy effects conditional on Priority**: Stratify the CIF analysis jointly by `cohort` and `priority`. *Reversal Risk*: Cohort B may appear faster globally only because non-random assignment shifted a higher volume of easy P3 cases to Cohort B. Adjusting for priority might reveal the automated policy degraded P1 performance.

# 7. Operational interpretation

- **Alerts**: Do not trigger alerts using baseline KM models. Build a Landmarked cause-specific hazard model at 24h and 48h. If a case is in `Review` at 48h and its conditional probability of missing the 72h SLA > 60%, trigger an alert.
- **Workload Actions**: Survival coefficients are not causal. If a subdistribution hazard shows `Escalate` strongly predicts SLA breach, do not ban escalations. `Escalate` is a proxy for underlying complexity, not the cause of the delay.
- **SLA Governance**: Report the 72h Aalen-Johansen CIF as the formal SLA metric to executives, as it naturally accommodates open cases and non-resolution competing risks without optimistic survivor bias.
- **Calibration/Fairness Checks**: Ensure the Cohort B automated screening policy does not systematically degrade SLA performance for P3 cases to artificially boost P1 cases, ensuring algorithmic fairness across priority strata.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

For stronger process-mining and survival inference, the following missing data is strictly required:
1. **Exact Event Timestamps**: Knowing only `duration_or_age_h` and `last_event_gap_h` prevents fitting true time-dependent covariate survival models (like Cox models with time-varying effects) because the intermediate times of `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, and `Escalate` are entirely missing.
2. **External Outcomes**: We do not know what happens to cases after `Transfer`. Securing eventual resolution linkages from the external organization would eliminate the need to run crude sensitivity analyses on transfers.
3. **Cutoff / Start Dates**: Without calendar dates, we cannot control for weekly seasonality, queue build-ups (time-varying workload), or exact dates of the Cohort B policy rollout.
4. **Time-varying Workload**: The number of active cases assigned to agents often confounds SLA performance; omitting this risks attributing delays entirely to case characteristics rather than resource capacity.
5. **Assignment Mechanism**: Cohort B was not randomly assigned and rollout was incomplete. We need the explicit routing logic or variables to perform propensity score matching to establish causal estimates of the policy's success.
