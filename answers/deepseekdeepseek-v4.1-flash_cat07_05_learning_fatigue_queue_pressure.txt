# 2. Integrated explanation

The work-period pattern is not a single learning curve or a simple fatigue curve. Service time falls across sequence position because at least four things move together: early cases are more complex and higher priority, often carryover from prior shifts; resources warm up; later cases are simpler; and high WIP late in shifts pressures workers to process faster. The early high service time is therefore partly case-mix and selection, not poor early performance. The middle positions look best because complexity is lower and the system has stabilized.

Late-shift speed is especially misleading if interpreted as learning. By positions 51–75 and 76+, queue length rises to 62 and 88, while complexity falls. The high-WIP table shows the classic speed-quality tradeoff: high WIP low-complexity cases take 9.4 min but have 5.8% error; high WIP high-complexity cases take 16.8 min but have 10.9% error. So workers are not simply getting better; they are often working under pressure, possibly batching, shortening checks, deferring Quality Check, or correcting bot outputs.

Errors rise only in some conditions because queue pressure, complexity, fatigue/circadian effects, batching, and automation interact. Seniors show stable quality even under high WIP, while mid-level and junior resources degrade more. Night staff handle fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, so raw error comparisons across shifts are unfair. Day shift late errors are high, but that is also when demand peaks and queue ownership/handover effects may accumulate. Early-shift errors are low despite high complexity, likely because fresh senior resources take complex carryover cases and perhaps spend more time on them.

The key distinction is this: service time is driven strongly by case-mix, warm-up, and queue-induced speed; error rate is driven by the interaction of queue pressure with complexity, experience, time-of-day, delayed quality checks, and automation cleanup. A late-shift worker who is fast may be responding rationally to WIP > 70, but the system pays later in rework and errors.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair because assignment is not random. Seniors may receive complex carryover cases early, juniors may receive different work, and night staff handle urgent exceptions. A worker with higher raw error rate may simply have been exposed to higher WIP, more complex cases, more bot corrections, or a shift with delayed Quality Check.

Measurement error matters. `error_flag` may detect only errors that surface in the log; some errors are never recorded. `rework_flag` within 7 days is right-censored: some rework may occur after the window. Quality Check delays can make errors appear in a later shift, blaming the wrong worker or shift.

Queue pressure is often created by management decisions: staffing levels, batching policies, deferring checks, demand peaks, and handover timing. Punishing workers for late-shift errors can therefore punish them for system constraints.

Automation quality is another confound. Bot_Risk may create classifications that humans must correct. Those corrections may appear as human error or rework even when the human is fixing an upstream automation problem.

Do not use raw late-shift error rates for punitive action. Use risk-adjusted rates, within-resource comparisons, confidence intervals, case-mix adjustment, WIP adjustment, and process context. The goal should be system improvement, fair workload, and safe quality checks—not worker ranking.
