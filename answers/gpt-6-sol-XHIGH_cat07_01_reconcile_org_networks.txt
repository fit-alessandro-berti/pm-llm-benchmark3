# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

- **Handover-of-work network.** A directed edge records one resource's event followed by another resource's event in a case. Mira has high out-degree (0.82), in-degree (0.51), and betweenness (0.77). The prominent Bot_Rules → Mira edge (920) and her outward edges to specialists and adjusters fit an exception-routing hub. Returns from Omar and Priya fit a coordinator who receives specialist results. These are event sequences, not proof that Mira supervises those people, makes the substantive decision, or personally causes the intervening delay. Betweenness depends on the graph construction and paths; edge counts can include repeated handovers within one claim. The bot's own high handover betweenness (0.41) illustrates how a routing node can be central without being a human manager.

- **Working-together network.** An undirected edge records that two resources appear on the same case, regardless of order or interaction. Mira's weighted degree (0.16) and pairwise co-case rates with adjusters (6–8%) are low beside the North and South team pairs (19–27%). This supports a distinction between sustained team casework and brief, cross-team intervention. It does not show that Mira rarely coordinates: she touches only 22% of all claims, whereas these co-case percentages use all cases as the denominator. Repeated handovers on a small set of exception cases can therefore coexist with low population-wide co-occurrence. Conversely, co-occurrence alone would not prove collaboration or authority. Her specialist and team relationships should also be examined conditional on cases eligible for exception handling.

- **Subcontracting network.** An A → B → A pattern within the specified two-event window means work returns to the same resource after another acts. Mira's high subcontracting betweenness (0.69), plus Mira–Omar–Mira (126) and Mira–Priya–Mira (98), fit referral and follow-up loops. The Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules pattern (62) may reflect manual clarification followed by a rules update. The term *subcontracting* here is a sequence pattern, not a finding of contractual delegation, managerial control, unnecessary rework, or a defect. The team leads and Temp_1 also have such loops, so the pattern itself is not unique to Mira.

- **Performance/time statistics.** Mira's 4.2-minute median event service time is consistent with short routing or clarification tasks; her high-variance case cycle-time contribution and the absence-period rise in the exception-case 90th percentile (8.4 to 13.9 days) suggest that the coordination function matters especially in the tail. Ordinary low-value claims were unchanged. These observations neither show how much time cases actually waited *for Mira* nor establish that her work makes cases slower: difficult cases are preferentially sent to her, and the six-day comparison could be affected by arrivals, staffing, or case mix. Elapsed time between handover events mixes queueing, off-system work, and processing. Omar and Priya have long review times, while Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay have sizable waiting-time components; those steps may contribute materially to delay even when Mira lies on the path. Bots' seconds-long events should not be analyzed as human workload.

- **Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity.** Mira's mix of Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Assign Specialist is consistent with an exception desk or cross-team coordinator. Activity-based similarity might place her near junior adjusters or Temp_1 on missing-information work, or near Alice on reopen work, but her combined profile appears different from routine team-lead approval, specialist review, or vendor processing. A discovered role is a cluster of observed tasks, not a formal job title, reporting line, decision right, or proof of equivalent skill. Rare but important assignments can also be obscured by frequency-based similarity.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

## Formal coordinator / exception desk

- **Mechanism:** A designated desk receives rules-engine exceptions, collects missing information, and dispatches cases to the appropriate adjuster or specialist under defined criteria.
- **Supporting evidence:** Bot_Rules → Mira is frequent; her recorded activities and 48% exception-case share fit that remit. She touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, with specialist responses often returning to her. Short event service times fit dispatch work.
- **Limits:** The log does not show the assignment policy, authority, or all off-system coordination. The process owner's “queue cleaner” description could mean a narrower mandate; many exception cases never touch her.
- **Predictions:** Documented assignment rules and queue records will match most of her routing decisions. Comparable cases handled by a trained backup will follow similar routes and outcomes.

## Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker

- **Mechanism:** Mira uses cross-team knowledge to connect automation, North and South adjusters, medical and legal specialists, and exception queues, while team leads retain routine team management.
- **Supporting evidence:** She has handovers to both teams and both specialists, but low within-team co-case rates and few direct co-cases with the team leads. Adjusters' account that she knows where difficult cases should go fits this pattern.
- **Limits:** Shared case sequences cannot distinguish valuable expertise from mechanical forwarding. The process owner may be right that much of the work is administrative. Low co-case rates partly reflect her limited case exposure.
- **Predictions:** Her intervention will disproportionately shorten time to appropriate specialist assignment, reduce misroutes or repeated specialist referrals, or avert formal escalations for comparable exception cases. Routing diversity will be higher than for team-specific leads even after case-mix adjustment.

## Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

- **Mechanism:** Exception decisions or knowledge accumulate at a single person; queues form when she is unavailable, and her short logged actions conceal long waits before assignment.
- **Supporting evidence:** Her handover betweenness is 0.77; exception queues accumulated during her six-day absence, and the exception-case 90th percentile rose by 5.5 days while ordinary low-value cases were stable.
- **Limits:** Centrality is not capacity utilization. The absence episode is short and observational; specialist capacity, arrivals, or complex case mix might explain part of the change. A quick event can still be a helpful step rather than a cause of delay.
- **Predictions:** Exception-queue age and time from Bot_Rules to first appropriate human or specialist action will rise when her availability or capacity falls, then fall with a trained backup, controlling for volume, complexity, and downstream capacity. Work will concentrate on her even when other qualified staff are free.

## Informal quality gatekeeper with discretionary routing

- **Mechanism:** Mira screens ambiguous cases and effectively influences which specialist or team reviews them, even if her formal title confers no such power.
- **Supporting evidence:** She is prominent on reopened claims and complaints, detects or clarifies problems, assigns specialists, and receives their responses. Compliance has identified a plausible concentration of detection and assignment discretion.
- **Limits:** No log evidence establishes biased referrals, improper approvals, or policy violations. The team leads' approval and payment-release handovers suggest some distinct downstream controls; their adequacy is unproven.
- **Predictions:** After adjusting for case features, Mira-handled cases may show distinctive referral, override, rework, or escalation rates. Audit trails should identify the reason and authorization for departures from standard routing; reviews of sampled decisions would establish whether discretion is appropriate.

## Measurement or logging artifact

- **Mechanism:** Automated routing, repeated short events, missing lifecycle distinctions, or an account used for several desk workers inflates apparent brokerage under resource-level aggregation.
- **Supporting evidence:** The log includes automated resources and a Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules loop. Mira's short routing events can create many edges without much case tenure. “Assign Specialist” appears in her activity profile and fragments but not in the stated 14-activity list, suggesting a taxonomy or extraction check is needed.
- **Limits:** Her exception concentration and absence-period queue build-up are operational observations that a pure graph artifact does not readily explain.
- **Predictions:** Deduplicating repeated case-level handovers, separating complete/start lifecycle events, resolving account identity, and harmonizing activity names will materially change her centrality if measurement is the main explanation. If centrality and absence effects persist, the artifact hypothesis weakens.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk:** Exception claims may queue at a routing step or at the specialists/vendors beyond it. Measure arrival rate, backlog, and waiting time at each point before attributing delay to Mira; her high centrality and the absence episode justify investigation.
- **Coordination risk:** Removing or bypassing a broker without a substitute could misroute difficult claims, duplicate specialist work, or increase formal escalations. Overreliance on informal knowledge can also make routing inconsistent across teams.
- **Key-person risk:** The absence result suggests weak coverage for exception work. A trained backup, shared decision rules, and visible queues would reduce this dependency without assuming Mira is at fault.
- **Compliance/control risk:** Detecting an issue and choosing its next reviewer creates potential for unchecked discretion or conflicts. Require a logged rationale and proportionate independent review for sensitive referrals, overrides, and reopened complaints. There is no direct evidence of wrongdoing. Confirm where team-lead approval and vendor payment controls actually operate.
- **Workload fairness risk:** Mira handles a disproportionate share of reopened claims and complaints, while specialists face high-exception, long-service work. Raw event or case counts understate cognitive load and difficulty; allocation should account for case type, priority, amount, region, channel, complexity, and time spent. Bot and vendor events should be reported separately from human workload.
- **Data-quality risk:** Resource accounts, missing events, inconsistent activity names, duplicate events, lifecycle transitions, timestamp precision/time zones, and flag timing can distort all three networks and elapsed-time estimates. In particular, an exception_flag assigned after Mira acts must not be treated as a pre-routing case attribute. Validate whether vendor durations represent service, waiting, or both.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

1. **Handover network:** With `pm4py.format_dataframe` and `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network`, compare directed, weighted edges and unique-case handovers for ordinary versus exception claims, reopened claims, and complaints. Analyze time slices before, during, and after the absence; compare paths through Mira, the team leads, specialists, bots, and vendors. Recompute centrality after suppressing repeated same-case loops and bot-only edges, while preserving the full graph as the operational baseline. `pm4py.discover_network_analysis` can support more detailed activity/resource and temporal views.
2. **Working-together network:** Use `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` on all claims and on eligible exception claims. Report pair co-case counts and rates under both denominators, then compare within-team cohesion and cross-team contacts. Check whether Mira's apparent peripherality persists once her limited exposure and case mix are accounted for; do not equate co-occurrence with communication.
3. **Subcontracting network:** Run `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with n=2 and sensitivity checks at other windows. Inspect actual activity fragments and unique cases for Mira–specialist–Mira, lead–adjuster–lead, Temp_1–Vendor_Verify–Temp_1, and Bot_Rules–Mira–Bot_Rules. Classify legitimate referral, clarification, approval, and avoidable rework by reviewing trace context; repeat-event and lifecycle artifacts need removal first.
4. **Activity profiles and roles:** Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles`, with bots and vendors clearly separated. Compare Mira with leads, juniors, temporaries, and specialists by task mix and case segment. Validate clusters against actual job descriptions, access rights, and assignment policy rather than naming a hierarchy from an algorithm.
5. **Cohort and sequence analysis:** Apply `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to `exception_flag`, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and specialist activities; use `pm4py.filter_variants` and time-range filters to compare comparable routes. Stratify or model by case_type, priority, amount_band, region, and channel. Check whether the flag is known at routing time and whether the absence period had different arrival volume or staffing.
6. **Service and waiting time:** Use `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`, after validating lifecycle start/complete semantics, to separate active work from queues. Estimate time from bot exception to first action, Mira assignment to specialist start, specialist completion to next action, approval to Vendor_Pay, and Vendor_Verify turnaround. Compare medians and upper tails with case-mix adjustment and available staffing. An absence-period comparison with matched controls can strengthen, but not by itself prove, a causal claim.

# 5. Recommendation

- **Change immediately:** Preserve the exception-routing function and establish named backup coverage for Mira's absence. Put exception queues, ownership, age, and escalation thresholds in a shared view; document referral criteria and require a brief reason for specialist assignments and overrides. Route sensitive or contested cases through a proportionate independent review. These are reversible control and resilience improvements, not judgments about Mira's conduct.
- **Monitor:** Track exception backlog, time to first assignment, specialist and vendor queues, rework and misroutes, complaint/reopen outcomes, distribution of complex work, and 90th/95th percentile cycle times. Review by cohort and availability period; separate bot, human, and vendor steps. Audit a sample of routing decisions against policy and outcomes, and revisit the network measures after data-quality checks.
- **Defer pending evidence:** Do not remove Mira, centralize more authority in her role, or automate all of her judgment from centrality scores alone. First establish which coordination tasks create value, which create waits, and which can be transferred to documented rules, trained deputies, or team leads. Any eventual removal would need explicit replacement of exception detection, routing, specialist follow-up, and complaint/reopen ownership.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira appears to coordinate difficult claims across the rules engine, two adjuster teams, and medical and legal reviewers. Her strong handover and return-loop connections show that cases frequently pass through her during exception handling. Her weaker working-together connections are compatible with brief interventions on only 22% of claims, rather than sustained casework with a fixed team. These measures do not establish her formal authority or show whether she causes delays. During her six-day absence, exception queues grew and their 90th-percentile cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days, while ordinary low-value claims were unaffected. That raises a practical concern about backup coverage, though case mix and downstream capacity need checking. Keep the coordination function, name and train a deputy, document referral rules, and review sensitive routing decisions independently. Measure queue waits, specialist and vendor delays, and outcomes for comparable cases before changing her authority or removing the role.
