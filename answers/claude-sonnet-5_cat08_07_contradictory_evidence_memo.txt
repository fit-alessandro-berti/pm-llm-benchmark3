# Fiber-Installation Redesign R: Decision Memo

## 1. Executive memo

**Recommendation: Adopt Alternative E — a segmented hybrid rollout — not full scale, not rollback.**

Redesign R is not one intervention; it is three bundled changes (coordinator ownership, survey skipping, post-billing activation) with different, sometimes opposite, effects across segments. Aggregate metrics mask this. Median cycle time improved (14.2  10.8 days) and handovers dropped sharply (34%  12% of cases with 3+ handovers), which is real operational gain. But the same redesign drove p95 duration from 45 to 62 days, cut first-time-right from 82% to 76%, raised 30-day complaints from 6.1% to 9.8%, and produced an unreliable process model (precision fell from 0.79 to 0.51). Segment data shows why: simple low-risk houses improved on nearly every metric, while apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, and business customers deteriorated — often sharply. Two of the three redesign components (survey-skip rule, post-billing activation) are implicated directly in the alignment diagnostics: frequent "billing before activation" and "installation without required permit check" violations are not noise — they are the redesign working as designed, and as designed incorrectly for higher-risk segments.

**Why not scale nationally (A):** Scaling now would nationalize the tail-risk, rework, and compliance-like failures (missing permit checks for older buildings, billing before activation confirmed) before we understand which building/customer types the skip-rule mishandles. The 93% coordinator utilization and the finding that cases stall when a coordinator is absent (loss of resilience) mean national scale would also concentrate operational fragility in a single role. Precision of 0.51 tells us the current model does not reliably predict pilot behavior — a red flag for committing capital before the process is understood.

**Why not roll back (B):** Rollback would discard a genuine, segment-validated win (34% of the pilot volume — simple houses — improved on cost, speed, and satisfaction with no complaint increase) and would forfeit the accountability benefit of single ownership. The redesign is not uniformly bad; it is unevenly good.

**Why hybrid, not just "coordinator-only" (C) or "pure experiment" (D):** We already have directional segment evidence strong enough to act on for simple houses now, while the apartment/old-building/business segments need both design fixes (reinstate survey and permit checks, keep activation before billing) and better measurement before any further exposure. Option C is close to right but treats the fix as uniform rather than differentiating what to scale by segment; Option D is safe but delays acting on evidence we already trust for the simple-house segment, and delays fixing known-bad practices (billing before activation) that should stop immediately regardless of what further data shows.

**The decision under uncertainty:** Cost data excludes downstream complaints after week 4, customer-segment/risk attributes are incomplete before week 3, and start timestamps are sparse — so today's estimates of full cost, cycle time, and root cause are directional, not final. Waiting for perfect data is not free either: every week of full-scale delay costs the simple-house benefit; every week of unmonitored skip-rule and post-billing activation use in higher-risk segments compounds rework and complaint costs. The hybrid gives us the acquirable benefit now while capping the downside and generating the missing data needed to decide on wider scale within one quarter.

**Immediate governance action independent of segment analysis:** billing before activation should be paused everywhere — this is a customer-facing and likely compliance-adjacent practice with no segment where it looked net-positive once complaints are counted.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness, low precision:** Fitness measures whether the model can replay observed behavior; precision measures whether it forbids behavior that doesn't occur. The pilot process has genuinely become more variable — cases now legitimately traverse many alternate paths (skip survey or not, manual or automatic activation, billing before or after activation, second visit or not). A model built to accommodate all this replays fitness well (0.94) but must permit combinatorially many paths, most of which are rarely taken in practice, so precision collapses (0.51). This is a symptom of the redesign injecting genuine structural variability, not a modeling artifact.

**Improved median, worse tail:** The redesign helps the modal case (simple house, low risk) via merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers, pulling the median down. But it introduces new failure paths (skipped survey then failed visit, manual activation, escalations) for a minority of harder cases, and those cases now take much longer — stretching p90/p95. Averages/medians hide tail risk that segment-level and quantile data expose.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience:** Handover count is a *structural* metric; resilience is an *organizational* one. Consolidating ownership on one coordinator reduces the number of documented transfers but concentrates knowledge and workload. When that single point of contact is unavailable, no one else holds context, so cases stall — a network-centralization effect visible in the social network becoming centered on coordinators/activation specialists.

**Lower visible cost, higher rework/downstream cost:** The visible cost metric (EUR 184161) is measured before complaint handling and is incomplete after week 4. Cheaper average handling per case (fewer visits, less checking) looks good until second visits (66%92% utilization), manual activation, and complaints are counted — these costs land later and outside the current measurement window, so the pre-complaint cost figure understates true cost.

**Improvement for simple houses, deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** The redesign's risk-reducing components (merged validation/scheduling, fewer handovers) benefit predictable, low-complexity cases. Its risk-increasing components (skipped survey, skipped permit check, deferred activation) specifically target and harm the segments where risk information is most needed — apartments and older buildings — because the predictive rule and reduced verification remove safety margins exactly where they're most necessary.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Option | Advantages | Risks | Conditions for reasonableness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale nationally** | Captures largest speed/handover gains fast; simplest to communicate | Nationalizes tail-duration, rework, complaint, and compliance-like risk; low model precision means behavior is not well understood; coordinator overload risk scales too | Only if segment-level fixes (permit check, activation-before-billing) are already validated to work at scale — not yet true |
| **B. Roll back entirely** | Removes known new failure modes immediately; simplest risk profile | Discards proven, segment-specific benefit for 34%+ of volume; loses accountability gains; ignores that only some redesign components are the problem | Reasonable only if simple-house benefit turns out to be a regional/pilot artifact under further testing |
| **C. Scale coordinator-ownership only; suspend skip and post-billing activation** | Directly targets the diagnosed root causes; retains handover/accountability benefit broadly | Coordinator utilization already at 93%; scaling ownership alone without addressing workload/resilience risk could reproduce overload elsewhere | Good default if E's segmentation infrastructure isn't ready; less precise than E because it doesn't yet differentiate by segment |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Maximizes learning quality (fixes attribute gaps, cost windows); low commitment risk | Delays realizing already-credible simple-house benefit; slower to react to urgent issues (billing before activation) | Best if leadership is not confident even the simple-house result is real, or if organizational bandwidth for parallel hybrid rollout is limited |
| **E. Hybrid — scale for simple/low-risk, add controls for apartments/old buildings, measure before wider scale (Recommended)** | Captures proven gains now, caps downside on risky segments, generates the missing data (M1–M8) to inform full-scale decision | Requires operational complexity of running two process variants concurrently; requires disciplined segment tagging, which is exactly what's incomplete pre-week-3 | Reasonable now: enough segment-level signal exists to act, but not enough precision/cost data to justify full national commitment |

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by how much each reduces decision-critical uncertainty and how directly it could change the recommended action:

1. **M7 – Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit-check absence.** Highest value: directly governs an action we're already pausing; ongoing monitoring tells us if the pause is holding and quantifies residual risk. Cheap, fast, actionable immediately.
2. **M6 – Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** Resolves whether the skip-rule is fixable/recalibratable or should be abandoned for medium/high-risk segments — this is the single largest lever on whether E's apartment/old-building carve-out can ever be lifted.
3. **M4 – Measure full downstream cost for 60 days post-billing.** Resolves the biggest financial uncertainty (EUR 198 vs 207 estimate is incomplete); directly determines whether the "lower visible cost" claim survives and thus whether A or C become financially justified.
4. **M1 – Randomized pilot of survey skipping by risk stratum.** Provides causal (not just correlational) evidence on skip-rule harm, strengthening or overturning the interpretation that skip-rule drives apartment/old-building deterioration. More rigorous than M6 alone but slower/costlier.
5. **M3 – Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit.** Turns currently coarse rework signals into diagnosable categories, needed to design targeted fixes rather than blanket suspensions.
6. **M5 – Compare coordinator vs shared-team ownership under absence.** Directly tests the resilience risk before scaling ownership model further (relevant to C and E); moderate urgency since coordinator model is already partially deployed.
7. **M8 – Separate CX measurement for speed vs working service.** Clarifies whether NPS decline is being driven by activation-quality issues (supports post-billing-activation rollback) versus scheduling speed (would defend that part of redesign); useful for messaging and prioritization but not decision-blocking.
8. **M2 – Instrument start timestamps.** Improves future measurement precision and process-mining model quality (helps fix low precision at its root), but has the longest lead time to affect this decision cycle; valuable for the next iteration, not urgent for the immediate call.

---

## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Adopt Alternative E — segmented hybrid rollout.**

- **Immediate action:** Halt post-billing activation network-wide effective immediately; this practice shows no segment where benefits outweigh complaint/compliance risk. Formally scale coordinator-ownership for simple, low-risk houses (34% of volume) where results are unambiguously positive across cost, speed, and satisfaction.
- **What to pause:** Survey-skipping for medium- and high-risk segments (apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings) until the predictive rule is validated (M6) or replaced. Post-billing activation everywhere, until activation-before-billing is re-established as the default across all segments.
- **What to scale:** Coordinator ownership model for simple/low-risk house segment now; extend to rural/business segments only after M1/M6 findings, given their mixed but less severe deterioration.
- **What to measure next (priority order):** M7 (conformance monitoring on the paused practices) and M6 (skip-rule validation) starting immediately; M4 (full 60-day downstream cost) and M1 (randomized skip-rule trial) launched in parallel; M3, M5, M8 as second-wave instrumentation; M2 as a durable process-mining capability improvement.
- **Threshold for broader rollout:** Extend Redesign R components beyond simple houses only when (a) M6/M1 show the risk rule (or a revised version) achieves 90% agreement with actual field-survey-determined risk for apartments/old buildings, (b) M7 shows near-zero incidence of billing-before-activation and missing permit checks in monitored regions, and (c) M4's full-cost figures show cost-including-complaints at or below baseline for the segment being considered for scale. Absent these three conditions being met, no segment beyond the current low-risk-house scope should move to full national rollout.
