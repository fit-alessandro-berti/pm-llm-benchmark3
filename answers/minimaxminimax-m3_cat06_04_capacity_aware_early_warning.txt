## 1. Decision rule

**Eligibility gate.** A case is intervention-eligible only if all four hold:
1. Current process stage  {Triage, Diagnosis Started} (intervention has near-zero effect after `Diagnosis Complete`, so I-03 is permanently ineligible regardless of risk).
2. Time-to-deadline  1.0 h (a 45-min swarm on a 1.5-h case buys little even when it works).
3. Either (a) no prior swarm on this case, **or** (b) a materially new event has been logged since the last swarm (new customer diagnostic data, scope change, customer-side escalation, infrastructure event).
4. Data-quality flag is not "blocking" — cases with imputed features are eligible only with an explicit imputation-confirmation step.

**Robustification of predicted probability.** Before any ranking, apply segment-specific calibration shifts to widen intervals, not to artificially raise scores:
- SME: shift point estimate up by +0.06 (mid of underprediction 0.04–0.09) and widen CI.
- Strategic Enterprise: shift point estimate down by 0.085 (mid of overprediction 0.05–0.12) and widen CI.
- Public Sector: no shift; CI at base width.
- Non-Native-Language Support: no point shift; widen CI to roughly ±0.15 to reflect sparse calibration.

**Alert-persistence gate.**
- Suppress any alert whose trigger is a 30-min model refresh with no new underlying event (the I-05 pattern).
- Re-arm only on (i) cool-down expiry, or (ii) a logged material change.

**Cool-down.** 4 hours after a swarm deployment, the same case cannot be re-swarmed unless a material event has occurred.

**Ranking.** For each eligible case compute a *robust* expected net effect, then rank:

> Net  ½·(lower+upper effect) × Value  Swarm cost (€450)  False-alarm burden  0.6·(spillover mid-hazard) × 6 × Avg untreated value.

The 0.6 multiplier on spillover is a deliberate "precautionary haircut" because spillover harm falls on cases the policy did not choose to help. Cases whose *adverse-scenario* net is still positive get Tier A; positive base but adverse-fragile cases get Tier B; marginal cases get Tier C (deploy only if capacity remains after regulatory-incident reservation).

**Minimum evidence change for re-alert.** A repeat alert on the same case must show either (a)  0.10 absolute change in robustified p_now, or (b) a logged material event, or (c) movement to a more time-constrained stage.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

**Cool-down.** 4 hours after a swarm deployment on a case ID, suppress any new alert on that case. Re-arm only on a logged material event.

**Refresh-driven suppression.** Any alert whose trigger event is the 30-min model refresh and for which no underlying case attribute has changed is suppressed automatically. This is the rule that disqualifies I-05 today.

**Evidence-change threshold for re-alert.** A repeat alert must show **either** (a) an absolute change of  0.10 in robustified `p_now`, **or** (b) a logged material event (new customer diagnostic log, scope change, customer escalation, infra incident), **or** (c) movement to a more time-constrained stage (e.g., Triage  Diagnosis Started with shrinking headroom).

**Escalation on repeat-with-no-action.** If a case fires  2 alerts within 60 minutes and no swarm has yet been deployed, raise its tier by one step (this is what elevates I-02 and I-09 from default Tier C to Tier B). This escalation is *only* a re-ranking signal — it does not by itself authorize a second swarm.

**Re-entry.** Once cool-down expires or a material event occurs, the case is re-evaluated from scratch using current `p_now`, current robustification, and current effect priors. Past swarm history does not by itself justify another swarm.

**I-05 vs I-07 — explicit comparison.**
- **I-05** is a third alert whose underlying case state has not changed since the first alert. Under the refresh-driven suppression rule it is *not* re-eligible, even though its robustified probability (0.57 + 0.06 = 0.63) and effect range (0.25–0.40) are individually attractive. The reason: acting on a refresh-driven alert trains the system to chase its own updates rather than the world, and it produces a labeled "swarm outcome" whose counterfactual is unobserved — see §5 on feedback loops.
- **I-07** is also a third alert, but the underlying world *has* changed: a new customer diagnostic log was uploaded. This satisfies the material-event criterion. I-07 is therefore re-eligible in principle. It is nonetheless not deployed today because (i) headroom is 1.5 h, (ii) value is €5 k, and (iii) net after spillover is negative — but it is correctly classified as a candidate, not suppressed.
- The asymmetry is principled: alert *count* is a model-side artifact; alert *content* is a world-side fact. Only the latter counts toward eligibility.

**Daily reset.** At 00:00 each day, cool-down counters reset but alert history persists. Persistent multi-day no-new-event alerts are escalated to a weekly review, not re-fired.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**Risk 1 — value-based prioritization starving low-value cases.**
Selecting on `Value × Effect  Cost` systematically favors Strategic Enterprise (€25 k–€100 k values) over Public Sector (€9 k–€15 k) and small SME contracts (€5 k–€11 k). If unchecked, this creates a portfolio where high-margin customers get protected and small/regulated customers absorb both their own breach risk and the spillover.
*Safeguard.* Impose a weekly "fairness floor": at least one of the four daily slots must go to a case outside the top-value quartile, unless no such case is eligible. Track the segment-mix of swarm deployments weekly. Also, give Public Sector cases a contractual-equivalent weighting (e.g., a 1.3× weight on breach-avoidance value when the contract carries regulatory reporting obligations).

**Risk 2 — Non-Native-Language Support calibration and effect uncertainty.**
The NNL segment has a small validation sample, so both `p_now` and the intervention-effect prior are wide. Without explicit attention, NNL cases will be deprioritized because their EV has wider intervals and looks "noisy" — but they may also be the segment where intervention matters most for service equity.
*Safeguard.* (a) Treat NNL cases as Tier B by default rather than by computed score, so they aren't excluded by CI-width alone. (b) Run a parallel prospective data-collection protocol on NNL cases — when a swarm is deployed, capture both the standard telemetry and NNL-specific linguistic/contextual features to enrich the segment's calibration over time. (c) Publish a quarterly segment-level calibration card including NNL specifically, even if the sample is small.

**Risk 3 — differential segment calibration.**
Strategic Enterprise is overpredicted by 0.05–0.12 (after a workflow redesign), and SME is underpredicted by 0.04–0.09. Without robustification, we would over-select Strategic cases and under-select SME cases — exactly the wrong direction if the goal is to protect genuinely at-risk work.
*Safeguard.* Apply the segment-specific shifts in §1 before ranking. Maintain separate calibration models per segment and recalibrate quarterly. Track realized vs predicted breach rates by segment on a rolling 30-day window; trigger an out-of-cycle recalibration if a segment's calibration error exceeds 0.07 in absolute terms.

**Risk 4 — selective intervention contaminating training labels.**
Every time we deploy a swarm on a high-`p_now` case, the post-swarm outcome becomes a labeled training example where intervention was non-random. Future models trained on this data will overestimate the value of intervention (because we always intervened on cases that looked bad) and may stop learning the structure of cases that recover without help.
*Safeguard.* Implement a **randomized holdout**: for each tier-A or tier-B eligible case, hold out  15% as a randomized control (no swarm unless an explicit human override). Compare post-window breach rates between swarm and holdout to estimate the causal effect rather than relying on observational comparison. Update the effect priors in the case table from this estimate, not from the supplied ranges, after the first 30 days.

**Risk 5 — spillover harms systematically falling on untreated groups.**
Spillover increases breach risk on six non-intervened diagnosis-stage cases per slot. These cases share the diagnosis queue with our intervened cases and tend to be the ones we *didn't* select — by construction, a less-favored group. Spillover therefore compounds rather than compensates for selection bias.
*Safeguard.* (a) Monitor breach rate on untreated diagnosis-stage cases as a function of swarm slots used per day; if untreated-case breach rate increases by more than 0.04 on swarm-heavy days, cap daily slots at 3 unconditionally. (b) Publish a weekly spillover ledger — estimated p × estimated value — alongside the direct-benefit ledger, so spillover is visible to operators, not just the algorithm. (c) Avoid scheduling two specialist interruptions within the same 90-minute window if avoidable, to reduce correlated congestion.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

**Operational monitoring (daily).**
- Slots used vs reserved; reason for any unused slot (specialist unavailable, all eligible cases suppressed, etc.).
- Time from swarm start to diagnosis-complete on intervened cases (process-side proxy for effect).
- Customer disruption events and false-alarm burden realization vs forecast.
- Specialist utilization rate; alert count per case per day.

**Predictive monitoring (weekly).**
- Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score, overall and by segment.
- Alert precision at the deployed-tier threshold; alert recall on cases that ultimately breached without intervention.
- Feature drift on the top-K model inputs, with PSI > 0.2 flagged.
- Refresh-driven alerts as a fraction of total alerts — a rising share is an early signal that suppression rules need tightening.

**Causal-effect monitoring (monthly, with quarterly deep-dive).**
- Randomized holdout ( 15% of eligible cases) provides the cleanest estimate; complement with regression discontinuity around deployment thresholds.
- Estimate the swarm's absolute risk reduction per segment, with confidence intervals. Compare against the supplied ranges (0.05–0.40). If the realized estimate falls outside the supplied range for any segment, update the prior.
- Track the "wrong-reason" rate: fraction of swarmed cases where post-swarm diagnosis shows the original alert signal was a red herring (this is the I-09-in-the-adverse-scenario case).

**Capacity and spillover monitoring (weekly).**
- Queue depth and average wait time at each stage.
- Untreated diagnosis-stage breach rate, separately for swarm-heavy days ( 3 slots) vs swarm-light days (0–1 slots). The difference is the realized spillover signal.
- Specialist interruption minutes per day; correlate with downstream stage dwell times.

**Fairness monitoring (monthly).**
- Segment-level swarm deployment rate; segment-level post-swarm breach rate.
- Share of swarm deployments going to cases in the bottom value-quartile (target  25% over a rolling 30-day window).
- NNL and other under-served segments tracked separately with their own calibration card, regardless of sample size.
- Audit log of suppressed refresh-driven alerts vs deployed material-event alerts.

**Recalibration triggers.**
- ECE drift > 0.05 absolute on any segment  trigger segment recalibration within 7 days.
- Realized effect estimate moves > 20% from the supplied prior  update priors, re-rank the entire portfolio.
- Untreated-case breach rate on swarm-heavy days exceeds swarm-light days by > 0.04  reduce default daily slots from 4 to 3; revisit specialist staffing.
- Refresh-driven alert share exceeds 40% of all alerts  tighten suppression to require  0.15 absolute robustified-p change.
- Fairness-floor breach (bottom-quartile share < 25% for two consecutive months)  hard-set at least one slot per day for non-top-quartile eligible cases.

**When to change the threshold or allocation rule.** The policy should be reviewed at fixed cadence (quarterly) and out-of-cycle when (i) any recalibration trigger fires twice in 30 days, (ii) the randomized holdout shows the effect estimate has shifted by  25%, or (iii) a regulatory-incident response reveals that the reservation policy mis-priced the option value of holding capacity. Changes to the rule itself — not just the parameters — require a documented decision record specifying the trigger, the alternatives considered, and the expected impact on fairness and spillover.
