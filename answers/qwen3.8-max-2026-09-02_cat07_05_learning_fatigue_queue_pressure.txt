# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is not the result of one mechanism. It is a composite of learning, case selection, queue pressure, batching, automation effects, and likely some fatigue/circadian interaction.

Early in a shift, service times are long mainly because the work is harder and more urgent. Positions 1–5 have the highest complexity, highest priority share, and backlog carryover from the previous shift. Seniors also appear to take complex cases early, especially after night or weekend backlog. Therefore, the high early service time should not be interpreted as poor warm-up alone. It is partly a case-mix and selection effect.

As the shift progresses, service time falls for several reasons at once:

1. **Learning / warm-up**: workers become faster at repeated activities, especially in the first 20–30 events.
2. **Easier case mix**: average complexity declines from 0.72 to 0.43 by late positions.
3. **Queue pressure**: when WIP and queue length rise, workers accelerate, simplify checks, or batch work.
4. **Batching**: late-shift Document Review batches and delayed Quality Check can make service time look shorter while increasing downstream error risk.
5. **Automation**: Bot_Risk can reduce measured classification time but may create later human correction work, especially under high queue pressure.

The key puzzle is that service time falls while errors rise only in some conditions. The data show that this happens when speed is produced under pressure, not when speed comes from learning or easier work. In low-WIP conditions, later error rates are stable for seniors and only modestly higher for other groups. In high-WIP conditions, errors rise sharply, especially for high-complexity cases and less-experienced workers. This is a classic speed-quality tradeoff: workers can go faster, but they do so by reducing verification, deferring checks, or relying on shortcuts. Errors then appear immediately or within the following days as rework.

Fatigue may contribute, but it is not the primary explanation. Pure fatigue would usually produce slower or more variable work late in the shift. Here, late work is faster. A more accurate interpretation is that fatigue may reduce resilience while queue pressure forces speed. The result is not necessarily slower service, but more errors, more rework, and more correction bursts near the end of the shift.

Circadian effects are also present but should not be overgeneralized. Night staff are slower and handle more urgent exceptions, but night volume is lower and the case mix is different. Day shift has high late-shift errors because demand peaks late and queue pressure builds. Late shift inherits Day backlog. Early shift clears Night backlog. Thus, time-of-day effects are entangled with shift design, handover, and demand patterns.

In short:

- **Early slow service** is largely complexity, backlog, and selection, with some warm-up.
- **Mid-shift improvement** is likely learning plus easier mix.
- **Late-shift fast service with high errors** is mainly queue pressure, batching, delayed Quality Check, automation corrections, and possibly fatigue as an amplifier.
- **Night-shift patterns** require separate treatment because of circadian load, lower volume, and urgent case mix.

```python
        mean_service=('event_service_minutes', 'mean'),
        error_rate=('error_flag', 'mean'),
        rework_rate=('rework_flag', 'mean')
    )
)
```

Expected finding: seniors are more resilient under high WIP, while juniors show larger error increases.

## 3.10 Statistical model

A suitable model should separate learning, pressure, complexity, shift, and resource effects. A mixed-effects logistic regression or equivalent should be used.

Conceptual model:

```text
error_flag ~
    spline(minutes_since_shift_start)
  + sequence_position_within_shift
  + wip_at_start
  + queue_length_at_start
  + case_complexity_score
  + priority
  + shift_id
  + hour_of_day
  + resource_experience_months
  + batch_flag
  + bot_correction_flag
  + wip_at_start:case_complexity_score
  + wip_at_start:sequence_position_within_shift
  + resource random intercept
  + resource random slope for WIP
  + shift random effect
```

Within-resource centering helps separate individual exposure from between-resource differences:

```python
for col in ['wip_at_start', 'queue_length_at_start', 'case_complexity_score']:
    df[col + '_resource_mean'] = (
        df.groupby('org:resource')[col].transform('mean')
    )
    df[col + '_within'] = df[col] - df[col + '_resource_mean']
```

A model specification in R-style pseudocode:

```text
glmer(
  error_flag ~
    bs(minutes_since_shift_start, df = 4) +
    wip_at_start_within +
    case_complexity_score_within +
    wip_at_start_within:case_complexity_score_within +
    priority +
    shift_id +
    experience_group +
    batch_flag +
    bot_correction_flag +
    (1 + wip_at_start_within | org:resource) +
    (1 | shift_id),
  family = binomial,
  data = df
)
```

For service time, use a gamma or log-normal model because service time is positive and skewed.

# 4. Intervention options

| Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk / unintended consequence | How to monitor impact with event-log indicators |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May reduce prolonged exposure to fatigue and sustained pressure. Could improve consistency if shifts are currently too long. | May increase handovers, backlog, and queue pressure at shift boundaries. Could reduce continuity for complex cases. May push work into already busy shifts. | Monitor last-two-hour error rate, rework within 7 days, queue length at shift end, handover count, waiting time for complex cases, overtime, and service time after handovers. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift | Could reduce early overload and allow workers to warm up before handling difficult cases. | May delay important decisions, increase waiting time for complex or high-priority cases, and shift complexity into later high-pressure periods. Could worsen outcomes if complex cases need early senior attention. | Monitor early-shift complexity mix, waiting time for complex/high-priority cases, late-shift complexity mix, error rate, rework, priority breaches, and case cycle time. |
| 3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70 | High WIP is strongly associated with errors. Alerts can trigger load leveling, extra help, or temporary slowdown of non-urgent work. | Alert fatigue if no support follows. Workers may game the metric or rush to clear WIP. Could create pressure to close cases prematurely. | Monitor time spent above WIP 70, error rate before/after alert, rework, service time, queue length, arrival-finish rate, and whether corrective action occurs within a defined response window. |
| 4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias | Can produce fairer comparisons and prevent systematic concentration of complex cases on certain workers or shifts. | Pure randomization may mismatch case difficulty with skill. Juniors may receive cases beyond capability. Seniors may be underused on simple cases. | Monitor complexity distribution by experience group, error rate, rework, service time, priority breaches, worker-load fairness, and downstream handovers. Use skill-constrained randomization rather than fully random assignment. |
| 5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours | May reduce fatigue and improve attention when queue pressure and end-of-shift rushing are highest. | If capacity is not protected, breaks may increase queue pressure and cause work intensification before/after breaks. | Monitor late-shift error rate, rework, service time, WIP before/after breaks, queue growth, break compliance, and whether errors shift to other periods. |
| 6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift | May reduce rushed end-of-shift Quality Check bursts and allow more deliberate review. | Risk of delayed detection, rework later, customer harm, and accumulation of unchecked work. Must not be used simply to remove quality control. | Monitor deferred QC backlog, QC aging, error detection lag, rework within 7 and 30 days, severity of escaped errors, waiting time for low-priority cases, and customer callbacks. |
| 7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks | Directly addresses the strongest risk zone: high WIP, high complexity, and late Day-shift pressure. Seniors may reduce errors and help coach juniors. | Costly. Could create dependency on seniors or reduce senior availability for complex casework. Needs clear scope to avoid bottleneck. | Monitor Day-shift WIP, late-shift error rate, rework, service time for high-complexity cases, senior utilization, waiting time, and fairness across resource groups. |
| 8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates | Theoretically intended to deter careless work. | Highly likely to be unfair because raw late-shift errors are confounded by queue pressure, case mix, batching, automation corrections, and detection timing. May encourage underreporting, gaming, blame, and morale loss. | If reviewed at all, monitor risk-adjusted error rates, reporting completeness, error severity, appeals, WIP exposure, complexity mix, and whether errors are system-driven. Do not use raw late-shift error rates for punishment. |

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair or misleading for several reasons.

1. **Selection bias**  
   Cases are not randomly assigned. Seniors may take complex cases early. Juniors may receive batchable work or cases left over from earlier shifts. A worker’s raw error rate may reflect the cases they were given, not their skill or effort.

2. **Case-mix confounding**  
   Early-shift cases are more complex and higher priority. Late-shift cases may be lower complexity but processed under much higher queue pressure. Comparing raw service times or error rates across shift positions without adjusting for complexity is invalid.

3. **Queue pressure is often a system condition**  
   High WIP may result from staffing levels, shift design, demand peaks, handover backlog, bot failures, or management targets. If workers are punished for errors that occur under high WIP, the organization may be penalizing workers for operational conditions it created.

4. **Measurement error and detection bias**  
   `error_flag` may depend on when and how quality checks occur. If Quality Check is delayed until the end of shift, more errors may be detected then, making late-shift work look worse. Conversely, if rushed checks miss errors, the true error rate may be underestimated.

5. **Automation quality matters**  
   Bot_Risk may reduce measured service time but create correction work for humans. If bot errors are attributed to human workers, performance measurement becomes distorted. Automation should be evaluated for its downstream correction burden.

6. **Batching can distort averages**  
   Short service times may reflect legitimate grouping of similar cases, but they may also reflect rushed processing. Without batch detection, averages can hide risky workarounds.

7. **Small samples and unstable rates**  
   Some resources, shifts, or activity combinations may have few events. Error rates based on small denominators can fluctuate wildly. Dashboards should show counts, confidence intervals, and risk-adjusted rates.

8. **Punitive use can degrade data quality**  
   If error rates are used punitively, workers may underreport errors, avoid complex cases, shift work to others, or optimize measured metrics rather than actual quality. This harms both operations and analytics.

9. **Fairness across experience groups**  
   Juniors may receive harder assignments or less support. Seniors may have more control over case selection. Comparing them without considering assignment rules can produce misleading conclusions.

10. **Transparency and due process**  
   Any performance insight should be reviewed with operational context, worker input, and appeal mechanisms. Metrics should support system improvement, not automatic discipline.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should monitor system health, not merely rank workers. It should support drill-down by shift, hour, activity, resource group, priority, complexity band, and WIP band.

| Indicator | Definition | Source / PM4Py method | Frequency | Alert / review condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Risk-adjusted event service time | Median or mean `event_service_minutes`, adjusted for complexity, priority, activity family, and case type. | `event_service_minutes`, `pm4py.get_service_time`, statistical adjustment | Hourly / shift | Sustained increase or decrease beyond control limits, especially when accompanied by error changes. |
| 2. Case waiting time | Waiting time before case work begins or between activities, segmented by priority and shift. | `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` | Hourly / daily | Priority breaches, rising waiting time for complex cases, or waiting spikes after shift handovers. |
| 3. WIP and queue pressure | Mean and share of events with `wip_at_start > 70`, mean `queue_length_at_start`, and sustained high-pressure duration. | `wip_at_start`, `queue_length_at_start`, `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` | Real-time / hourly | WIP > 70 for more than a defined duration, or rising queue length without corresponding finish rate. |
| 4. Risk-adjusted error rate | `error_flag` count per 100 events, adjusted for complexity, priority, WIP, shift, and activity. | `error_flag`, statistical model output | Shift / daily | Increase beyond baseline after adjusting for mix, not raw late-shift error rate alone. |
| 5. Rework within 7 days | Share of cases or events leading to `Rework Correction` within 7 days, including time to rework. | `rework_flag`, `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity`, path analysis | Daily / weekly | Rising rework after specific activities, resources, batches, shifts, or bot-assisted classifications. |
| 6. Complexity and priority mix | Mean complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, and first-hour complexity by shift. | `case_complexity_score`, `priority` | Shift / daily | Sudden changes in mix that could explain service or error changes. |
| 7. Batching indicator | Number of batches, average batch size, share of events in batches above threshold, and late-shift batch share. | `pm4py.discover_batches`, custom consecutive-event detection | Shift / daily | Large late-shift batches with short service times and elevated downstream error/rework. |
| 8. Quality Check delay | Time between Eligibility Decision or Risk Classification and Quality Check, especially in final shift hour. | Event timestamps, activity sequence, performance DFG | Shift / daily | Increasing QC delay, end-shift QC bursts, or delayed QC associated with later rework. |
| 9. Automation correction rate | Share of Bot_Risk classifications later corrected by humans, stratified by WIP and complexity. | `Bot_Risk`, `concept:name`, DFG paths, rework flags | Daily | Bot correction rate rises above threshold, especially under high WIP. |
| 10. Handover / queue ownership changes | Share of cases with resource or shift changes, especially cross-shift handovers and reassignments after waiting. | Case-level resource sequence, `shift_id`, `org:resource` | Shift / daily | Rising handover count, handover-related waiting, or rework after handover. |
| 11. Worker-load fairness | Distribution of event count, complexity-weighted workload, and high-WIP exposure across resources within a shift. | `org:resource`, `case_complexity_score`, `wip_at_start` | Shift / weekly | High inequality in complexity-weighted load or concentrated exposure to high WIP. |
| 12. Arrival-finish pressure | Ratio of case arrivals to finishes by shift and hour, indicating whether backlog is growing. | `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` | Hourly / shift | Arrival rate consistently exceeds finish rate, especially during Day-shift peak. |

Recommended dashboard views:

1. **Shift overview**: queue start/end, WIP, service time, error rate, rework, complexity mix, arrival-finish rate.
2. **Time-within-shift view**: sequence position bands and minutes since shift start, separated by WIP band.
3. **Pressure view**: error and rework rates for low, medium, and high WIP, with complexity stratification.
4. **Experience view**: senior, mid, junior, and night groups, showing adjusted error rates and workload fairness.
5. **Batching and QC view**: batch size distribution, late-shift batch share, QC delay, and correction bursts.
6. **Automation view**: Bot_Risk volume, correction rate, downstream rework, and interaction with queue pressure.
7. **Fairness and governance view**: confidence intervals, sample sizes, risk adjustment method, and exclusion criteria.

The dashboard should never use raw late-shift error rates as a direct individual performance score. It should instead identify where the system is producing risk: high WIP, poor handover design, delayed Quality Check, automation correction burdens, unfair complexity assignment, and insufficient senior support during demand peaks.
