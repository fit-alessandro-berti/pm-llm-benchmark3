## 1. Stress scenarios

The peak is a change in work content as well as volume: orders rose from about 2,300 to 3,300 per week, but Urgent orders rose from about 414 to 957 and Complex-panel orders from about 299 to 660. Routine orders stayed near 1,400 per week. Design against these workloads and the tails of cycle time, especially the 18-hour Urgent contract and 72-hour Routine target. At peak, Urgent median/p90 are 17/33 hours and Routine median/p90 are 62/120 hours; the medians alone conceal substantial exposure.

| Scenario | Plausible stress to test |
| --- | --- |
| S1. Winter mix shift | The observed peak mix (29% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel) persists or rises while total arrivals remain near 3,300/week. More orders need multiple assays and medical validation. |
| S2. Assay degradation or outage | M2 failure/repeat frequency rises after night shifts, or one machine loses a shift during a peak week. Effective assay capacity falls while repeats consume additional runs. |
| S3. Validator/reviewer absence | An evening medical validator is unavailable or the specialist's alternate-Friday absence coincides with high Complex-panel arrivals. Mandatory reviews cannot be skipped. |
| S4. Courier compression or delay | Routes converge at 10:00/17:00, or delayed specimens arrive together late in the day. The order-to-receipt clock grows before the laboratory can act. |

Also stress-test a compound day, such as S1 plus S2 plus S3. If projected Urgent demand exceeds qualified assay and validation capacity, a priority rule alone cannot meet every deadline; the contingency must explicitly allocate scarce capacity and escalate imminent breaches.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

- **S1:** The first queues form at intake/pre-analytical check (96% peak utilization, 9.5-hour median queue) and accession correction (91%, 14 hours). Clearing those queues sends work to preparation (11 hours), assays (98%, 18 hours), and technical validation (101%, 16 hours). The peak DFG shows more batch-wait entries and repeat-assay entries per week than normal; Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%. Accelerating preparation or imposing strict Urgent-first dispatch therefore moves congestion to machines and technical validation while Routine ages. More Complex-panel work then reaches medical validation (93%, 22 hours) and specialist review (36 hours); its 3.1-times medical-validation and 2.4-times specialist-review propensities make those queues especially sensitive to mix. A medical shift without upstream control can simply expose specialist or assay scarcity.
- **S2:** Lost M2 capacity or extra failed runs first expands the assay queue and the Repeat assay → Assay run loop (10 to 26 hours on that performance edge from normal to peak). Rerouting everything critical to M1 protects some deadlines but makes M1 the next bottleneck; assigning all Routine work to the faster, less reliable M2 can create still more repeats. Once machines recover, accumulated runs land at technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization, then at medical validation for Urgent and Complex-panel orders. Assay dispatch must include expected repeat work, not just first runs.
- **S3:** The medical queue already has a 22-hour peak median, longer than the entire Urgent contract, although that aggregate queue is not every Urgent patient's wait. Specialist review has a 36-hour peak queue, and Hold for specialist review → Medical validation grows from 18 to 48 hours. Protecting medical slots for Urgent can strand Complex-panel cases awaiting review; adding medical coverage may reveal the specialist as the constraint. Conversely, clearing specialist holds transfers a burst into medical validation and then release. Technical validation can also become the immediate constraint if its staff are borrowed for accession corrections.
- **S4:** The receipt-to-check performance edge expands from 2 to 9 hours and the registration-to-receipt edge from 5 to 8; courier intake's median queue rises to 7.5 hours. Compressed deliveries overload check and correction, causing recollections whose return edge is 60 hours at peak. Smoothing arrivals or automating registration moves the constraint to pre-analytical check; faster defect triage and corrected specimens then feed preparation, machines, and technical validation. Later pickup slots may smooth intake but consume the Urgent 18-hour budget before receipt, so the whole order-to-release clock governs routing.

These are directional inferences, not exact case probabilities: the DFG counts are transitions over periods of different length, include loops, and flatten multi-tube orders. Validate routing and workload with order-level timestamps, tube/assay counts, case type, machine, shift, and validator before fixing capacity shares. Do not add selected edge medians to estimate a case's total time.

## 3. Robust redesign package

1. **Triage quality early (I6), with a rapid recollection path.** Check specimen identity, viability, and metadata within two hours of *receipt*; notify the sender immediately and reserve intake capacity for returning specimens. At peak, pre-analytical recollection transitions are roughly 260/week versus 95/week normally, and the recollection return edge takes 60 hours. Fewer avoidable bad specimens protect every downstream resource. This depends on reliable, clinically valid rejection criteria and fast sender contact. It fails if premature rejection increases recollections or the returning specimens swamp intake; track both rates. Provide accession correction help, but do not routinely move I4's two technical validators from a station already at 101% utilization. Train them as relief in advance and redeploy only with measured technical headroom.
2. **Smooth arrivals and control work release (I7 plus a preparation-to-assay WIP limit).** Negotiate staggered routine courier slots around the 10:00/17:00 spikes while preserving fast Urgent pickup. At preparation, release work against available machine and downstream validator slots rather than filling every bench whenever it is free. This reduces simultaneous queues across scenarios and avoids accumulating incomplete work. It depends on visibility of courier ETA, machine status, assay minutes, and validator rosters. It can fail if a rescheduled pickup worsens order-to-receipt time or a WIP limit delays a time-critical case; Urgent cases retain an explicit exception. The next constraints after successful smoothing are assay and technical validation.
3. **Use deadline-aware, capacity-protected dispatch rather than strict I1.** Schedule by remaining time to contractual/clinical deadline and expected assay/validation workload. Reserve an adjustable Urgent lane through preparation, assay, technical, and medical validation, but guarantee bounded service to aging Routine and Complex-panel work. Release unused reserved slots promptly. Routine batches may expand from 12 toward 24 (I2) only when a maximum wait and machine/validator capacity check are satisfied; Urgent bypass stays available and is measured. This depends on accurate case labels and predicted work, not equal priority for every class. It can fail when Urgent demand alone consumes capacity; then trigger contingency rather than silently starving other classes. Larger batches or bypass bursts otherwise overload assays and technical validation.
4. **Route by reliability and end-to-end workload, not a permanent machine split.** Use M1 preferentially for deadline-critical or repeat-sensitive runs when justified by validated assay compatibility, but retain qualified M2 work for all appropriate case types and set a repeat-rate gate. Do not adopt I3's blanket assignment of all Routine volume to M2. A faster run with more repeats can reduce effective capacity. Machine recovery or improved first-pass yield moves the queue to technical validation, so reserve corresponding validation slots. This assumes reliable per-machine failure and runtime data and can fail if M1 becomes a single point of failure.
5. **Automate only complete registration (I8) and preserve immediate release.** Straight-through registration can reduce clerical effort and overtime errors, but maintain exception checks and pace its rollout against check and assay capacity. Do not adopt I9's routine release holds: they create a new queue after validation and endanger Routine's 72-hour target without improving upstream flow. More registered work can overload pre-analytical check; more completed validations can overload result release, so monitor both.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Use the following as provisional shift-level triggers, calibrated against observed assay and validation minutes. Switch off only after the recovery condition holds for two consecutive shifts to avoid oscillation.

| Scenario | Activate and act | Deactivate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 mix surge | If Urgent plus Complex-panel reaches about 45% of the next-day forecast (normal 31%, observed peak 49%) **and** predicted assay or technical load exceeds 90%, tighten the preparation WIP limit, protect Urgent slots across the full path, cap Routine batch wait, and staff the bottleneck rather than simply accelerating intake. Review Routine age and Complex-panel holds each shift. | Mix below 40%, projected bottleneck load below 85%, and Urgent/Routine aging queues falling. |
| S2 machine trouble | If a machine fails QC, loses a shift, or rolling repeat-assay events per 100 starts exceed 1.5 times that machine's normal shift baseline, stop using the affected setup, reroute compatible work, reduce routine batch releases, and reserve machine/technical slots for deadline-critical repeats. Use M2 again only after QC and first-pass yield recover. | Machine available and repeat rate within normal band for two shifts, with assay queue below 8 hours. |
| S3 validator/reviewer gap | If a qualified absence is known or projected medical wait exceeds 8 hours for Urgent work or specialist wait exceeds 12 hours, activate I5's temporary qualified evening medical shift where it actually clears the constraint; arrange qualified specialist cover for the Friday gap. Schedule review-dependent Complex-panel work against both specialist and medical slots. Use I10 for eligible Complex-panel overflow only when predicted local completion exceeds partner completion *including* its 12-hour transport, with confirmed sample integrity, traceability, turnaround, and result integration. | Coverage restored, Urgent projected deadlines safe, and medical/specialist queues below 4/12 hours for two shifts; stop new outsourcing once the local route is faster. |
| S4 courier bunching | If planned arrivals exceed 1.5 times available hourly intake for two hours, or projected receipt-to-check wait exceeds 4 hours (normal edge 2, peak 9), stagger Routine pickups, call in trained intake relief, open an Urgent receiving lane, and invoke the two-hour defect triage. If courier delay has already used much of an Urgent deadline, expedite receipt and downstream slots together. | Two arrival waves within intake capacity and receipt-to-check wait below 3 hours, without rising order-to-receipt time. |

On a compound day, rank work by remaining deadline and clinical risk, declare the expected breach count early, seek temporary qualified capacity or validated partner routing, and explicitly preserve some service for aged Routine and Complex-panel work. Do not label all work Urgent.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- A two-machine assay pool has little spare peak capacity (98% reported utilization), and technical validation is already over capacity (101%). A prolonged outage or simultaneous Urgent surge and absence may make some deadlines infeasible even with optimal sequencing. Escalation and an explicit, clinically governed priority order remain necessary.
- Specialist review has a single-person absence pattern and a 36-hour peak queue; an evening medical shift cannot replace specialist expertise. Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure tails may remain long (peak p90 210 and 240 hours) until their causes and review capacity are addressed.
- The redesign creates its own risk: reserved Urgent slots, preparation WIP caps, and larger Routine batches can strand Routine or Complex-panel work, raise total WIP, or hide imminent 72-hour breaches. Monitor aging by class and release unused protected capacity quickly. Outsourcing introduces transport loss, custody, and result-reconciliation failure modes; automation can move defects and congestion to check and assay.
- Aggregate, flattened logs cannot reveal tube-level machine minutes, concurrent assays, exact Urgent breach rate, or whether a long edge belongs disproportionately to one variant. Set numerical capacity shares only after validating these joins and measuring service times by type and resource.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Review by shift during peaks, with an hourly Urgent view. The bands below are starting triggers, not claims of measured service capacity.

| Leading indicator | Warning band and action |
| --- | --- |
| Next 24-hour arrivals by type, required assay minutes, and qualified validator minutes versus available minutes | Amber at >85% of any pool; red at >95% or a known absence/outage. Show Urgent + Complex-panel share; around 45% prompts S1 review. |
| Projected order-to-release time and remaining slack by class | Red for any Urgent order projected beyond 18 hours; amber when <4 hours of slack remain. Flag Routine orders projected beyond 72 hours and aged Complex-panel/Repeat orders for review. |
| Queue age and WIP at receipt/check, correction, preparation/assay, technical, medical, and specialist review | Amber when a queue exceeds about twice its normal median; red if assay or technical waits exceed 12 hours, medical Urgent wait exceeds 8 hours, or specialist wait exceeds 12 hours. Display class-specific oldest age, not only averages. |
| Courier arrival concentration and order-to-receipt / receipt-to-check time | Amber above planned hourly intake or >4-hour predicted receipt-to-check wait; red at >1.5 times intake for two hours or rising Urgent order-to-receipt time. |
| First-pass sample acceptance, corrections/recollections, machine uptime, and repeat events per 100 assay starts by machine and shift | Amber when defect or repeat rate rises above its comparable normal shift baseline; red at >1.5 times baseline or failed QC. Track returning specimens because triage can generate new arrivals. |
| Protected-lane use and downstream handoffs | Flag falling Urgent batch bypass (74% normal versus 58% peak), unused reserved slots, growing Routine/Complex-panel age, or a cleared upstream queue paired with rising next-station WIP. |

Lagging outcomes, reviewed daily but not used alone to dispatch work, are the actual Urgent >18-hour breach rate, Routine >72-hour breach rate, cycle-time p90 by type, overall rework (7.5% normal versus 11.8% peak), and the share with a queue over 12 hours (22% versus 54%). Their movement tests whether the leading triggers and policy switches are improving the whole path rather than just one station.
