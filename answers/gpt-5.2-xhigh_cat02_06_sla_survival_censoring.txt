# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

## Time origin, horizon, and observed data structure

- **Time origin**: \(t=0\) at `Submit` for every case.
- **SLA horizon**: \(H = 72\) hours elapsed time since `Submit`.
- **Terminal outcomes observed in-system**: `resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, `transferred`.
- **Administrative right-censoring at freeze**: `open` cases are observed up to the common cutoff; `duration_or_age_h` is their age at cutoff.

Let \(T\) be the (unobserved) time from `Submit` to the *first* terminal outcome observed by the internal system, and \(J \in \{\text{Resolve}, \text{Withdraw}, \text{Reject}, \text{Transfer}\}\) its type. Let \(C\) be the administrative cutoff time for a case. We observe:

- \(Y = \min(T, C)\) as `duration_or_age_h`
- \(\Delta = \mathbb{1}[T \le C]\) (`open` implies \(\Delta=0\))
- \(J\) when \(\Delta=1\), otherwise undefined.

## Two primary estimands (plus one complementary tail estimand)

### Estimand E1 (customer-success SLA): probability of **not being internally resolved by 72h**

Define the cumulative incidence function (competing risks) for internal resolution:

\[
F_R(t) = \Pr(T \le t, J=\text{Resolve})
\]

Then:

\[
p_{\text{NR72}} = \Pr(\text{not resolved internally by }H) = 1 - F_R(H)
\]

Interpretation: a submitter-facing SLA for “resolved (internally) within 72h,” where `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and (depending on definition) `Transfer` can prevent internal resolution.

### Estimand E2 (workload tail risk): probability of **still having no terminal disposition at 72h**

Let \(T_D\) be time to first *terminal disposition* under a chosen definition of “disposition” (e.g., any internal terminal outcome; or excluding `Transfer` if transfer means “lost to follow-up”). Then:

\[
p_{\text{NoDisp72}} = \Pr(T_D > H)
\]

Interpretation: internal workload/backlog and “still active” tail risk at 72h.

### Complementary tail estimand E3 (late-success burden): cumulative incidence of **resolution after 72h**

For any \(\tau > H\) within follow-up:

\[
p_{\text{LateResolve}}(\tau) = \Pr(H < T \le \tau, J=\text{Resolve}) = F_R(\tau) - F_R(H)
\]

Interpretation: how much “successful work” occurs in the SLA tail (useful for penalty exposure, reputational risk, and sizing “late queue” volume).

## How specific cases contribute (examples required by prompt)

Below, “contributes” means how the case is encoded in the risk set up to \(H=72\), and whether the SLA statement is already determined at 72h.

- **Resolved at 76h** (e.g., case `S03`, `duration_or_age_h`=76, `observed_status`=`resolved`):
  - **E1**: event type Resolve at \(t=76\). At \(t=72\), it is *known* not resolved yet, so it contributes to \(p_{\text{NR72}}\) as a breach at 72. For estimating \(F_R(72)\), it stays in the risk set through 72 and does not count as a resolution event by 72.
  - **E2**: has *no terminal disposition by 72* (terminal happens at 76), so it contributes to \(p_{\text{NoDisp72}}\) as active at 72.
  - **E3**: counts as a late-resolution event in \((72, \tau]\) for any \(\tau \ge 76\).

- **Open at 92h** (e.g., case `S09`, age 92, `observed_status`=`open`):
  - **Right censoring** at \(C=92\).
  - **E1**: since \(C>72\) and no event occurred by 92, it is *known* not resolved by 72, so it contributes to \(p_{\text{NR72}}\) as a breach at 72; it contributes to estimation as “event-free” through 72 and censored at 92.
  - **E2**: since \(C>72\), it is *known* to have no terminal disposition by 72 and contributes to \(p_{\text{NoDisp72}}\) as active at 72 (censored later).
  - **E3**: no resolution observed; it contributes only through risk-set membership and censoring.

- **Withdrawn at 27h** (e.g., case `S06`, 27h, `observed_status`=`withdrawn`):
  - **E1**: competing event Withdraw at \(t=27\). It is definitively “not resolved by 72,” but importantly it exits the risk set at 27 and should not be treated as non-informative censoring unless the estimand is explicitly hypothetical.
  - **E2**: terminal disposition at 27, so it does **not** contribute to being active at 72.
  - **E3**: not a resolution; contributes as a competing outcome.

- **Transferred at 68h** (e.g., case `S08`, 68h, `observed_status`=`transferred`):
  - **E1/E2 depend on how `Transfer` is conceptualized** (see Section 2). At minimum, it is *not internally resolved by 72*; whether it is treated as a competing event, a censoring event, or a “successful handoff” changes the estimated SLA-tail risk materially.


# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The core distinction:

- **Right censoring**: we stop observing the case, but (under the model) the event process could have continued; if censoring is independent given covariates, standard estimators are consistent.
- **Competing events**: events that *change the meaning* of “time to resolution” because they preclude internal resolution (e.g., `Reject` or `Withdraw`).
- **Already-observed breaches**: any case with \(Y>72\) and no resolution before 72 is deterministically a breach-at-72 for estimands that ask about resolution by 72.

## Status-to-encoding map

Event type codes below are for a competing-risk representation where the event-of-interest is internal resolution (code 1). Censoring is code 0.

| observed_status | Primary interpretation in this extract | Competing-risk code (E1: resolve CIF) | Censoring rule | E2 (“no disposition by 72”) event? | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | internal success observed | 1 (Resolve) at `duration_or_age_h` | none | yes (terminal) | also split into on-time vs late (\(\le 72\) vs \(>72\)) for reporting; not a different event, just a horizon-based summary |
| `withdrawn` | terminal internal exit chosen by customer/agent | 2 (Withdraw) at `duration_or_age_h` | none | yes (terminal) | if the operational question is “time to any closure,” treat as “success” for that estimand; if the question is “would it have resolved if not withdrawn,” treat as censoring (hypothetical, strong assumption) |
| `rejected` | terminal internal exit (screened out) | 3 (Reject) at `duration_or_age_h` | none | yes (terminal) | same logic as `withdrawn`; notably, all observed rejects here are P3 (e.g., `S05`, `S14`, `S23`), so handling affects priority-stratified comparisons |
| `open` | not terminal by cutoff; right-censored | 0 (censored) at `duration_or_age_h` | administrative censor at cutoff age | no (censored) | treat as potentially informative censoring using `last_event_gap_h`, stage-at-cutoff (last activity in prefix), and priority; bound analyses (best/worst case for breach) |
| `transferred` | case leaves internal visibility; external outcome unknown | **depends** (see below) | depends | depends | must be treated under multiple scenarios because it is plausibly informative and changes the estimand |

## Three plausible treatments of `transferred` (must be explicit)

Let \(t_{\text{tr}}\) be the time of transfer.

1. **T1: Transfer as a competing terminal outcome (internal failure to resolve)**  
   - **Encoding**: event type 4 (Transfer) at \(t_{\text{tr}}\).  
   - **Use when**: SLA is strictly “internal resolution,” and transfer is operationally a handoff that ends internal responsibility but does not count as “resolved.”  
   - **Implication**: `S08` (transfer at 68) is definitively not resolved by 72 and removes probability mass from internal resolution.

2. **T2: Transfer as right censoring (loss to follow-up for eventual resolution)**  
   - **Encoding**: censor at \(t_{\text{tr}}\) (code 0).  
   - **Use when**: the estimand is “time to ultimate resolution anywhere,” but the internal log stops at transfer; or when you treat transfer as missing outcome.  
   - **Risk**: likely **informative censoring** here because all transfers are P1 and escalated (`S08`, `S21`, `S32`), suggesting transfer is not random.

3. **T3: Transfer counts as SLA-satisfying “disposition” (success-as-handoff)**  
   - **Encoding**: treat transfer as the event-of-interest for an alternative success definition (Resolve-or-Transfer) at \(t_{\text{tr}}\).  
   - **Use when**: governance defines SLA as “provide a definitive disposition/handoff within 72h,” not necessarily internal resolution.  
   - **Implication**: materially changes breach rates, especially when transfers occur early (e.g., `S21` at 26h).

These are different *estimands*, not just different estimators. The analysis should report them side-by-side rather than selecting one silently.


# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Completed-case analysis typically means “look only at cases with `resolved` and compute the share with duration \(\le 72\) or the mean/median resolution time.” That is biased for every operational interpretation requested here.

## Survivor / selection bias from dropping `open`

- Cases `S09` (open at 92), `S11` (open at 81), `S25` (open at 79), and `S26` (open at 104) are **already known** to be unresolved at 72. Excluding them mechanically **understates tail risk** and overstates on-time performance.
- Even open cases below 72 (e.g., `S10` at 47, `S15` at 35, `S31` at 23) are not “easy cases”; they are **right-censored** before the SLA horizon, so their eventual SLA status is unknown and must be handled via risk sets rather than deletion.

## Competing risks are not ordinary censoring

- `S05` is rejected at 9h, `S23` rejected at 11h, `S06` withdrawn at 27h. If you treat these as “censored” in a Kaplan–Meier time-to-resolve curve, you implicitly assume they *could have* resolved later with the same distribution as those still at risk. That is a different hypothetical estimand and generally inflates estimated resolution probability.
- Competing risks are also **priority-dependent** here: all observed rejections are P3, while transfers are all P1. Dropping or censoring them can create artificial differences across priority groups.

## Transfer is both ambiguous and plausibly informative

- Transfers (`S08`, `S21`, `S32`) occur only for P1 and only when `escalated`=yes, suggesting transfer is triggered by severity/complexity. Treating transfer as non-informative censoring (T2) can bias any estimate of “ultimate resolution” or “internal resolution” unless adjusted (and even then requires assumptions).

## Cohort comparisons are confounded and completion-biased

- Cohort B starts after a policy change, but assignment is not randomized and compliance incomplete. Cohort B also contains more P1 cases (5 vs 3 in A) and more transfer events (2 vs 1). Comparing “resolved-case SLA compliance” between A and B conflates:
  - different case mix by priority,
  - different competing-risk mix (reject/withdraw/transfer),
  - different censoring patterns (more open in B),
  - and potentially different logging behavior.

## “Open below 72” vs “open above 72” are qualitatively different

- `S10` (open at 47) is *not yet* a known SLA breach at 72; its contribution is censored before horizon.
- `S09` (open at 92) is a **certain** breach-at-72 for any “resolved by 72” statement (because it is observed event-free well past 72). Mixing these without survival encoding misstates both current backlog and SLA compliance.


# 4. Statistical analysis plan

This dataset provides only one time per case (time to terminal outcome, or age at cutoff). That supports strong analyses for **absorbing competing risks**, and weaker (proposal-only) analyses for within-process stage durations (because intermediate timestamps are missing).

## Method comparison (what each answers, and pitfalls here)

- **Kaplan–Meier (KM)**:
  - Good for: time-to-*single* event when other outcomes are truly non-informative censoring, or for time-to-“any terminal disposition” if disposition is a single composite event.
  - Pitfall here: if used for time-to-resolve while treating `withdrawn`/`rejected`/`transferred` as censoring, it estimates a hypothetical “resolution if competing risks did not happen,” not the actual probability of being resolved by 72.

- **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence (AJ/CIF)**:
  - Good for: real-world probability of each terminal outcome over time under competing risks; directly yields \(F_R(72)\) and \(F_R(\tau)-F_R(72)\).
  - Strength here: matches E1 and E3 without dropping open cases, and makes competing risks explicit.

- **Cause-specific hazards models (e.g., Cox for each cause)**:
  - Good for: covariate effects on the instantaneous risk of a cause among those still event-free.
  - Caveat: small sample (32) and sparse events by subgroup; can still be used descriptively with stratification (priority) and minimal covariates (cohort), but uncertainty will be large.

- **Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray)**:
  - Good for: covariate effects directly on CIF for the event-of-interest (often closer to “risk by 72” messaging).
  - Caveat: same sample-size issue; also depends on a chosen definition of transfer (T1/T2/T3).

- **Restricted mean time (RMST)**:
  - Good for: “expected hours unresolved up to 72” or “expected hours without disposition up to 72,” robust summary of tail burden.
  - Advantage here: avoids proportional hazards assumptions and is interpretable for workload.

- **Landmarking**:
  - Needed for: using post-start process events (e.g., `Escalate`) without immortal-time bias. Define a landmark \(\tau\) (e.g., 24h or 48h), condition on being active at \(\tau\), and model risk of breach from \(\tau\) to 72 given information available by \(\tau\).
  - Limitation here: requires timestamps of when `Escalate` happened; not available in this extract.

- **Multi-state model**:
  - Best for: stage hazards (e.g., Review→Escalate, Escalate→Resolve) and time spent in each stage; aligns with “process-aware” framing.
  - Limitation here: intermediate event times are not supplied, so transition times cannot be estimated; only a coarse absorbing competing-risk view is identifiable.

## Recommended primary and secondary analyses for these data

### Primary (for SLA tail risk reporting): nonparametric competing-risks CIFs (Aalen–Johansen)

- Encode \((Y, J)\) with `resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and *one of the three transfer treatments* (T1/T2/T3).
- Estimate CIFs for all causes and report:
  - \(F_R(72)\) (resolved by 72),
  - \(1 - F_R(72)\) (not resolved by 72, E1),
  - \(F_R(\tau) - F_R(72)\) for \(\tau\) at the largest credible follow-up (late resolution burden, E3),
  - plus CIFs of withdrawal/rejection/transfer by 72 for governance insight.
- Stratify descriptively by `priority` and `cohort`; given small \(n\), emphasize uncertainty intervals (e.g., Greenwood-type or bootstrap) rather than point estimates alone.

### Secondary (for workload operations): KM/RMST for time to “disposition” + sensitivity to transfer definition

- Define disposition event as:
  - (a) any internal terminal including transfer (internal workload closure), and
  - (b) excluding transfer if transfer means “lost to follow-up” (customer waiting for final outcome).
- Use KM to estimate \(p_{\text{NoDisp72}}\) and RMST up to 72.
- Compare cohort/priority strata to identify where “still active near/after 72” risk concentrates.


# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Even with only total time-to-terminal, prefixes provide **process-aware stratification** and suggest a **multi-state design** once timestamps are available.

## Features derivable from `prefix` and other fields

### Baseline (available at \(t=0\))

- `cohort` (A vs B) and `priority` (P1/P2/P3).

### Near-baseline / early-path (available after the first few events; less leakage than full path)

- First-step routing: whether the path starts with `Submit>AutoCheck` (common in cohort B) vs `Submit>Screen` (common in cohort A).
- Early automation presence: whether `AutoCheck` occurs before the first `Review` in the observed sequence (still post-start, but could be used in a landmark design).

### Time-varying or post-start (must not be treated as baseline without time-aware design)

- `Escalate` occurrence (`escalated` flag / presence in prefix): can only happen if the case survives long enough; baseline use creates **immortal-time bias**.
- `Rework` loop and repeated `Review` (e.g., `S07`, `S13`, `S27`): suggests complexity and tail risk, but occurs during execution.
- Current stage at time \(t\): e.g., being in `Review` vs `Escalate`. This is a state variable for a multi-state/landmark model, but requires event timestamps.

### Potentially informative censoring / logging quality signals

- `last_event_gap_h` for open cases: large gaps (e.g., `S26` has 72h) indicate long inactivity and may correlate with eventual transfer/withdraw/late resolution, violating independent censoring assumptions if “open at cutoff” is treated as non-informative.

### Leakage-prone (should be avoided for prospective prediction)

- Using the *full* prefix including terminal activities (`Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`, `Transfer`) to predict SLA breach is tautological leakage.
- Even non-terminal late-occurring activities (e.g., `Rework`) leak if the prediction time is earlier than their occurrence.

## Proposed state-transition diagram (coarse, time-stamped version recommended)

Textual multi-state sketch consistent with observed prefixes:

- Start: `Submit`
- Early routing: `Submit` → `Screen` OR `Submit` → `AutoCheck`
- `Screen` → `AutoCheck` OR `Screen` → `Review` OR `Screen` → `Withdraw` OR `Screen` → `Reject` OR `Screen` → `Escalate`
- `AutoCheck` → `Screen` OR `AutoCheck` → `Review` OR `AutoCheck` → `Escalate`
- `Review` → `Rework` → `Review` (loop possible) OR `Review` → `Escalate` OR `Review` → `Resolve` OR `Review` → `Withdraw` OR `Review` → `Reject`
- `Escalate` → `Review` OR `Escalate` → `Resolve` OR `Escalate` → `Transfer`
- Absorbing terminal states: `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`
- Administrative censoring: `Open@cutoff` (right censoring)

With timestamps per activity, one can estimate transition hazards (e.g., Escalate→Resolve) and state-occupation probabilities at 72h (directly answering “still in review/escalation at SLA”).


# 6. Sensitivity analysis

Given ambiguity and likely informative censoring, conclusions should be stress-tested across multiple plausible encodings. At minimum:

## Sensitivity A: transfer treatment (T1 vs T2 vs T3)

- **A1 (T1 competing)**: transfer is a competing terminal outcome (internal non-resolution).
- **A2 (T2 censor)**: transfer is right censoring for ultimate resolution.
- **A3 (T3 success-as-handoff)**: transfer is treated as SLA-satisfying success for a disposition-based SLA.

Potential reversal: whether cohort B “improved SLA” could flip depending on whether transfer is treated as failure (T1) or success (T3), since transfers occur only in P1 and appear in both cohorts.

## Sensitivity B: handling withdrawal/rejection (competing vs censor vs “closure success”)

Compare:

- **B1**: withdrawal/rejection as competing outcomes (real-world “resolved by 72”).
- **B2**: withdrawal/rejection as censoring (hypothetical “time to resolve if not withdrawn/rejected”).
- **B3**: withdrawal/rejection counted as “closure” (time to any terminal decision; aligns with certain customer-service SLAs).

Potential reversal: priority comparisons can change because rejections are concentrated in P3.

## Sensitivity C: informative censoring for `open`

Because open cases differ systematically (and have large `last_event_gap_h`), compare:

- **C1**: assume independent right censoring (baseline AJ/KM).
- **C2**: IPCW-style adjustment where censoring depends on baseline variables and process-at-cutoff proxies (priority, cohort, last observed stage, `last_event_gap_h`).
- **C3**: bounding:
  - best case for SLA: all cases censored before 72 are resolved by 72;
  - worst case: all cases censored before 72 are not resolved by 72.

Potential reversal: any statement about the *level* of \(p_{\text{NR72}}\) is sensitive when multiple cases are censored before 72 (e.g., `S10`, `S15`, `S31`).

## Sensitivity D: cohort-policy effects under confounding and incomplete compliance

- **D1**: stratify by priority and compare cohorts within strata.
- **D2**: standardize cohort comparisons to a common priority distribution.
- **D3**: treat cohort as an imperfect proxy for policy exposure; if compliance data exists, compare intention-to-treat (cohort) vs per-protocol (actual automation/escalation usage) carefully, recognizing post-treatment bias risks.


# 7. Operational interpretation

## Matching estimands to decisions

- **SLA governance / reporting**:
  - Use **E1** (not resolved by 72) with explicit competing-risk handling; report CIF of resolve by 72 plus CIFs of reject/withdraw/transfer by 72 so stakeholders see *why* resolution is not achieved.
  - Use **E3** (late-resolution incidence) to quantify “how much success happens late,” which drives penalty exposure and continuous improvement targets.

- **Workload management / staffing**:
  - Use **E2** (no disposition by 72) to estimate the fraction of cases expected to remain active at SLA, which is directly actionable for staffing buffers and escalation queues.

## Turning survival outputs into actions (without treating them as causal effects)

- **Alerts**:
  - Operationally, you want \( \Pr(\text{breach by }72 \mid \text{still active at time }t, \text{current stage info}) \). This is obtained from conditional survival/CIF in a landmark or multi-state model, not from leakage-prone full-path features.
  - In this extract, `last_event_gap_h` suggests an “inactivity” signal; with timestamps, one can trigger alerts when inactivity exceeds a threshold (e.g., “no event for 24h and already in Review”).

- **Work allocation**:
  - Convert group-level estimates into expected counts: sum breach probabilities over active cases to forecast how many will exceed SLA within the next window.
  - Separate lanes by priority: P1 transfers/escalations indicate different pathways than P3 quick rejects; governance should avoid “gaming” SLA by rejecting quickly if the SLA is meant for customer outcomes.

- **Calibration and monitoring**:
  - If producing risk scores (even descriptive), calibrate predicted vs observed breach rates at 72 using time-dependent calibration (e.g., within landmark cohorts).
  - Track calibration separately by **priority** and **cohort** (required here), since both affect competing risks and censoring.

- **Fairness checks by priority and cohort**:
  - Check whether model or policy changes produce systematically different error rates or calibration drift across P1/P2/P3 and A/B (e.g., underpredicting breach risk in P1 where transfers are common).
  - Report not only “resolution CIF” but also “transfer CIF” by group to ensure escalations/transfers are not disproportionately applied without visibility.

All such outputs are **predictive/operational**, not causal: cohort differences should be framed as associations unless a defensible identification strategy exists.

# 2) Encode at least two estimands and three transfer-treatment scenarios
transfer_scenarios = ["competing", "censor", "success"]  # T1, T2, T3

def encode_competing(df_in: pd.DataFrame, transfer_mode: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # Event types: 0=censor, 1=success (Resolve, or Resolve+Transfer under 'success'), 2=Withdraw, 3=Reject, 4=Transfer
    if transfer_mode == "competing":
        m = {"resolved":1, "withdrawn":2, "rejected":3, "transferred":4, "open":0}
    elif transfer_mode == "censor":
        m = {"resolved":1, "withdrawn":2, "rejected":3, "transferred":0, "open":0}
    elif transfer_mode == "success":
        # Alternative estimand: SLA satisfied by Resolve OR Transfer (handoff counts)
        m = {"resolved":1, "withdrawn":2, "rejected":3, "transferred":1, "open":0}
    else:
        raise ValueError("unknown transfer_mode")
    out = df_in.copy()
    out["event_type"] = out["observed_status"].map(m).astype(int)
    return out

def aalen_johansen_cif(df_enc: pd.DataFrame, event_of_interest: int = 1, horizon: float = SLA_H) -> float:
    # Nonparametric CIF estimate with right censoring and competing risks
    d = df_enc[["time","event_type"]].copy().sort_values("time")
    event_times = np.sort(d.loc[d["event_type"] != 0, "time"].unique())
    S = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        if t > horizon:
            break
        n_risk = (d["time"] >= t).sum()
        if n_risk == 0:
            break
        at_t = (d["time"] == t)
        d_all = (at_t & (d["event_type"] != 0)).sum()
        d_k = (at_t & (d["event_type"] == event_of_interest)).sum()
        cif += S * (d_k / n_risk)
        S *= (1.0 - d_all / n_risk)
    return float(cif)

def kaplan_meier_survival(df_time_event: pd.DataFrame, horizon: float = SLA_H) -> float:
    # KM survival for a single event indicator (event=1) with right censoring (event=0)
    d = df_time_event[["time","event"]].copy().sort_values("time")
    event_times = np.sort(d.loc[d["event"] == 1, "time"].unique())
    S = 1.0
    for t in event_times:
        if t > horizon:
            break
        n_risk = (d["time"] >= t).sum()
        if n_risk == 0:
            break
        d_events = ((d["time"] == t) & (d["event"] == 1)).sum()
        S *= (1.0 - d_events / n_risk)
    return float(S)

def encode_disposition(df_in: pd.DataFrame, transfer_mode: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # Estimand E2: time to "disposition". If transfer_mode == 'censor', treat transfer as loss-to-follow-up (not a disposition).
    out = df_in.copy()
    if transfer_mode == "censor":
        terminal = {"resolved", "withdrawn", "rejected"}  # transfer excluded
    else:
        terminal = {"resolved", "withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"}
    out["event"] = out["observed_status"].isin(terminal).astype(int)
    return out

def summarize(df_sub: pd.DataFrame, transfer_mode: str):
    enc = encode_competing(df_sub, transfer_mode)
    cif_success72 = aalen_johansen_cif(enc, event_of_interest=1, horizon=SLA_H)
    p_not_success72 = 1.0 - cif_success72
    disp = encode_disposition(df_sub, transfer_mode)
    p_no_disp72 = kaplan_meier_survival(disp[["time","event"]], horizon=SLA_H)
    return cif_success72, p_not_success72, p_no_disp72

# 9. Limitations and additional data

## Key limitations of this extract

- **No intermediate event timestamps**: prefixes give order but not when `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, or `Rework` happened. This blocks true stage-duration estimation, time-varying covariates, and multi-state hazard modeling beyond a coarse absorbing outcome model.
- **Ambiguity of SLA definition**: whether SLA is “internal resolve,” “any disposition,” or “handoff/transfer counts” must be specified; otherwise results are not comparable.
- **Transfer outcomes missing**: without eventual external outcomes after `Transfer`, any “ultimate resolution” analysis is underidentified; treating transfer as censoring requires strong assumptions and likely violates independence.
- **Single cutoff snapshot**: without calendar times (submit date, cutoff date), non-stationarity (policy rollout, staffing, demand) cannot be modeled.
- **Potential informative censoring**: open cases with large `last_event_gap_h` may differ systematically from those that terminate; standard KM/AJ assume independent censoring conditional on included covariates.
- **Cohort policy not randomized + incomplete compliance**: cohort comparisons are associative; causal statements require a design (e.g., interrupted time series with dates, or compliance instrumentation).
- **Small sample size (32 cases)**: subgroup analyses by cohort × priority × outcome are sparse; uncertainty should be emphasized.

## Highest-value additional data to collect next

1. **Full event log with timestamps for every activity** (at least `Submit`, `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`, terminal events) to enable landmarking, multi-state models, and time-varying covariates.
2. **External outcomes after transfer**: whether and when cases resolve externally, plus linkage keys.
3. **Explicit SLA policy definitions**: whether transfer stops the clock, whether rejection/withdrawal count as closure, and which cases are in-scope.
4. **Calendar dates and operational context**: submit timestamps, cutoff timestamp, staffing/workload metrics, backlog levels, and demand shocks.
5. **Policy rollout compliance instrumentation**: per-case indicators of whether automated screening/escalation rules were applied, overrides, and reasons.
6. **Case-type/complexity covariates**: categories, required documents, customer segment, and known risk factors that drive both escalation/transfer and time-to-resolve.
