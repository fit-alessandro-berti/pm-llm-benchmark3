# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is not a single learning curve, fatigue curve, or queue-pressure effect. It is a layered process. Early in a shift, observed service time is high partly because the first cases are systematically harder and more likely to be carryover or urgent backlog: sequence 1–5 has mean complexity 0.72, high-priority share 41%, and evidence that Seniors take complex cases early after weekend or night backlog. Some warm-up is nevertheless plausible: later events at the same resource are faster, and the 16–30 bin has both lower service time and lower error rate than the first bin. But learning is entangled with case-mix stabilization: as complexity drops from 0.72 toward 0.54, service time would be expected to fall even without learning.

Queue pressure explains the dangerous part of the pattern better than simple fatigue. Under high WIP, service time is shorter rather than longer—9.4 minutes for low-complexity high-WIP cases versus 12.7 minutes at low WIP—while error rates rise, especially for high-complexity cases at high WIP. Late in the shift, queue length reaches 88 and sequence 76+ shows the shortest mean service time but the highest error and rework. This is consistent with a speed-accuracy trade-off: workers appear to move faster under load, but they may shortcut, batch, defer Quality Check, rely on or correct automation under pressure, or postpone rework-triggering checks. The fact that errors rise only in some conditions—high WIP, high complexity, Junior/Mid resources, late Day demand peaks—argues against a universal fatigue interpretation.

Fatigue and circadian effects may still contribute, but they should be treated as modifiers rather than the sole cause. Day shift shows the highest last-two-hour error rate, Night handles urgent exceptions, and Quality Check is delayed to end of shift. These are consistent with fatigue, time-of-day, and accumulated queue pressure. However, later low-WIP events do not show the same error escalation, and workers speed up rather than slow down under high WIP. That means the dominant late-shift mechanism is likely workload-driven adaptation, with fatigue/circadian effects amplifying risk when WIP and complexity are already high.

Batching and automation interactions further complicate the picture. Junior_H and Junior_I batch Document Review late in Day with very short service times, which can make throughput look better while creating hidden quality risk. Bot_Risk performs Risk Classification instantly, but human corrections increase when queue > 70, shifting work downstream. Quality Check bursts in the last 45 minutes may represent deferred corrections rather than genuine improvement in throughput. Overall, service time falls because of a mixture of warming up, lower late-shift complexity, queue-pressure speed-up, batching, and automation; errors rise because high WIP and complexity overwhelm quality controls, especially for less experienced resources, while seniors preserve quality better. The pattern is not “workers learn and then get fatigued”; it is a dynamic interaction between case selection, queue pressure, work design, automation, batching, and limited human capacity.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Statistical and process-mining design**

- Model event-level service time and binary outcomes separately. Use mixed-effects or fixed-effects models with random intercepts for resource, shift, and case, or cluster-robust standard errors. Include splines for minutes since shift start and clock hour, WIP/queue at start, complexity, priority, activity family, resource experience months, shift ID, batch flag, and bot-handled flag. Test interactions: resource group × WIP, resource group × minutes since shift start, complexity × WIP, and late-shift × queue pressure.
- For within-resource comparisons, de-mean variables within resource or use resource fixed effects. Compare early vs later events within the same resource, activity, shift, complexity band, and WIP band. This reduces selection bias from seniors taking complex cases early.
- For within-shift position effects, fit nonlinear splines or piecewise models rather than assuming linear learning/fatigue. Test separate effects for first 5 events, 6–15, 16–30, 31–50, 51–75, and 76+, and test whether WIP > 70 creates a threshold.
- Stratify WIP and queue: low (<30), medium (30–70), high (>70). Estimate adjusted error rates and service times within strata. Use threshold or discontinuity models around WIP > 70 to test whether pressure triggers a change in batching, service time, and rework.
- Adjust for complexity and priority using standardized predictions or inverse probability weighting. Compare raw vs complexity-adjusted error rates by shift position and resource group.
- Control for clock time and elapsed shift time simultaneously. A late-shift effect that remains after clock-hour controls is more consistent with fatigue/elapsed time; a clock-hour effect that remains after elapsed-time controls suggests circadian or demand timing. Compare the same resources across Early/Day/Late/Night where feasible.
- Detect batching with PM4Py and mark events as batch/non-batch. Compare service time, error, and downstream rework for batch vs non-batch events, adjusting for resource, activity, queue, and complexity. Analyze inter-event times and same-queue sequences.
- Analyze automation interaction by filtering `Bot_Risk` vs human events and tracing human corrections/overrides after bot classification. Test whether correction rates increase with queue > 70 and whether bot-handled cases have higher downstream rework.
- Analyze downstream rework/errors: use case-level rework counts, path performance, and temporal linkage of errors to later Quality Check and Rework Correction. Model whether late-shift short service predicts rework within 7 days after adjusting for complexity and queue.
- Test resource-experience interactions: resource group × WIP × elapsed/position. Specifically compare Seniors, Mid-level, Juniors, and Night staff under low vs high WIP and early vs late shift.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions from these data can be unfair or misleading because case assignment is not random. Seniors appear to take complex cases early, Juniors may batch document review late, Night staff handle urgent exceptions, and Day-shift workers face demand peaks. A worker with a higher raw error rate may simply have been exposed to higher WIP, more complex cases, more bot overrides, or more deferred Quality Checks. Queue pressure is often created by management through staffing, demand timing, shift handover, and backlog policies; penalizing workers for errors that occur under management-created WIP is ethically and analytically inappropriate.

Measurement error also matters. `error_flag` may be detected only after Quality Check or rework, so late-shift or deferred checks can change apparent error rates. Rework within 7 days may be censored if cases are still open or if downstream corrections are not linked back to the original case. Automation quality is another confounder: Bot_Risk may be fast but inaccurate under certain queue conditions; human correction rates then reflect both automation and human performance. Batching can make service times look excellent while moving quality problems downstream. Selection bias means that comparing raw error rates across resources or shifts is not a valid performance ranking.

Operationally, avoid punitive use of raw late-shift error rates. Use adjusted, uncertainty-bounded, system-level indicators; investigate patterns by activity, queue, complexity, and resource group; and give workers feedback without ranking them on unadjusted metrics. Do not propose removing Quality Check simply to improve throughput. Quality Check is part of the measurement and control system; reducing it may improve apparent speed and reduce detected errors while increasing hidden rework and customer risk.
