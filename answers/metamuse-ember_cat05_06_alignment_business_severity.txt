## 1. Materiality Framework

Severity is an ordinal business judgment about the control objective, exposure, and harm if the supplied facts are taken as authoritative. It is not calculated from raw alignment cost, and it is not a sum of alignment moves.

**Severity 1 — No material business deviation.** Fitting behaviour, harmless telemetry, transport duplication with no customer or payment effect, or a model-scope artefact for a legitimately terminated or denied claim.

**Severity 2 — Minor or evidential deviation.** The substantive activity probably occurred, but labelling, linkage, or audit traceability is weak. Correction is needed, but no proven payment loss or customer harm is indicated.

**Severity 3 — Material policy or control breach.** A business rule was bypassed or evidence is materially unreliable, with moderate financial exposure or regulatory/audit consequence. Payment correctness still requires retrospective review.

**Severity 4 — Serious control failure.** An irreversible payment was made through a bypassed or inappropriate approval route, especially where an aggravating factor such as a recent beneficiary-bank change is present.

**Severity 5 — Severe control failure.** A high-exposure payment lacks, or precedes, a required fraud or manual approval control, or the evidence needed to demonstrate that control is absent for a high-risk claim. Immediate investigation and containment are required.

The assessment considers:

- **Control objective:** whether the activity controls fraud, payment authorization, beneficiary integrity, assessment quality, or customer notification.
- **Amount and exposure:** absolute amount, amount relative to approval thresholds, and whether money has already left the insurer.
- **Reversibility:** whether a bank payment can realistically be recalled, corrected, or re-performed.
- **Timing:** whether approval preceded payment, whether notification met the 24-hour requirement, and how recently a bank change occurred.
- **Customer and regulatory harm:** incorrect payment, loss to the claimant, duplicate or missing communication, inability to evidence a regulated control, and audit failure.
- **Recurrence:** whether the case appears isolated or represents a recurring system, logging, or model pattern.
- **Evidence reliability:** bank acceptance and signed external documents are stronger than core-log absence alone; mutable free-text links and verbal recollections are weaker.
- **Model scope:** a model move for an activity that was never required because the claim was denied or withdrawn is a scope or model-completeness issue, not automatically an operational failure.

**Confidence is separate from severity.** Confidence expresses how strongly the evidence supports the factual interpretation. High confidence that a duplicate notification was a retry does not make it severe. Conversely, a potentially severe fraud-screen omission may have only medium confidence that the activity never occurred, because a missing log event is not proof that no activity took place. High-severity, lower-confidence findings therefore require investigation and containment before a final operational conclusion.

A useful priority interpretation is: high severity with high confidence requires correction; high severity with medium or low confidence requires immediate evidence preservation and discriminating investigation; low severity with high confidence requires process or logging repair rather than case intervention.

## 3. Repeated Deviation Patterns

### Pattern A — Retry duplicates and other technical noise

- **Frequency evidence:** Seventy-four cases in the full month have a log-only Notify Claimant immediately after a synchronous notification with the same message identifier, all associated with one retrying connector. CL-603 is the supplied example, and CL-601 contains three UI telemetry events in a single case.
- **Business risk:** Low direct customer or financial risk in the observed cases, but high diagnostic risk. Duplicate and telemetry events inflate non-conformance, distort trend analysis, and can mask a genuine duplicate customer communication if delivery receipts are not checked.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** Transport retry without idempotent ingestion, combined with failure to separate UI telemetry, autosave, and transport events from business activities.
- **Confidence:** High for the retry explanation because message identifiers match and the pattern is concentrated in one connector.
- **Recommended owner:** Event platform and integration owner, with the claims process owner responsible for the business-activity taxonomy.

### Pattern B — External-adjuster work missing from the core log

- **Frequency evidence:** Nine high-value cases have a model move for Assess Loss together with an external-adjuster document. Two of the nine lack a stable document-to-case identifier. CL-606 is the detailed example.
- **Business risk:** High-value claims may be substantively assessed but not auditable in the core system. Missing stable linkage creates disputes over assessment scope, timing, version, and whether the document belongs to the claim.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** External adjusters work in a separate document or supplier system; the core system receives no structured assessment-completion event.
- **Confidence:** Medium-High that the activity occurred where a signed document exists; lower where document linkage is unstable.
- **Recommended owner:** Claims operations and external-adjuster management, jointly with core-system integration.

### Pattern C — Cross-system notification evidence with weak linkage

- **Frequency evidence:** CL-608 is the supplied example. The additional pattern evidence does not quantify how often CRM-only notification occurs across the month.
- **Business risk:** The claimant may have been notified on time, but the insurer may be unable to prove recipient, content, timestamp, and case linkage to an auditor or regulator. A mutable text field can be edited, copied incorrectly, or linked to the wrong claim.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** Notification is fragmented across core, CRM, and secure-portal systems without an immutable shared message identifier.
- **Confidence:** Medium for the reported notification; High that the evidence design is unreliable.
- **Recommended owner:** CRM and digital-channel owner, with compliance responsible for evidence standards.

### Pattern D — Label and mapping drift

- **Frequency evidence:** CL-611 shows Assess Damage in the core log against Assess Loss in the model. The mapping table was updated after the case. No additional monthly frequency is supplied.
- **Business risk:** False non-conformance, distorted activity statistics, and uncertainty over whether a retrospective mapping is being used to make historical behaviour appear compliant.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** Synonym drift between systems and procedure documents, combined with unversioned or post hoc mapping maintenance.
- **Confidence:** Medium-High that one assessment activity occurred under two names; Medium that the later mapping was valid for the case date.
- **Recommended owner:** Data modelling owner and claims process owner.

### Pattern E — Denied and withdrawn claims forced through a payable-claim model

- **Frequency evidence:** CL-607 and CL-612 both have raw cost 4. The additional evidence states that denied and withdrawn cases account for most alignments with raw cost of at least 4.
- **Business risk:** The main risk is governance and measurement failure, not payment loss. High raw costs consume investigation capacity, falsely suggest operational non-compliance, and obscure genuinely severe cases.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** The model represents the payable-claim path only, while conformance is being run over intake outcomes that terminate before approval or payment.
- **Confidence:** High that the two supplied cases are model-scope or model-completeness issues; Low on the unresolved business decision about whether denied leads are in scope.
- **Recommended owner:** Process owner and model governance board.

### Pattern F — Data-aware auto-approval failures invisible to control-flow alignment

- **Frequency evidence:** Four cases in the month have fully synchronous Auto Approve paths despite violating P1 or P2. CL-609 violates the amount and risk conditions; CL-610 violates the recent-bank-change condition and omits the required manual route.
- **Business risk:** Serious. Auto approval can release payment without the human and fraud controls intended for larger, riskier, or bank-change claims. Payment to a changed beneficiary account is especially difficult to reverse.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** The process tree encodes only the choice between auto and manual approval. It does not encode amount, fraud-risk, or bank-change predicates, and the operational rule engine may not evaluate those predicates at decision time.
- **Confidence:** High that control-flow alignment and policy compliance diverge; Medium concerning whether any individual payment was substantively wrong or fraudulent.
- **Recommended owner:** Insurance risk and compliance, jointly with the claims rules-engine and payment product owner.

### Pattern G — Missing or late substantive fraud and approval controls

- **Frequency evidence:** CL-602 lacks evidence of Fraud Screen before payment on a high-risk, bank-change claim. CL-604 shows payment accepted before manual approval was recorded and no Fraud Screen in the log.
- **Business risk:** Highest in the portfolio. These are direct payment-authorization control failures with irreversible financial consequences.
- **Likely operational, logging, or model cause:** Verbal or off-system controls, absence of a hard payment gate, payment instructions created before approval, or approval recorded after the actual decision.
- **Confidence:** High that required evidence or correct sequencing is absent; Medium that the underlying human activity never occurred.
- **Recommended owner:** Claims control owner and fraud management, with finance and payment operations accountable for release gating.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

Raw alignment cost is an edit-distance measure under a chosen cost function. In the supplied data, every visible log move and model move costs 1. That design is useful for diagnosing where a trace differs from a control-flow model, but it does not measure the business objective of the missing activity, the amount at risk, the probability of loss, the reliability of the evidence, or whether harm is reversible.

**Cost-function design.** Uniform cost makes a missing Fraud Screen in CL-602 cost 1, a duplicate Notify Claimant in CL-603 cost 1, and a missing Notify Claimant in CL-608 cost 1. These have radically different control objectives and consequences. The cost function contains no term for EUR 480,000 of exposure, high fraud risk, a one-day-old bank change, or the fact that payment has already occurred. A cost function designed for sequence repair therefore cannot be read as a risk function.

**Scope.** CL-607 and CL-612 have raw cost 4 because the model expects Assess Loss, Auto Approve, Pay Claim, and in CL-612 also Notify Claimant after a denial or withdrawal. Those model moves do not represent obligations that a denied or withdrawn claim failed to perform. They reveal that the payable-claim model is being applied outside its intended population or lacks terminal branches. High cost here measures model-scope mismatch, not operational risk.

**Hidden moves.** CL-605 has raw cost 0 because the tau transition is a silent routing move in the converted Petri net. That low cost is appropriate: tau does not represent a missing human approval. The case also shows the limit of the measure. Zero cost means that the visible sequence fits the model; it does not independently prove that the data conditions for Auto Approve were satisfied. In CL-605 those conditions are confirmed by the business context. In CL-609 and CL-610 they are not.

**Data conditions.** CL-609 and CL-610 are fully synchronous with raw cost 0, yet both violate the policy overlay. CL-609 exceeds the EUR 5,000 and LOW-risk conditions for Auto Approve. CL-610 satisfies the amount and risk conditions but violates the recent-bank-change condition and omits the manual route required by P2. The process tree encodes the choice between Auto Approve and the Fraud Screen plus Manual Approve branch, but it does not encode the predicates that determine which branch is permitted. Control-flow fit can therefore be perfect while data-aware compliance is absent.

**Activity granularity.** One unit of cost can represent very different business meanings. In CL-611, one activity-name difference produces both a log move for Assess Damage and a model move for Assess Loss, giving raw cost 2. In CL-601, three UI events produce raw cost 3. Conversely, CL-602 receives raw cost 1 for the absence of a fraud control on a EUR 480,000 high-risk claim. Edit distance counts symbols, not control criticality, granularity, or semantic equivalence.

**Duplicated telemetry and transport events.** CL-603’s extra Notify Claimant is a retry with the same message identifier. CL-601’s Open Workbench, Autosave, and Close Workbench events are not business activities under P5. Alignment cost correctly identifies that these events are not in the model, but it does not establish that the claimant was harmed, that a control was bypassed, or that an operator behaved incorrectly. Treating all log-only moves as operational deviations confuses event-log quality with process performance.

**Missing-but-external evidence.** CL-606 receives a model move for Assess Loss even though a signed external-adjuster report may prove that the assessment occurred. CL-608 receives a model move for Notify Claimant even though a CRM-linked portal message may prove timely notification. In both cases the alignment measures absence from the core log, not absence from the enterprise. The business risk is evidence fragmentation and linkage reliability, which is lower than an actual omitted assessment or notification but still material for high-value claims.

**Irreversibility and timing.** CL-604’s raw cost is 3, but its business significance comes from the bank’s acceptance of payment at 10:03 and manual approval being recorded at 10:20. An alignment can insert a model Fraud Screen and a later model Pay Claim to restore a model-consistent order, but it cannot reverse the movement of money or recreate the approval that should have preceded release. Timing relative to an irreversible bank transaction is not represented by the number of edit operations.

**Insertion of fictional model activities.** A model move is a repair in the alignment, not evidence that the activity occurred. CL-602’s inserted Fraud Screen does not supply a fraud report. CL-604’s inserted Fraud Screen and later Pay Claim do not show that fraud screening happened or that a second payment occurred. CL-607’s inserted Assess Loss, Auto Approve, and Pay Claim do not create obligations after denial. Reading model moves as real missing work without considering scope and external evidence overstates some findings; reading them as completed repairs understates others.

**Confidence is not encoded.** Alignment cost does not distinguish a bank timestamp from a verbal recollection, a signed report from a mutable CRM text field, or a contemporaneous mapping from a mapping created after the case. CL-602, CL-606, CL-608, and CL-611 all require evidence-quality judgments that the cost function cannot represent. A low-cost finding may still demand urgent investigation, while a high-cost finding may require only model or logging repair.

The examples form an inverse or non-monotonic relationship: raw costs of 3 and 4 occur for telemetry, denial, and withdrawal; raw cost 1 occurs for a missing fraud control on a EUR 480,000 claim; and raw cost 0 occurs for two data-aware auto-approval failures. Raw cost is therefore informative about sequence distance from the supplied model, but it is not a measure of compliance risk, financial exposure, customer harm, or evidentiary confidence.
