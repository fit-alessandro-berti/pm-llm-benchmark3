# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Time origin is `Submit` at elapsed time `t = 0`. The main horizon is `tau = 72` hours. Let `T_resolve` be time to internal `Resolve`, `T_term` be time to first observed internal terminal disposition in `{Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}`, and `C` be administrative censoring at the observation cutoff for cases that are still `open`.

The phrase "probability of breaching the SLA" should be split into at least three estimands because they answer different operational questions.

1. Internal-resolution miss by 72 hours: `P(T_resolve > 72)`.
This is the best estimand for a promise stated as "resolved within 72 hours." It counts any case not internally resolved by 72 as a miss, whether it is still open, later resolved, withdrawn, rejected, or transferred.

2. No internal terminal disposition by 72 hours: `P(T_term > 72)`.
This is the state-occupation probability of still being active internally at 72. It is the best estimand for queue control, breach-prevention alerts, and backlog governance because it isolates work still in the system at the horizon.

3. Late internal resolution: `P(72 < T_resolve < infinity)`.
This is the cumulative incidence of resolving after the SLA has already been missed. It is the best estimand for "rescue" planning: how much miss volume is late-but-recoverable internally, versus permanently diverted to withdrawal, rejection, or transfer.

These estimands are not interchangeable, and they differ from censoring. A case can already be a known SLA miss without having a terminal outcome by cutoff.

| Case pattern | `P(T_resolve > 72)` | `P(T_term > 72)` | `P(72 < T_resolve < infinity)` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolved at 76 hours (`S03`-like) | Counts as an observed miss | Counts as active at 72, then exits at 76 | Event of interest: late internal resolve |
| Open at 92 hours (`S09`-like) | Counts as an observed miss because it survived unresolved past 72 | Counts as active at 72 | Right-censored at 92 for eventual disposition; no late resolve observed yet |
| Withdrawn at 27 hours (`S06`-like) | Counts as a miss if the SLA means "resolved by 72" | Does not count as active at 72 because it terminated before 72 | Competing event `Withdraw` at 27 |
| Transferred at 68 hours (`S08`-like) | Internal-SLA view: observed miss because there was no internal resolve by 72. Enterprise-wide view: outcome after 68 may be unknown and may need censoring or bounds | Internal-terminal view: not active at 72 because it terminated before 72. Enterprise-wide continuation view: censor or latent continuation | Usually competing event `Transfer` at 68 for internal-process analysis; censor at 68 in a sensitivity analysis if external resolution could still satisfy the business SLA |

The key time-handling distinction is:

- `open` with age below 72 is right-censored with respect to the 72-hour horizon outcome because the cutoff happened before the case reached the horizon.
- `open` with age above 72 is not right-censored for the 72-hour breach indicator: it is already a known miss, even though its final outcome remains censored for later-disposition analysis.
- `resolved` after 72 is an already-observed breach, not a censored case.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The cleanest default is to encode terminal internal outcomes as competing events and keep administrative cutoff censoring only for `open` rows. `Transfer` is the main ambiguity and needs explicit sensitivity treatment rather than a silent default.

| observed_status | Encoding for `I(T_resolve > 72)` | Encoding for `I(T_term > 72)` | Competing-risk code for first internal terminal outcome | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | `0` if `duration_or_age_h <= 72`, else `1` | `0` if `duration_or_age_h <= 72`, else `1` because the case was still active at 72 | `1 = Resolve` at observed time | Never censored for these estimands | None needed; late resolves remain observed breaches for the 72-hour SLA |
| `withdrawn` | `1` for customer-facing "resolved by 72" SLA because no resolve occurred by 72 | `0` if withdrawn by 72; if withdrawal were after 72 it would still imply active at 72 | `2 = Withdraw` | Not ordinary censoring | Sensitivity: for a narrower business question, treat withdrawal as a separate acceptable outcome, but still not as non-informative censoring |
| `rejected` | `1` for customer-facing "resolved by 72" SLA because no resolve occurred by 72 | `0` if rejected by 72; if rejection were after 72 it would still imply active at 72 | `3 = Reject` | Not ordinary censoring | Sensitivity: separate from misses if the business treats early rejection as correct triage rather than failed service, but do not censor it by default |
| `transferred` | Internal-SLA default: `1` if transfer occurs before or after 72 because internal resolve by 72 did not occur | Internal-terminal default: `0` if transferred by 72 because the internal process terminated before the horizon | `4 = Transfer` in the default internal-process analysis | Do not assume ordinary censoring by default because transfer is plausibly selective | At least three alternatives: `(a)` competing absorbing transfer at transfer time; `(b)` right-censor at transfer time for enterprise-wide resolution if external outcomes are missing only because of system boundary; `(c)` worst-case breach rule, counting pre-72 transfer as a miss; `(d)` optimistic bound, assuming pre-72 transfer could still meet the external SLA |
| `open` | `1` if `duration_or_age_h >= 72`, otherwise unobserved at the horizon | `1` if `duration_or_age_h >= 72`, otherwise unobserved at the horizon | `0 = censored` at cutoff age because final terminal outcome is unknown | Administrative right censoring at cutoff age | Sensitivity: treat very long `last_event_gap_h` as evidence that censoring may be informative rather than independent |

Recommended default coding for a first internal-outcome multi-state analysis:

- `1 = Resolve`
- `2 = Withdraw`
- `3 = Reject`
- `4 = Transfer`
- `0 = administratively censored open case`

Recommended transfer sensitivity set:

1. Internal-terminal transfer: `Transfer` is an absorbing competing event.
2. External-unknown transfer: `Transfer` is right-censored at handoff.
3. Bounded business-SLA analysis: pre-72 transfer is counted once as worst-case breach and once as best-case external success, to show how much of the conclusion depends on unobserved downstream outcomes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Analyzing only completed resolved cases is biased for several distinct reasons in this extract.

- Survivor bias. If only resolved cases are kept, overdue open cases such as `S09`, `S11`, `S25`, and `S26` disappear even though they are already known 72-hour misses. The resolved-only subset also hides unresolved sub-72 cases such as `S10`, `S15`, `S24`, `S29`, and `S31`, which were still at risk at cutoff.
- Selection on terminal success. Resolved-only analysis compares `S01`, `S02`, `S03`, `S04`, `S07`, `S13`, `S16`, `S17`, `S18`, `S19`, `S20`, `S27`, and `S28`, while dropping withdrawals, rejections, transfers, and opens. That turns a case-management process into a conditional sample of survivors.
- Informative transfer censoring. `S08`, `S21`, and `S32` are all transferred, and all three are on escalated paths; `S21` and `S32` are also `P1`. That pattern is not compatible with a casual independent-censoring assumption.
- Informative open-case censoring. The overdue open cases are not random missingness. `S11` has `last_event_gap_h = 55`, `S25` has `48`, and `S26` has `72`. These are exactly the kinds of stalled cases likely to have different future resolution prospects from actively progressing cases.
- Competing risks. `S05`, `S06`, `S12`, `S14`, `S22`, `S23`, and `S30` reached terminal outcomes other than `Resolve`. Treating those as ordinary censoring in a Kaplan-Meier for resolution would overstate eventual internal resolution because those cases are no longer able to resolve internally under the observed process.
- Cohort imbalance and non-random policy rollout. Cohort `B` has more `P1` cases than cohort `A` (`5` versus `3`), and rollout compliance was incomplete because `AutoCheck` appears in both cohorts while some cohort `B` cases still follow non-`AutoCheck` starts such as `S20`, `S23`, `S26`, and `S30`. So crude `A` versus `B` differences mix case mix, calendar time, and compliance.
- Open-below-72 is different from open-above-72. `S10`, `S15`, `S24`, `S29`, and `S31` are censored before the SLA horizon for a 72-hour outcome. `S09`, `S11`, `S25`, and `S26` are already observed 72-hour misses. Collapsing both groups into one "open/missing" bucket discards useful information.

The resolved-only picture can be materially misleading. Among resolved cases alone, cohort `B` looks faster than `A`, but that ignores cohort `B`'s overdue opens and `P1` transfers. A process-aware survival view will usually shrink or even reverse such apparent improvements.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | What it estimates here | Value for these data | Main limitation here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier for time to `Resolve` | `P(T_resolve > t)` under independent censoring | Useful only as a sensitivity or upper-bound style estimate if non-resolve terminals are censored | Wrong as a primary method if `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are real competing endpoints or informative censoring |
| Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence | Cause-specific cumulative incidence for `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`, plus state occupancy of remaining active | Best primary nonparametric summary because it keeps open cases, respects competing terminal outcomes, and directly answers "resolved by 72", "transferred by 72", and "still active at 72" | Requires a clear transfer assumption; with only 32 cases, inference should be descriptive, not overfit |
| Cause-specific hazards | Transition intensity into each terminal outcome | Good for diagnosing which factors are associated with faster `Resolve` versus `Transfer` or `Withdraw` | Needs larger samples and, ideally, exact intermediate timestamps for valid time-varying covariates |
| Subdistribution hazards (Fine-Gray style) | Covariate association with cumulative incidence | Useful if the goal is direct modeling of cumulative incidence of a terminal cause | Awkward with time-varying stages, easy to misread causally, and unstable in this small extract |
| Restricted mean unresolved time to 72 hours | Expected unresolved hours up to 72 | Good secondary service-level KPI for capacity planning | Does not explain which terminal outcome prevented resolution |
| Landmarking | Conditional risk from a fixed age such as 24 or 48 hours | Essential for using `Escalate`, `Rework`, current stage, and `last_event_gap_h` without immortal-time bias | Requires choosing operational landmark times and conditioning on cases still alive/observed then |
| Multi-state model | Occupancy and transition risks across process stages and terminal states | Conceptually the best process-aware representation | Not fully estimable here because intermediate activity timestamps are absent; prefixes give order but not transition times |

Recommended primary approach for this extract:

- Primary descriptive estimator: Aalen-Johansen for first internal terminal disposition from `Submit`, with states `{Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}` and administrative censoring only for open cases. Report by `t = 72`:
  - cumulative incidence of `Resolve`;
  - cumulative incidence of `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`;
  - probability of still being active at 72.
- From the same fit, derive late-resolution mass as `CIF_resolve(max follow-up) - CIF_resolve(72)`.
- Report transfer-sensitive alternatives side by side rather than hiding the assumption.

Recommended secondary operational approach:

- Landmark at 24 hours and 48 hours for cases still active and still internally observed.
- At each landmark, predict conditional `P(T_resolve > 72 | alive and observed at landmark)` and conditional cause-specific risk using only information available by that landmark:
  - current stage from the prefix observed so far;
  - repeated review / rework;
  - whether escalation has already occurred by the landmark;
  - `last_event_gap_h` and age.

Why this is better than generic method listing for these data:

- Exact intermediate timestamps are missing, so a full transition-timed multi-state model is conceptually right but not operationally identified from this extract alone.
- Open cases must not be dropped, and some open cases are already known breaches.
- Transfer is ambiguous and plausibly informative, so any single-method answer that censors transfers without sensitivity work is too strong.
- Sample size is only `32`, so nonparametric curves and stratified summaries are more defensible than heavily parameterized regression.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Prefixes should be used, but only with time awareness. The full observed prefix at terminal time or cutoff is safe for descriptive pathway analysis; it is not automatically safe as a baseline predictor.

| Feature | Derivation from supplied data | Role | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| `cohort` | Given | Baseline | Proxy for policy era; useful for stratification and calibration audit, not automatically causal |
| `priority` | Given | Baseline | Safe baseline covariate |
| Initial route | Whether prefix begins `Submit>Screen...` or `Submit>AutoCheck...` | Early time-varying or first-event landmark feature | Not a guaranteed baseline covariate unless the first post-`Submit` event is effectively instantaneous and always observed |
| Current stage | Last non-terminal activity in the observed prefix, for example `Review` or `Escalate` | Time-varying state | Use only at the age when that stage is already known |
| `review_visits` | Count `Review` tokens in the prefix | Time-varying control-flow intensity | Repeated review can indicate looping or queue re-entry |
| `rework_count` | Supplied field and corroborated by prefix | Time-varying | Do not use future rework as a baseline predictor |
| Escalation seen yet | Presence of `Escalate` in the prefix up to the landmark | Time-varying | The supplied `escalated` flag is leakage if treated as baseline because the case had to survive long enough to escalate |
| Escalate-return pattern | Prefix contains `Escalate>Review` | Time-varying path feature | Could signal rescue attempt followed by more work rather than immediate resolution |
| Path length / number of hops | Count of activities in prefix | Time-varying complexity measure | Longer paths partly reflect survival time, so compare at common landmarks |
| Variant / path pattern | Exact prefix or coarsened variant class | Time-varying control-flow signature | Rare variants need pooling |
| `last_event_gap_h` | Supplied | Time-varying inactivity / stall feature | Could be predictive of breach and also of informative censoring |
| `gap_share` | `last_event_gap_h / duration_or_age_h` | Time-varying stall severity | High values in overdue opens suggest latent queueing or hidden external dependence |

Observed qualitative stage-hazard signals in this extract:

- Cases that re-enter `Review` after `Escalate` or `Rework` tend to be long-path cases, for example `S03`, `S13`, and `S28`.
- Overdue open cases often sit in `Review` or `Escalate` with long inactivity gaps, for example `S11`, `S25`, and `S26`.
- Escalation is not uniformly "bad" or "good": `S04` and `S18` are fast successful escalations, while `S08`, `S21`, `S25`, and `S32` show escalation on transfer or overdue paths. That is exactly why escalation must be modeled as time-varying and descriptive, not as a baseline causal factor.

Proposed state-transition diagram in text:

- `Submit -> Screen | AutoCheck`
- `Screen -> AutoCheck | Review | Escalate | Resolve | Withdraw | Reject`
- `AutoCheck -> Screen | Review | Escalate | Resolve`
- `Review -> Resolve | Withdraw | Reject | Rework | Escalate`
- `Rework -> Review`
- `Escalate -> Resolve | Review | Transfer`
- Terminal states: `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`

For the current extract, that diagram is best used as a conceptual multi-state template plus landmark features, because transition times between intermediate states are not observed.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least four sensitivity analyses are warranted here.

1. Transfer treatment sensitivity.
Default internal-process analysis treats `Transfer` as a competing terminal event. A second analysis censors at transfer time, representing enterprise-wide resolution that becomes unobserved after handoff. A third analysis reports bounds by counting pre-72 transfers as worst-case misses and best-case external successes. In this extract, the known-case 72-hour miss proportion moves materially across those transfer assumptions, and the broader bound widens further once pre-72 open censoring is acknowledged. That means any strong single-number claim about "the" breach rate is overstated.

2. Withdrawal and rejection definition sensitivity.
For a customer-facing "resolved within 72 hours" SLA, `Withdraw` and `Reject` are non-successes by 72 and should count against the success probability. For a backlog-control metric, they are useful competing outcomes because they remove work before 72 and should not be confused with active congestion. Because `P3` contains many early rejects/withdrawals, priority comparisons can look very different depending on which estimand is used.

3. Informative-censoring sensitivity.
Compare a standard independent-censoring analysis with an IPCW or pattern-mixture sensitivity that uses `priority`, `cohort`, current stage, escalation history, and `last_event_gap_h` to model censoring/open persistence. The overdue open cases with large gaps (`S11`, `S25`, `S26`) suggest that ordinary administrative censoring assumptions may be optimistic.

4. Cohort-policy sensitivity.
Compare:
  - crude cohort `A` versus `B`;
  - priority-stratified cohort comparison;
  - path-restricted comparison on routes seen in both cohorts;
  - if compliance data become available, compliance-adjusted comparison.
Because cohort assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete, the apparent benefit of cohort `B` could shrink or reverse after adjustment.

Qualitative conclusions that could reverse under sensitivity work:

- Whether `P1` is the worst SLA-risk group. All observed transfers are `P1`, so the priority ranking changes sharply depending on whether transfers are treated as competing internal failures, external censoring, or optimistic external success.
- Whether cohort `B` really improved outcomes. Resolved-only summaries make `B` look faster, but that can be offset by `B`'s `P1` transfers and overdue opens once all starts are retained.
- Whether escalation appears harmful. A naive baseline escalation indicator is biased by immortal time and case complexity. A landmarked view may show escalation as an early rescue mechanism for some `P1` cases and a marker of late complexity for others.

For this dataset, I would report a sensitivity band rather than a single point estimate. Descriptively, the 72-hour miss rate is at least substantial under every plausible transfer handling rule, but the exact level and subgroup ranking depend on how external handoffs are interpreted.

# 7. Operational interpretation

The survival outputs should drive queue management and governance, not causal claims about interventions.

Operationally, I would convert the analysis into three dashboard probabilities rather than one:

- probability of internal `Resolve` by 72;
- probability of still being active at 72;
- probability of each competing terminal path by 72: `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`.

Those support different actions:

- Intake and early routing: use baseline-stratified curves by `priority` and cohort-era to set realistic expectations and identify groups needing tighter monitoring.
- Mid-course alerts at 24 and 48 hours: trigger review when the conditional miss probability is high and the case is in a stalled stage such as `Review` or `Escalate`, especially with long `last_event_gap_h` or repeated `Review`.
- Workload actions: separate "needs immediate work to hit 72" from "likely transfer / rejection / withdrawal" because the staffing response differs.
- P1 handling: split alerts into late-resolution risk and transfer/handoff risk, since those are conflated if transfer is ignored.

Governance recommendations:

- Report three KPIs, not one blended SLA metric: `resolved by 72`, `still active at 72`, and `diverted before 72` by cause.
- Keep transfer assumptions explicit in governance packs.
- Do not present hazard ratios or risk scores as causal effects of `Escalate`, `AutoCheck`, or the policy change.

Calibration and fairness checks should be done by both `priority` and `cohort`:

- reliability / calibration plots for 24-hour and 48-hour landmark predictions;
- Brier score or calibration error by subgroup;
- false-negative rate for cases that actually miss the SLA;
- alert burden by subgroup, so one group is not overloaded with false alarms;
- transfer-sensitive subgroup calibration, especially for `P1`, because transfer handling can change the apparent risk ordering.

I would use `cohort` mainly for monitoring, adjustment, and recalibration, not as a direct prioritization lever, because it is partly a policy-era proxy rather than a stable customer attribute.

# Estimand 2: competing-risk coding for first internal terminal outcome.
def cr_code(row, transfer_mode):
    s = row["observed_status"]
    if s == "resolved":
        return 1
    if s == "withdrawn":

def aalen_johansen(frame, time_col, code_col, horizon):
    """
    Manual nonparametric competing-risk estimator.
    No external survival library required.
    code 0 = censored, positive integers = competing terminal causes.
    """
    d = frame[[time_col, code_col]].copy().sort_values(time_col)
    causes = sorted([c for c in d[code_col].unique() if c != 0])
    S = 1.0
    cif = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    history = []
    for t in sorted(d[time_col].unique()):
        n_risk = int((d[time_col] >= t).sum())
        if n_risk == 0:
            continue
        d_all = int(((d[time_col] == t) & (d[code_col] > 0)).sum())
        S_prev = S
        for c in causes:
            d_c = int(((d[time_col] == t) & (d[code_col] == c)).sum())
            cif[c] += S_prev * d_c / n_risk
        S = S_prev * (1 - d_all / n_risk)
        history.append((t, S, cif.copy()))
    def snapshot(h):
        S_h = 1.0
        cif_h = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
        for t, S_t, cif_t in history:
            if t <= h:
                S_h = S_t
                cif_h = cif_t
        return S_h, cif_h
    return history, snapshot

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The main inferential limitations are structural, not cosmetic.

- Exact event timestamps inside each prefix are missing. Without those, transition-specific hazards between `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Rework`, and `Escalate` cannot be estimated properly; only terminal times and ordered activity histories are observed.
- External outcomes after `Transfer` are missing. This is the biggest reason the transfer assumption can move conclusions, especially for `P1`.
- Observation cutoff date and case start calendar dates are missing. Without them, calendar-time confounding, day-of-week effects, and changing workload context cannot be separated from process behavior.
- Time-varying workload and resource data are missing. Queue length, staffing, reviewer availability, and escalation backlog would help explain long inactivity gaps such as `S11`, `S25`, and `S26`.
- The assignment mechanism for cohort `B` is not randomized, and rollout compliance is incomplete. Stronger policy inference needs who was eligible, who actually received the new routing/escalation logic, and why.
- Reasons for withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are missing. Those reasons matter for whether those endpoints should count as service misses, correct triage, or external handoff.
- Case-level severity or complexity beyond `priority` is missing. Without that, `Escalate` and `Rework` remain entangled with underlying difficulty.
- Sample size is very small. I would emphasize descriptive uncertainty bands, subgroup calibration, and sensitivity ranges rather than formal hypothesis tests.

The highest-value next data additions are:

1. exact timestamps for every event in the prefix;
2. downstream outcomes and timing after transfer;
3. cutoff date and case start dates;
4. queue/workload/staffing snapshots over time;
5. policy assignment and compliance fields;
6. richer pre-treatment case descriptors.

With those additions, the analysis can move from a horizon-plus-sensitivity survival study to a proper transition-timed multi-state model with more credible adjustment for informative censoring and rollout confounding.
