## 1. What is observed

Automation availability began in April for Alpha and Beta and in July for Gamma; Delta and Epsilon remained unavailable through August. Availability is not equivalent to case-level treatment. The completed automated-fast-path share was already 4–6% before formal adoption, then rose gradually from roughly 25–27% in the first rollout month to 48–50% in Alpha and Beta and 32% in Gamma. This may reflect pilots, classification rules, or multiple treatment versions.

Rollout was endogenous: Alpha and Beta were chosen because of high volume and historically poor cycle times, while Gamma followed a readiness pilot. Thus adopters differ systematically from controls and may regress toward typical performance.

Several operational changes coincide with adoption. The centralized fraud desk started in March; routing increased demand at adopters; staffing changed in four branches; high-complexity shares rose after the May product change; and managers could alter local practices. Workload per productive FTE therefore changed materially and differently by branch.

The outcomes are branch-month aggregates rather than case-level observations. Medians and p90s cannot reveal within-path performance, open-case censoring, or queue-specific waiting. Rework and escalation percentages may change compositionally when simple cases move to a fast path. CSAT is a mean among survey respondents, with variable 20–45% response and no response counts, precluding appropriate weighting or assessment of nonresponse bias. Only eight months and five branches are observed.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cohort-specific staggered-adoption event study with group-time ATTs **(primary)** | Separate April cohort (Alpha/Beta) and July cohort (Gamma); event-time effects, never using already-treated branches as controls | For April: Gamma, Delta, and Epsilon while untreated; for July: Delta and Epsilon; estimates also shown using never-treated branches only | Pre-rollout outcome levels/slopes, baseline complexity, baseline workload; calendar time. Contemporaneous complexity, FTE, and routing enter sensitivity analyses rather than an unquestioned main adjustment | E1, E2, E5–E8 | Conditional parallel trends for each cohort, no anticipation, comparable treatment versions, overlap, and either no fraud-desk interference or an explicitly defined exposure mapping | Event-time leads; leave-one-control-out estimates; cohort-specific estimates; placebo adoption dates; raw versus adjusted estimates; wild-cluster or permutation sensitivity | Targeted rollout and synchronized routing/staffing changes make counterfactual trends doubtful; only five clusters |
| Matched comparative interrupted time series / augmented synthetic control | Branch-specific interruption at April or July, permitting level and slope changes | Weighted combinations of untreated branches selected using pre-period outcomes and covariates | Pre-period outcome trajectory, complexity, arrivals/FTE, FTE, escalation and rework history | Branch-specific availability effect, especially E1 and E2 | Stable donor relationship absent treatment, no unmodeled branch-specific shock at adoption, no anticipation or interference | Pre-fit RMS error; placebo interventions in donors; leave-one-donor-out; forecast residuals; alternative slope specifications | Three pre-months for Alpha/Beta are insufficient for reliable synthetic matching; donor pool is tiny |
| Longitudinal dose-response model using g-computation or marginal structural weights | Monthly continuous intensity and lags, rather than a binary switch | Branch-months with different uptake levels, primarily within adopters | Prior outcomes, prior intensity, eligibility mix, workload, staffing, queue states, month and branch | E3 | Sequential exchangeability, positivity, correct temporal ordering, measured treatment versions, no simultaneous determination of uptake and outcomes | Uptake-propensity overlap; lag/placebo tests; nonlinear dose curves; compare assigned encouragement with completed-path share | Current intensity is a completed-case composition measure and is likely caused by queues, selection, and learning |
| Prospective randomized stepped-wedge plus saturation experiment | Randomized launch order for Delta/Epsilon and randomized automation saturation by team/week or eligible case | Delayed branch, manual-eligible cases, and lower-saturation periods | Randomization strata such as branch, risk class, week, and product | Future E1–E4 and spillover-aware effects | Assignment is enforced, contamination measured, stable eligibility, and interference handled through randomized saturation | Balance, compliance, randomization inference, queue spillover contrasts, attrition and survey-response checks | Operational override of assignment or shared fraud-desk congestion without measured exposure |

The primary strategy uses the staggered timing without imposing the homogeneous-effect assumptions of a naïve two-way fixed-effects coefficient. It estimates April and July cohort contrasts separately, so Gamma can serve as a not-yet-treated comparison in spring but not after its own adoption. Dynamic estimates also distinguish initial implementation from mature use.

This is still a conditional design, not a repair for endogenous selection. Alpha and Beta were selected for weak historical performance, their routing changed at adoption, and only three pre-months are available. Consequently, estimates should be presented alongside raw changes, never-treated-only comparisons, pretrend plots, and leave-one-branch-out results. Time-varying workload, staffing, and complexity should be handled in two ways: first as sensitivity adjustments for an automation-isolated estimand, and second as part of a bundled-rollout estimand when they are genuine components of implementation. Conditioning mechanically on variables affected by rollout would otherwise introduce post-treatment bias.

The approach is preferable to synthetic control because the donor pool and pre-period are extremely small, and preferable to a single interrupted series because broad March–May shocks are visible in controls. With five clusters, conventional clustered p-values are unreliable; cohort consistency, placebo timing, design sensitivity, and prospective randomization should carry more weight than nominal significance.

The median contrasts equal roughly **9.5%** for Alpha/Beta and **9.1%** for Gamma relative to their respective pre-window medians. These similarities are suggestive, but the assumptions needed to interpret them causally are not demonstrated.

**3. Treatment-intensity analysis.** Comparing the first availability month with August:

| branch | fast-path intensity change | median change | p90 change | rework change | escalation change | CSAT change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alpha | 2548 (+23 pp) | 5.0 h | +3.9 h | +3.3 pp | +0.8 pp | +0.06 |
| Beta | 2750 (+23 pp) | 2.3 h | +3.6 h | +2.3 pp | +0.5 pp | +0.03 |
| Gamma | 2532 (+7 pp) | 2.4 h | 4.4 h | 0.0 pp | 0.0 pp | +0.04 |

For Alpha/Beta, a 23-point use increase coincides with a **3.65-hour median reduction but a 3.75-hour p90 increase**—approximately 1.59 and +1.63 hours per 10 intensity points. This is not a dose effect: use, learning, arrival volume, complexity, and calendar time move together.

**4. Pretrends and regression to the mean.** Alpha’s pre medians are 50.4, 46.9, and 49.4; Beta trends upward from 45.4 to 46.4. Delta and Epsilon also rise in March, and all three later controls deteriorate around the May complexity change. Gamma reaches its worst observed median and p90 immediately before adoption, making its large July improvement particularly susceptible to mean reversion and seasonal recovery. Selection of historically weak Alpha and Beta creates the same concern, although neither has an extreme final pre-month.

**5. Sensitivity ranges.** Across raw and calendar-adjusted specifications, the median envelope is **8.45 to +0.73 hours** and the p90 envelope is **15.60 to +9.73 hours**. If residual differential trend uncertainty is set to ±5 hours for medians and ±10 hours for p90—comparable to visible monthly shocks—the two DiD estimates imply about **9.5 to +0.8 hours** for the median and **17.7 to +3.7 hours** for p90. A conservative planning range that also respects the adverse raw p90 result is approximately **10 to +4 hours for the median** and **18 to +10 hours for p90**. These are scenario ranges, not confidence intervals.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for poor historical performance and volume; Gamma had a readiness pilot | Regression to the mean likely favors apparent benefit, though persistent capacity problems can bias toward harm | Longer prehistory; model selection score; matched pretrends; historical placebo adoption dates | Existing contrasts cannot support a causal percentage |
| Nonparallel trends | Branches have different pre-levels and short, noisy trajectories | Ambiguous | Event-study leads; branch-specific slopes; placebo windows; compare multiple donor sets | DiD counterfactual is invalid |
| Time-varying confounding | Fraud desk, product change, routing, staffing, and local practice changes coincide with rollout | Mixed | Weekly covariates and event logs; controlled interrupted series; negative-control outcomes | Effect is attributable only to the bundled change, not automation |
| Treatment intensity and versions | Availability ranges from 25% to 50% actual fast-path use, with nonzero pre-adoption use | Binary availability can attenuate effects; endogenous uptake can bias either way | Record eligibility, assignment, override, software version, and actual path; randomized encouragement | “Automation” has no stable intervention definition |
| Fraud-desk spillovers | All branches share the desk, and automation refers more borderline cases | Control contamination may attenuate direct effects; differential adopter load may worsen adopter p90 | Desk arrival/service timestamps, branch of origin, queue length, saturation analysis | Standard no-interference estimands are inappropriate |
| Staffing changes | Alpha and Gamma lose FTE while Beta and Delta gain FTE | Loss biases toward harm; gains toward benefit | Shift-level productive hours, skill mix, leave, overtime; workload-per-FTE sensitivity | Branch effects may primarily reflect labor capacity |
| Case mix | High-complexity shares rise after May and differ across branches | Usually biases toward longer time and more rework, but risk classification may itself change | Case-level risk balance, richer severity fields, risk-stratified outcomes | Aggregate estimates do not transport to Delta/Epsilon |
| Routing campaign | Incoming volume rises specifically at adopters | Likely biases cycle outcomes toward harm, but routed cases may differ in difficulty | Source-channel and eligibility data; compare rerouted versus organic arrivals | Cannot isolate software from demand allocation |
| Anticipation and readiness | Gamma had a pilot; managers could prepare before launch | Pre-period improvement biases toward null; backlog accumulation can reverse the direction | Usage and training logs; exclude anticipation window; interview/process audit | Event time zero is misspecified |
| Survey nonresponse | Response rates range from about 20% to 45%, with no counts | Unknown; could create apparent improvement if dissatisfied customers respond less | Invitation and response counts, case linkage, inverse-probability weighting, nonresponse follow-up | CSAT effect for all customers is unidentified |
| Measurement aggregation | Branch-month medians, p90s, and means conceal distributions and path composition | Ecological and weighting bias can go either way | Case-level timestamps and quantile treatment effects; report case counts by path | Branch-month associations cannot identify per-case effects |
| Small number of branches | Only five clusters and three adopters are available | Standard errors tend to be overconfident | Randomization/placebo inference, wild-cluster sensitivity, leave-one-branch-out | Nominal p-values and narrow intervals should be disregarded |
| Dynamic treatment effects | Learning raises use over time; congestion may emerge later | Pooled effects can mask early harm or mature benefit | Cohort-specific event-time effects; minimum stable-use follow-up | A single average is not policy relevant |
| Managerial co-interventions | Local practices could change during rollout | Unknown | Change logs, implementation fidelity audits, manager interviews | Estimate becomes an effect of the implementation package |
| Completion/censoring bias | Monthly summaries may omit unresolved cases or assign cross-month cases inconsistently | Backlog growth can make completed cases look artificially fast | Arrival-cohort analysis, open-case age, censoring rules, backlog counts | Median and p90 changes can be misleading even descriptively |

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Delta and Epsilon should form the next evaluation pair, but leadership should not activate both fully on the same date. Randomly select one branch for the first wave and retain the other as a delayed-start comparator for at least 12 weeks. Randomization avoids repeating the historical policy of choosing the weakest branch first. Within the first-wave branch, use randomized saturation—such as eligible team-weeks assigned to 25%, 50%, or 75% automated routing—if operationally feasible. This permits estimation of both direct fast-path effects and congestion spillovers. The delayed branch should then cross over using a prespecified date rather than a performance-triggered date.

Retain at least six historical monthly observations, but collect a minimum of 12 weekly pre-periods and 16 weekly post-periods, with the first four post-weeks identified as learning rather than silently averaged into mature performance. Follow cases to completion rather than relying only on calendar-month completions.

The event log should contain case eligibility, randomized assignment or encouragement, actual triage route, overrides, every activity timestamp, repeated requests, specialist handoffs, reopenings, resolution quality, risk at registration, routing source, productive staffing hours, and software version. Record queue arrivals, service starts, queue length, abandonment, and waiting separately for local specialists and the centralized fraud desk. Survey data must include invitations, responses, timing, branch, path, and risk group.

Monitor Delta/Epsilon outcomes alongside fraud-desk outcomes for the already adopted branches. Guardrails should stop or reduce saturation if risk-adjusted p90 exceeds the concurrent manual group by more than 5%, rework rises more than 1 percentage point, CSAT falls more than 0.10, safety/reopen errors increase, or fraud-desk service levels breach their limit for two consecutive weeks.

Scale further only if mature-use randomization estimates show at least a 5% median improvement, p90 noninferiority within 5%, no material rework or quality harm, and acceptable specialist-queue spillovers. Use randomization inference and report branch-specific effects; do not require a fragile five-cluster regression p-value.

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(CSV), parse_dates=["month"])
df = df.sort_values(["branch", "month"]).reset_index(drop=True)
df["month_label"] = df["month"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m")
df["load_per_fte"] = df["arrivals"] / df["fte"]

expected_branches = {"Alpha", "Beta", "Gamma", "Delta", "Epsilon"}
expected_months = pd.date_range("2026-01-01", "2026-08-01", freq="MS")

assert set(df["branch"]) == expected_branches
assert len(df) == 40
assert not df.duplicated(["branch", "month"]).any()
assert df.isna().sum().sum() == 0
assert (df.groupby("branch").size() == 8).all()
assert set(df["month"]) == set(expected_months)
assert df["automation"].isin([0, 1]).all()
assert df["fte"].gt(0).all()
assert df["arrivals"].gt(0).all()
assert df["high_complexity_pct"].between(0, 100).all()
assert df["automated_fast_path_pct"].between(0, 100).all()
assert df["rework_pct"].between(0, 100).all()
assert df["escalation_pct"].between(0, 100).all()
assert df["csat_1to5"].between(1, 5).all()

# Availability must be monotone once it switches on.
monotone = df.groupby("branch")["automation"].apply(
    lambda s: s.diff().fillna(0).ge(0).all()
)
assert monotone.all()

warnings.warn(
    "Only five branch clusters are available. Cluster-robust standard errors, "
    "p-values, and confidence intervals are not reliable in this sample."
)
warnings.warn(
    "median_cycle_h and p90_cycle_h are aggregated branch-month quantiles. "
    "They cannot be pooled as if they were case-level observations, and their "
    "regression coefficients are associations between summaries."
)

print("Validated panel shape:", df.shape)
print("\nAvailability by branch and month:")
print(df.pivot(index="month_label", columns="branch", values="automation"))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Print trends for the five policy outcomes. No subplots or event records
# are used; these remain branch-month aggregate summaries.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
outcomes = [
    "median_cycle_h",
    "p90_cycle_h",
    "rework_pct",
    "escalation_pct",
    "csat_1to5",
]

for outcome in outcomes:
    print(f"\nTREND: {outcome}")
    print(
        df.pivot(index="month_label", columns="branch", values=outcome)
          .round(3)
          .to_string()
    )

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Transparent adopter-specific pre/post contrasts.
# These are arithmetic changes only. A causal interpretation would require
# parallel counterfactual trends, no anticipation, consistent treatment,
# and no interference through the shared fraud desk.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
adoption_dates = (
    df.loc[df["automation"].eq(1)]
      .groupby("branch")["month"]
      .min()
      .to_dict()
)

contrast_rows = []
contrast_vars = ["automated_fast_path_pct"] + outcomes

for branch, adoption_date in adoption_dates.items():
    branch_df = df.loc[df["branch"].eq(branch)]
    pre = branch_df.loc[branch_df["month"] < adoption_date]
    post = branch_df.loc[branch_df["month"] >= adoption_date]

    row = {
        "branch": branch,
        "adoption_date": adoption_date.strftime("%Y-%m"),
        "n_pre": len(pre),
        "n_post": len(post),
    }
    for variable in contrast_vars:
        row[f"{variable}_pre"] = pre[variable].mean()
        row[f"{variable}_post"] = post[variable].mean()
        row[f"{variable}_change"] = (
            post[variable].mean() - pre[variable].mean()
        )
    contrast_rows.append(row)

contrasts = pd.DataFrame(contrast_rows)
print("\nADOPTER-SPECIFIC PRE/POST CONTRASTS:")
display_cols = ["branch", "adoption_date", "n_pre", "n_post"]
for variable in contrast_vars:
    display_cols += [
        f"{variable}_pre",
        f"{variable}_post",
        f"{variable}_change",
    ]
print(contrasts[display_cols].round(3).to_string(index=False))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Construct staggered-adoption relative time.
# Event time -1 is the omitted reference category in the model below.
# Never-treated branches have all event indicators equal to zero.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
df["month_index"] = df["month"].dt.to_period("M").astype("int64")
adoption_index = {
    branch: date.to_period("M").ordinal
    for branch, date in adoption_dates.items()
}
df["adoption_index"] = df["branch"].map(adoption_index)
df["relative_time"] = df["month_index"] - df["adoption_index"]

treated_ever = df["adoption_index"].notna()
df["rt_le_m2"] = (
    treated_ever & df["relative_time"].le(-2)
).astype(int)
df["rt_0"] = (
    treated_ever & df["relative_time"].eq(0)
).astype(int)
df["rt_1"] = (
    treated_ever & df["relative_time"].eq(1)
).astype(int)
df["rt_ge2"] = (
    treated_ever & df["relative_time"].ge(2)
).astype(int)

print("\nRELATIVE-TIME COUNTS (NaN denotes never treated):")
print(df["relative_time"].value_counts(dropna=False).sort_index())
print("\nRELATIVE-TIME INDICATORS:")
print(
    df[
        [
            "branch", "month_label", "automation", "relative_time",
            "rt_le_m2", "rt_0", "rt_1", "rt_ge2"
        ]
    ].to_string(index=False)
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# DESCRIPTIVE EVENT-TIME PANEL MODEL.
#
# This is not presented as a causal estimator. In particular:
# 1. conditional parallel trends may fail because adoption was targeted;
# 2. routing, staffing, complexity, and local practice changed over time;
# 3. shared fraud-desk queues create interference;
# 4. treatment effects and treatment intensity may be dynamic/heterogeneous;
# 5. clustered asymptotics with five branches are unreliable.
#
# The pooled event-time indicators avoid interpreting one homogeneous
# automation coefficient, but they are not a substitute for cohort-specific
# group-time ATT estimation in a larger sample.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
event_formula = (
    "median_cycle_h ~ rt_le_m2 + rt_0 + rt_1 + rt_ge2 "
    "+ C(branch) + C(month_label)"
)
event_model = smf.ols(event_formula, data=df).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"], "use_correction": True},
)

event_terms = ["rt_le_m2", "rt_0", "rt_1", "rt_ge2"]
event_results = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "coefficient": event_model.params,
        "branch_clustered_se": event_model.bse,
        "p_value_small_cluster_warning": event_model.pvalues,
    }
).loc[event_terms]

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE EVENT-TIME PANEL MODEL FOR median_cycle_h:")
print(event_results.round(4).to_string())

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# TREATMENT-INTENSITY MODEL.
#
# The coefficient is a descriptive conditional association per one
# percentage-point increase in completed automated-fast-path share.
# A causal dose-response interpretation would require sequential
# exchangeability, positivity, correct temporal ordering, stable treatment
# versions, and measured queue spillovers. Those assumptions are not
# established here. Complexity and workload may also be co-interventions or
# post-treatment variables, so conditioning on them changes the estimand and
# can introduce bias.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
intensity_formula = (
    "median_cycle_h ~ automated_fast_path_pct "
    "+ high_complexity_pct + load_per_fte "
    "+ C(branch) + C(month_label)"
)
intensity_model = smf.ols(intensity_formula, data=df).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": df["branch"], "use_correction": True},
)

intensity_terms = [
    "automated_fast_path_pct",
    "high_complexity_pct",
    "load_per_fte",
]
intensity_results = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "coefficient": intensity_model.params,
        "branch_clustered_se": intensity_model.bse,
        "p_value_small_cluster_warning": intensity_model.pvalues,
    }
).loc[intensity_terms]

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE TREATMENT-INTENSITY MODEL FOR median_cycle_h:")
print(intensity_results.round(4).to_string())

print(
    "\nINTERPRETATION WARNING: Neither regression proves causality. "
    "Use the coefficients as descriptive sensitivity analyses only; "
    "prioritize cohort-specific contrasts, queue data, and prospective "
    "randomization."
)

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automation availability was followed by steadily increasing fast-path use, mixed raw median changes, worse raw Alpha/Beta p90, higher escalation, and no clear deterioration in respondent-mean CSAT.
- Suggestive: Calendar-aligned comparisons for both adoption cohorts indicate roughly four hours better median performance, six to eight hours better p90 performance, and about two percentage points less all-case rework than contemporaneous controls.
- Conditionally causal: If cohort-specific parallel trends, no anticipation, stable implementation, and adequately modeled fraud-desk interference hold, the supplied contrasts are compatible with an availability effect of about a 9% median reduction and roughly a 1 percentage-point escalation increase.
- Not supported: “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a universal, per-case, branch-independent effect—or any claim that customer satisfaction or tail performance necessarily improves.
