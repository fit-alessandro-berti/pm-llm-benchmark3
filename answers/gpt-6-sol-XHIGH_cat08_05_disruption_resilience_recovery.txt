The temporal deviations reinforce the service and evidence tails: backlog-to-pick exceeded baseline p95, dispatch-to-phone-POD delay varied widely, and invoice-close-to-audit-remediation extended work beyond apparent completion.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience here means protecting clinically important delivery **while preserving the control's purpose with trustworthy evidence**, even when the usual electronic step is unavailable. An offline authorization verified by trained staff is a legitimate process variant; a shipment based only on customer history is not. Likewise, a signed, contemporaneous manual handoff can substitute for a failed scan's evidence function, while an unrecorded dispatch cannot. Deviation from the baseline therefore signals a question to investigate, not automatic failure or success. The fall in fitness from 0.96 to 0.71 and in fitting traces from 91% to 44% identifies the scale of process change, but neither metric proves the clinical value or safety of each case.

B is the clearest control-preserving adaptation. The capacity choices in A, I, and J demonstrate resilience **when** authorization, product controls, and transparent allocation rules remain intact. K is an essential recovery capability. C and unrecorded G are risky shortcuts: they preserve movement by dropping critical verification. A as actually used, and D, E, F, H, and I, are mixed adaptations whose value depends on the strength, timing, and coverage of their compensating controls. L is valuable only as an exception-resolution process; treating after-the-fact remediation as proof of real-time compliance conceals noncompliance. Management may accept longer waits and bounded manual work for lower-risk orders. Its risk appetite cannot waive mandatory authorization, temperature, dual-control, lot-traceability, or custody requirements.

# 4. Recovery process model

Use two explicit statuses for each case: **delivery state** (held, picked, dispatched, delivered) and **control state** (verified, open exception, quarantined/investigating, closed). Delivery or invoicing must not automatically close a control exception.

1. **Activate and govern.** Trigger the disruption protocol when WMS or authorization is unavailable, scans or inventory synchronization are unreliable, cyber response isolates systems, or reconciliation detects conflicting stock. The incident commander records the start time, affected functions, approved manual procedures, staff roles, and current snapshot version. Use manual mode for affected functions through T2 and T3; restore each electronic step only after validation, not merely when it comes online.
2. **Receive and triage.** Assign a unique order ID and log receipt time, customer, destination, clinical urgency, requested time, products, and any previous attempts. Prioritize immediate hospital needs, then other time-critical hospital and essential pharmacy needs, then routine pharmacy, then bulk. Within a tier, consider promised time and waiting age; reserve capacity for essential pharmacy demand and review deferrals each shift. Product risk determines control intensity independently of customer priority.
3. **Apply pre-dispatch gates.** For **every** order, verify customer and product authorization through a current offline source or a documented callback to an independently verified contact; verify item, quantity, lot/expiry as applicable, physical availability, and a manual reservation; obtain a release decision. Hold an order if authorization or material identity cannot be established. For **cold-chain**, add documented starting condition, continuous or validated interval temperature monitoring, packaging/route limits, and excursion disposition before release. For **controlled substances**, require two authorized people for count, release, custody transfer, and discrepancy handling, with secure storage and recipient verification. For **recall-sensitive** items, capture lot/expiry and destination so affected units can be found rapidly. Where categories overlap, apply all relevant controls. A clinician's urgency may expedite checks but does not remove them.
4. **Pick, release, and hand off with contemporaneous evidence.** Use controlled, numbered paper or offline forms with order ID, source and timestamp of authorization, snapshot/physical count, reservation, item/lot/expiry/quantity, picker and independent checker, release decision, temperature record, dual-control signatures, courier identity, handoff time, and recipient POD. Protect originals and record corrections with author, time, and reason; do not backdate or fabricate scans. If a required high-risk record or control is missing, hold or quarantine the goods and escalate to quality/compliance. Log lower-risk evidence gaps as open exceptions under a documented disposition, never as silently completed checks.
5. **Dispatch, backlog, and communication.** Dispatch only after applicable gates pass. Record the actual handoff and expected delivery; obtain appropriate recipient evidence. Place deferred orders in a visible backlog with reason, owner, age, priority, next review time, and customer communication. Reassess priority at least each shift; prevent repeated deferral of pharmacy orders and report fill, waits, and cancellations by segment.
6. **Restore, reconcile, and close.** On system return, validate authorization data, inventory synchronization, scanning, and interface timestamps before switching each step back. Import manual records without overwriting originals; match each order to stock movement, lot, courier handoff, POD, temperature/custody evidence, and invoice. Resolve duplicates and stock discrepancies before affected inventory is available for new reservation. Route each gap to a named remediation queue, with same-shift escalation for controlled, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, or possibly misdelivered goods. Recover evidence only from authentic contemporaneous sources; if proof never existed, document the gap, assess product/customer impact, quarantine or recall/escalate/report where required, and do not mark it compliant. Close the control state only after quality/compliance disposition; close the financial case only under the defined evidence and invoice policy. Track the remaining queue separately from service cycle time until it reaches an accepted, risk-reviewed level.

# 5. Resilience indicators

| Indicator | Leading or lagging; measurement and use |
|---|---|
| Manual capacity and service protection | **Leading:** staffed manual authorization, release, pick, and courier slots versus urgent demand; hospital and essential-pharmacy orders approaching their due times. **Lagging:** fill rate and median/p90 order-to-delivery time by segment and product. Track hospital protection without hiding the pharmacy decline seen in T2. |
| Throughput | **Leading:** accepted orders, completed reservations, picks, releases, and dispatches per shift; bottleneck queue lengths. **Lagging:** delivered orders per business day and on-time completion by segment, with case mix and period length stated. |
| Traceability | **Leading:** percentage of active orders with contemporaneous order ID, lot/expiry, manual movement, custody, and recipient fields complete before dispatch; scan outages and age of unuploaded records. **Lagging:** traceability exceptions and unresolved lot/destination mismatches. Compare with 0.8% baseline and monitor the T2/T4 excess. |
| Control completeness | **Leading:** percentage of orders with verified authorization and release before dispatch; cold-chain records and controlled dual-control completed at the point required; number of hard-stop overrides. **Lagging:** confirmed missing temperature and dual-control rates, excursions, and compliance findings. Required pre-dispatch controls should have zero unauthorized waivers; baseline rates of 0.4% and 0.2% show historical defects to improve. |
| Backlog and evidence debt | **Leading:** backlog age distribution and number beyond promised time by priority/customer; open remediation cases by risk, owner, and age, including cases already delivered or invoiced. **Lagging:** p90 backlog-to-pick time, days to reconcile manual stock, days from delivery to control closure, and aged audit cases. |
| Rework and loss | **Leading:** duplicate reservations, count mismatches, failed handoffs, and incomplete forms caught before release. **Lagging:** correction/rework and cancellation rates, including reasons; compare T2 12.5%/7.8% and T4 5.8%/3.0% with baseline 3.5%/2.1%. |
| Fairness | **Leading:** share of capacity reserved for essential pharmacy demand, deferral age and frequency by customer group, and documented priority exceptions. **Lagging:** fill and on-time gaps among comparable urgency/risk groups, cancellations, and repeat deferrals. Investigate the T2 hospital 91% versus pharmacy 61% fill gap rather than treating aggregate throughput as sufficient. |

Use consistent denominators and show these measures by period, segment, and product risk; report both incident cases and the open post-incident queue. An improving cycle-time median alone is not a recovery criterion.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Action | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Approve an offline control matrix and hard stops: verified authorization for every order; product-specific temperature, dual-control, release, lot, and custody requirements; named quality/compliance escalation. | Medium | Preserves essential deliveries without repeating C or unrecorded G; makes allowable variants explicit. | Manual verification is slower and source lists can age, so they need owners and refresh tests. |
| 2 | Build a controlled manual kit: current offline eligibility lists, numbered order/pick/handoff forms, secure temperature and custody logs, a live reservation ledger, and trained alternates. Exercise it on hospital, pharmacy, cold-chain, controlled, and recall scenarios. | Medium to high | Reduces dependence on stale snapshots and reconstructed scans; spreads work beyond a few compliance staff. | Human error and physical-document loss remain; independent checks and secure storage are needed. |
| 3 | Establish a published allocation and communication rule with an essential-pharmacy capacity floor, age limits for deferral, and shift-level review of fill and cancellations. | Low to medium | Retains hospital fast response while reducing the long pharmacy and bulk tails and opaque decisions. | Severe scarcity may still force delays; record the clinical rationale and notify affected customers. |
| 4 | Require staged restoration with interface/stock validation, manual-to-electronic matching, and a temporary hold on disputed lots or quantities before reuse. | Medium to high | Prevents T3 unreliable synchronization from becoming duplicate allocation or false traceability. | Reconciliation can slow normal throughput and uncover losses needing investigation. |
| 5 | Operate a staffed exception and audit-remediation cell from the first missing event, with risk-based deadlines, executive visibility, and separate delivery/control closure reports. | Medium | Exposes the hidden T4 workload and resolves authentic evidence gaps before they become an indefinite tail. | Some missing real-time evidence cannot be repaired; those cases require honest disposition and possible reporting or product action. |
| 6 | Test the complete protocol in disruption drills and review process-mining paths, service results, control exceptions, and fairness after each exercise or incident. | Medium, recurring | Finds bottlenecks such as backlog-to-pick and variable POD delay before a real event. | Drills cannot reproduce every cyber or supply-chain constraint; revise rules from actual results. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower routine service, temporary manual work, and documented, bounded deferral to protect urgent patients, while funding the later reconciliation work as part of the incident cost. It should not accept unverified authorization, unrecorded dispatch, missing mandatory temperature or dual-control evidence, or invoice closure that hides unresolved control exceptions. The recommendation would change only if contemporaneous case-level evidence showed that an alternative manual control met the same authorization, product-quality, custody, and traceability requirements, and segment-level results showed that the allocation rule protected essential pharmacy demand as well as hospitals.
