## 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Objective and functional unit.** Optimize environmental impact **per delivered, conforming line item**, while reporting order-level impacts because the current log is flattened. Once line-item identifiers and shipped quantities are available, also report impacts per shipped item and per kilogram shipped. “Per order” alone can favor batching that produces more items than customers ultimately order.

**Environmental boundary includes:**

- Manufacturing energy recorded in `energy_kwh`, including energy attributable to additional visits and rework.
- All transportation legs recorded in `transport_kgco2e`, including additional pickups, replacement shipments, and return/rework movements once identified.
- `material_scrap_kg`, packaging material, production scrap, and obsolete or cancelled production.
- Batch waiting and WIP only insofar as they lead to extra energy use, missed shipments, or obsolescence.
- Packaging rework, inspection failures, damage-related returns, and replacement production.

Supplier fabrication, inbound material production, product use, and end-of-life disposal are **outside the quantified boundary because the necessary evidence is absent**. They must not be assumed negligible. A complete lifecycle claim would require supplier-specific energy/material data, part weights, actual packaging specifications, return logistics, and disposal or reuse information.

**Evidence limitations and operating assumptions:**

- Segment averages describe different route and production mixes; they are not causal estimates of an intervention’s effect.
- The selected-path table is not exhaustive. DFG edge counts cannot be added as independent orders because of rework loops, repeated events, and disruptions.
- The log has 12,000 starts, 11,200 closes, and 530 observed cancellations. The remaining 270 cases may be unfinished, outside the extraction window, or missed by the model. Batch-wait paths also have residual discrepancies. These should be resolved rather than treated as a measured cancellation or failure rate.
- The low precision of emergency and partial-shipment combinations argues for pilots, fallback capacity, and explicit uncertainty rather than large irreversible changes.
- Energy and transport should remain separate metrics unless a consistent, time- and location-specific emissions factor is available. Per-item batch savings cannot be multiplied into an order-level saving without knowing batch quantities and actual shipments.
- Service-contract protection, R1 route redundancy, and required heat treatment and inspection remain constraints—not optional environmental trade-offs.

## 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Report these indicators by case segment, machine group, route, and intervention exposure. Track both order and line-item denominators.

| Dimension | Indicators and process evidence |
|---|---|
| **Energy** | Total `energy_kwh` per order and per accepted/shipped line; kWh per shipped item or kg; first-pass and rework energy; machine-level metering reconciled with event estimates. Segment separately by CNC_A, CNC_B, heat treatment, inspection, and emergency production. |
| **Transport** | All-leg `transport_kgco2e` per order and per shipped item/kg; emissions by mode and leg; number of shipment attempts; vehicle/load utilization; empty dedicated-van returns. Report both original and revised delivery promises. |
| **Materials and waste** | `material_scrap_kg` per accepted item; total production scrap; packaging kg per shipped item; proportion of standard versus reinforced packaging; obsolete inventory and write-offs; material associated with cancellations. Do not add unlike material categories into one kg figure without a defined common basis. |
| **Rework and returns** | Rework cases and loops per started order; additional machine, packaging, and transport visits; first-pass inspection pass rate; damage-related and other returns; material and energy consumed by rework. Distinguish packaging rework from production rework. |
| **Batching and WIP** | Batch-size distribution; energy/item by machine; batch wait; heat-treatment wait; queue and WIP age; percentage of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days; forecast error and associated obsolete material. |
| **Service and flow** | Median and p90 cycle time against the **first promised date**; late-delivery rate against original and customer-accepted revised promises; first-fill rate; partial-shipment and backorder rates; complete-order cycle time; cancellations; expedite rate. |
| **Quality** | Inspection-cell pass rate and queue; escaped defects; damage-related returns; packaging nonconformance; Q1 conformance; defects by machine/part combination. No reduction in inspection should be counted as efficiency if it increases later failures. |
| **Resilience** | Percentage of route decisions with a feasible ground/consolidated alternative at greater than 85% predicted on-time probability; tested alternative-route availability for Service-contract customers; emergency-order response and recovery time; retained surge capacity; disruption-related reattempts. |
| **Policy conformance** | SP1 violations and their reasons; SP2 missing-justification rate; Q1 violations and valid low-damage certifications; exception rate, approval, expiry, and subsequent service/environmental outcomes. |

Useful reference points are the Standard-stock Service-contract average of **7.8 versus 4.2 transport kgCO2e/order** for Standard-stock Regular, and the selected stock-path difference of **18.5 for air versus 3.6 for ground**. Critical dedicated-van service is much more transport-intensive, while MTO impacts are more closely associated with production energy, scrap, and rework. These comparisons identify opportunities but do not establish intervention effects.

## 3. Intervention options

### S1. Green shipping recommender

- **Target:** All route decisions, especially `Package standard/reinforced -> Ship air/ground/consolidated truck`; priority for Standard-stock Service-contract cases with feasible ground service.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Calculate route feasibility from promised lead time, calibrated route reliability, current slack, customer segment, and disruption status. Recommend ground or consolidation only when the promised date remains achievable at the SP1 probability threshold.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Should reduce unnecessary air use without changing product quality. Poor reliability estimates could cause lateness, and eliminating too many alternatives could weaken resilience. Retain an eligible backup route for Service-contract customers.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Predicted versus actual on-time probability, original-promise lateness, p90 transit time, transport emissions across all legs, SP1 overrides, and tested alternative-route availability. The DFG’s 620 standard-package air paths are not all Standard-stock Service-contract orders, so the affected subset must be established through case-level joins.

### S2. Dynamic batching with segment-specific waiting caps

- **Target:** `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC -> Heat treatment/Inspection`, primarily MTO.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Form batches using actual demand, due-date slack, and downstream capacity. The observed CNC_A reduction from **2.8 to 1.3 kWh/item** between batch sizes 1–3 and 12–20 supports batching, but the associated wait increase from **4h to 38h** makes a universal large-batch policy unsuitable.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Waiting and correlated queue risk can increase; MTO Service-contract cases should receive tighter caps than cases with an explicitly negotiated feasible date. Do not skip heat treatment or inspections to recover delay. Large WIP pools also increase disruption exposure.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Batch wait and queue percentiles, energy/item and order, heat-treatment-associated late risk, WIP age, forecast error, and the 14-day unshipped-item rate. Calibrate caps by segment rather than using batch size 12–20 as a universal target.

### S3. Prefer CNC_A over CNC_B when capacity and slack permit

- **Target:** MTO assignments currently using CNC_B, including the `CNC_B -> inspection -> rework` path.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Use CNC_A’s lower observed unit energy and rework rate—**9% versus 18% on CNC_B**—to reduce processing and hidden material waste.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** A queue shift to CNC_A could initially increase waiting or overload inspection. Do not eliminate CNC_B, skills, or qualified capacity without a recovery plan; otherwise a CNC_A outage could create emergency production.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Machine assignment, CNC_A queue, inspection capacity, first-pass yield, rework/return rates, energy per accepted item, and on-time completion. Control for part complexity because the observational machine comparison is not necessarily causal.

### S4. Replace avoidable air shipment with priority picking plus ground for Standard-stock Service-contract orders

- **Target:** Air shipments for this segment where ground still satisfies the promise at greater than 85% probability.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Address the finding that these orders are often sent by air even with sufficient lead time. Air emits approximately **4–6 times** the transport emissions of ground.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** A ground shipment may take about **24 hours longer** than air but need not violate the promise. Keep air where slack is inadequate, and retain alternative carriers under R1. The reported **22% SP1 violation rate is not itself the avoidable-air share**.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Eligibility, original-promise and p90 delivery performance, priority-pick response, actual mode, transport emissions, customer contacts, and disruption recovery. Do not improve the headline late rate merely by changing the promised date.

### S5. Twice-daily consolidated truck departures for non-urgent stock orders

- **Target:** `Package standard -> Ship ground` orders for Standard-stock Regular and other cases with substantial route slack.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Replace individual parcel movements with scheduled consolidated departures. The prompt supports approximately **35–55% lower transport emissions on comparable eligible legs**.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Adds a **12–24-hour consolidation wait**. A missed departure leaves no same-day recovery margin, so only genuinely non-urgent orders should participate. Keep a later backup departure or carrier.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Eligible versus delayed orders, p90 promise performance, load factor, actual departure and delivery times, all-leg emissions, missed cutoff recovery, and customer-requested expedite. Do not extrapolate per-leg savings to all orders.

### S6. Packaging–route–geometry compatibility check before packaging

- **Target:** Both `Package standard/reinforced -> Rework packaging` branches. The DFG records 620 packaging-rework entries and 550 subsequent packaging exits.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Validate part weight, dimensions, fragility, and route handling requirements before selecting packaging, preventing incompatible material, repacking, and damage-related returns.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Should reduce packaging loops and avoid unnecessary duplicate material. The check must not downgrade Q1 protection: MTO parts over 8 kg still require reinforced packaging unless an approved inspection certifies low damage risk.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Packaging rework loops, packaging kg/item, route incompatibility causes, damage returns, packing time, and Q1 violations. Distinguish correct heavy-part reinforcement from unnecessary over-packaging.

### S7. Inspection quality gate for high-rework machine/part combinations

- **Target:** `Inspection -> Rework production` and repeated CNC visits, particularly combinations associated with rushed inspection or CNC_B.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Use a structured defect check, adequate inspection time, and containment of suspect lots before release. This addresses upstream defects that otherwise become rework loops, returns, and repeat freight.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Adds a gate wait but can reduce total lead time through fewer loops. It must retain required inspection and heat-treatment steps; simply recording a defect as “rework” does not improve quality.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Inspection queue, first-pass yield, false rejects, escaped defects, rework loops, scrap, repeat energy, and return causes. The DFG’s 470 rework entries and 390 CNC re-entries are event counts, not necessarily distinct defective cases.

### S8. Pilot shared emergency courier lanes

- **Target:** Selected Critical-downtime dedicated-van trips, especially those with overlapping destinations or incomplete return loads.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Pool emergency requests into scheduled shared lanes or multi-stop routes, potentially reducing empty repositioning and per-stop vehicle use.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Pooling may increase waiting or total route distance. Do not remove dedicated capacity until shared lanes demonstrate equivalent tail service and disruption recovery; retain a tested fallback for true downtime.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Pickup-to-delivery median and p90, load factor, empty returns, all-leg emissions, failed first dispatch, recovery capacity, and service under simulated disruption. The **52 kgCO2e/order** dedicated-van path is a reason to test alternatives, not evidence that any substitution preserves service.

### S9. Distinguish true downtime from precautionary urgency

- **Target:** `Availability check -> Emergency production` and Critical-downtime classification.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Verify whether urgency reflects actual downtime consequences, safety/production criticality, or only conservative customer classification. Reassess available stock and feasible routes for precautionary cases.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Better classification can direct scarce capacity to genuine emergencies. Misclassification could be severely damaging, and the stock/ground Critical exception already has **23h median cycle and 9% late delivery**, versus 14h and 3% for the selected emergency-van path. It is not a safe blanket substitute.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Verified urgency reason, consequence of delay, stock feasibility, emergency frequency, p90 response, emissions, and misclassification outcomes. Do not relabel precautionary cases merely to bypass SP2.

### S10. Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular MTO customers

- **Target:** MTO Regular cases, whose segment averages are **18% late, 18.5 kWh/order, and 0.90 kg scrap/order**.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Before acceptance, offer capacity-backed dates aligned with planned batching; let customers choose a reliable slower option where desired. This can avoid rushed small batches, rework, and emergency freight.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Honest dates can improve delivery reliability, but customers may over-order, cancel, or later demand air. Service-contract cases should retain tighter protection. Negotiation must not simply transfer an unfulfilled original promise into a “success.”
- **Evidence to monitor:** Original and accepted promises, acceptance/decline, complaints, repeat orders, actual batch size, energy, expedited transport, and obsolescence. Measure emissions per item eventually shipped, including items that are never shipped.

### S11. Make availability checks line-aware and reduce avoidable partial shipments

- **Target:** `Availability check -> Pick -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait`, observed in 1,010 partial-shipment entries; the selected partial/backorder path has **79h median cycle time and 24% late delivery**.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Check available-to-promise by line, offer complete or partial fulfillment according to customer preference, and show a reliable backorder date. Avoid creating a partial shipment when a coordinated full shipment can meet the promise.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Full-order blocking could delay lines already available; forced waiting could also worsen resilience. Do not suppress partial shipments by concealing shortages or charging penalties for an unavoidable split.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Line-level first fill, partial and backorder edges, complete-order cycle time, cancellations, second pickups, emissions, and customer choice. Report available-line service separately from complete-order service.

### S12. Sustainability exception and override log

- **Target:** SP1, SP2, Q1, and any intervention guardrail.
- **Improvement mechanism:** Capture the decision, affected orders, reason, predicted service/quality consequence, emissions impact, approver, and expiry. Review recurring exceptions for redesign rather than treating them as permanent exemptions.
- **Service, quality, and resilience:** Provides auditability and prevents unsafe green optimization. SP1/SP2 exceptions must be genuine, not route relabeling. Q1 exceptions require the specified low-damage certification, not merely an override comment.
- **Evidence to monitor:** Missing versus unnecessary exceptions separately, approval and expiry compliance, downstream lateness/returns, and emissions. This option has no direct environmental effect; its value is control.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching shifts impact into waiting and lateness.**  
   Larger batches reduce CNC_A energy/item, but the 12–20 batch range increases median wait from 4h to 38h. Heat-treatment batching has the lowest unit energy above ten parts, yet waiting cases have **1.7 times the late-delivery risk**. More queueing can trigger expediting, extra shifts, or emergency production, offsetting the energy gain.  
   **Control:** Segment-specific wait caps, due-date feasibility checks, WIP limits, and monitoring of expedite substitution.

2. **Production efficiency becomes stranded inventory.**  
   Approximately **6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days** when forecasts are wrong. Their unit energy may be efficient, but their total production and eventual disposal or obsolescence still consume resources.  
   **Control:** Include unshipped production, inventory age, and write-offs in decisions; do not reward only `kWh/item` at the machine.

3. **Ground or consolidation displaces emissions into later air shipment.**  
   A missed 12–24h consolidation cutoff, unreliable ground route, or customer escalation can cause a second expedited movement. Air is already approximately 4–6 times more emitting than ground. Dedicated vans with empty returns create a similar whole-route inefficiency.  
   **Control:** Use only orders with route-recovery margin, retain later departures and alternate carriers, and measure all attempts rather than the initial route.

4. **Packaging reduction displaces material into damage and returns.**  
   Removing reinforcement can lower immediate package mass, but it can cause damage, replacement production, customer returns, and reverse freight. For heavy MTO parts, reinforced packaging reduces damage-related returns by **40%** despite doubling material.  
   **Control:** Enforce Q1, validate part geometry and fragility, and evaluate packaging plus return logistics together. Do not apply a blanket lightweight-packaging rule.

5. **Rework reduction through inspection acceleration creates more external failure.**  
   Shifting more work to CNC_A or promising faster releases can overload the inspection cell. Rushed inspection may make the initial energy and queue look better while increasing hidden rework loops, returns, and customer disruption.  
   **Control:** Use the S7 gate, track escaped defects, and retain adequate inspection capacity. Measure rework by accepted conforming output, not by completed rework activities.

6. **Carbon-aware promises can change customer behavior.**  
   Customers may over-order to hedge uncertain dates, cancel more often, or demand air after a delay. A later promised date can therefore lower current-production emissions while increasing obsolete stock or subsequent freight.  
   **Control:** Use transparent capacity-backed choices, measure acceptance and subsequent orders, and evaluate cohorts rather than treating negotiated lead time as a permanent carbon reduction.

7. **Efficiency concentration removes fallback options.**  
   Eliminating CNC_B, concentrating large CNC_A batches, or replacing dedicated vans with a single shared-courier model can improve average resource use but make the process brittle during disruption. The low-precision emergency paths make this especially important.  
   **Control:** Retain qualified alternative capacity until pilots demonstrate recovery performance; cross-train resources; and stress-test batch and transport dependencies.

8. **Local improvements shift cost or emissions to another segment or lifecycle stage.**  
   A quota of greener Standard-stock routes must not be met by degrading Critical-downtime or MTO Service-contract service. Supplier production and returns can also move environmental cost outside the logged boundary.  
   **Control:** Use segment-specific guardrails, retain original-promise reporting, require lifecycle evidence for material changes, and audit whether lower measured emissions coincide with more returns or outsourced impacts.

## 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a **promise-to-production-to-route control loop**, with the selected interventions operating together:

1. **Establish credible promises and urgency at order entry.**  
   Use S11 for line-level availability and customer fulfillment choice. Use S9 to distinguish verified downtime from precautionary urgency. Use S10 only for Regular MTO cases where a capacity-backed alternative is explicitly accepted. Report both original and revised promises.

2. **Produce to a feasible, quality-protected schedule.**  
   Combine S2, S3, and S7: use bounded dynamic batches, prefer CNC_A when its queue and due-date slack permit, and strengthen inspection for high-risk combinations. MTO Service-contract orders receive tighter waiting protection and capacity priority. CNC_B remains a qualified fallback rather than being eliminated solely for its average environmental performance.

3. **Select packaging and transport before committing resources.**  
   Apply S6 before packaging, then use S1 and S4 to remove demonstrably avoidable air shipments. Use S5 for non-urgent stock movements with recovery margin. Reinforced packaging remains in place when Q1 requires it. Transport decisions should use actual route reliability, not just the nominal transit-time saving.

4. **Preserve and test emergency resilience.**  
   S8 should begin as a controlled pilot, not a fleet-wide replacement. S9 must be operating before emergency transport is pooled. Retain sufficient dedicated or equivalent response capacity and a tested fallback. Neither true Critical-downtime service nor R1 Service-contract redundancy should be traded for lower average emissions.

5. **Wrap the redesign in S12 governance.**  
   Every route, batch, quality, and urgency exception should have a reason, approver, expected consequence, and expiry. Repeat exceptions indicate a process-design problem, not a reason to normalize the override.

**Why this package is balanced:** It addresses the high-emission stock air paths and avoidable consolidation opportunities, but also tackles the much larger MTO energy, scrap, and rework burden. It does not make service contracts absorb all environmental improvement. It preserves air and dedicated transport where service value is genuine, protects Q1 and inspection, and maintains alternative capacity.

A practical rollout is:

- **First:** data repair, S12, packaging compatibility, targeted inspection, and review of avoidable Standard-stock Service-contract air shipments.
- **Then:** controlled pilots of S2, S3, S5, S8, S10, and S11, with existing fallback processes.
- **Scale only where:** segment-level service and quality are non-deteriorating, emergency recovery is demonstrated, and total environmental impact improves after accounting for rework, extra transport, and unshipped production.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

### A. Repair and enrich the evidence base

Add stable `order_id` and `line_id` relationships, part quantity, actual weight, dimensions, fragility, and promised/accepted dates. Record:

- Original promise, promise revision, customer acceptance, predicted route probability, and actual delivery.
- Batch quantity, release time, actual wait, WIP age, and forecast vintage.
- Machine assignment, inspection result, defect root cause, rework reason, and accepted production quantity.
- Packaging selected, packaging kg, route/geometry validation, damage return, and replacement shipment.
- Every transportation leg, load factor, empty return, and failed dispatch.
- Emergency justification, verified consequence of delay, approval, and exception expiry.

Reconcile machine meters with event estimates so shared batch energy is not double-counted. Resolve the 270-case start/close difference and model residual paths before portfolio reporting.

### B. Establish segment-specific baselines and test causality

Maintain baselines for all six reported segments and the relevant variants. For example, service and quality should not be judged only against the blended **5% Standard-stock Service-contract late rate** or the **11% MTO Service-contract rework rate**.

Use controlled pilots, matched comparisons, or staged rollout where feasible. Adjust for part complexity, machine, order size, season, disruption, and customer segment. Test both normal operation and a carrier/production disruption. Do not sum the selected-path averages to estimate total savings.

Validate impact per **delivered, conforming line item**, with order-level results also retained. Include:

- All manufacturing and rework energy.
- All shipment and return legs.
- Scrap, packaging, obsolete production, and cancellation impacts.
- Service, quality, and resilience effects.

### C. Monitor policy conformance explicitly

- **SP1:** For every ground or consolidated recommendation, retain the route-reliability model result, evaluation timestamp, and predicted probability of meeting the original promise. Flag decisions below or at the required greater-than-85% threshold. Review SP1 overrides by customer and route.
- **SP2:** Require a justified Critical-downtime event for dedicated-van use. The **31% missing-justification rate** is a documentation failure to investigate, not proof that every affected trip was unnecessary. Do not use S9 relabeling to evade SP2.
- **Q1:** Join actual part weight to packaging. Flag every MTO item over 8 kg without reinforced packaging unless a valid inspection certification supports the stated exception. Review associated damage, returns, and rework.
- **R1:** Verify an alternative route for Service-contract customers, including its capacity, predicted reliability, and operational activation—not merely its existence in a route table.

### D. Use segment-level guardrails and automatic rollback

For Standard-stock, MTO, and Critical-downtime segments separately, monitor median and p90 cycle time, original and accepted-promise late rates, batch waits, queue lengths, first-pass quality, rework, returns, and emissions.

Pause expansion of an intervention if it causes a material deterioration in a segment, breaches Q1 or required processing, displaces emissions through repeated expedited shipments, or eliminates tested resilience capacity. Record batch-wait cap breaches and missed consolidation cutoffs even when the order is eventually on time.

### E. Govern exceptions and prevent metric gaming

Assign joint ownership by operations, sustainability, quality, and customer service. Require evidence for SP1/SP2/Q1 exceptions and review them after the actual promised date. Audit for:

- Air movements disguised as another ground-service code.
- Revised promises that conceal original lateness.
- Cancelled or unshipped production omitted from sustainability results.
- Rework classified as a normal production run.
- Environmental gains for Standard-stock achieved by worsening Service-contract or Critical-downtime outcomes.
- A shared-courier “reduction” that actually removes emergency capacity without a tested replacement.

Finally, recalculate discovery-model precision after implementation. Emergency and partial-shipment behavior should be re-mined under the new process, with confidence bounds where volumes are small. The redesign should be scaled only on observed net lifecycle and service outcomes, not on the assumption that an average-efficient path is always environmentally or operationally preferable.
