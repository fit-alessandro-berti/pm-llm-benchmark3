# 2. Integrated explanation

No single mechanism produces the observed pattern; the data are best explained as **several mechanisms operating in sequence over the shift, layered on a case-mix gradient that runs in the opposite direction to workload.**

**The start of a shift is slow and error-prone mostly because of what arrives, not who works.** Positions 1–5 carry the previous shift's backlog: mean complexity 0.72, 41% high priority, queue of 46. Early Early-shift and Night-shift work is dominated by complex carryover (48% and 56% high-complexity first hour). Seniors self-select onto these hard cases. So the 18.8-minute average and 3.4% error rate at shift start are largely **case-mix and selection**, with a genuine but smaller **warm-up** component — visible in the fact that Juniors improve much more from their first 20 events than Seniors do, which pure case-mix would not produce.

**The middle of the shift looks like a sweet spot** (positions 16–50: ~13 min, ~2.5–2.7% errors) because three curves cross: warm-up is complete, complexity has drifted down, and queues are still moderate. Calling this a "learning effect" is only half right; if you re-ran the comparison at fixed complexity, the improvement would shrink substantially.

**The end of the shift is where the interesting divergence happens: service time keeps falling while errors and rework rise sharply.** This is the signature of a **speed–quality tradeoff under queue pressure**, not of learning (learning improves both) and not primarily of fatigue (fatigue would typically slow people down, and crucially, late-shift error rates stay near baseline when WIP happens to be low — Seniors 2.2%, Mids 2.9%, Juniors 4.4%). The WIP-stratified table shows the dose–response cleanly within complexity bands: under high WIP, low-complexity work is done 26% faster with nearly triple the error rate, and high-complexity work is done 25% faster with 2.6× the error rate. Workers respond to visible queues by cutting corners: shorter document reads, less verification, rubber-stamped bot classifications (bot corrections spike above queue 70 — meaning under normal load some bot errors slip through unexamined, or under pressure the handoff itself becomes error-generating), and deferred quality checks that get crammed into the last 45 minutes where they are least effective.

**Fatigue and circadian effects are real but secondary and conditional.** The residual late-shift error elevation under *low* WIP (e.g., Juniors 5.1% → 4.4% is actually an improvement, but Night staff stay flat at 3.6–3.7%) suggests fatigue alone adds little where pressure is absent. However, fatigue plausibly **amplifies** the pressure effect — tired workers under high WIP degrade more (Juniors 12.4% vs. Seniors 4.9%), consistent with an interaction rather than a main effect. The circadian contribution shows mainly in the Night shift's slower pace and elevated errors at modest queues, though even that is confounded with its exceptional case mix.

**Measured late-shift speed is also partly artifact.** Junior batching of Document Review late in the Day shift injects runs of very short service times into exactly the bins (positions 76+, Day-shift last hours) where "throughput" looks best, and end-of-shift Quality Check bursts shift error detection timestamps late even when the defect was introduced earlier.

**Why errors rise only in some conditions:** errors are the product of (task difficulty) × (effort per case), and the shift compresses effort only when queues force it. Early: hard cases, full effort → slow, moderate errors. Middle: easier cases, full effort → fast, few errors. Late with low WIP: easier cases, full effort → fast, few errors. Late with high WIP: easier cases, rationed effort, tired workers, skipped checks, unexamined bot output → fastest, most errors. The heterogeneity across cells is the fingerprint of interacting mechanisms, and any explanation that names one cause will prescribe the wrong fix.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Preparation.**

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("claims_events.csv")
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id="case:concept:name",
                            activity_key="concept:name",
                            timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
                            start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp")
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

# event-level service time (minutes)
df["service_min"] = (df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# case-level KPIs for cross-checks
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)      # service vs. waiting decomposition
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df)       # arrival/finish rate to validate WIP columns
temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df)  # hourly workload/time features
```

**(a) Within-resource comparisons.** The key identification move: every comparison should be within-worker so that stable skill differences and case-assignment styles drop out.

```python
# fixed-effects style: demean service_min and error_flag within org:resource
for col in ["service_min", "error_flag"]:
    df[col + "_dm"] = df[col] - df.groupby("org:resource")[col].transform("mean")
# then regress demeaned outcomes on position, WIP, complexity, hour-of-day
```

Model: `service_min ~ C(resource) + position_bin + wip_band + complexity + priority + C(activity_family) + C(shift_id) + hour_of_day`; logistic analogue for `error_flag`. Report the position coefficients before and after adding controls — the shrinkage measures how much of the raw curve was case-mix/pressure.

**(b) Within-shift position effects.** Estimate the position curve separately per resource group and per shift, at fixed complexity tercile and fixed WIP band. A genuine warm-up effect should (i) appear in every shift, (ii) reset at shift boundaries, (iii) be larger for low `resource_experience_months`. Plot with dotted charts:

# 4. Intervention options

1. **Strictly cap shift length for all workers.** *Expected benefit:* limits exposure to the late-shift high-error zone; modest, because the error driver is WIP, not hours per se. *Risk:* reduces capacity, which raises queues, which is the actual error mechanism — the intervention could worsen the problem it targets; also blunt across groups (Seniors show little late-shift degradation under low WIP). *Monitor:* error rate and rework by `minutes_since_shift_start` at fixed WIP; queue length trajectories before/after; total throughput.

2. **Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift.** *Expected benefit:* smooths the case-mix gradient; complex cases handled after warm-up may see fewer errors; makes performance metrics more comparable across positions. *Risk:* complex carryover cases are often the most urgent (41% high priority early); delaying them harms customers and may just relocate, not remove, the complexity–error interaction; conflicts with sensible senior self-selection onto hard cases. *Monitor:* waiting time and SLA breach rate for high-complexity/high-priority cases (`insert_case_service_waiting_time`); error rate on complex cases by position bin.

3. **Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70.** *Expected benefit:* directly targets the strongest mechanism; if paired with an actual response (surge staffing, triage-lite protocol, pausing non-urgent intake) it attacks the speed–quality tradeoff where it lives, including the bot-oversight failure above queue 70. *Risk:* an alert without a playbook adds stress and may worsen rushing; threshold gaming (holding cases out of the queue). *Monitor:* time-above-threshold per shift, error and rework rate conditional on WIP band, service-time distribution during alert windows vs. matched non-alert windows.

4. **Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias.** *Expected benefit:* as a *temporary experiment*, randomization is excellent — it cleanly identifies learning/fatigue/pressure effects free of selection. *Risk:* as a *permanent policy* it destroys valuable matching: juniors would receive complex cases they handle poorly (their high-complexity error rates suggest real harm); expect overall quality decline. *Monitor:* run as a time-boxed A/B; compare error, rework, and service time by (experience × complexity) cell against the matched-assignment baseline.

5. **Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours.** *Expected benefit:* if fatigue amplifies the pressure effect (as the junior high-WIP numbers hint), breaks may lower the interaction term cheaply; well-supported in ergonomics literature. *Risk:* under high WIP, breaks reduce serving capacity, raising queues further; workers may skip breaks under pressure, making the policy nominal. *Monitor:* diff-in-diff on late-shift error rates at fixed WIP for break-compliant vs. non-compliant periods; queue impact of break scheduling.

6. **Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift.** *Expected benefit:* eliminates the end-of-shift Quality Check burst, spreading checks to fresh, unpressured workers; may increase defect-catch rates. *Risk:* delays defect discovery (longer feedback loops, later rework); risk of deferred checks becoming permanently skipped; the fresh shift starts with even more carryover, worsening the early-shift complexity spike. This is acceptable only if checks are deferred, never dropped — removing quality checks to boost throughput is explicitly off the table. *Monitor:* Quality Check coverage rate (checks performed / checks due), defect-catch rate by check timing, rework latency (`get_rework_cases_per_activity` plus time-to-rework), next-shift first-hour load.

7. **Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks.** *Expected benefit:* directly targets the Day-shift pattern (queue 38 → 75, 7.5% late errors): extra capacity lowers WIP, and senior review catches errors including bot misclassifications; likely the highest-yield intervention alongside #3. *Risk:* cost; senior time diverted from complex-case handling; risk that added capacity is absorbed by increased intake (Parkinson effect). *Monitor:* Day-shift end queue, late-two-hours error and rework rate, bot-correction rate above queue 70, senior utilization split between review and own casework.

8. **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates.** *Expected benefit:* essentially none as formulated. *Risk:* raw late-shift error rates measure queue pressure and case assignment, not diligence — punishing them penalizes whoever management scheduled into high-WIP windows and whoever takes hard cases; predictable responses include error under-reporting, cherry-picking easy cases, deferring work to the next shift, and gaming of `error_flag`. This intervention should be rejected; if quality accountability is wanted, it must use complexity- and WIP-adjusted, within-worker metrics, and even then coaching beats penalties. *Monitor (if any accountability scheme is used):* adjusted vs. raw error-rate divergence, error-reporting rates (a sudden drop signals suppression), case-selection shifts per worker.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Selection bias makes raw worker comparisons unfair.** Seniors *choose* complex early-shift cases; juniors inherit what remains; night staff receive concentrated urgent exceptions. A league table of raw error rates therefore ranks *case assignments*, not competence. Worse, punishing raw rates penalizes exactly the prosocial behavior (volunteering for hard cases) the organization depends on, and teaches everyone to avoid difficult work.

**Queue pressure is created by management, not workers.** The dominant error mechanism in this data — high-WIP rushing — is a consequence of staffing levels, demand scheduling, shift design, and the decision to let Day-shift queues grow to 75+. Attributing the resulting errors to individual workers reverses the causal arrow: workers are adapting rationally (triage under load) to conditions they do not control. Accountability should flow to the level that controls the cause.

**Measurement error contaminates every headline number.** `error_flag` depends on Quality Check actually occurring — and checks are deferred, batched into the last 45 minutes, and presumably less thorough there, so late-shift "errors" partly reflect *when detection happens*, not when defects are made. Batched Document Review deflates late-shift service times artificially. Recorded WIP may not match true concurrency. Rework within 7 days censors slower-emerging defects. Any worker-level conclusion drawn before correcting these artifacts is unreliable.

**Automation quality must be audited, not assumed.** Bot_Risk's classifications are corrected more under high queues — this could mean humans catch more bot errors when alert, rubber-stamp them when pressured, or that the bot degrades on the case mix that arrives during peaks. If the bot has a constant error rate and humans stop checking under load, then "human errors" downstream partly originate in the machine, and penalizing the human reviewer is doubly unjust. Establish the bot's ground-truth accuracy by WIP band before attributing any handoff errors.

**Punitive use of error rates corrupts the data and the process.** Once error metrics carry sanctions, expect Goodhart dynamics: under-flagging, quality-check avoidance, informal rework hidden outside the log, and easy-case hoarding. The event log then stops reflecting reality, destroying the very monitoring capability the organization needs. Error data should feed system redesign and coaching; if individual metrics are ever used, they must be within-worker, complexity- and WIP-adjusted, aggregated over long windows, and reviewed with worker participation. Also respect privacy norms (e.g., works-council/GDPR constraints on individual performance monitoring): analyze at group level by default, individual level only with governance.

**Operationally**, beware interventions that fix a symptom while feeding the cause: shorter shifts and mandatory breaks reduce capacity and can raise the WIP that drives errors; deferring quality checks can silently become skipping them; randomized assignment as policy sacrifices real matching gains for analytic purity. Pilot everything with a control group and event-log-based before/after measurement.
