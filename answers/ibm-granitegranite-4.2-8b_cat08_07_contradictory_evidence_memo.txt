### 3. Explanation of metric conflicts  

The evidence shows these conflicts are consistent, not contradictory, due to sensitivity to segment, tail behavior, and process centralization:  
- **High fitness but low precision**: The pilot model achieves higher fitness (0.94 vs 0.87) because it fits the observed frequency of the simplified coordinator path (e.g., many cases follow "combined validate/schedule"  "installation visit" without survey). However, low precision (0.51 vs 0.79) indicates the model permits too many logically inconsistent or rarely observed combinations (e.g., skipped survey + manual activation + second visit), as noted by the process-mining team. This reflects over-fitting to pilot patterns rather than general process truth; precision drops because the model does not restrict these combos, leading to misrepresentation of realistic paths (hence alignment diagnostics show frequent move-ons like "Billing start before Activation test").  
- **Improved median but worse tail (p90/p95)**: Median cycle time is a central tendency measure; it improved (14.2  10.8 days) because simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot) saw consistent shortening (6 to 8 days). However, the tail (p90/p95) worsened (31.0  38.5 days; 45.0  62.0 days) because higher-risk segments (apartments ~29%, old buildings ~18%) experienced increased second visits and manual activations (18% vs 11%, 19% vs 8%), causing longer outliers. The DFG shows InstallationActivation dropped (78%  60%) and FailedSecond rose (10%  17%) for these segments, directly lengthening the upper tail. Thus median and tail improvements/worsings coexist due to segment heterogeneity—median reflects the benefit zone, tail reflects the harm zone.  
- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience**: Coordinator ownership reduced the observed count of cases with 3 handovers (34%  12%), improving handover efficiency for simple houses (where it works well). However, centralization around the coordinator (reduced handovers of context) increased vulnerability: when a coordinator was absent, cases stalled because shared context (e.g., risk assessment, permit status) was not readily available to other teams (field-survey utilization fell, second-visit team utilization rose to 92%). Thus "fewer handovers" improves visible coordination flow for stable cases but reduces resilience under disruption (absence), consistent with resource findings and alignment diagnostics ("when a coordinator was absent, cases stalled").  
- **Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost**: Visible cost (before complaint handling) fell (184  161 EUR) due to fewer handovers reducing internal scheduling/communication time. However, downstream cost (including complaints/revisits) likely rose (estimated 207 vs 198 EUR), because manual activations (15% vs 7%), second visits (18% vs 11%), and complaints (9.8% vs 6.1%) increased field rework, complaint handling, and potential billing disputes (e.g., "billing before activation" complaints). The cost estimate excludes downstream handling after week 4, so the visible cost improvement understates the full picture; the conflict arises from measuring cost at different points (visible vs full).  
- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings**: Segment data shows clear directional effects: simple low-risk houses benefit (median 6 days, FTright 3pp, complaints 1pp), while apartments (medium risk) and old buildings (permit-sensitive) suffer (median 2–1 days for old buildings, 12–25 days tail for apartments, FTright 8–11pp, complaints 5–7pp). This aligns with DFG/temporal findings: skip rule often wrong for medium-risk apartments (predictive rule incomplete before week 3), leading to more failed visits  second visits; old buildings miss permit checks (alignment finding), causing delays and complaints. Thus the redesign’s simplification helps low-risk cases but misfits higher-risk segments due to incomplete risk input early in pilot—explaining simultaneous median improvement (simple segment dominates baseline median) and tail worsening (higher-risk tail grows). All conflicts stem from segment-dependent behavior, measurement timing (visible vs downstream cost), and centralization effects—none imply the redesign is inherently good or bad; they reflect where it works vs where it fails under current conditions.

M6 (rule validation) is most critical because it tells us whether skip rule can safely be re-enabled; M3 (reason codes) clarifies causes of rework/complaints; M7 (conformance monitoring) tests safeguards in practice. M4 (cost), M8 (NPS), M1 (randomized skip), M5 (absence test), and M2 (timestamps) are useful but address secondary or future questions; M1 and M5 depend on M6 outcome, and M2 needs timestamps for precise tail measurement. Thus ranking reflects decision urgency and direct impact on E’s threshold and pause decision.

### 6. Final recommendation  

- **Immediate action (within 7 days):** Suspend the predictive survey-skip rule and post-billing activation check for all non-simple-house cases (i.e., all medium-risk apartments and old-building cases). Deploy coordinator ownership *only* for simple lowrisk houses (30% medium-risk share in the case) and enforce permit checks for all old and apartment cases. Begin M3 (structured reason codes), M7 (Billing start  Activation conformance monitoring), and M8 (separate "speed" vs "working service" NPS) in the two pilot regions with highest apartment density. Do not assign survey-skip decisions before week 3 risk data completeness; use permit check as default for non-simple cases.  
- **What to pause:** Full deployment of the survey-skip predictive rule and post-billing activation check in all segments. No national or regional rollout of R with skip rule; no experimentation with skip rule without rule validation (M6).  
- **What to scale:** Coordinator ownership (single installer after address validation) *only* for simple lowrisk houses (where segment data shows clear benefit: median 6 days, FTright 3pp). Enforce permit checks for old buildings and apartments (aligns with alignment diagnostic finding). Keep reduced handovers for simple houses (where it works). *Do not scale survey skip or post-billing activation.*  
- **What to measure next:** Prioritize **M6** (validate building-risk rule vs field-survey outcomes), **M3** (reason codes), and **M7** (Billing start  Activation conformance monitoring) in the two pilot regions. Collect M8 (NPS split) concurrently. Schedule M4 (60-day downstream cost) and M2 (timestamps) for after M6 results are in (if needed for cost threshold). Do not run M1 or M5 until M6 shows <15% rule error (i.e., after validation).  
- **Threshold for broader rollout:** Only extend survey skip (and optionally post-billing activation) to medium-risk apartments *after* M6 shows **actual skip-rule error <15%** (i.e., <15% of medium-risk addresses where field survey would have been needed), *and* after M3/M7 confirm 12% second-visit rate for apartments under permit enforcement, *and* FTright 80% for apartments in the test period. Only then consider rolling coordinator ownership + skip rule (with controls) to all low and medium-risk segments nationally. If M6 shows 20% error or second-visit rate >15%, continue using E (simple houses only + permit enforcement) and refine the rule with more risk data.  

> *Rationale for recommendation (concise):* The evidence shows clear segment-specific trade-offs (benefit for simple houses; harm for apartments/old buildings). Skipping the survey rule for higher-risk segments directly addresses the tail worsening (p90/p95), rework (second visits), and complaint rise while preserving the coordinator ownership benefit for simple houses. Pausing skip rule avoids repeating pilot harm; scaling only simple houses avoids national risk. Measuring M6 first resolves the core uncertainty (rule accuracy); thresholds (error <15%, second-visit 12%, FTright 80%) are observable, evidence-based, and directly tied to the segment harm. This meets all constraints: no claim that R works everywhere; no national scale without tail/rework controls; no rollback ignoring simple-house benefit; facts/interpretations/assumptions are cleanly separated; process-mining artifacts (fitness/precision, alignment, segment outcomes, resilience signal) are integrated. The recommendation is defensible, actionable within weeks, and uses minimal new measurement focused on the highest-value uncertainty.
