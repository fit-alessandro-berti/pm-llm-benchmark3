## 1. Executive memo

**Decision**: Proceed with a **guardrailed hybrid rollout (E)**: expand the redesign only for **simple, low-risk houses** and scale the **case-ownership/coordinator** mechanism with added resilience, while **pausing** (a) survey skipping for medium/high-risk addresses and (b) billing start before a successful activation test. In parallel, run a **controlled experiment (D)** with improved measurement to decide whether any parts of R are safe to extend to apartments/old buildings.

**Facts (directly supported by pilot evidence)**:
- **Speed/flow**: Median cycle time improved (14.2 → 10.8 days) and cases with 3+ handovers dropped (34% → 12%).
- **Tail/quality worsened**: p90/p95 cycle time worsened (31.0 → 38.5; 45.0 → 62.0), first-time-right fell (82% → 76%), complaints rose (6.1% → 9.8%), manual activation rose (7% → 15%), and second visits rose (11% → 18%).
- **Segment split**: Simple low-risk houses improved strongly (median -6 days; p90 -8 days; first-time-right +3pp; complaints -1pp). Apartments and old/permit-sensitive buildings deteriorated, especially in the tail (e.g., old buildings p90 +25 days; first-time-right -11pp; complaints +7pp).
- **Conformance signals**: Frequent “Billing start before successful Activation test” and missing expected permit checks for older apartment buildings; complaint escalation after billing increased (Billing start → Complaint escalation edge 3% → 8%).
- **Operational strain**: Coordinator utilization reached 93% (in two pilot regions); second-visit team utilization rose to 92% while field-survey utilization fell to 52%.
- **Process-mining artifact**: Pilot discovered model has high fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51), and alignments show repeated/optional paths (e.g., repeated installation visits; manual activation; post-billing complaints).

**Interpretation (plausible causal synthesis, not proven)**:
- The redesign bundles **beneficial simplification** (merged validate/schedule + single ownership) with **risk-shifting changes** (survey skipping and deferring activation assurance). The latter plausibly drives more failed visits, manual activation, rework, and post-billing complaints—creating a worse long tail even as the median improves.
- Reduced handovers improved accountability but increased **centralization and fragility**: high coordinator load and absence-related stalling reduce resilience, consistent with longer delays from failed visit to second visit in high-utilization regions.

**Why not “scale nationally” or “roll back now”**:
- National scaling without guardrails would amplify the observed tail, rework, and billing/activation conformance issues.
- Full rollback would discard clear segment-level gains (simple houses) and the handover reduction that likely drives part of the median improvement.

**Recommendation**:
Lock in the redesign where evidence is consistently positive (simple low-risk houses), stop/contain the parts most associated with tail and downstream dissatisfaction, and invest in targeted measurement plus controlled experimentation so a broader rollout decision can be made on total cost, tail risk, and conformance—not only median speed.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision**: Fitness rises when a discovered model can replay most observed traces; precision falls when the model must allow many optional paths/loops to cover the observed variability. In pilot R, the log shows more combinations of skipping survey, manual activation, repeat visits, billing-before-activation, and complaint escalations; plus sparse start timestamps and merged tasks blur structure. The result is a model that “matches” what happened (high fitness) but is too permissive to be a tight prescription (low precision).

- **Improved median but worse tail duration**: The median is dominated by the large share of straightforward cases that benefit from merged early steps and coordinator ownership (notably simple houses). The tail is driven by a smaller subset where failures, second visits, manual activation, missing permit checks, and post-billing complaint loops create long waits and repeated work—especially apartments and old buildings, and regions with high coordinator utilization.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience**: Handover reduction centralizes ownership and reduces coordination overhead for normal flow. But it also concentrates context and decision-making in a few coordinators/specialists; when they are overloaded (93% utilization) or absent, work stalls because knowledge is not distributed. The work network becomes simpler yet more fragile.

- **Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost**: Upstream costs fall when surveys are skipped and handovers decrease, and when faster throughput reduces handling time. But rework (second visits, manual activations), post-billing complaint handling, and billing disputes can accumulate after the “visible cost” window—especially because downstream complaint-handling cost is missing after week 4 and the “estimated total cost” is incomplete.

- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings**: The redesign changes are not equally safe across contexts. Apartments/older buildings have higher access, wiring, permit, and activation complexity; skipping surveys and deferring activation assurance increases failure probability there. Simple houses have lower hidden constraints, so simplification yields speed gains without the same quality penalty.

## 4. Decision alternatives

- **A. Scale Redesign R nationally**
  - **Advantages**: Captures median-cycle-time and handover reductions quickly; standardizes ownership.
  - **Risks**: Amplifies worsened p90/p95, rework, complaints, and billing/activation conformance issues; coordinator bottlenecks could worsen at scale.
  - **Reasonable if**: Guardrails remove billing-before-activation and permit omissions; survey-skip rule is validated and scoped; coordinator capacity/resilience targets are met; tail/complaint KPIs recover in a controlled expansion first.

- **B. Roll back to baseline**
  - **Advantages**: Likely reduces manual activation, second visits, and post-billing complaint loops; simplifies compliance posture.
  - **Risks**: Loses strong gains for simple houses and structural handover reduction; signals “pilot failure” despite segment wins.
  - **Reasonable if**: After adding measurement, total 60-day cost and complaint harm remains materially worse even in low-risk segments, or conformance violations cannot be contained rapidly.

- **C. Scale coordinator ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**
  - **Advantages**: Keeps accountability and some early-flow speed, while removing two levers most associated with quality/tail risk.
  - **Risks**: Coordinator overload and absence fragility remain; may give up some speed/cost gains from reduced surveys.
  - **Reasonable if**: Coordinator case caps, backup coverage, and workload balancing are implemented; permit and activation controls are enforced; second-visit capacity is stabilized.

- **D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**
  - **Advantages**: Addresses confounding (pilot regions differ; attributes incomplete); isolates causal impact of each lever; supports defensible scaling.
  - **Risks**: Delays benefits; requires operational discipline and instrumentation changes.
  - **Reasonable if**: Management can time-box (6–10 weeks) and maintain a safe “business-as-usual + guardrails” operating model during the experiment.

- **E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses; add controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale**
  - **Advantages**: Captures proven segment benefits while containing tail/compliance risk; aligns with heterogeneous outcomes.
  - **Risks**: Requires accurate segmentation; early pilot shows attributes can be incomplete; misrouting cases could recreate harms.
  - **Reasonable if**: Risk/segment data completeness is enforced early in the case and guardrails are monitored continuously.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

**Ranked by decision value (highest → lowest):**

1. **M4. Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start**  
   - **Resolves**: Whether R is truly cheaper once complaints, revisits, billing disputes, and repeat contacts are counted.  
   - **Could change decision**: If total cost is materially lower for simple houses, scale E faster; if materially higher overall, narrow E further or roll back risky elements.

2. **M1. Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum**  
   - **Resolves**: Causal effect of skipping surveys on first-time-right, second visits, tail time, and complaints.  
   - **Could change decision**: Validates safe strata for skipping (enabling broader efficiency) or justifies permanent suspension outside very low risk.

3. **M7. Conformance monitoring for Billing start before Activation test and Permit check absence**  
   - **Resolves**: Frequency and operational controllability of key “must-not-happen” deviations and their association with downstream harm.  
   - **Could change decision**: Enables safe scaling with hard guardrails; if violations remain high, forces pause of post-billing activation and strengthens permit gating.

4. **M6. Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes**  
   - **Resolves**: Whether the rule is systematically wrong for apartments/old buildings and how to recalibrate it.  
   - **Could change decision**: Turns E from a blunt “no skipping” policy into a targeted policy, reducing cost without raising rework.

5. **M2. Instrument start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, and Manual activation**  
   - **Resolves**: Where time is spent (waiting vs working), and whether tail is driven by scheduling delays, field execution, activation backlog, or rework loops.  
   - **Could change decision**: If tail is mostly waiting/capacity, invest in staffing/queues; if execution-time is longer, fix process/quality.

6. **M3. Add structured reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, and Second visit**  
   - **Resolves**: Root-cause mix (access issues, missing permits, equipment, network readiness, customer reschedule).  
   - **Could change decision**: Supports targeted fixes (e.g., pre-visit access confirmation, permit workflow, equipment logistics) instead of broad rollbacks.

7. **M5. Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership under coordinator absence**  
   - **Resolves**: Resilience/throughput trade-off of central ownership vs shared coverage.  
   - **Could change decision**: May lead to “buddy coordinator” or pooled ownership models before scaling C nationally.

8. **M8. Collect customer experience separately for speed-to-appointment vs working-service-after-installation**  
   - **Resolves**: Which experience dimension drives NPS and complaints in each segment.  
   - **Could change decision**: Refines KPIs and communication strategy; less likely to overturn the core rollout choice than cost/tail/conformance evidence.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision**: Choose **E (hybrid)** immediately, paired with a time-boxed **D (controlled experiment)** for medium/high-risk segments.

- **Immediate action (next 2 weeks)**:
  - **Stop** “Billing start before successful Activation test” as a default; allow only explicitly approved exceptions and monitor them via conformance alerts (M7).
  - **Reinstate/strengthen permit gating** for permit-sensitive/old buildings; treat missing permit checks as a stop-the-line condition (M7).
  - **Suspend survey skipping** for apartments, old buildings, and any medium/high-risk strata until the risk rule is validated (M6) and tested causally (M1).
  - **Reduce coordinator fragility**: implement case caps, backup/buddy coverage, and standardized shared case notes to prevent stalling under absence; rebalance capacity from underutilized survey teams to second-visit/activation backlogs.

- **What to pause**:
  - Post-billing activation (billing before activation test) except whitelisted cases with explicit customer consent/communication and measured outcomes.
  - Predictive survey skipping outside clearly validated low-risk strata.

- **What to scale (next 4–6 weeks)**:
  - Coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule for **simple low-risk houses** across additional regions (phased), with coordinator utilization targets and backup coverage in place.

- **What to measure next (run in parallel, decision in 6–10 weeks)**:
  - **M4, M1, M7, M6** as top priority; then **M2 and M3** to sharpen tail diagnosis and root causes; use **M5** to harden resilience before any broader coordinator scaling.

- **Thresholds to justify broader rollout beyond simple low-risk houses** (measured by segment and region, sustained for ≥4 weeks):
  - **Tail**: p90 no worse than baseline (or ≤5% worse) and p95 not worse than baseline by >5 days after adjusting for segment mix.
  - **Quality**: first-time-right ≥82% (or within 1pp of baseline) and second visits ≤11–12%.
  - **Customer outcomes**: 30-day complaint rate ≤6.5% and trending down; NPS not declining further.
  - **Conformance**: billing-before-activation <1% of cases (or explicitly justified exceptions only); permit-check absence in permit-sensitive segment <2%.
  - **Capacity/resilience**: coordinator utilization ≤85% with documented backup coverage; activation and second-visit queues stable (no growing backlog).
