- **Critical-case load:** Urgent + Complex cases per week rise from about 713 to about 1,617, an increase of about 127%.
- **Validation load:** peak-period technical-to-medical validation edges plus repeat-to-medical and hold-to-medical edges imply about 1,367 medical-validation events per week versus about 530 per week in the normal period, an increase of about 158%.
- **Specialist-review load:** peak hold-for-specialist-review events are 610 over three weeks, about 203 per week, versus 290 over eight weeks, about 36 per week in normal periods.
- **Primary stress:** assay scheduling, technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review, especially for Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

## S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage

- **Disruption pattern:** M2 availability degrades, or M1 is temporarily unavailable. M2 has a higher failure rate but faster runtime; Repeat-after-failure cases are clustered after night shifts and M2 usage.

- **Case-type stress:**
  - **Routine** cases are most exposed if routed to M2.
  - **Repeat-after-failure** cases consume assay capacity twice and amplify the outage.

- **Primary stress:** Test preparation, batch wait, assay run, and the repeat-assay loop.

## S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence

   - Accession correction specialists are only two people.
   - Peak utilization is 91% with a 14h median queue.
   - Manual-to-test-preparation median duration rises from 5h to 16h.

6. **Downstream burst into preparation and assay**
   - If corrections and recollections are released unevenly, preparation and assay see secondary spikes.
   - Assay utilization is already 98% in peak.

### How relieving one station may overload another

- **I8, automate registration for complete digital metadata**
  - Relieves registration queue and clerk error pressure.
  - But more cases enter pre-analytical check and assay faster.
  - Without WIP limits, pre-analytical and assay bottlenecks worsen.

- **I7, shift courier pickup slots**
  - Smooths arrival peaks and reduces registration/pre-analytical spikes.
  - But if clinics cannot adjust, or weather delays all routes, smoothing may fail.
  - It can shift pressure to pre-analytical if arrival volume is still high.

- **I6, early pre-analytical defect triage**
  - Reduces long recollection loops and clerk explanation time.
  - But requires pre-analytical capacity and clear rejection criteria.
  - If too aggressive, it can create false rejections and urgent-sample delays.

- **I1, strict Urgent-first dispatch**
  - Does not solve arrival compression.
  - Urgent samples may still be delayed if courier or pre-analytical queues are long.
  - Routine and Complex cases may accumulate and create later validation spikes.

# 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign should not be “add capacity everywhere.” It should create a **controlled flow architecture** that manages tail risk, protects the final constraints, and prevents local fixes from creating downstream overload.

## Target process flow

1. **Register order with digital completeness check**
   - Use I8 only for orders with complete metadata.
   - Incomplete orders go to a guided manual registration path.
   - Do not automate all registration without an intake governor.

2. **Scheduled courier receive**
   - Use I7 to smooth 10:00 and 17:00 spikes.
   - Reserve limited express slots for urgent samples, but cap urgent arrivals so pre-analytical and assay are not flooded.

3. **Pre-analytical triage gate within 2 hours**
   - Use I6 to reject or correct poor samples early.
   - Create a fast recollection lane and a priority accession-correction lane.
   - Do not let recollection loops consume clerk capacity.

4. **Slack-based case routing**
   - Do not use a simple “Urgent always first” rule.
   - Classify cases into:
     - **Urgent-contract**: due within 18h.
     - **Complex-specialist**: multiple assays, medical validator, possible specialist review.
     - **Routine-stable**: tolerates batching if slack is sufficient.
     - **Rework/recollection**: separate lane to avoid contaminating primary flow.
   - Priority is based on remaining slack, downstream constraint, and rework risk.

5. **Dynamic test preparation and assay scheduling**
   - Use I2 only for Routine cases with sufficient slack and protected urgent/complex slots.
   - Use I3 dynamically, not permanently:
     - M1 gets critical reservation when Urgent/Complex queues are high.
     - M2 can take Routine only when its repeat rate and failure rate are acceptable.
   - Urgent cases should use small reserved assay slots rather than continuous interruption, reducing schedule fragmentation.

6. **Exception-based technical validation**
   - Technical validators are a peak bottleneck at 101% utilization.
   - Low-risk Routine results can use rule-based or exception-based validation.
   - Any increase in technical throughput must be coordinated with medical validation and specialist review capacity.

7. **Surge medical validation and specialist triage**
   - Use I5 during peaks or medical queues, not as a permanent default.
   - Reserve specialist review for clinically ambiguous Complex cases.
   - Use I10 only for non-urgent Complex overflow with acceptable transport delay and traceability.

8. **Controlled release**
   - Use I9 only for low-risk Routine cases with sufficient slack.
   - Do not hold Urgent, Complex, or Repeat-after-failure releases unless clinically required.

## Robust component A: Intake governor

**Interventions:** I6 + I7 + staged I8.

**Why robust:**
- It attacks the upstream causes of rework and queue spikes.
- It reduces recollection loops, which are long: 42h normal, 60h peak median.
- It reduces manual accession correction demand, which is constrained by only two specialists.
- It prevents registration automation from pushing unfiltered volume into pre-analytical and assay.

**Assumptions:**
- Clinics and couriers can accept some scheduling discipline.
- Many pre-analytical defects are detectable within 2 hours.
- Triage criteria are clear and not overly conservative.

**Where it may fail:**
- Early triage may falsely reject valid samples, especially urgent samples.
- If pre-analytical capacity is not protected, I6 can worsen the 96% peak pre-analytical utilization.
- I8 without WIP limits can expose assay and technical validation bottlenecks.

## Robust component B: Slack-based critical lane

**Interventions:** modified I1, modified I3, modified I2.

**Why robust:**
- It avoids the failure mode of I1: strict Urgent-first can starve Routine and Complex cases.
- It recognizes that Urgent and Complex both stress scarce validation resources but in different ways.
- It uses due-date slack and downstream constraint, not just case type.
- It protects Complex cases from being starved by urgent noise.

**Design:**
- Urgent cases get priority only when within or approaching the 18h contractual window.
- Complex cases get reserved assay, technical, and medical capacity because they require multiple assays and medical validation.
- Routine cases can be batched only when slack is sufficient.
- Repeat-after-failure cases are placed in a separate rework lane with WIP limits.

**Assumptions:**
- Due dates and contractual targets are reliable.
- Case classification is accurate at registration.
- Staff can follow dynamic priority rules without gaming.

**Where it may fail:**
- If thresholds are too aggressive, low-priority cases may wait excessively.
- If Complex cases are not protected, specialist review queues may worsen.
- If urgent arrivals are uncontrolled, urgent-first behavior can still create WIP imbalance.

## Robust component C: Dynamic assay scheduling and repeat containment

**Interventions:** modified I3, modified I2, machine-health routing.

**Why robust:**
- Assay is at 98% utilization in peak with an 18h median queue.
- Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and are clustered after night shifts and M2 usage.
- Permanent M1 dedication can overload M2 and increase repeats.
- Dynamic routing can protect critical cases while limiting M2 exposure.

**Design:**
- M1 is reserved for Urgent and Complex during high-risk periods, but only when M2 repeat rate is acceptable.
- M2 can process Routine if:
  - M2 repeat rate is below threshold.
  - Night-shift repeat clustering is not elevated.
  - Machine health alerts are clear.
- If M2 repeat rate rises, shift some Routine to M1, reduce batch size, and trigger maintenance/root-cause analysis.
- Use small urgent assay windows rather than continuous urgent interruption to reduce schedule fragmentation.

**Assumptions:**
- Machine health and repeat rate can be monitored by machine and shift.
- M2 failure rate is controllable enough to allow conditional use.
- Setup time savings from batching are real but not so large that they justify urgent delay.

**Where it may fail:**
- During severe M1 outage, critical cases may still be delayed.
- During severe M2 failure, Routine volume may have nowhere to go.
- If thresholds oscillate, scheduling can thrash and reduce effective capacity.

## Robust component D: Medical and specialist surge with overflow discipline

**Interventions:** I5 + conditional I10.

**Why robust:**
- Medical validation and specialist review are final constraints during mix shifts.
- Peak medical queue is 22h; specialist queue is 36h.
- Complex cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist review.
- Urgent cases have contractual exposure if medical validation is delayed.

**Design:**
- Activate I5 evening medical validation when medical queue exceeds 12h or urgent p90 exceeds 18h.
- Use specialist triage to reduce unnecessary holds.
- Activate I10 only for non-urgent Complex overflow when specialist queue exceeds 24h or a specialist is absent.
- Require transport SLA, chain-of-custody, and reconciliation before using I10.

**Assumptions:**
- Medical validators can work evening shifts without quality degradation.
- Partner lab can handle Complex-panel overflow without creating hidden backlog.
- Release and reconciliation capacity can absorb increased validated output.

**Where it may fail:**
- I5 does not solve specialist absence.
- I10 adds 12h transport delay and may breach Complex targets if used too broadly.
- If technical validators are already at 101% utilization, increased medical throughput may simply expose technical validation and specialist review.

## Robust component E: Cross-training with guardrails

**Interventions:** modified I4, plus exception-based technical validation.

**Why robust:**
- Technical validators are over capacity in peak.
- Accession correction is constrained by only two specialists and has a 14h peak median queue.
- But moving technical validators to accession correction can worsen the larger bottleneck.

**Design:**
- Do not default to I4.
- Use I4 only when:
  - Technical validator utilization is below 90%.
  - Technical validation queue is below 8h.
  - Accession correction queue is above 8h and blocking urgent/complex cases.
- Prefer cross-training registration or pre-analytical staff to support accession correction where feasible.
- Use exception-based technical validation for low-risk Routine cases to reduce technical load without moving validators away.

**Assumptions:**
- Technical validators are not the most constrained resource at the moment of reassignment.
- Accession corrections are routine enough to be performed safely by cross-trained staff.
- Exception-based validation is clinically and operationally acceptable.

**Where it may fail:**
- If technical validation is already at 101% utilization, I4 creates a shortage.
- If more technical throughput is enabled, medical validation or specialist review may become the next bottleneck.

## Robust component F: Conditional Routine batching and release

**Interventions:** modified I2 + conditional I9.

**Why robust:**
- Routine cases tolerate batching, but only when urgent and complex exceptions are controlled.
- Larger batches can improve setup efficiency, but peak batch-wait-to-assay median is already 22h.
- Holding Routine releases can reduce release/reporting work but adds waiting.

**Design:**
- Increase Routine batch size from 12 to 24 only when:
  - Urgent queue age is below threshold.
  - Complex reserved assay slots are protected.
  - Routine cases have more than 24h slack.
- Use I9 only for low-risk Routine cases with more than 48h slack and no clinical urgency.
- Never apply I9 to Urgent, Complex, or Repeat-after-failure cases.

**Assumptions:**
- Routine cases have predictable due dates.
- Release/reporting workload is actually a constraint in the scenario.
- Batching does not materially increase rework.

**Where it may fail:**
- I2 can worsen Urgent p90 if exceptions are weak.
- I9 can worsen Routine p90 even if it improves average release workload.
- Larger batches can increase the impact of a single machine failure.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Each policy should be activated by leading indicators and switched off when the condition persists below threshold.

## P1. Intake-compression policy

**Trigger:**
- Courier arrival peak-hour volume exceeds 1.5× average for two consecutive days.
- Registration queue exceeds 2h.
- Recollection rate exceeds 6%.
- Pre-analytical queue exceeds 8h.

**Actions:**
- Activate I7: shift courier pickup slots away from 10:00 and 17:00.
- Throttle I8 for non-urgent automated registration.
- Activate I6 fast recollection lane.
- Prioritize accession correction only for cases blocking Urgent or Complex.
- Add temporary pre-analytical triage support if available.

**Switch off when:**
- Arrival peak ratio is below 1.3 for three days.
- Registration queue is below 1h.
- Recollection rate is below 4.5%.
- Pre-analytical queue is below 6h.

## P2. Peak critical-mix policy

**Trigger:**
- Urgent + Complex weekly mix exceeds 40%.
- Urgent queue age exceeds 12h.
- Medical validation queue exceeds 12h.
- Complex p90 exceeds 150h.

**Actions:**
- Activate I5 evening medical-validation shift.
- Reserve M1 capacity for Urgent and Complex.
- Reduce Routine batch size to 12 unless slack is clearly sufficient.
- Apply slack-based priority rather than strict Urgent-first.
- Protect Complex specialist-review capacity.

**Switch off when:**
- Urgent p90 is below 18h for three days.
- Medical queue is below 8h.
- Complex p90 is below 150h.
- Technical validator utilization is below 90%.

## P3. Assay-degradation policy

**Trigger:**
- M2 repeat rate exceeds 10%.
- M2 night-shift repeat rate exceeds 12%.
- Assay queue exceeds 18h.
- Machine availability falls below 85%.

**Actions:**
- Stop routing Routine cases to M2.
- Shift some Routine to M1 with WIP limits.
- Reduce batch size to prevent large batches waiting on degraded machines.
- Trigger preventive maintenance and root-cause analysis for M2/night shifts.
- Throttle preparation output to avoid WIP buildup.

**Switch off when:**
- M2 repeat rate is below 7% for three days.
- Assay queue is below 8h.
- Machine availability is above 90%.
- Night-shift repeat clustering is resolved.

## P4. Medical/specialist absence policy

**Trigger:**
- Specialist unavailable.
- Specialist hold queue exceeds 24h.
- Medical validation queue exceeds 16h.
- Complex hold rate exceeds 5%.

**Actions:**
- Activate I5 evening medical shift.
- Activate specialist triage and reserve slots for clinically ambiguous Complex cases.
- Activate I10 only for non-urgent Complex overflow with acceptable transport delay.
- Extend Complex due dates where contractually possible.
- Do not cross-train technical validators to accession correction while technical validation is constrained.

**Switch off when:**
- Specialist queue is below 12h.
- Medical queue is below 8h.
- No specialist absence.
- Complex hold rate is below 3%.

## P5. Rework-spike policy

**Trigger:**
- Overall rework exceeds 9%.
- Repeat assay rate exceeds 10%.
- Recollection rate exceeds 7%.
- Cancellation after recollection exceeds 40%.

**Actions:**
- Freeze I2 and I3.
- Activate I6 more aggressively but with false-rejection review.
- Separate Repeat-after-failure cases into a rework lane.
- Review M2/night-shift root causes.
- Increase accession-correction priority only for cases blocking urgent/complex flow.

**Switch off when:**
- Rework is below 7.5%.
- Repeat assay rate is below 7%.
- Recollection rate is below 4.5%.
- Cancellation after recollection is below 35%.

## P6. Technical-validation overload policy

**Trigger:**
- Technical validator utilization exceeds 95%.
- Technical validation queue exceeds 12h.
- Technical-to-medical edge exceeds 20h median.

**Actions:**
- Activate exception-based technical validation for low-risk Routine cases.
- Do not activate I4.
- Throttle assay output if medical/specialist capacity cannot absorb validated results.
- Prioritize technical validation by due-date slack and downstream constraint.

**Switch off when:**
- Technical utilization is below 90%.
- Technical queue is below 6h.
- Medical queue is below 8h.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

Even after the redesign, several vulnerabilities remain.

## 1. Early triage can create false rejections

I6 is robust because it reduces long recollection loops, but it depends on accurate defect detection. If the triage gate is too aggressive, valid samples may be rejected and recollected, worsening the 60h recollection loop and damaging physician trust.

## 2. Intake smoothing can shift pressure downstream

I7 and I8 can reduce registration and courier spikes, but if pre-analytical and assay are not WIP-limited, smoother intake can simply push more cases into already constrained stations. This is a redesign-induced vulnerability: the intake governor can make downstream bottlenecks more visible and more severe if not coordinated.

## 3. Slack-based priority can starve low-priority cases

A due-date/slack policy is better than strict Urgent-first, but it can still create hidden backlogs for Routine or Repeat-after-failure cases if thresholds are too strict. If due-date data are inaccurate, the system may prioritize the wrong cases.

## 4. Dynamic machine routing can oscillate

If M1/M2 routing thresholds are too sensitive, the lab may thrash between configurations. This can reduce effective assay capacity and increase setup time. Hysteresis and stable decision windows are needed.

## 5. Medical surge may not solve specialist absence

I5 can relieve medical validation, but specialist review is a different constraint. Complex cases that require hold-for-specialist-review may still wait 36h or more. If specialist absence is prolonged, medical surge alone is insufficient.

## 6. Partner overflow creates external dependency

I10 can relieve specialist review, but it adds transport delay, traceability complexity, and partner capacity risk. If partner turnaround is longer than 12h, Complex p90 may worsen despite lower internal specialist queue.

## 7. Cross-training technical validators can degrade the wrong bottleneck

I4 can relieve accession correction, but technical validators are already at 101% utilization in peak. If cross-training is triggered by accession queue alone, it may create a larger technical-validation bottleneck.

## 8. Order-level WIP limits may hide tube-level complexity

The logs are flattened by lab order, but Complex-panel orders may contain multiple assays. Order-level WIP limits may understate actual assay load. A redesign based only on order counts can still overload machines.

## 9. Routine release batching can worsen tail performance

I9 may reduce release workload, but it directly increases waiting. If used too broadly, it can worsen Routine p90 even if average release time looks acceptable.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Monitor leading indicators daily during peak periods. Lagging outcome metrics confirm whether the redesign worked, but they are too slow for daily control.

## Leading indicators

|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Courier arrival spike ratio: peak 2h arrivals / average 2h arrivals | Predicts registration and pre-analytical congestion | <1.3 | 1.3–1.8 | >1.8 |
| Pre-analytical defect/recollection rate per 100 received cases | Predicts long recollection loops and clerk time loss | <4% | 4–6% | >6% |

| Assay repeat rate by machine and shift | Predicts assay capacity loss and validation flood | <7% | 7–10% | >10% |

## Daily control rule

- If **two or more leading indicators are red**, activate the relevant scenario policy.
- If **one leading indicator is red and one is yellow**, activate monitoring and prepare policy.
- If **lagging metrics are red but leading indicators are green**, investigate data quality, external dependencies, or hidden tube-level complexity.
- If **leading indicators are red but lagging metrics are green**, act anyway; leading indicators predict tail failure before averages show it.
