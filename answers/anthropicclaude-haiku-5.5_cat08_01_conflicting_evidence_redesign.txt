## 1. Current-process diagnosis

**Performance.** Speed is concentrated in one path. 3,600 of 5,000 cases pass the automated screen (72%), and 2,550 of those go directly to Fast approval (71% of screened cases). Fast approval then dispatches replacements with a median of 1.8h, and dispatch-to-close has a median of 3.2h. The slow path is the reviewed path: manual review, evidence requests, and repair quotes. Median cycle time is 46–120h for reviewed or repair cases, and the unclear/missing-evidence segment (22% of cases) has a median of 140h with the highest abandonment. The performance DFG shows that Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review has a median of 26h and a p90 of 88h, and that Manual review -> Repair quote has a p90 of 60h. Part-price confirmation is the stated cause of much of the repair-quote delay.

**Quality.** Rework and reopen are concentrated in the fast path. Complaint/reopen occurs in 9.2% of all cases, but in 14.1% of Fast-approval cases and only 3.3% of Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device cases. Rework repair occurs in 12.5% of repaired high-value devices. Dispatch replacement -> Complaint/reopen (260 edges, mean 96h, p90 180h) is a long-tail pattern linked to wrong replacement part selection. Fast approval is about 31% cheaper before rework and only about 9% cheaper once complaints, reshipments, and service credits are included. The fast path's apparent cost advantage largely disappears downstream.

**Compliance.** Token-based replay gives fitness 0.91, but only 68% of traces fit, so roughly 1,600 cases deviate. The alignment diagnostics point to three recurring move-on-log deviations: Fast approval before evidence completeness, complaint/reopen after close, and dispatch without risk review for high-value devices. Mapped to the normative rules:

- **R1 (>EUR 400 needs manual or fraud review before fast approval).** The auto screen sends 2,550 cases straight to Fast approval, and high-value segments make up 35% of cases (about 1,750). The prompt does not give a value distribution, so the exposure cannot be quantified exactly, but the structure of the auto path makes R1 violations likely. Only 240 cases pass through Fraud/risk review -> Fast approval.
- **R2 (no replacement before evidence confirmed, except Premium low-risk accessories).** Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement (2,300 edges) is the main route to dispatch, and it does not require evidence confirmation beyond the auto screen.
- **R3 (reopened claims must pass manual review before close or denial).** Escalation board -> Dispatch replacement (65) and Escalation board -> Claim denied (10) bypass manual review. Of 120 claims that reach the escalation board, up to 75 can reach dispatch or denial without it. This is a structural R3 violation, not only a rare deviation.

Precision is low because many low-frequency paths combine auto approval, risk review, and repair in ways policy does not allow. The discovered model therefore describes what happens, not what should happen.

**Customer experience.** Segment outcomes diverge sharply. Premium high-value devices have complaint/reopen rates of 22% on the fast path and 5% on the reviewed/repair path. Standard high-value devices show 18% vs 6%. For low-risk accessories the reverse trade-off applies. Premium low-risk accessories have 6% complaints on the fast path (8h median) and 3% on the manual path (46h median), but the manual path exceeds the 24h threshold where service-credit exposure rises. Standard low-risk accessories tolerate 72h if status is visible, and their manual path (52h median) is within tolerance.

**Resource constraints.** Manual review has 7 reviewers at 36 productive hours per week, costs EUR 34/h, and its queue grows sharply above about 82% utilization. Fraud/risk review has 2 specialists (EUR 52/h) and bottlenecks on Mondays and after campaigns. Quality inspectors (EUR 38/h, 2 people) are underused except after repair campaigns. The escalation board is a single 45-minute daily slot. The wrong-replacement cost is EUR 95 for low-risk accessories and EUR 410 for high-value devices.

**The contradiction.** The most frequent path (auto screen -> fast approval -> dispatch -> close) is cheapest and fastest per case but generates the most complaints, the most R1/R2 exposure, and the largest share of alignment deviations. The slow reviewed path is not uniformly better. For Premium and Standard low-risk accessories, the fast path is defensible on value because its complaint rate is only modestly higher and it meets the Premium 24h threshold. For high-value devices, the reviewed path has materially better quality and compliance, but it is too slow because of part-price waits, quote-to-approval gaps, and a scarce risk-review bottleneck. The problem is therefore not "fast vs. slow." It is that routing does not match risk, and the controls sit in the wrong places.

**Data reconciliation flags.** Several edge counts do not reconcile. Dispatch replacement has 2,365 inflows but 2,580 outflows. Manual review has 2,600 inflows and only 1,400 listed outflows. The stated 9.2% complaint rate is not reproduced by the edge counts (430 edges into complaint is 8.6%). The 14.1% figure for Fast-approval cases is a cohort measure, not the 5.0% edge-level rate. These may reflect duplicate events, timestamp ordering, or incomplete DFG filtering. They should be resolved before any redesign is judged on its numbers.

## 2. Redesign A: Risk-Segmented Triage Lanes with Enforced Gates

**Target segments and paths.** All segments, with the main effect on high-value devices (R1, R2, and the Premium HV complaint gap) and on Standard low-risk accessories (manual-review load). It also targets the reopen path (R3).

**Process flow.**
1. **Intake with mandatory value, segment, and defect tagging.** Routing is decided at intake, not after the auto screen.
2. **Triage router, four lanes:**
   - **Lane 1, Premium low-risk accessory:** segment-aware auto eligibility, then fast dispatch. Target is completion inside 24h.
   - **Lane 2, Standard low-risk accessory:** a low-touch verification queue staffed by trained generalists rather than manual reviewers. SLA is 72h with proactive status updates. Sampled post-close audit.
   - **Lane 3, High-value (Premium and Standard):** value/risk gate before any approval (satisfies R1). Pre-dispatch part-verification gate before replacement (satisfies R2). Premium repair quotes run in parallel with the risk gate rather than after it.
   - **Lane 4, Unclear defect / missing evidence:** a named case owner, one consolidated evidence request, and a time-boxed window (for example 72h) before assisted outreach to prevent abandonment.
3. **Reopen path.** All reopened claims go to a single case owner for manual review (R3). The escalation board receives only cases that have passed that review.

**Mechanism of improvement.** The design moves controls to the point where they are cheapest and most accurate: value is known at intake, so R1 is enforced by routing rather than by a fast path that bypasses review. Reviewer capacity is freed because Standard low-risk accessories no longer consume manual review. Premium low-risk accessories get the speed they need without a reviewed path that breaches 24h. High-value devices get the review and verification that reduces their complaint gap (22% vs 5% for Premium).

**What it sacrifices or leaves unresolved.**
- It removes the fast path for high-value devices, so Standard HV cases (current fast-path median 28h) will be slower. The gain is in quality and compliance, not in average time.
- Routing errors at intake are a new failure point. If a high-value device is tagged as low-risk, it reaches the wrong lane.
- The evidence loop is only partly addressed. Lane 4 needs better evidence tools, which are not part of this design.
- Part-price confirmation still constrains repair quotes unless a catalog is added (see Redesign C).

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-Complete Before Decision

**Target segments and paths.** The Request missing evidence loop (1,430 edges), the Manual review <-> Request evidence cycle (890 return edges), the unclear/missing-evidence segment (22%, 140h median), and the R2 exposure created by dispatching before evidence is confirmed. This design is aimed mainly at the front of the process.

**Process flow.**
1. **Single structured evidence bundle at intake.** A portal lets customers submit evidence in one pass. An assisted channel supports customers with low digital literacy.
2. **Refreshed auto-screen rules.** Rules become segment-aware and value-aware. They are deployed in about two weeks, with a capacity buffer for the expected 15–35% rise in manual inflow.
3. **Complete-or-hold gate.** No decision (approval, quote, or dispatch) is made until the evidence bundle is complete. Incomplete claims stay in a hold state with a defined owner and clock.
4. **Decision on complete evidence.** Complete low-risk claims go to fast approval. Complete high-value or ambiguous claims go to the reviewed path with the full evidence set, so reviewers do not need repeated information requests.
5. **Abandonment prevention.** Claims that stall in the hold state receive proactive outreach through the assisted channel before they are closed as abandoned.

**Mechanism of improvement.** Most of the cost and delay in the reviewed path comes from repeated information requests and loops, not from review itself. Requiring completeness before decision removes the rework loops that drive the 140h unclear-segment median and the 26h median for evidence-to-review returns. It also removes R2 exposure, because replacement dispatch cannot occur until evidence is complete. Fewer manual touches per case reduces load on the reviewer pool, which is constrained at 82% utilization.

**What it sacrifices or leaves unresolved.**
- The portal may raise abandonment among customers with low digital literacy, who are already overrepresented in the unclear segment. The assisted channel offsets this only partly and adds cost.
- Holding decisions until evidence is complete adds time to some claims that are currently fast-approved on partial evidence. Premium low-risk claims face the 24h threshold risk here.
- The design does not address the wrong-replacement failure mode, because part selection can be wrong even when evidence is complete. A pre-dispatch check is still needed.
- It leaves the repair-quote and fraud-review bottlenecks largely unchanged.

## 4. Redesign C: Pre-Authorized Envelopes and Recovery-by-Design

**Target segments and paths.** Repair-quote delays (Manual review -> Repair quote p90 60h; Repair quote -> Customer approval mean 30h), Customer approval -> Repair device, Dispatch replacement -> Complaint/reopen (the wrong-part long tail), and the escalation board's capacity limit. This design works mainly at the back end and at the fulfillment interface.

**Process flow.**
1. **Pre-authorized repair envelopes.** A standing catalog of part prices and approved repair scopes, by device family and defect type, lets the technician issue a quote within a pre-approved band. Quotes outside the band still go to review. This removes most of the wait for part-price confirmation.
2. **Scheduled risk review for high-value devices.** Fraud/risk specialists work scheduled batch windows (not only on Mondays or after campaigns), so R1 is satisfied without a queue spike. The batch is planned against the 82% utilization threshold.
3. **Lightweight pre-dispatch part-verification gate for high-value replacements.** Staffed from the underused quality-inspector pool. It adds 6–18h depending on resource design, so it is applied only to high-value replacements where wrong-part cost is EUR 410.
4. **Fast dispatch for low-risk accessories** is retained, with a post-dispatch recovery track.
5. **Proactive recovery desk.** Outreach happens before the complaint median of 50–70h, and a recovery specialist can resolve common issues without the escalation board. Cases that the desk cannot resolve go to the escalation board. All reopened cases go through manual review before close or denial (R3).

**Mechanism of improvement.** The design keeps the speed of the fast path where it is justified, and it targets the specific causes of delay and complaint in each path. Pre-agreed price bands remove waiting on part-price confirmation. The verification gate targets the wrong-replacement failure directly, using capacity that is already available. The recovery desk moves complaint handling to a point before the escalation board becomes the bottleneck, and it closes the R3 gap by making review mandatory on the reopen path.

**What it sacrifices or leaves unresolved.**
- It does not change the front-end routing or the rule set, so R1 violations at the auto screen persist unless a separate rule change is made.
- Catalog accuracy depends on maintenance. A stale catalog creates quote disputes and new reopen cases.
- The recovery desk can treat symptoms if root-cause analysis is weak.
- Premium high-value claims still incur the pre-dispatch gate delay unless the gate is designed to be fast for Premium cases.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A (triage lanes)**

- *Failure mode 1: routing errors.* If value or defect is mis-tagged at intake, high-value devices enter the low-risk lane and R1 violations continue. The routing error rate becomes the key quality driver and is hard to observe in the current data.
- *Failure mode 2: lane 2 becomes a new backlog.* If Standard low-risk verification is staffed by generalists who are not trained well enough, the 72h SLA is missed and complaints rise.
- *Bottleneck migration.* The constraint moves from manual review to fraud/risk specialists (2 people, Monday peaks) and to the pre-dispatch gate inspectors. Expect Monday queues to grow once HV routing is concentrated.

**Redesign B (evidence-complete before decision)**

- *Failure mode 1: abandonment in the most vulnerable customers.* Portal-driven completion may lower completion rates for customers with low digital literacy. Abandonment is already highest in the unclear segment.
- *Failure mode 2: rule-update inflow exceeds capacity.* The expected 15–35% increase in manual inflow could push the manual-review queue above the 82% utilization point, where queues grow sharply.
- *Bottleneck migration.* The bottleneck moves earlier, to the evidence-completeness check and the assisted-channel staff. Manual review may be relieved in aggregate but overloaded in the weeks immediately after the rule change.

**Redesign C (pre-authorized envelopes and recovery-by-design)**

- *Failure mode 1: stale or incorrect price catalog.* Quotes outside accurate bands generate disputes, customer approval delays, and new reopen cases.
- *Failure mode 2: recovery desk treats symptoms.* If complaints are resolved without root-cause analysis of wrong-part selection, complaint volume may fall in the short term but the underlying error persists.
- *Bottleneck migration.* The constraint shifts from part-price confirmation to the inspector pool (2 people, loaded after repair campaigns) and to customer approval (mean 30h). If the recovery desk misses cases, the escalation board (one 45-minute slot per day) becomes the binding constraint again.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0: Data reconciliation and baseline (2–4 weeks, no customer impact).**
- Resolve the edge-count inconsistencies (for example, dispatch inflow vs outflow; manual review inflow vs outflow) and define complaint denominators consistently.
- Measure R1 violations by value band, R2 violations by evidence status, and R3 violations on the escalation path. These counts set the baseline for compliance.

**Stage 1: First pilot (zero-regret, compliance-first).**
- Implement the R3 fix: all reopened claims go through manual review before close or denial. This affects only about 120 escalation cases and is cheap.
- Run Redesign A's value-tagging and HV risk gate in shadow mode for 4–6 weeks, comparing routing decisions against actual outcomes without changing customer treatment.
- In parallel, pilot the pre-dispatch part-verification gate for Standard high-value replacements in one region or segment.

**Stage 2: Live pilots by segment.**
- Launch Redesign A lane 1 (Premium low-risk) and lane 3 (HV) in a stepped rollout by region or segment.
- Run Redesign B's rule update with a capacity buffer, only after manual-review utilization is confirmed to be well below 82%.
- Introduce Redesign C's price catalog for one device family first, with quote-dispute monitoring.

**Stage 3: Scale and integrate.**
- Add the evidence portal and assisted channel once the rule update has shown stable inflow and the abandonment effect is measured.
- Expand the catalog and recovery desk, and tune the escalation board only after upstream complaint volume has been reduced.

**Sequencing logic.** Fix compliance gaps first, because they are inexpensive and their risk is not contingent on other decisions. Then fix routing so that controls are applied by risk. Then reduce information loops and repair waits. Scale only after the pilot has shown that downstream metrics improved too.

**Stop/go thresholds (qualitative and ranges).**
- *Go:* a clear relative reduction in complaint/reopen rate for the target segment (for example, the HV fast-path gap narrows materially); R1 and R3 violation counts fall to near zero in sampled audits; manual-review utilization stays below about 80%; Premium low-risk p90 stays under 24h.
- *No-go or pause:* abandonment in the unclear segment rises clearly above baseline; manual-review queues grow sharply after a rule change; the cost per resolved claim, including complaints, credits, and reshipments, does not fall; or new loops appear in the conformance check.
- *Mixed:* cycle time falls but complaints do not, or complaints fall but cycle time for Premium exceeds tolerance. In these cases, adjust the segment rule before expanding.

**How to tell whether improvements are real or only shifted downstream.**
- **Measure end-to-end, not per stage.** Track time-to-final-resolution and cost per resolved claim for cohorts defined by start date, not by stage exit. Include complaints, reopens, credits, and reshipments.
- **Use a long outcome window.** Complaint lag has a median of 50–70h and a p90 of 150–180h, and reopens can arrive weeks later. Observe for at least 30 days after close before judging quality.
- **Use controls.** Use a stepped rollout or matched segments and compare with difference-in-differences. Pre-register the metrics so they are not chosen after the fact.
- **Check queue conservation.** For each resource, compare arrivals and departures. If one stage speeds up and the next queue grows, the gain is a shift, not an improvement.
- **Audit independently.** Use random sampling of closed cases to measure hidden R1, R2, and R3 violations that automated replay may miss.
- **Track hidden contacts.** Monitor customer contact volume outside the logged process to catch complaints that do not appear as reopens.
- **Re-run conformance.** Compare fitness, precision, and the rule-violation counts with the Stage 0 baseline. A real improvement should show fewer deviations, a more precise model, and fewer fast-path edges that bypass controls, not just a different set of loops.
