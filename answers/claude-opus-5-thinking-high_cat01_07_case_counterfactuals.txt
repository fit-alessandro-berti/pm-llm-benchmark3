## 1. Feasibility model

```
PARTIAL ORDERS  ( ≺ = must precede,  ∥ = may run concurrently,  { } = unordered set )

COMMON SKELETON (all four cases)
  Register ≺ Triage
  Triage   ≺ IdentityCheck
  Triage   ≺ DocumentTaskRelease
  DocumentTaskRelease ≺ DocumentCheck
  { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck } ≺ Assessment_final
  Assessment_final ≺ Decision ≺ Notify
  (no ordering asserted between IdentityCheck and DocumentCheck except via case rules)

K-104  high-risk & high-value, no originals, complex corporate documents
  Triage ≺ { IdentityCheck ∥ DocumentCheck(D1) }
  Triage ≺ EscalationAdvice(S1)
  EscalationAdvice(S1) ∥ { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck }     (advisory, non-blocking)
  PrelimComplianceScreen(C1) ∥ { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck , EscalationAdvice }
  { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck } ≺ Assessment(A1)
  Assessment(A1) ≺ ComplianceReview_final(C1) ≺ Decision(M1) ≺ Notify(M1)
  PrelimComplianceScreen ⊀⊐ ComplianceReview_final   (screen never substitutes for final)

K-219  low-risk & low-value, originals required, standard handwritten
  Triage ≺ IdentityCheck(I2)
  Triage ≺ OriginalsReceipt(external)
  OriginalsReceipt ≺ DocumentCheck(D1 or D2)
  IdentityCheck ∥ { OriginalsReceipt , DocumentCheck }
  { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck } ≺ Assessment(A2) ≺ Decision ≺ Notify
  no ComplianceReview node in this case's order

K-337  high-risk & high-value, originals required, slow external supplier
  Triage ≺ IdentityCheck(I1)
  Triage ≺ OriginalsReceipt ≺ DocumentCheck_1
  { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck_1 } ≺ Assessment_1
  loop generation k (k ≥ 1):
     Assessment_k ≺ RequestMoreInfo_k ≺ DocumentCheck_{k+1} ≺ Assessment_{k+1}
  Assessment_last ≺ ComplianceReview_final(C1) ≺ Decision(M1) ≺ Notify
  factual: k ∈ {1,2}; counterfactual: k = 1 (loop generation 2 forbidden)

K-411  low-risk & low-value, no originals, digital standard documents
  factual (defective release):  IdentityCheck(I2) ≺ DocumentTaskRelease ≺ DocumentCheck(D2)
  counterfactual (both tasks released at Triage):
     Triage ≺ { IdentityCheck(I2) ∥ DocumentCheck(D2) }
  { IdentityCheck , DocumentCheck } ≺ Assessment(A2) ≺ Decision ≺ Notify
```

```
CONDITIONAL AND DECLARATIVE CONSTRAINTS

C1  originals_required = true  →  OriginalsReceipt ≺ DocumentCheck (every generation that
    re-examines originals); IdentityCheck is not blocked by OriginalsReceipt.
C2  originals_required = false →  IdentityCheck ∥ DocumentCheck permitted (K-104, K-411).
C3  high_risk OR high_value  →  exists ComplianceReview_final with
    Assessment_last ≺ ComplianceReview_final ≺ Decision.
C4  after RequestMoreInfo  →  a NEW DocumentCheck and a NEW Assessment are mandatory before
    Decision; if high_risk OR high_value, a new OR explicitly reaffirmed (documented,
    signed) compliance review is also mandatory.
C5  four-eyes: resource(Assessment_last) ≠ resource(ComplianceReview_final).
    With A1/A2 and only C1 certified, C1 may never perform the final Assessment.
C6  preliminary compliance screen is permitted at any point ≥ Triage but is
    non-substitutable for C3; it may only reduce the service time of the final review.
C7  resource non-overlap: for every resource r, its service intervals are pairwise disjoint
    (S1's advisory work may overlap other resources' work, never its own).
C8  C1 is a single-server, non-substitutable resource → capacity, not sequence, is binding.
C9  Decision is M1-only and observed to occur in discrete signing windows
    (~08:30, ~14:00, ~18:00), so readiness gains smaller than the gap to the next window
    do not become cycle-time gains.
C10 queue figures in the snapshots are forecasts; treat them as ±30-50% and decaying
    between snapshots, not as commitments.
C11 log integrity: recorded concurrency is valid only if two distinct resources hold two
    distinct tasks; batch-entered timestamps that create self-overlap violate C7 in the
    data even when the physical work was legal.
```

Which lever each intervention actually pulls:

- **Timing only (same routing, same decision rule):** K-104 and K-411. K-104 moves S1 advisory and part of C1's reading effort earlier and reserves a slot, but the activity set, the resources, and the required compliance sequence are unchanged. K-411 changes only *when* the document task is released, so both checks overlap; the path, resources, and decision rule are identical.
- **Routing:** K-219. The document work migrates from D1 to D2, which changes the service-time distribution, the queue that is consumed, and the rework hazard. The control-flow order is untouched.
- **Allowed decision policy:** K-337. Prohibiting the second `Request More Info` does not merely delete waiting time; it forbids a legal control-flow branch and forces `Decision` on an evidence set that the factual process judged insufficient. The output value (approve / approve-with-conditions / reject) is inside the counterfactual, not outside it.
- K-104 has a secondary routing flavour (C1 capacity is reserved rather than queued FIFO), which is why its system-level behaviour is worse than its single-case behaviour.

## 3. Counterfactual derivations

### K-104

**Factual mechanism:** The sanctions ambiguity is information that existed at Triage (Mon 08:50) but was not converted into work until Assessment (Tue 15:10-19:10). Because of that, three things happened serially that need not have: A1 re-derived the risk question inside the assessment, S1's advice was consumed only afterwards (Tue 19:20-21:20), and C1 entered the case cold. The measured critical path is a chain of queues, not of work: 13 h of D1 backlog on complex corporate documents, ~9.5 h of A1 backlog, then ~18 h of C1 backlog (the Mon 16:00 snapshot shows C1 at 18 h with two urgent cases just added), and finally M1's evening signing window. Actual service time on the path is about 15 h inside a 58.5 h elapsed span, so roughly three-quarters of the case is waiting.

**Minimal intervention:** Change nothing about resources, activity set, or the compliance sequence. Only (i) emit the escalation packet at Triage instead of after Assessment, and (ii) convert C1's position in the queue from "arrive at Tue 19:10 and wait" to a reserved slot with pre-read evidence. `Compliance Review` still follows the final `Assessment`, C1 still performs it, A1 still assesses, so rule 5, rule 9, and rule 10 are all intact.

**Temporal propagation:** Identity and document timings do not move, because the intervention adds no document capacity and does not re-route: D1 still starts Mon 21:40 and finishes Tue 05:40. A1 still starts around Tue 15:10 because the assessment queue is untouched — this is the first place where "earlier readiness" fails to become "earlier completion." What does change is downstream. Assessment service time falls modestly (4 h to about 3-3.5 h) because the sanctions question arrives pre-framed with S1's advice attached rather than being discovered mid-task, and the separate 2 h serial S1 step disappears from the path (it was moved into the Monday parallel window where I1 and D1 were busy anyway). The large gain is the removal of the ~18 h compliance wait: a reserved slot lets C1 begin within roughly an hour of assessment completion, and having already read the risk packet the review takes 3-3.5 h rather than 4.5 h. The saved hours are then partly re-absorbed by M1, who signs in windows; if compliance ends Tue 23:00 the decision still waits for Wed 08:30. Net base saving is therefore 7-12 h, materially less than the ~19 h of queue time nominally removed.

**Process-feasibility check:** Preliminary screening by C1 while checks run is explicitly allowed (rule 10) and does not substitute for the final review, which still starts after Assessment. S1's advisory work is permitted to overlap other checks (pool note), and S1 does not double-book itself. Four-eyes holds: A1 assesses, C1 reviews. C1 performs two non-overlapping intervals (prescreen, then final review), respecting rule 11. No activity precedes its prerequisites, and because originals are not required, the identity and document branches legitimately run in parallel.

**Best/base/worst case:** Best (~44-47 h): the packet is complete, S1 answers Monday morning, C1 uses the reserved slot Tuesday evening, M1 signs Wed 08:30 — approve with conditions, better documented than the factual outcome. Base (~47-51 h): as above but compliance runs Wednesday early morning; saving 7-12 h. Worst (58-62 h or more): the early packet is missing evidence that only D1's complex document check produced, so the prescreen must be redone, the reserved slot is released or wasted, and the case rejoins the ordinary C1 queue; in the tail, early S1 involvement identifies a further evidence gap and issues `Request More Info`, which under rule 7 forces a new document check and a new assessment plus a new or reaffirmed compliance review, adding 8-36 h of external wait and pushing the case past Thursday. That tail is genuinely more likely under this intervention than under the factual path, and it is a cost of looking earlier.

**Evidence that would reverse the conclusion:** Logged evidence that packets assembled at Triage are complete less than about 60% of the time; a measurement showing that C1's realised service time is insensitive to prior reading (no 1-3 h saving); a C1 utilisation series showing no exploitable slack, so every reserved hour is taken from another urgent case; or a decision-log analysis showing M1's signing windows are rigid enough that upstream savings under 12 h never reach the customer. Conversely, if D1's queue turned out to be the binding constraint on most high-risk cases, the compliance-side gain would be real but rarely on the critical path.

### K-219

**Factual mechanism:** Triage made a skill-based routing choice — handwriting to D1 — and paid for it with queue time. Originals were ready Mon 17:30 and the identity check was already complete at 16:20, so the case was document-ready Monday evening, yet D1's backlog (11 h at the Mon 16:00 snapshot) deferred the 4 h check to Tue 09:30-13:30. A2 then waited about 6.5 h and worked Tue 20:00-22:30, and M1's Wed 08:30 window closed the case at 48 h. The routing bought accuracy: no rework, one document check, one assessment.

**Minimal intervention:** Keep every activity, order, and other resource identical; substitute D2 for D1 on the single `Document Check`, triggered by originals receipt rather than by D1 availability.

**Temporal propagation:** The gain is created upstream and eroded downstream. D2's queue is ~5 h at Mon 16:00, so D2 can plausibly start Mon 21:00-22:00 instead of Tue 09:30 — about 11 h earlier. But D2 is the generalist and handwritten material sits high in its 4-8 h band, so the check consumes 6-8 h and completes Tue 03:00-05:00, only about 9 h earlier in readiness terms. The assessment queue then converts that unevenly: A2's Tue backlog is ~6 h, so a start between Tue 10:00 and 14:00 is plausible, completing Tue 12:30-16:30. Whether that becomes cycle time depends entirely on M1: a Tue afternoon or evening signing window yields a 33-38 h case, while waiting for Wed 08:30 yields 42-46 h and almost no visible improvement despite the earlier document work. The rework branch inverts everything: a handwriting clarification issued Tue morning sits in an 8-36 h external wait, then needs a fresh document check and a fresh assessment (rule 7), landing Wed evening or Thursday morning for a 66-80 h case. The mechanism matters — the loss is not the extra 2-4 h of checking, it is the external wait that the loop imports.

**Process-feasibility check:** Originals precede the document check in both worlds (rule 4). The identity check remains parallel and unaffected. The case is neither high risk nor high value, so no compliance review is required and four-eyes is not engaged. D1 and D2 are in the same pool and formally interchangeable, so the substitution is legal; only the service-time distribution and error hazard change. In the rework branch, the mandatory new document check and new assessment are both present.

**Best/base/worst case:** Best (~33-36 h): D2 starts Monday evening, finishes near the 6 h end, A2 picks it up Tuesday morning, M1 signs Tuesday afternoon — save 12-15 h with a clean approval. Base (~38-44 h): D2 takes 7-8 h, assessment lands Tuesday afternoon, decision waits for Wed 08:30 — save 4-10 h. Worst (66-80 h, probability 25-40%): a clarification request on the handwriting, an external wait spanning a night or more, a second document check and second assessment, decision Thursday — 18-32 h worse than factual. A separate, more troubling worst case has good cycle time and bad quality: D2 misreads a handwritten figure, no rework is triggered, and an incorrect approval is issued that surfaces later as a complaint or write-off. Probability-weighted, expected cycle time is roughly flat (about -3 to +5 h) with much fatter tails and worse expected accuracy.

**Evidence that would reverse the conclusion:** A measured D2 clarification rate on handwritten standard documents below roughly 10-12%, which would make the substitution clearly positive; or evidence that D2's handwritten service time stays near 4-5 h rather than 6-8 h. In the other direction, evidence that D1's queue is systematically shorter than 6-8 h by mid-morning would remove most of the upside, and evidence that M1 rarely signs before the next morning would remove the rest.

### K-337

**Factual mechanism:** This case is dominated by external response time and calendar position, not by internal capacity. Each loop generation costs a supplier wait, a fresh D1 document check, and a fresh A1 assessment, and each re-entry rejoins queues at its own arrival time, which is why every generation lands on the following morning. Loop 1 costs about 20.5 h of external wait; loop 2 costs about 70.5 h because it was issued Fri 10:30 and the weekend sits inside it. Total elapsed time is roughly 214 h, of which the two loops plus their downstream re-queuing account for well over half. Note that assessment 2 already asked for only "one more confirmation" — the marginal information sought in loop 2 was small relative to its cost.

**Minimal intervention:** Do not touch anything before Fri 08:00. Forbid `Request More Info 2` and require that `Assessment 2` be closed as the final assessment, with the residual inconsistency documented rather than suppressed, then run the mandatory compliance review and decision.

**Temporal propagation:** Deleting the loop does not simply subtract its 70.5 h wait; it also removes `Document Check 3` (3.5 h plus its Monday queue), `Assessment 3` (2.5 h plus its Tuesday queue), and the re-queuing at C1 and M1 that followed them. Against that, two things get longer. The final assessment must now reach a defensible conclusion on incomplete evidence and record the discrepancy, so 2.5 h becomes 3-3.5 h. And the compliance review becomes harder, not easier: C1 must judge a known unresolved item, likely consult S1, and document a condition set, so 4 h becomes 4-6 h. The binding question is whether Friday can absorb all of it. With C1's queue extrapolated at 4-8 h on Friday, a 14:00 start and a 19:00 finish are plausible, and M1 has demonstrated evening signing (Wed 18:00 in K-104), giving a Friday-night close near 105 h. If C1's Friday queue is longer, or C1 will not sign at the end of a day, everything shifts across the weekend to Monday morning and the case closes near 170 h — still a large saving, but half of the headline figure. This asymmetry is the whole point: most of the apparent benefit is the weekend, and it is captured only if the internal chain closes before Friday evening.

**Process-feasibility check:** Rule 7 is satisfied without any shortcut, because `Document Check 2` and `Assessment 2` already exist after `Request More Info 1`; the final compliance review therefore is a genuine new review over the latest assessment, not a reaffirmation. Rule 5 holds (compliance after the last assessment, before decision). Four-eyes holds: A1 assesses, C1 reviews, and C1 never performs the assessment. Originals preceded every document check. The unresolved discrepancy is explicitly on the record, satisfying the intervention's own condition that it not be silently ignored. Feasibility is not the constraint here — admissibility of the decision is.

**Best/base/worst case:** Best (~100-108 h): the residual item is judged immaterial, C1 signs Friday afternoon with conditions, M1 approves Friday evening, and the conditions are cleared post-decision at low cost. Base (~105-120 h, or ~165-175 h if compliance slips to Monday): approve with conditions including a holdback, a reduced limit, or a post-decision verification obligation; a 15-30% chance of rejection instead. Worst: the discrepancy was material — an approval creates realised loss, or a rejection destroys a good case that the factual third loop would have cleared (recall the factual loop 3 ended with "evidence accepted," which is direct evidence that the extra information was decision-relevant at least once). A second worst case is procedural: C1 declines to certify incomplete evidence, forcing an exception route that costs more time than the loop it replaced.

**Evidence that would reverse the conclusion:** A base-rate study showing that second-loop information changes the decision in a material share of cases — even 10-15% would make a blanket prohibition expensive relative to its time saving. Similarly, evidence that conditional approvals generate substantial downstream monitoring, appeal, or loss costs would show that the saving is metric displacement rather than efficiency. Conversely, evidence that second loops almost never change the outcome, or that a materiality threshold can separate the cases that do, would strengthen a narrowly scoped version of the policy — for example, forbidding new information requests after Thursday noon unless the open item exceeds a materiality threshold, which captures the weekend benefit without a general ban.

### K-411

**Factual mechanism:** Nothing about this case required serialisation. Originals are not required and the documents are digital and standard, so rule 3 permits full concurrency. The serialisation is an artefact: Triage's legacy system created only the identity task, and a case lock prevented anyone from owning the document task until identity completed at Tue 13:20. D2 then started at 15:00 and finished at 19:30, too late for the assessment pool that day, so the case sat overnight and closed in M1's Wed 14:00 window at 29.5 h.

**Minimal intervention:** Create both verification tasks at Triage and permit concurrent ownership through a controlled workaround, with real-time timestamp capture. No resource, activity, or decision rule changes.

**Temporal propagation:** The correct way to think about the gain is that the case becomes ready at max(identity branch, document branch) rather than at their sum. The identity branch is short (1.83 h of service behind a decaying ~2 h queue) and the document branch dominates: D2's Tuesday queue is ~4 h at 08:00, so with the task released at 09:35 D2 plausibly starts around 12:00-13:30 and finishes 16:30-18:00. Readiness therefore improves by only about 1.5-3 h, not by the 5.7 h of identity-check-plus-handoff that a naive reading suggests, because the document task still has to wait for D2. Converting that readiness into cycle time is a second, weaker filter: A2's queue is ~5 h and grew during the day, so the case either catches A2's evening capacity (as A2 did in K-219 at Tue 20:00) and closes in M1's Wed 08:30 window at ~24 h, or it does not, in which case the assessment and decision windows are unchanged and the saving is essentially zero. This is the clearest instance in the four cases where a real structural improvement produces little visible benefit because two downstream queues absorb it.

**Process-feasibility check:** Concurrency is explicitly legal here (rule 3, no originals). I2 and D2 are distinct resources with distinct tasks, so rule 11 is respected in reality. The case needs no compliance review, so rules 5, 9, and 10 are not engaged. The genuine feasibility exposure is in the data, not the process: if the workaround is reconciled by later batch entry, the log may show a single resource with overlapping intervals or a document check starting before its task existed, which violates C7/C11 in the record and would make any before/after evaluation of this policy unreliable. Real-time timestamping is not a nicety in this intervention; it is what makes the intervention measurable.

**Best/base/worst case:** Best (~24 h): both checks done by 17:00, A2 works the evening, M1 signs Wed 08:30 — save about 5.5 h with unchanged quality. Base (~28-29.5 h): checks done 1.5-3 h earlier, assessment still Wednesday morning, decision still Wed 14:00 — save 0-2 h, essentially only slack and schedule optionality. Worst: no time saved and negative side effects — concurrent editing under a bypassed lock creates version ambiguity between the identity and document views of the case, or the log becomes unusable and the pilot cannot be evaluated. Note that the intervention is still worth doing in the base case, because it removes a defect and creates option value even when it does not move the mean.

**Evidence that would reverse the conclusion:** Evidence that the case lock exists for a substantive reason — for example, that the document check must read an identity-verified attribute — would convert this from a defect to a real dependency and make the workaround a control failure. Evidence that the assessment pool is saturated would show that earlier readiness cannot be converted at all, so the benefit is purely optical. And evidence that manual timestamping is unreliable in practice would mean the policy degrades the event log in exchange for roughly one hour of median improvement.

Applying all four policies to 100 similar cases in one week changes which resource binds. Compliance is the hardest constraint: C1 is the only certified reviewer, so with roughly 35-40 productive hours and 3-6 h per final review the pool supports about 7-13 final reviews per week. If even a quarter of 100 cases are high risk or high value, demand is 25-30 reviews and C1 saturates regardless of scheduling. Reserving provisional slots therefore does not create capacity; it reorders a queue whose total work is conserved. Target cases gain, non-reserved urgent cases lose as much or more, and the waiting tail worsens because an unused reserved slot is pure idle time on the binding server. The policy is positive-sum only if preliminary reading genuinely cuts mean review time from about 4.5 h to about 3.5 h. Because waiting near saturation behaves like ρ/(1-ρ), a 20% service-time cut on the bottleneck shortens the compliance queue far more than proportionally, while a low packet-completeness rate that adds 1-2 h of prescreen per case pushes utilisation the other way and the queue diverges. I would gate rollout on measured completeness above roughly 70%.

Substituting D2 for D1 looks free case by case, but D2 needs roughly 1.5-1.8 times as long on complex or handwritten material, so routing that work to D2 lowers the document pool's throughput even while shortening one case's wait. Add a 25-40% clarification rate and each substitution risks an extra document check, an extra assessment, and an 8-36 h external wait, so the queue that shrinks on D1 grows more than one-for-one on D2 and spills into assessment. A wait-threshold rule dominates a blanket switch.

Suppressing rework loops is the only intervention that removes work rather than moving it, freeing hours of D1 and A1 time per affected case plus the external wait. But its headline benefit is largely a weekend artefact, so a uniform rollout averages far less, and the bottleneck migrates to C1 and to a new conditions-and-appeals queue. Earlier task release in K-411 relocates waiting from document handoff to the assessment pool; and if the lock workaround is batch-timestamped, part of the measured gain is a logging artefact that also produces impossible overlapping service intervals, destroying the evidence needed to evaluate any of these policies.

## 7. Final synthesis

The most robust intervention is K-411's dual task release with genuine, well-logged concurrency. It corrects a defect rather than trading one queue for another: serialising the two checks had no basis in the rules, since originals were not required and rule 3 permits parallel verification. Its honest benefit is small — roughly 1.5 to 3 hours of earlier readiness and often zero end-to-end saving once the assessment queue and signing windows absorb it — but the sign of the effect never flips, it is instantly reversible, and it is the only intervention that improves the measurement substrate the others depend on.

The most dangerous despite its speed is K-337's prohibition of the second rework loop. It shows the largest cycle-time reduction, yet most of that figure is a calendar accident: the suppressed request was issued on a Friday morning, so the deleted wait contained a weekend. More fundamentally it changes decision policy rather than schedule, and the factual log argues against it — the third loop ended with evidence accepted, so the extra information was decision-relevant at least once. The costs land where cycle-time dashboards do not look: conditions monitoring, appeals, loss exposure, and defensibility. A narrower rule — no new request after Thursday noon unless the open item exceeds a materiality threshold — captures most of the benefit with far less risk.

Local reasoning is least transportable for K-104. Its 7 to 12 hour gain depends on C1 having exploitable slack, and C1 supports perhaps 7 to 13 final reviews weekly. At 100 cases per week, reservations only reorder a conserved workload, so the local gain becomes a transfer from non-reserved urgent cases.

The most valuable additional data are C1's utilisation and idle-reservation minutes, per-resource service-time and rework distributions by document type and handwriting difficulty, external response times by day of week, timestamp entry latency as a log-integrity measure, and evidence on whether later-loop information ever changed a decision.

## 8. Assumption audit

1. **structural** — The case lock in K-411 is a legacy technical artefact with no substantive data dependency: the document check does not require any output of the identity check, so full concurrency is genuinely admissible rather than merely convenient.
2. **temporal** — The queue figures in the snapshots are forecasts that decay roughly monotonically between observations and are accurate to within about ±30-50%; the extended evening and overnight working pattern visible in the factual logs (D1 finishing Tue 05:40, A2 working to Tue 22:30) continues in the counterfactuals.
3. **resource** — D1/D2 and A1/A2 remain formally interchangeable at approximately the stated service-time ratios (D2 needing roughly 1.5-1.8 times D1's time on complex or handwritten material), and C1 has no certified substitute anywhere in the window, so compliance capacity cannot be expanded, only resequenced.
4. **behavioral** — M1 signs decisions in discrete windows near 08:30, 14:00, and 18:00 rather than continuously, so upstream savings smaller than the gap to the next window do not convert into cycle time; this is why several interventions produce earlier readiness without earlier completion.
5. **behavioral** — External parties respond in 8-36 hours independently of how many prior requests they have received, with no priority, fatigue, or goodwill effect from a second request, and weekend hours contribute no progress.
6. **quality** — D2's probability of requesting clarification on difficult handwriting is 25-40% versus roughly 5-10% for D1, and each clarification costs a full external wait plus one additional document check and one additional assessment.
7. **quality** — Preliminary compliance work reduces final review time by 1-3 hours only when the early packet is at least about 80% complete; below that it adds 1-2 hours of wasted C1 time and risks anchoring the reviewer on an incomplete risk framing.
8. **interference** — The arrival process and routing of non-target cases are unchanged by these interventions (no strategic gaming by triage staff), and capacity reserved on C1 displaces other work rather than destroying it, so aggregate compliance workload is conserved while its distribution across cases is not.
