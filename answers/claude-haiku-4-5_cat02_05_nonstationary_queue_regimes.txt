# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Queueing quantities and units

**Opening hours convention:** 07:00–19:00 (12 hours per day). All arrival and completion counts are restricted to this window. Service times (`median_service_min`, `p90_service_min`) are active handling durations, excluding queueing delays. `mean_wip` is computed from 15-minute snapshots of cases in system (queued + in service).

**Key aggregates:**
- **Arrivals (_day):** count of cases starting service during the 12-hour window.
- **Completed (C_day):** count of cases finishing service during the 12-hour window.
- **median_service_min, p90_service_min:** quantiles of service duration conditional on service occurring; these do *not* represent mean flow time or queueing delay.
- **mean_wip (L):** time-averaged number of cases in system during 07:00–19:00.
- **staff_fte:** mean concurrent full-time-equivalent capacity (servers), e.g., 6.0 FTE  6 parallel servers with unit service rate.
- **complex_pct:** percentage of arrivals classified as multi-step variants (case-mix indicator, not a throughput measure).
- **peak_08_10_pct:** percentage of daily arrivals in the 08:00–09:59 window (burstiness proxy).
- **outage_min:** minutes of system unavailability during the day.

**Implicit daily flow time:** L /  or L / C, but only under strict conditions (stationarity, boundary neutrality).

## Five key threats to naive pooled inference

1. **Outage and recovery transients (2026-04-15, 2026-04-16):** The 180-minute outage on 2026-04-15 causes completed < arrivals, WIP spike (47), and a compensatory overshoot on 2026-04-16 (completions = 129 > arrivals = 112). Pooling these days with baseline weeks masks the distinction between demand-driven queueing and backlog recovery.

2. **Non-stationary case mix (complex_pct: 19%  47%):** Complex cases increase dramatically from 2026-04-06 through 2026-04-28 (19%–22%  41%–47%). Service medians and p90s grow proportionally (24–26 min  34–36 min). If service time is log-normal or mixture, pooling weekly cohorts ignores time-varying composition and confounds demand growth with service-process change.

3. **Staffing level shift at intervention (2026-04-29):** Staff FTE rises from 6.0 to 7.0 on 2026-04-29 (Wednesday) and maintains 7.0 or 6.5 through 2026-05-01. Prior week shows sustained imbalance (arrivals >> completions, WIP  43). Pooling pre- and post-intervention obscures the causal effect and masks whether the added capacity was necessary or reactive.

4. **Weekday–weekend structural difference:** Weekend arrivals are ~40–45% of weekday (44–51 vs. 92–121), staff is ~42% (2.5 FTE vs. 6.0), but service times are ~10% longer (28–29 min vs. 24–26 min baseline). `mean_wip` is lower in absolute terms but capacity pressure (WIP / FTE) is comparable. 5. **Intraday burstiness and backlog carry-over:** Representative hourly vectors show strong 08:00–09:00 peaks (peak_08_10_pct = 26–50%). Moreover, because the system closes at 19:00 and does not serve overnight, backlog must clear or be carried forward invisibly; the supplied daily counts do not show opening backlog, risking misattribution of completion spikes to demand rather than carryover processing.

### Comparison of segmentations

**Primary (temporal) segmentation:**
- **Strength:** Chronologically clear; reflects the actual intervention event.
- **Weakness:** Conflates multiple drivers (demand shift, backlog history, staffing change) within each regime; harder to isolate root causes.

**Alternative (demand vs. capacity) segmentation:**
- **Strength:** Separates case-mix drift from staffing decisions; highlights the bottleneck (understaffing given high-complex arrivals).
- **Weakness:** Overlaps temporally (the high-complex period *is* 2026-04-20 onwards); does not easily distinguish when the mix shift occurred vs. when staffing responded. Provides less narrative closure about operational changes.

**Reconciliation:** The two segmentations are complementary. The primary segmentation answers "what changed operationally over time?" (demand mix, then staffing response). The alternative answers "what is the root cause of WIP growth?" (elevated service times due to case-mix shift; insufficient capacity; then staffing correction). Together, they suggest the service center faced a **demand-side shock (case complexity)** that was initially **absorbed as backlog** (2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28) and then **mitigated by capacity investment** (2026-04-29 onwards).

## Threat 1: Weekday/Weekend composition

**Pooled:** If days are equally weighted, weekends (7 days, ~45 arrivals/day) are overrepresented relative to their operational importance. Weekends contribute ~315 arrivals (7 × 45) vs. weekdays ~2057 arrivals (21 × 98), but both are counted equally in daily averaging.

**Segmented:** Weekday baseline has 101 mean arrivals; weekend baseline has 40 mean arrivals. Pooling suggests the "typical" day has ~70 arrivals (unweighted mean), which misrepresents the operational workload. Weekday staffing is 6.0 FTE; weekend 2.5 FTE. Service time is also 4 min longer on weekends (28.5 vs. 24.5 min baseline). A manager relying on pooled 31 min median would underestimate the ease of baseline weekday operations.

**Quantified impact:** WIP is driven by arrivals and service time. Pooled mean WIP = 31.6. Weekday baseline WIP = 19.9 (good); weekend baseline WIP = 13.0 (excellent despite longer service times due to reduced demand). Post-intervention weekday WIP  37; post-intervention weekend WIP = 20.5. If one plans staffing using pooled WIP, the recommended FTE would be intermediate; actually, weekends need only ~42% of weekday capacity, yet pooling suggests ~5.7 FTE average.

---

## Threat 2: Complex-case mix shift

**Pooled:** mean complex_pct = 33.4%. This obscures the fact that complex cases are *not uniformly distributed*: they range from 18% (baseline) to 47% (peak overload).

**Segmented breakdown:**
- Baseline: 20.8% complex
- Overload: 44.3% complex (2.1× baseline)
- Post-intervention: 44.0% complex (same as overload, not regressed)

**Service-time consequence:** If we assume the service-time increase is proportional to the mix shift, we can decompose it:
- Baseline median service: 24.8 min
- Overload median service: 35.2 min (difference = +10.4 min, or +42%)
- Post-intervention median: 32.7 min (improvement of 2.5 min vs. overload, despite complex % still 44%)

**Interpretation:** The jump from 20.8% to 44.3% complex does *not* fully explain the +10.4 min service increase (that would be ~+4 min if service scaling is linear with complex %). The remaining ~6 min increase suggests either:
1. Service-time variance (p90 jumps from 48 to 81 min, suggesting heavier tails / resource contention for complex cases);
2. Feedback effect: longer service times  queue buildup  queueing delays inflate recorded service time (if the median is contaminated by queue-time observations—*possible if the data conflates handling + queue time*);
3. Complex-case service time itself is longer, and the proportion increase combines with per-case service increase.

**Pooled inference error:** A manager using pooled stats (median 31 min, 33% complex) might assume linear scaling and predict that increasing complex % from 30% to 45% adds only ~2 min per case. The actual data show +10 min; pooling loses the interaction.

- Mean arrivals = 113.4; mean completions = 101.0; **throughput deficit** = 12.4 cases/day.

- WIP grows monotonically: 37  50 over 7 days.

**Post-intervention week (2026-04-29 to 2026-05-03, adjusted for weekend):**
- Arrivals  completions in *all* days (net –1 to +6 cases backlog reduction).
- Mean weekday (3 days): arrivals = 115, completions = 121; **throughput surplus** = 6 cases/day.
- Mean weekend (2 days, partial data): arrivals = 49, completions = 49; balanced.
- Staff: 7.0 FTE Wed–Thu, 6.5 FTE Fri, 3.0 FTE weekend.
- WIP declines: 43  36  32 (on weekday subset) or stabilizes at ~20 (weekends).

**Pooled summary:** If pre- and post-intervention periods are equally weighted, arrivals  completions overall, and mean WIP  31.6. This **hides the critical fact that the system was in deficit pre-intervention and surplus post-intervention**—a reversal that signals the intervention was necessary and effective.

**Quantified impact:** 
- Pre-intervention arrival rate: 113.4/day ÷ 12 hrs = **9.45 cases/hour** (weekday mean).
- Pre-intervention completion rate: 101.0/day ÷ 12 hrs = **8.42 cases/hour**.
- Deficit: **1.03 cases/hour** or **6% unsustainable imbalance**.

- Post-intervention arrival rate: 115.0/day ÷ 12 hrs = **9.58 cases/hour** (weekday mean).
- Post-intervention completion rate: 121.0/day ÷ 12 hrs = **10.08 cases/hour**.
- Surplus: **0.50 cases/hour** or **5% capacity cushion**.

**Pooled inference error:** Reporting "mean arrivals  completions across all days" suggests the system has adequate capacity, obscuring that it was inadequate before the intervention and adequate after. This could lead to erroneous rollback of the staffing increase.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## 5.1 Fano factor and index of dispersion

The **Fano factor** (variance-to-mean ratio for counts) quantifies over-dispersion relative to Poisson assumptions. If arrivals follow a Poisson process, Fano  1. Fano > 1 indicates burstiness.

**Calculation on daily arrivals:**

Pooled (all 28 days):
- Mean arrivals: 104.1
- Variance: 245.0 (from day-to-day variation)
- Fano factor: 245.0 / 104.1  **2.35**

Baseline regime (n=8 days, excl. outage):
- Mean: 101.0
- Variance: 27.0
- Fano factor: 27.0 / 101.0  **0.27**

Overload regime (n=6 weekdays):
- Mean: 113.4
- Variance: 33.5
- Fano factor: 33.5 / 113.4  **0.30**

**Interpretation:**
- Pooled Fano  2.35 >> 1: Strong over-dispersion, driven by regime switching (low demand in baseline, high in overload) and weekday/weekend cycles. A Poisson model fit to pooled data would severely underestimate variance and lead to underestimation of queue-length tail probabilities.
- Within-regime Fano  0.27–0.30 << 1: *Under-dispersion* relative to Poisson. Actual daily arrivals are more stable than Poisson predicts. This suggests arrivals are *smoothed* by operational constraints (e.g., scheduled appointments or batch processing), not random walk-ins.

**Implication:** A queueing model pooling all data would incorrectly parameterize the arrival process. Separate models per regime with lower-variance arrival streams would be more accurate.

## 5.5 Why four representative-hourly vectors cannot establish a full stochastic model

1. **Insufficient sample size:** Four days are not representative of the full 28-day period. We do not have hourly data for:
   - The outage day (2026-04-15) in detail, only completion gaps.
   - The transition period 2026-04-16 to 2026-04-19 (recovery dynamics).
   - The peak of overload (2026-04-27 to 2026-04-28) in hourly detail.
   - Post-intervention stabilization (2026-04-30 to 2026-05-03).

2. **Unrepresentative selection:** The four vectors are labeled "candidates" and notes suggest hand-picked examples (e.g., "ordinary weekday," "bursty post-mix-change weekday"). This introduces selection bias and does not support inference about the population of all days.

3. **Lack of within-day stationarity validation:** Do the 12 hourly bins represent i.i.d. samples, or is there systematic time-of-day effect (e.g., peak 08-09, decline through evening)? The data show strong morning peak (26–50% of daily arrivals in 08-10 window), implying intra-hour arrivals are not homogeneous Poisson. We cannot assess the shape of the inter-arrival-time distribution or the queueing-length process within hours.

4. **Confounded regimes:** The "bursty" (2026-04-21) and "ordinary" (2026-04-07) vectors differ in both case mix and demand volatility. Without controlling for case mix, we cannot isolate the effect of operational decisions (e.g., appointment scheduling) on intraday burstiness.

5. **No autocorrelation structure:** Hour-to-hour arrivals might be correlated (e.g., batch arrivals from a batch process, or appointments clustered by staff schedule). Four vectors do not permit lag-structure estimation or assessment of queue state carry-over across hours.

**Conclusion:** The four hourly vectors are useful for **descriptive visualization** of intraday patterns and **hypothesis generation** (e.g., "peak 08-10 suggests morning rush"), but they do not support **parametric stochastic modeling** (fitting a specific inter-arrival-time distribution, service-time distribution, or queueing-process model). A full analysis would require event-level or higher-frequency time-series data.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law: **L = W** or **L =  × C**, where:
- L = mean number of cases in system (WIP)
-  = mean arrival rate (cases/unit time)
- W = mean time in system (flow time, arrival to completion)
- C = mean time in system (if measured as "cumulative duration" or "cycle time")

The law holds under mild conditions (non-anticipating queueing discipline, sample-path average convergence). However, it requires:
1. **Boundary stability:** No large backlog entering or leaving the window.
2. **Throughput stationarity:**  and C should be constant during the window, or average values should apply.
3. **WIP consistency:** L measured at same boundary conditions as  and C.

# 7. Operational implications

## 7.1 Recommended staffing policy per regime

### Baseline (low-complex, 20% complex cases)
- **Staffing:** 6.0 FTE weekday, 2.5 FTE weekend.
- **Target:** Maintain WIP  18–20 and mean flow time  2.4 hours.
- **Rationale:** Current staffing is adequate. Service median  24 min; queue wait  2.0 hr is acceptable for non-urgent service-center work.

### High-complexity demand (40%+ complex cases, low-complexity approach inadequate)
- **Staffing immediately post-demand-shift (2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28):** Inadequate at 6.0 FTE weekday.
  - Actual throughput deficit: 12.4 cases/day unprocessed.
  - Service time increases to 34 min (42% increase); if complex service time follows per-case logic, capacity available  6 × 720 / 34  127 cases/day (vs. demand 113), but contention/WIP growth suggests real capacity is ~101 cases/day. This implies setup time, context switching, or queueing overhead consumes ~26 cases' worth of capacity.
  - **Recommendation:** Increase weekday FTE from 6.0 to 7.0 to restore throughput surplus (estimated +17 cases/day capacity) and allow backlog clearance.

- **Staffing post-intervention (2026-04-29 onwards):** 7.0 FTE weekday effective.
  - Achieved throughput  121 cases/day vs. demand 115. Backlog now clearing.
  - Mean flow time improved from 5.4 hr (overload) to 3.6 hr (post-intervention).
  - Recommendation: **Maintain 7.0 FTE weekday** to sustain throughput surplus until backlog clears; then reassess. If complex % regresses to <25%, may reduce to 6.0 FTE; if complex % persists at 40%+, retain 7.0 FTE.

- **Weekend staffing:** Increase from 2.5 to 3.0 FTE if complex % stays elevated.
  - Weekend baseline (complex 20%) at 2.5 FTE: WIP  13, arrivals  40, completions  40 (balanced).
  - Weekend overload (complex 40%) at 2.5 FTE: WIP  21, arrivals  47, completions  41 (deficit 6/day).
  - Post-intervention weekend data (May 2–3) at 3.0 FTE: arrivals  49, completions  49 (balanced). WIP  20 (still elevated vs. baseline 13, suggesting residual complex-case backlog).
  - **Recommendation:** Increase weekend to 3.0 FTE during high-complex period; reduce back to 2.5 FTE only after complex % regresses and weekend WIP stabilizes 14.

## 7.2 Intraday peak-smoothing and queue-length resilience

**Problem:** Peak arrivals concentrated in 08:00–10:00 (26–50% of daily demand). Bursty profiles (e.g., 2026-04-21: 34 arrivals in 11-12 hour, 24 in 14-15 hour) cause intraday queue spikes and suboptimal resource utilization (idle periods in 15-19 window).

**Options:**

1. **Appointment scheduling or triage:** Encourage off-peak appointments to smooth 08-10 peak.
   - Baseline data suggest 08-10 peak is 28–33% of weekday demand (relatively smooth).
   - Overload bursty case shows 08-10 = 44% and additional bursts at 11-12 and 14-15 (internal structure breaks Poisson assumptions).
   - **If appointments are used:** Offer 30% incentive (fast-track) for 14:00–16:00 off-peak arrivals; reserve 50% of 08-10 slots for walk-ins/urgent cases.
   - **Expected impact:** Reduce peak hourly arrival from ~20 to ~12, lower transient WIP spikes, reduce likelihood of exceeding queue-length SLA (e.g., max queue 10 cases at any time).

2. **Cross-training and inline capacity flex:** When peak load is detected (real-time WIP  15), activate temporary floating staff.
   - Baseline analysis shows WIP peak is ~19 (manageable); overload WIP peaks ~50 (unsustainable).
   - With 6.0 FTE, WIP  20 is a signal to call in 0.5–1.0 FTE flex staff.
   - **Expected impact:** Reduce likelihood of reaching WIP 50; limit max WIP to ~25 even during high-complex periods (vs. observed 50 pre-intervention).

## 7.3 Case-mix shift management

**Observation:** complex_pct jumped from 20% (baseline) to 44% (overload). Cause is unknown (demand-side: more complex customers arrived; supply-side: classification rules changed; mixed: both).

**Recommended data collection:**
- Log case type and complexity classifier *at intake*, not post-hoc. Compare intake classification with case-type changes.
- If demand-side: investigate customer base change (new contract, new geography, seasonal peak).
- If supply-side (classification change): validate against service times. If actual service time did not increase proportionally, the classification may be inconsistent.

**Interim policy:** Assume demand-side case-mix change is permanent (conservative). Then:
- Invest in staff training to standardize complex-case handling and reduce per-case service time (currently 34 min; target 30 min would save 19.2 cases/day at 6.0 FTE).
- Conduct time-motion study on complex cases: identify bottlenecks (e.g., multiple sign-offs, system integration delays) and redesign workflow.
- If service time can be reduced to 30 min and complex % stabilizes at 44%, staffing can drop back to 6.5 FTE (vs. current 7.0 FTE) while maintaining throughput  demand.

---

## 7.4 Policy reversal: Pooled vs. segmented perspective

**Example: Should the staffing increase of 2026-04-29 be sustained?**

**Pooled perspective:**
- Mean arrivals: 104.1 cases/day.
- Mean completions: 101.8 cases/day.
- Conclusion: "Balanced on average; capacity is adequate; consider rolling back staffing increase to save labor cost."

**Segmented perspective:**
- Pre-intervention (2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28): arrivals 113.4, completions 101.0  **11% deficit**. System unsustainable.
- Post-intervention (2026-04-29 to 2026-05-01): arrivals 115.0, completions 121.0  **5% surplus**. System sustainable.
- Backlog history: WIP grew from 37 (Mon 20) to 50 (Tue 28), then declined to 32 (Fri May 1). Staffing increase enabled recovery.

**Segmented recommendation:** **Maintain the staffing increase.** Rolling back would revert to 6.0 FTE, which is insufficient for the (now-permanent) 40%+ complex case mix. The pooled average (104 cases, 6.0 FTE) is feasible for low-complex baseline (20%), but not for current case mix (44%). The appearance of balance in pooled data is a **Simpson's-paradox-like reversal:** pre- and post-intervention regimes have opposite surplus/deficit signs; averaging obscures both.

**Financial implication:** 
- 1.0 FTE weekend increase (2.5  3.0): ~$60K/year if regional labor cost is $60/hour.
- 0.5–1.0 FTE weekday peak flex (weekday 7.0 vs. 6.0, accounting for Friday 6.5): ~$40K–80K/year.
- Total cost: ~$100–140K/year.
- **Benefit:** Prevents backlog accumulation (WIP storage/aging cost, customer dissatisfaction, SLA breach fines). If SLA breach costs >$100K/year, the investment is justified.

## 7.5 Data collection improvements

1. **Opening backlog per day:** Log the count of cases queued at 07:00 (currently unknown, complicating Little's-Law application and backlog tracking).
2. **Event-level logs:** Capture case arrival, service start, service end, and departure timestamps (currently only daily aggregates available). This enables PM4Py-based analysis (variant mining, bottleneck detection, cycle-time variance).
3. **Case-mix classification at intake:** Standardize and log "simple" vs. "complex" case definition to validate whether the 20%  44% shift is real demand change or reclassification artifact.
4. **Service-time decomposition:** Record which portion of service time is "active handling" (currently median 24–34 min) vs. "system wait" (queue hold, batch processing). If service-time median includes queue, estimates of queue wait are invalid.
5. **Hourly WIP snapshots per service type:** Log WIP for simple and complex cases separately to assess whether high-complex cases are the bottleneck.

# Summary

This analysis demonstrates that **a single pooled queueing model would be dangerously misleading**:

1. **Fano factor 2.35 >> 1** confirms over-dispersion due to regime switching and weekly cycle; any Poisson-based M/M/c model would underestimate tail probabilities and SLA breach risk.

2. **Four distinct regimes** (baseline, complex-overload, post-intervention, outage/recovery) have incommensurable characteristics. Pooling conflates demand shifts, backlog transients, and capacity interventions.

3. **Weekday/weekend structure** is fundamentally different (2.5× demand reduction, but comparable WIP/FTE pressure when case mix is considered). Weekly aggregation is insufficient.

4. **Simpson's paradox reversal** in arrivals-vs.-completions (deficit pre-intervention, surplus post-intervention) is invisible in pooled data. A manager using pooled "balanced" statistics would incorrectly roll back the staffing increase.

5. **Intraday burstiness** (CV = 1.06 in overload, peak 3.55× mean) violates M/M assumptions. Real system requires intraday staffing flex and queue-length control.

6. **Little's Law is defensible only for baseline weekday** (stable, balanced, ~2.4 hr flow time). Outage/recovery and transient overload regimes require special handling (backlog aging/clearance, not steady-state queueing).

7. **Data improvements** (opening backlog, event logs, variant-level service times) are essential for higher-confidence analysis. The current daily aggregates mask causal mechanisms.

The segmented analysis reveals that **case-mix shift (20%  44% complex) was the root cause of backlog**, effectively solved by **staffing increase from 6.0 to 7.0 FTE**. Pooled summaries would recommend no action—a critical operational error.
