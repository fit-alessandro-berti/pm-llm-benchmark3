# 1. Objectives and constraints

## 1.1 Optimization objectives (multi-objective)

The policy is explicitly **multi-objective**, not “throughput only”. For each decision batch we aim to:

- **Meet SLAs while avoiding last‑minute firefighting**
  - Minimize the probability and severity of SLA breaches, especially for *urgent* and *high* priority cases.
  - Use SLA slack on *normal/low* priority cases to create room for training and fairness.
- **Control error and rework risk**
  - Prefer better skill matches and appropriate supervision to avoid rework and appeals, especially in Security Review, Final Approval, and high‑complexity Rework.
  - Limit harmful reassignments and handovers that historically increase rework and appeal rates.
- **Balance workload and avoid burnout**
  - Keep **open cases** and **hours remaining** per resource in a reasonable band.
  - Avoid adding load to high overtime‑risk resources unless SLA risk is severe and alternatives are exhausted.
- **Preserve continuity and customer relationship**
  - Prefer keeping the current owner when complexity and handover cost are high, provided the case is safe from a skill and SLA viewpoint.
- **Support structured skill development**
  - Ensure that at least **20% of normal/low‑priority assignments** explicitly support training priorities (diagnosis, quality, negotiation, approval, security).
  - Use supervised pairs (trainee + supervisor) instead of unsupervised trainee assignments for risky work.
- **Increase resilience and avoid bottlenecks**
  - Avoid single points of failure (e.g., all Security Reviews going to one person).
  - Maintain cross‑coverage within roles/teams so the system is robust to absences.

These objectives are expressed as **scores** (0–1) per dimension and combined with weights that depend on case priority (e.g., SLA and skill dominate for urgent work; training and fairness matter more for normal/low work).

## 1.2 Hard safety and compliance constraints

These are strict constraints; the algorithm may not violate them:

- **C1 – Skill eligibility and legal rules**
  - Activities with `required_skill = Security`:
    - Must be performed by **Security skill level 3**, or
    - By skill level **1–2 only under explicit supervision** by `R7_Gita` or `R8_Hugo`.
  - Activities with `required_skill = Approval` (Final Approval):
    - Must be performed (signed off) by **Approval skill level 2–3**.
    - Skill level 1 may assist/prepare but not finalize.
  - Any resource with **skill level 0** for the required skill is **not eligible** as the performing resource.
- **C2 – Priority vs trainee constraint**
  - No **high‑priority** (urgent/high) case may be assigned to **skill < 2** unless a qualified supervisor (skill ≥ 2) is explicitly assigned as well.
- **C3 – Batch fairness cap**
  - In a decision batch, **no resource receives more than 4 new assignments** (including supervised or trainee roles).
- **C4 – Overtime‑risk protection**
  - For resources with `Overtime risk = "high"` (R2_Bruno, R4_David, R9_Iris):
    - Do **not** assign new work unless:
      - The case is urgent/high *and*
      - There is no safer eligible alternative.
- **C5 – Continuity for high‑complexity, high‑handover‑cost cases**
  - For cases with **high complexity** and **high handover cost**:
    - Prefer to keep the **current owner** unless:
      - The owner’s skill is **0 or 1** for the required skill, *and*
      - Adequate supervision or reassignment is necessary to avoid SLA or quality risk.
- **C6 – Quality Check batching rule**
  - `Quality Check` may be batched only when:
    - Priority is **normal/low**, and
    - `SLA time remaining > 24h`.
  - Batching itself is optional; if done, it must respect this rule.
- **C7 – Respect of SLA feasibility**
  - Never assign a case to a resource who clearly cannot execute it within their remaining hours when SLA slack and typical service time are considered.

## 1.3 Soft constraints and trade-off preferences

When multiple eligible assignments satisfy all hard constraints, we use preferences:

- **P1 – SLA urgency vs other goals**
  - For **urgent/high** cases:
    - SLA risk and skill match are weighted highest.
    - Training and fairness are secondary (but still considered).
  - For **normal/low** cases:
    - SLA risk is still considered but with a lower weight.
    - Training, fairness, and resilience receive higher weight.
- **P2 – Skill match vs continuity**
  - Prefer **higher skill** if the SLA is tight or the activity is error‑sensitive (Security Review, Final Approval, high‑complexity Rework).
  - Prefer **continuity** if handover cost is high and skill is at least competent (≥2), possibly augmenting with supervision instead of full reassignment.
- **P3 – Workload fairness vs local efficiency**
  - Prefer assignments that **smooth workload** (open cases and hours remaining) across resources and teams over purely local efficiency.
  - Accept slightly sub‑optimal local throughput to avoid chronic overload of a few people.
- **P4 – Training vs SLA/quality**
  - Use **normal/low priority cases with good SLA slack** as training opportunities, especially when they align with declared training priorities.
  - Training is reduced or disabled when SLA is tight or the activity is high‑risk.
- **P5 – Resilience vs specialist bottlenecks**
  - Avoid routing all cases of a given skill to a single specialist when there are other capable resources.
  - Use supervision and cross‑training to create **backup coverage** for each critical skill (e.g., Security, Approval, Rework).

The policy is therefore **explainable** as a set of rules and weighted preferences rather than a black‑box optimizer.


# 2. Assignment policy

Below is an explainable, rule‑based scoring policy suitable for implementation and process‑mining audit.

## 2.1 Preparation and inputs

For each decision batch:

1. Take the queue snapshot of activities awaiting assignment.
2. For each case, retrieve from the event log:
   - `case:concept:name`, `concept:name` (activity), `required_skill`, `case_priority`,
     `sla_deadline` or `SLA time remaining`, `case_complexity`, `current_owner`,
     `reassignment_count`, `handover_cost_band`, `team`, `workload_snapshot`.
3. For each resource, maintain a state:
   - Skills by activity: Eligibility, Diagnosis, Negotiation, Security, Approval, Quality, Rework.
   - `Current open cases`, `Hours remaining today`, `Team`,
     `Training priority`, `Overtime risk`, `new_assignments_in_batch`.

## 2.2 Eligibility filtering

For each case `c` and each resource `r`, build the **candidate set** `Cand(c)` as follows:

1. **Skill zero check**
   - If `skill_r(required_skill(c)) == 0`, **exclude** `r`.
2. **Activity‑specific rules**
   - If `required_skill(c) == "Security"`:
     - Include `r` only if:
       - `skill_r(Security) == 3`, or
       - `skill_r(Security) in {1, 2}` **and** `r` will be supervised by `R7_Gita` or `R8_Hugo`.
   - If `required_skill(c) == "Approval"` and activity is `Final Approval`:
     - Include `r` only if `skill_r(Approval) in {2, 3}` as the *sign‑off* owner.
3. **Priority vs trainee**
   - If `case_priority(c) in {high, urgent}` and `skill_r(required_skill(c)) < 2`:
     - Exclude `r` unless a supervisor with skill ≥ 2 is assigned as a pair.
4. **Overtime risk**
   - If `Overtime risk(r) == "high"`:
     - Exclude `r` unless `case_priority(c)` is urgent/high **and** all safer alternatives are excluded by the above rules.
5. **Batch fairness**
   - If `new_assignments_in_batch(r) ≥ 4`, exclude `r`.
6. **Feasible capacity**
   - If, given typical service time for this activity, remaining hours and current workload make SLA infeasible for `r`, exclude `r`.

If `Cand(c)` is empty, the policy emits an **“exception required”** flag and routes the case to a human dispatcher for override; the override is logged for later analysis.

## 2.3 Scoring dimensions

For each candidate `r ∈ Cand(c)` we compute scores in [0,1]:

1. **SLA urgency score `SLA(c)`**
   - Compute `sla_slack_hours = SLA_remaining(c)` and priority bucket:
     - `urgent`: base = 1.0
     - `high`: base = 0.8
     - `normal`: base = 0.5
     - `low`: base = 0.3
   - Increase the score if `sla_slack_hours` is small (e.g., <4h) and decrease it if large.
2. **Skill‑match score `Skill(c,r)`**
   - Map skill levels to numeric scores per activity:
     - Level 1 (trainee): 0.3
     - Level 2 (competent): 0.7
     - Level 3 (specialist): 1.0
   - If `r` is acting as **supervisor** in a pair, use the supervisor’s skill score.
3. **Continuity and handover score `Cont(c,r)`**
   - Start from:
     - If `r == current_owner(c)`: base continuity = 1.0
     - Else: base continuity = 0.4
   - Adjust for:
     - `handover_cost_band` (high, medium, low),
     - `case_complexity` (high complexity amplifies handover cost),
     - `reassignment_count` (≥2 increases penalty for new handovers).
   - E.g., if `handover_cost_band == "high"` and `case_complexity > 0.7`, then:
     - If `r == current_owner`: `Cont(c,r) = 1.0`
     - Else: `Cont(c,r) = 0.1` (strong penalty).
4. **Workload and fairness score `Load(c,r)`**
   - Combine:
     - Normalized **open cases** (fewer is better),
     - Normalized **hours remaining** (more is better),
     - Overtime risk (penalty for medium, strong penalty for high).
   - Example:
     - `Load(c,r) = 0.5 * (1 - norm_open_cases(r)) + 0.5 * norm_hours_remaining(r)`
     - Then subtract 0.2 if overtime risk = medium.
5. **Training and development score `Train(c,r)`**
   - If `case_priority` is **normal/low**, SLA slack is comfortable, and
     `required_skill(c)` matches `Training priority(r)`:
     - Give a strong positive score (e.g., 1.0).
   - If case is urgent/high or SLA slack small:
     - `Train(c,r) = 0` (training turned off).
   - For supervised trainee + supervisor pairs, apply `Train` to trainee, and ensure `Skill` and `SLA` are still acceptable at pair level.
6. **Resilience / bottleneck score `Res(c,r)`**
   - Using historical data, estimate how concentrated the skill is on this resource:
     - If `r` already handles disproportionately many cases of this skill, slightly penalize `Res`.
     - If assigning to `r` improves backup coverage (e.g., cross‑team resource learning a new skill), increase `Res` when SLA slack allows.

## 2.4 Priority‑dependent weighting and selection

For each case `c`, we compute a **total score** for each candidate:

- For **urgent**:

```text
Score(c,r) =
  0.35 * SLA(c)
+ 0.30 * Skill(c,r)
+ 0.15 * Cont(c,r)
+ 0.10 * Load(c,r)
+ 0.05 * Res(c,r)
+ 0.05 * Train(c,r)
```

- For **high** (not urgent):

```text
Score(c,r) =
  0.30 * SLA(c)
+ 0.25 * Skill(c,r)
+ 0.15 * Cont(c,r)
+ 0.15 * Load(c,r)
+ 0.10 * Res(c,r)
+ 0.05 * Train(c,r)
```

- For **normal/low**:

```text
Score(c,r) =
  0.20 * SLA(c)
+ 0.20 * Skill(c,r)
+ 0.20 * Cont(c,r)
+ 0.20 * Load(c,r)
+ 0.10 * Res(c,r)
+ 0.10 * Train(c,r)
```

Then:

1. Choose the **single resource or supervised pair** `(r or (r_trainee, r_supervisor))` with the highest `Score(c,·)` that respects all hard constraints.
2. Increment `new_assignments_in_batch` for any resource involved (including supervisors/trainees).
3. Write a structured explanation into `assignment_reason` for audit, e.g.:
   - “Owner kept due to high continuity benefit under high handover cost; SLA slack sufficient; skill competent.”
   - “Reassigned from R4 to R3 due to high overtime risk and loop pattern; skill equal; slight continuity loss accepted.”

## 2.5 Tie-breaking and exception handling

When two or more candidates have similar scores (within a threshold, e.g., 0.02):

1. Prefer the **current owner** if handover cost is medium/high.
2. Otherwise prefer the resource that:
   - Has fewer open cases,
   - Is in the same team as current owner or case customer segment (for relationship continuity),
   - Provides better future resilience (e.g., broadening coverage).
3. If still tied, break ties randomly but log the tie in `assignment_reason` for later analysis.

If no eligible candidate exists due to strict rules (e.g., all qualified Security specialists unavailable), mark the case for **manual escalation** and log:

- Which rules prevented assignment,
- Which potential supervisors or cross‑training options might be needed future‑wise.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

## 4.1 Expected throughput

- **First‑available routing**
  - Likely achieves **low average waiting time** because cases go to whoever has fewest open cases and enough hours, ignoring specialization (except skill 0).
  - However, it may generate **longer service times** when skill mismatches occur (e.g., trainee handling complex Security or Rework without supervision).
- **Specialist‑only routing**
  - For each skill, the highest‑skill resource takes almost everything; this often **reduces rework and rework‑driven delays** for that skill.
  - But specialists quickly become **bottlenecks**, increasing waiting time and queue length, especially for Security and Approval.
- **Proposed dynamic policy**
  - Sits **between the two**:
    - Uses skill and SLA scoring to avoid severe mismatches, improving average service time compared with first‑available.
    - Uses workload and resilience scoring to avoid overloading specialists, reducing queues compared with strict specialist‑only.
  - Overall throughput is expected to be **moderate-to-good**, with fewer extremes (not minimum average waiting time at all costs, but more stable).

## 4.2 SLA risk

- **First‑available**
  - Treats all activities mostly equally by workload; some urgent/high cases may wait behind non‑urgent work if assigned to a busy but “first available” resource.
  - SLA breaches may increase when urgent tasks land on trainees or on resources with little relevant experience.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - SLA adherence is good **when specialists are not saturated**, but:
    - SLA risk spikes as soon as a specialist’s queue grows (e.g., many Security Reviews waiting for R7/R8).
    - Absence of a specialist (e.g., vacation) hits SLA performance hard because there is little backup.
- **Dynamic policy**
  - Explicitly scores **SLA urgency** and **feasible service time**, so urgent/high cases (C001, C002, C005, C007, C008, C011, C016) are directed to strong skill owners first.
  - Uses training mainly on normal/low cases with slack, thereby **protecting SLA performance**.
  - SLA risk becomes more predictable and manageable, with spikes primarily driven by external surges rather than routing policy.

## 4.3 Error and rework risk

- **First‑available**
  - High risk: ignores skills beyond skill 0 exclusion, increasing mismatches and unassisted trainee work on complex cases.
  - More reassignments occur when early mistakes are discovered, increasing the >2 reassignments pattern associated with high rework and appeals.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - Low error risk for individual steps because specialists handle everything; short‑term rework and appeals decline.
  - However, pushing all complex work to a few people can increase **fatigue‑driven errors**.
- **Dynamic policy**
  - Reduces error risk by:
    - Enforcing skill thresholds and supervision for high‑priority or risky activities.
    - Considering reassignment history and handover cost to avoid excessive owner changes.
  - The policy consciously accepts **some additional coordination complexity** (supervised pairs) to keep error risk lowwhile retaining training and resilience.

## 4.4 Fairness of workload

- **First‑available**
  - Appears fair because it looks at “fewest open cases”, but:
    - Ignores overtime risk and case complexity (a person with fewer but very complex cases may still be overloaded).
    - Does not cap new assignments per batch.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - Often **unfair** in practice:
    - Specialists accumulate heavy, complex workloads; others mainly handle simple tasks.
    - Overtime risk rises for key specialists (as observed for some resources).
- **Dynamic policy**
  - Explicitly:
    - Uses workload and overtime risk in scoring.
    - Caps new assignments per batch.
    - Avoids high‑overtime resources unless SLA forces it.
  - As a result, it should achieve **more balanced and sustainable workloads** across the team.

## 4.5 Skill development

- **First‑available**
  - Provides random exposure; some people incidentally receive training, but it is **not aligned with declared training priorities** and can be unsafe.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - Maximizes deep specialization for a few, but **starves others of learning opportunities**, hurting long‑term resilience.
- **Dynamic policy**
  - Embeds **structured training**:
    - At least 20% of normal/low‑priority assignments are configured to support declared training priorities.
    - Uses supervised pairs so trainees gain experience without jeopardizing quality.
  - Over time, this should **grow the pool of competent and specialist resources**, enabling more flexible routing in the future.

## 4.6 Continuity of case ownership

- **First‑available**
  - Does not explicitly consider continuity; cases often jump owners when queue conditions change.
  - This can produce the >2 reassignment patterns associated with higher rework and appeals.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - Preserves continuity **only when** the case starts with a specialist; otherwise, it induces a discontinuity when the case is moved to a specialist.
- **Dynamic policy**
  - Explicitly scores continuity and penalizes handovers, especially for high-complexity, high handover‑cost cases.
  - Keeps owners where safe (e.g., C007, C016) and only reassigns when SLA, skill, or overtime constraints justify it (e.g., C003, C014).

## 4.7 Resilience to absence and bottlenecks

- **First‑available**
  - Some natural cross‑training occurs, but with no guarantees:
    - Some skills remain underdeveloped because random routing may rarely give them to certain people.
  - Sudden absence of a specialist still creates gaps if no one else has gained enough experience.
- **Specialist‑only**
  - **Low resilience**:
    - If a key specialist is absent, there is often no trained backup.
    - Large queues build up for that skill.
- **Dynamic policy**
  - By tracking resource roles and similarity and deliberately creating **supervised training assignments**, it:
    - Builds backup coverage for Security, Approval, and Rework.
    - Spreads knowledge across teams (A, B, Risk, Quality).
  - Over time, this makes the operation **more robust** to absences and demand spikes.


# 5. Explanation to workers

Below is a worker‑facing explanation that avoids technical jargon:

> We are introducing a routing approach that tries to balance our commitments to customers with fairness and learning opportunities for everyone. When a new task appears in the queue, we first check who is allowed to do it, based on skill and compliance rules (for example, only qualified people can sign off security checks and final approvals).
>
> Among the people who are allowed, the system looks at several factors at once: how urgent the case is, how close it is to its deadline, who handled it before, how complex it is, and how busy each person is. For urgent or risky work, the main focus is on getting it to someone with the right skills quickly, even if that means less training in that moment. For routine work with more time, the system gives more weight to fairness and training, so that people can grow into new skills over time.
>
> The system also tries to keep ownership stable for complex cases that are expensive to hand over. If you already own such a case and have the right skills, you are likely to keep it. On the other hand, if you are at high risk of overtime or already carrying too many difficult cases, some new work will be routed to others, even if you could technically do it. This is meant to protect you from burnout, not to punish performance.
>
> For training, some lower‑risk tasks are intentionally given to colleagues who are developing specific skills (for example, diagnosis, quality checks, security, or approvals). In those cases, you may see assignments listed as “under supervision”. This means the trainee does the work with a named supervisor available to support and review. These supervised assignments are tracked so that we can show how training is progressing.
>
> Every assignment decision is logged with a short reason (such as “urgent and needs specialist”, “training opportunity with supervision”, or “owner kept due to high handover cost”). This makes the policy auditable and allows us to adjust the rules when we see that something is unfair or not working well in practice. Feedback from you is important: if the assignments don’t feel right, we can check the data and refine the rules together.


# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

To audit this policy using event logs, we define the following indicators over time windows (e.g., weekly, monthly), using columns such as `org:resource`, `required_skill`, `case_priority`, `reassignment_count`, `handover_cost_band`, `case_complexity`, `workload_snapshot`, `overtime risk`, and `assignment_reason`.

## 6.1 Handover frequency

- **Definition**: Average and distribution of the number of **owner changes per case**.
- **Computation**:
  - For each case, count transitions where `current_owner` or `org:resource` changes across events.
  - Break down by activity, priority, and handover cost band.
- **Target**: Lower handover counts for high‑complexity, high‑handover‑cost cases, without excessively freezing ownership for simple ones.

## 6.2 Reassignment count

- **Definition**: Distribution of `reassignment_count` per case and proportion of cases with **>2 reassignments**.
- **Computation**:
  - Use the existing `reassignment_count` field or compute increments when owner changes.
- **Target**: Reduce the share of cases with >2 reassignments, especially in high‑complexity segments, and correlate this with improved rework/appeal outcomes.

## 6.3 Skill mismatch

- **Definition**: Frequency of assignments where the performing resource’s skill level for `required_skill` is:
  - Below 2 for high‑priority work, and/or
  - Below the policy’s recommended level (e.g., trainee without supervision).
- **Computation**:
  - Join event log with the skill matrix by `org:resource` and activity.
  - Mark events violating the trainee + supervision rules or Approval/Security rules.
- **Target**: Very low or zero mismatches for high‑priority and regulated steps; controlled, supervised trainee involvement for normal/low work.

## 6.4 Workload distribution

- **Definition**: Distribution of **open cases** and **handled activities** per resource, adjusted for complexity and hours worked.
- **Computation**:
  - From `workload_snapshot` and events per resource, compute:
    - Average open cases,
    - Standard deviation across resources,
    - Share of complex/high‑priority cases per person.
- **Target**: Limited spread between the most and least loaded resources, and no persistent overloading of specific individuals.

## 6.5 Overtime risk exposure

- **Definition**: Number and share of assignments made to **high overtime‑risk** resources, split by priority bucket (urgent/high vs normal/low).
- **Computation**:
  - Filter events by resources with `Overtime risk = "high"`.
  - Count assignments for each case priority category.
- **Target**: Very few normal/low‑priority assignments to high‑risk resources; urgent/high assignments only when necessary.

## 6.6 SLA misses

- **Definition**: Rate of **SLA violations** per priority level and per activity.
- **Computation**:
  - Use SLA information and timestamps to mark cases that finish after their `sla_deadline`.
  - Break down by routing policy period (before/after deployment), case priority, and skill category.
- **Target**: Reduced SLA miss rate compared with pre‑policy behavior, especially for urgent/high cases, without substantial increases for lower priorities.

## 6.7 Rework and appeals after assignment

- **Definition**: Frequency of **rework activities** and **appeals** following specific assignments or patterns (e.g., trainee involvement, >2 reassignments).
- **Computation**:
  - Identify Rework Correction and appeal‑related activities in `concept:name`.
  - Correlate with previous assignment history, skill levels, and presence of supervision.
- **Target**: Lower rework/appeal rates for cases routed under the new policy, especially in high‑risk activities like Security Review, Diagnosis, and Final Approval.

## 6.8 Training assignment share

- **Definition**: Share of **normal/low-priority** assignments that explicitly support declared training priorities (`Training priority` field).
- **Computation**:
  - Use `assignment_reason` and `required_skill` to identify training‑labeled assignments.
  - Count them among normal/low-priority cases and evaluate their share (should be ≥20%).
- **Target**: Maintain or exceed the 20% training target without harming SLA or quality indicators.

## 6.9 Specialist bottleneck indicators

- **Definition**: Measures of whether a small number of specialists carry a disproportionate share of work for certain skills.
- **Computation**:
  - For each `required_skill`, compute the Gini coefficient or top‑k share of cases handled per resource.
  - Combine with waiting times and queue lengths for activities like Security Review and Final Approval.
- **Target**: Over time, reduce concentration (e.g., Security not handled almost exclusively by one person) while keeping quality and SLA stable.

## 6.10 Deviations from assignment policy

- **Definition**: Cases where the **actual assignment** conflicts with what the policy would recommend given the same inputs.
- **Computation**:
  - Reconstruct decisions offline: for each historical assignment, recompute candidate scores with the current rule set.
  - Flag events where the chosen resource is far from the policy’s top candidate.
  - Inspect `assignment_reason` for documented manual overrides.
- **Target**: Low but non-zero deviation rate, reflecting justified human overrides rather than systematic bypass of the policy.

## 7.7 Using the policy for “replay” and deviation detection

To audit adherence to the policy, we can **replay** historical decisions:

1. For each assignment event in the log, reconstruct:
   - Case attributes at that time (priority, SLA slack, complexity, owner, etc.).
   - Resource states (`open cases`, `hours remaining`, `new_assignments_in_batch` approximation).
2. Apply the **same eligibility and scoring rules** as in Section 2 to compute what the policy would have recommended.
3. Compare the **actual assigned resource** with the top‑scoring candidate(s).
