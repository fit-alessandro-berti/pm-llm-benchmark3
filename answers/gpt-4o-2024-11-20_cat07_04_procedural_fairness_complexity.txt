# 1. Fairness dimensions  

### Outcome parity  
Outcome parity is not achieved because Group A has a significantly higher approval rate (78%) compared to Groups B (63%), C (69%), and D (58%). However, this disparity cannot be interpreted as discrimination without considering case mix and process complexity.

### Adjusted outcome disparity  
When controlling for relevant factors (e.g., risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, etc.), Groups B, C, and D still experience lower approval probabilities compared to Group A (-5.5, -2.2, and -3.1 percentage points, respectively). While this adjusted disparity is smaller, it suggests that complexity alone does not explain all disparities.

### Procedural burden  
Applicants in Groups B, C, and D face additional procedural burdens, including more frequent supervisor reviews, document requests, and handovers. These burdens are exacerbated by the requirements for language support (Group B), accommodation review (Group C), and fraud review (Group D). Even if these steps are rights-preserving, they add delays and complexity that disproportionately impact these groups.

### Timeliness  
Cycle times for Groups B (13.7 days), C (15.9 days), and D (12.1 days) are significantly longer than for Group A (8.1 days). This delay is partly linked to bottlenecks in rights-preserving steps (e.g., bilingual staff shortage, specialized queues for accommodations). Despite controlling for case mix, timeliness disparities persist with Group B having the largest adjusted difference (+2.6 days).

### Consistency of routing  
Group-specific routing patterns influence fairness. For example, Group B cases often involve multiple handovers (e.g., general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker), while Group C cases involve accommodation specialists and supervisors. These routing inconsistencies may reflect resource limitations and policy requirements but create procedural complexity and variability.

### Quality/accessibility of support  
Rights-preserving steps (e.g., language support, accommodation review) are crucial for accessibility but are constrained by limited resources (e.g., only seven bilingual workers, delays in specialized queues). These constraints undermine the timeliness and fairness of access to support.

### Appeal/rework burden  
Groups B (13%), C (10%), and D (16%) face substantially higher appeal rates than Group A (6%). While appeal rates might indicate effective mechanisms for review and correction, they also signal dissatisfaction or procedural errors. Rework rates follow a similar pattern, exacerbating the burden on these groups.

### Resource-allocation fairness  
Resource allocation is uneven, with Group B concentrated among overloaded bilingual workers and Group C cases routed to understaffed accommodation specialists. This uneven distribution contributes to longer delays and higher procedural burdens for these groups.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy  

### Matching or stratification variables  
Control for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. This ensures comparisons are made between cases with similar observed complexity factors.

### Path/variant comparison  
Analyze the top process variants (V1-V6) to determine whether specific paths (e.g., rights-preserving steps) disproportionately impact certain groups. Compare adjusted cycle times and frequencies across groups for each variant.

### Resource-assignment comparison  
Examine whether resource availability systematically disadvantages certain groups. Compare caseworker workloads, handover rates, and waiting times for critical steps (e.g., language support, accommodation review) across groups.

### Waiting-time decomposition  
Decompose delays into their sources (e.g., submission-to-verification, waiting for specialized queues). Assess whether observed delays are proportional to case complexity or reflect resource constraints.

### Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates  
Evaluate whether documentation_completeness or risk_score_band exhibit bias due to historical inequities (e.g., language barriers affecting documentation). Re-run models excluding or adjusting these variables to determine their influence on adjusted disparities.

### Subgroup checks by region and channel  
Disaggregate findings by region and channel (e.g., online vs. in-person submissions), as local resource constraints and routing policies may impact fairness differently across subgroups.

# 3. Path and resource disparities  

### Important procedural disparities  
1. Group B disproportionately follows Variant V4 (24%), involving language support and frequent document requests, with higher waiting times (e.g., 3.8 days for language support) due to limited bilingual staff.  
2. Group C cases heavily follow Variant V5 (31%), involving accommodation reviews with a median cycle time of 21.6 days and a 4.7-day delay before eligibility assessment.  
3. Group D cases frequently follow Variant V3 (28%), involving fraud reviews and supervisor routing, with longer delays at Risk Screening  Fraud Review (2.8 days).  

### Justification vs. concern  
- **Justified disparities**: Routing to rights-preserving steps (e.g., language support, fraud review) aligns with policy objectives based on applicant needs.  
- **Concerning disparities**: Long delays and handover rates in specialized queues suggest resource insufficiencies, creating undue procedural burdens for Groups B and C. Appeal and rework burdens for Groups B and D suggest potential issues with initial decisions and explanations.

---

# 4. Plausible mechanisms  

### Legitimate mechanisms  
1. **Higher complexity**: Groups B, C, and D present higher proportions of incomplete documentation, risk_score_band, and other complexity factors that necessitate additional review steps.  
2. **Rights-preserving steps**: Language support and accommodation reviews are necessary for accessibility and equity.  

### Concerning mechanisms  
3. **Resource constraints**: Limited bilingual staff and accommodation specialists create bottlenecks, resulting in slower processing for Groups B and C.  
4. **Bias in risk_score_band**: Historical disparities or subjective assessment factors could inflate risk scores for Group D, driving procedural inequities.  

---

# 5. Limitations of available attributes  

1. **Measurement bias in risk_score_band**: Risk scores may encode pre-existing biases, disadvantaging certain groups irrespective of current risk factors.  
2. **Missing applicant needs**: We lack data on unobserved needs (e.g., digital literacy, implicit biases during interactions) that may affect outcomes.  
3. **Unobserved resource workload**: Daily-level resource data are insufficient to capture intra-day workload fluctuations that impact processing times.  
4. **Appeal quality**: Case_outcome provides no information on whether decisions were adequately explained or perceived as fair.  
5. **Language/completeness interaction**: Documentation_completeness may itself reflect systemic language barriers, affecting observed disparities.  

---

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects  

1. **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review**:  
   - **Benefit**: Reduces bottlenecks and delays for Groups B and C.  
   - **Side effect**: Requires funding and recruitment, potentially delaying implementation.  

2. **Service-level targets for support steps**:  
   - **Benefit**: Ensures timeliness even for rights-preserving steps.  
   - **Side effect**: Could unintentionally pressure staff to rush complex cases.  

3. **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**:  
   - **Benefit**: Minimizes procedural complexity and delays for Groups B and C.  
   - **Side effect**: Risks overburdening individual caseworkers without increasing capacity.  

4. **Decision-explanation improvements**:  
   - **Benefit**: Reduces appeal rates by providing clearer and fairer explanations.  
   - **Side effect**: Increases workload for decision drafters.  

5. **Monitoring adjusted disparities**:  
   - **Benefit**: Identifies residual inequities after controlling for complexity.  
   - **Side effect**: Requires ongoing data collection and analysis.  

6. **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**:  
   - **Benefit**: Detects and mitigates potential biases in these inputs.  
   - **Side effect**: Could require recalibrating historical data for consistency.
