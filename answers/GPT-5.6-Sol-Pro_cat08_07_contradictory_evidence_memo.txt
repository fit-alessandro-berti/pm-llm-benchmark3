# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Steering Committee
**Subject:** Next decision on Fiber Installation Redesign R

**Decision requested:** Adopt **Alternative E: a segment-gated hybrid**, using controlled expansion rather than national scaling or full rollback. Keep the redesign for clearly identified simple, low-risk houses; retain the coordinator-ownership concept only where capacity and absence coverage are adequate; restore surveys, permit checks, and activation-before-billing controls for higher-risk segments; and run a pre-registered experiment before any broader rollout.

The pilot demonstrates a real but narrow benefit. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%, and simple low-risk houses improved on median, p90, first-time-right installation, and complaints. Those gains are commercially meaningful and argue against wholesale rollback.

The same evidence rejects national scaling of R in its current form. The p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and the p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days; first-time-right fell six percentage points; complaints rose 3.7 points; manual activation and second visits increased sharply; and SLA breach worsened overall. Harm is concentrated in apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and rural installations. Coordinator utilization reached 93% in two regions, while second-visit utilization reached 92%, indicating that work was not eliminated so much as shifted from early validation and survey activity into exception queues.

The process-mining evidence supports this interpretation without proving a single cause. Pilot fitness is high because the discovered model can replay the dominant simplified coordinator path. Its low precision and lower generalization show that it also permits many combinations of survey skipping, manual activation, repeat visits, and billing start that were not tightly controlled. Alignments and temporal profiles identify the operationally important deviations: billing before successful activation, missing permit checks, repeat visits without surveys, slower failed-visit recovery, and a new long-tail path from billing to complaint escalation. The simpler handover network therefore reflects less transfer, but the more centralized working-together network exposes bottleneck and absence risk.

The economic case is unresolved. Visible pre-complaint cost fell by EUR 23, but the estimated full cost rose by EUR 9, and downstream complaint cost is missing after pilot week 4. Because start timestamps are sparse, the log also cannot yet distinguish active work from waiting in activation and second-visit queues. Pilot regions contained more apartments and older buildings, and key risk attributes were incomplete before week 3, so exact aggregate effect sizes are confounded. These limitations justify measurement—not ignoring the observed quality and sequencing failures.

Management should act now on what is sufficiently clear. Permit-sensitive buildings should not skip permit or survey controls. Billing should normally follow a successful activation test; any exception should require an explicit reason, accountable owner, and rapid cure path. Coordinator ownership should have a named deputy, shared case context, and a utilization ceiling rather than functioning as a single point of failure.

Broader rollout should be conditional on segment-specific evidence, not one aggregate average. The next decision should be based on validated risk classification, randomized evidence on survey skipping, complete 60-day downstream cost, and live conformance controls. This preserves the demonstrated speed benefit while containing the tail, rework, customer, and compliance-like risks that make the current full redesign unsuitable for national deployment.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### High fitness but low precision

Fitness asks whether the discovered model can reproduce observed traces. Precision asks whether it excludes behavior that was not observed. A permissive model can therefore have both high fitness and low precision.

The pilot has a frequent simplified coordinator path, so replay is easy. At the same time, the observed combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeated visits, early billing, and complaint escalation lead the discovered model to allow many alternative combinations. That raises fitness while lowering precision. The lower generalization score further warns that the structure learned from six pilot weeks may not behave reliably on new regions or segments.

High fitness is consequently not evidence that R is compliant, controlled, or effective. The alignments show important observed behavior that conflicts with expected sequencing despite the high aggregate fitness.

### Improved median but worse tail duration

The pilot is a mixture of fundamentally different treatment effects:

* Simple low-risk houses, representing 34% of the pilot, improved by six days at the median and eight days at p90.
* Apartments and old buildings received smaller median benefits or deterioration but much larger p90 penalties.
* Manual activation, failed visits, second visits, and complaint escalation became more frequent.

The common simple path pulls down the median. The exception-heavy segments determine p90 and p95. Near-saturation at the coordinator and second-visit queues can amplify this effect: once an installation fails, recovery waiting grows nonlinearly. Thus R can make a majority of uncomplicated work faster while making difficult cases much slower.

The pilot mix means the exact aggregate causal effect remains uncertain, but composition alone does not explain away the adverse within-segment results for apartments and old buildings.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience

A handover count measures transfers, not redundancy, shared knowledge, or replaceability. A single coordinator can reduce transfers and clarify nominal ownership. However, at 93% utilization, that person becomes a bottleneck. When the coordinator is absent and context has not been shared, another worker cannot readily continue the case.

The network evidence captures both sides:

* The handover-of-work network became simpler.
* The working-together network became more centralized.
* Activation specialists became more central as manual exceptions increased.
* Cases stalled during coordinator absence.

R therefore improved continuity for normal work but weakened resilience to overload, absence, and complex exceptions.

### Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

The EUR 161 figure ends before the full economic consequences of installation. It excludes incomplete downstream complaint costs and may not fully capture revisits, billing disputes, manual activation effort, and customer-service recovery.

R also appears to move work across the measurement boundary. Less survey effort and fewer handovers reduce visible front-stage cost, while more failures and complaints create later costs. This is consistent with the estimated lifecycle comparison of EUR 207 for R versus EUR 198 at baseline, although that estimate is not yet complete enough for a definitive financial verdict.

### Improvement for houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

Merged validation and scheduling are well matched to predictable, low-risk work. In those cases, a survey may add delay without adding much information.

Apartments and older buildings have greater uncertainty about access, internal routing, shared infrastructure, permits, and signal conditions. For these cases:

* The survey-skip rule was often wrong.
* Expected permit checks were sometimes absent.
* Installation-to-activation performance deteriorated.
* Repeat visits and complaints increased.

R removed low-value steps for one segment but removed valuable risk-reduction steps for another. This is treatment heterogeneity, not a contradiction. It supports segment-gated design rather than an enterprise-wide average process.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E—a controlled, segment-gated hybrid—implemented with a decomposed test of R’s individual components.**

**Immediate action**

Continue R only for auditable **simple, low-risk houses**, using staged and reversible expansion. Reinstate the standard process for other segments while controlled evidence is collected. Establish live segment-level reporting rather than relying on a single aggregate median.

**What to pause**

* National rollout of the complete redesign.
* Survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old buildings, and other cases whose risk classification is incomplete or unvalidated.
* Any omission of an applicable permit check.
* Billing before a successful activation test as a standard operating path.
* Uncontrolled expansion of coordinator caseloads in regions already near saturation.

**What to scale**

* Combined validation and scheduling for verified simple, low-risk houses.
* Coordinator ownership for that segment, with a named deputy, shared case notes, load balancing, and escalation coverage.
* Coordinator ownership in other segments only as a controlled test separated from survey skipping and post-billing activation.
* Real-time conformance alerts for activation/billing order and permit-check completion.

**What to measure next**

Prioritize **M6 and M1** to decide where survey skipping is causally beneficial; **M4** to establish lifecycle economics; and **M7** to determine whether activation and permit controls are enforceable. Begin M3 and M2 immediately to diagnose exception causes and distinguish queue time from service time. Use M5 to decide whether coordinator ownership can scale without creating a single point of failure.

**Proposed gates for broader rollout**

Expansion beyond simple low-risk houses should require concurrent-control or randomized evidence, evaluated separately for every target segment, showing:

1. **Tail performance:** p90 cycle time no more than two days worse than control, with no material p95 deterioration.
2. **Reliability:** first-time-right, complaint rate, and SLA breach each non-inferior within a pre-agreed margin of one percentage point.
3. **Lifecycle economics:** 60-day total cost no more than 3% above control at the upper confidence bound, with a credible path to net savings.
4. **Control integrity:** at least 99% completion of applicable permit checks and at least 99% activation-before-billing compliance; all remaining exceptions must be reason-coded and reviewed.
5. **Capacity and resilience:** coordinator and second-visit utilization sustained at or below a proposed 85% ceiling, with no material case stalling during tested coordinator absence.
6. **Process stability:** problematic activation and permit alignments become rare and explainable on held-out logs; precision and generalization improve without merely adopting a more permissive model.

Failure on a control-integrity gate should stop expansion regardless of median speed. Failure on an economic or performance gate should keep the affected segment on the controlled baseline while preserving R for segments that continue to demonstrate net benefit.
