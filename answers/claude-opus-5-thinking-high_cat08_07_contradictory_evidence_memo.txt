# Redesign R: Decision Memo on Scaling, Rollback, or Staged Adoption

## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee
**From:** Process Intelligence & Operations Excellence
**Subject:** Redesign R — adopt a segmented rollout with hard guardrails; do not scale nationally, do not roll back

**Recommendation.** Choose **Alternative E**, implemented with the controls from Alternative C and the measurement discipline of Alternative D. Keep and extend the part of R that clearly works (single-coordinator ownership, merged validate/schedule for simple cases). Immediately suspend the two mechanisms that are producing the damage (predictive survey skipping and activation checks after billing start). Gate any further expansion on pre-agreed thresholds rather than on a second round of ambiguous averages.

**Why not scale.** R did not make the process better; it made it *faster on the easy path and worse on the failure path*. Median cycle time fell 3.4 days, but p90 rose 7.5 days and p95 rose 17 days. First-time-right fell 6 points, 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, second visits from 11% to 18%, and manual activations from 7% to 15%. The only cost figure that improved (EUR 184 to EUR 161) excludes exactly the work that R created; the fuller estimate (EUR 198 to EUR 207) is worse and is itself incomplete, because complaint-handling cost stops being counted after week 4. On present evidence the business case is at best unproven and more likely negative.

**Why not roll back.** The benefits are real and concentrated: simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume) improved on *every* dimension — median -6.0 days, p90 -8.0 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints -1pp — and cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. Rolling back would discard a genuine structural gain to avoid harm that is traceable to two separable design choices, not to coordinator ownership itself.

**What is actually broken.** Survey skipping transfers work from a cheap, scheduled activity to an expensive, unscheduled one: field-survey utilization fell from 78% to 52% while the second-visit team rose to 92%. The skip rule misfires precisely where buildings are hard (apartments -8pp first-time-right; old buildings -11pp and +25 days at p90). Moving activation after billing start created a new failure relation, Billing start -> Complaint escalation (3% to 8%), and alignments show billing occurring before a successful activation test. Missing permit checks on older apartment buildings is a compliance exposure, not merely a quality issue. Coordinator utilization of 93% removes the slack needed to absorb these exceptions, and single ownership removed the shared context that previously kept cases moving during absences.

**Immediate actions (this month).**
1. Stop billing start before a passed activation test or an explicit signed-off manual activation; run conformance alerts on every violation.
2. Restore mandatory permit checks for permit-sensitive and old-building cases; make it a blocking step.
3. Suspend the survey-skip rule for all non-simple segments; retain it only for simple, low-risk houses.
4. Cap coordinator load near 85% and pair each coordinator with a named backup holding case context.
5. Keep coordinator ownership everywhere; it is not the source of the harm.

**Measurement before wider scale.** Validate the risk rule against actual survey outcomes (M6), close the cost picture with 60-day downstream measurement (M4), add reason codes (M3), and run conformance monitoring (M7). Only if the rule proves discriminating should we invest in a randomized, stratified skip experiment (M1).

**The trade-off we are accepting.** This path forgoes roughly half of the headline speed gain in the short term and costs one to two quarters of measurement. In exchange we stop paying for speed with reliability, complaints, and billing-integrity risk in 47% of volume. We will not pretend a single design dominates: R is a good process for simple cases and a bad one for complex ones, and the correct answer is to route by risk rather than to pick one process for everything.

**Decision requested:** approve E with the guardrails above and the rollout thresholds in Section 6.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

The apparent contradictions are not measurement errors. Each pair of metrics answers a different question, and R moved those questions in opposite directions.

**High fitness with low precision.** Fitness asks "can the model replay what happened?"; precision asks "does the model forbid what never happens?". They are traded off, and a permissive model scores well on the first and badly on the second. R created many legitimate optional paths — survey performed or skipped, activation automatic or manual, billing before or after activation, one visit or two. Discovery algorithms encode optionality as free choice, so the resulting model can replay 94% of behaviour precisely because it allows combinations nobody executed. The rise in fitness is therefore *not* evidence that the process became more orderly; read together with precision 0.51 and generalization 0.56, it is evidence of the opposite: behavioural variety grew faster than the log could pin it down. Generalization falling alongside precision is the signature of a log with many thin, rarely repeated variants — the statistical fingerprint of an under-standardized process, and directly consistent with alignment diagnostics showing four distinct frequent deviation patterns.

**Improved median with worse tail.** The median is a statement about the typical case and is blind to the extremes; p90 and p95 are statements about the worst cases and are blind to the typical one. R shifted probability mass in both directions simultaneously. Merging validation and scheduling and removing handovers compressed the front end for the 34% simple-house population, pulling the median down. Skipped surveys, failed visits, second visits, and post-billing complaint escalations lengthened the 47% of volume in apartment and old-building segments, pushing the upper percentiles out. A single distribution can become both more peaked and more heavy-tailed; that is exactly what a "fast path plus fragile exception path" design produces. Note also that the tail metrics carry the customer and cost weight: a 17-day increase at p95 is felt as SLA breach (13% to 16%) and complaint escalation, whereas a 3.4-day median gain is largely invisible per case.

**Fewer handovers with lower resilience.** Handovers are simultaneously a cost and a control. Each handover imposes delay and coordination overhead, but it also forces context to be externalized into a record another person can pick up, and it distributes load across a pool of servers. Reducing 3+ handover cases from 34% to 12% removed the overhead and the redundancy together. The social-network evidence makes this concrete: the handover-of-work network simplified while the working-together network centralized on coordinators. A centralized network has shorter paths in normal operation and no alternative path under failure. At 93% utilization there is no slack to absorb variability, so queueing theory predicts sharply convex delay growth precisely in the high-utilization regions — which is what the temporal profile shows for Failed visit -> Second visit.

**Lower visible cost with higher rework and downstream cost.** The EUR 23 per-case saving is measured where the redesign removed activity (surveys not performed, handovers not made). The costs it created land outside that measurement boundary and later in time: second visits (11% to 18%), manual activations (7% to 15%), complaint handling (6.1% to 9.8%), and billing disputes from business customers. The estimated full cost already reverses the sign (EUR 198 to EUR 207) and is still truncated — complaint-handling cost is not counted after week 4, and complaints by construction arrive *after* billing start, so the excluded period contains the densest part of the cost. Any cost saving whose measurement window closes before the induced failures materialize will look favourable.

**Improvement for simple houses with deterioration for apartments and old buildings.** R replaced information-gathering steps (field survey, permit check) with a predictive rule. For simple low-risk houses the prediction is easy and the removed steps were genuinely redundant, so the saving is pure. For apartments and older buildings the removed steps were the mechanism that handled real physical and legal variability, and the rule is documented as "often wrong". The value of a control step scales with the uncertainty it resolves; applying one policy uniformly across segments with very different uncertainty guarantees a gain in one segment and a loss in the other. The aggregate numbers are then just a volume-weighted average of two opposite effects — which is why the correct response is segmentation, not a single verdict on R.

## 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally.**
*Advantages:* Fastest realization of the median speed gain and the handover reduction; single national operating model, so no routing logic, dual training, or dual tooling; the coordinator role becomes a clear accountability anchor.
*Risks:* Scales a design whose fuller cost estimate is already unfavourable (EUR 207 vs 198) and still incomplete; nationalizes the billing-before-activation practice, which is a compliance and revenue-assurance exposure, not just a quality defect; nationalizes missing permit checks on old buildings; the second-visit team is already at 92% utilization in the pilot and would break at scale; the pilot ran 6 weeks in four regions with attributes missing for the first third, which is thin evidence for an irreversible national change.
*Reasonable only if:* full 60-day cost came in clearly below baseline, the skip rule validated as accurate across strata, and the billing/permit deviations proved to be rare instrumentation artifacts. None of these conditions currently hold, so A is not defensible today.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process.**
*Advantages:* Immediately restores first-time-right, complaint rates, and the tail; removes compliance exposure; requires no new measurement investment; organizationally simple and reversible.
*Risks:* Discards a demonstrated, segment-specific gain — simple houses improved on every metric, and 3+ handover cases fell by two-thirds; destroys the learning and the coordinator capability just as the data needed to steer it becomes available; signals to the organization that redesign attempts are punished for partial failure; forgoes a real 6-day median improvement for a third of volume.
*Reasonable only if:* the harm were traceable to coordinator ownership itself rather than to two separable policies, or if regulatory pressure demanded an immediate return to a fully validated process. Neither applies: the harmful mechanisms are separable, so rollback is over-correction.

**C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
*Advantages:* Surgically removes the two mechanisms that the alignments, DFG edges, and segment table all implicate, while keeping the handover reduction that no evidence criticizes; restores permit and activation controls; likely recovers most of the tail and rework deterioration; conceptually simple to communicate.
*Risks:* Still applies one model to all segments, so it under-exploits the strong simple-house result and may under-protect complex cases that need more than ownership; does not address the 93% coordinator utilization or the absence-resilience problem, which persist under pure ownership consolidation; without new measurement, the organization stays blind to whether the residual design is net positive; abandoning the skip rule permanently forfeits its potential value where it is actually accurate.
*Reasonable if:* speed of risk reduction matters more than optimization, or if measurement capacity is unavailable. C is the right *safety core*, but as a standalone endpoint it stops learning.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement.**
*Advantages:* The only alternative that produces causal rather than correlational evidence; fixes the two structural data defects (missing attributes before week 3, sparse start timestamps) that currently make every conclusion contestable; supports a genuinely defensible national decision later.
*Risks:* If run without immediate guardrails, it continues to bill customers before activation and to skip permit checks for the duration of the experiment — ethically and legally unacceptable; delays realization of benefits that are already well supported for simple houses; experiments on a design known to be partly defective waste power on questions already answered.
*Reasonable if:* combined with immediate suspension of the harmful mechanisms, so the experiment tests only the genuinely open questions (skip-rule accuracy by stratum, ownership model under absence).

**E. Hybrid: deploy R for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments and old buildings, measure before wider scale.**
*Advantages:* Matches policy to the uncertainty each segment carries, which is what the segment table directly supports; captures the full benefit where it is proven (34% of volume, improvements on every metric) while restoring controls where harm is proven; preserves the coordinator role universally; converts the national decision into a staged, threshold-gated sequence rather than a single bet; naturally accommodates C's guardrails and D's measurement.
*Risks:* Requires reliable segment classification at intake — the very attribute that was incomplete in the pilot, so misrouting is a real failure mode and must be conservative (default to the controlled path when risk is unknown); creates two operating variants, raising training, tooling, and audit complexity; benefits realized more slowly than A; risks becoming a permanent two-speed process if the measurement programme is not enforced.
*Reasonable if:* segment classification can be made reliable at intake and management commits to the measurement gates. Both are achievable, which is why E is recommended.

**Ranking:** E > C > D > B > A. E and C differ mainly in whether we exploit the simple-house result and keep learning; D is a necessary component of E rather than a competing option; B is defensible only as a fallback if the guardrailed version still underperforms; A is not defensible on current evidence.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by decision value — the probability that the result changes the decision, weighted by the magnitude of the change, divided by cost and time to answer.

**1. M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** *Resolves:* whether survey skipping is a fixable targeting problem or a fundamentally unsound idea. *Why highest:* it is largely retrospective — pilot cases where the survey was skipped and a failed or second visit followed already provide labelled outcomes — so it is cheap and fast. It sits upstream of the largest single source of harm (apartments -8pp and old buildings -11pp first-time-right). *How it changes the decision:* if the rule discriminates well and only its threshold is miscalibrated, we recalibrate per stratum and proceed to M1. If discrimination is poor (near-random within medium-risk strata), we retire survey skipping permanently, which removes the main argument against broad rollout and makes A-with-controls plausible sooner. Either result is decision-changing; almost no result leaves us where we are.

**2. M4 — Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start.** *Resolves:* the sign of the business case. Today we hold two contradictory numbers (EUR 161 improved, EUR 207 worse) and the worse one is truncated after week 4, precisely when complaint costs peak. *How it changes the decision:* if guardrailed R lands below EUR 190 per case on a complete 60-day window, broad rollout becomes economically justified. If it stays above EUR 198, no amount of median improvement justifies scaling, and the case for B strengthens materially. This is the single number the executive committee will be held to, and we currently do not have it.

**3. M3 — Structured reason codes for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, Second visit.** *Resolves:* attribution. Right now we can see that failed visits rose from 13% to 19% but not whether the cause is a missing survey, coordinator overload, wrong appointment information, or customer unavailability. *Why high:* cheap, fast, and it is a prerequisite for interpreting M1, M5, and M6 — without reason codes those experiments will produce effects we cannot explain. *How it changes the decision:* if most manual activations trace to the post-billing policy, restoring the activation gate fixes it outright; if they trace to network provisioning, the redesign is not the cause and we should look elsewhere before constraining it.

**4. M7 — Conformance monitoring for Billing start before Activation test and Permit check absence.** *Resolves:* the frequency, regional concentration, and trend of two compliance-relevant deviations that alignments flag as "frequent" without quantifying. *Why high:* near-zero marginal cost on existing conformance infrastructure, and it doubles as an operational control rather than pure measurement — it should be switched on immediately regardless of the rollout decision. *How it changes the decision:* a high and rising violation rate makes any expansion untenable until the process is hard-gated; a low rate concentrated in one region turns a design problem into a local training problem. It also provides the ongoing audit evidence any broader rollout will require.

**5. M1 — Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.** *Resolves:* the causal effect of skipping, per stratum, free of the confound between pilot-region building mix and treatment. *Why not higher:* it is the most expensive and slowest option, it deliberately exposes some customers to a known-harmful treatment, and M6 may answer the practical question at a fraction of the cost. *How it changes the decision:* it is the only evidence that would justify reinstating skipping for medium-risk addresses at national scale. Run it only if M6 shows the rule is close to viable, and run it stratified with a strict harm-stopping rule.

**6. M5 — Coordinator ownership versus shared-team ownership under coordinator absence.** *Resolves:* whether the resilience loss is intrinsic to single ownership or fixable with a backup and shared case notes — currently an anecdote ("cases stalled") driving a real organizational design choice. *How it changes the decision:* if a small pod preserves the handover reduction while removing the single point of failure, it becomes the national target operating model instead of the individual coordinator, and the 93% utilization problem becomes tractable through pooling. Medium value because a cheap mitigation (named backup, mandatory case notes) can be deployed now without waiting for the answer.

**7. M2 — Instrument start timestamps for Installation visit, Activation test, Manual activation.** *Resolves:* the split between waiting time and work time, which currently makes the p90/p95 deterioration undiagnosable — we cannot tell whether the tail is queueing behind the 92%-utilized second-visit team or longer execution on harder buildings. *Why not higher:* foundational and enduring, but it improves future diagnosis more than it changes today's decision; the guardrails we recommend do not depend on it. *How it changes the decision:* if the tail is predominantly waiting, the fix is capacity and load-balancing rather than process redesign, which would substantially reframe the whole programme. Worth starting now because the payoff compounds and instrumentation lead times are long.

**8. M8 — Collect customer experience separately for "speed to appointment" and "working service after installation".** *Resolves:* why NPS moved only 4 points when complaints rose 3.7pp — almost certainly two offsetting effects. *Why lowest:* it confirms a mechanism we already infer from the complaint-theme shift, and it is unlikely to reverse any alternative. *How it changes the decision:* it sharpens target-setting and communication, and it prevents a future scenario where an improving composite NPS masks a deteriorating reliability component. Useful, not urgent.

**Sequencing.** M3, M4, M6, and M7 form a no-regret bundle to start immediately: together they cost little, run in parallel with the guardrailed rollout, and jointly resolve attribution, economics, rule quality, and compliance. M2 and M5 start in parallel as instrumentation and organizational-design work. M1 is conditional on M6. M8 follows once the reliability problem is contained.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E — a segmented, guardrailed rollout of Redesign R, with the harmful mechanisms suspended and expansion gated on explicit thresholds.** Coordinator ownership is retained everywhere; survey skipping and post-billing activation are stopped now; the fully simplified path runs only for simple low-risk houses; everything else waits on evidence.

**Immediate action (weeks 0–4).**
- Retain single-coordinator ownership for all segments and keep the merged validate/schedule task for simple low-risk cases; this is the proven component (median -6.0 days, p90 -8.0 days, first-time-right +3pp in that segment; 3+ handovers down from 34% to 12% overall).
- Hard-gate billing: Billing start becomes technically impossible before a passed activation test or an explicitly signed-off manual activation. Route existing premature-billing cases to proactive credit and customer contact.
- Reinstate the permit check as a blocking step for permit-sensitive and old-building cases, with a conformance alert on every bypass.
- Cap coordinator utilization at approximately 85% and assign each coordinator a named backup with mandatory shared case notes, addressing both the load and the absence-stall failure mode.
- Rebalance capacity: redeploy the freed field-survey capacity (utilization 78% to 52%) to relieve the second-visit team (66% to 92%) rather than banking it as savings.
- Switch on conformance monitoring (M7) and reason codes (M3) from day one.

**What to pause.**
- The predictive survey-skip rule for every segment except simple low-risk houses — it is the documented driver of the apartment and old-building deterioration.
- All post-billing activation checks, without exception.
- Any national rollout planning, procurement, or hiring commitment premised on the EUR 161 visible-cost figure.
- Expansion into rural long-distance and business-customer segments, where the tail worsened (+9 and +5 days at p90) and manual activation is generating billing disputes.

**What to scale.**
- Coordinator ownership and the merged validate/schedule task, nationally, for simple low-risk houses.
- Coordinator ownership with the full control set (survey performed, permit checked, activation before billing) for apartments, old buildings, business, and rural cases — these keep the accountability benefit without the control removals.

**What to measure next.** In priority order: M6 (validate the risk rule retrospectively), M4 (complete 60-day downstream cost), M3 (reason codes), M7 (continuous conformance), then M2 and M5 in parallel, then M1 conditional on M6, then M8. Report weekly by segment, never in aggregate only — aggregation is what produced the contradictory picture in the first place.

**Thresholds that would justify broader rollout.** Expansion of the simplified path to a new segment requires *all* of the following, sustained over at least 8 weeks and at least 3,000 cases in that segment:
- p90 cycle time at or below the baseline 31.0 days and p95 at or below 45.0 days for that segment — the tail must not be worse than what we replaced.
- First-time-right at or above 82% and 30-day complaints at or below 6.1%, i.e. at least baseline parity on quality.
- Complete 60-day cost per case at or below EUR 190 (a real margin below the EUR 198 baseline, not the truncated EUR 207 estimate).
- Zero billing-before-activation events and 100% permit-check presence for permit-sensitive cases, verified by continuous conformance monitoring.
- For any reinstatement of survey skipping: a validated rule with a false-skip rate at or below 5% within the specific stratum, confirmed by M6 and, for medium-risk strata, by a stratified randomized test (M1).
- Coordinator utilization at or below 85% with documented backup coverage, and second-visit team utilization at or below 80%.
- Discovered-model precision recovered to at least 0.75 with fitness maintained above 0.85, indicating the process has re-standardized rather than merely become more permissive.

**Stopping rule.** If, after the guardrails are in place, the guardrailed process still shows complete 60-day cost above EUR 198 or first-time-right below 80% for two consecutive months, escalate to Alternative B for the affected segments — that is the point at which coordinator ownership itself, not the removed controls, becomes the suspect.

**Honest statement of the trade-off.** No option on the table improves every metric, and we should not present one as if it did. E deliberately sacrifices part of the headline median gain and roughly one to two quarters of time in exchange for protecting reliability, downstream cost, and billing integrity across the 47% of volume where R is currently doing harm. If the measurement programme comes back favourable, we will have a defensible national case within two quarters. If it does not, we will have avoided nationalizing a process that costs more, breaks more often, and bills customers for service that does not yet work.
