## 1. Drift diagnosis table

The strongest interpretation is a sequence of overlapping incidents. April is primarily a pipeline failure; June changes the routing mix; July–September introduce slower parts delivery and likely changes in the risk relationship; September also changes the population. October's reported performance is heavily confounded by selective label availability. A drift in recorded activities is not automatically a drift in the underlying repair process.

Here, covariate drift means a change in the distribution of prediction-time inputs, P(X); concept drift means a change in P(breach | X). Process drift concerns path frequencies, transitions, or timing. Population shift concerns who supplies or receives the service. These categories can overlap: a new partner can change both P(X) and path frequencies, while supplier deterioration can change both process timing and P(breach | X).

## 2. Competing explanations and timeline

**January.** Establish the reference period: fully mature labels, AUROC 0.81, Brier 0.132, ECE 0.025, approximately aligned mean risk and breach rate, and low unknown/missing-feature rates. V3 is already substantially riskier than V1/V2. Its 28 h parts edge is a useful baseline, not a service-level threshold by itself.

**February.** Metrics remain stable: AUROC 0.82 and ECE 0.022. The small changes in prevalence, mix, and parts time provide no compelling evidence of failure. Use January–March jointly to estimate normal variability rather than treating one month as an exact reference.

**March.** Predictive performance remains stable despite parts transit increasing to 35 h. This gradual timing increase is worth monitoring, but the stated supplier incident has not begun. Neither a small latency change nor a small metric fluctuation alone establishes concept drift.

**April.** The migration supplies a direct mechanism for corruption: the scoring trigger no longer recognizes many diagnosis events; some prefixes are missing or anchored incorrectly; duplicates and unknown labels alter traces and derived features. AUROC drops to 0.67, Brier worsens to 0.184, and ECE reaches 0.110 while mature breach prevalence remains near baseline, at 0.19. Mean risk rises to 0.28. The apparent path fragmentation into 18% unmapped/other, 17% unknown activities, and 14% queue missingness triangulates a schema/pipeline incident. April's modest increases in variant breach rates leave room for smaller genuine changes, but these do not plausibly account for the dominant failure without further evidence. Label immaturity is not an explanation here.

**May.** The 6 May patch reduces unknown activities to 2%, unmapped variants to 1%, and queue missingness to 3%. AUROC recovers to 0.78 and ECE to 0.050. Recovery following repair strengthens the pipeline attribution. Incomplete cached-feature backfills and uneven duplicate cleaning prevent declaring April fixed or May uniformly clean. Split May around the patch and April around the 20 April deduplication change, by source and feature version. Check whether corrected event histories actually propagated into stored feature vectors.

**June.** The remote-routing policy creates a real process mix change: V2 rises to 52%, while V1 falls to 30%. Remote service time remains stable, and matured V2 breach risk is 0.09. A move toward this lower-risk route can reduce aggregate prevalence without any changed relationship within a route. Mature observed prevalence falls to 0.15 while mean risk is 0.20; ECE rises to 0.060, but AUROC remains near baseline at 0.79. This supports changed composition and calibration more strongly than broad concept drift. V3's modest risk and latency increases warrant investigation, but June is not evidence of the severe later supplier failure. Whether this is also covariate drift depends on which routing predictors were available at the scoring trigger.

**July.** The supplier incident begins on 10 July. Parts transit reaches 65 h and matured V3 breach risk rises to 0.46, while V1/V2 remain near their earlier levels. Aggregate prevalence rises to 0.19 despite mean risk of 0.18, and AUROC drops to 0.76. A localized process-performance change with possible concept drift is now plausible. Test pre/post-10 July cases and exposure to the affected supplier; a whole-month average dilutes the onset. At 98% label maturity, selection bias is smaller than later but still requires checking.

**August.** The supplier problem becomes severe: parts transit reaches 130 h and matured V3 breach risk 0.64. V3's share also increases to 35%, adding a composition effect to worsening performance within that route. Mean risk of 0.19 substantially understates the currently observed 0.28 breach rate; AUROC falls to 0.68 and ECE reaches 0.120. Low unknown-activity and queue-missingness rates weaken the hypothesis that the April-type corruption is the primary August cause. Partner Z is absent, so the August deterioration cannot be attributed to it. The 93% maturity limits exact magnitude estimates, but the process evidence and supplied matured variant rates support genuine operational deterioration.

**September.** Supplier slowness persists at 148 h. V3's share rises again to 42%, and Partner Z supplies 24% of cases with much heavier V3 routing and a different customer profile. V3's matured breach rate is 0.61, slightly below August's 0.64: this is not evidence that every underlying risk relationship worsened again. The higher aggregate observed rate, 0.31, can reflect mix changes and other routes as well as selection. AUROC 0.66 and ECE 0.140 are consistent with continuing misfit, but only 82% of labels are available and Z matures more slowly. Decompose source composition, route composition, within-route deterioration, and label selection before assigning an additional partner-specific effect.

**October.** Mean risk is 0.23 and currently observed breach prevalence falls to 0.16, but only 55% of labels are available. The backlog selects against longer paths, plausibly hiding disproportionately risky cases. AUROC 0.64, Brier 0.205, and ECE 0.130 describe the labeled subset, not established performance of the full October cohort. Parts transit reaches 155 h and the population remains heavily V3/Partner Z, so process evidence supplies no clear recovery signal. Even the parts median can be biased downward when still-waiting orders are omitted.

If October's maturity and observed breach rate use the same case denominator, the full-cohort breach rate has a worst-case bound of 0.55 × 0.16 to 0.55 × 0.16 + 0.45, or **8.8%–53.8%**, before making assumptions about missing labels. The 16% observed rate cannot establish improvement. The median delay of available labels also need not describe the unobserved long tail.

The supplied tables should not be forced into an exact arithmetic decomposition. Variant proportions refer to case mixes, variant breach rates are described as matured, and aggregate metrics use labels available at cutoff. For example, weighting August's variant rates by its variant shares gives about 31.3%, rather than the observed 28%. Align case identifiers, cohort definitions, scoring coverage, and maturity vintages before interpreting the difference. Compare mean predicted risk and observed prevalence on the same cases as well.

Thus April is a measurement and execution incident; June is a genuine policy-driven routing change; August–September combine deteriorating delivery performance, likely conditional-risk changes, changing mix, and a new population; October adds severe outcome-availability bias to unresolved underlying problems.

## 3. Diagnostic tests

Use the canonical diagnosis trigger, immutable prediction logs, and case-level records joining source, start date, promise date, event history, feature version, score, outcome, and outcome availability date. Retain both the original production cohort and any reconstructed cohort; reconstruction cannot retroactively turn an unscored case into a deployed prediction.

1. **Label-maturity curves and fixed-vintage backtesting.** For each start and prediction month, estimate label availability versus cohort age, stratified by source, observed/intended route, promise horizon, and resolution-path length. Separate outcome-event time from API-arrival time and track labels still unavailable. Re-evaluate the same immutable predictions at, for example, 30, 60, 90 days and a sufficiently mature final vintage; select the final horizon using the actual delay tail, not the median alone. Compare cohorts at equal age and report coverage alongside every metric.
   - Under selective label delay, October's cohort metrics and apparent prevalence change materially as late long-path labels arrive; Z's curve lags other sources. The apparent October reversal shrinks or disappears.
   - Under genuine predictive deterioration, miscalibration or rank loss remains after comparable maturity and ascertainment. Under benign random delay, estimates mainly gain precision rather than shifting systematically.
   - Treat missing outcomes as unknown. Use censoring/availability weighting only with defensible identification assumptions and adequate overlap; otherwise report bounds and sensitivity analyses.

2. **Normalization, duplicate cleaning, and feature replay.** Replay April–May from raw records, normalizing both diagnosis labels before trigger selection. Reconcile case/event identities across sources; remove confirmed repeated records while retaining genuine repeated activities. Rebuild the correct prediction prefix and recompute all downstream features, including the cached count and queue joins, using only information available at the original diagnosis time. Compare raw production scores, corrected scores from the frozen original model, and scoring coverage.
   - Under pipeline failure, wrong/missing triggers, event counts, and feature gaps align with the migration/source boundaries; repair substantially restores April discrimination and calibration and removes much of the unmapped mass.
   - Under genuine process or concept change, repaired features remain valid but residual outcome errors persist; a correctly reconstructed parts-latency change remains visible.
   - If only the activity-name distribution improves while cached features or scores remain abnormal, the patch was incomplete. Report production and replay performance separately.

3. **Calibration and discrimination by variant, source, and month.** On aligned mature cohorts, report reliability curves with fixed bins, calibration intercept/slope, Brier and its uncertainty, AUROC, and precision/recall or utility at the operational threshold. Include sample sizes, outcome counts, label coverage, and case-level uncertainty intervals. Cross variant with source where support permits; inspect intended route at prediction time separately from realized final route. Compare observed prevalence with mean prediction on exactly the evaluated cases.
   - Under a route-mix explanation, pooled metrics change but mature within-route/source score–outcome relationships stay stable; standardizing the mix restores much of the aggregate result.
   - Under supplier-driven concept drift, V3 develops residual underprediction and possibly rank loss even after standardization; V1/V2 act as useful comparison groups.
   - Under pipeline corruption, damage clusters by source, migration period, or feature version and improves after replay. Small segments may remain inconclusive rather than proving stability.

4. **Variant and DFG/performance-DFG comparison.** Compare normalized complete-trace variant frequencies and transition counts, both raw and standardized for source. Separate topology, transition frequency, and elapsed-time distributions. For Order Part → Part Received, report counts, quantiles, outstanding-order ages, and a censoring-aware time-to-receipt analysis, stratified by supplier. Define whether edges are assigned to order month, receipt month, or case-start cohort. Apply the same analysis to diagnosis → Remote Fix.
   - Under the June routing change, V2 frequency increases with little change in its time distribution or conditional breach relationship.
   - Under supplier performance deterioration, parts waiting times lengthen within comparable supplier/path groups while remote times remain stable, even if the DFG topology is unchanged.
   - Under April schema failure, normalization and cleaning restore recognizable variants and edges; the apparent structural discontinuity largely vanishes. If a latency shift vanishes only after correcting timestamps or duplicates, it was a data artifact.
   - Analyze complete variants separately from provisional open traces. Event-calendar clipping and right censoring can otherwise manufacture apparent path changes.

5. **Conditional outcome relationship on the same path and comparable inputs.** Within V3, compare pre-incident and post-incident breach risk using matching, stratification, or a flexible outcome model over the same pre-diagnosis X: promise slack, triage/diagnosis duration, known queue state, device/customer characteristics, source, and supplier information already known at prediction. Test month interactions with risk scores/features and residual calibration. Repeat for V1/V2, and, where credible, use an affected-versus-unaffected supplier comparison around 10 July. Check common support and uncertainty.
   - Under pure composition change, excess post-incident risk disappears after matching/reweighting; a frozen relationship transports reasonably within shared support.
   - Under concept drift relative to the deployed X, time/incident interactions or residual V3 risk persist after repair and maturity adjustment. A localized change in the affected supplier strengthens that interpretation.
   - The same final path is not the same covariate profile. Final route selection may itself respond to difficulty; conditioning on it can induce selection bias. This is diagnostic evidence, not a causal proof. Actual future receipt delay must not be added to a diagnosis-time prediction model to explain away the change.

6. **Partner Z population reweighting and transport checks.** In September, reweight contemporaneous non-Z cases toward Z's pre-diagnosis covariate distribution, including rural distance, device age, promise slack, intended routing, and service region. Compare mature outcome and calibration gaps before/after adjustment. Separately standardize realized variant mix for descriptive analysis. Audit overlap and effective sample size; use a same-month comparison to reduce confounding by the supplier timeline.
   - Under population/composition shift with a stable conditional relationship, weighting substantially reproduces Z's outcome and error profile; there is little residual source interaction in shared support.
   - Under an additional Z-specific service or conditional-risk change, mature gaps persist within matched profiles and routes.
   - Under selective label delay, source gaps move materially with reporting vintage. Without overlap, transport is not identified: neither a successful weighting story nor a partner-specific concept/fairness claim is established.

7. **Feature missingness, trigger coverage, and lineage audit.** Trace raw source values through ingestion, alias mapping, event ordering, deduplication, trigger creation, queue joins, cached transformations, and scoring. Compare missingness and feature values by source/date/version, inspect sampled cases end to end, and verify reproducibility from immutable snapshots. Audit timestamp time zones, ties, event-ID namespaces, and the target's promised-date definition despite the absence of a documented outcome-definition change.
   - Under pipeline failure, defects occur at release boundaries, missingness correlates with wrong prefixes, and cached values disagree with recomputed values; repair removes them.
   - Under genuine covariate/process drift, reconstructed vectors agree with lineage and contain valid distribution shifts that persist after repair.
   - Under a real queue-information change, raw source fields themselves are absent; under a join/schema bug they exist upstream but disappear downstream. Low overall missingness does not falsify a concentrated source defect.

These tests give each explanation a counterfactual check: repair for pipeline failure, mature vintages for ascertainment bias, standardization for composition, matched conditional outcomes for concept drift, and cleaned event/edge comparisons for process drift.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision

**Immediate containment.** Fix canonical trigger recognition, event identity, duplicate handling, queue lineage, and dependent caches. Reprocess the affected records and validate prediction coverage. For invalid prefixes or unreliable feature rows, route cases through a validated fallback or explicit operational review until scoring is sound. Restore the outcome API and mark recent metrics provisional. Preserve shipped scores, repair versions, and failure records for the audit.

At the same time, address supplier risk operationally: prioritize overdue/high-exposure parts cases using valid information available at diagnosis, review dispatch capacity and supplier alternatives, and measure the effect. Do not wait for every October label to mature before responding to independent evidence of slow delivery. Evaluate workload and intervention costs before broadly lowering risk thresholds.

**Short-term recalibration.** Fit a simple intercept or intercept-and-slope adjustment on a recent, clean, sufficiently mature validation window, then validate it on a later mature window. June's mature overprediction with relatively preserved AUROC is a reasonable candidate if within-segment ordering is sound. A mixture-aware adjustment may be useful where the relevant segment is known at prediction time.

For the later supplier period, recalibration can temporarily improve estimated risk when the score still orders cases adequately within an affected segment. It cannot restore lost within-segment discrimination: one global monotone transformation preserves ranking, and it may overcorrect V1/V2 while leaving V3 underpredicted. Diagnose August's low AUROC within segments before relying on calibration alone. Revalidate action thresholds and intervention capacity after calibration.

**Feature repair and extension.** Recompute April's stale event count and every dependent feature for all affected sources/dates, rather than merely updating the activity map. Test shadow scoring from corrected diagnosis prefixes. Consider historical supplier reliability, current backlog, estimated lead times, intended route, travel distance, device age, and remaining promise slack if each is known and reliable at the trigger. Features describing this case's eventual part receipt, eventual route, final duration, or later interventions are unavailable at diagnosis and would leak future information. Historical supplier summaries must use only observations available at that historical prediction time.

**Full retraining.** Retrain when mature, repaired, temporally validated evidence shows persistent conditional-risk change, inadequate ranking, or important new predictive features that the old model cannot use. July–September's V3 evidence makes this a likely need, but the aggregate AUROC decline alone does not establish it. Use chronological training/calibration/test splits, simulate historical label availability, and exclude unresolved outcomes rather than coding them as non-breaches. Balance recency against sample size and maintain representative source/route coverage. Compare the retrained candidate with the repaired frozen model and recalibrated alternatives on the same cases, including operational utility.

| Cohort | Suitability for training or calibration |
|---|---|
| January–March | Safest baseline: labels are mature and no documented pipeline incident. Still verify trigger/feature lineage; this period alone does not represent the later supplier regime or Partner Z. |
| April | Unsafe as originally materialized. Use only after full normalization, deduplication, prefix reconstruction, and cache recomputation, with verified historical feature availability. Mature labels do not repair feature leakage or absent predictions. |
| May | Labels are mature, but pre-patch and inherited/cached defects require source/date/version validation. Admit only records with verified repaired features; do not certify the whole month from its averages. |
| June | Generally suitable after lineage checks: fully mature labels and a real policy regime. Useful for recalibration/route-mix evaluation, but not representative of severe supplier deterioration. |
| July | Partially mature and contains two supplier regimes. Use a verified mature cohort with representative ascertainment, or wait for labels; model the 10 July boundary. Do not assume the remaining 2% are random. |
| August–September | Needed to represent the later regime, but unrestricted cutoff-label training is unsafe at 93%/82% maturity. Prefer subsequent mature snapshots. A labeled-only subset is usable only with demonstrated representativeness or a justified selection/censoring method; September also needs adequate Partner Z support. |

A segment-aware global model is a reasonable first candidate, with interactions for features describing route propensity, supplier exposure, and customer context. A separate V3 model or calibration layer is justified only if mature evidence shows distinct residual behavior and sufficient data, and V3 membership is known or reliably anticipated at diagnosis. Because all listed variants diverge after diagnosis, a model selected using the eventual completed variant would leak information. Use an intended route already recorded at the trigger, or a validated routing-probability model, and evaluate routing errors.

Do not create a Partner Z model merely because its marginal rate is higher. Compare a shared model augmented with rural/device/service information against source-specific calibration or models on mature data. Separate models need enough observations, overlap, stable deployment routing, and better prospective utility. Where evidence is sparse, a shared model with partial pooling is preferable to unstable source estimates.

## 5. Monitoring architecture

Maintain an immutable case-and-prediction ledger and versioned event/feature snapshots. Store case/source identities, canonical trigger time, raw and normalized activities, feature/model/calibration versions, prediction, original promise, outcome-event time, outcome-ingestion time, and label status. Publish both start-month and prediction-month cohorts at multiple reporting vintages. Process evidence should also have a separate event-calendar view. This supports replay without rewriting history.

| Monitor | Measures and stratification | Alert dependency and response |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive performance | AUROC, threshold precision/recall, intervention utility, coverage, sample sizes, and uncertainty by month/source/route on fixed-vintage cohorts | Gate outcome-based model conclusions on label maturity, target validity, and feature lineage. Persistent deterioration on clean mature cohorts triggers model investigation. Report provisional metrics separately. |
| Calibration | Matched-cohort mean risk versus prevalence, reliability curves, ECE with consistent bins, intercept/slope, and Brier | Recalibration requires clean inputs, representative labels, and adequate score ordering. Cross-segment differences trigger segment analysis before a global adjustment. |
| Label maturity | Availability by cohort age, ingestion versus event delay, unresolved labels, source/path curves, API queue depth | An ascertainment alarm routes to outcome ingestion and suspends automatic retraining/recalibration from affected cohorts. It does not suppress independent process or data-quality alarms. |
| Data quality | Unknown activities, mapping coverage, duplicate IDs and ambiguous repetitions, missingness, timestamp order, trigger coverage/position, cache/version consistency | Schema/feature alarms trigger repair, replay, and containment first. Training on affected inputs remains gated until verified. Include concentrated source failures even when global averages look acceptable. |
| Process variants | Completed-path frequencies, provisional open paths, intended routing, transition counts, normalized unmapped share by source | First validate event normalization and completeness. With clean data, route-frequency changes trigger mix-standardized analysis, not automatic concept-drift declarations. |
| Performance DFG | Edge counts and median/tail waiting times, supplier-specific parts latency, outstanding-order ages, censoring-aware time-to-receipt, remote-time controls | Severe supplier delays trigger operational response independently of label availability. Require adequate support and separate completed-edge selection from genuine latency shifts. |
| Population and inputs | Partner/source/region mix, rural distance, device age, promise slack, actual model features, missingness, distribution distance and support/overlap | New-population alerts trigger transport and stratified calibration checks. Low overlap warrants targeted evidence collection and carefully evaluated fallback decisions. |
| Interventions and deployment | Model/calibration/feature releases, policy dates, supplier changes, threshold versions, intervention exposure, workload and resolution outcomes | Annotate causal candidates and preserve treatment records. Use shadow/canary evaluation, rollback criteria, and an ethically appropriate holdout or controlled rollout where possible; intervention effects must not be mistaken for uncontrolled model drift. |

Use baseline variability, adequate sample/edge counts, effect-size thresholds, and persistence rules rather than alerting on every statistical significance test. Block-bootstrap or otherwise account for correlated supplier/source observations where relevant. Distinguish warning severity from remediation routing.

The key dependency is: **an outcome-metric alert plus poor label maturity prompts ascertainment investigation; an outcome-metric alert plus invalid features prompts repair; persistent errors with clean features and mature labels prompt recalibration or retraining according to ranking and conditional-risk evidence.** Several branches can remain active simultaneously. A supplier-delay alarm should still reach operations during October's label backlog.

The following sketch assumes a local CSV with reconciled `case_id`, `source`, `activity`, and `timestamp` columns, and optional durable `event_id` and `queue` columns. It returns process summaries and cleaning evidence, not a fitted model. Conflicting durable IDs fail validation; ambiguous same-time/activity repetitions are retained for review. File order supplies deterministic tie ordering here; production must verify ties using a reliable source sequence. Use the installed PM4Py version's public APIs.

```python
from numbers import Integral
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

DIAG = "Initial Diagnosis Completed"
C, A, T = "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"

def monthly_process_evidence(path="field_service_events.csv", *, cutoff,
                             year=2026):
    raw = pd.read_csv(path)
    raw["_row"] = range(len(raw))  # provenance and deterministic tie order
    raw["timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(raw["timestamp"], utc=True,
                                      errors="coerce")
    required = ["case_id", "source", "activity", "timestamp"]
    if raw[required].isna().any().any():
        raise ValueError("Quarantine missing identities/activities/timestamps")
    asof = pd.to_datetime(cutoff, utc=True)
    raw = raw.loc[raw["timestamp"] <= asof].copy()
    raw["raw_activity"] = raw["activity"]
    # Normalize BEFORE diagnosis-prefix or other feature extraction.
    raw["activity"] = raw["activity"].replace({
        "Diagnostic Assessment Completed": DIAG
    })
    removed = raw.iloc[0:0].copy()
    if "event_id" in raw:
        keys = ["source", "case_id", "event_id"]
        known = raw["event_id"].notna()
        payload = [c for c in raw if c not in keys + ["_row", "raw_activity"]]
        conflicts = raw.loc[known].groupby(keys)[payload].nunique(dropna=False)
        if conflicts.gt(1).any().any():
            raise ValueError("Conflicting event IDs: reconcile before extraction")
        duplicate = known & raw.duplicated(keys, keep="first")
        removed = raw.loc[duplicate].copy()
        raw = raw.loc[~duplicate].copy()
    # Cross-source copies or repetitions without durable IDs need verification.
    ambiguous = raw.loc[raw.duplicated(
        ["case_id", "activity", "timestamp"], keep=False)].copy()
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(raw, case_id="case_id",
                               activity_key="activity", timestamp_key="timestamp")
    df = df.sort_values([C, T, "_row"], kind="stable")
    starts = df.loc[df[A].eq("Case Created")].groupby(C)[T].min()
    closed = set(df.loc[df[A].eq("Case Closed"), C])

    def summarize(g):
        if g.empty:
            return {"cases": 0, "events": 0}
        g = g.sort_values([C, T, "_row"], kind="stable")
        dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(g)
        perf, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(g)
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(g)
        counts = {v: int(n) if isinstance(n, Integral) else len(n)
                  for v, n in variants.items()}
        # Compute explicit MEDIANS; do not assume the API's default is median.
        edges = g[[C, A, T]].copy()
        edges["next_activity"] = edges.groupby(C)[A].shift(-1)
        next_time = edges.groupby(C)[T].shift(-1)
        edges["seconds"] = (next_time - edges[T]).dt.total_seconds()
        edge_stats = (edges.dropna(subset=["next_activity"])
                      .groupby([A, "next_activity"])["seconds"]
                      .agg(count="size", median_s="median").reset_index())
        ids = set(g[C])
        return {"cases": len(ids), "events": len(g),
                "unknown_share": g[A].eq("UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY").mean(),
                "missing_queue_share": (g["queue"].isna().mean()
                                        if "queue" in g else None),
                "closed_by_cutoff_share": len(ids & closed) / len(ids),
                "variants": counts, "dfg": dfg, "performance_dfg": perf,
                "edge_stats": edge_stats, "start_activities": start_activities,
                "end_activities": end_activities,
                "temporal": pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
                    g, grouper_freq="MS")}

    summaries = []
    for month in range(1, 13):
        lo = pd.Timestamp(year=year, month=month, day=1, tz="UTC")
        if lo > asof:
            break
        hi = lo + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)
        # Event-calendar view: activity during this month, with clipped traces.
        calendar = pm4py.filter_time_range(
            df, lo.isoformat(), hi.isoformat(), mode="events")
        calendar = calendar.loc[(calendar[T] >= lo) & (calendar[T] < hi)]
        # Case-start view: ALL observed events through cutoff for these starts.
        ids = starts.index[(starts >= lo) & (starts < hi)]
        cohort = df.loc[df[C].isin(ids)]
        for view, g in [("event_calendar", calendar), ("case_start", cohort)]:
            summaries.append({"month": lo.strftime("%Y-%m"), "view": view,
                              "cutoff": asof.isoformat(), **summarize(g)})
    audit = {"removed_duplicate_rows": removed,
             "ambiguous_rows_retained": ambiguous,
             "cases_without_creation_event": sorted(set(df[C]) - set(starts.index)),
             "renamed_events": raw.loc[raw["raw_activity"].eq(
                 "Diagnostic Assessment Completed"), ["_row", "case_id", "source"]]}
    return summaries, audit
```

Event-calendar summaries describe workload and transitions visible during a month. They truncate paths at month boundaries and omit cross-boundary edges, so their variants are partial sequences. Case-start summaries retain the observed histories of cases started that month, including later events through the specified cutoff; recent cases remain right-censored. The returned closure share helps expose that limitation. Do not interpret either view's open traces as completed V1/V2/V3 distributions.

For the supplier question, additionally assign each original consecutive Order Part → Part Received pair to a stated anchor month and retain outstanding orders in a time-to-receipt analysis; neither a clipped DFG nor completed-edge medians alone capture all waiting. `missing_queue_share` in this sketch is an event-column diagnostic, not the table's missing diagnosis-time feature rate, which requires a separate prefix/feature-ledger join. Diagnosis-prefix reconstruction must precede that feature audit. The temporal API supplies descriptors, not a drift verdict. No PM4Py drift detector is assumed.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis

1. **Retraining on immature labels.** Coding missing outcomes as non-breaches, or training only on quickly available labels, can teach the model that long V3 and Partner Z cases are safer than they are. October's apparent prevalence decrease can then become a self-reinforcing underprediction problem. Mitigate with fixed-vintage mature cohorts, explicit unknown-label states, ascertainment audits, and justified censoring methods or bounds where full maturity is unavailable.

2. **Calibrating around April's pipeline bug.** A calibration adjustment can hide the average overprediction while retaining wrong prefixes, stale event counts, lost discrimination, and unscored cases. Once the pipeline is repaired, that adjustment may itself miscalibrate scores. Repair and replay first, validate coverage and feature lineage, and fit calibration only on clean data. Preserve original production performance so corrected replay does not erase the incident.

3. **Dismissing supplier-driven deterioration as harmless mix drift.** More V3 cases do raise aggregate risk, but V3's own matured breach rate also rises sharply. Reweighting routes alone can leave parts-exposed cases systematically underpredicted and delay operational action. Mitigate with mature within-route conditional tests, supplier-specific waiting-time monitoring including outstanding orders, fresh prediction-time supplier features, and measured operational containment.

4. **Treating Partner Z's marginal gap as proof of unfair model behavior.** The observed 0.45 versus 0.27 rate mixes different customer profiles, routes, service conditions, and label delays. A blanket partner penalty or isolated model could encode a spurious source effect or allocate interventions poorly. Compare mature calibration, decision quality, and service outcomes within comparable profiles; test overlap and transport. Investigate fairness with appropriate group definitions and stratified evidence rather than inferring it from the source breach-rate gap alone.

5. **Retraining unnecessarily after June's policy change.** A lower-risk route gaining share can change prevalence and pooled metrics despite stable within-route relationships. Unnecessary replacement may reduce stability or learn noisy short-window patterns. Standardize route mix, evaluate calibration and utility on June's mature labels, and use the least extensive change that fixes the measured failure.

6. **Introducing future information through variant-specific models.** Selecting the model using the final V3 trace or adding this case's realized parts delay can produce excellent retrospective results that cannot be reproduced immediately after diagnosis. Enforce timestamped feature availability, route using information recorded at the trigger, and replay the complete prediction process chronologically.

7. **Mistaking incomplete traces or cleaned event counts for real process improvement.** Calendar clipping, deduplication artifacts, and still-unreceived orders can shorten observed paths or bias latency estimates. Mitigate with reconciled identities, retained cleaning audits, separate event-calendar and case-start views, complete/provisional variant labels, edge support counts, and censoring-aware waiting-time analysis.

8. **Ignoring intervention and deployment feedback.** New routing, revised thresholds, or targeted supplier interventions can alter both paths and labels; a model may appear worse or better because its actions changed outcomes. Record policy/model versions and intervention exposure, compare appropriately controlled cohorts, and assess service benefit alongside prediction accuracy before interpreting the next metric change as model drift.
