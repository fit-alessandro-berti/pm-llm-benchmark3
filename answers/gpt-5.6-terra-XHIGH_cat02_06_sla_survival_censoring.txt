# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let \(t=0\) be the recorded `Submit` event and let the SLA horizon be \(H=72\) elapsed hours. A `Resolve` at exactly 72 hours is treated as timely; thus “after the SLA” means \(t>72\).

Define:

- \(T_R\): time from `Submit` to an internally recorded `Resolve`.
- \(T_D\): time to any internal terminal disposition: `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`.
- \(C\): administrative observation time at the frozen extract. For `open` cases, `duration_or_age_h` is observed censoring age; for terminal cases it is an event time.
- \(J\): terminal type, where \(J\in\{\text{Resolve},\text{Withdraw},\text{Reject},\text{Transfer}\}\).

A necessary scope choice is whether the SLA is about internal successful resolution, any internal disposition, or end-to-end resolution including an external organization. The supplied system can directly measure only the first two.

Useful estimands are:

1. **Internal successful-resolution SLA risk**
   
   \[
   q_R(72)=P(T_R>72)=1-F_R(72),
   \]
   
   where \(F_R(72)\) is the cumulative incidence of internal `Resolve` by 72 hours in the presence of competing `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and, under the strict internal definition, `Transfer`.

   This is the best primary estimand for a management question such as: “What fraction of submitted cases has not achieved the operationally successful `Resolve` outcome by the SLA deadline?” A withdrawal or rejection before 72 is still a known non-`Resolve` outcome under this definition; it should not be silently discarded.

2. **Late-resolution cumulative incidence**
   
   For a predeclared follow-up horizon \(L>72\),
   
   \[
   p_{\text{late},R}(72,L)=P(72<T_R\leq L,\ J=\text{Resolve})
   =F_R(L)-F_R(72).
   \]
   
   This estimates the arrival-level frequency of cases that eventually resolve late, rather than the proportion late only among observed resolved cases. It supports remediation and tail-capacity decisions: how much late work is actually completed after the deadline? “Eventually,” with \(L=\infty\), is not identifiable from this frozen extract because some cases remain open and external outcomes are unobserved.

3. **Outstanding internal-work risk at the SLA**
   
   \[
   q_D(72)=P(T_D>72).
   \]
   
   This is the probability that a case still has no internal terminal disposition at 72 hours. It is a queue/backlog and staffing metric, not a successful-resolution metric. Under this definition, a `Transfer` is an internal disposition because the internal system has handed the case off.

4. **Cause-specific cumulative incidences**
   
   Report \(F_R(72)\), \(F_W(72)\), \(F_J(72)\), and \(F_T(72)\) for Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer respectively. These separate a low timely-resolution rate caused by slow work from one caused by early rejection, withdrawal, or external handoff.

The cases in the question contribute differently to these estimands:

| Example | Contribution to \(q_R(72)\): no internal Resolve by 72 | Contribution to late-resolution incidence | Contribution to \(q_D(72)\): no internal disposition by 72 |
|---|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76 h | Known SLA non-resolution at 72; an already observed successful-resolution breach | Adds a `Resolve` event at 76 h if \(L\geq76\) | Had no terminal disposition at 72, then left the active state at 76 |
| S09, open at 92 h | Known to have no Resolve by 72 | Event-free through 92 and then right-censored at 92 for later-resolution analysis | Known to have no disposition at 72 |
| S06, withdrawn at 27 h | Known not to have reached `Resolve` by 72 under the strict success definition | A competing `Withdraw` event at 27; cannot later internally resolve on this trace | Does not contribute to outstanding internal work at 72 |
| S08, transferred at 68 h | Strict internal definition: known not to have an internal `Resolve` by 72 | Strict internal definition: competing `Transfer` at 68; end-to-end late resolution is unknown | Not outstanding internally at 72 because `Transfer` is an internal disposition |

The last row is the main ambiguity. If the SLA is end-to-end customer resolution rather than internal `Resolve`, S08’s status after 68 hours is not observed, so its end-to-end result by 72 is unknown rather than known compliant or noncompliant.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use the following cause codes for an Aalen-Johansen competing-risk analysis under the strict internal-success scope:

- 0: administratively censored / no terminal event observed
- 1: `Resolve`
- 2: `Withdraw`
- 3: `Reject`
- 4: `Transfer`

The fixed-horizon endpoint is not identical to the event code. For example, a case resolved at 76 has cause code 1 at 76, but is still known to have failed the “resolved by 72” endpoint.

| Observed status and timing | Event indicators | Strict internal competing-risk code | Status for \(q_R(72)\) | Status for \(q_D(72)\) | Censoring rule and sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| `resolved`, duration \(\leq72\) | `Resolve=1`, `late_resolve=0` | 1 at recorded duration | 0: timely internal Resolve | 0: terminal by 72 | Not censored |
| `resolved`, duration \(>72\) | `Resolve=1`, `late_resolve=1` | 1 at recorded duration | 1: known no Resolve by 72 | 1: no disposition at 72 | Not censored; contributes to late-resolution CIF |
| `withdrawn` | `Withdraw=1` | 2 at recorded duration | 1 under a successful-Resolve SLA | 0 if duration \(\leq72\) | Competing event, not ordinary censoring. If withdrawals are contractually SLA-exempt, redefine the eligible population before analysis rather than censoring them after observing withdrawal |
| `rejected` | `Reject=1` | 3 at recorded duration | 1 under a successful-Resolve SLA | 0 if duration \(\leq72\) | Competing event, not ordinary censoring. It can be a valid timely disposition for \(q_D\), but not a timely `Resolve` |
| `transferred`, duration \(\leq72\) | `Transfer=1` | 4 at recorded duration | 1 for strict internal Resolve; unknown for end-to-end resolution | 0: internal disposition occurred | Three alternatives: strict competing event; right-censor at transfer under an independent-censoring assumption; or external-outcome bounds/mixture assumptions |
| `transferred`, duration \(>72\) | `Transfer=1` | 4 at recorded duration | 1: no internal Resolve by 72; also no transfer occurred by 72 | 1: no internal disposition at 72 | For end-to-end scope, this conclusion assumes external processing starts at the recorded transfer; the eventual external outcome remains unknown |
| `open`, age \(<72\) | No terminal event | 0 at observed age | Unknown at 72; not a compliant case and not yet a known breach | Unknown at 72 | Right-censored at age. Retain in the risk set; do not label as timely or drop it |
| `open`, age \(\geq72\) | No terminal event | 0 at observed age | 1: known no Resolve by 72 | 1: known no disposition by 72 | Right-censored after the SLA horizon. It remains informative for the fixed-horizon endpoint even without later outcome data |

The three defensible treatments of `Transfer` are materially different:

1. **Strict internal-performance scenario.** `Transfer` is a competing terminal event. It is a known failure to achieve internal `Resolve`, though it can be a valid timely internal handoff.
2. **Independent external-follow-up scenario.** `Transfer` is right-censoring at handoff. This estimates an end-to-end Resolve process only if transfer timing and external resolution, conditional on measured history, are independent of the unobserved outcome. That is a strong and likely questionable assumption.
3. **External-outcome bounds or mixture scenario.** For transfers before 72, let \(\theta\) be the unknown fraction externally resolved by 72. Their contribution to end-to-end non-resolution is \(1-\theta\), with \(\theta\in[0,1]\). This gives transparent best- and worst-case bounds without asserting non-informative censoring.

A fourth operational convention is possible but should be named differently: count a timely `Transfer` as a successful **handoff**. That measures “handoff within 72 hours,” not “resolved within 72 hours.”

For a cause-specific hazard model, competing events remove a case from the cause-specific risk set after their occurrence. That mathematical risk-set convention should not be described as ordinary independent right censoring, and it does not make a Resolve-only Kaplan-Meier curve an estimator of actual timely resolution.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A resolved-only analysis would use 13 cases and ignore the rest of the process. Of those 13 observed resolves, nine are timely and four are late: S03 at 76, S20 at 74, S28 at 88, and S13 at 110 hours. The ratio of late resolves among resolved cases answers neither the arrival-level probability of missing the SLA nor the late-resolution cumulative incidence.

Several concrete biases arise:

- **Survivor and completion bias.** S09, S11, S25, and S26 are still open at ages 92, 81, 79, and 104 hours. Each is already known to lack `Resolve` at 72, but a resolved-only analysis excludes them. It also excludes S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31, whose ages are below 72 and whose SLA outcomes remain unknown at the cutoff.

- **Competing outcomes are not missing data.** S05 was rejected at 9 hours, S12 withdrew at 15 hours, S06 withdrew at 27 hours, and S14 was rejected at 63 hours. These cases did not reach operational success, but neither did they occupy the internal queue at 72. Treating them as resolved, dropping them, or treating them as ordinary censoring answers three different questions.

- **Transfers are outcome-dependent in plausible ways.** S21 transferred at 26 hours after escalation, and S08 transferred at 68 hours after review and escalation. It is implausible to assume automatically that their handoff was unrelated to unobserved difficulty or external resolution. S32 transferred at 96 hours, which at minimum establishes that it had no internal terminal disposition by 72.

- **Administrative censoring may be informative for extrapolation.** The extract was frozen, so censoring for open cases is administrative in one sense. However, the open cases show materially different inactivity gaps: S09 has 31 hours since its last event, S11 has 55, S25 has 48, and S26 has 72. Those gaps suggest stalled or dormant work may have a different future event distribution from actively progressing work. This does not prove informative censoring, but it weakens an unconditional independent-censoring assumption. The terminal rows have a mechanical gap of zero because the terminal event is the final recorded event, so using `last_event_gap_h` naively also leaks closure status.

- **The below-72 and above-72 open cases are not interchangeable.** S10 at 47 hours, S15 at 35, S24 at 67, S29 at 51, and S31 at 23 have not yet had the opportunity to be observed through the 72-hour endpoint. In contrast, S09, S11, S25, and S26 are observed SLA non-resolutions. Coding all nine open cases simply as “not resolved” overstates observed breach evidence; dropping all nine understates it.

- **Cohort comparison is confounded.** Cohort A has 15 cases and cohort B has 17. B contains five P1 cases while A contains three P1 cases; P1 is the highest priority and likely related to both routing and timing. The traces also show `AutoCheck` in 13 of 17 B cases but in 2 of 15 A cases, consistent with overlapping policy exposure rather than clean treatment assignment. Because B started after a policy and escalation change, a raw A-versus-B contrast also mixes priority composition, calendar time, rollout compliance, and potentially changed workload.

Under the strict internal-success endpoint, the data directly show nine timely resolves, 18 known non-resolutions by 72, and five cases whose 72-hour endpoint is not yet observed because they were open below 72 at cutoff. Those counts are descriptive evidence, not a substitute for a censoring-aware estimator.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | Appropriate use here | Important limitation or interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier | Estimate \(q_D(72)=P(T_D>72)\), treating any internal terminal disposition as the event | A Resolve-only KM that censors withdrawal, rejection, and transfer estimates a hypothetical net Resolve process, not actual timely Resolve probability |
| Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence | Primary method for \(F_R(t)\), \(F_W(t)\), \(F_J(t)\), and \(F_T(t)\) with open cases administratively censored | Requires independent administrative censoring conditional on the modeled history; transfer treatment remains a scope/sensitivity issue |
| Cause-specific hazards | Describe associations with instantaneous Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, or Transfer transitions | Competing events leave the cause-specific risk set. With 32 cases and sparse events, multivariable coefficients will be unstable |
| Subdistribution hazards | Model covariate association with the Resolve CIF directly | Fine-Gray coefficients are not causal effects and are difficult to justify with time-varying post-entry features; not appropriate as the primary analysis at this sample size |
| Restricted mean time | Estimate restricted mean time without an internal disposition, \(\int_0^{72}P(T_D>t)\,dt\), as case-hours of queue exposure | More operationally meaningful for backlog than restricted time to Resolve, because withdrawal/rejection/transfer change the process state |
| Landmarking | At, for example, 24 or 48 hours, predict remaining probability of no Resolve by 72 among cases still active | Requires state and event history known at the landmark. The supplied prefixes lack timestamps for intermediate activities |
| Multi-state model | Separate progression through Screen, AutoCheck, Review, Rework, Escalate, terminal dispositions, and perhaps external handoff | Appropriate future architecture, but transition hazards cannot be validly estimated from only whole-case duration, ordered prefix, and final inactivity gap |

**Primary analysis.** Use an Aalen-Johansen competing-risk analysis from `Submit` to 72 hours with causes Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer. In the primary scope, treat Transfer as a competing internal terminal event. Report:

- \(F_R(72)\): timely internal Resolve incidence;
- \(q_R(72)=1-F_R(72)\): no internal Resolve by the deadline;
- cause-specific CIFs for Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer;
- risk tables showing numbers still under observation before 72 and censored before 72.

This respects that S03 and S13 are late resolves, that S06 is a withdrawal rather than missing data, and that S09 is known unresolved at the deadline.

**Secondary analyses.**

- Estimate \(q_D(72)\) with Kaplan-Meier for time to any internal disposition. This is the internal backlog measure.
- Estimate \(F_R(L)-F_R(72)\) for one or more predeclared post-SLA horizons \(L\), but do not call it eventual late resolution unless follow-up is sufficient.
- Re-run the Resolve CIF under transfer-as-censor and transfer-bound scenarios.
- Present unadjusted cohort-by-priority strata descriptively. With this sample, do not fit a large adjusted model or present a cohort coefficient as a policy effect.
- If larger timestamped data become available, fit flexible cause-specific hazards with baseline covariates and properly time-varying process state, then derive standardized CIFs.

For uncertainty, report pointwise confidence intervals and risk-set sizes, but emphasize their fragility with 32 cases. Case-resampling bootstrap intervals can be used as a robustness check, while acknowledging that no resampling method fixes informative censoring or unmeasured cohort confounding.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The prefixes contain useful control-flow information, but it must be aligned to decision time.

| Candidate feature | Type | Safe use and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort | Baseline calendar/policy-period label | Descriptive adjustment variable, not randomized treatment |
| Priority | Baseline only if assigned at Submit and immutable | The extract does not prove assignment timing; verify operationally |
| Current state at a landmark | Time-varying | Use the last known nonterminal activity as of the landmark, not the final full prefix |
| Review visit count | Time-varying history | Repeated review can represent complexity or rework; it is not baseline |
| Rework count | Time-varying and post-entry | Useful for dynamic prediction after observed; inappropriate as a baseline policy-effect control without causal mediation design |
| Escalation occurrence and timing | Time-varying and post-entry | `escalated` is derived from later process history. “Ever escalated” at baseline creates immortal-time bias because a case must remain active long enough to escalate |
| AutoCheck occurrence and ordering | Time-varying and likely policy-mediated | `AutoCheck` may occur after Submit. Comparing “ever AutoCheck” against “never AutoCheck” from time zero creates immortal-time and selection bias |
| Path pattern, such as ScreenAutoCheck versus AutoCheckScreen | Dynamic sequence feature | Use only prefix history available at the prediction time; complete paths include future terminal activity and leak the outcome |
| Elapsed age | Time-varying | Essential for a dynamic SLA model; should be evaluated at the alert time |
| Last-event gap | Time-varying if recomputed uniformly at a landmark | At this extract it is asymmetric: terminal cases have gap 0 by construction, so it must not be used as a baseline or pooled outcome predictor |

For example, S03’s repeated `Review` around escalation, S07’s `Review>Rework>Review`, S13’s rework and escalation path, and S29’s `Escalate>Review` are operationally meaningful histories. They may identify cases needing intervention, but they cannot be treated as baseline causes of breach.

A proposed state structure, subject to business validation and timestamps, is:

- `Submit`  `Screen` or `AutoCheck`
- `Screen`  `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, or `Reject`
- `AutoCheck`  `Screen`, `Review`, or `Escalate`
- `Review`  `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Escalate`, or `Rework`
- `Rework`  `Review` or `Resolve`
- `Escalate`  `Review`, `Resolve`, or `Transfer`
- `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are terminal internal states

The listed arcs are grounded in at least one observed prefix, but this is a descriptive candidate graph, not proof that every business-valid transition has been observed.

For future data, model transient-state occupancy and transition-specific hazards. A case’s dynamic alert quantity could be:

\[
P(T_R>72 \mid \text{active at age }a,\ \text{observed history through }a).
\]

That is fundamentally different from a baseline model using an eventual `Escalate` flag or a complete path ending in `Resolve`.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

| Analysis | Transfer treatment | Withdrawal/rejection treatment | Censoring and cohort treatment | What could change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strict internal-success primary | Competing `Transfer` event | Competing non-Resolve outcomes; count as no successful Resolve by 72 | Open cases administratively censored at their observed age; cohort/priority reported descriptively | Measures internal operational success. Timely handoffs can make internal Resolve performance look worse even if external service is good |
| 2. Transfer-as-independent-censoring | Censor at transfer | Still competing outcomes | Assumes transferred cases would have the same unobserved end-to-end resolution distribution as comparable non-transferred cases | Can materially alter the Resolve CIF if escalated transfers are systematically harder or easier than retained cases |
| 3. External-outcome bounds or \(\theta\)-mixture | Transfers before 72 vary from all externally timely to all externally late | Same as primary | No independent-censoring claim; vary external timely-resolution fraction \(\theta\in[0,1]\) | Establishes the range of plausible end-to-end SLA risk. For this extract, S08 and S21 are the key pre-72 transfer uncertainty |
| 4. Any-disposition SLA alternative | Transfer is a timely internal disposition if before 72 | Withdrawal/rejection are also timely dispositions if before 72 | Kaplan-Meier for \(q_D(72)\) | Can reverse the operational interpretation: a process may have poor timely Resolve but acceptable timely disposition and low backlog |
| 5. Informative-censoring stress test | Use each transfer scenario above | Keep as competing risks | Compare ordinary administrative-censoring AJ with selection models or IPCW-style sensitivity assumptions based on baseline priority, observed state, and inactivity gap | The five cases open below 72 can change the estimate. Long-gap cases may be less likely to resolve than a naive censoring model assumes |
| 6. Cohort/policy comparison | Keep transfer sensitivity explicit | Keep competing outcomes explicit | Compare raw cohort curves, priority-stratified curves, and, only with sufficient data, baseline-standardized curves; do not condition naively on AutoCheck or Escalate | A raw B-versus-A conclusion can reverse after priority composition, calendar period, workload, and rollout compliance are considered |

For the informative-censoring analysis, IPCW would require a model for remaining observed through 72 based on information available before censoring. The present extract lacks exact event times, workload, start dates, and rich case attributes, so an IPCW result would be exploratory rather than credible causal correction. A simpler transparent stress test is to vary the timely-resolution probability assigned to the five cases open below 72, separately for short and long inactivity gaps.

The principal qualitative conclusions that could reverse are:

- whether transfers represent SLA failures, acceptable handoffs, or unknown external outcomes;
- whether early withdrawal/rejection is an operational failure or an acceptable timely disposition;
- whether cohort B appears better or worse after priority and calendar-period composition are considered;
- the estimated tail level if the cases censored before 72 are systematically more stalled than cases observed through the horizon.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Operational reporting should separate three dashboards rather than collapse all outcomes into one “breach rate”:

1. **Successful-resolution SLA dashboard:** timely Resolve CIF, no-Resolve-by-72 risk, and late Resolve incidence.
2. **Internal workload dashboard:** probability of no terminal disposition by 72, restricted mean active case-hours, stage occupancy, and inactivity backlog.
3. **Disposition and dependency dashboard:** withdrawal, rejection, escalation, and transfer CIFs, with transfer destination and external closure performance when available.

A dynamic alert should be based on the case’s current age and history, not on its eventual complete trace. Examples of operational actions are:

- repeated `Review` or observed `Rework`: quality support, specialist review, or documentation remediation;
- current `Escalate` state: reserve specialist capacity or investigate escalation queue delay;
- long inactivity gap while active: owner reminder, stalled-work escalation, or data-quality check;
- increasing transfer incidence: investigate handoff policy, partner capacity, and missing external outcome feeds.

These are prioritization actions, not causal claims. A high risk associated with `Escalate` does not imply that escalation causes delay; difficult cases may be escalated precisely because they were already likely to miss the SLA.

Before deploying alerts, evaluate:

- **Calibration:** predicted versus observed fixed-horizon outcomes overall and separately by cohort and priority, using temporally later data rather than the same frozen extract.
- **Competing-risk calibration:** calibrate both timely Resolve and no-disposition outcomes; a model can be calibrated for one and poor for the other.
- **Fairness and service checks:** compare calibration, false-alert rates, missed-breach rates, and intervention burden across P1/P2/P3 and cohorts A/B. Equal alert rates are not automatically appropriate if base risks or contractual priority obligations differ, but systematic miscalibration or unequal unnecessary escalation requires review.
- **Governance:** document the transfer definition, SLA exclusions for withdrawal/rejection, external-partner accountability, and the policy version active when each case entered.

With only 32 cases, this extract is suitable for process diagnosis and measurement design, not for production risk-score training.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Direct Aalen-Johansen / Kaplan-Meier fallback: no survival package required
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def aj_cif_at(frame, code_col, cause, horizon):
    """
    Aalen-Johansen CIF for one cause at horizon.
    code 0 means censoring; positive codes are mutually exclusive events.
    Returns CIF(cause), all-cause event-free survival, and a risk-set table.
    """
    x = frame[["duration_or_age_h", code_col]].copy()
    x.columns = ["time", "code"]
    times = np.sort(x.loc[x["time"] <= horizon, "time"].unique())

    survival = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    rows = []

    for t in times:
        n_risk = int((x["time"] >= t).sum())
        codes_at_t = x.loc[x["time"] == t, "code"]
        d_all = int((codes_at_t > 0).sum())
        d_cause = int((codes_at_t == cause).sum())

        if n_risk == 0:
            continue

        survival_before = survival
        cif += survival_before * d_cause / n_risk
        survival *= (1.0 - d_all / n_risk)

        rows.append({
            "time_h": t,
            "n_risk": n_risk,
            "all_events": d_all,
            "target_events": d_cause,
            "cif_target": cif,
            "event_free_survival": survival
        })

    return cif, survival, pd.DataFrame(rows)

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The most important missing data, in priority order, are:

1. **Exact event timestamps for every activity.** Ordered prefixes and whole-case duration cannot estimate stage-specific waiting times, escalation timing, transition hazards, or valid landmark states. The supplied inactivity gap gives only limited and asymmetric timing information.

2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`.** Needed are transfer destination, handoff time, external Resolve/Reject/Withdraw time, and whether the internal SLA remains accountable after handoff. Without these, end-to-end Resolve by 72 is not identified for pre-72 transfers.

3. **Actual Submit dates and extract cutoff date.** These are needed to assess delayed entry, calendar effects, seasonality, freeze-date censoring, and whether administrative censoring is plausibly independent.

4. **Time-varying workload and capacity.** Queue length, staffing, specialist availability, holidays, partner capacity, and system outages could explain both process routing and delay. They are important confounders for cohort and policy comparisons.

5. **Assignment and policy-rollout data.** Record policy eligibility, actual AutoCheck execution, policy version, escalation rules, compliance exceptions, and the reason a case did not receive the intended path. Cohort is not an adequate substitute.

6. **Baseline case attributes and priority assignment history.** Case complexity, source, geography, customer segment, issue type, required evidence, and whether priority changed after submission are needed for risk adjustment and fairness assessment.

7. **Outcome definitions and SLA policy.** Clarify whether withdrawn and rejected cases are SLA-exempt, whether a timely transfer is success, whether reopens are possible, and whether the SLA is internal-only or end-to-end.

8. **Intervention records.** To evaluate whether alerts, escalation, or AutoCheck improve outcomes, collect when interventions were applied and why. Otherwise, associations between process states and SLA risk should not be interpreted causally.
