# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** `Submit` (t = 0 for every case). All 32 rows already express time as hours since Submit, so no re-anchoring is needed.

**Horizon.** H = 72 h (the SLA). Follow-up beyond 72 h is still analytically relevant for the tail (late resolution, stalls) but 72 h is the decision horizon.

**Time scale limitation (observation).** Only two time quantities are supplied per case: `duration_or_age_h` (T) and `last_event_gap_h` (G). Therefore the only intermediate event that can be time-anchored is the *last* recorded activity, at T − G. All other activity times are unknown; this shapes what is a legitimate covariate (Section 5).

**Events and non-events.**
- Event of interest (success): `Resolve`.
- Competing terminal dispositions: `Withdraw`, `Reject` (internal, definitive; after them internal resolution is impossible).
- `Transfer`: terminal *for the internal system*, but the case may or may not be resolved externally. Its role is a modelling choice, not a fact (Section 2).
- Right censoring: `open` rows are censored at their age T at the cutoff. Their disposition and its time are unknown; they are still at risk when observation stops.
- Already-observed breach: any case with no `Resolve` by 72 h whose history covers the interval [0, 72] — resolved at T > 72, or `open` with age ≥ 72. These are **not** censored for a 72 h binary estimand; their 72 h status is known.

**Estimands (defined before estimators).**

| ID | Estimand | Formal definition | Operational decision it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Probability of not being resolved by 72 h | 1 − F_res(72), where F_res is the cumulative incidence (sub-distribution) of Resolve in the presence of competing dispositions. Population: all submitted cases. | SLA attainment reporting ("what share of submitted cases meet the SLA"), capacity and penalty exposure. |
| E2 | Cumulative incidence of resolution *after* 72 h | F_res(∞) − F_res(72): mass of cases that eventually resolve but late. Complement within "not resolved by 72": cases that never resolve internally (withdraw/reject/transfer/stall). | Recovery management: how much of the breach tail is recoverable work vs. permanently lost cases; backlog sizing. |
| E3 | Probability of having no terminal disposition at 72 h | S_any(72) = P(T_any > 72) where T_any = time to first of {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}. | Live queue governance: expected count of cases still sitting in the system at the SLA boundary; workload alerts. |
| E4 | Cause-specific risks at 72 h | F_k(72) for k ∈ {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}; they sum with S_any(72) to 1. | Diagnosing *why* cases fail the SLA (rejected early vs. withdrawn because they waited vs. transferred out) and whether the SLA definition itself should treat these differently. |
| E5 (conditional) | Landmark breach risk | P(no Resolve by 72 \| no disposition at L, state at L), L = 24, 48 h. | Real-time alerting on individual open cases (Section 7). |
| E6 | Restricted mean undisposed time | ∫₀⁷² S_any(t) dt: expected hours a case spends without a disposition inside the SLA window. | Governance metric robust to the tail, comparable across cohorts. |

E1 is the "SLA breach probability" that matches the contractual wording; E3 matches what a queue manager sees; E2 and E4 explain the composition of the breach.

**Transfer under three assumptions (all reported in Sections 6 and 8):**

| Scenario | Encoding | Reading | When it is the right assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| T_competing | code 4 at T | Transfer terminates the possibility of *internal* resolution; for the internal SLA it is a failure to resolve (and the case leaves the internal queue). | SLA is owed by the internal unit and hand-over does not stop the clock or discharge the obligation. |
| T_censored | code 0 at T | Transfer is loss to follow-up; the case is assumed to have the same future resolution hazard as comparable non-transferred cases. | External organisation resolves cases like internal ones and the SLA follows the case. Almost certainly *informative* here (all three transfers are escalated P1 cases; their future is not exchangeable with a P3 in Review). |
| T_resolved | code 1 at T | Hand-over is the successful outcome for the internal unit (clock stops). | Contract defines transfer as a compliant disposition. |

A fourth encoding — transfer as a transient state in a multi-state model with an unobserved absorbing outcome — is the honest representation but is unidentifiable without external outcome data (Section 9).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

The naive analysis takes the 13 resolved cases and reports that 4 breached (S03 76 h, S13 110 h, S20 74 h, S28 88 h): 4/13 = 30.8 % (cohort A 2/6, cohort B 2/7). Against E1 estimated on all 32 cases (0.70 by Aalen-Johansen; assumption-free bounds 0.56–0.72, Section 6) this understates breach risk by a factor of two. The mechanisms:

**Survivor / selection bias (conditioning on the outcome).** The naive denominator contains only cases that *did* reach Resolve. Cases that never reach it are exactly the ones that most often miss the SLA: S09 (open at 92 h), S11 (open at 81 h), S25 (open at 79 h), S26 (open at 104 h) and S32 (transferred at 96 h) have all provably passed 72 h without resolution and are silently deleted. A 19-case "everything else" group disappears.

**Right censoring versus already-observed breaches.** Among the 9 open cases, two groups behave completely differently for the 72 h question:
- Open below 72 h (S31 23 h, S15 35 h, S10 47 h, S29 51 h, S24 67 h): their SLA status is genuinely unknown. They are right-censored and the only source of uncertainty in E1. Dropping them assumes they resolve at the same rate as cases we did observe; keeping them in the risk set until their age (KM/AJ) uses their information without asserting an outcome.
- Open at or above 72 h (S25, S11, S09, S26): their 72 h status is *known* (breach). Calling them "censored" and excluding them from a 72 h breach rate is a coding error, not a modelling choice. They remain censored only for the question "will they ever resolve".

**Informative censoring.** Non-informative censoring means the censored case is exchangeable with the uncensored cases still at risk. Two features of the data argue against this:
- Inactivity gaps. S26 has had no event for 72 of its 104 h (last event Review at 32 h), S11 for 55 h (Escalate at 26 h), S25 for 48 h (Escalate at 31 h), S09 for 31 h (Review at 61 h). Cases that have been idle this long are plausibly in a different, lower resolution-hazard regime (lost, awaiting external input, or pending transfer) than active cases such as S24 (gap 4 h) or S15 (gap 2 h). Treating them as exchangeable with active cases *inflates* estimated eventual resolution (Section 6: eventual resolution incidence 0.61 vs. 0.46–0.47 when stalls are treated as terminal).
- Transfers. S08, S21, S32 are all P1, all escalated, and transfer is the *only* terminal outcome ever observed after `Escalate` for a P1 case other than S04/S18. If transfers are censored, the assumption is that S08 would go on to resolve like an average case at risk at 68 h — implausible. The two long-idle escalated P1 open cases (S11, S25) look like the pre-transfer pattern (Escalate followed by a long gap); their censoring is likely informative in the same direction.

**Competing risks.** Withdrawals (S06 27 h, S12 15 h, S22 44 h, S30 70 h) and rejections (S05 9 h, S23 11 h, S14 63 h) remove cases from the resolvable pool. A KM curve for "time to Resolve" that censors them estimates a hypothetical world with no withdrawals or rejections, and 1 − KM overstates the real-world probability of resolution: 1 − KM_net(72) = 0.361 vs. F_res(72) = 0.304 overall. In P1 the distortion is extreme: 1 − KM_net(72) = 0.27 (because the three transfers are censored away) vs. E1 = 0.75 (bounds 0.625–0.75). Whether a late withdrawal such as S30 (70 h, after a long Review) is an "exit" or a disguised breach is a substantive question the completed-case analysis never asks.

**Cohort imbalance.** Cohort B (17 cases) contains 5 P1 cases (S18, S21, S25, S29, S32) vs. 3 in cohort A (S04, S08, S11) and 2 of the 3 transfers. B's non-compliant cases (no AutoCheck-first: S20, S23, S26, S30) are all P3 and look bad (74 h resolution, stall at 104 h, withdrawal at 70 h), so "compliance" is confounded with priority; A's P3 cases are equally poor (S02 the only one resolved, S09 stalled, S14 rejected at 63 h). Because B started later in calendar time its cases have, in principle, less potential follow-up; in this extract the open-case ages happen to be similar (A: 35–92 h; B: 23–104 h), but the completed-case comparison "A 2/6 vs B 2/7" has no way to know that. Assignment was not randomised, so any A-vs-B difference mixes policy effect, case-mix (priority, P1 share), calendar workload, and compliance.

**Net effect.** The naive 31 % is a statement about the subgroup of cases that happened to resolve, in a world with no withdrawals, rejections, transfers, or stalls. It answers none of the estimands in Section 1.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | Estimand it targets | Fit for these data | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier on T_any (all terminal dispositions as event) | E3, E6 | Valid if open-case censoring is non-informative; 23 events, 9 censored; no competing-risk problem because *all* dispositions are the event. | Use for E3 and RMST. |
| Kaplan-Meier on Resolve with others censored | Hypothetical net resolution probability | Systematically overstates resolution (0.361 vs 0.304 at 72 h; P1 0.27 vs 0.75). Not an estimate of E1. | Report only as the "naive comparison". |
| Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence | E1, E2, E4 | Nonparametric, needs no proportional-hazards or model assumptions; handles 4 competing causes with 3–13 events each; keeps open cases in risk sets until their age. Estimates sum to 1 with S_any, which gives a built-in consistency check. | **Primary.** |
| Cause-specific hazard regression (Cox per cause) | Effect of covariates on the *rate* of each disposition | Etiologic question (does AutoCheck-first speed resolution?). With 13 resolution events at most 1 covariate is estimable; time-varying stage covariates need timestamps we do not have. | Design target for the full log; here only stratified nonparametric versions. |
| Sub-distribution hazard (Fine-Gray) | Effect of covariates on F_res(t) directly | Right target for *prediction* of breach probability, but same event-count constraint; its coefficients are not interpretable as rates. | Later, with more cases. |
| Restricted mean (RMST/RMTL to 72 h) | E6 | Robust to heavy tails and to the tail censoring problem (S26, S13); interpretable in hours. | Secondary governance metric (50.4 h undisposed within the window; 47–52 h across strata). |
| Landmarking (L = 24, 48 h) | E5 | Directly matches how an alert operates: condition on being open at L and on the state at L. Avoids immortal-time bias for post-baseline features (escalation, rework) because only what has happened *by L* is used. Constraint: we can time-anchor only the last event, so the state at L is only partially known here. | **Secondary (operational).** |
| Multi-state model (Submit → Screen/AutoCheck → Review → Escalate/Rework → dispositions) | Stage-specific transition hazards, state-occupation probabilities, E1–E4 as functionals | The most process-faithful design; requires every transition time. Not fittable on this extract beyond descriptive state-occupation at the last event. | Design of record once timestamps exist. |

**Recommendation.**
- Primary: Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence for E1/E2/E4 and KM for E3/E6, with open cases censored at age, transfers as a distinct competing cause (T_competing), stratified by priority and cohort, and always reported alongside the assumption-free bounds for E1 (Section 6). Justification: the sample is small (n = 32) and heterogeneous (4 outcomes), so a regression model would either be underpowered or force proportionality on curves that visibly cross by priority; a nonparametric estimand-first approach with bounds says exactly what the data can and cannot support.
- Secondary: landmark analysis at 24 h and 48 h, because the operational question is "for a case that is still open at L, how likely is a breach", and because conditioning on the landmark removes the immortal-time problem for escalation and rework.
- Planned extension: multi-state model with cause-specific transition hazards once event-level timestamps and post-transfer outcomes are available; Fine-Gray for breach-probability prediction with priority, intake pattern and stage-at-landmark as covariates.

Interval estimation: with these event counts, use nonparametric bootstrap over cases (stratified by cohort) for AJ point estimates, and report the bounds of Section 6 as the primary statement of uncertainty about E1.

**What is robust.**
- Breach risk at 72 h is high under every defensible encoding: between 0.56 and 0.72 for all submitted cases, and no lower than 0.50 even if transfers count as success. The naive 0.31 is outside every interval.
- P3 is the worst stratum under every encoding (0.80 with no unknown cases: S02 is the only P3 resolved within 72 h; S05 and S23 were rejected early, S12 withdrew, and the rest waited).
- Roughly one third of the probability mass at 72 h is still undisposed (E3 ≈ 0.36–0.44), and roughly 0.32 has already left via withdrawal, rejection or transfer — the SLA problem is as much about exits as about slowness.

**What could reverse.**
- P1 ranking. Under transfer = competing, P1 is the second-worst priority (0.75); under transfer = resolution it becomes the best (0.375–0.50). Whether the hand-over stops the clock is therefore the single most consequential SLA-governance decision in these data, and it is a policy question, not a statistical one.
- Cohort B "improvement". ITT shows a 2-point difference with fully overlapping bounds; per-protocol shows 0.63 vs 0.80, but the non-compliant group is entirely P3 and A's P3 cases are equally poor, so the apparent per-protocol effect is explainable by priority mix alone. With n = 17 no direction is supportable; the qualitative conclusion "the policy helps" can flip to "no effect" or "worse" by moving one or two cases.
- Size of the recoverable tail. Under non-informative censoring 30 % of cases resolve late and 61 % resolve eventually; under a stall assumption for long-idle cases only 16–17 % resolve late and 46–47 % ever resolve. The recovery-workload story (E2) is fragile; the 72 h figure (E1) is not, because all stalled cases are already past 72 h.
- Withdrawals. If late withdrawals (S30 at 70 h, S22 at 44 h) were reclassified as breach-driven exits, E1 is unchanged (they are already counted as not resolved), but the "disposition SLA" reading (E3-type success) would deteriorate; if instead withdrawals were removed from the population, E1 falls (S4-style reasoning) and the SLA looks better for reasons unrelated to process speed.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**From curves to alerts (E5).** The landmark conditional probabilities are the natural alert thresholds. In this extract, a case with no disposition at 24 h has a 60–76 % probability of breaching (25 at risk, 6 resolved in (24, 72], 4 unknown); at 48 h it is 75–88 % (16 at risk, 2 resolved in (48, 72]). Implications:
- A warning at 24 h (not 48 h or 60 h) is where intervention can still change the outcome; by 48 h the SLA is largely decided.
- Escalation of a live case should use the state at the landmark: cases in Review at 24 h with no second activity, and escalated cases with an idle gap > 12 h, are the groups where the observed tail lives (S09, S26, S10, S11, S25).
- Age ≥ 72 h and open (S09, S11, S25, S26) is a breach-management queue, not a prevention queue: the action is recovery, communication, or a formal decision (transfer, withdraw, close) — and a decision on whether idle escalated P1 cases are de facto pending transfers.

**Workload actions (E3, E6).** The expected number of cases undisposed at 72 h is Σ over the currently open cases of their conditional S_any(72 | age, state); with S_any(72) ≈ 0.38 and about 50 undisposed hours per case within the window, a steady inflow of N cases per day implies ≈ 0.38 N cases crossing the SLA boundary daily, which sizes the recovery team. Stage-specific hazards (once timestamps exist) indicate where capacity should go: prolonged Review (P3) versus post-escalation waiting (P1).

**SLA governance (E1, E4).** Report the SLA as a decomposition, not a single number: resolved ≤ 72 h (≈ 0.30), still open at 72 h (≈ 0.38), withdrawn (≈ 0.14), rejected (≈ 0.10), transferred (≈ 0.07), with explicit bounds for the open-case uncertainty. Decide and document, before any dashboard is built, whether transfer stops the clock and whether rejection within 72 h counts as compliant; Section 6 shows these choices move P1 from worst to best.

**Not causal.** A high predicted breach probability for escalated or P1 cases reflects the path they are on (and, for `escalated`, immortal time), not the effect of escalating; a lower point estimate for cohort B or for AutoCheck-first cases is not evidence that the policy works, because assignment was not randomised and compliance is confounded with priority. Risk scores drive attention and resourcing; policy evaluation requires the design in Section 9.

**Calibration.** For the landmark model, compare predicted breach probability with observed 72 h outcome among cases whose 72 h status is known, using inverse-probability-of-censoring weights for those censored before 72 h (time-dependent Brier score at 72 h), and plot observed vs. predicted within deciles or within priority × cohort cells. Recalibrate whenever the transfer or withdrawal rules change, because they change the outcome definition.

**Fairness checks.** By priority: P3 has the highest breach probability with zero unknown cases; if alerts or workload are weighted by priority, P3 breaches become invisible even though the SLA applies to them. Check that alert recall (share of eventual breaches alerted at 24 h) and precision are comparable across P1/P2/P3 and across cohorts, and that an idle-gap alert does not fire disproportionately on P1 escalated cases only because their transfer waiting time is long. Report per-stratum calibration slopes, not only pooled ones.

Self-contained: pandas + numpy + pm4py. lifelines (KaplanMeierFitter / AalenJohansenFitter)

df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(RAW), sep="|")
H = 72.0  # SLA horizon in hours from Submit

#-- ---------------- 1. Event / competing-risk encoding, three transfer scenarios ----------------
#-- code 0 = right-censored (still at risk when observation stops), 1 = Resolve, 2 = Withdraw,
#-- 3 = Reject, 4 = Transfer as its own competing outcome, 5 = 'stalled' (used only in sensitivity)
BASE = {"resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2, "rejected": 3, "open": 0}
SCENARIOS = {
    "T_competing": {**BASE, "transferred": 4},  # transfer ends internal resolution chance (SLA failure)
    "T_censored": {**BASE, "transferred": 0},   # transfer = non-informative loss to follow-up
    "T_resolved": {**BASE, "transferred": 1},   # transfer = successful hand-over, counts as resolution
}

def encode(d, scen):
    out = d.copy()
    out["code"] = out["status"].map(SCENARIOS[scen])
    return out

#-- ---------------- 2. Nonparametric estimators (Aalen-Johansen; KM is the 1-cause special case) --------
def aalen_johansen(t, code):
    d = pd.DataFrame({"t": t, "code": code}).sort_values("t")
    causes = sorted(c for c in d["code"].unique() if c != 0)
    S, cif, rows = 1.0, {c: 0.0 for c in causes}, []
    for tt in sorted(d["t"].unique()):
        n_risk = int((d["t"] >= tt).sum())          # open cases stay in the risk set until their age
        here = d[d["t"] == tt]
        for c in causes:
            cif[c] += S * int((here["code"] == c).sum()) / n_risk
        S *= 1 - int((here["code"] != 0).sum()) / n_risk
        rows.append({"t": tt, "n_risk": n_risk, "S_any": S, **{f"CIF_{c}": cif[c] for c in causes}})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def value_at(curve, h, col):
    sub = curve[curve["t"] <= h]
    if len(sub) == 0:
        return 1.0 if col == "S_any" else 0.0
    return float(sub[col].iloc[-1]) if col in sub else 0.0

def rmst_no_disposition(curve, h):
    """Area under S_any up to h = expected hours spent without a terminal disposition within the SLA window."""
    g = curve[curve["t"] < h]
    ts, Ss = [0.0] + g["t"].tolist() + [h], [1.0] + g["S_any"].tolist()
    return float(sum(Ss[i] * (ts[i + 1] - ts[i]) for i in range(len(Ss))))

def summarize(d, label):
    aj = aalen_johansen(d["t"].values, d["code"].values)
    net = aalen_johansen(d["t"].values, np.where(d["code"].values == 1, 1, 0))  # KM, others censored
    print(f"  {label:<26} n={len(d):2d} | E1 P(not resolved by 72h)={1 - value_at(aj, H, 'CIF_1'):.3f}"
          f" | E3 P(no disposition at 72h)={value_at(aj, H, 'S_any'):.3f}"
          f" | naive 1-KM_net(72)={1 - value_at(net, H, 'S_any'):.3f}"
          f" | RMST_undisposed(72)={rmst_no_disposition(aj, H):.1f}h")
    return aj

try:  # optional cross-check with lifelines; not required
    from lifelines import AalenJohansenFitter, KaplanMeierFitter  # noqa: F401
    LIFELINES = True
except Exception:
    LIFELINES = False
print(f"lifelines available: {LIFELINES} (fallback: built-in Aalen-Johansen/KM above)")

print("\n=== Estimands under three transfer scenarios, all 32 cases, open cases censored at age ===")
curves = {s: summarize(encode(df, s), s) for s in SCENARIOS}
aj = curves["T_competing"]

      round(value_at(aj, np.inf, "CIF_1") - value_at(aj, H, "CIF_1"), 3))

#-- ---------------- 3. Assumption-free bounds for the fixed-horizon estimand E1 ----------------
def bounds_e1(d, transfer_is_resolution=False):
    ok = d["status"].eq("resolved") | (transfer_is_resolution & d["status"].eq("transferred"))
    known_ok = int((ok & (d["t"] <= H)).sum())
    unknown = int((d["status"].eq("open") & (d["t"] < H)).sum())  # only these are genuinely censored at 72h
    n = len(d)
    return (n - known_ok - unknown) / n, (n - known_ok) / n, unknown

print("\n=== E1 bounds (optimistic: young open cases resolve now; pessimistic: none do) ===")

#-- ---------------- 4. Stratified analysis and sensitivity comparisons ----------------

    print(f"  L={L}h: at risk={len(r)}, resolved in (L,72]={ok}, unknown={unk} -> "
          f"[{(len(r) - ok - unk) / len(r):.3f}, {(len(r) - ok) / len(r):.3f}]")

# 9. Limitations and additional data

Ranked by how much they would change the inference:

1. **Event-level timestamps for every activity.** Without them, escalation, rework and repeated review cannot be entered as time-varying covariates or as multi-state transitions; only the last event is anchored (T − G). This is the single blocker for the landmark and multi-state designs and for removing immortal-time bias from `escalated`. It also enables a proper inactivity-gap hazard (time since last event as a running covariate).
2. **Post-transfer outcomes** (resolution and its time at the external organisation, or at least whether the case came back). This converts `Transfer` from an assumption (three scenarios spanning 0.62–0.70 for E1 and flipping P1's ranking) into an observed transition, and lets the SLA definition be applied consistently across organisations.
3. **Cutoff date and submission dates.** Needed to check that open-case ages are compatible with calendar time, to quantify differential follow-up between cohorts (B started later, so should have systematically younger open cases), to detect batch effects and weekends/holidays inside the 72 h window, and to model calendar-time trends separately from the policy step.
4. **Assignment mechanism for cohort B and rollout compliance records.** Which cases were supposed to receive AutoCheck-first and why the four P3 cases in B did not. Without this, "compliance" is a post-baseline behaviour confounded with priority; with it, one can do intention-to-treat vs per-protocol comparisons, propensity adjustment, or an interrupted-time-series design on the step change.
5. **Time-varying workload and resource data** (queue length at Submit, staff availability, concurrent open cases per reviewer). These are strong confounders of both cohort effects and stage hazards, and they are legitimate baseline/time-varying covariates for prediction because they are known at prediction time.
6. **Sample size.** 32 cases, 13 resolutions, 3 transfers. Bootstrap intervals for stratified AJ estimates will be very wide; any regression beyond one covariate is not credible. A few hundred cases with full event logs is the realistic minimum for Fine-Gray or multi-state estimation with priority, intake pattern, and stage.
7. **SLA rule details.** Whether the clock pauses (e.g., awaiting customer input), whether Reject or Transfer within 72 h counts as compliant, and whether withdrawn cases are in the denominator. These define the estimand and must be fixed before estimation.
8. **Reasons and actors for Withdraw and Reject**, and reopen events. Late withdrawals (S30 at 70 h) may be disguised breaches; reopened cases violate the absorbing-state assumption.
9. **Case attributes beyond priority** (channel, complexity, customer segment) to improve prediction and support fairness auditing across groups the current table cannot see.
