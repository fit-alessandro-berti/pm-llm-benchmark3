# 1. Data audit and estimands

All quantities below refer to opening hours, 07:00–19:00, unless explicitly stated. I use the following estimands:

- **Arrival intensity** \(\lambda\): daily `arrivals` divided by 12 opening hours, averaged over the selected days.
- **Completion intensity** \(\mu\): daily `completed` divided by 12 opening hours, averaged over the selected days.
- **WIP**: `mean_wip`, i.e. mean number of cases in the system from 15-minute snapshots. This is not a queue-length distribution; it is a point-in-time average.
- **Service behavior**: daily `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min`. These exclude waiting and are not means. I therefore do not substitute them for mean service time in queueing formulas.
- **Capacity pressure**: no utilization can be computed because mean service time is unknown. I use `mean_wip / staff_fte` and net accumulation `arrivals - completed` as rough pressure proxies.

The supplied aggregate tables are sufficient to identify regimes but not sufficient to identify a single stationary queueing model.

Major threats to naive inference:

1. **Overnight carry-over is unmeasured.** No start-of-day or end-of-day backlog is supplied, so daily `completed` cannot be matched to daily `arrivals`.
2. **Daily aggregation hides intraday timing.** Bursts, peak-hour congestion, and service interruptions matter for queueing behavior; one daily count cannot represent them.
3. **Median and p90 service times exclude waiting and are not means.** The service-time distribution is unknown, so standard \(M/G/k\)-type calculations are not defensible.
4. **The outage is incomplete.** Exact outage start/end times are not logged; only a representative hour vector suggests an approximate 11:00–14:00 interruption.
5. **The complex-case percentage may be a coding change rather than a true case-mix shift.** Without variant identifiers, this cannot be resolved.
6. **Daily rows are serially dependent.** Weekend/weekday cycles, backlog accumulation, and the intervention mean these are not independent observations.
7. **Staffing FTE is a daily mean, not an hourly schedule.** It may not align with arrival bursts.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different segmentation is a **day-type-only model**:

- **Weekdays**: all Monday–Friday dates, regardless of outage, case-mix change, or intervention.
- **Weekends**: all Saturday–Sunday dates.

This segmentation represents an operational story in which the weekly calendar is the main driver: high arrivals on weekdays, low arrivals on weekends, lower weekend staffing, and service-time differences by day.

| Segment | Days | Arrival/h | Completion/h | WIP | Complex% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| All weekdays | 20 | 9.00 | 8.60 | 34.9 | 33.4 |
| All weekends | 8 | 3.74 | 3.60 | 17.4 | 31.6 |

Strengths:

- Captures the dominant weekly seasonality.
- Simple to implement for staffing models.
- Uses many observations per segment.

Weaknesses:

- Hides the outage and the recovery bounce: 2026-04-15 completion rate is 4.83/h, while 2026-04-16 is 10.75/h; pooling them into one weekday average makes both look moderate.
- Hides the case-mix shift at 2026-04-20: weekday WIP rises from about 20 to about 44, then falls to 37 after the intervention.
- Would falsely imply that the high-mix pre-intervention period and the intervention period have the same weekday characteristics.

The primary event-driven segmentation is preferable for explaining queue behavior; the day-type-only model is preferable only if the goal is a coarse capacity-planning average.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Pooling all 28 days gives:

- Total arrivals 2,519; total completions 2,409.
- Pooled arrival rate: 7.50/h.
- Pooled completion rate: 7.17/h.
- Pooled mean WIP: 29.9.
- Pooled complex%: 32.9%.
- Pooled service median: 31.0 min; pooled p90: 67.1 min.

These pooled values obscure at least three important behaviors.

1. **Weekday/weekend composition.**  
   Weekday arrival rate is 9.00/h and WIP is 34.9; weekend arrival rate is 3.74/h and WIP is 17.4. A pooled queueing model places the center between the two modes and is correct for neither. Pooling overstates weekend congestion and understates weekday congestion.

2. **Outage and recovery.**  
   On 2026-04-15, arrivals are 105 and completions are 58, so WIP jumps to 47. On 2026-04-16, completions are 129, which looks like a productivity surge. Pooling gives a completion rate of 7.17/h, hiding a day on which completions were 4.83/h and another on which they were 10.75/h. This is a Simpson-like reversal: the average completion rate is not representative of either the outage day or the recovery day.

3. **Case-mix shift.**  
   Baseline weekdays have complex% 20.9, median service 25 min, p90 47 min, and WIP 20.3. High-mix pre-intervention weekdays have complex% 44.6, median service 35 min, p90 83 min, and WIP 44.3. Pooled complex% 32.9 hides a step change. Pooled service p90 67.1 hides the jump from 47 to 83.

4. **Staffing intervention.**  
   Pre-intervention high-mix weekdays: arrivals 9.50/h, completions 8.38/h, WIP 44.3. Intervention weekdays: arrivals 9.58/h, completions 10.08/h, WIP 37.0. Pooling these two periods produces an average WIP of about 42, masking the fact that the backlog began to drain after the intervention.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

Using the four representative hourly arrival vectors, I calculate the **Fano factor** (variance/mean) and **coefficient of variation** of the 12 hourly arrival bins.

| Representative day | Arrival mean/h | Arrival variance | Fano factor | CV |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2026-04-07 ordinary | 8.42 | 24.91 | 2.96 | 0.59 |
| 2026-04-15 outage | 8.75 | 33.19 | 3.79 | 0.66 |
| 2026-04-21 bursty | 9.58 | 88.91 | 9.28 | 0.98 |
| 2026-04-25 weekend | 4.25 | 5.02 | 1.18 | 0.53 |

All Fano factors exceed 1, so a homogeneous Poisson arrival assumption is not supported. The 2026-04-21 vector is especially bursty: peaks of 34 arrivals in 10:00–11:00 and 24 arrivals in 13:00–14:00, with quieter hours elsewhere.

The daily arrival series has a strong weekday/weekend oscillation. A simple lag-1 autocorrelation is negative because high weekdays are followed by low weekends. A lag-7 autocorrelation is more relevant, but with only 28 daily rows, it is estimated from only 21 pairs and is not stable. This is a boundary: four representative days cannot establish a full stochastic model.

A transparent change-point heuristic based on `complex_pct` places the largest shift around 2026-04-20, when complex% jumps from 23–27 to 41–47. A second smaller shift occurs at 2026-04-29 when staffing increases. The outage on 2026-04-15 is a known, externally caused change point.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law requires a stable average system, a consistent throughput proxy, and comparable WIP measurements. I use average completions per opening hour as the throughput proxy where a descriptive ratio is reported, but I call the law `not defensible` when the backlog or nonstationarity violates the steady-state condition.

| Window | Boundary stability | Throughput proxy | WIP comparability | Carry-over risk | Stationarity | Implied flow-time calculation | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pooled all 28 days | No; start/end backlog unknown | Completions/h = 7.17 | Mixed weekday/weekend | High | No | 29.9 / 7.17 = 4.17 h descriptive only | Not defensible |
| Baseline weekdays 04-06 to 04-14 | Approximate; WIP 18–23 | Completions/h = 8.30 | Comparable | Moderate | Approximately stable | 20.3 / 8.30 = 2.45 h | Approximate only |
| Outage + bounce 04-15 to 04-17 | No; WIP spikes | Completions/h = 8.31 | Comparable only as daily mean | Severe | No | 44.7 / 8.31 = 5.38 h, not a sojourn time | Not defensible |
| High-mix pre-intervention weekdays 04-20 to 04-28 | No; WIP rising | Completions/h = 8.38; arrivals 9.50/h | Comparable | Growing backlog | No | 44.3 / 8.38 = 5.29 h descriptive ratio only | Not defensible as Little’s Law |
| Intervention weekdays 04-29 to 05-01 | No; WIP falling | Completions/h = 10.08 | Comparable | Backlog decline | No | 37.0 / 10.08 = 3.67 h descriptive ratio only | Not defensible as Little’s Law |
| All weekend days | Partial; overnight closure | Completions/h = 3.60 | Comparable | Moderate | Within weekends, approximately stable | 17.4 / 3.60 = 4.83 h descriptive ratio only | Not defensible |

The implied flow-time values above are **not actual mean sojourn times**. They are simple \(L/\lambda\) ratios whose interpretation depends on a steady state that the data do not fully support.

# 7. Operational implications

- **Staffing should be segmented by regime, not pooled.** The high-mix period from 2026-04-20 to 2026-04-28 has arrivals 9.5/h and completions 8.38/h, so backlog is building. Adding one FTE during 2026-04-29 to 2026-05-01 appears to move completions above arrivals and reduce WIP from about 43 to 32. This supports temporary extra staffing when complex% is high.

- **Backlog recovery should be monitored explicitly.** The high completions on 2026-04-16 and 2026-04-17 are partly recovery work after the outage. Treating them as normal productivity would lead to overstaffing once the backlog is gone.

- **Peak smoothing should focus on the bursty hours.** Representative 2026-04-21 vector shows extreme peaks in 10:00–11:00 and 13:00–14:00. Appointment spacing or triage before those hours would reduce WIP peaks more than adding uniform capacity across the day.

- **Data collection needs timestamps.** The most useful next steps are event-level timestamps for arrivals, service start, service completion, and departure; exact outage start/end; and case variant identifiers. Without these, change-point effects and queueing laws cannot be estimated reliably.

- **Risky pooled policy example:** Using the pooled average to justify raising weekend staffing from 2.5 to 3.0 FTE when the pooled WIP appears moderate. Under a segmented view, weekend WIP is already low relative to weekday loads; the additional weekend staff would be better used during weekday peak bursts or during the high-mix period.
