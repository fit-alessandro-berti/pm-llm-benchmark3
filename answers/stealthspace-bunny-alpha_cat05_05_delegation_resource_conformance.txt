## 2. Rule Interpretation

### RR1 — Drafter/approver separation

For each case, compare the **normalized human identities** performing Draft_Change_Request and Approve_Change.

- The prohibition applies regardless of risk classification.
- A different account name does not establish a different human.
- Splitting one human’s work across aliases does not satisfy RR1.
- Conversely, a recorded “draft” generated under one person’s account by a separately authorized human would require reliable session evidence; the account label alone cannot settle that question.
- Clara’s Close_Change in CHG-501 is not prohibited by RR1: the rule does not separate the drafter from the closer.

### RR2 — Implementer/verifier separation

Compare the normalized humans performing Implement_Change and Verify_Implementation.

- RR2 is also unconditional by risk.
- A service account does not pass this check merely because it is the only recorded resource.
- Strong session attribution can make a service-account action attributable to a human, but that human must still be different from the implementer.
- Different identities establish technical separation, not necessarily the verifier’s competence.

### RR3 — High-risk approval authority

For a HIGH-risk case, approval must be performed by:

1. A QM at the event timestamp; or
2. A delegate whose delegation simultaneously satisfies:
   - effective interval;
   - change scope;
   - principal absence/unavailability where required;
   - absence of prohibited subdelegation;
   - sufficient evidence that the delegation actually existed.

Consequences here:

- Ben’s D-QM-01 approvals are provisionally supported for CHG-501 and CHG-503, and conditionally supported for CHG-506 if Ben actually performed the action.
- Asha’s direct CHG-504 approval has standing QM authority if account attribution is accepted.
- Eva has no QM authority for CHG-507 or CHG-509.
- D-QM-02 has the correct date and scope for CHG-509, but its absence condition is unproved.
- D-IC authority does not confer QM approval authority.

### RR4 — Emergency substitution

I treat RR4 as the stated emergency path: IC or valid IC-delegate approval, followed by a timely QM retrospective review. It is not a blanket waiver of RR1, RR2, or the requirement for credible emergency justification.

**Business-day convention:** Because timestamps are UTC and no alternative site timezone or business-hour calendar is supplied, count full business days after implementation, excluding weekends and configured holidays:

- The first business day after the implementation date is day 1.
- The deadline is the end of the second business day, `23:59:59 UTC`.
- A review later in the second business day is timely.

Thus:

- CHG-505: implementation Tuesday, 2026-08-11; days 1 and 2 are August 12 and 13. Review on August 14 at 16:00 is **timely**.
- CHG-510: implementation Tuesday, August 11; review on Wednesday, August 12 is **timely**.

Neither case includes the actual safety rationale or sufficient incident-link evidence in the supplied record. An `emergency=true` flag is evidence to investigate, not proof of a justified emergency by itself.

For CHG-505, Farid’s authority also requires an affirmative link to **CI-44**. “Cooling system” is not equivalent to “CI-44.”

### RR5 — Identity evidence

- Normalize known aliases before testing human separation.
- Retain unresolved raw identities rather than inventing a human.
- Service-account conformance requires reliable session-to-human attribution.
- Workstation ownership, badge entry to a room, and repeated collaboration are insufficient individually.
- Strong attribution does not cure missing substantive approval authority.

### RR6 — Suspicion versus proof

Repeated pairs, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations, and concentrated handovers are **investigation indicators**. They do not establish collusion, intent, or misconduct.

This distinction is important for CHG-503, CHG-506, and the repeated Clara–Ben pattern: each has plausible benign explanations and adverse explanations that require different evidence.

### Principal actions while on leave

The supplied rules do not say that annual or sick leave automatically revokes standing role authority.

Therefore:

- Asha may retain QM authority while on leave. Her actual work during leave may violate HR, access, or site policy without necessarily violating RR3.
- Asha’s annual leave supports the **formal-absence condition** for D-QM-01. Her later VPN activity is a contradiction to investigate, not automatic proof that the delegation was invalid at earlier events.
- Ben’s sick leave is not an express condition of D-QM-01. If Ben personally approved CHG-506, the delegation is not automatically invalid merely because he was ill. If another person used his account, the approval lacks a valid human actor.

### Can a later database entry prove an earlier delegation?

A later database entry **alone** does not prove that an effective delegation existed earlier.

Here, however, D-QM-01 has a signed PDF timestamped before both its effective interval and the relevant actions. That is materially different from a database row created only after the fact.

Accordingly:

- The later migration record is an audit-trail and data-quality issue.
- It does not, by itself, invalidate D-QM-01 for CHG-501 or CHG-503.
- Authenticity, integrity, delegation intent, and the absence of later alteration should still be confirmed.
- A site procedure requiring prospective QMS registration could create a separate procedural violation, but that requirement is not stated in RR3 itself.

## 4. Collusion and Alternative-Explanation Analysis

### Repeated Clara–Ben approval pair

**Observation:** Clara drafted and Ben approved 18 of 23 high-risk production changes in the preceding 60 days—approximately 78%.

| Benign explanation | Adverse explanation | Discriminating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| They work in the same production cell; Ben’s QM delegation and technical expertise made him a frequent authorized backup | The cell has captured approval discretion, reducing effective independence and creating a controlled exchange | Full 60-day population by drafter/approver, delegation periods, expected staffing matrix, case complexity, independent approval pool, and pre/post organizational changes |
| High-risk changes naturally follow stable technical ownership | The pair may influence drafting so that later approval becomes predictable or self-serving | Draft authorship metadata, revision history, engineering comments, requests for changes after approval, and comparison with non-Ben technical owners |
| No formal pair-rotation policy existed, so concentration was not prohibited | Concentration is itself a governance weakness even without proven collusion | Board/quality governance records, conflict declarations, rotation requirements, audit observations, and independent review sampling |

The concentration is a strong **organizational-control risk indicator**, not proof of collusion.

### Reciprocal low-risk training changes

**Observation:** Ben drafted four low-risk training changes that Clara approved. The alias map confirms these were not four unrelated account pairs.

| Benign explanation | Adverse explanation | Discriminating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Normal task coverage and reciprocal expertise between trainers and process engineers | Possible reciprocal clearing of low-risk changes or balancing of informal influence | Change content and urgency, communications, case ownership, policy-required approver categories, and whether either person actually needed the other’s approval |
| Low risk may not have required independent QM approval | Low risk does not waive RR1, and informal reciprocal approvals can become a control bypass | Workflow design, local delegations, audit interviews, and comparable training changes outside the pair |

No direct financial benefit is known. Financial benefit is not the only possible form of benefit, but its absence does not disprove collusion.

### CHG-503 shared workstation/service session

**Observation:** `svc_qms` operated on Diego’s normally assigned workstation; Diego’s badge showed room entry; there was no individual re-authentication or CCTV.

| Benign explanation | Adverse explanation | Discriminating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| An authorized independent verifier used a shared terminal while Diego was elsewhere in the room | Diego implemented and then used the service account to verify, avoiding explicit same-user SoD flags | Per-action FIDO/MFA, application audit, keyboard/session evidence, authorized proxy records, camera retention, and network/device logs |
| A legitimate service workflow lacked proper attribution controls | Someone else improperly used Diego’s account or an inherited service credential | Credential vault access, privileged-session recording, account-sharing alerts, and endpoint forensics |

This is a high-priority identity and SoD gap. It is not yet a proven RR2 violation.

### CHG-506 sick-leave/account-use conflict

**Observation:** Ben’s account approved a high-risk change one hour after sick leave began. Ben denies acting. The source was an office workstation rather than his home.

| Benign explanation | Adverse explanation | Discriminating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| An office/team account was used under an approved emergency coverage arrangement | Another employee used Ben’s identity to obtain delegated QM approval | Individual MFA, device certificates, login/session audit, badge corroboration, and team-account governance records |
| Ben personally acted despite leave | Ben’s account was compromised or misused | Detailed account statement, endpoint forensics, document interaction history, and access-policy exceptions |

An office IP does not identify the human. If Ben personally acted, the stated D-QM-01 conditions still support formal approval authority, although working while ill and any local availability policy remain separate issues.

### Asha’s leave and VPN activity

**Observation:** Asha was on leave during August 10–14. Her account had a VPN session during CHG-504, and she says she personally handled an urgent case.

| Benign explanation | Adverse explanation | Discriminating evidence |
|---|---|---|
| She was permitted to handle an urgent case while formally on leave | Her account was shared, or she worked without authorization | MFA/device ownership, VPN session audit, HR-approved leave exceptions, confirmation of the case handled, and security interview/endpoint evidence |
| A family member or colleague caused some account activity | Someone used her identity while absent to perform privileged actions | Session re-authentication, user interaction evidence, and case-specific audit trails |

The evidence does not justify either declaring the delegation invalid or declaring the CHG-504 approval unauthorized.

### Handover and working-together networks

PM4Py handover edges show workflow succession across cases; working-together edges show resources associated through cases. They do not distinguish:

- routine teamwork from deliberate influence;
- approved delegation from collusion;
- a shared service identity from its actual user;
- a benign concentration from an unusual one.

The networks should be regenerated using:

1. raw accounts;
2. normalized known human identities;
3. service-account actions excluded;
4. service-account actions attributed only where supported;
5. the complete 60-day population, rather than this selected ten-case extract.

Any alert should be a review trigger with a documented baseline, not a disciplinary score.

    sub = log[log["activity"].isin([activity_a, activity_b])].copy()

            status = "HOLD_ATTRIBUTION_REVIEW"

            status = "PASS_HUMAN_SEPARATION"

            "check": label,
            activity_a + "_human": "|".join(sorted(a_values)),
            activity_b + "_human": "|".join(sorted(b_values)),
            "status": status,
            "pm4_rejected_case": case_id not in retained_cases,
        })

## 6. Control Improvements

1. **Delegation creation**
   - Require structured, named-human delegations with exact case/category scope, risk level, effective period, reason, absence condition, and explicit subdelegation prohibition.
   - Require independent authorization of the delegation record before use.
   - Prohibit wildcard or informal “production changes” scope where feasible.

2. **Effective-time recording**
   - Store `created_at`, `approved_at`, `effective_from`, and `effective_to` separately, all in UTC.
   - Preserve immutable versions, signer identity, hash, and change history.
   - Prohibit migration jobs from silently rewriting creation timestamps.
   - If prospective registration is mandatory, distinguish late registration from substantive invalidity and require a documented exception.

3. **Principal availability**
   - Integrate leave and on-call calendars with delegation eligibility.
   - Distinguish formal unavailability from technical account activity.
   - Alert when a supposedly absent principal or unavailable delegate authenticates.
   - Require approved exceptions for working during leave and for delegate fitness/coverage.

4. **Alias management**
   - Assign an immutable canonical person identifier and maintain aliases as separate, effective-dated references.
   - Govern merges and identity corrections through independent review.
   - Store `account_used`, `person_attributed`, `acting_on_behalf_of`, and `attribution_basis` separately.
   - Reconcile HR joiner/mover/leaver records against QMS identities.

5. **Individual authentication**
   - Require phishing-resistant MFA or FIDO re-authentication for high-risk approvals and verifications.
   - Lock shared workstations to controlled kiosk mode.
   - Prohibit shared service sessions for human approval or verification.
   - Protect service credentials in a vault, rotate them, and log every privileged invocation.

6. **Emergency review**
   - Require an incident identifier, IC confirmation, and concise safety rationale before emergency execution.
   - Make the retrospective deadline calendar/timezone explicit.
   - Block same-human draft/approval and implement/verification even in emergency mode.
   - Treat a timely retrospective review as corrective evidence—not as retroactive authorization of an invalid approval.

7. **Pair rotation**
   - Establish risk-weighted rotation and a sufficient independent approval pool.
   - Review the 18-of-23 concentration and reciprocal training pattern.
   - Track concentration by cell, delegation, shift, and technical owner, without treating concentration itself as misconduct.

8. **Network monitoring**
   - Build event-time, alias-normalized networks and retain raw-account networks for sensitivity analysis.
   - Compare actual edges with expected staffing and case-flow baselines.
   - Require human review of unusual handover or reciprocal patterns; do not use a network score as an automatic disciplinary conclusion.

9. **Evidential retention**
   - Retain QMS audit history, delegation originals, migration logs, AD/VPN/FIDO logs, endpoint evidence, badge records, and available CCTV.
   - Preserve clock synchronization, timezone, chain of custody, and cryptographic hashes.
   - Set retention periods long enough to investigate later migration, identity, or emergency events.
   - Protect medical and absence information with appropriate access restrictions.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

On this deliberately selected ten-case extract, the best-supported reading is:

- **Four cases contain clear authority or segregation failures:** CHG-502, CHG-507, CHG-509, and CHG-510.
- **CHG-508 is an alias-resolved actual RR1 failure**, not an innocent false positive.
- **Two high-risk cases are primarily identity/authority attribution failures:** CHG-503 and CHG-506.
- **CHG-504 has technically valid authority if attributed to Asha**, but requires identity and leave-policy review.
- **CHG-505 could be a valid emergency substitution**, but its CI-44 linkage and emergency justification are not established.
- **CHG-501 is a supported QM delegation.**

Thus, roughly half of the cases contain clear control nonconformance, while another fifth is primarily an evidential identity problem. This is descriptive of the extract, not an estimate of plant-wide incidence or culpability. Identity/data-quality issues are substantial but do not explain away the clear failures.

The three highest-priority investigations are:

1. **CHG-510 and the CHG-505 emergency chain:** recover the original incident records, safety rationale, CI-44 linkage, Farid’s email/audit trail, and evidence of who directed Clara’s approval.
2. **CHG-503 and CHG-506 attribution:** obtain endpoint, individual MFA, session, device, badge, and authorized-proxy evidence for the service verification and Ben’s disputed approval.
3. **CHG-507 and CHG-509 approval authority:** determine whether contemporaneous valid QM authority exists and, for CHG-509, whether Asha’s required August 15 absence can be substantiated.

No operational harm or collusion is established by the supplied evidence, but the combination of high-risk approval gaps, ineffective emergency delegation, and weak human attribution is a serious systemic control concern.
