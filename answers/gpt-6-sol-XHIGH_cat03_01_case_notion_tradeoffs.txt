## 1. Analytical assumptions

A case notion specifies which object supplies the case identifier: an event enters a case only when that event has a direct E2O link to that object. Within each case, I order events by timestamp, breaking ties by event ID if necessary. A direct-follows edge says that two activities are consecutive in that projection; it does not establish causation or exclusive use of a resource. I call it **convergence** when one event enters several cases of the chosen type, inflating case-event occurrences (for example, e16 enters three Order cases). I call it **divergence** when one case interleaves work on several related objects or branches (for example, O3 contains work on P3 and P4). These are projection effects, not data errors.

A copied event is both evidence that each linked object participated and duplication if a metric purports to count distinct executions. Thus e16 really concerns O1, O2, and O3, but three Order-log copies are still one dispatch event. I would retain both distinct event IDs and case-event occurrences in reports. The E2O table is authoritative for the direct flattenings below. For a separate, clearly labeled analytical variant, I would permit typed O2O joins, such as package -> packed item -> containing order, delivery -> carried package, or payment -> settled invoice. I would retain the path and qualifier as provenance, avoid unrestricted transitive closure, and never silently replace the supplied E2O links. In particular, the O2O qualifiers distinguish M2's partial settlement from M3's completion, although their E2O activities are both “Record Payment.”

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order; shared cross-order package P3.** e13 packs P3 with I21 from O2 and I31 from O3. It appears once in each Order trace, so two apparent Pack Package executions represent one event. O2 continues e13->e16, while O3 continues e13->e14 because its other item goes into P4. Counting e13 twice is harmful for package throughput; the two traces are informative about each order's participation.

2. **Package; mixed ownership of P3.** P3's trace e13->e16->e24 is a valid physical-unit path, but by itself it cannot show that this unit combines O2 and O3. If that trace is reported as an “O2 package” and an “O3 package,” P3 is counted twice as a unit. Its e16->e24 interval is informative for P3's exposure to D2; attributing the entire duration or cost separately to both orders is context-dependent and needs an allocation rule.

3. **Order; shared cross-order package P4.** e14 packs P4 with I32 from O3 and I41 from O4; e17 and e27 likewise enter both Order cases. The e14->e17->e27 path appearing twice is one package and one delivery path. In O3, e13->e14 creates an apparent sequence of packing activities, although P3 and P4 are distinct branches. The branch history is informative; a serial packing bottleneck inferred from that edge would be harmful.

4. **Package; P3 and P4 have the same variant but different contexts.** Both yield Pack Package->Dispatch->Confirm Delivery. P3 is shared by O2/O3 and moves on D2 (e13/e16/e24); P4 is shared by O3/O4 and moves on D3 (e14/e17/e27). Variant equality hides different customer exposure and radically different dispatch-to-confirm intervals: D2 runs 11:05 to 16:00 on February 3, while D3 runs 11:15 on February 3 to 10:00 on February 4. The common variant is useful for the physical lifecycle, harmful if treated as proof of equivalent service.

5. **Order and Item; consolidated delivery D2.** e16 and e24 each appear in three Order cases (O1/O2/O3) and three Item cases (I12/I21/I31), although D2 is one delivery carrying P2 and P3. A frequency DFG based on either flattening gives multiple Dispatch->later-activity and later-activity->Confirm occurrences attributable to the same movement. This is informative as three orders or items exposed; it is harmful as a count of transport executions. In a Delivery trace, e16->e24 correctly counts once, but hides that D2 consolidated three orders.

6. **Order; split fulfilment of O1.** O1 has e11 on P1, e12 on P2, e15 on D1, e16 on D2, e23 confirming D1, and e24 confirming D2. Its Order DFG contains e11->e12, e15->e16, and e23->e24 as consecutive same-activity steps. These edges record chronological interleaving, not rework or a requirement to finish one branch before the other. Calling the 14:00 e23 confirmation “the order's delivery completion” would prematurely shorten O1's fulfilment by two hours. This is harmful for order completion, useful for seeing split service.

7. **Order and Invoice; consolidated invoice V2.** e19 is one invoice issue linked to O2 and O3, but appears twice in the Order flattening. The Order DFG gives both orders Issue Invoice->Record Payment at e22, which can misleadingly look like two invoices and two independent payment paths. The Invoice V2 trace counts issuance once but suppresses the two-order scope unless joined through V2 bills O2/O3. Duplication is harmful for invoice volume; the Order projection is informative about which customers are exposed to V2.

8. **Order, Invoice, and Payment; instalments.** V2 receives M2 at e22 and M3 at e25. Both events are labeled Record Payment, but O2O says M2 partially_settles V2 and M3 completes_settlement V2. O2 and O3 each show both records, creating four Order-case payment occurrences for two actual events. V2's e22->e25 repeated-activity edge is a true instalment sequence, not necessarily a failed or duplicate payment. A Payment flattening separates M2 and M3 into singleton traces and loses the sequence. Treating e22 as full payment is harmful; invoice-level timing and qualified links are needed.

9. **Item; fulfilment-only lifecycle.** I11 starts at e05 and ends at e23; I12 starts at e06 and ends at e24. Neither Item trace contains O1's e01 creation, e18 invoice, e21 payment, or e28 closure. Measuring “end-to-end order time” from Item traces would omit long waits after item delivery and before order closure. The truncation is harmful for order-to-cash analysis, appropriate for item delivery time.

10. **Payment; near-singleton lifecycle.** M1 has only e21, M2 only e22, M3 only e25, and M4 only e26. A Payment DFG has no edges and no within-case throughput time. The apparent one-step variant describes the observation scope, not instant financial processing. It is useful for counting payment records, harmful for a lifecycle-performance claim.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would create all six direct flattenings as diagnostic views, retain source event IDs, and label their counting units. Order answers order-facing questions such as whether O1 was split and when each O1–O4 case closed; Item answers per-item fulfilment and delay; Package answers handling of P1–P4; Delivery answers D1–D3 dispatch-to-confirm time; Invoice answers V1–V3 issuance-to-recording and, after adding O2O qualifiers, V2 settlement; Payment supports a record inventory only. A flattened DFG should be interpreted at that unit. For example, the three Item copies of e16->e24 estimate item exposure to D2's 4-hour-55-minute interval; the one Delivery copy estimates delivery execution frequency.

I would use an OC-DFG for object-type-specific direct follows and for comparing event, unique-object, and total-object frequencies, especially around Pack Package, Dispatch, Confirm Delivery, and Record Payment. An OTG and object interaction summary should expose P3's O2/O3 coupling, P4's O3/O4 coupling, D2's P2/P3 consolidation, and V2's shared billing. An ET–OT graph should check which activity types genuinely touch which object types: Close Order touches Order only; Record Payment touches Order, Invoice, and Payment, not Item or Package. Those graphs keep the direct E2O semantics visible while O2O paths explain cross-object associations. Equivalent-execution clustering could help compare repeated physical patterns, but I would cluster within a declared perspective and retain object identities; P3 and P4 have equal package activity patterns yet different orders and durations.

When findings conflict, I would first state each denominator: e16 is one event, three related orders, three related items, two related packages, and one delivery. I would then inspect the event IDs, timestamps, direct E2O links, and the relevant O2O path. If an Order DFG suggests serial dispatch at e16->e17 for O3 while the object view shows D2 and D3 as separate delivery objects, I would report both the order's chronological sequence and the separate delivery executions. An O2O-propagated analysis is a sensitivity variant, not a correction to the original relation set.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**C. Reject a single lead object for this process.**

- **Decision criteria.** A dominant lead would need to support order-to-cash timing, physical execution counts, financial settlement meaning, and cross-order consolidation without routinely changing the unit of analysis. I value coverage, low duplication, interpretable elapsed times, and preservation of shared-object structure together. Trace length alone is not a criterion.
- **Strongest supporting evidence.** Order has complete E2O event coverage, but its 42 occurrences for 31 events duplicate e13/e14, e16/e24, and V2 events. Delivery and Invoice avoid within-type duplication yet D2 combines three orders and V2 bills two. Package retains P3/P4 as units but each crosses order boundaries. Payment traces are singletons. No one of these units represents both the physical consolidation and the commercial lifecycle faithfully.
- **Strongest counterargument.** Order is a practical primary customer-facing case: every event directly names at least one order, and O1–O4 have clear Create Order and Close Order endpoints. For an order-status dashboard, it can be the operational default, with explicit counting rules for shared events.
- **Residual risk.** A portfolio makes summary metrics harder to compare, and users may still conflate order exposure with unique dispatches or invoice events. Typed joins can also over-attribute an event if paths are allowed to traverse too far, such as carrying an O3/P4 delivery event e27 into O2 merely because O2 shares V2 with O3.
- **Conditions for changing the decision.** I would choose an Order lead with secondary lifecycles if the stated objective were specifically customer order elapsed time and stakeholders accepted event-ID deduplication plus package, delivery, and invoice side views. I would choose Delivery or Invoice as a lead only for a narrower shipping or collections study, respectively. The present end-to-end objective spans all of them.

The skeletons and DFGs are diagnostics, particularly weak for Payment's one-event cases. If a PM4Py release exposes a different column name for a flattened event ID, inspect that schema before attempting distinct-ID comparisons; the code above deliberately reports only row counts from those logs. I omit equivalent-execution clustering from the block because the decision rejects a process-wide lead; a later, explicitly scoped package or delivery study could use it.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Shared-event weighting.** Compare ordinary flattened DFG frequencies with counts deduplicated by event ID and, for exposure metrics, weights of 1/k for an event linked to k cases of the chosen type. Specifically check e16/e24 at k=3 for Order and Item and k=2 for Package. Report both exposure and execution counts; do not discard participation evidence.
2. **Direct versus propagated relations.** Recompute selected summaries after typed O2O propagation, with every derived event-object link marked by its path. Check that D2's inferred order set agrees with direct e16/e24 links to O1/O2/O3, and that e27 remains confined to O3/O4 under a bounded delivery-to-package-to-item-to-order path. Reject paths that wander via V2 from O3 to O2.
3. **Change the lead perspective.** Compare Order, Item, Package, Delivery, and Invoice variants and durations for O1/P1/P2/D1/D2 and O3/P3/P4/D2/D3/V2. Record which apparently sequential same-activity edges disappear when the physical unit changes; Payment supplies a deliberate singleton baseline.
4. **OC-DFG frequency semantics.** For edges involving e13/e14, e16/e24, and e22/e25, compare event-based frequency, unique-object frequency, and total-object frequency. Explain any reversal of rankings using P3/P4's mixed ownership, D2's consolidation, and V2's two instalments.
5. **Endpoint and timing validation.** Compare O1's first versus last confirmation (e23 at 14:00 versus e24 at 16:00), D2's 4-hour-55-minute dispatch-to-confirm interval, and D3's 22-hour-45-minute interval. Explicitly decide whether a statistic measures first item delivered, all items delivered, one delivery completed, or order closed.
6. **Financial qualification.** Recompute V2 settlement timing once with activity labels alone and once with M2 partially_settles and M3 completes_settlement. Validate that e22 is not reported as completion and that two V2 payments are not transformed into four independent order-level payment executions.
