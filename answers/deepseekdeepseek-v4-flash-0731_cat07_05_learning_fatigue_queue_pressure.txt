# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern—service time falling throughout the shift while error rates first dip and then climb—cannot be explained by any single mechanism. The data reveal a dynamic interplay of case-mix, queue pressure, learning, and batching, with circadian and fatigue effects playing a secondary, often confounded, role.

At the start of each shift, the work queue is inherited from the previous period. Early-shift cases are disproportionately complex (mean complexity 0.72 in positions 1–5 versus 0.43 later) and include more high-priority items. This backlog of complex cases inflates service time (workers spend more time on them) and also raises error rates (complex work is harder). As the initial backlog is cleared, the remaining cases are on average simpler, so service time drops naturally and errors dip as well (positions 6–30). This period looks like “warm-up” or learning, but much of the improvement is simply case-mix stabilization.

From position 31 onward, queue length begins to rise again, especially after position 50, driven by incoming demand (day shift ends with a bigger queue than it started). Workers respond to growing queue pressure by reducing the time they spend per case—service time continues to fall (from 12.9 to 10.8 min). However, this speed comes at the cost of quality: error rates escalate from 2.7% to 7.9%. The quality degradation is most pronounced for high-complexity cases under high WIP (10.9% error rate versus 5.8% for low complexity), suggesting that workers are cutting corners on the hardest cases when they feel pressure to keep moving.

Batching and workarounds exacerbate this effect. Under high queue pressure, resources like Junior_H and Junior_I process Document Review in rapid consecutive batches, skipping the usual per-case checks. Similarly, the delay of Quality Check until the end of the shift creates a cascade of corrections that are themselves done quickly and therefore error-prone. Automation also contributes: Bot_Risk’s instant classifications are accepted without full human review when the queue is long, increasing the rework rate.

Fatigue and circadian rhythm do play a role, but their effects are hard to separate from queue pressure because the late shift (14:00–22:00) and night shift (22:00–06:00) naturally inherit or build high queue pressure. The night staff, who work during a known circadian low, show longer service times (15.2 min) and moderate error rates even when WIP is low—suggesting alertness dips. Yet the dominant driver of the late-shift error spike is not fatigue alone but the combination of sustained time-on-task and rising queue pressure. If fatigue were the sole cause, we would expect service time to also lengthen, not to fall.

In summary, service time declines because (1) early complex backlog is replaced by simpler cases, and (2) later queue pressure induces faster processing. Errors decline initially because complexity drops and workers settle into routine, but rise later because queue pressure forces trade-offs between speed and accuracy, especially when combined with batching, delayed quality checks, and insufficient human oversight of automation.

# 4. Intervention options

1. **Strictly cap shift length for all workers.**  
   - Expected benefit: Reduces cumulative fatigue and circadian exposure, possibly lowering late-shift error rates.  
   - Risk: Capping may exacerbate queue build-up, increasing pressure for the next shift. It ignores the fact that errors rise mainly under high WIP, not necessarily after long shifts.  
   - Monitoring: Compare error rates and service times for the same hour of day before/after cap, controlling for WIP.

2. **Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift.**  
   - Expected benefit: Avoids worker overload at shift start when they are still settling; may reduce initial errors and long service times.  
   - Risk: Complex cases delayed could become urgent later; also, the “first hour” backlog is pushed later, potentially colliding with peak demand.  
   - Monitoring: Track complexity mix per hour, error rates for complex cases, and waiting time for high-priority complex cases.

3. **Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70.**  
   - Expected benefit: Raises awareness, prompts workers to slow down and be careful, or calls for additional resources.  
   - Risk: If alert is not paired with actionable help, workers may ignore it or respond by rushing even more.  
   - Monitoring: Trigger-based analysis of service time and error rate before vs after alerts; also track alert frequency.

4. **Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias.**  
   - Expected benefit: Eliminates systematic assignment of hard cases to certain resources, making performance comparisons fairer.  
   - Risk: Random assignment ignores resource skill and case urgency, may increase complexity mismatches (e.g., juniors receiving high-complexity cases) and raise overall error rate.  
   - Monitoring: Compare case-complexity distribution per resource and global error rate; check if junior workload becomes too heavy.

5. **Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours.**  
   - Expected benefit: May reduce fatigue and improve alertness, potentially lowering errors without reducing service time too much.  
   - Risk: Breaks remove workers from queue, increasing pressure on remaining workers; may not address queue pressure root cause.  
   - Monitoring: Compare shift segments around break times; check error rate and queue length before/after breaks.

6. **Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift.**  
   - Expected benefit: Reduces end-of-shift bursts of corrections, which are error-prone; evens out workload.  
   - Risk: Errors may go undetected longer, leading to more downstream rework; also may create an incomplete loop.  
   - Monitoring: Track time to rework, rework rate, and quality-check timeliness by priority class.

7. **Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks.**  
   - Expected benefit: Provides high-quality oversight when queue pressure is highest, reducing error costs for complex cases.  
   - Risk: Cost of additional staff; may cause idle time off-peak; may also disrupt team dynamics.  
   - Monitoring: Compare error rate and rework rate during peak hours with and without extra reviewer, using time series analysis.

8. **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates.**  
   - Expected benefit: Could escalate quality focus, but likely to be counterproductive.  
   - Risk: Penalizing raw error rates ignores case-mix and queue pressure, punishing workers who are forced to process high-WIP workloads. It may also encourage hiding errors or underreporting.  
   - Monitoring: This intervention is not recommended. If implemented, it would require a risk-adjusted error metric controlling for complexity, WIP, and shift.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias:** Cases are not randomly assigned; senior workers may deliberately take complex cases early to manage risk. Any performance comparison must control for case-mix, priority, and sequence position. Raw error rates are not worker characteristics.
- **Measurement error:** Error flags may be incomplete or depend on detection effort. If quality checks are deferred or skipped under pressure, errors will be undercounted. Similarly, service time may be artificially reduced by batching, which can hide true cognitive effort.
- **Queue pressure is a management lever:** High WIP is often a consequence of staffing decisions, arrival rates, and automation throughput—not a worker’s choice. Penalizing workers for errors under high WIP shifts blame onto individuals for systemic capacity shortfalls.
- **Automation quality:** Bot_Risk errors may be accepted by humans under pressure. Penalizing humans for not catching automation failures is unfair if the automation is known to be unreliable and the queue pressure prevents review.
- **Punitive use of error rates:** Using error rates for penalties without a transparent, risk-adjusted, and fair model will demoralize staff, lead to gaming, and suppress incident reporting. It may also disproportionately affect juniors and night staff, who already face harder conditions.
- **Fatigue and circadian effects are real but not morally blameworthy:** Late-shift errors may reflect biological constraints and workload, not laziness. Interventions should address work design, not punish individuals.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should be built from event-log data and updated in near real-time. It should include at least these eight indicators:

1. **Mean service time by hour and resource group** – line chart with average service time (using `pm4py.get_service_time`) split by seniority.
2. **Average waiting time between case arrival and first activity** – from `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`, showing queue delays.
3. **Work-in-progress (WIP) at each hour** – computed as number of open cases at that timestamp; use a rolling average over 15-minute windows.
4. **Rework rate within 7 days** – percentage of cases that have a rework event (rework_flag=1) after a given activity; filter by activity family.
5. **Error rate by resource and complexity band** – error_flag proportion, normalized by case complexity score bands (low/high), to reveal hidden inequities.
6. **Complexity mix of incoming cases** – share of high-complexity cases per time window, to contextualize performance changes.
7. **Batching indicator** – number of batches detected per hour (using `pm4py.discover_batches`), and average batch size; highlight when batch frequency exceeds a threshold.
8. **Handover or queue ownership changes** – number of times a case is handled by different resources across activities (i.e., handovers), and average time between handovers; indicates workflow fragmentation.
9. **Worker-load fairness** – Gini coefficient of total service minutes per resource per shift, or coefficient of variation of active time, to detect unbalanced workloads.
10. **Queue length at shift boundaries** – to capture how each shift hands off workload to the next, aligning with the table above.

The dashboard should have drilldown capabilities by shift, resource group, activity, and complexity. Alerting thresholds should be set on high WIP (>70), error rate spike (e.g., >5% in two hours), high batch frequency, and low complexity-adjusted service time. All metrics should be computed over a rolling 4-hour window to smooth noise while remaining responsive.
