# 1. Mechanism map
- **Nominal handover reduction:** Centralizing shared services aims to reduce informal back-and-forth loops (e.g., local caseworker to embedded tech specialist) by replacing them with formal queue-based routing, theoretically decreasing total handovers per case.
- **Central queue formation:** Pooling N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech into a Technical Shared Service creates a single central queue. While it balances load (benefiting the overloaded East team), it risks creating a hidden bottleneck if the shared queue is overwhelmed by high-complexity cases and additional triage duties.
- **Specialist utilization:** Centralization increases specialist utilization (management expects +15%) by ensuring they are always fed work from the shared queue. However, pushing utilization closer to 100% increases sensitivity to variability and can exponentially inflate wait times (Kingman's formula).
- **Rework:** Standardized shared approval queues and specialist reviews may reduce rework caused by local inconsistencies, but the loss of regional customer context (e.g., language/regional accommodations) might increase rework for complex cases.
- **Local expertise loss:** Removing embedded specialists degrades early informal advice. Local caseworkers may stop developing technical skills because complex cases are routed away, leading to a deskilled local workforce over time.
- **Role clarity:** Shared approval queues can standardize decisions but obscure accountability. It becomes unclear who "owns" the case when it bounces between local intake, central tech, and central approval.
- **Resilience to absence:** Centralization improves resilience to individual specialist absences (cross-cover is easier in a shared pool compared to the current poor cross-team cover), but reduces systemic resilience if the central queue itself fails or blocks.
- **Fairness across teams/customer segments:** The East team (currently suffering from high E_Tech utilization and slow cycles) will likely see fairer, faster processing. However, North and South teams may experience degraded performance. Customers needing regional accommodations might face unfair delays due to standardized, context-free central processing.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

**Optimistic Scenario**
- **Handover pattern:** Formal handovers to the Technical Shared Service smoothly replace multiple informal local loops. Total handovers decrease.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** The central queue efficiently balances the load. Wait times for the East team drop significantly; North and South see only minor increases.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Average cycle time improves, and the 90th percentile cycle time drops as the East team's backlog is cleared.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework rates drop due to standardized, high-quality specialist input and shared approval consistency.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Clear routing rules (`required_specialty`) ensure everyone knows their scope.
- **Fairness implications:** Service levels equalize across North, South, and East teams.
- **Early warning signs:** High volume of cases successfully resolved on the first pass through the shared queue; stable utilization around 80-85%.

**Central/Base Scenario**
- **Handover pattern:** Expected management frequencies materialize (e.g., local to tech shared service at 41/100).
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** Wait times for specialists increase (from 3.2h to 4.0h as predicted) due to higher utilization and queueing effects.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Average cycle time remains stable or slightly improves, but tail latency (90th percentile) increases for complex cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework decreases for standard cases but increases slightly for regional/complex cases due to lost local context.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Some confusion arises over who owns the customer communication when central specialists request more information.
- **Fairness implications:** East team improves, but North and South teams experience slower service than before.
- **Early warning signs:** Slight increase in `reassignment_count` between local caseworkers and the shared queue.

**Adverse Scenario**
- **Handover pattern:** "Ping-pong" handovers explode. Central specialists repeatedly return cases to local caseworkers due to missing local context.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern:** The Technical Shared Service becomes a severe bottleneck. Wait times spike to 10+ hours as utilization hits 95%+.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Both average and 90th percentile cycle times degrade significantly.
- **Rework and quality pattern:** Rework skyrockets because early informal advice is gone, leading to poor initial case preparation.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Accountability collapses. Local staff blame the central queue for delays; central staff blame local staff for poor intake.
- **Fairness implications:** Customers needing regional accommodations suffer massive delays. North and South teams are penalized for a system designed to fix East's problem.
- **Early warning signs:** Rapid growth in the shared queue backlog; spike in `rework_flag` and `handover_reason` indicating "missing information".

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time
1. **Tail latency explosion:** Higher specialist utilization mathematically guarantees longer wait times for the unlucky cases in the queue (Kingman's formula). Average cycle time might look fine while the 90th/95th percentile degrades severely.
2. **High-complexity case starvation:** Complex cases might be repeatedly deprioritized or bounced back and forth between local and central teams, hiding their individual massive delays inside a large volume of fast, simple cases.
3. **Local knowledge and skill loss:** As local caseworkers stop handling technical issues, their skills atrophy. This won't show in immediate cycle times but will manifest months later as an inability to handle even basic technical triage.
4. **Fairness and regional bias:** The East team's improvement might perfectly offset the North and South teams' degradation, keeping the global average stable while masking a localized drop in service quality and customer satisfaction for specific regional segments.
5. **Accountability diffusion:** When multiple shared queues (Tech, Legal/Risk, Approval) touch a case, no single person feels responsible for the outcome. Average times might hold, but exception handling for "stuck" cases will suffer.
6. **Loss of informal exception handling:** The current embedded model allows a quick 5-minute chat to resolve a blocker. The new model requires a formal ticket and queue wait. The average might not capture the frustration and friction of losing this informal resilience.

# 5. Pilot design
- **Pilot scope:** Roll out the Technical Shared Service and Shared Approval Queue for the **East team only** initially, as they are already experiencing capacity issues and stand to benefit the most. Alternatively, pilot with a specific subset of medium-complexity cases across all teams.
- **Comparison group:** Keep North and South teams on the current embedded model as the control group.
- **Pre/post observation window:** 4 weeks of pre-pilot baseline data, followed by an 8-week pilot observation window to allow queue dynamics to stabilize.
- **Segmentation:** Analyze metrics sliced by `team` (East vs. North/South), `case_complexity` (High vs. Medium/Low), `required_specialty`, and `customer_segment` (regional accommodations).
- **Guardrail metrics:** Maximum wait time in the shared queue (e.g., must not exceed 6 hours); rework rate after shared queue handover (must not exceed 10%); SLA miss rate for high-complexity cases.
- **Staff feedback collection:** Weekly pulse surveys for local caseworkers and central specialists focusing on role clarity, perceived bottlenecking, and ease of accessing advice.
- **Rollback or adjustment triggers:** Revert or adjust if the shared queue wait time exceeds 8 hours for 3 consecutive days, if specialist utilization exceeds 95% (indicating imminent failure), or if customer satisfaction drops below 3.5/5.

# 6. Recommendation
**Recommendation: Pilot first, with strict modifications.**
Do not proceed with a full, immediate rollout. While the restructuring logically addresses the East team's bottleneck and improves theoretical resilience to absence, it carries severe risks of creating a central bottleneck, destroying local expertise in North/South, and diffusing accountability. 

Management must not infer success solely from a stable or improved average cycle time in the early weeks. An improved average may simply reflect the East team clearing its backlog, while masking the deterioration of service in the North and South, or hiding severe tail delays for complex cases.

The pilot should isolate the East team to test the shared service model. If successful, gradually integrate North and South, but maintain a "liaison" or "champion" role locally to preserve regional context and informal advice channels.
