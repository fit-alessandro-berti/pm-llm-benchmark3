# 1. Mechanism map

The proposed model can improve pooling and standardization, but it changes where coordination occurs rather than automatically eliminating coordination. The relevant unit of analysis is therefore not only the total number of handovers, but also the destination, waiting time, repeat loops, case mix, and distribution of outcomes.

| Restructuring action | Intended mechanism | Observable process-mining effect | Important qualification / distributional test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech into Technical Shared Service | Pool specialist capacity and spread East’s overload across a larger pool | Handover-of-work network becomes more centralized; Technical Shared Service has higher in-degree, out-degree, and network centrality; working-together network broadens across teams | Pooling can benefit East, where E_Tech is already at 82% utilization, but may remove useful low-friction support from North and South |
| Route all high-complexity cases to Technical Shared Service | Ensure difficult work receives specialist attention | Higher share of high-complexity cases entering specialist queue; more specialist-review variants; longer specialist service and waiting times may emerge | A higher referral rate may be good if it prevents errors, or harmful if it reflects loss of local triage skill |
| Put Technical, Legal, Risk, and difficult Quality Check work into a shared-service queue | Standardize specialist work and improve utilization | Queue concentration, growing WIP, aged work, longer p90/p95 waiting time, increasing queue-related handover reasons | Legal and Risk are already at 93% and 91% utilization. A shared queue may become a bottleneck unless capacity, priority classes, and service discipline are redesigned |
| Create a shared approval queue and remove one local supervisor | Standardize approval decisions and reduce supervisory duplication | More cases routed to shared approval; approval queue becomes a hub in handover network; role-discovery output may show fewer distinct local approval roles | Standardization can improve consistency, but unclear case ownership may produce reassignment, “chasing” handovers, and escalation loops |
| Keep Intake and simple Eligibility Review local | Preserve local responsiveness for routine work | Stable Bot_Intake  local intake and local intake  local caseworker flow; potentially lower median cycle time for simple cases | Faster simple cases can improve the average while complex cases deteriorate |
| Use routing rules for legal, risk, and security | Improve detection of regulated or specialist cases | Increased routing to required_specialty in `{legal, risk, security}`; more consistent decision paths and fewer missed specialist reviews | Legal/Risk handovers are forecast to rise from 9 to 12 per 100 cases, with median wait increasing from 9.6h to 10.5h |
| Reduce ad hoc cross-team transfers | Replace absence-cover transfers with formal shared service | Cross-team handovers may decline from 6 to 3 per 100 cases; fewer location-to-location edges | This is beneficial only if formal queue transfers do not replace them with more local-to-central-to-local transfers |
| Remove embedded specialist availability from local teams | Increase formal specialist utilization | Lower share of technical work performed locally; lower local_expertise_flag use on complex cases; more formal referrals | Local staff may lose tacit knowledge and stop resolving borderline technical issues before they enter the central queue |
| Pool specialist staff | Improve absence resilience through shared coverage | Fewer failures caused by a single local specialist absence; more stable service if capacity has slack | A central queue is also a single operational dependency. A system at very high utilization is less resilient to absences, demand spikes, or system outages |
| Standardize routing and decisions | Improve consistency and potentially fairness | Reduced unexplained variation in outcome, rework, and SLA performance after controlling for complexity, specialty, priority, and customer segment | Uniform routing is not automatically fair if regional language, customer accommodation, or location-specific needs are ignored |

## Nominal handover reduction should be tested, not assumed

The management estimate does not show an aggregate reduction in the listed handover categories:

- Current listed handovers: **343 per 100 cases**
- Proposed listed handovers: **359 per 100 cases**
- Difference: **+16 per 100 cases**, or about **+4.7%**

The proposed model reduces:

- Local intake  local caseworker: 88 to 83
- Cross-team handovers: 6 to 3

However, it increases:

- Local caseworker  technical service: 36 to 41
- Technical service  local caseworker: 31 to 36
- Local caseworker  approval: 42 to 49
- Approval  implementation: 39 to 43
- Legal/Risk routing: 9 to 12

Therefore, the likely effect is not fewer handovers overall, but fewer informal or ad hoc local/cross-team handovers and more formal handovers through centralized queues. That may still be beneficial if the formal handovers are faster, clearer, and higher quality. It is not beneficial if they add waiting, rework, or ownership ambiguity.

## Queue exposure implied by management’s estimate

Multiplying each handover frequency by its stated median post-handover wait gives a rough queue-exposure proxy:

- Current listed handover-wait exposure: about **588 hours per 100 cases**
- Proposed listed handover-wait exposure: about **722 hours per 100 cases**
- Change: about **+23%**

This is not a precise estimate of total case waiting time because medians cannot be summed as if all cases follow the same path, and activities may overlap. It is, however, a warning that the proposed routing table implies more waiting exposure unless reductions in rework, repeat loops, or unnecessary activities compensate.

## Specialist utilization and nonlinear queueing risk

A 15% utilization increase is not automatically a benefit.

- East’s E_Tech is already at 82% utilization. A literal 15% increase would put an equivalent individual workload near 94%.
- Legal is already at 93%; a literal 15% increase would exceed 100%.
- Risk is already at 91%; a literal 15% increase would also exceed 100%.

Pooling may reduce variability and use idle time in North and South to relieve East. However, queueing delay grows nonlinearly as utilization approaches capacity. Management should use service-level targets, queue-age targets, and resilience capacity rather than treating higher utilization as an unqualified success.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Exact cycle-time forecasts require arrival rates, service-time distributions, staffing schedules, queue priorities, and case mix. The scenarios below describe plausible outcomes conditional on those factors.

## Optimistic scenario: pooled capacity with retained local support

| Dimension | Forecast |
|---|---|
| Handover pattern | Cross-team absence-cover handovers decline. Central specialist and approval handovers rise, but unnecessary repeat loops and reassignments fall. The total number of logged handovers may remain flat or rise slightly, while avoidable handovers decline. |
| Queue and waiting-time pattern | Technical Shared Service has sufficient staffed capacity and separate priority classes. High-complexity cases wait no longer than current local technical cases; East’s queues improve materially. Legal/Risk capacity is protected rather than merged into an undifferentiated queue. |
| Cycle-time distribution | East’s median and p90 cycle time improve because E_Tech load is relieved. North and South remain broadly stable. Aggregate median cycle time may improve modestly without deterioration in p90/p95 performance. |
| Rework and quality pattern | Standardized specialist decisions reduce avoidable technical rework. Local technical liaison support preserves early informal advice, so referrals do not become excessive. Rework after central specialist handovers is no worse than current embedded-specialist handovers. |

| Fairness implications | East benefits most because its local specialist was overloaded. North and South do not lose performance because local knowledge is preserved through liaison roles, documentation, and escalation channels. This scenario requires more than centralization. It requires protected specialist capacity, good case notes, triage discipline, explicit ownership, and a mechanism for local staff to obtain timely technical guidance.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency can worsen while the mean improves.**  
   Automation and simpler local paths may make routine cases faster. At the same time, high-complexity cases can accumulate in shared queues. A stable mean can coexist with much worse p90, p95, p99, and SLA performance.

2. **High-complexity cases can be disproportionately harmed.**  
   The routing rule sends all high-complexity cases to the same service. If this creates priority competition or insufficient capacity, the cases most needing expertise may wait longest. Reported averages should be stratified by case_complexity and required_specialty.

3. **Local knowledge loss may not appear immediately in outcome data.**  
   North and South currently benefit from embedded expertise and regional customer conventions. The loss of informal “ask N_Tech/S_Tech first” support may initially look like better utilization, but later appear as more referrals, weaker case notes, rework, and lower local problem-solving capability.

4. **Fairness problems can be hidden by aggregate success.**  
   East may improve substantially because E_Tech is overloaded, while North and South worsen after losing embedded support. Likewise, particular customer segments or locations may experience more handbacks, waits, or poorer outcomes. Overall averages can improve while adjusted disparities widen.

5. **Learning and capability erosion are slow-moving risks.**  
   If local staff never handle difficult technical work, they may lose the ability to identify issues, prepare good referrals, or cover specialists during disruption. This is not visible in a short-term cycle-time dashboard.

6. **Accountability failures can be masked by fast completion of easy cases.**  
   Shared approval and shared specialist queues may create uncertainty about who owns customer communication, documentation, escalation, and closure. Some cases will be resolved quickly; exceptions may sit idle because each participant believes another role owns the next action.

7. **Exception handling may degrade even if standard paths improve.**  
   Standardization is most effective for repeatable cases. Cases involving unusual regulation, language needs, regional conventions, urgent customer accommodation, or incomplete evidence may require more context and iterative discussion. Rare variants and escalation loops need separate monitoring.

8. **Resilience can decline despite higher utilization.**  
   A centralized service with little slack may appear efficient in normal demand. It can fail badly during absence, peak demand, outage, or policy change. Current cross-team cover already performs poorly because case notes are inconsistent; centralization does not solve that unless documentation quality is improved.

9. **Right-censoring can create false early optimism.**  
   Soon after rollout, quick cases have closed while slow high-complexity cases remain open. Calculating cycle time only for completed cases can make the new model look better before the backlog matures. Use aging-WIP measures and survival analysis for open cases.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot scope

Use a phased pilot rather than a full organizational rollout.

1. **Phase 0: instrumentation and operating-model preparation**
   - Add queue-entry, assignment, activity-start, activity-complete, queue-exit, and case-owner timestamps.
   - Preserve `home_team` separately from the current `team` performing each activity.
   - Add a controlled taxonomy for `handover_reason`.
   - Record a named case owner and an explicit escalation owner.
   - Standardize case-note templates before relying on centralized review.

2. **Phase 1: Technical Shared Service pilot**
   - Pilot centralized technical review only for a stratified subset of eligible high-complexity technical cases.
   - Include North, South, and East so East is not treated as the sole test case.
   - Keep local technical liaison capacity, office hours, or escalation access during the pilot.
   - Do not interpret East’s improvement as proof that North and South will also benefit.

3. **Phase 2: Shared approval pilot**
   - Introduce shared approval for selected medium/high-value cases only after the technical pilot has shown stable ownership and queue performance.
   - Retain local supervisory escalation for urgent, exceptional, or customer-sensitive cases.

4. **Phase 3: Legal/Risk and difficult Quality Check**
   - Do not place Legal, Risk, Technical, and difficult Quality Check into one undifferentiated queue without demonstrated capacity.
   - Legal/Risk should have protected capacity, explicit priority rules, and service-level targets because current utilization is already very high.

## Comparison group

Use a contemporaneous comparison group rather than only a before/after comparison.

- Within each team, assign eligible cases to the new route or the current embedded route using stratified random assignment where operationally and legally safe.
- Stratify assignment by:
  - home team: North, South, East
  - case_complexity
  - required_specialty
  - priority
  - customer_segment
  - location
- If random assignment is not feasible, use a stepped-wedge rollout with matched control cases and difference-in-differences analysis.
- Keep cases that are legally required to receive specialist review outside randomization, but use them as monitored safety cohorts.

## Observation window

- Baseline: at least **8–12 weeks** of stable pre-pilot data.
- Pilot operation: at least **12–16 weeks**, with enough time for complex cases to close.
- Follow-up: at least **30–60 days** after the final pilot assignment to observe rework, reopening, and delayed outcomes.
- Exclude or separately analyze cases spanning the go-live boundary to avoid mixing old and new routing models.
- Adjust for seasonality, workload peaks, staff absence, policy changes, and demand-mix changes.

## Guardrail metrics

Pre-agree thresholds before the pilot begins. Examples include:

| Guardrail | Trigger for review or pause |
|---|---|
| High-complexity p90/p95 queue wait | Sustained deterioration versus contemporaneous control after case-mix adjustment |
| High-complexity SLA miss rate | Material increase beyond a pre-agreed tolerance |
| Rework after central specialist or approval handover | Sustained rise relative to embedded-service baseline |
| Legal/Risk queue age | Queue age approaches or breaches regulatory/service buffer |
| Queue arrivals versus completions | Arrivals exceed completions for multiple consecutive review periods |
| Reassignment_count and unclear-ownership handovers | Sustained increase, especially in shared approval cases |
| Fairness-adjusted gap | Worsening p90 cycle time, rework, SLA, or outcome gap by team/location/customer segment |
| Local expertise retention | Sharp decline in local technical participation without compensating quality improvement |
| Staff workload and resilience | Central service lacks coverage during absence or demand spikes |
| Customer-sensitive outcomes | Deterioration in satisfaction, complaint, appeal, or accommodation-related outcomes |

## Staff feedback collection

Collect structured feedback weekly during the pilot from:

- Local caseworkers
- Technical specialists
- Legal/Risk specialists
- Supervisors and approval staff
- Quality staff
- Customer-facing staff
- Staff from North, South, and East separately

Use short pulse surveys plus facilitated case reviews. Ask specifically about:

- Who owns the case at each stage?
- Whether the shared-service advice is actionable
- Whether regional/customer context is being lost
- Whether queue priorities match operational risk
- Whether staff still receive enough exposure to develop technical capability
- Which handover reasons are avoidable
- Whether documented routing differs from actual informal workarounds

## Rollback or adjustment triggers

Immediately stop new central assignments for a route if there is a regulatory, safety, or serious customer-harm event. For operational triggers, pause or reduce intake to the shared route if guardrails are breached for a pre-agreed sustained period.

Possible adjustments before rollback include:

- Add protected specialist capacity.
- Separate Legal/Risk from Technical/Quality work queues.
- Restore a local technical liaison or on-call model.
- Add queue priority classes for urgent, complex, and customer-sensitive work.
- Restore a local supervisor escalation path.
- Improve case-note requirements before queue entry.
- Tighten or relax routing rules based on observed rework and queue behavior.
- Return selected case types to embedded handling.

# 6. Recommendation

**Modify the proposal and pilot it before any full rollout.**

The restructuring has plausible benefits:

- East’s overloaded technical capacity may benefit from pooling.
- Standardized specialist and approval decisions may reduce avoidable variation.
- Formal shared services may reduce some poor-quality cross-team absence-cover handovers.
- Better routing may improve compliance for legal, risk, and security cases.

However, the proposal should not proceed unchanged because the stated estimates already imply:

- More listed handovers, not fewer: 359 versus 343 per 100 cases.
- More handover-wait exposure: roughly 23% higher using the stated frequencies and median waits.
- Additional Legal/Risk demand despite Legal and Risk already operating near capacity.
- A risk that the shared service becomes a central bottleneck and a single point of failure.
- Uneven consequences: East may benefit while North and South lose embedded local expertise.

Management should modify the design before piloting:

1. Keep a named local case owner throughout the case.
2. Establish explicit RACI rules for advice, approval, escalation, customer communication, and closure.
3. Maintain local technical liaison access and deliberate local learning opportunities.
4. Do not combine Technical, Legal, Risk, and difficult Quality Check work in one undifferentiated queue.
5. Staff and prioritize queues based on service-level and resilience targets, not a blanket 15% utilization target.
6. Instrument queue-entry, start, completion, ownership, and reassignment events.
7. Assess fairness by home team, location, customer segment, complexity, and specialty.

A broader rollout should occur only if the pilot demonstrates, over a sufficiently long outcome window, that:

- East improves without material deterioration in North or South.
- High-complexity p90/p95 cycle time and SLA performance do not worsen.
- Rework after shared-service handovers does not rise.
- Legal/Risk queues remain within safe service limits.
- Fairness-adjusted outcome gaps do not widen.
- Staff report clear ownership and continued access to technical learning.
- The central service can recover from normal absence and demand shocks.

Management should not infer success from early average cycle time. Early averages can improve because simple cases close quickly while complex cases remain open in queues. The decision should depend on tails, open-case aging, rework, fairness, accountability, learning, and resilience.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The code below is a PM4Py-style analysis outline. Exact parameter names can vary slightly by PM4Py version, so the implementation should be pinned to a tested version. The current event log has only one timestamp. To measure true service and queue waiting time, add activity-start and queue-entry timestamps; otherwise, inter-event time is only a proxy.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"

TEAM = "team"
HOME_TEAM = "home_team"
COMPLEXITY = "case_complexity"
SPECIALTY = "required_specialty"
SEGMENT = "customer_segment"
LOCATION = "location"
PRIORITY = "priority"
HANDOVER_REASON = "handover_reason"
REASSIGNMENTS = "reassignment_count"
LOCAL_EXPERTISE = "local_expertise_flag"
OUTCOME = "outcome"
REWORK = "rework_flag"

# These are recommended additional fields. They are not in the supplied schema.
START_TS = "activity_start_timestamp"
QUEUE_ENTERED = "queue_entered_at"
SLA_DUE = "sla_due_timestamp"
CASE_OWNER = "case_owner"

raw = pd.read_parquet("event_log.parquet")
raw[TS] = pd.to_datetime(raw[TS], utc=True, errors="coerce")
raw = raw.dropna(subset=[CASE, ACT, TS]).sort_values([CASE, TS])

# Preserve home team separately from the team that performs later centralized work.
intake_home = (
    raw.loc[raw[ACT].eq("Intake"), [CASE, TEAM, TS]]
    .sort_values([CASE, TS])
    .drop_duplicates(CASE)
    .rename(columns={TEAM: HOME_TEAM})
    [[CASE, HOME_TEAM]]
)

raw = raw.merge(intake_home, on=CASE, how="left")
raw[HOME_TEAM] = raw[HOME_TEAM].fillna(raw[TEAM])

# Define clean pre/post case cohorts.
# Exclude cases that straddle go-live, because they mix the two operating models.
go_live = pd.Timestamp("2025-07-01", tz="UTC")
analysis_end = pd.Timestamp("2025-12-31", tz="UTC")

case_bounds = (
    raw.groupby(CASE)[TS]
    .agg(case_start="min", case_end="max")
    .reset_index()
)

case_bounds["period"] = np.select(
    [
        case_bounds["case_end"].lt(go_live),
        case_bounds["case_start"].ge(go_live)
        & case_bounds["case_end"].le(analysis_end),
    ],
    ["before", "after"],
    default="exclude",
)

raw = raw.merge(case_bounds[[CASE, "period"]], on=CASE, how="left")
raw = raw.loc[raw["period"].isin(["before", "after"])].copy()

before_raw = raw.loc[raw["period"].eq("before")].copy()
after_raw = raw.loc[raw["period"].eq("after")].copy()


def as_pm4py_log(df):
    """
    PM4Py dataframe formatting. If start timestamps exist, retain them for
    service/waiting-time calculations.
    """
    x = df.copy()

    if START_TS in x.columns:
        x[START_TS] = pd.to_datetime(x[START_TS], utc=True, errors="coerce")
        return pm4py.format_dataframe(
            x,
            case_id=CASE,
            activity_key=ACT,
            timestamp_key=TS,
            start_timestamp_key=START_TS,
        )

    return pm4py.format_dataframe(
        x,
        case_id=CASE,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS,
    )


before_log = as_pm4py_log(before_raw)
after_log = as_pm4py_log(after_raw)


def select_full_cases(events, filters):
    """
    Select full traces using case-level attributes. This avoids fragmenting a
    trace by filtering individual events when attributes are intended to be
    case-static. Validate that such fields are actually stable within cases.
    """
    ordered = events.sort_values([CASE, TS])
    case_attrs = ordered.groupby(CASE).first()

    keep = pd.Series(True, index=case_attrs.index)
    for field, allowed_values in filters.items():
        keep &= case_attrs[field].isin(allowed_values)

    selected_ids = case_attrs.index[keep]
    return events.loc[events[CASE].isin(selected_ids)].copy()


cohorts_before = {
    "north": as_pm4py_log(select_full_cases(before_raw, {HOME_TEAM: ["North"]})),
    "south": as_pm4py_log(select_full_cases(before_raw, {HOME_TEAM: ["South"]})),
    "east": as_pm4py_log(select_full_cases(before_raw, {HOME_TEAM: ["East"]})),
    "high_complexity": as_pm4py_log(
        select_full_cases(before_raw, {COMPLEXITY: ["high"]})
    ),
    "legal_risk_security": as_pm4py_log(
        select_full_cases(

def handover_transition_table(events):
    """
    Custom transition table complements PM4Py social networks by calculating
    handovers per 100 cases and an elapsed-time proxy after a handover.

    If activity_start_timestamp is available, use:
       next activity start - previous activity completion.

    Without it, the result is only time between recorded event timestamps,
    not pure waiting time.
    """
    x = events.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()

    x["from_resource"] = x.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift()
    x["from_team"] = x.groupby(CASE)[TEAM].shift()
    x["previous_end"] = x.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift()

    if START_TS in x.columns:
        x["next_start"] = pd.to_datetime(x[START_TS], utc=True, errors="coerce")
    else:
        x["next_start"] = x[TS]

    x["is_handover"] = (
        x["from_resource"].notna()
        & x[RES].notna()
        & x["from_resource"].ne(x[RES])
    )

    x = x.loc[x["is_handover"]].copy()
    x["elapsed_after_handover_h"] = (
        (x["next_start"] - x["previous_end"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    )

    # Negative elapsed time can occur with parallel work; do not interpret it as waiting.
    x["elapsed_after_handover_h"] = x["elapsed_after_handover_h"].clip(lower=0)

    x["handover_type"] = (
        x["from_resource"].astype(str) + " -> " + x[RES].astype(str)
    )

    denominator = events[CASE].nunique()

    result = (
        x.groupby(
            ["from_team", TEAM, "from_resource", RES, "handover_type"],
            dropna=False,
        )
        .agg(
            handover_count=(CASE, "size"),
            median_elapsed_h=("elapsed_after_handover_h", "median"),
            p90_elapsed_h=(
                "elapsed_after_handover_h",
                lambda s: s.quantile(0.90),
            ),
            median_reassignments=(REASSIGNMENTS, "median"),
        )
        .reset_index()
    )

    result["handovers_per_100_cases"] = (
        100 * result["handover_count"] / denominator
    )

    return result.sort_values(
        "handovers_per_100_cases",
        ascending=False,
    )

def is_true_flag(series):
    return (
        series.fillna(False)
        .astype(str)
        .str.lower()
        .isin(["true", "1", "yes", "y"])
        .any()
    )


def make_case_metrics(events):
    """
    Create case-level metrics for cycle-time distributions, rework, outcomes,
    fairness analysis, and team comparisons.
    """
    ordered = events.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    grouped = ordered.groupby(CASE)

    metrics = grouped[TS].agg(case_start="min", case_end="max")
    metrics["cycle_time_h"] = (
        (metrics["case_end"] - metrics["case_start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    )

    metrics["rework_flag_event"] = grouped[REWORK].apply(is_true_flag)
    metrics["rework_activity"] = grouped[ACT].apply(
        lambda s: s.eq("Rework").any()
    )
    metrics["has_rework"] = (
        metrics["rework_flag_event"] | metrics["rework_activity"]
    )

    first_attrs = (
        ordered.groupby(CASE)[
            [
                HOME_TEAM,
                LOCATION,
                COMPLEXITY,
                SPECIALTY,
                PRIORITY,
                SEGMENT,
                OUTCOME,
                LOCAL_EXPERTISE,
            ]
        ]
        .first()
    )

    metrics = metrics.join(first_attrs)

    # Calculate SLA misses only if a due timestamp exists.
    if SLA_DUE in ordered.columns:
        due = pd.to_datetime(
            ordered.groupby(CASE)[SLA_DUE].first(),
            utc=True,
            errors="coerce",
        )
        metrics["sla_miss"] = metrics["case_end"].gt(due)

    # If SLA status is encoded in outcome, replace the values below with the
    # organization's validated outcome coding.
    elif OUTCOME in metrics.columns:
        sla_miss_outcomes = {"sla_miss", "missed_sla"}
        metrics["sla_miss"] = (
            metrics[OUTCOME]
            .astype(str)
            .str.lower()
            .isin(sla_miss_outcomes)
        )

    return metrics.reset_index()

)

# Role clarity / skill-mix diagnostic.
# Higher entropy is not inherently bad: it may mean healthy cross-training or
# blurred roles. Interpret it with RACI audits and staff feedback.
def resource_activity_entropy(events):
    matrix = pd.crosstab(events[RES], events[ACT])
    proportions = matrix.div(matrix.sum(axis=1), axis=0).replace(0, np.nan)
    entropy = -(proportions * np.log2(proportions)).sum(axis=1)
    return entropy.rename("activity_mix_entropy").reset_index()
```
