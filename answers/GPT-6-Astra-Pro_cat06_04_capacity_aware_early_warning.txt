## 1. Decision rule

**Use a stage-gated, capacity-constrained policy that values intervention effects—not simply predicted breach risk.** Separate three decisions: whether a case needs attention, whether this particular swarm can help, and whether helping it is preferable to using or preserving capacity elsewhere.

**Eligibility and feasibility.** A case may receive a swarm only while it is before `Diagnosis Complete`, has an actionable diagnostic question, and has enough remaining time for the swarm to plausibly improve the contractual resolution outcome. Having 45 minutes before the deadline is not sufficient: necessary resolution and validation work must also remain feasible. Recheck eligibility immediately before dispatch; never delay diagnosis completion to retain swarm eligibility.

Check the intervention ledger, not just alert history. A second alert is not a second swarm. A previously untreated case can remain eligible despite unchanged evidence; a previously treated case normally cannot receive another swarm without a materially new diagnostic indication.

These are **working correction bands, not confidence intervals**. Moreover, calibration against observational outcomes does not establish risk under “no new swarm” if historical cases already received selective interventions.

Let \(p_i^0\) denote breach risk without an additional swarm and \(\delta_i\) its incremental absolute risk reduction. A scenario can use \(p_i^1=p_i^0-\delta_i\), subject to valid probability bounds, but the supplied predictions do not identify either causal quantity. In particular, do **not** multiply the absolute reduction by `p_now`, and do not assume that correcting a probability automatically corrects the intervention-effect estimate.

**Economic screening and portfolio evaluation.** For the initial economic ranking, calculate

$$
D_i^L=L_iV_i-450-F_i,
\qquad
D_i^M=M_iV_i-450-F_i,
$$

where \(L_i\) and \(M_i\) are the supplied lower and midpoint effects, \(V_i\) is breach-avoidance value, and \(F_i\) is false-alarm burden.

I conservatively charge the **full listed false-alarm burden on every treated case**. This avoids relying on uncertain counterfactual false-alarm probabilities. If the burden applies only when a case would not otherwise breach, a less conservative calculation would use \((1-p_i^0)F_i\). Successful treatment must not itself be classified as a false alarm.

For a selected set \(A\), assess:

$$
G_s(A)=
\sum_{i\in A}\left(\delta_{is}V_i-450-F_i\right)
-\sum_{j\notin A}h_{js}(A)V_j,
$$

where \(h_{js}(A)\) is the additional breach risk imposed on untreated case \(j\) in scenario \(s\). Track expected breach counts and non-monetary obligations alongside this monetary quantity. The supplied lower effects are planning inputs—not guaranteed causal lower bounds—so also stress-test nonresponse.

**Allocation rule.** Normally allocate at most three slots, preserving one reserve. For today, protect one allocatable slot for a feasible, imminent-deadline case selected primarily by urgency and plausible breach reduction rather than financial value. Use the remaining two for strong conservative economic opportunities, after accounting for queue exposure and evidence quality. This protected access allocation is an explicit policy choice, not a mathematically optimal consequence of the data.

Before releasing the portfolio, require positive midpoint economic value under the **upper base spillover estimate**, positive midpoint net breach reduction, and review of any serious harm to particular untreated cases. Missing queue information prevents unconditional approval; it does not justify assuming zero externality. An exception for a verified obligation requires recorded authorization.

Keep one persistent action card per case. New notifications or repeat treatment require more than a refreshed probability: verified information must change the diagnostic action, expected swarm usefulness, or operational urgency. An unexplained score jump initiates a data check, not automatic priority.

## 3. Expected-impact table

The table assumes **all three selected swarms are delivered**. Adaptive cancellation can change both benefits and costs.

The six spillover-exposed cases are not identified. Therefore, their financial exposure cannot be reconstructed from the displayed cases. For auditable calculations, make the following explicit approximation: the same six untreated cases are exposed throughout the three swarms, and incremental risks add without saturation. Let

$$
W=\sum_{j=1}^{6}V_j
$$

be their combined breach-avoidance value, in euros. With per-slot, per-case spillover \(q\),

$$
\text{additional expected breaches}\approx 3\times6\times q,
\qquad
\text{economic spillover harm}\approx3qW.
$$

This calculation does not require independent outcomes. Congestion dependence does, however, prevent interpreting these figures as independent-trial confidence intervals. Actual dispatch calculations must use the changing queue and cap probabilities appropriately.

**Direct arithmetic.** Midpoint effects for I-04, I-09, and I-07 sum to

$$
0.19+0.24+0.26=\mathbf{0.69}.
$$

Their midpoint gross avoided-breach value is

$$
0.19(60{,}000)+0.24(20{,}000)+0.26(5{,}000)
=\mathbf{€17{,}500}.
$$

Total conservatively charged false-alarm burden is \(€2,000+€800+€50=€2,850\). Intervention cost is \(3\times€450=€1,350\). Thus midpoint direct net benefit **before spillovers** is **€13,300**.

Because “at least one” permits multiple nonresponders, the table also includes complete nonresponse. The breach-count and monetary endpoints represent different responder combinations, not one common trajectory. The additional adverse \(q\) assumption is a sensitivity test, **not a claimed bound**; delay minutes alone cannot establish it.

**A useful release boundary.** Under the upper base spillover, monetary value remains positive only if

$$
13{,}300>0.09W
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
W<€147{,}778,
$$

or an average exposed-case value below approximately **€24,630**.

For illustration only, if the six exposed cases average €20,000, the monetary net is approximately **€9,700–€16,600 optimistic**, **€2,500–€9,700 base**, and **−€22,200 to −€5,400 under the adverse stress**. That average is not an observed fact.

I-07’s individual midpoint direct margin is only €800. Its marginal monetary value becomes negative when six-case spillover exceeds that amount. Thus the portfolio’s positive base value must not be misrepresented as evidence that every selected swarm is individually profitable.

This is a **conditional base-case policy with a fairness allocation**, not robust dominance across scenarios. A decision-maker demanding nonnegative value under complete nonresponse would allocate fewer or no swarms. Without scenario probabilities and untreated-case values, there is no defensible unique expected-value optimum.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

**One action card, not one work item per refresh.** Key the card by case, diagnostic stage, and evidence version. Refresh risk, deadline slack, and priority every 30 minutes and after material events, but preserve the original eligibility time. Alert counts do not increase priority, while prolonged eligible waiting can trigger an aging review.

**Notification cool-down.** Adopt a 90-minute notification cool-down as an initial policy parameter. Identical evidence remains suppressed beyond that period unless urgency or circumstances change; expiry of the cool-down does not itself justify another message. Crossing a latest-safe-start boundary, receiving an actionable new diagnostic event, or correcting a serious data fault can trigger earlier review.

**Repeat-treatment suppression.** Normally permit only one swarm per case. Another swarm requires a documented new diagnostic indication, an estimate of the *additional* benefit after the previous swarm, and senior approval relative to untreated alternatives. Do not reuse the original effect range automatically. Neither persistent high risk nor the passage of 30 minutes satisfies this exception.

**I-05 versus I-07.** I-05’s unchanged prefix warrants suppression of duplicate alerts, not removal from consideration. If it has never received a swarm, the original opportunity remains live and can rise in priority as its deadline approaches. If it already received one, the absence of new evidence normally blocks another.

I-07 has a genuinely new input, so reopen the diagnostic assessment despite `p_now` falling from 0.75 to 0.72. However, an uploaded file is not automatically a new treatment indication: the owner must identify what it changes. The prompt does not establish whether I-07 previously received a swarm, so the ledger check is essential.

**Escalation and exit.** Escalate cases approaching their last feasible intervention time to the incident lead. Escalation can mean alternative help or explicit acceptance of residual risk; it does not guarantee a swarm. After treatment, review diagnostic progress, the next valid prediction, and queue conditions without treating a score decrease as proof of causal success. Remove the swarm action card at diagnosis completion, resolution, or documented loss of usefulness.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**Financial value can systematically dominate access.** A policy based solely on euros would repeatedly favor large enterprise cases over lower-value SME incidents. The protected urgency allocation gives I-07 access today. For repeated operation, start with one of every three discretionary allocations available to a non-monetary lane, provided a useful candidate exists. Select within that lane using urgency, independently assessed diagnostic need, plausible breach reduction, and eligible waiting time—not alert count.

After two allocation reviews in which a feasible case remains unserved, require an owner review of continued waiting. This is not a guaranteed swarm, but it prevents quiet starvation. Report foregone economic value transparently rather than disguising fairness as profit maximization.

**Sparse language-support data should not become an exclusion mechanism.** I-09 should receive neither automatic preference nor an automatic uncertainty penalty solely because its group is underrepresented. Apply a common substantive evidence check, provide appropriate language support for the diagnostic review, and monitor decision reversals, delay, and outcomes for this context. Pool information cautiously while retaining group-specific uncertainty. When genuine equipoise exists, include these cases in controlled evaluation instead of permanently withholding treatment and perpetuating the evidence gap.

**Differential calibration requires differential correction, not different standards of care.** Apply the supplied SME and Strategic Enterprise corrections consistently. Audit whether equally urgent, similarly actionable cases receive different access after correction. Do not assume that Public Sector status alone creates a mandatory swarm entitlement, or that Strategic Enterprise overprediction establishes low treatment benefit.

**Treatment changes the labels used for future learning.** A high-risk case that avoids breach after a successful swarm is not necessarily a false positive. Naively retraining on its observed non-breach can teach the model to underestimate precisely the cases that benefit from treatment. Record treatment history and distinguish prediction under the operating policy from estimated risk without an additional swarm. Preserve the full candidate set, including untreated eligible cases and reasons for nonselection.

**Spillovers can reverse apparent fairness gains.** Treating one SME case may be inequitable if senior interruptions disproportionately delay many untreated SME cases. Record queue exposure and waiting-time changes by segment, language context, and their intersections. Evaluate **treated benefits and untreated harms together**. If comparable untreated groups repeatedly bear materially greater interruption exposure, reduce swarm intensity, change its timing, or provide compensating diagnosis capacity. Equal treatment rates alone are insufficient.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

**Operational monitoring, at every dispatch.** Record the stage, event prefix, deadline slack, feature availability, model and calibration versions, effect range, diagnostic indication, prior swarms, candidate ranking, allocation lane, queue snapshot, expected spillovers, and selection or rejection reason. Record actual swarm start/end times, specialist participation, diagnostic progress, and any override.

Track after-stage swarms, duplicate treatments without new indications, missed useful windows, canceled bookings, alert volume, suppressed duplicates, and time from eligibility to review and intervention. The contractual resolution milestone must define the target; do not automatically substitute `Closed` if the contract uses an earlier milestone.

**Predictive monitoring on mature outcomes.** Assess calibration, Brier score or log loss, and discrimination by segment, stage, deadline horizon, and data-quality status. Separate treated and untreated outcomes, recognizing that the untreated subset is itself selectively chosen. Do not present calibration on treated outcomes as calibration of untreated risk.

Use case-level or appropriately clustered uncertainty estimates; repeated prefixes from the same incident are not independent cases. For sparse language-support data, report uncertainty and pooled estimates rather than unstable point estimates. Cases whose deadlines have not matured must not be labeled non-breaches merely because no breach has yet been observed.

**Causal-effect and spillover estimation.** Begin controlled evaluation among eligible cases where either immediate treatment or ordinary care is genuinely acceptable. Randomize timing or assignment within such groups, recording assignment probabilities. Preserve required escalation for cases with overriding obligations.

Individual randomization alone is insufficient when treatment also affects the shared queue. Use a two-stage design where feasible: vary swarm intensity across comparable queue-time blocks, then randomize eligible cases within a block. Account for congestion carryover between blocks. This supports separate estimates of direct treatment effects, untreated spillovers, and the **total effect of operating the policy**.

Analyze assigned treatment as well as delivered treatment, retain cancellations, and follow all cases exposed to the queue. Estimate effects by stage, diagnostic blocker, remaining time, segment, and prior treatment—not just by risk score. Where randomization is unavailable, use logged treatment propensities and sensitivity analyses, while acknowledging that unmeasured assignment reasons can still bias estimates. Replay alone cannot establish the counterfactual queue trajectory.

**Capacity and congestion monitoring.** Track senior minutes diverted, ordinary diagnostic throughput, waiting-time distributions, deadline slack among untreated cases, queue exposure, reserve utilization, and opportunities lost while reserving capacity. The two-specialist scheduling assumption implies 90 specialist-minutes per swarm; the €450 cost already includes labor and coordination, so do not charge those wages twice.

Before each dispatch, forecast marginal queue delay and risk. Pause further discretionary releases when updated conditions move outside the base congestion envelope—for example, predicted marginal aggregate delay above 20 minutes per slot—or when a particular untreated obligation is threatened. Observed waiting time is not automatically attributable to a swarm; causal attribution requires the comparison design above.

**Fairness monitoring.** Measure timely treatment access among comparably eligible cases, eligible waiting time, human overrides, repeated deferrals, disruption burden, and untreated interruption exposure. Review both group averages and intersections. A starting review trigger could be a sustained access ratio below 0.8 relative to comparable cases, but sample uncertainty and case mix must accompany it. Small groups still require individual case audits even when statistical evidence is inconclusive.

**Rules for changing the policy.** Apply the known calibration corrections now. Review mature cohorts weekly and investigate, for example, residual calibration error exceeding five percentage points across two cohorts when uncertainty supports a material problem. A feature failure or severe drift requires immediate fallback rather than waiting for that review.

For the ordinary economic lane, the relevant break-even effect is approximately

$$
\delta_i^*=
\frac{450+F_i+\text{marginal queue harm}+\text{capacity opportunity cost}}
{V_i}.
$$

Raise this effect hurdle or reduce the slot cap as congestion worsens. Reduce reliance on a subgroup effect estimate when controlled evidence contradicts it, but first distinguish genuine treatment heterogeneity from calibration or implementation problems. Revise the protected allocation when access or untreated-harm audits show it is failing its purpose.

Treat negative total-policy value as a reason to reduce or suspend discretionary deployment, not merely to retune the prediction model. When evidence is inconclusive, limit deployment and continue controlled evaluation rather than claiming either success or failure. Change reserve policy using observed qualified arrivals, displaced opportunities, and queue harm, with versioned approval.

**The governing outcome is timely resolution across the whole portfolio—including cases that never receive a swarm—not the success rate of the selected cases alone.**
