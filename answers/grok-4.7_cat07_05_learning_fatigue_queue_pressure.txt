# 2. Integrated explanation

The shift begins with yesterday’s and the previous shift’s unfinished work. The first events are slower because they are harder: mean complexity 0.72, high-priority share 41%, queue still elevated at 46, and seniors disproportionately picking up complex carryover after night and weekend backlogs. A modest warm-up sits on top of that mix. By positions 16–30, complexity has fallen to 0.54, the queue to 33, service time to 13.6 minutes, and errors to their low of 2.5%. That early improvement is what learning would look like, and the junior low-WIP contrast (5.1% → 4.4%) is the clearest piece of it. It is also what clearing a hard backlog looks like.

The second half of the shift does not continue that story. From positions 31–50 to 76+, mean complexity only edges from 0.50 to 0.43 and the high-priority share from 25% to 19%, so the remaining work is easier on the measured case mix. Service time still falls, from 12.9 to 10.8 minutes, but errors rise from 2.7% to 7.9% and 7-day rework from 5.0% to 13.7%. The queue moves from 31 to 62 to 88 over the same stretch. Easier work would be expected to shorten service time somewhat and to lower errors. The observed combination — still faster, and substantially less accurate — is the signature of throughput being pulled down by congestion, with batching on top for some junior document review and with Quality Check released as an end-of-shift burst.

The WIP × complexity table shows the same split inside mix bands. Low-WIP, low-complexity work takes 12.7 minutes with a 2.1% error rate. High-WIP, low-complexity work takes 9.4 minutes with a 5.8% error rate. High-WIP, high-complexity work takes 16.8 minutes with a 10.9% error rate. On these coarse margins, complexity and high WIP each raise errors, and the high/high cell (10.9%) sits about three points above a simple sum of the two separate increments (2.1 + 2.1 + 3.7 = 7.9). That interaction is descriptive — activity and resource mix remain inside the cells — and it marks the operational danger zone: hard cases handled while the system is already over WIP 70.

Experience changes the size of the effect, not the direction. Under low WIP, later-in-shift error rates stay at the first-20 level for seniors, mid-level staff, and night staff. Under high WIP, the same later work produces 4.9%, 6.8%, 12.4%, and 8.2% respectively. Juniors are exposed both to a harder early error rate (5.1%) and to the worst pressure penalty. Seniors remain the most stable, and they also speed up, so their shorter later service times are compatible with rhythm, easier residual cases, and some rushing, without a large quality collapse until WIP is high.

Shift identity modifies both mechanisms. The early shift inherits a queue of 61 and a first hour that is 48% high complexity, then hands over a queue of 42; its last two hours are 4.2% errors and 12.7 minutes. The day shift inherits 38, builds to 75, and finishes at 7.5% errors and 10.9 minutes. The late shift inherits the day backlog (70) and stays there (66), with a 6.8% late error rate. The night shift starts lighter (29), faces the most complex first hour (56%), works more urgent exceptions, and stays slower (15.2 minutes) with a 5.1% late error rate. Time-on-task and clock time are real background conditions. They do not line up with the quality break as closely as the queue path does: the early and day shifts are both at the end of a full shift in their last two hours, and those two hours look different because the queues are going in different directions.

Detection timing adds a further layer. Quality Check bunched in the last 45 minutes will timestamp defects at the congested end of the shift even when the defect was introduced earlier. Bot_Risk classifications corrected more often above queue 70 add automated failures, and human review of those classifications, to the same window. Seven-day rework moving in parallel with the error rate means there is downstream harm, not only a relabeling of when the defect was noticed. A fair reading is stacked: case mix and some warm-up dominate the first part of the shift; queue pressure, experience, and batching dominate the quality reversal; circadian load and exception mix are most plausible for the night shift’s persistently slower handling; measurement at Quality Check and after the bot can exaggerate how late the defect was created.

# 3. Proposed analyses

Use event-level service time (`time:timestamp` minus `start_timestamp`) as the speed outcome. Use `error_flag` and 7-day rework as separate quality outcomes, and always re-attach both to the producing activity as well as to the event that recorded them. Keep Bot_Risk out of human service-time averages. Keep `queue_length_at_start` and `wip_at_start` as distinct regressors.

**Within-resource comparisons.** Demanding each resource against their own baseline removes seniority, typical assignment, and stable skill from the contrast. The question for each person is how their own service time and error rate change with sequence position once complexity, priority, activity, queue, and WIP are held constant.

**Within-shift position.** Estimate the position curve inside shift, then interact position with WIP band. The early decline and the post-50 reversal should be estimated separately rather than forced into one linear slope.

**WIP and queue stratification.** Recompute service time, error, and 7-day rework by sequence bin inside low / medium / high WIP, and again by queue bands. Mutually adjust the two congestion measures. Compare the early shift’s last two hours with the day shift’s last two hours at matched complexity.

**Complexity adjustment.** Standardize each sequence bin and each shift hour to a common distribution of `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, and `concept:name`. Report the unadjusted curve and the mix-adjusted curve. Model the assignment itself: complexity and priority as outcomes of resource group, shift, sequence position, and queue at shift start, so senior early selection is measured rather than assumed.

**Shift and time-of-day controls.** Include shift fixed effects and hour-of-day, and run the overlap contrasts (09:00–14:00 early vs day; 14:00–17:00 day vs late). A circadian effect is an hour effect that survives shift and elapsed-time controls. A fatigue effect is an elapsed-time effect that survives hour, WIP, and queue controls.

**Batching detection.** Discover sequential, simultaneous, and end batches, then quantify what share of Junior_H / Junior_I day-shift Document Review, and of end-of-shift Quality Check, those batches represent. Re-estimate the headline curves with batched events removed.

**Resource-experience interactions.** Interact experience group with WIP, with sequence position, and with complexity. The prediction from the tables is a small position slope for errors at low WIP, and a steep WIP slope that is largest for juniors.

**Rework and error downstream.** Separate three clocks: when the case was handled, when an error flag was written, and when rework occurred within seven days. Performance-DFG and path-duration filters should show which upstream activities feed Rework Correction, and whether those paths are concentrated in high-WIP producing events. Long cases that cross a shift boundary should be analyzed as carryover, because their complexity shows up in the next shift’s first hour.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

df = pd.read_csv("claims_events.csv")
df["start_timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"], utc=True)
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
df["shift_start_time"] = pd.to_datetime(df["shift_start_time"], utc=True)

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)

# Case-level sojourn pieces and demand context. Event service time is separate.
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df)
temporal = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id_column="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_column="time:timestamp",
    activity_column="concept:name",
)
df = df.merge(temporal, left_index=True, right_index=True, how="left")

df["svc_min"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
df["hour"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
df["human"] = df["org:resource"].ne("Bot_Risk")

# Attach each case's rework / error back to the producing human event,
# not only to the Quality Check that happened to record it.
case_quality = (
    df.groupby("case:concept:name")
    .agg(case_error=("error_flag", "max"), case_rework=("rework_flag", "max"))
    .reset_index()
)
df = df.merge(case_quality, on="case:concept:name", how="left")

human = df[df["human"]].copy()

def wip_band(x):
    if x < 30:
        return "low"
    if x <= 70:
        return "medium"
    return "high"

human["wip_band"] = human["wip_at_start"].map(wip_band)
human["pos_bin"] = pd.cut(
    human["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, 10_000],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
)

# 1. Unadjusted and mix-relevant position curves
position_curve = (
    human.groupby(["shift_id", "pos_bin"], observed=True)
    .agg(
        n=("svc_min", "size"),
        svc=("svc_min", "mean"),
        err=("error_flag", "mean"),
        rework7=("case_rework", "mean"),
        complexity=("case_complexity_score", "mean"),
        queue=("queue_length_at_start", "mean"),
        wip=("wip_at_start", "mean"),
        hipri=("priority", lambda s: (s == "high").mean()),
    )
)

# 2. Within-resource contrast: each person vs their own low-WIP mid-shift baseline
base = (
    human[human["wip_band"].eq("low") & human["pos_bin"].isin(["16-30", "31-50"])]
    .groupby("org:resource")["svc_min"]
    .mean()
    .rename("svc_base")
)
human = human.merge(base, on="org:resource", how="left")
human["svc_vs_self"] = human["svc_min"] - human["svc_base"]

# Experience x WIP x timing (matches the manager table, with complexity held in view)
exp_table = (
    human.groupby(["resource_experience_months", "wip_band"])
    .agg(
        svc=("svc_min", "mean"),
        err=("error_flag", "mean"),
        complexity=("case_complexity_score", "mean"),
        n=("svc_min", "size"),
    )
)

# 3. Direct standardization of error and service time to the global
#    complexity x priority x activity mix, within shift and position bin.
mix_cols = ["case_complexity_score", "priority", "concept:name"]
# Bin complexity before weighting so sparse scores do not explode weights.
human["cx_band"] = pd.qcut(human["case_complexity_score"], q=4, duplicates="drop")
target = (
    human.groupby(["cx_band", "priority", "concept:name"], observed=True)
    .size()
    .div(len(human))
    .rename("w_target")
)
curves = []
for keys, g in human.groupby(["shift_id", "pos_bin"], observed=True):
    obs = (
        g.groupby(["cx_band", "priority", "concept:name"], observed=True)
        .size()
        .div(len(g))
        .rename("w_obs")
    )
    cell = (
        g.groupby(["cx_band", "priority", "concept:name"], observed=True)
        .agg(svc=("svc_min", "mean"), err=("error_flag", "mean"), n=("svc_min", "size"))
    )
    m = cell.join(obs, how="left").join(target, how="left").dropna(subset=["w_target"])
    m["w"] = m["w_target"] / m["w_obs"].clip(lower=1e-6)
    curves.append({
        "shift_id": keys[0],
        "pos_bin": keys[1],
        "svc_raw": g["svc_min"].mean(),
        "err_raw": g["error_flag"].mean(),
        "svc_std": (m["svc"] * m["w"]).sum() / m["w"].sum(),
        "err_std": (m["err"] * m["w"]).sum() / m["w"].sum(),
    })
standardized = pd.DataFrame(curves)

# 4. Overlap sample for circadian vs elapsed time
overlap = human[
    human["hour"].between(9, 13)
    & human["shift_id"].isin(["Early", "Day"])
]

log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

# Activity service times. Compute human Risk Classification separately:
# Bot_Risk is instantaneous and would dominate a pooled mean.
svc_by_activity = pm4py.get_service_time(log)
human_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, "org:resource", ["Bot_Risk"], level="event", retain=False
)
svc_human = pm4py.get_service_time(human_log)
rework_by_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)

# Long cross-shift cases vs short same-shift cases
long_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(log, 8 * 3600, 14 * 24 * 3600)
rework_paths = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    log,
    ("Quality Check", "Rework Correction"),
    0,
    7 * 24 * 3600,
)

# Day-shift window, then batches and charts
day_log = pm4py.filter_time_range(
    log, "2026-01-01 09:00:00", "2026-06-01 17:00:00", mode="events_contained"
)
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(day_log)
pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(
    day_log, "dotted_day.png", attributes=["org:resource", "concept:name"]
)
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(
    log, "events_by_hour.png", distr_type="hours"
)

perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(human_log)
# Cluster trace variants so night exception paths can be compared with day routine paths
variants = pm4py.cluster_log(log)

# Statistical layer (statsmodels or a mixed model), one row per human event:
#   svc_min, error_flag, case_rework
#   ~ C(pos_bin) * C(wip_band) + queue_length_at_start
#   + case_complexity_score * C(wip_band)
#   + C(priority) + C(concept:name) + C(shift_id) + C(hour)
#   + C(resource_group) * C(wip_band)
#   + C(org:resource)          # fixed effect
# Fit service time by OLS/cluster-robust SE on case id.
# Fit error and 7-day rework by logistic regression with the same controls.
# Repeat after dropping events that discover_batches marks as sequential batches,
# and after dropping Quality Check events in the last 45 minutes of a shift.
```

Read the models in this order. If the position coefficient on service time shrinks sharply once complexity and priority are included, the early slowdown is mostly case mix. If the error coefficient on positions 51+ and 76+ shrinks once WIP and queue are included, the late quality break is mostly congestion. If an hour-of-day effect remains inside the overlap windows after elapsed time and queue are included, that remainder is the circadian piece. If juniors’ early error gap remains at low WIP after complexity adjustment, that remainder is consistent with warm-up or skill. If removing batches and end-of-shift Quality Check events removes the error spike, the spike is a detection and batching artifact; if the spike remains, and 7-day rework remains, the quality loss is in the work itself.

# 4. Intervention options

**1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers.**  
Expected benefit: fewer events in the 76+ bin, where raw errors are 7.9% and 7-day rework is 13.7%.  
Risk: those events are bad mainly because the queue is ~88 and WIP is high. The early shift already runs to a full end-of-shift window at 4.2% errors while draining its queue. A hard cap moves unfinished work to the next crew, and the next crew’s first hour is already the complex-carryover window (early 48%, night 56%). Handover queue and cross-shift cases would rise.  
Monitor: error and 7-day rework by position bin inside each WIP band; queue at shift start and shift end; share of cases touched by two shifts; service time in the receiving shift’s first hour.

**2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift.**  
Expected benefit: a shorter first hour on paper, because the first hour is genuinely complex.  
Risk: this fights the pattern that currently protects quality. Early complexity coincides with the better error rates (3.4% down to 2.5%), and seniors already take hard carryover when WIP is more manageable. High complexity under high WIP is the 10.9% cell. Pushing hard cases into the day-shift peak would place them in that cell. The first hour should be protected capacity for complex work, not emptied of it.  
Monitor: complexity and high-priority share by shift hour; error rate in the high-complexity × WIP>70 cell; senior vs junior complexity mix; 7-day rework on cases deliberately delayed past the first hour.

**3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70.**  
Expected benefit: the alert targets the region where low-complexity errors rise from 2.1% to 5.8% and high-complexity errors reach 10.9%, and where Bot_Risk corrections become more common above queue 70.  
Risk: an alert with no paired action becomes noise. If the response is to rush, skip review, or batch harder, service time will fall further and errors will rise. Pair the alert with a capacity response (senior pull, intake throttle, or deferral of new low-priority arrivals) and with a stop on opening extra sequential batches.  
Monitor: minutes spent above WIP 70 and above queue 70; error rate, 7-day rework, and service-time median inside that region before and after; bot-correction rate; arrival rate vs finish rate so a demand spike is visible separately from slower handling.

**4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias.**  
Expected benefit: fairer comparisons across people, because current assignment puts complex early work onto seniors and can leave juniors with a different mix.  
Risk: the current selection is also a quality control. Seniors’ high-WIP error rate is 4.9%; juniors’ is 12.4%. Random allocation of complex carryover to juniors would make the measurement cleaner and the process worse. Adjust the metric for mix, and assign by skill on purpose.  
Monitor: complexity and priority by resource before and after; overall and junior error rates on high-complexity cases; 7-day rework; senior idle time during peaks (a sign that skill is being withheld from the queue).

**5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours.**  
Expected benefit: possible relief if a time-on-task effect remains after congestion is accounted for, particularly for the night shift’s slower late window.  
Risk: the final two hours of the day shift are the demand peak (queue ending at 75, errors 7.5%, service time 10.9 min). Pulling capacity out of that window can raise WIP further. Under low WIP, later errors are already flat, so a break aimed at “fatigue” will often be applied where the fatigue main effect is not visible. Pilot it where elapsed time is high and WIP is still low, and separately test a peak-hour staffing response.  
Monitor: last-two-hour error and service time stratified by WIP; queue trajectory on pilot days; handover queue; 7-day rework. A useful break lowers errors inside the same WIP band.

**6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift.**  
Expected benefit: smoother last 45 minutes and a higher apparent finish rate for the current shift.  
Risk: Quality Check is already being deferred to the end of the shift, and that workaround bunches corrections and muddies when defects were made. Moving checks across the handover delays detection, lengthens the rework path, and dumps work onto a first hour that is already carrying complex backlog. Throughput would look better because defects would be counted later, not because the work got better. Keep quality checks inside the shift. If the last-45-minute burst is the problem, spread checks through the shift at low-WIP moments rather than exporting them.  
Monitor: lag from producing activity to Quality Check; share of checks done in the last 45 minutes; 7-day rework; next-shift first-hour queue, complexity, and service time; escaped-defect rate on cases closed without a same-shift check.

**7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks.**  
Expected benefit: this matches the worst observed window. The day shift builds from queue 38 to 75 and ends at 7.5% errors and 10.9 minutes. Seniors have the lowest high-WIP error rate (4.9%) and already take hard cases successfully when they have the chance. An extra senior at the day peak, triggered by WIP or by the afternoon arrival surge, adds capacity where finish rate is losing to arrivals.  
Risk: one person cannot absorb an arrival surge by themselves. If the extra senior is simply given the hardest residual cases without authority to slow intake, their own high-WIP error rate will rise toward the senior high-WIP figure and the queue will remain. Define the trigger (WIP approaching 70, or day-shift hour with rising arrival rate) and the action (review, unblock, and throttle low-priority intake).  
Monitor: day-shift queue at 15:00 and at shift end; last-two-hour error and 7-day rework; waiting time; complexity handled by the surge senior; junior high-WIP exposure; arrival vs finish rate during the peak.

**8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates.**  
Expected benefit: none that these tables can support. Late-shift raw error rates mix queue pressure, inherited backlog, case mix, batching, bot corrections, Quality Check timing, and experience.  
Risk: penalties would fall on juniors (later high-WIP errors 12.4%) and on whoever is scheduled into the day-shift peak, including people assigned the harder residual work. The predictable responses are avoiding complex cases, deferring or gaming error flags, narrowing recorded rework, and shifting burden to the next shift. That worsens selection bias and measurement quality together. Use the late-shift rate as a system signal, stratified by WIP and complexity, not as an individual sanction.  
Monitor: if any individual feedback is used, restrict it to mix-adjusted, WIP-stratified rates with a minimum event count, and watch for a drop in complexity accepted by penalized resources, a rise in handover queue, and a change in error-flag timing. Those are the gaming indicators.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

A worker-level error rate is a property of the queue they were given, the cases they were assigned, the hour they were rostered, and the way defects are recorded. Seniors look fast and accurate partly because they are skilled and partly because they take complex work at moments when they can still do it carefully. Juniors look worse in the raw late-shift numbers because their high-WIP error rate is 12.4% and their early rate is already 5.1%, under an assignment policy they do not control. Night_L and Night_M handle fewer, more exceptional cases and will lose a leaderboard that ignores that mix.

Selection runs through the whole log. High-priority share falls from 41% to 19% and complexity from 0.72 to 0.43 as the shift progresses. Comparing a senior’s first five events with a junior’s 80th event compares different work. Random assignment would make the comparison fairer only by making the process harsher for less experienced staff. The fair analytic response is to adjust for complexity, priority, activity, and WIP, and to publish rates at a level where the denominator is large enough to mean something.

Measurement error is structural. An error flag written at a Quality Check in the last 45 minutes attributes the defect to the congested end of the shift even when Document Review or Eligibility Decision earlier produced it. Sequential batching makes short service times look like skill. Bot_Risk’s instantaneous classifications lower the average for Risk Classification and, when humans correct the bot above queue 70, can put the bot’s miss on the human reviewer’s error flag. Seven-day rework is the cleaner harm measure, and it should be traced to the producing event’s conditions.

Queue pressure is largely an operating choice. The day shift starts at a queue of 38 and ends at 75 because arrivals outrun staffing. The late shift inherits that backlog. WIP above 70 is the region management can see and staff. Treating the resulting errors as individual fatigue or individual carelessness relocates a capacity problem onto the people working the peak.

Automation quality needs its own indicator. Corrections of Bot_Risk rise in the same high-queue region as human errors. Folding those corrections into human scores both overstates human failure and hides a model that may be weakest on the cases that arrive when the queue is already long.

Punitive use of raw late-shift error rates is not justified by this evidence. The early shift’s last two hours and the day shift’s last two hours are both “late,” and they do not look alike. Any individual metric used for coaching has to be stratified by WIP, complexity, and activity, kept separate for bot-originated corrections, and paired with the person’s assigned mix. Sanctions based on the unadjusted rate will drive case avoidance and under-reporting, which makes the next round of data less honest and the queue less fairly shared.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Refresh at least daily, with a within-shift view updated every hour during the day shift. Slice every indicator by shift, hour, sequence-position bin, resource group, activity, WIP band, and complexity band. Show the system rate first and the resource-level rate only with a minimum volume and with mix and WIP attached. Keep Bot_Risk on its own series.

| # | Indicator | Definition | Why it is on the board | Alert to investigate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Service time | Median and 90th percentile of `time:timestamp − start_timestamp`, human events only, by activity | Tracks the speed curve without letting Bot_Risk’s zero times distort Risk Classification | Median in a WIP band falls more than ~15% vs the prior 4-week baseline for that activity and mix |
| 2 | Waiting time | Case waiting time from `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, plus wait before each activity | Separates queue delay from handling speed, so a fast service time cannot hide a long wait | Median wait rising while service time is falling (rushing under a growing queue) |
| 3 | WIP and queue | `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` at shift start, shift end, and share of events above 70; time above each threshold | These are the congestion measures that mark the quality break | WIP > 70 or queue > 70 sustained; day-shift end queue approaching 75 |
| 4 | Rework | 7-day rework rate, plus `get_rework_cases_per_activity`, linked to the producing event’s WIP and position | Downstream harm, less dependent on the moment Quality Check happened to run | 7-day rework in the 76+ bin or high-WIP band above the early-shift baseline (~5%) |
| 5 | Error rate | `error_flag` raw, and the same rate standardized for complexity, priority, and activity; bot-originated corrections split out | Quality alongside speed; standardization stops easier late work from flattering the rate | Mix-adjusted error rising inside a WIP band; high-complexity × high-WIP cell nearing 10% |

Use one performance DFG per shift, built on human events, with median delay on edges into Rework Correction and Close Case. Use a dotted chart for the day shift colored by resource, and an events-distribution chart by hour, so batches and the afternoon volume peak are visible next to the tables. The board is working when a rise in indicator 5 or 4 can be read against indicators 3, 6, 7, and 10 in the same hour, and when no individual’s raw late-shift error rate is displayed without WIP, complexity, and assignment context.
