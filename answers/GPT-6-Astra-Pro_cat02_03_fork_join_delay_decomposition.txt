# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

All durations below are in **minutes**. Calculations use only the supplied cases; counterfactual scheduling rules are explicitly labeled assumptions.

**Measurement assumption:** Treat the supplied `*_start` fields as actual activity starts. Define service as the elapsed start-to-end interval. Calling this *active* service additionally assumes no unlogged suspension, preemption, or internal waiting; completion timestamps alone would not establish that.

For case \(c\) and branch \(i\in\{A,B,C\}\), let:

$$
a_c=\text{arrival},\quad r_c=\text{fork release},\quad
s_{ci}=\text{branch start},\quad e_{ci}=\text{branch end},
$$

$$
f_c=\text{final start},\qquad g_c=\text{final end}.
$$

The decomposition is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_c &= r_c-a_c &&\text{upstream elapsed time},\\
Q_{ci} &= s_{ci}-r_c &&\text{branch pre-start waiting},\\
S_{ci} &= e_{ci}-s_{ci} &&\text{branch service interval},\\
D_{ci} &= Q_{ci}+S_{ci}=e_{ci}-r_c &&\text{branch completion offset},\\
J_c &= \max_i e_{ci} &&\text{join-ready timestamp},\\
T_c &= J_c-r_c=\max_i D_{ci} &&\text{elapsed fork–join stage},\\
W_{ci} &= J_c-e_{ci}=T_c-D_{ci} &&\text{branch synchronization waiting},\\
Q^F_c &= f_c-J_c &&\text{post-join waiting},\\
S^F_c &= g_c-f_c &&\text{final service},\\
L_c &= g_c-a_c &&\text{total case flow time}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, “queue” is an operational interpretation of pre-start waiting—not proof that a resource was busy. Under the stated AND-join rule, the **non-double-counting elapsed-time identity** is:

$$
\boxed{L_c=U_c+\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})+Q^F_c+S^F_c.}
$$

Equivalently, following **any one branch** through its synchronization wait:

$$
\boxed{L_c=U_c+Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci}+Q^F_c+S^F_c.}
$$

The critical-branch set is:

$$
K_c=\{i:e_{ci}=J_c\}.
$$

A tied maximum must retain every tied branch; selecting one representative for display does not make it uniquely critical.

**Why synchronization waits cannot all be added to elapsed time.** Each branch’s queue, service, and synchronization wait already spans the whole fork–join stage:

$$
Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci}=T_c.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\sum_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci})=3T_c,
$$

not \(T_c\). Moreover, synchronization intervals overlap. For F02, A waits during 09:15–09:25 and C during 08:50–09:25. Their sum is 45 **branch-minutes**, although their union occupies only 35 elapsed minutes—and that interval is already inside the 60-minute fork–join stage.

Synchronization waiting describes a completed branch’s output awaiting the others. It does **not** establish that the branch’s resource remains occupied.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

**B is the strongest observed controller of join readiness and the most promising branch-level flow-time target. A’s longer service does not make it the most influential branch.**

In the next table, service and queue summaries are **mean / median / range**; completion-offset summaries are **mean / P90 / maximum**.

| Branch | Service summary       | Queue summary     | Completion offset \(Q+S\) | Last-completing frequency | Mean synchronization wait |
| ------ | --------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------: | ------------------------: |
| A      | 50 / 50 / 50–50       | 0 / 0 / 0–0       | 50 / 50 / 50              |              2/12 = 16.7% |                     11.00 |
| B      | 37.50 / 37.50 / 30–45 | 19.17 / 20 / 5–30 | 56.67 / 74 / 75           |              8/12 = 66.7% |                      4.33 |
| C      | 20.17 / 20 / 18–30    | 10.42 / 5 / 5–35  | 30.58 / 53 / 57           |              2/12 = 16.7% |                     30.42 |

**Average and tail influence.** When B is critical, its mean completion offset is 65 minutes: 25 minutes of queue plus 40 of service. It controls both 75-minute fork–join stages, F07 and F12, which also have the two longest total flows, 130 and 125. C controls two exceptions, F04 and F08; administrative work is not inherently outside the critical path.

A supplies a persistent **50-minute stage floor** unless A itself is accelerated. This matters after improving B, but differs from saying A presently causes most avoidable delay.

C’s large synchronization wait usually means it finishes early. Ranking branches by synchronization-wait magnitude would therefore give a misleading answer.

**Marginal influence rather than service magnitude.** Define the exclusive critical margin:

$$
M_{ci}=
\left[D_{ci}-\max_{j\ne i}D_{cj}\right]^+.
$$

If branch \(i\)’s completion advances by \(\delta_{ci}\), while other branch completions remain fixed, its direct join-readiness benefit is:

$$
\boxed{\Delta J_{ci}=\min(\delta_{ci},M_{ci}).}
$$

This also handles ties: accelerating only one co-critical branch gives zero benefit while another remains at the old maximum.

| Branch | Mean exclusive margin across all cases | Maximum exclusive margin | Mean join gain from a hypothetical 10-minute completion advance |
| ------ | -------------------------------------: | -----------------------: | --------------------------------------------------------------: |
| A      |                                   1.25 |                       10 |                                                            1.25 |
| B      |                                  10.00 |                       25 |                                                            6.25 |
| C      |                                   1.00 |                        7 |                                                            1.00 |

The exclusive-margin mean is a **single-branch acceleration ceiling**, holding all other branches fixed—not an additive allocation of total stage time or a proposal to omit mandatory work.

The defensible ranking is **B first; A and C approximately tied for secondary attention**. A has a slightly greater marginal acceleration opportunity here; C has greater completion-time variability and two queue-driven exceptions. There are no observed critical ties, but 12 cases do not support a strong general ranking between A and C.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

## Counterfactual assumptions and propagation

**Local-change assumption:** Hold arrivals, fork releases, unchanged branch trajectories, and final service fixed. Reduce A’s service from 50 to 40 minutes, or move B’s start and end earlier by half its observed queue. These are arithmetic intervention targets; feasibility with actual branch resources is not established.

An earlier join does not automatically produce an equally early final completion:

$$
\boxed{\Delta L_c=\Delta J_c+(Q^F_c-Q^{F\prime}_c).}
$$

Three final-station interpretations are useful.

**Fixed final slots:** Keep recorded final starts. Every branch improvement is absorbed into additional post-join waiting, so branch-only total-flow savings are zero.

**Retained-lag, resource-feasible replay:** Preserve each case’s observed post-join lag as a *not-before response lag*, and enforce the recorded resource assignment and per-resource job order. If \(p(c)\) is the preceding case on that resource:

$$
f'_c=\max\left(J'_c+\alpha Q^F_c,\ g'_{p(c)}\right),
\qquad
g'_c=f'_c+S^F_c.
$$

Use \(\alpha=1\) for branch-only changes. This reproduces the baseline schedule before intervention and prevents impossible overlapping jobs.

**Immediate-dispatch replay:** Use the same recurrence with \(\alpha=0\). Assume F1 and F2 are continuously available, handle only these jobs, require no setup, and process one job at a time without preemption.

These are explicit sensitivity models, **not inferred dispatch policies**. In particular, preserving observed waiting as a response lag is not a claim that waiting would causally remain unchanged.

### 1. Reduce A service by 20%

The intervention removes 120 branch service-interval minutes across the cohort, but advances joins by only 15 case-minutes.

F01 gains five minutes before B becomes limiting. F11 gains ten minutes, at which point A and B tie. The other ten cases gain nothing at the join because B or C remains critical.

**Downstream masking is concrete in F11.** Its new join is 11:20. Preserving its 20-minute response lag targets an 11:40 final start, but F1 is processing F09 until 11:50. F11’s final start therefore does not advance. Simply subtracting ten minutes from its original final start would create a resource conflict.

Under this replay, mean total flow falls only to **114.83**, and the maximum remains **130**. Potential secondary benefits include freed A capacity, but A has no observed pre-start queue in these cases. Faster noncritical A completions often increase A’s synchronization wait rather than shorten case flow.

### 2. Reduce B queue by 50%

This removes 115 branch queue-minutes and advances joins by 92.5 case-minutes. The difference occurs because other branches eventually limit improvement.

F01, F04, F08, and F11 have zero direct gain: A or C remains critical. In F10, reducing B’s queue by ten minutes improves the join by only five because A then controls readiness.

All 92.5 join-gain minutes propagate to final completion under the retained-lag replay. Mean total flow becomes **107.54**, P90 becomes **114.5**, and maximum becomes **115**. This is markedly stronger tail improvement than accelerating A.

Afterward, A is solely critical in four cases and tied with B in two. B remains solely critical in four; C remains critical in two. Thus the intervention changes the bottleneck pattern. Earlier B processing could also increase contention elsewhere or affect unobserved cases; halving queues is not itself a feasible resource-allocation algorithm.

### 3. Reduce final-station waiting

Eliminating every post-join wait would save:

$$
\sum_c Q^F_c=176\text{ case-minutes},
\qquad \overline{Q^F}=14.67.
$$

This is a **final-stage-only upper envelope**, keeping joins and final service fixed.

Immediate dispatch with the existing two resources saves **171 case-minutes**, or **14.25 per case**. Only F11 retains waiting: five minutes while both resources are occupied. Mean flow becomes **101**, P90 **114**, and maximum **115**.

A third immediately available resource could recover the remaining five case-minutes in this scenario. However, adding capacity while retaining fixed release slots could recover none.

The observed schedule does not establish continuous capacity congestion. For example, F02 is ready at 09:25, but no supplied job occupies F2 before its 09:40 start; neither resource has a supplied job during 09:28–09:40. Conversely, both resources are occupied during 10:10–10:20 while F05 is ready. **Policy-related waiting and capacity-related waiting are both plausible.**

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Redesign 1: Pilot B queue reduction, with protection for documentation exceptions

Prioritize reducing B’s pre-start waiting rather than shortening A merely because A has the longest service. First establish whether B waiting comes from resource contention, availability calendars, batching, dispatch priorities, or delayed handoffs. Then pilot the corresponding change—such as earlier reservation, revised dispatch, or targeted availability.

**Flow time and tail:** B is critical in eight cases and controls both longest stages. The modeled queue reduction lowers the maximum case flow from 130 to 115. That is a pilot hypothesis, not a guaranteed outcome.

**Throughput and utilization:** Shorter waiting does not reduce B’s service requirement or automatically increase its nominal capacity. Throughput improves only if the intervention removes an actual capacity or coordination constraint. Measure B utilization, available capacity, other-job delays, and starvation rather than treating queue reduction as proof of higher productivity.

**New bottlenecks and synchronization:** A becomes more frequently critical, while C still controls F04 and F08. Faster B also changes the timing of demand at F. Completed A and C outputs generally wait less for synchronization, but that benefit could be replaced by downstream waiting.

**Pilot first:** Record B resource assignments, resource availability, queue-entry and dispatch decisions, and competing workloads. Track total-flow tails, final waiting, documentation exceptions, and technical-result quality together. Do not achieve the target by silently displacing delay onto other cases or compromising testing.

## Redesign 2: Make final dispatch responsive to join readiness before adding permanent capacity

Create an explicit join-ready signal and test prompt dispatch to an eligible available final resource. Retain required controls and legitimate eligibility restrictions; do not assume every observed delay is waste.

**Flow time and tail:** The two-resource replay’s 14.25-minute mean saving is attractive, but depends on removing the observed response lags. Validate actual reasons for those lags before treating this as an investment forecast.

**Throughput and utilization:** The supplied final service totals 240 resource-minutes. Over the explicitly defined 09:00–12:40 window, that is \(240/(2\times220)=54.5\%\) logged occupancy under continuous availability—not verified actual utilization. This weakens a capacity-only explanation but does not exclude local congestion or unlogged work. Better dispatch can improve responsiveness without increasing long-run capacity.

**New bottlenecks and synchronization:** This redesign does not directly change branch synchronization waits. It prevents earlier joins from merely becoming longer final waits. More responsive dispatch may expose eligibility restrictions, setup requirements, or short bursts of simultaneous demand.

**Pilot first:** Log resource calendars, all final-station work, eligibility, dispatch reasons, and “ready while eligible resource available” intervals. Introduce flexible or additional capacity only where these measurements show genuine contention. Under the stated replay, a third resource adds little after dispatch is improved.

F12 10:35 10:55 10:55 11:45 11:25 12:10 11:00 11:18 12:20 12:40 F2
"""
BRANCHES = ("A", "B", "C")
raw = pd.read_csv(StringIO(RAW), sep=r"\s+")
for col in raw.columns.difference(["case", "final_resource"]):
    raw[col] = pd.to_datetime("2026-05-19 " + raw[col],
                              format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M", errors="raise")
assert raw["case"].is_unique and not raw.isna().any().any()


def minutes(delta: pd.Series) -> pd.Series:
    return delta.dt.total_seconds() / 60.0


def p90(values: pd.Series) -> float:
    return float(values.quantile(0.90, interpolation="linear"))


def decompose(data: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    d = data.copy()
    d["upstream_elapsed"] = minutes(d["fork_release"] - d["arrival"])
    ends = pd.DataFrame({b: d[f"{b}_end"] for b in BRANCHES})
    d["join_ready"] = ends.max(axis=1)
    d["fork_join_elapsed"] = minutes(d["join_ready"] - d["fork_release"])
    # Preserve all exact ties in fixed A, B, C order; never silently use idxmax.
    critical_sets = ends.apply(
        lambda row: tuple(b for b in BRANCHES if row[b] == row.max()), axis=1)
    d["critical_branch"] = critical_sets.map("+".join)
    d["critical_count"] = critical_sets.map(len)
    d["critical_primary"] = critical_sets.map(lambda values: values[0])
    # primary is ONLY a deterministic representative for one additive path.
    for b in BRANCHES:
        d[f"{b}_queue"] = minutes(d[f"{b}_start"] - d["fork_release"])
        d[f"{b}_service"] = minutes(d[f"{b}_end"] - d[f"{b}_start"])
        d[f"{b}_elapsed"] = d[f"{b}_queue"] + d[f"{b}_service"]
        d[f"{b}_sync"] = minutes(d["join_ready"] - d[f"{b}_end"])
        path = d[f"{b}_elapsed"] + d[f"{b}_sync"]
        assert (path - d["fork_join_elapsed"]).abs().max() < 1e-8
    d["downstream_queue"] = minutes(d["final_start"] - d["join_ready"])
    d["final_service"] = minutes(d["final_end"] - d["final_start"])
    d["total_flow_time"] = minutes(d["final_end"] - d["arrival"])
    parts = ["upstream_elapsed", "fork_join_elapsed", "downstream_queue",
             "final_service"]
    assert (d[parts].sum(axis=1) - d["total_flow_time"]).abs().max() < 1e-8
    nonnegative = parts + [f"{b}_{v}" for b in BRANCHES
                           for v in ("queue", "service", "sync")]
    assert (d[nonnegative] >= 0).all().all()
    return d


base = decompose(raw)


def replay(ready: pd.Series, lag_factor: float) -> pd.Series:
    """Explicit sensitivity model, NOT an inferred dispatch policy.

    Keep recorded resource assignments and per-resource order. Assume resources
    continuously available, no other jobs/setups, and nonpreemptive final service.
    lag_factor=1 preserves observed post-join lags as not-before response lags;
    lag_factor=0 removes those lags and starts as soon as the resource is free.
    Actual downstream waiting is start-ready, not lag plus another counted queue.
    """
    starts = pd.Series(pd.NaT, index=base.index, dtype="datetime64[ns]")
    for resource, group in base.groupby("final_resource", sort=True):
        free = base["fork_release"].min()
        for k in group.sort_values(["final_start", "case"]).index:
            gate = ready.loc[k] + pd.Timedelta(
                minutes=lag_factor * base.loc[k, "downstream_queue"])
            starts.loc[k] = max(gate, free)
            free = starts.loc[k] + pd.Timedelta(
                minutes=base.loc[k, "final_service"])
    return starts


assert replay(base["join_ready"], 1.0).equals(base["final_start"])


def counterfactual(a_scale: float, b_queue_scale: float,
                   lag_factor: float) -> pd.DataFrame:
    d = raw.copy()
    d["A_end"] = d["A_start"] + pd.to_timedelta(
        base["A_service"] * a_scale, unit="min")
    d["B_start"] = d["fork_release"] + pd.to_timedelta(
        base["B_queue"] * b_queue_scale, unit="min")
    d["B_end"] = d["B_start"] + pd.to_timedelta(base["B_service"], unit="min")
    ready = d[[f"{b}_end" for b in BRANCHES]].max(axis=1)
    d["final_start"] = replay(ready, lag_factor)
    d["final_end"] = d["final_start"] + pd.to_timedelta(
        base["final_service"], unit="min")
    return decompose(d)

# 7. Limits of inference

**Causality and intervention feasibility.** The timestamps establish arithmetic criticality under the supplied join rule. They do not establish why B queues, whether its queue can be halved, or whether earlier starts would displace other work. Counterfactual savings depend on explicitly chosen scheduling responses and unchanged service quality.

**Branch resource capacity.** Branch resource identities, staffing, machine availability, batching, and competing jobs are absent. Overlapping intervals show overlapping case activity, but do not identify the number of exclusive resources or sustainable branch capacity.

**Active effort and preemption.** Start-to-end duration may contain suspension, unattended processing, internal holds, or interrupted work. Neither resource effort nor preemptive versus nonpreemptive branch scheduling is identifiable. The final-station replay’s nonpreemption assumption is likewise a modeling choice.

**Queue versus release policy.** Both branch pre-start waiting and final post-join waiting could include dispatch policies, appointments, eligibility restrictions, or availability calendars. Apparent idle periods in this table do not prove actual staff idleness. Conversely, recorded resource overlaps explain some potential contention but not every delay.

**Upstream decomposition and readiness semantics.** Arrival-to-fork time cannot be separated into upstream service and waiting. Likewise, \(J_c=\max_i e_{ci}\) is valid only if the logged branch ends mean the required outputs are usable and no additional readiness condition exists.

**Generalization and precision.** Twelve completed cases from one day do not identify population tails, long-run throughput, demand variability, or future bottlenecks. Fractional counterfactual minutes are mathematical results, not increased measurement precision. A pilot should therefore test whether earlier branch readiness actually becomes earlier final completion, while monitoring effects on the rest of the workload.
