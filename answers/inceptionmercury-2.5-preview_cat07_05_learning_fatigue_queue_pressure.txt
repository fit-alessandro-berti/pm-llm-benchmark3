# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed performance curves are not driven by a single factor but by the intersection of selection pressure, cognitive load, and systemic constraints. Early in the shift (sequence 1–5), performance is dominated by **case-mix/selection**: resources handle high-complexity carryover cases (mean complexity 0.72) while managing moderate WIP. This explains slower service times (18.8 min) but relatively contained error rates (3.4%) as experienced resources prioritize quality on hard cases.

As the shift stabilizes (16–30), a combination of **learning/warm-up** and reduced complexity yields the best performance (13.6 min service, 2.5% errors). However, late-shift dynamics are driven primarily by **queue pressure** rather than fatigue alone. Service times drop to 10.8 min at sequence 76+, which is inconsistent with fatigue. Instead, this reflects a speed-quality tradeoff under high WIP (88 cases); resources accelerate to clear the queue, sacrificing quality.

This mechanism explains why error rates spike to 7.9% despite lower complexity (0.43). **Fatigue** likely compounds the issue in the last two hours, particularly for Mid-level and Junior resources who degrade most under pressure. Finally, **batching** by Juniors late in the Day shift artificially depresses service times while increasing rework risk, suggesting procedural workarounds to meet targets.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Concluding that workers are "lazy" or "incompetent" in late shifts is unjustified and likely inaccurate. The data shows that speed increases while errors rise, suggesting workers are reacting to system-level queue pressure rather than individual performance decay. 

*   **Selection bias:** Workers receive complex cases first. Penalizing late-shift errors without controlling for WIP or case complexity ignores that the system pushes more urgent, high-volume tasks toward shift ends.
*   **Measurement error:** Batching (e.g., Junior_H/I) creates artificially low service times in logs. Metrics relying solely on service time reward shortcuts.
*   **Management-created pressure:** High WIP thresholds are often the result of shift handover policies and demand peaks, not worker inefficiency. Punitive measures punish symptoms of systemic capacity issues.
*   **Automation quality:** Errors attributed to humans correcting Bot_Risk should be examined. If the bot introduces systematic errors under pressure, the root cause is automation, not worker fatigue.
*   **Punitive use of error rates:** Linking penalties directly to raw late-shift error rates will incentivize workers to hide errors or decline cases to avoid metrics.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

**Process Mining Dashboard Indicators:**

1.  **Mean Service Time by Shift & Sequence Position:** Detect late-speedups vs. genuine efficiency.
2.  **Error Rate Stratified by WIP:** Separate high-WIP errors from low-WIP errors.
3.  **WIP Distribution at Shift Start/End:** Monitor backlog accumulation.
4.  **Rework Rate within 7 Days:** Lagged quality metric independent of immediate error flags.
5.  **Complexity Mix Over Time:** Ensure case distribution isn't skewed toward simple tasks at end of shift.
6.  **Batching Detection Rate:** Fraction of events occurring within tight time clusters (0–5 min gaps).
7.  **Automation Correction Rate:** Human corrections of bot-generated decisions by queue length.
8.  **Resource Load Fairness:** Mean cases per worker vs. complexity score to detect selection bias.
9.  **Service Time Variance:** High variance suggests inconsistent effort or workflow bypassing.
10. **Handover/Queue Ownership Changes:** Number of times a case moves between resources late in shift.

These indicators provide a holistic view balancing speed, quality, and fairness.
