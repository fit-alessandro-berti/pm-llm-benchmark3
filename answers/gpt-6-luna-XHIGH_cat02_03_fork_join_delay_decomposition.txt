# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

All durations below are elapsed minutes for case \(c\). Let \(i\in\{A,B,C\}\), with branch start and completion timestamps \(s_{ci}\) and \(e_{ci}\). Let arrival, fork release, final start, and final completion be \(a_c,r_c,f_c,z_c\), respectively.

- Upstream elapsed time: \(U_c=r_c-a_c\).
- Branch queue delay: \(Q_{ci}=s_{ci}-r_c\), interpreted as time from permission to begin until branch start.
- Branch service time: \(S_{ci}=e_{ci}-s_{ci}\), the observed start-to-completion interval. It is not necessarily hands-on labor if work can pause.
- Join ready: \(J_c=\max_i e_{ci}\), under the stated rule that all three branches must finish.
- Fork-join stage time: \(T_c=J_c-r_c=\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})\).
- Branch synchronization wait: \(W_{ci}=J_c-e_{ci}=T_c-(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})\), nonnegative by definition.
- Downstream wait before final activity: \(D_c=f_c-J_c\), interpreted as queueing only if no other release or hold policy applies.
- Final service: \(G_c=z_c-f_c\).
- Total case flow time: \(L_c=z_c-a_c=U_c+T_c+D_c+G_c\).

For each branch separately, \(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci}=T_c\): each branch path from fork release through synchronization spans the same fork-join interval. These are parallel paths, so they must not be added together. In particular, synchronization intervals \([e_{ci},J_c]\) overlap in clock time. Summing their durations counts overlapping waits more than once; for F07, for example, the three waits total \(25+0+52=77\) minutes although the fork-join stage is only 75 minutes. The max-based \(T_c\), not the sum of branch durations or waits, is the elapsed time to the join.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

These are arithmetic what-if estimates, not causal forecasts. For each intervention, unchanged branch queues/services, downstream wait, and final service are held fixed unless that item is the target. The join is recomputed as the maximum branch completion. Savings are per-case reductions in total flow time. Fractional minutes are retained for the 50% reductions; they represent scenario arithmetic despite source timestamps being minute-granular.

| case | A service −20% | B queue −50% | downstream wait −50% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| F01 | 5 | 0 | 4 |
| F02 | 0 | 10 | 7.5 |
| F03 | 0 | 10 | 7.5 |
| F04 | 0 | 0 | 9 |
| F05 | 0 | 10 | 5 |
| F06 | 0 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F07 | 0 | 15 | 7.5 |
| F08 | 0 | 0 | 7.5 |
| F09 | 0 | 12.5 | 7.5 |
| F10 | 0 | 5 | 10 |
| F11 | 10 | 0 | 10 |
| F12 | 0 | 15 | 5 |
| **Total case-minutes saved** | **15** | **92.5** | **88** |
| **Mean per case** | **1.25** | **7.71** | **7.33** |
| **Cases with zero flow-time saving** | **10** | **4** | **0** |

1. **Reduce A service by 20%.** A falls from 50 to 40 minutes for each case, releasing 120 branch service-minutes in aggregate. But only F01 and F11 have A on the observed critical path, so only those cases see flow-time savings: 5 and 10 minutes. At F11, the faster A ties B at the new join; the case still gains 10 minutes. B remains critical in eight cases and C in two, so their completions mask A's faster service. The sample's longest flow remains 130 minutes (F07, B-critical).
2. **Reduce B queue delay by 50%.** This removes 115 minutes of B waiting across the cases, but reduces case flow by 92.5 minutes because B is not critical in F01, F04, F08, and F11. Savings among the eight originally B-critical cases range from 5 to 15 minutes; in F02, for example, B becomes tied with A after the change. In this fixed-wait calculation, the maximum flow falls from 130 to 115 minutes. A real queue intervention could also change other cases' B waits, resource usage, or arrival pattern, so the figure is not a guaranteed gain.
3. **Reduce downstream wait by 50%.** This stylized final-station intervention saves half of each recorded downstream interval, 4–10 minutes per case, for 88 case-minutes total. It does not depend on which branch was critical and reduces the observed maximum from 130 to 122.5 minutes under the fixed-branch assumption. Eliminating downstream wait entirely would be a more aggressive arithmetic upper bound of 176 case-minutes (14.67 per case), not a prediction that simply adding one resource achieves it.

The observed downstream intervals are 8–20 minutes. If these are true resource queues and final work can start as soon as a resource is available, they can absorb a branch improvement only if the earlier join does not translate into an earlier final start (for example, due to a fixed appointment or batch policy). Conversely, an earlier join can increase or bunch demand at F and create additional waiting. The calculations keep downstream wait fixed, so they isolate direct branch effects but do not model those feedbacks. Because the meaning of `final_start - join_ready` is not proven by timestamps alone, the half-wait and zero-wait scenarios should be read as target reductions to measure, not capacity forecasts.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

1. **Pilot a B queue-reduction change.** First locate why B waits 5–30 minutes after fork release, then pilot capacity or dispatch changes on B during comparable periods. B is critical in two-thirds of these cases, and the simple 50% queue-delay scenario saves 92.5 case-minutes, with the largest direct gains of 15 minutes in F06, F07, and F12. If the queue reflects a binding machine/resource constraint, reducing it can raise throughput and shorten the flow-time tail; the fixed-wait scenario lowers the observed maximum from 130 to 115 minutes. The additional utilization of the B resource may rise, and improvements may move the bottleneck to A, C, or F. Earlier B finishes also mean longer synchronization waits for other branches in some cases; that is not a regression in total flow if the join advances, but it can change local waiting and work-in-process. Before scaling, measure B resource occupancy and availability, queue reason, starts and completions at finer resolution, branch arrival/release rates, and the join/final response for pilot versus comparison cases. Keep an eye on F04/F08-style C delays and A's 50-minute path.
2. **Pilot a final-station release/dispatch improvement before committing extra capacity.** If the measured 8–20 minute downstream intervals are avoidable resource waits, improve readiness notification, assignment, or staffing alignment so final work begins sooner. A 50% target reduction corresponds to 88 case-minutes in this sample and benefits every case; unlike accelerating one branch, it can reduce flow even when another branch controls the join. It may improve throughput if F is the actual constraint and reduce the end-to-end tail, although the fixed-wait calculation lowers the maximum only to 122.5 minutes. It could also raise F resource utilization or cause arrivals to bunch, shifting delay to a branch or to an unobserved stage. The records show 240 final service-minutes over the observed 09:08–12:40 span; with both resources assumed continuously available for that whole span, this is 56.6% aggregate utilization (240 divided by 2×212 resource-minutes), only a slice-based illustration, not a shift utilization estimate. Measure each F resource's available/busy time, assignment rules, ready-to-start reason codes, release policy, and concurrent work before deciding whether added capacity is warranted. Pilot a dispatch or scheduling change and compare waiting, throughput, and utilization together.

df["fork_join_stage"] = df[[f"{b}_from_fork" for b in "ABC"]].max(axis=1)

    return "+".join(b for b in "ABC" if abs(vals[b] - latest) < 1e-9)

# 7. Limits of inference

These 12 completed cases from one day establish observed intervals, not causal drivers or stable population distributions. The critical-branch counts and maxima are exact for this sample; they do not establish future probabilities or a reliable tail quantile.

The timestamps do not identify why a branch waited after fork release: possible explanations include a busy resource, intentional sequencing, priority, staffing, batching, or other release rules. Resource capacity and availability for A, B, and C are not logged. The final resource label and 20-minute final intervals do not reveal the complete F1/F2 calendars, assignment policy, whether both were continuously available, or whether waiting was avoidable. Thus actual utilization, sustainable throughput, and the effect of adding capacity cannot be inferred from the records alone.

Start-to-completion intervals are observable, but work preemption, pauses, setup, rework, and hands-on effort are not. A measured service interval should therefore not automatically be treated as labor content. Similarly, `join_ready` is reconstructed as the latest branch completion; the data contain no explicit join event or evidence about extra validation after branch completion. The stipulated max rule is necessary for this decomposition.

Finally, `final_start - join_ready` is an elapsed gap. Calling it a resource queue assumes the final activity is eligible immediately at join and starts as soon as capacity permits. A scheduled release, review window, batching rule, hold, or other policy could create the same gap. Counterfactuals that hold this gap fixed isolate arithmetic branch effects; real redesigns can shift load, bunch ready cases, alter queue lengths, or expose a different bottleneck. A pilot should therefore measure reasons for waiting and resource states alongside end-to-end flow and throughput.
