# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let (c) index cases and (i\in{A,B,C}) index parallel branches. Define:

* (a_c): process arrival.
* (r_c): `fork_release`.
* (b_{ci}), (e_{ci}): branch start and completion.
* (f^s_c), (f^e_c): final-activity start and completion.

All duration formulas below are in minutes.

**Upstream elapsed time**

[
U_c=r_c-a_c
]

This includes all elapsed time between overall arrival and release of the parallel branches. It is not branch queueing.

**Branch-specific queue waiting**

[
Q_{ci}=b_{ci}-r_c
]

This is the delay after the branch is permitted to start but before its observed start.

**Branch active service**

[
S_{ci}=e_{ci}-b_{ci}
]

Given only interval timestamps, this is observed elapsed service duration. Calling it active labor implicitly assumes no unlogged preemption or pauses.

**Branch fork-to-completion path**

[
P_{ci}=Q_{ci}+S_{ci}=e_{ci}-r_c
]

This, rather than service time alone, determines whether a branch controls the join.

**Join-ready time and critical branch set**

[
J_c=\max_i(e_{ci})
=r_c+\max_i(P_{ci})
]

[
K_c=\arg\max_i(P_{ci})
]

If several branches finish at the same latest timestamp, all belong to (K_c). The supplied observations contain no ties; the Python implementation reports ties in deterministic `A/B/C` order.

**Branch-specific synchronization waiting**

[
W^{\text{sync}}*{ci}=J_c-e*{ci}
=T^{\text{FJ}}*c-P*{ci}
]

A branch that controls the join has zero synchronization wait. An earlier branch waits locally until every branch has completed.

**Elapsed fork-join stage time**

[
T^{\text{FJ}}*c=J_c-r_c
=\max_i(Q*{ci}+S_{ci})
]

This maximum must appear exactly once in the case-level elapsed-time decomposition.

**Downstream queueing before the final activity**

[
D^F_c=f^s_c-J_c
]

This has a queue interpretation only if `join_ready` is the sole release condition for (F) and (F) starts as soon as the relevant dispatch policy permits.

**Final service**

[
S^F_c=f^e_c-f^s_c
]

**Total case flow time**

[
T^{\text{flow}}_c=f^e_c-a_c
=U_c+T^{\text{FJ}}_c+D^F_c+S^F_c
]

The branch-local quantities are explanatory vectors, not additive elapsed-time components. In particular,

[
Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W^{\text{sync}}_{ci}=T^{\text{FJ}}_c
]

for every branch, and therefore

[
\sum_{i\in{A,B,C}}
\left(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W^{\text{sync}}_{ci}\right)
=3T^{\text{FJ}}_c.
]

Adding the three branch paths would count the same fork-join calendar interval three times.

Even summing only synchronization waits double-counts overlapping waiting. For F02, A waits from 09:15 to 09:25, or 10 minutes, while C waits from 08:50 to 09:25, or 35 minutes. A’s entire synchronization interval is nested inside C’s. Their sum is 45 branch-minutes, but the union of those calendar intervals is only 35 minutes. Synchronization waits are useful measures of branch-level idle exposure, not additional case flow time.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

The overall mean fork-join stage is 61.0 minutes, with a descriptive 90th percentile of 74.0 and a maximum of 75. Total flow time has mean 115.25, median 115, 90th percentile 124.5, and maximum 130 minutes.

The table uses pandas’ linear-interpolation 90th percentile. With only 12 observations, maxima and case-level patterns are at least as important as the interpolated percentiles.

Define the branch’s **unique critical margin** as:

[
M_{ci}=
\max\left(0,,
P_{ci}-\max_{j\ne i}P_{cj}
\right).
]

It is the join-ready reduction obtainable by shortening branch (i) until the next-longest branch takes control. It is zero whenever the branch is not uniquely critical.

| Branch | Service mean / p90 / max | Queue mean / p90 / max | Queue + service mean / p90 / max | Critical frequency | Mean synchronization wait | Unique critical margin total / max | Influence assessment          |
| ------ | -----------------------: | ---------------------: | -------------------------------: | -----------------: | ------------------------: | ---------------------------------: | ----------------------------- |
| A      |           50.0 / 50 / 50 |            0.0 / 0 / 0 |                   50.0 / 50 / 50 |        2/12, 16.7% |                      11.0 |                            15 / 10 | Slight second                 |
| B      |           37.5 / 45 / 45 |         19.2 / 30 / 30 |                   56.7 / 74 / 75 |        8/12, 66.7% |                       4.3 |                           120 / 25 | Clear first                   |
| C      |         20.2 / 21.8 / 30 |         10.4 / 24 / 35 |                   30.6 / 53 / 57 |        2/12, 16.7% |                      30.4 |                             12 / 7 | Tail-specific near-rival to A |

**B most strongly controls total completion among the branches.** The evidence is mutually consistent:

* B is last in 8 of 12 cases.
* Its mean fork-to-completion path is 56.7 minutes, greater than A’s 50 despite B’s lower mean service time.
* Its path has p90 74 and maximum 75, almost exactly the fork-join stage tail.
* Its mean synchronization wait is only 4.3 minutes, meaning B generally finishes at or near the join.
* Its total unique critical margin is 120 minutes, compared with 15 for A and 12 for C. Conditional on being critical, B’s mean margin is 15 minutes, versus 7.5 for A and 6 for C.

A has the largest mean service time, but that fact alone is misleading. A starts immediately in every case, has no observed branch queue, and is often overtaken by B’s queued path.

The ranking below B is less decisive. A is generally closer to the join and has a slightly larger critical-margin total than C, so (A\gtrsim C) on average. C nevertheless creates two meaningful administrative-tail cases—F04 and F08—through queues of 35 and 25 minutes. Thus the defensible ranking is:

[
B \gg A \approx C,
]

with A slightly second in average structural influence and C a localized tail-risk branch.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

For the two branch counterfactuals, branch starts other than the explicitly changed queue are held fixed, other service times are unchanged, and the resulting fractional minutes are not rounded.

Three downstream-response interpretations are distinguished:

1. **Preserved downstream delay:** the duration (D^F_c) moves with `join_ready`; the join advancement becomes the same case’s flow-time saving.
2. **Fixed observed final slots:** `final_start` does not move; every upstream gain is absorbed as additional downstream waiting.
3. **Work-conserving F sensitivity:** each case keeps its observed F1/F2 assignment and within-resource order, but each resource starts the next ready listed case immediately. This assumes no hidden work, breaks, priorities, or release holds and is therefore a model, not an observation.

The per-case results are:

The directly reduced quantities are not economically equivalent: A reduction is active service workload, whereas B and final reductions are elapsed waiting.

**Counterfactual 1: reduce A service by 20%.** A service falls from 50 to 40 minutes in every case, removing 120 branch-service minutes. Nevertheless, only F01 and F11 have earlier joins. F01 gains 5 minutes before B becomes critical; F11 gains 10 minutes before A and B tie. Ten cases receive no case-level join benefit because B or C is already later.

Only 12.5% of the removed A service minutes convert into intrinsic join advancement:

[
15/120=12.5%.
]

A may still gain throughput capacity by freeing A resources, but the current data show no A queue through which that capacity benefit would immediately appear.

The work-conserving final-station sensitivity realizes only 5 of the 15 join minutes. F11’s join moves from 11:30 to 11:20, but F1 remains occupied by an earlier final case until 11:35 in that model; its entire upstream gain is masked. With the observed final starts treated as fixed slots, neither F01 nor F11 finishes earlier.

**Counterfactual 2: reduce B queue delay by 50%.** This removes 115 minutes of branch waiting and advances `join_ready` by 92.5 minutes across eight cases. The four zero-benefit cases are F01, F04, F08, and F11, where B is not critical.

The conversion of reduced B waiting into intrinsic join advancement is much stronger:

[
92.5/115\approx80.4%.
]

Several bottleneck crossovers occur:

* F02 and F06 become A/B ties.
* F05 and F10 become A-critical.
* F03, F07, F09, and F12 remain B-critical despite the reduction.

The work-conserving final-station model produces 97.5 system flow minutes of saving, 5 more than the direct join advancement. The extra benefit is a positive queue spillover: earlier completion of preceding final work frees F1 sooner for F11, even though F11’s own join is unchanged. This illustrates why system-wide completion effects need not equal the sum of directly treated cases’ join effects.

**Counterfactual 3: reduce final-station queueing.** Every case has an observed downstream delay of 8–20 minutes, totaling 176 minutes. Under the closed-world, work-conserving two-resource model, only F11 retains a 5-minute queue. The resulting saving is 171 minutes, reducing mean total flow time from 115.25 to 101.0 minutes, descriptive p90 from 124.5 to 114, and maximum from 130 to 115.

A third immediately available resource, or unlimited capacity, would remove the remaining 5 minutes and provide an upper-bound saving of 176 minutes. Under this closed-world model, therefore, work-conserving use of the existing two resources captures 97% of the unlimited-capacity benefit:

[
171/176\approx97.2%.
]

That does **not** establish that the real station is overstaffed or poorly dispatched. Of the 176 observed downstream-wait minutes, only 73 coincide with the preceding listed case occupying the same assigned resource at the moment of join readiness; 103 occur when the resource is not occupied by that preceding listed case. Those 103 minutes could reflect hidden work, breaks, release policies, batching, priorities, or unlogged prerequisites. Consequently, adding capacity alone could have little or even zero effect if the delay is policy-driven.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Redesign 1 — Control the B queue at fork release

Pilot a pull or reservation rule that gives Machine Stress Test capacity visibility at `fork_release`, with a target such as halving (B_queue). Possible mechanisms include protected B slots, earlier dispatch, load leveling, or temporary flex capacity during the observed 30-minute queue peaks.

**Throughput.** Resequencing B earlier reduces flow time and WIP but does not reduce B’s active work content. Throughput capacity increases only if the intervention adds effective B capacity or removes avoidable resource idle time. If B is already fully utilized, queue reduction may require capacity rather than a dispatch rule.

**Flow-time tail.** This intervention directly targets the 74–75 minute fork-join tail. The modeled savings are 15 minutes for F06, F07, and F12, 12.5 for F09, and 5–10 minutes for four other cases. It is substantially more tail-relevant than a uniform A service reduction.

**Resource utilization.** Reserved capacity may lower apparent B utilization during low-load periods, while added capacity may be expensive. Utilization should therefore be assessed together with ready-queue time and avoidable idle time, rather than maximized as an isolated percentage.

**New bottlenecks and synchronization.** A becomes critical or co-critical in F02, F05, F06, and F10. Further B improvement would then have diminishing returns unless A is also addressed. Earlier B completion reduces synchronization exposure for A and C, but may give B synchronization waiting after bottleneck crossover. Final-station waiting may also absorb some branch gains.

**Pilot and measurement.** Run a controlled pilot by time block or eligible case cohort. Log B resource, queue length at `fork_release`, dispatch reason, actual availability, interruptions, and priority. Primary measures should be B queue p90, B critical frequency, fork-join p90, synchronization waits by branch, final queue, and total-flow p90. Throughput and resource occupancy should be monitored as guardrails.

## Redesign 2 — Make the final station explicitly join-ready-driven before buying capacity

Create a visible `join_ready` queue and require a reason code whenever a ready case does not start at the next feasible F1/F2 opportunity. Pooling should be piloted where skill rules allow, while preserving any genuinely necessary separation between F1 and F2.

**Throughput.** A work-conserving rule does not change the 20-minute final service requirement, so it need not change long-run capacity. It can nevertheless reduce idle gaps, WIP, and within-day completion delays. Extra capacity should be considered only after measuring whether resources are actually unavailable when ready work is waiting.

**Flow-time tail.** Under the closed-world sensitivity, the redesign saves 8–20 minutes for every case and reduces the maximum total flow from 130 to 115 minutes. This is broader than either branch intervention, although its causal feasibility is less certain.

**Resource utilization.** Better dispatch could increase productive utilization without adding headcount. Conversely, a third resource is likely to be lightly utilized for these 12 cases because only a five-minute peak conflict remains in the work-conserving two-resource schedule. That conclusion must be re-evaluated after including other work handled by F1 and F2.

**New bottlenecks and synchronization.** Removing downstream queueing exposes B as the dominant remaining elapsed-time controller. It does not alter branch synchronization waits directly; it simply prevents an additional queue from hiding the fork-join improvement.

**Pilot and measurement.** First collect actual resource-availability intervals, other-task assignments, breaks, queue position, priorities, batching rules, release approvals, and hold reasons. Then compare a join-ready pull cohort with the current policy. Measure ready-to-start delay, final utilization, final queue length, flow-time p90, priority compliance, and displacement of other work. A capacity pilot is justified only if ready cases repeatedly wait while both resources are genuinely occupied.

    return delta.dt.total_seconds().div(60)

# -----------------------------

    Ties are reported deterministically in BRANCHES order, for example A/B.

    values = {branch: float(row[branch]) for branch in BRANCHES}
    maximum = max(values.values())
    tied = [
        branch
        for branch in BRANCHES
        if np.isclose(values[branch], maximum)
    ]
    return "/".join(tied)

def schedule_assigned_resources(
    ready_times: pd.Series,
) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Closed-world, work-conserving final-station schedule.

    Each case preserves its observed F1/F2 assignment and observed
    within-resource sequence. A resource begins its next listed case as soon
    as both the case is join-ready and the resource is free.

    This is a sensitivity model, not an assertion that the observed station
    had no other work, breaks, holds, batching, or release policies.
    """
    jobs = pd.DataFrame({
        "case": df["case"],
        "ready": ready_times,
        "service": df["final_service"],
        "resource": df["final_resource"],
        "observed_order": df["final_start"],
    }).sort_values(
        ["resource", "observed_order", "case"]
    )

    records = []
    for resource, group in jobs.groupby("resource", sort=True):
        available = group["ready"].min() - pd.Timedelta(days=1)

        for row in group.itertuples(index=False):
            start = max(row.ready, available)
            end = start + pd.to_timedelta(
                row.service, unit="m"
            )
            records.append({
                "case": row.case,
                "cf_final_start": start,
                "cf_final_end": end,
                "cf_final_wait":
                    (start - row.ready).total_seconds() / 60,
            })
            available = end

    schedule = pd.DataFrame(records)
    return df[["case"]].merge(
        schedule, on="case", how="left"
    )

# 7. Limits of inference

**Causality is not identified.** The data are 12 observational cases from one day. The counterfactuals hold most timestamps fixed and do not establish what an actual staffing, automation, or sequencing intervention would cause.

**Branch resource capacity is unknown.** No resources, availability calendars, queue lengths, competing work, or utilization observations are supplied for A, B, or C. B’s queue is visible, but whether it is caused by insufficient capacity, batching, prioritization, equipment availability, or another rule cannot be determined.

**Observed service intervals may not equal labor effort.** The calculations treat each start-to-end interval as active service. If work is paused, preempted, performed asynchronously, or waiting inside an activity interval, the active-work interpretation is overstated.

**The final station is not fully observed as a queueing system.** F1 and F2 may perform work outside these 12 cases, take breaks, have skill restrictions, or follow appointments and priority rules. Therefore, the work-conserving model is a closed-world sensitivity rather than a reconstruction of actual available capacity.

**`final_start - join_ready` may represent a release policy rather than queueing.** Possible alternatives include batching, scheduled decision windows, supervisor release, a hidden prerequisite, or delayed timestamp entry. Additional capacity would not necessarily reduce such delays.

**Branch independence is assumed.** The local counterfactuals assume changing A service or B queue does not change other branch starts, services, priorities, or resource allocations. Real interventions may create displacement, learning, congestion, or upstream scheduling effects.

**Final-station spillovers are model-dependent.** The 5-minute masking of A improvements and the 5-minute positive spillover under the B intervention follow from the stated work-conserving scheduling sensitivity. Under fixed final slots, all branch gains are masked; under preserved downstream delays, the full join advancement passes through.

**Minute precision limits tie interpretation.** Two branches recorded at the same minute may have completed seconds apart, and apparent one-minute differences may not be operationally meaningful. Deterministic tie handling is a reporting convention, not evidence of causal priority.

**Tail estimates are unstable.** A p90 computed from 12 cases is primarily descriptive and depends on quantile interpolation. Case-level maxima, critical-branch identities, and replication over more days should carry more weight.

**Throughput cannot be derived from these flow times alone.** Reducing active service may increase sustainable capacity even when it barely changes current joins, while reducing waiting may lower cycle time without changing long-run service capacity. Arrival rates, WIP, shift length, and competing demand are needed to quantify throughput effects.

**A directly-follows model is not a fork-join decomposition.** A PM4Py performance DFG can summarize observed pairwise order and durations, but it does not by itself infer the AND-join, the maximum branch completion, or overlapping synchronization waits. Those quantities require the explicit max-based pandas calculations used here.
