# 1. Queue-by-queue diagnosis

The evidence supports combinations of rules, not four clean textbook disciplines. The central distinction is between **eligibility**—which jobs a resource can actually take—and **ranking** among those jobs. A violation of a ranking rule is conditional on the jobs being released, ready, and available to that resource.

These scores express confidence in the stated working diagnoses, not fitted probabilities or evidence that management actually implemented those rules. QD's skill restrictions are much more certain than its complete dispatch policy; QC's specific sequence of overrides remains speculative.

# 2. Reconstructed dispatch decisions

**Conventions and working assumptions.** The tables cover every listed service start, including resumptions, and both recorded interruptions. Two additional QD availability opportunities expose important omissions. For reconstruction, assume that the supplied jobs are the relevant backlog, that recorded arrivals normally become ready immediately, and that resources are usable outside their stated exceptions and service intervals. Unrecorded holds, assignments, or other work would change these sets; they are alternatives to investigate, not observations to invent.

A listed arrival can represent physical arrival up to two minutes earlier. `?job` denotes a job that might therefore already have been present despite a later scan. Whether dispatch could see it before scanning is unknown. Minute resolution also makes boundary ordering uncertain. These effects can add candidates or create close arrival ties; they cannot make a job scanned well before a decision disappear from its backlog.

`W` is the set of unfinished, non-running jobs plausibly ready for the named resource, including appointment jobs whose early-service eligibility is unresolved. In QB, a just-interrupted B01 is also shown as a possible continuation/resumption candidate. FIFO uses original recorded arrival order, retaining B01's original position unless stated otherwise. Priority uses the smallest number, with FIFO as an **assumed tie-break**. SEPT uses the supplied arrival-time estimates, not realized durations. For B01, remaining estimates assume elapsed service represents completed work, with no lost progress.

At a free server, preemptive and non-preemptive priority predict the same highest-priority eligible job. Their different behavior while a server is busy is discussed separately. Ordinary FIFO and SEPT do not themselves authorize interruption. Appointment scheduling is incomplete without windows, reservation rules, and a fallback order: `—` below means that no appointment determines a unique next job. The skill column reports the eligible alternatives, not a ranking invented from skills.

A03 is the decisive FIFO exception: its appointment explains its exact start, whereas neither priority nor SEPT predicts it because A04 was already waiting. Conversely, A02 at 08:32 and A05 at 09:05 strongly favor a FIFO base over those alternatives. A04 at 08:58 is not discriminating: it is simultaneously oldest, shortest, and tied for highest priority.

Under immediate strict preemptive priority, A04 should interrupt A01 after arriving at 08:13, and A08 should interrupt A02 after arriving at 08:40. Neither happens.

The first stop establishes a priority-related preemptive component much more directly than an overtaking pattern alone. It does not establish a universal three-level priority discipline: B01 resumes ahead of B03 at 09:31 and B06 at 10:14. A binary distinction between urgent priority 1 and everyone else, with FIFO/resumption preference otherwise, fits those decisions better.

The second stop is explicitly attributed to dispatcher reassignment. That identifies an intervention, not why B03 outranks B05. B05 was scanned at 09:46 and could have arrived even earlier, so scan delay cannot remove it from the 09:48 candidate set. A previously committed dispatch, readiness restriction, or other override is required to reconcile that choice with highest-priority-first service. B03 also continues until 10:00 while B05 waits, contradicting immediate strict preemptive priority if both are ready.

After B01 completes, B04–B06–B07 is FIFO order. B07's appointment has no visible overriding effect. Its 26-minute lateness rules out an exact guarantee, but an unspecified appointment window cannot be declared violated categorically.

The initial 10:10 backlog has SEPT order C02–C03–C04–C01. Its relative execution order is exactly that, but later arrivals are interleaved. A fixed initial batch could explain why C06 does not displace C04 at 10:32; it cannot alone explain C05 and C06 both being inserted before C01. Thus neither continuously recomputed SEPT nor an inviolable initial batch explains the full trace.

C05 at 10:54 is both highest priority and earliest deadline. That choice cannot distinguish a priority override from an overdue-SLA rule. However, both its priority and its deadline were already known at 10:18 and 10:32, when it was bypassed. A plausible policy change is therefore an override triggered by **actual overdue status**, crossed at 10:50 during C04, rather than urgency being continuously applied from arrival. This remains a hypothesis.

C05's timing sharpens that distinction. With a recorded 10:11 arrival and 40-minute estimate, immediate service would finish at 10:51; a delayed scan could mean physical arrival early enough for a 10:10 start and exact 10:50 completion. By the unambiguous 10:18 free-server decision, however, even immediate service would finish eight minutes late. Actual completion at 11:34 is 44 minutes late.

C07 provides a stronger challenge to a general SLA-protection claim. At 11:34, C06 followed by C07 would finish C07 at 11:58, within its noon deadline. At 11:40, selecting C01 instead predicts C07's actual 12:34 completion, 34 minutes late. C01 is oldest but has a later deadline and twice C07's estimated duration. Aging, an initial-batch commitment, or a manual override could explain this choice; generic priority, SEPT, earliest-deadline-first, and deadline-feasibility protection do not. No numerical aging threshold can be inferred from this single switch.

D09's arrival scan coincides with D-X's 11:20 availability, so its immediate visibility is uncertain; removing it changes none of that row's predicted winners. The optional early presence of D03 at 11:01 likewise cannot establish a violation without knowing scan visibility and release timing.

Several global overtakes are explained by skills. D04 can run on D-M while older D03 awaits an electrical resource; D07 cannot use idle mechanical-only capacity. But skills cannot explain D04 over D08 at 11:25 or D07 over D10 at 11:48: each pair is compatible with the selecting resource. Both episodes support local FIFO over priority or SEPT. Strict preemptive priority would also call for urgent D08 to interrupt D01 after its arrival at 11:16; that does not occur.

D-X's exact 11:30 appointment start is consistent with both FIFO among its then-waiting candidates and appointment scheduling. Its lack of listed work from 11:20 is additional evidence for a reservation, but not proof. D08's eight-minute estimate fits before 11:30, and D04's ten-minute estimate fits exactly. Reservation with setup or uncertainty buffers, preassignments, or unlogged resource work would be needed to explain why neither was backfilled.

D05 could have fit on D-F at 11:21, finishing at 11:51, but D03 was chosen. At 11:36, D05's 30-minute estimate crosses the noon meeting. A non-preemptive rule that forbids crossing known unavailability could then explain its deferral to 12:20. **That explanation does not account for D10:** starting it at 11:36 would finish at 11:48, well before the meeting. The note about D-F being unavailable until 12:20 explains D10's eventual use of D-X at 12:16, not its earlier wait. This remains a material gap in the inferred policy.

Preemption has two separate consequences: interruption of a job's progress and any additional cost of switching or restarting. B01 spends 15 minutes suspended after its first stop and 26 after its second, for 41 suspended minutes. Those intervals are occupied by other jobs; **they are not 41 minutes of measured switching overhead**. Including its initial two-minute wait gives 43 minutes without service between arrival and completion. Its logged service still totals 45 minutes.

The shared timestamps at switches show no separately recorded handover gap, but minute-level data and the definition of a service segment could conceal overhead. A rule that preempts only for substantial urgency, finishes a nearly completed task, or favors a warm resumption is therefore plausible. The supplied trace does not identify such thresholds. Similarly, uninterrupted service in QA, QC, and QD does not prove those queues can never preempt, although several ready higher-priority arrivals contradict immediate strict priority preemption under the reconstruction assumptions.

# 6. Additional event data needed

Collect decision-level evidence first, then a broader sample. More completion timestamps alone will not resolve the central ambiguities.

1. **True queue entry, readiness, and eligibility snapshots.** Record physical arrival, scan timestamp, logical queue-entry time, ready/released time, hold start/end, hold reason, eligible resources, and the complete eligible-job set at each decision. Capture exclusions with reasons. Prioritize C05 at 10:10–10:32, C06 at 10:32, B05 at 09:48, and D10 at 11:36 and 11:48. This separates absent candidates from candidates deliberately passed over.

2. **Dispatch decisions and binding commitments.** Record decision ID and timestamp, decision trigger, job selected or explicit no-dispatch result, resource, commitment/assignment time, assignment mutability, dispatcher identity or automation source, and override reason. Link execution starts back to those decisions. This directly tests whether QB's 09:48 start followed an earlier commitment and whether D10 was locked to D-X. Include resource-release and resource-activation events so D-X at 11:20 and D-F at 11:36 are represented even without a service start.

3. **Complete resource-state and qualification histories.** Record available, busy, setup, cleaning, calibration, meeting, break, blocked, and reserved intervals, including other work absent from these queue extracts. Record the effective skill matrix, certification restrictions, and whether compound requirements may be met jointly or must be met by one person. For D-F, obtain the pre-meeting cutoff, setup buffers, and permission to pause work across the meeting; for D-X, obtain the 11:20–11:30 reservation or other state.

4. **Appointment contracts and enforcement events.** Capture scheduled start, earliest/latest allowed start, whether the appointment specifies check-in or actual service, reserved resource, expected service duration, permitted backfilling, late-arrival handling, and missed-window fallback. Obtain the exact tolerances for A06 and B07 and capacity reservations for A03 and D06. This separates late-but-compliant starts from failed guarantees.

5. **Time-varying priority, SLA, policy version, and batch membership.** Record deadline type, effective priority and every change, SLA-risk calculation, threshold crossing, aging score, policy version with effective time, active batch or planning horizon, and the rule actually invoked at each decision. For QC, focus on why C05 was bypassed at 10:18 and 10:32, why it became selectable at 10:54, and why C01 displaced C07 at 11:40. A policy-version log tests an actual policy change rather than assuming one from the sequence.

6. **Estimate revisions, remaining work, and interruption lifecycle.** Preserve arrival-time `est_min`, its source and model/version, all later estimates with timestamps, and current estimated remaining time as seen by dispatch. For B01, record suspend, resume, preemption reason, progress retained/lost, restart/setup duration, requeue timestamp, retained queue position, and whether suspended jobs get a special claim on B1. This distinguishes original-arrival FIFO, re-entry FIFO, resume preference, SEPT, and shortest-remaining-time behavior. Estimate provenance and revision audits are needed before assessing strategic reporting.

7. **A broader prospective sample with decision-level validation.** Sample across resources, shifts, load levels, appointment conflicts, and SLA-risk states. Seek decisions where the oldest, shortest, highest-priority, and earliest-deadline jobs are different. For each candidate rule, reconstruct its eligibility set first, record tied predictions, then measure agreement at informative decisions and unexplained inactivity. Calibrate any thresholds on one period and assess prediction on another; report sensitivity to the two-minute scan uncertainty and unknown windows.

For process-mining implementation, represent service starts, suspensions, resumes, and completions as distinct lifecycle events, retaining job ID, resource ID, and decision ID. A completion-only case log loses both B01's interruptions and QD's concurrent choices. A conventional process model can check feasible lifecycle sequences, but FIFO, priority, and SEPT require a resource-aware decision reconstruction using queue attributes and availability calendars. No policy-conformance percentage is defensible until eligibility and timing semantics are specified.

# 7. Final synthesis

**QA is the most identifiable overall dispatch pattern:** FIFO explains every first-start position except A03's clearly timed appointment insertion. **QD has the most identifiable routing constraints**, but its complete dispatch policy remains uncertain because of unexplained available-capacity opportunities. **QC is least identifiable:** the initial SEPT pattern is real, yet the subsequent choices require undocumented gates, commitments, or changing preferences. QB establishes selective urgent preemption, combined with resumption preference and dispatcher intervention.

The most dangerous claim to infer is **strict, immediate preemptive priority for QB**. Its explicit urgent interruption makes that label tempting, but B05 waits while B03 is selected, and lower-priority B01 repeatedly resumes ahead of priority-2 work. Treating the isolated preemption as a guarantee would give management an unsupported assurance about urgent-job response. Likewise, QC's short initial jobs do not justify predicting performance under pure SEPT or assuming that deadlines will be protected.
