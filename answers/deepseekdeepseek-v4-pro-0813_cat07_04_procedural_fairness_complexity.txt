# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity**  
  Raw approval rates differ materially: Group A 78%, Group B 63%, Group C 69%, Group D 58%. This is an important monitoring signal, but raw outcome differences alone cannot be interpreted as procedural unfairness because case mix and routing also differ.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity**  
  After controlling for observed risk score, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior case count, region, and month, residual differences remain: Group B 5.5 percentage points approval, Group C 2.2 points, Group D 3.1 points. This suggests either unmeasured complexity/need or possible disparate treatment, but it does not prove discrimination.

- **Procedural burden**

Adjusted probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents are higher especially for Group B and Group C. These are procedural burdens even if partly justified by case needs.

- **Timeliness**

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate disparity from discrimination, identify legitimate routing, and quantify uncertainty.

- **Matching or stratification variables**  
  Stratify or match cases on: `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `language_preference`, `disability_accommodation_requested`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and month. Use exact matching or coarsened exact matching within comparable risk/complexity strata before comparing applicant groups. Avoid simple linear adjustment alone.

- **Path/variant comparison**  
  Compare variant distributions within strata. For example, among complete-documentation, online, low-risk cases, compare shares of V1, V2, V4, etc. across groups. This helps distinguish whether different paths are explained by measured case mix or persist within similar cases.

- **Resource-assignment comparison**  
  Compare number of resources per case, number of handovers, role concentration, and queue involvement by group. Use handover-of-work networks and resource-similarity metrics. Pay special attention to Group B’s general  bilingual  general handovers and Group C’s general  accommodation specialist  supervisor handovers.

- **Waiting-time decomposition**  
  Decompose cycle time into service time and waiting time at each transition. Compare waiting times at support and routing points across groups within matched strata. This identifies whether delays come from legitimate processing time, resource queues, or handover friction.

- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates**  
  `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities, and `documentation_completeness` may be affected by language barriers. Re-run adjusted comparisons excluding each of these variables, or treating them as mediators rather than confounders. If estimates change materially, the adjustment is fragile. Use DAG-informed modeling and quantitative bias analysis where possible.

- **Subgroup checks by region and channel**  
  Re-estimate group differences within region and channel. If disparities are concentrated in certain regions or in-person/mixed channels, that points to operational bottlenecks or regional resource allocation rather than a uniform process failure.

- **Uncertainty reporting**  
  Report confidence intervals, not just point estimates. Compare model specifications. Distinguish statistical uncertainty from model uncertainty. Avoid interpreting small residual differences as definitive evidence of discrimination.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity-driven routing**  
   Group B needs language support, Group C requests accommodation review, and Group D has higher measured risk and prior case count. These characteristics legitimately trigger additional steps such as Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and Fraud Review. Longer paths and some extra waiting are an expected consequence of rights-preserving or risk-management procedures.

2. **Capacity bottlenecks at support steps**  
   The process has very few bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists. Queue delays at Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review are substantial. This creates disproportionate waiting and handover burden for Group B and Group C even if their routing is procedurally correct.

3. **Administrative churn and communication friction**  
   Language and disability-related needs may cause more missing-document requests, more supervisor review, and more handoffs. Group B has large adjusted increases in Request Missing Documents and Supervisor Review. This could reflect caseworker caution, lower first-time resolution quality, or difficulty communicating—not necessarily intentional discrimination, but still procedural burden.

4. **Biased or imperfect risk and documentation flags**  
   `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities in enforcement, appeal, fraud referral, or prior case outcomes. `documentation_completeness` may reflect language barriers and applicant-facing communication quality rather than true applicant non-compliance. If these flags are biased, then Group D’s fraud routing and Group B’s missing-document burden may be procedurally unfair even though the system treats the flags as neutral complexity.

5. **Stricter scrutiny or discretionary review**  
   Adjusted Supervisor Review is elevated for Group B and Group C. This may represent a legitimate response to complex cases, but it can also indicate risk-averse or discretionary behavior concentrated on minority-language and disability-related cases. Stricter scrutiny may feed appeal and rework rates.

6. **Explanation and accessibility deficits**  
   Decision notices may be less accessible to applicants with language or accommodation needs. If applicants do not understand decisions, they may appeal or re-open cases more often. This would raise appeal/rework rates without necessarily producing unfair outcomes.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Historical bias in risk scores**  
  `risk_score_band` is not necessarily neutral. It may have been built from prior appeals, rework, fraud referrals, or outcomes that already reflected group disparities. Adjusting for it may overcontrol or legitimize a biased routing factor.

- **Documentation completeness is not exogenous**  
  `documentation_completeness` may be affected by language barriers, unclear requests, or resource burdens. Treating it as a case-mix control may absorb procedural unfairness rather than explain it.

- **Missing applicant needs**  
  The data lack measures of digital literacy, health status, mental health, income, social support, trauma, or communication difficulty. These unobserved needs may affect both group membership and process outcomes.

- **Outcome quality is unobserved**  
  `case_outcome` gives broad approval/denial but not quality of explanation, dignity, accessibility, or applicant understanding. Formal `appeal_flag` may miss applicants who could not appeal due to language, fear, or burden.

- **Resource workload is coarse**  
  Resource assignment is observed only at daily granularity. Actual worker effort, queue depth at time of assignment, staff experience, overtime, and handover coordination costs are not measured.

- **Timestamps may be noisy**  
  In-person or paper applications may be entered later than actual submission. Waiting times may include data-entry lag rather than true operational delay.

- **No counterfactual routing**  
  We observe the path an applicant actually followed, not the path they would have followed absent language, accommodation, or risk flags. This limits causal inference.

- **Smaller group sizes and multiple comparisons**  
  Groups B, C, and D are smaller than Group A. Adjusted subgroup models may have low power, and multiple outcome comparisons increase the risk of overinterpreting noisy differences.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review**  
  Could reduce queue delays, handovers, and cycle time for Groups B and C.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* higher cost; need to recruit, train, and retain qualified staff; possible temporary productivity loss; if specialists remain isolated, may still create handovers; may simply shift bottleneck to another step.

- **Service-level targets for support steps**  
  Would make language-support and accommodation-review waits visible and accountable.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* risk of gaming timestamps or premature termination of support work; may shift pressure to other activities; targets could become compliance metrics rather than quality metrics; may not address upstream case complexity.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers**  
  Assigning language-support or accommodation-trained caseworkers to own cases end-to-end, or creating parallel support teams, could reduce general  specialist  general handoffs.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* generalists may lack specialized skill; may reduce separation-of-duties for fraud review; could overload specialists; may create inconsistent decisions if new teams are less experienced.

- **Decision-explanation improvements**  
  Plain-language, translated, accessible decision notices could reduce appeals and rework, especially for Groups B and D.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* longer notification drafting; translation and accessibility costs; may delay notification if not managed well; may not help applicants with very low literacy or digital access.

- **Monitoring adjusted disparities**  
  Regular dashboards tracking adjusted approval, cycle time, waiting time, variant share, supervisor review, and appeals can catch emerging inequities.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* risk of overreaction to noisy metrics; may incentivize “gaming” or quota-like behavior; requires careful model interpretation; may mislead if risk or documentation measures remain biased.

- **Auditing `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`**  
  Auditing whether risk scoring is biased and whether documentation requests are applied consistently can reduce unfair routing.  
  *Side effects/trade-offs:* audits are resource-intensive; may require data not currently collected; could reduce fraud detection if risk model is changed; may create tension with external accountability targets; findings may be contested.
