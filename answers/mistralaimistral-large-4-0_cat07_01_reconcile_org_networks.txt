# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

## Handover-of-work network

**What it measures.** Directed edges between resources when one completes an event and another completes the next event on the same case. It captures immediate, sequential work transfer.

**What it implies about Mira.** Her out-degree centrality (0.82) is the highest in the process, meaning she hands work to many different resources. Her in-degree (0.51) is also high, meaning many resources hand work to her. Her betweenness (0.77) is the highest, meaning she lies on many shortest paths between other resources. The top edges show `Bot_Rules  Mira` (920, exception desk routing), `Mira  Omar_Medical` (210), `Mira  Priya_Legal` (180), `Mira  Carla_Adjuster` (350), `Mira  Diego_Adjuster` (330), `Mira  Elise_Adjuster` (290), and `Mira  Farid_Adjuster` (280). This implies she is the primary immediate router for exception cases: automated rules send exceptions to her, and she sends them to specialists or adjusters across both teams.

**What it does not imply.** It does not imply she works closely with these resources (that is the working-together network). It does not imply she is slow—handover edges carry no duration information. It does not imply she has formal authority; high handover centrality can arise from informal routing. It also does not distinguish between value-adding routing and mere queue-passing. The automated resources (`Bot_Ingest  Bot_Rules`, 4040 edges, 10 sec) are expected to be highly connected but are not human decision points.

## Working-together network

**What it measures.** Undirected co-occurrence of two resources on the same case, regardless of direct handover. It captures sustained shared involvement in a case.

**What it implies about Mira.** Her weighted degree (0.16) is low, and co-case rates with adjusters are 6–8%, with team leads 4–5%. This means that although she hands work to many people, she rarely appears on the same case as them for sustained periods. The additional evidence explains this: her events are short (median 4.2 min) routing, assignment, reopen, or clarification events, after which she disappears for several events while others do the substantive work. By contrast, team lead–adjuster pairs (Alice–Carla 27%, Bruno–Diego 26%) show high co-occurrence, reflecting sustained collaboration on substantive work. Temporaries (`Temp_1 — Temp_2`, 3%) rarely co-work, consistent with their peripheral, task-specific roles.

**What it does not imply.** It does **not** imply she is peripheral or unimportant. Low co-occurrence is expected for a coordinator who touches many cases briefly. It does not measure influence, control, or decision rights. It also does not imply she is socially isolated—she may be highly influential through brief interventions. The low co-occurrence with team leads (4–5%) is notable: it suggests she operates outside the formal team structures, which could be either efficient cross-team brokering or problematic bypassing of formal leads.

## Subcontracting network

**What it measures.** Patterns where resource A performs an event, resource B performs a later event, and A returns within *n* subsequent events. It captures delegation-and-return patterns.

**What it implies about Mira.** Her subcontracting betweenness (0.69) is the highest. Top patterns: `Mira  Omar  Mira` (126), `Mira  Priya  Mira` (98), `Mira  Carla  Mira` (86), `Mira  Diego  Mira` (82), `Temp_1  Vendor_Verify  Temp_1` (68), `Bot_Rules  Mira  Mira` (62). This implies she does not simply hand off and disappear; she delegates to specialists/adjusters and then resumes control within two events. Typical fragments show: `Request Missing Information  Medical Review  Assign Specialist/Reopen`; `Escalate Complaint  Legal Review  Reopen/Assign Specialist`. This is a dispatcher/broker pattern: she orchestrates, monitors, and re-intervenes. The `Temp_1  Vendor_Verify  Temp_1` pattern shows that external vendor verification also creates delegation-and-return loops, but with a temporary worker rather than a coordinator.

**What it does not imply.** It does not imply she is doing the specialist work herself. It does not imply the subcontracting is inefficient—it may be necessary for exception handling. However, with *n*=2, the metric may over-represent her because her short events naturally create A-B-A patterns. It also does not tell us whether her re-interventions are value-adding or rework.

## Performance/time statistics

**What they measure.** Service time per event, case counts, exception-flag shares, cycle-time contributions, and absence-period comparisons.

**What they imply about Mira.** Her median service time is 4.2 min—short, indicating quick decisions. She touches 905 distinct cases (22% of all cases) but 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Her exception-flag share is 0.48, the highest among human coordinators/adjusters (specialists Omar 0.62 and Priya 0.71 are higher, but they only see referred cases). Her case count percentile is 0.91, and her cycle-time contribution is "high variance." The absence experiment is critical: during her 6-day absence, ordinary low-value claims had unchanged median cycle time, but `exception_flag=True` cases accumulated, and p90 cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. This implies she is not a bottleneck for ordinary flow but is a critical resource for exception flow. The high variance in her cycle-time contribution suggests she is involved in highly heterogeneous, complex cases. Note that `Vendor_Verify` (2.1 hours) and `Vendor_Pay` (1.3 hours) have long service times, but these are external waiting-time components, not human decision bottlenecks; `Bot_Ingest` and `Bot_Rules` have minimal service times (4–7 sec).

**What it does not imply.** It does not imply she is slow—her service times are short. It does not imply she causes the exceptions—her high exception-flag share may reflect that exceptions are routed *to* her, not that she creates them. The absence evidence shows correlation, not causation; other factors during those 6 days could have contributed. It also does not tell us whether her short service times reflect efficiency or superficial handling.

## Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

**What it measures.** Clustering of resources based on activity profiles or discovered organizational roles, using activity-based similarity or role-discovery algorithms.

**What it implies about Mira.** Her activity profile—`Request Missing Information`, `Reopen Claim`, `Escalate Complaint`, `Assign Specialist`—is distinct from adjusters (`Assess Damage`, `Negotiate Settlement`), team leads (`Triage Claim`, `Approve Settlement`), and specialists (`Medical Review`, `Legal Review`). She is a hybrid: part coordinator, part exception handler, part dispatcher. Role discovery would likely either (a) cluster her with other coordinators (Alice, Bruno) if the algorithm emphasizes coordination activities, or (b) isolate her as a unique role if it emphasizes her exception-specific activities. Activity-based resource similarity would likely show low similarity to team leads and adjusters, and moderate similarity to other coordinators, but with a distinct exception-handling signature.

**What it does not imply.** It does not imply she has a formally defined role. Role discovery is descriptive, not normative. It does not tell us whether her actual influence matches her formal role. The process owner's statement that she is "not a real team lead" suggests a gap between formal role and discovered role.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

## Hypothesis 1: Formal coordinator / exception desk

**Mechanism.** Mira is the officially designated exception desk. `Bot_Rules` routes exception cases to her; she triages, requests missing information, and assigns specialists or adjusters. Her role is to clean the queue of cases that automation cannot handle.

**Evidence supporting.** High in-degree from `Bot_Rules` (920 edges, "exception desk routing"). High exception-flag share (0.48). Activities include `Assign Specialist`, `Escalate Complaint`, `Request Missing Information`. Process owner calls her "just a queue cleaner." Her absence caused exception cases to accumulate, consistent with a formal queue owner.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting.** Her subcontracting patterns (`Mira  Omar  Mira`, `Mira  Priya  Mira`) show she re-engages after specialist work, which is more than queue cleaning—it suggests ongoing control. Her low working-together with team leads (4–5%) is odd for a formal coordinator who should be embedded in team workflows. If she were purely formal, we might expect documented procedures and substitutability, but the absence evidence suggests otherwise.

**Observable predictions.** If formal, her routing decisions should follow documented rules; other coordinators should be able to substitute for her with minimal training; her absence should cause delays but not qualitative changes in routing. We would see standardized activity sequences after her assignment. Her event log should show consistent decision patterns (e.g., always assigning medical cases to Omar, legal to Priya).

## Hypothesis 2: Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

**Mechanism.** Mira possesses tacit knowledge about how to route complex exception cases. She is the only person who knows "where hard cases should go." The process depends on her personal judgment, not on documented procedures. She is a single point of failure for exception flow.

**Evidence supporting.** Absence experiment—exception cases accumulated, p90 cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days, while ordinary cases were unaffected. Adjusters say she is "the person who knows where hard cases should go." High betweenness (0.77) and subcontracting betweenness (0.69). High exception-flag share (0.48) and involvement in 64% of reopens, 71% of complaints. No evidence of cross-training or documented routing rules.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting.** Her median service time is only 4.2 min, so she is not slow per event. The bottleneck is her availability as a decision point, not her processing speed. If she were a pure bottleneck, we might expect long waiting times before her events in normal periods, but the data shows accumulation only during absence. Also, high centrality alone does not prove key-person dependency; it could be a well-designed hub.

**Observable predictions.** If key-person dependency, her absence should cause exception cases to stall specifically at routing/assignment points; other resources should show idle time waiting for her; when she returns, a backlog should clear rapidly. Other coordinators (Alice, Bruno) should not absorb her exception load during absence. Her decisions should be hard to replicate—variability in routing decisions should be high, and no other resource should show similar patterns.

## Hypothesis 3: Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker

**Mechanism.** Mira operates across formal boundaries: between automation (`Bot_Rules`) and humans, between Team North and Team South, between adjusters and specialists, and between routine and exception flows. She brokers work across silos, translating automated outputs into human assignments and resolving cross-boundary conflicts.

**Evidence supporting.** High handover betweenness (0.77). Edges from `Bot_Rules` (automation) to her, and from her to Omar, Priya (specialists), Carla, Diego (senior adjusters, both teams), Elise, Farid (junior adjusters, both teams). Low working-together with team leads (4–5%) suggests she bridges teams rather than belonging to one. She touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints—cross-cutting issues that span teams. Subcontracting patterns show her dispatching across team and specialist boundaries.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting.** If she were a true boundary spanner, we might expect higher working-together with team leads (as a bridge between teams). Her low co-occurrence with Alice and Bruno (4–5%) could mean she bypasses formal team leads, which could be a control issue rather than effective brokering. Her high exception-flag share might mean she handles problems after they occur rather than preventing boundary conflicts. Also, boundary spanning can be a symptom of poor process design—if the process were well-designed, fewer cases would need cross-boundary brokering.

**Observable predictions.** If boundary spanner, removing her should increase handoffs between teams and specialists, causing more formal escalations (as the operations manager warns). Cases should show more cross-team handoffs when she is absent. Her presence should correlate with smoother cross-boundary flows (fewer rework loops). We would see her intervening at points where cases cross team or specialist boundaries.

## Hypothesis 4: Data artifact / measurement bias

**Mechanism.** Mira's high centrality is partly an artifact of event logging granularity. Her short, discrete events (assign, reopen, escalate, request info) create many handover edges and A-B-A subcontracting patterns. The working-together network undercounts her because her events are brief and separated in time from substantive work.

**Evidence supporting.** Her median service time is 4.2 min; she has 960 events across 905 cases (~1.06 events per case). Short events create more handover opportunities. The working-together network's low co-occurrence is explicitly explained by her events being short "routing" events. The subcontracting network with *n*=2 may over-represent her because she often appears, leaves, and returns within 2 events. The contradiction between high handover and low working-together centrality is a classic sign of granularity effects.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting.** The absence evidence is behavioral, not just a logging artifact—during her absence, exception cases actually accumulated and cycle times rose. This suggests a real operational dependency. Her exception-flag share (0.48) and involvement in reopens (64%) and complaints (71%) are substantive, not just logging effects. If it were purely an artifact, her absence would not have operational impact.

**Observable predictions.** If artifact, aggregating her short events into a single "coordination" activity should reduce her handover centrality dramatically. Filtering out `Assign Specialist` and `Reopen Claim` should weaken her network position. Using a longer time window for working-together (e.g., co-occurrence within 24 hours) should increase her co-occurrence with adjusters and specialists. However, the absence impact would persist if the dependency is real.

## Hypothesis 5: Quality gatekeeper / informal control point (with uncontrolled discretion)

**Mechanism.** Mira acts as a human control point in an otherwise automated process. `Bot_Rules` performs initial fraud screening and routing, but exceptions go to her for human judgment. She decides whether to escalate, reopen, or assign specialists. She is a quality gatekeeper, but her discretion is uncontrolled—she both detects problems and selects the next specialist, creating a segregation-of-duties concern.

**Evidence supporting.** High exception-flag share (0.48). Compliance reviewer's concern about "uncontrolled discretion." Her activities include `Escalate Complaint` and `Reopen Claim` (control-type activities). She appears after `Bot_Rules` on exception cases. Her absence caused exception cases to accumulate, suggesting she is the gatekeeper for exception flow. She touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints—sensitive control points.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting.** If she were an effective quality gatekeeper, we might expect lower exception rates downstream of her, but we do not have that data. Her low working-together with team leads might mean she operates outside formal quality controls. The compliance concern is about potential risk, not evidence of wrongdoing. Also, her short service times (4.2 min) might suggest superficial review rather than thorough gatekeeping.

**Observable predictions.** If quality gatekeeper, cases she handles should have different downstream outcomes (e.g., fewer reopens, lower fraud rates) compared to cases routed directly to team leads. Her decisions should be auditable and consistent; we would expect to see decision patterns in the log. If her discretion is uncontrolled, we might see variability in her assignments that is not explained by case attributes (e.g., assigning similar medical cases to different specialists without clear rationale).

# 3. Risk assessment

**Bottleneck risk: Moderate to high, but only for exception flow.** Mira's median service time is 4.2 min, so she is not slow per event. The bottleneck is her availability as a decision point. The absence experiment shows that when she is unavailable, `exception_flag=True` cases accumulate and p90 cycle time rises from 8.4 to 13.9 days. Ordinary low-value claims are unaffected. This is a concentrated bottleneck risk: the process has a single point of failure for exception handling. However, the risk is not that she is overloaded in normal times (her service times are short), but that the process cannot function without her for exceptions. Note that `Vendor_Verify` (2.1 hours) and `Vendor_Pay` (1.3 hours) are separate waiting-time components, but they are external and not human decision bottlenecks.

**Coordination risk: High.** Mira is the primary bridge between `Bot_Rules`, team leads, adjusters, and specialists. Her high handover betweenness (0.77) and subcontracting betweenness (0.69) indicate that many work paths pass through her. Her low working-together with team leads (4–5%) suggests coordination is episodic and one-to-many, not collaborative. If she is removed or unavailable, coordination may fragment. The operations manager warns that removing her would increase formal escalations and specialist overload. This is a coordination risk: the process relies on her as an informal coordination mechanism.

**Key-person risk: High.** The evidence strongly suggests key-person dependency: tacit knowledge ("knows where hard cases should go"), absence impact on exception cases, high centrality, and no evidence of documented procedures or cross-training. If Mira leaves or is incapacitated, the organization risks losing critical routing knowledge. This is a classic key-person risk. Mitigation requires documentation, cross-training, and redundancy.

**Compliance/control risk: Moderate to high, but no evidence of wrongdoing.** The compliance reviewer's concern is valid: Mira both detects problems (exception routing from `Bot_Rules`) and selects the next specialist (`Assign Specialist`). This concentration of decision rights creates a segregation-of-duties issue. Her high involvement in reopens (64%) and complaints (71%) means she is involved in sensitive decisions. However, there is no direct evidence of wrongdoing, fraud, or misconduct. The risk is structural: uncontrolled discretion. Her assignments should be auditable, and high-value or high-risk cases should have a review step. The risk is not that she is corrupt, but that the control design is weak.

**Workload fairness risk: Moderate.** Mira touches 22% of all claims but 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Her exception-flag share (0.48) is much higher than team leads (0.21–0.26) and adjusters (0.29–0.36). Her work is concentrated in difficult, exception-heavy cases. This could lead to burnout, especially since exception work is cognitively demanding even if her logged service times are short (4.2 min). The fairness issue is that exception work is disproportionately routed to one person. Other coordinators (Alice, Bruno) have lower exception shares. This may be a workload distribution problem.

**Data-quality risk: Moderate.** The contradiction between high handover centrality and low working-together centrality could be partly a logging artifact. Her short events may inflate handover metrics and A-B-A subcontracting patterns. The subcontracting network with *n*=2 may over-represent her. There is also a risk that `Assign Specialist` is logged as a separate event when it is actually part of another activity, or that timestamps are imprecise for short events. Data-quality issues could lead to misinterpretation of her role. However, the absence experiment provides behavioral evidence that mitigates the data-quality concern: the operational impact during her absence suggests her role is real, not just a logging artifact.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

1. **Do not remove Mira_Coordinator.** The absence evidence shows that exception cases would stall without her. Any removal must be preceded by a replacement plan.

2. **Implement cross-training and documentation.** Have `Alice_TeamLead`, `Bruno_TeamLead`, or a senior adjuster shadow Mira for exception routing. Document her routing logic in a decision tree or rules table. This reduces key-person risk.

3. **Add a backup exception desk.** Designate a second resource (e.g., a senior adjuster or a second coordinator) who can handle exception routing during Mira's absence. This reduces single-point-of-failure risk.

4. **Improve logging for auditability.** Ensure that Mira's `Assign Specialist` events include a reason code (e.g., case attribute, specialist availability, complexity score). This makes her discretion auditable and addresses the compliance concern.

5. **Implement segregation of duties for high-value cases.** For cases above a certain `amount_band` or `priority`, require a second review of Mira's specialist assignment (e.g., by a team lead). This mitigates the uncontrolled discretion risk without removing her coordination function.

## What should be monitored

1. **Exception-case cycle times.** Monitor p90 cycle time for `exception_flag=True` cases, especially during any absence or high-load period. Set alerts if p90 exceeds a threshold (e.g., 10 days).

2. **Queue lengths before Mira.** Monitor the waiting time between `Bot_Rules` exception routing and Mira's first event. If this grows, it indicates a bottleneck forming.

3. **Workload distribution.** Monitor the share of exception cases, reopens, and complaints handled by Mira vs other coordinators. If her share exceeds a threshold (e.g., 50% of exceptions), redistribute.

4. **Network centrality over time.** Recompute handover and subcontracting centrality for Mira monthly. If her centrality increases, key-person risk is growing.

5. **Compliance audits.** Audit a random sample of Mira's specialist assignments quarterly. Check for consistency, appropriateness, and adherence to documented rules.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

1. **Do not restructure the exception desk or reassign her cases to team leads.** The operations manager warns this would increase formal escalations and specialist overload. Without understanding her routing logic, redistribution could degrade performance.

2. **Do not automate her routing decisions.** Replacing her with rules or a bot is premature. Her tacit knowledge is not yet documented, and the absence evidence suggests human judgment is currently critical for exception cases.

3. **Do not conclude she is peripheral based on working-together centrality.** The low co-occurrence is likely a measurement artifact of short events. Do not use working-together centrality alone to assess her importance.

4. **Do not assume wrongdoing or fraud.** The compliance concern is about uncontrolled discretion, not evidence of misconduct. No direct evidence of wrongdoing exists in the data.

5. **Do not change the event logging granularity without testing.** Aggregating her short events might reduce centrality metrics, but it could also lose valuable information about coordination patterns. Test the impact on analysis before changing logging.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator is the central routing hub for exception claims. Process mining shows she has the highest handover and subcontracting centrality, yet she rarely co-occurs with other resources on the same case. This is not a contradiction: her work consists of short, discrete coordination events—assigning specialists, reopening claims, escalating complaints—after which she hands the case back to adjusters or specialists. She touches only 22% of claims but handles 64% of reopens and 71% of complaints.

Her importance is confirmed by a natural experiment: during her six-day absence, ordinary claims were unaffected, but exception cases accumulated and their 90th-percentile cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. This indicates a key-person dependency, not a general bottleneck.

The main risks are key-person dependency, uncontrolled discretion (she both detects problems and selects specialists), and workload concentration on difficult cases. She should not be removed. Instead, management should cross-train a backup, document her routing logic, add audit logging for her assignments, and monitor exception-case cycle times. Further analysis should test whether her centrality is partly a logging artifact.
