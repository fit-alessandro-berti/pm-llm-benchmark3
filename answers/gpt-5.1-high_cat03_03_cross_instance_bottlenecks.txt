## 1. Interaction-graph description

We represent the system as a typed interaction graph whose nodes are **concrete objects** and whose edges represent time-bounded interactions derived from events.

- **Node types**
  - Jobs: `J1…J6`
  - Components: `C1…C6`
  - ComponentLots: `L7, L8`
  - Machines: `CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1`
  - Tools: `FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3`
  - Batches: `B1, B2`
  - Shipments: `S1, S2, S3`

- **Core edge types (with qualifiers)**
  - `Job -requires→ Component` (e.g. `J2 -requires→ C2`)
  - `Component -in_lot→ ComponentLot` (e.g. `C2 -in_lot→ L7`)
  - `Job -batched_in→ Batch` (e.g. `J1 -batched_in→ B1`)
  - `Batch -processed_on→ Machine` (e.g. `B1 -processed_on→ OVEN-1`)
  - `Batch -uses_tool→ Tool` (e.g. `B1 -uses_tool→ RACK-7`)
  - `Job -uses_machine→ Machine` during `Machine Process` or `Final Test`
  - `Job -uses_tool→ Tool` during `Acquire/Release` or calibration (e.g. `J3 -uses_tool→ FIX-A`)
  - `ComponentLot -status_change→ {Quarantine, Release}` with incident jobs (`L7 -quarantine→ {J2,J5}`)
  - `Job -packed_into→ Shipment`, `Shipment -dispatches→ (time)` (e.g. `J1 -packed_into→ S1`)
  - Temporal edges such as `Job -ready_for_batch→ Batch` and `Job -ready_for_test→ TEST-1` are inferred from readiness timestamps.

- **Higher-order motifs (≥ 3 types)**

  1. **Batch–tool–machine synchronization motif**  
     `Job → Batch → {OVEN-1, RACK-7}`  
     Jobs `J1,J2,J3` join `B1`, which is cured on `OVEN-1` using `RACK-7`. Readiness times (08:10, 08:25, 11:55) plus a single `Batch Cure` event at 12:05 create a synchronization barrier: early jobs cannot proceed until the last job and shared rack are available.

  2. **Tool-gated machine motif at CNC-2**  
     `Job → Machine (CNC-2) ← Tool (FIX-A)`  
     `J3` holds `FIX-A` on `CNC-2` from 09:00–11:40. `J4` reaches `CNC-2` at 09:50, while `CNC-2` is idle 10:05–10:47. The absence of a `Wait for Tool` event means the edge `J4 -blocked_by→ FIX-A` is inferred from the joint pattern: job present, machine idle, tool held by another job.

  3. **Lot-quarantine coupling motif**  
     `Job → Component → ComponentLot (L7) → {Quarantine, Release}`  
     Components `C2` and `C5` for `J2` and `J5` share lot `L7`. A `Quarantine Lot` event at 10:20 and a `Release Lot` at 13:50 both relate to `L7, C2, C5, J2, J5`. `J2` enters `B1` before release due to an override, while `J5` is held back, so the lot state synchronizes two otherwise independent jobs.

  4. **Shipment-consolidation motif**  
     `Job → Shipment → Dispatch` and cross-job coupling via shared shipments.  
     `J1` and `J4` are both assigned to `S1`. `J1` is packed at 13:30, `J4` at 19:10, and `S1` dispatches at 19:25. Thus the shipment node creates a long, cross-instance dependency from `J4` back to `J1`.

  5. **Final-test calibration motif**  
     `Job → TEST-1 → CAL-3`  
     `J5` and `J6` both require `TEST-1` and `CAL-3`. `J5` tests 16:10–16:35, `CAL-3` then recalibrates 16:35–17:20, and only then can `J6` test 17:25–17:50. Tool recalibration on an otherwise idle machine creates a non-obvious blocking edge.

- **Observed vs. inferred edges**

  - **Directly observed**: edges connecting all objects that share an event in the log:
    - `Job–Component` from `requires`
    - `Component–ComponentLot` from `contains`
    - `Job–Batch–Machine–Tool` from `Batch Cure` on `OVEN-1` with `RACK-7`
    - `Job–Machine–Tool` from `Machine Process`, `Acquire Tool`, `Release Tool`
    - `ComponentLot–Job` at `Quarantine Lot` and `Release Lot`
    - `Job–Shipment` from `Pack`, `Load`, `Dispatch`
  - **Inferred**:
    - `Job -waits_for_batch→ Batch` from `(ready_for_batch time, batch start time)`
    - `Job -blocked_by_tool→ Tool` where job has reached machine, machine is idle, but tool is in use elsewhere (e.g. `J4` vs `FIX-A`)
    - `Job -affected_by_lot_state→ ComponentLot` when lot state changes (`Quarantine`, `Release`) fall between the job’s upstream and downstream events
    - `Job -waits_for_shipment→ Shipment` from time between `Pack` and shipment `Dispatch`

- **Compact adjacency-style view (selected)**

  - `J1: requires→C1, batched_in→B1, lot→L8 (via C1), cured_on→OVEN-1 (via B1), uses_tool→RACK-7 (via B1), shipped_in→S1`
  - `J2: requires→C2, lot→L7, batched_in→B1, cured_on→OVEN-1, uses_tool→RACK-7, shipped_in→S2`
  - `J3: requires→C3, lot→L8, batched_in→B1, cured_on→OVEN-1, uses_tool→RACK-7, uses_tool→FIX-A, processed_on→CNC-2, shipped_in→S2`
  - `J4: requires→C4, processed_on→CNC-2 (needs FIX-A), batched_in→B2, cured_on→OVEN-1, uses_tool→RACK-7, shipped_in→S1`
  - `J5: requires→C5, lot→L7, batched_in→B2, cured_on→OVEN-1, uses_tool→RACK-7, tested_on→TEST-1 (uses CAL-3), shipped_in→S3`
  - `J6: requires→C6, tested_on→TEST-1 (uses CAL-3), shipped_in→S3`

These interlocking motifs explain how jobs that look independent in a job-only DFG are in fact coupled through shared batches, tools, lots, and shipments.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

All calculations are **“what-if” magnitudes**, not proofs of causality.

### 2.1 Synchronization-induced waiting for J1 in B1

- Observed:
  - `J1` ready for batch at **08:10**
  - `B1` starts `Batch Cure` at **12:05**
- Calculation:  
  - Waiting\(_{J1,B1}\) = 12:05 − 08:10 = **3 h 55 min** (235 min).
- Counterfactual assumption: with identical cycle time and available `OVEN-1`/`RACK-7`, `J1` could have been cured in a single-job or earlier partial batch starting at 08:10. Under that assumption, the **full 3 h 55 min** is attributable to batch synchronization (waiting for `J2`, `J3`, or tool availability).

### 2.2 Synchronization-induced waiting for J2 in B1

- Observed:
  - `J2` ready for `B1` at **08:25**
  - `B1` starts at **12:05**
- Calculation:  
  - Waiting\(_{J2,B1}\) = 12:05 − 08:25 = **3 h 40 min** (220 min).
- Counterfactual assumption: same as for `J1`—`J2` could have been cured in an earlier batch starting at 08:25 if not constrained to move with the rest of `B1`. Thus **3 h 40 min** is the synchronization-induced component for `J2`.

### 2.3 Delay plausibly associated with FIX-A for J4

Key facts:

- `J3` holds `FIX-A` 09:00–11:40.
- `J4` reaches `CNC-2` at **09:50**.
- `CNC-2` is idle **10:05–10:47**.
- `J4`’s `Machine Process` starts at **11:45** and uses `FIX-A`.

If `J4` had its own dedicated fixture, it could in principle start as soon as `CNC-2` is idle and the job is present.

- Earliest feasible start without fixture contention (counterfactual): **10:05** (job at machine and machine idle).
- Actual start: **11:45**.
- Calculation:  
  - Delay\(_{J4,FIX-A}\) ≈ 11:45 − 10:05 = **1 h 40 min** (100 min).

We attribute this 1 h 40 min to fixture-driven contention **under the counterfactual** of unconstrained fixture availability. The data also show that some of this period (10:05–10:47) overlaps with `CNC-2` idle time, which strengthens the tool-gating interpretation but does not prove it.

### 2.4 Delay imposed on J1 by S1 consolidation

- `J1` is packed at **13:30**.
- `J4` (the other job in `S1`) is packed at **19:10**.
- `S1` dispatches at **19:25**.

If shipping policy allowed `J1` to leave as soon as it was packed (job-specific shipment) the dispatch time could be 13:30 plus a short handling lag.

- Simple counterfactual: dispatch `J1` immediately at **13:30**.
- Actual dispatch: **19:25**.
- Calculation:  
  - Delay\(_{J1,S1}\) = 19:25 − 13:30 = **5 h 55 min** (355 min).

Even if we use a more conservative baseline equal to “25 minutes after packed” (in line with `S2`’s 25-minute lag), `J1` would dispatch at ~13:55, so consolidation still adds about **5 h 30 min** of extra calendar time.

### 2.5 Delay plausibly associated with CAL-3 for J6

Key facts:

- `J5` ready for final test: **16:00**.
- `J6` ready for final test: **16:05**.
- `J5` tests 16:10–16:35 (occupying `TEST-1` and `CAL-3`).
- `CAL-3` recalibrates 16:35–17:20.
- `J6` tests 17:25–17:50.
- `TEST-1` is otherwise idle 16:35–17:25.

Total waiting for `J6` from readiness to test start:

- 17:25 − 16:05 = **1 h 20 min** (80 min).

Decomposition:

1. **Queue behind J5’s test** (machine and tool both busy):  
   16:10–16:35 (~25 min). Some of the 5 minutes between 16:05 and 16:10 is also queueing/setup but is small.

2. **Calibration-induced wait with idle machine**:  
   16:35–17:20 = **45 min** while `TEST-1` is idle but `CAL-3` is recalibrating 16:35–17:20.

Under the counterfactual “no recalibration between `J5` and `J6`” (or faster/parallel calibration), `J6` could start immediately after `J5` at 16:35. Then:

- Delay\(_{J6,CAL-3}\) = 17:25 − 16:35 = **50 min**, dominated by the 45-minute calibration plus a small setup lag.

This quantifies how much of `J6`’s 80-minute waiting time is plausibly attributable to calibration policy around `CAL-3`, without asserting that policy change is feasible or risk-free.

## 4. What flattening misses

### Job-centric flattening

- **Visible**: per-job activity sequences (`Release Job → Stage Component → Machine Process → Inspect → Pack → Dispatch`), activity-level performance, and evidence that no single job is an extreme outlier (no job >1.8σ above mean duration).
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Cross-job synchronization via `B1/B2`, `RACK-7`, `FIX-A`, `CAL-3`, and shipments; tool holds and lot quarantine appear only indirectly as longer waiting between activities.
  - Lot events are entirely missing, so `J2`/`J5`’s shared dependence on `L7` is invisible.
  - Shipment consolidation is reduced to `Pack → Dispatch`, not pack-to-dispatch waiting.
- **Misleading conclusion**: One might conclude “the system is balanced and there is no meaningful bottleneck, as job durations are similar,” overlooking that jobs can be jointly delayed by batches, tools, and shipments even when none stands out individually.

### Machine-centric flattening

- **Visible**: utilization (44–58%), long idle gaps (up to 73 minutes), and neat sequences like `Setup → Process → Release`.
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Reasons why machines are idle (e.g., waiting for `RACK-7`, `FIX-A`, lot release, or shipment capacity).
  - Whether idle time overlaps with jobs being ready, which is crucial for diagnosing synchronization vs capacity.
- **Misleading conclusion**: With utilization well below 80%, an analyst might dismiss machine capacity as a concern and search elsewhere, even though machines are idle **because** of upstream synchronization and tool/lot/shipment policies—which are not visible in this view.

### Batch-centric flattening

- **Visible**: `B1` and `B2` lifecycles on `OVEN-1`, including start and finish times and association with `RACK-7`.
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Individual job readiness times; `B1` appears as a single event even though `J1` and `J2` were ready hours earlier.
  - Interactions with downstream shipments (`S1,S2,S3`), so batch-induced lateness propagating into shipping is obscured.
- **Misleading conclusion**: A batch DFG might show that `Batch Cure` steps are short and ovens underutilized, tempting the conclusion that batching is harmless, when in fact the **choice of when to start each batch** creates large, invisible queues at the job level.

### Tool-centric flattening

- **Visible**: acquire/release intervals for `FIX-A`, cleaning/quarantine for `RACK-7`, calibrations for `CAL-3`, and overlaps between tools and machines.
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Which jobs could have proceeded on idle machines but did not because a tool was missing (requires joining with job and machine timelines).
  - Business rules (e.g., mandatory calibration or cleaning frequencies) that turn these tool intervals into constraints rather than inefficiencies.
- **Misleading conclusion**: A tool-centric DFG might emphasize high “busy time” for `CAL-3` or `RACK-7` and suggest tools are the sole bottleneck, ignoring that some tool intervals (e.g., calibrations) are policy-driven and may be non-negotiable, while others (e.g., long holds of `FIX-A`) may be avoidable.

### ComponentLot-centric flattening

- **Visible**: quarantine and release episodes for `L7`, showing a 3 h 30 min constrained window (10:20–13:50) that jointly affects `C2`/`C5` and thus `J2`/`J5`.
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Job paths are only partially linked: the pipeline that generated the job DFG omitted lot events, so many downstream consequences of quarantine are not obvious.
  - Overrides (like the supervisor override for `J2`) live in a separate system and would be missing even in a lot-centric view unless integrated.
- **Misleading conclusion**: One might overstate lot quarantine as the *primary* driver of all variability, because its durations seem large in isolation, without seeing that some jobs escape via overrides or that other bottlenecks (batches, shipments) can dominate for unaffected jobs.

### Shipment-centric flattening

- **Visible**: `Create Shipment → Load → Dispatch` sequences and their durations (e.g., median 0.8 h then 0.3 h).
- **Hidden/duplicated**:
  - Pack-to-dispatch waiting per job (`J1` waits from 13:30 to 19:25) is not represented; the DFG starts at `Create Shipment`.
  - Cross-job coupling within a shipment: early-ready jobs’ waiting is folded into shipment-level idle time, not obvious as per-job delay.
- **Misleading conclusion**: “Shipments are fast; they leave within about 1.1 hours of creation,” leading to the belief that logistics is efficient, when in fact **shipment creation itself may be delayed for hours** to consolidate jobs, as seen for `S1`.

### Why low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting

Machines are underutilized because **constraints reside at other layers of the interaction graph**:

- Jobs wait for **batches** to be filled (`B1,B2`), even when ovens are idle.
- Jobs wait for **tools** (`FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3`) or for their cleaning/calibration cycles, during which machines can sit idle.
- Jobs wait for **component-lot release** (`L7` quarantine) or **shipment partners** (`S1` consolidation), neither of which consume machine time.
- Some constraints are further hidden by **incomplete logging**, so job- and machine-centric flattenings attribute the waiting to generic “slowness”.

Thus, job waiting is dominated by **synchronization and policy-induced couplings across objects**, not by pure lack of machine capacity, allowing long queues to coexist with modest machine utilization.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

### 5.1 Narrative A: J1 from early readiness to late shipment

- **Temporal precedence**
  - `J1` becomes ready for batch at **08:10**.
  - `B1` starts curing at **12:05** on `OVEN-1` with `RACK-7`.
  - `J1` is packed at **13:30**.
  - `J4` (sharing shipment `S1`) is packed at **19:10**.
  - `S1` dispatches at **19:25**.
- **Resource feasibility**
  - `OVEN-1` later shows a long idle period before `B2`; nothing in the data says it was fully loaded before 12:05, so earlier processing of `J1` in a separate batch is technically feasible.
  - Shipping infrastructure is capable of dispatching `S2` and `S3` much sooner after pack, so the long wait for `S1` is not purely a transport-capacity issue.
- **Policy constraints**
  - Batching policy on `OVEN-1` appears to favor filling `B1` with multiple jobs (`J1,J2,J3`) before curing.
  - Shipment policy for `S1` appears to require both `J1` and `J4` to be present before dispatch.
- **Causal speculation**
  - `J1`’s completion time is stretched by **two distinct synchronization layers**: first by `B1` on `OVEN-1`, then by `S1` consolidation with `J4`. These cross-instance couplings plausibly explain why `J1` experiences long calendar time in system despite no extreme per-activity durations.

### 5.2 Narrative B: J4’s path through CNC-2, B2, and S1

- **Temporal precedence**
  - `J4` reaches `CNC-2` at **09:50**.
  - `J3` holds `FIX-A` 09:00–11:40; `CNC-2` is idle 10:05–10:47.
  - `J4`’s `Machine Process` on `CNC-2` starts at **11:45** with `FIX-A`.
  - `J4` is then part of `B2`, ready at **13:10** while `J5` is ready only at **14:40**; `B2` starts at **14:50** on `OVEN-1` with `RACK-7`, which emerges from cleaning/quarantine at 14:35.
  - `J4` is packed at **19:10** and shipped in `S1` at **19:25**.
- **Resource feasibility**
  - Between 10:05 and 10:47, `CNC-2` is idle and `J4` is present; absent fixture constraints, processing could have started earlier.
  - `OVEN-1` is idle from 12:55–14:50, so once `J4` is ready at 13:10, oven capacity is not binding; start of `B2` is governed by `J5`’s readiness and `RACK-7`’s availability.
- **Policy constraints**
  - Implicit rule: `CNC-2` cannot process `J4` without `FIX-A`, and the system does not record an explicit waiting state.
  - Batch `B2` seems to require both `J4` and `J5` and the specific rack `RACK-7`.
  - Shipment `S1` waits for both `J1` and `J4` before dispatch.
- **Causal speculation**
  - `J4` is linked into a chain of cross-instance dependencies: fixture sharing with `J3`, batch synchronization with `J5` and `RACK-7`, and shipment consolidation with `J1`. Each link plausibly adds tens of minutes to hours of delay, without generating any severe per-machine utilization signal.

### 5.3 Narrative C: J2 and J5 coupled via lot L7 and downstream flows

- **Temporal precedence**
  - Components `C2` (`J2`) and `C5` (`J5`) share `L7`.
  - `Quarantine Lot` at **10:20** and `Release Lot` at **13:50** both refer to `L7, C2, C5, J2, J5`.
  - `J2` manages to enter `B1` before 12:05 due to a supervisor override recorded in another system.
  - `J5` does not proceed until after 13:50, and is later ready for `B2` at **14:40**, which then starts at 14:50.
- **Resource feasibility**
  - The quarantine interval 10:20–13:50 forms a window where use of `L7`-components is restricted; other jobs not tied to `L7` proceed.
  - Since `J2` progresses despite quarantine, process and tools are available if organizational rules allow overrides.
- **Policy constraints**
  - Lot quarantine policy restricts use of `L7` until investigation or quality assurance is complete.
  - Overrides are allowed but exceptional, indicating strong governance over when lot-related risk is accepted.
- **Causal speculation**
  - For `J5`, lot quarantine is plausibly a primary blocker: it cannot proceed until `L7` is released. For `J2`, the override weakens the coupling, but still indicates that **lot state is a potential cross-job gate**: in other instances without override, multiple jobs could be delayed together. This lot-level policy introduces a non-machine bottleneck that is largely invisible in traditional DFGs.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

### 6.1 Reduce batch synchronization on OVEN-1

- **Target mechanism**: Batch synchronization (`B1,B2`) with `RACK-7` on `OVEN-1`.
- **Expected benefit**: Reduce waiting of early-ready jobs like `J1`, `J2`, `J4` by allowing smaller or more dynamically scheduled batches.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: Jobs frequently ready earlier than their batch-mates; batches using `RACK-7`; `OVEN-1` utilization may become more uniform.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: Late-ready jobs may experience slightly more waiting if batch sizes shrink; energy consumption or setup overhead for `OVEN-1` and `RACK-7` may increase.
- **New cost or risk**: Higher number of oven cycles, potential wear on `RACK-7`, and operator workload; risk of underfilling batches and raising unit cost.
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Average oven utilization” or “average batch size” could push planners to overconsolidate batches, reintroducing long waits.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: Compare **per-job ready-to-cure waiting times** for jobs in `B1/B2`-like batches before and after policy change, controlling for due dates, using OCEL features (e.g., `extract_ocel_features` for job-level waiting) rather than only batch-level metrics.

### 6.2 Add or reschedule critical fixtures (FIX-A)

- **Target mechanism**: Shared-tool contention at `CNC-2` due to `FIX-A`.
- **Expected benefit**: Shorten delays like the ~1 h 40 min postponement of `J4` by either adding a second fixture or scheduling jobs with awareness of fixture availability.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: Jobs routed through `CNC-2` requiring `FIX-A`; `CNC-2` utilization becomes more aligned with job readiness instead of tool availability.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: Investment in an extra fixture might be underused outside peak periods; other tools or machines may become the new constraint.
- **New cost or risk**: Capital cost, storage, maintenance (e.g., more calibration, inspections of multiple fixtures).
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Fixture utilization” could motivate keeping fixtures artificially busy (e.g., doing unnecessary setups) rather than minimizing job lead time.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: Track **job-level CNC-2 waiting times** and **overlaps of idle machine vs. tool holds** (from tool timelines) before/after adding a fixture, using object-interaction summaries rather than just machine OEE.

### 6.3 Adjust shipment consolidation policy (especially S1-type patterns)

- **Target mechanism**: Shipment consolidation delays for early-complete jobs.
- **Expected benefit**: Reduce pack-to-dispatch waiting for jobs like `J1` by allowing earlier partial shipments or time-bounded consolidation windows.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: Early-completing jobs tied to slow partners; customers whose orders include such jobs.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: Later-finishing jobs might ship separately, increasing freight cost or reducing consolidation efficiency; warehouse operations may become busier with more, smaller shipments.
- **New cost or risk**: Higher shipping cost, more packaging materials, potential loss of volume discounts.
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Average cost per shipment” could discourage releasing early shipments, reintroducing long waits; conversely, “number of shipments per day” could push too many small shipments.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: For shipments with multiple jobs, compare **per-job pack-to-dispatch times** and customer-facing lead times before and after tweaking consolidation rules, using shipment–job object relations rather than shipment-level aggregates alone.

### 6.4 Improve calibration scheduling and/or CAL-3 capacity

- **Target mechanism**: Calibration-driven blocking at `TEST-1` via `CAL-3`.
- **Expected benefit**: Reduce waits like the 45–50 min calibration-induced delay for `J6` by grouping tests between calibrations or adding a second calibration tool.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: Jobs requiring final test late in the day or after long calibration cycles; `TEST-1` utilization becomes more aligned with job readiness.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: Larger test batches between calibrations might increase risk if calibration drift is not well understood; regulatory compliance must be preserved.
- **New cost or risk**: Additional calibration tool cost, more complex calibration schedule, possible quality risk if intervals between calibrations are lengthened.
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Number of calibrations per day” could be driven down at the expense of quality; “throughput of final tests” alone might push unsafe reductions in calibration frequency.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: Use OCEL to compare **job-level ready-to-test vs. test-start delays** for final-test jobs and correlate with calibration episodes on `CAL-3`, ensuring that waiting assessments do not rely solely on machine idle gaps.

### 6.5 Strengthen logging and OCEL integration (non-operational intervention)

- **Target mechanism**: Data-integration artifacts masking true dependencies.
- **Expected benefit**: Make tool waits, lot quarantine, and overrides explicit, enabling more reliable diagnosis and monitoring of bottlenecks.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: All jobs, indirectly; better visibility allows targeted improvements without guesswork.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: None operationally, but additional logging can increase analyst workload and system complexity.
- **New cost or risk**: Implementation effort, risk of noisy logs or privacy/compliance issues if fields are mishandled.
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Number of events per case” could motivate under-logging to make dashboards look cleaner.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: Evaluate completeness of **object-interaction graphs** before/after integration by comparing counts and types of `Job–Tool`, `Job–ComponentLot`, and `Job–Batch` edges, and verifying that inferred waits (e.g., for tools) can be replaced by explicit `Wait` events.

### 6.6 Refine lot-quarantine and override policy

- **Target mechanism**: Component-lot release coupling via `L7` and similar lots.
- **Expected benefit**: Reduce unnecessary waiting for jobs like `J5` while maintaining quality; clarify when overrides (like for `J2`) are appropriate.
- **Jobs/objects helped**: Jobs whose components belong to lots that pass investigation quickly; quality/QA teams gain clearer decision rules.
- **Jobs/objects potentially harmed**: If policy becomes too permissive, risk of shipping defective parts; if too restrictive, more jobs share long quarantines.
- **New cost or risk**: More QA work, need for better traceability; potential rework or recall if overrides misfire.
- **Metric with perverse-incentive risk**: “Number of quarantined lots” or “average quarantine duration” might push hasty releases; “zero overrides” could discourage justified flexibility.
- **Object-centric evaluation design**: Compare **lot-level quarantine durations** and **job-level delays for jobs tied to quarantined lots** before/after policy change, stratified by whether overrides occur, using ComponentLot–Job relations.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

### 8.1 Observations that would weaken the top-ranked explanation

Assuming the primary explanation is **batch synchronization with shared racks on OVEN-1**, the following observations would reduce confidence in that ranking:

1. Many instances of batches like `B1` where all constituent jobs are ready within a few minutes of each other, yet similar long delays still occur for unrelated jobs—suggesting other constraints dominate.
2. Evidence that `OVEN-1` and `RACK-7` were actually occupied by other (currently unlogged) batches or maintenance tasks during the 08:10–12:05 and 13:10–14:50 windows, so earlier starts were infeasible.
3. Data showing that early-ready jobs (`J1,J2`) consistently have due dates much later than late-ready jobs (`J3,J5`), and batching is intentionally aligned with due dates, making the observed waits policy-optimal rather than bottleneck-induced.
4. Cases where tool and lot policies (e.g., `FIX-A`, `CAL-3`, `L7`) clearly generate large waits even when batch policies are flexible and ovens start promptly.
5. Additional logs indicating that pack-to-dispatch waits like `J1`’s 5 h 55 are rare exceptions rather than typical behavior for multi-job shipments, undermining the role of consolidation as a recurrent contributor.
6. A richer OCEL showing that jobs not in any oven batch (or in single-job batches) still experience long end-to-end durations indistinguishable from batched jobs, reducing the explanatory power of batch synchronization.

### 8.2 Additional fields or event types to discriminate among hypotheses

To better distinguish between batch, tool, lot, shipment, and machine-capacity mechanisms, the following additional data would be valuable:

1. **Explicit `Wait for Batch` and `Ready for Batch` events per job**, with references to the batch and oven, to separate processing time from synchronization-induced waiting.
2. **Explicit `Wait for Tool` and `Tool Unavailable` events** linked to both the job and tool (`FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3`), making tool-driven queues observable instead of inferred from idle machines.
3. **Complete linkage of lot events to all affected jobs**, plus a `Lot Override` event type referencing both the lot and the overridden jobs (as in `J2`), to quantify how often lot quarantine truly gates progress.
4. **Shipment planning events** such as `Assign Job to Shipment`, `Freeze Shipment`, and target departure times, so analysts can distinguish consolidation-driven waits from transport-window constraints.
5. **Per-job attributes** like due date, priority, and customer order, to test whether observed waits reflect bottlenecks or deliberate scheduling choices aligned with priorities.
6. **Calibration policy metadata and events**, e.g., `Calibration Required Because` fields (time-based, job-count-based, or drift-based) and `Calibration Window` definitions, to assess whether CAL-3 is a hard regulatory constraint or an adjustable operational choice.

Collecting these fields and events in an object-centric log would enable more rigorous tests of each hypothesis, clarifying whether observed cross-instance couplings are avoidable bottlenecks or intentional, policy-driven trade-offs.
