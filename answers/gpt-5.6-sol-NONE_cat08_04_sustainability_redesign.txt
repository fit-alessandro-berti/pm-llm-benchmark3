# 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Unit of analysis:** one spare-part order, with supplementary line-item, shipment, and batch measures where possible. Because the current log is flattened by order, per-order averages may conceal multiple items, partial shipments, and shared production batches.

**Inside the boundary**

- Order receipt, availability checking, allocation, backorder, and cancellation.
- Picking, production, batch waiting, CNC processing, heat treatment, inspection, and production rework.
- Packaging, packaging rework, and package material.
- Outbound transport through confirmed delivery, including second shipments and empty-return implications where attributable.
- Operational impacts represented by:
  - `energy_kwh`
  - `transport_kgco2e`
  - `material_scrap_kg`
  - `package_material_kg`
- Service, quality, and resilience consequences: cycle time, lateness, cancellation, damage/return or rework loops, route availability, and emergency exceptions.
- Obsolescence of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days.

**Outside or incompletely represented**

- Embodied impacts of machines, buildings, vehicles, and supplier-produced raw materials.
- Full upstream material footprints beyond recorded scrap and packaging mass.
- Customer-site impacts and the economic/environmental cost of customer downtime.
- Full reverse logistics and repair impacts unless returns are linked back to the original order.
- Exact dedicated-van empty-return emissions, carrier network allocation, and mode-specific lifecycle factors.
- Inventory holding impacts apart from observed obsolescence.
- Customer travel or behavior outside the order process.

**Assumptions and uncertainty**

- Operational energy and transport emissions are directionally comparable, but should not be combined into one score without approved conversion factors.
- Promised lead time is treated as a constraint, not merely a performance target—especially for Service-contract and true Critical-downtime orders.
- SP1’s 85% on-time probability threshold should use calibrated route- and time-specific reliability, not median transit time alone.
- Emergency and partial-shipment variants have low model precision, so redesigns should be piloted rather than imposed solely from the aggregate DFG.
- The intervention ranges below are directional. They are not full lifecycle estimates.

Indicators should be reported by `case_type`, `customer_type`, route, machine group, batch-size band, expedite status, part weight/geometry, and disruption state. Aggregate improvements must not mask deterioration for Critical-downtime or Service-contract customers.

# 3. Intervention options

## 3.1 S1/S4: Slack-aware shipping recommender and priority pick

- **Target:** Standard-stock Service-contract orders, especially Package standard  Ship air cases. SP1 is currently violated in 22% of this segment.
- **Mechanism:** Use remaining promise slack, priority-pick completion, carrier reliability, cut-off time, and destination to select ground or consolidated transport when on-time probability exceeds 85%. Preserve air where slack or disruption risk is insufficient.
- **Effects:** Large transport reduction for eligible orders: observed stock-air emissions are 18.5 kgCO2e versus 3.6 for stock-ground. Priority picking may add small warehouse effort but can retain service. A blanket air ban would be unacceptable.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Recommendation and selected `route_mode`; predicted on-time probability; pick/package timestamps; `promised_lead_time_hours`; `expedite_flag`; actual lateness; SP1 override reason; route reliability calibration.

## 3.2 S5: Twice-daily truck consolidation for non-urgent stock orders

- **Target:** Stock  standard pack  ground orders with at least 12–24 hours of slack, primarily Regular customers.
- **Mechanism:** Pool compatible shipments into twice-daily consolidated-truck departures. Evidence indicates 35–55% lower transport emissions than individual ground parcels.
- **Effects:** Transport falls, but consolidation adds 12–24 hours. Eligibility must exclude orders whose on-time probability would fall below 85%. A fixed schedule may also improve predictability.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Package-ready to departure wait, truck fill, `route_mode`, transport emissions, missed cut-offs, late rate by segment, and conversion from consolidated to express mode.

## 3.3 S2: Dynamic batching with segment-specific waiting caps

- **Target:** Plan production  Batch wait  CNC and heat-treatment paths.
- **Mechanism:** Optimize batch size jointly with due-date slack. Seek CNC_A batches near the efficient range—energy per item falls from 2.8 kWh at size 1–3 to 1.3 at size 12–20—but stop waiting before segment-specific caps. Use shorter caps for Service-contract and urgent MTO orders.
- **Effects:** Lower processing energy without automatically accepting the 38-hour median wait associated with large batches. Heat-treatment batching requires stricter controls because batch waiting raises late risk 1.7 times. Demand-based caps also limit obsolescence.
- **Monitoring evidence:** `batch_id`, `batch_size`, batch-wait duration, machine group, kWh/item, due-date slack at release, lateness, expedite after waiting, and share unshipped after 14 days.

## 3.4 S3: Due-date-aware CNC_B-to-CNC_A routing

- **Target:** MTO cases otherwise routed through CNC_B, particularly the 5% path with rework, 32 kWh, 1.8 kg scrap, 160-hour median cycle time, and 32% late delivery.
- **Mechanism:** Prefer CNC_A when capability, queue, and due-date slack permit because its MTO rework rate is 9% versus 18% for CNC_B. Retain CNC_B as a fast or resilience alternative where CNC_A would cause unacceptable lateness.
- **Effects:** Likely reduces energy, scrap, and rework. Uncontrolled migration could overload CNC_A and create new batch or inspection queues.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Machine assignment decision, queue lengths, capability/part family, expected completion time, energy, scrap, first-pass yield, rework loop, and lateness by machine.

## 3.5 S7: Risk-based inspection quality gate

- **Target:** High-rework machine/part combinations, rushed inspections, and CNC_B outputs.
- **Mechanism:** Add protected inspection capacity or enhanced checks before packaging for combinations with high predicted defect risk. Prevent rushed inspection from displacing defects into packaging rework, returns, or customer failure.
- **Effects:** Modest inspection time and energy can avoid much larger rework impacts. The control should be risk-based rather than inspecting every order more intensively.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Inspection queue and duration, rush status, inspector load, machine/part combination, first-pass yield, Inspection  Rework path, packaging rework, linked returns, scrap, and downstream energy.

## 3.6 S6: Packaging-route-geometry compatibility decision

- **Target:** Package standard/reinforced  Rework packaging paths and heavy MTO products.
- **Mechanism:** Select packaging only after considering weight, geometry, fragility, route mode, and handling exposure. Enforce Q1 for MTO parts over 8 kg unless inspection certifies low damage risk.
- **Effects:** Cuts duplicate packaging and damage returns. Reinforced packaging should not be removed indiscriminately: it doubles material but reduces damage-related returns by 40% for heavy MTO parts.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Part weight/geometry, planned route, package type and material mass, compatibility result, Q1 certificate or override, repackaging, damage, returns, and shipment-mode changes after packing.

## 3.7 S8/S9: Triage Critical demand and create shared emergency lanes

- **Target:** Critical  emergency production  reinforced pack  dedicated van, currently 52 kgCO2e/order but only 3% late.
- **Mechanism:** Split Critical-downtime into verified active downtime and precautionary urgent. Preserve dedicated vans where downtime and slack justify them; route precautionary cases to stocked ground, shared emergency courier, or a scheduled priority lane. Pool emergency movements where geography and service windows permit.
- **Effects:** Potentially large transport reductions without abandoning emergency capability. Shared lanes can reduce empty returns, but pooling must not delay true downtime cases.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Criticality classification, downtime evidence, remaining slack, SP2 justification, van occupancy and empty-return distance, shared-lane wait, actual delivery time, downtime escalation, and alternate-route availability.

## 3.8 S11: Promise- and value-aware partial-shipment control

- **Target:** Stock  Partial shipment  Backorder paths, which show 79-hour median cycle time, 24% lateness, 7.2 kgCO2e, and two-shipment exposure.
- **Mechanism:** At availability check, compare customer downtime/service value with the impacts of a second shipment. Hold and combine non-urgent Regular orders when the complete order can still meet an accepted promise; permit partial shipment for needed Critical lines or explicit Service-contract requirements.
- **Effects:** Reduces transport, packaging, contacts, and handling. Waiting for completeness can worsen service or cancellation, so rules must be line-level and customer-approved.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Line availability, completeness date prediction, partial-shipment reason, number of consignments, package material, transport emissions, cancellation, lateness, and customer authorization.

## 3.9 S10: Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular MTO

- **Target:** Regular MTO orders with flexible requested dates.
- **Mechanism:** At order receipt, offer a transparent lower-impact promise window that enables efficient CNC/heat-treatment batching or ground transport, while retaining a faster option. Do not silently lengthen promises.
- **Effects:** Can lower energy and transport while reducing operational expedites. Excessively broad windows may reduce customer acceptance or create large speculative batches.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Offered and selected promise, estimated mode/batch, conversion or cancellation, realized cycle time, kWh, transport emissions, obsolescence, and later expedite requests.

## 3.10 S12: Structured sustainability and quality exception log

- **Target:** All SP1, SP2, and Q1 decision points.
- **Mechanism:** Add explicit events for recommendation, override, reason, evidence, approver, disruption state, and expiry. This addresses missing SP2 justification in 31% of dedicated-van cases and improves interpretability of low-precision emergency paths.
- **Effects:** Primarily enables control and learning; it may reduce unjustified high-impact modes. Poor implementation could add administrative delay, so the event should be integrated into dispatch decisions.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Conformance rates, override frequency by user/segment, decision latency, outcome of overrides, false-blocked emergencies, and repeat exception patterns.

## 3.11 Targeted critical-spares stocking

- **Target:** Repeat Critical-downtime parts currently requiring emergency production and dedicated transport.
- **Mechanism:** Use demand history and criticality to hold a small, rotating stock of stable, frequently demanded critical parts, enabling Stock  ground/shared priority routes.
- **Effects:** The observed critical stock/ground path has much lower impact but 9% lateness versus 3% for emergency van. Stocking should therefore be selective, with expiry/rotation and emergency fallback. Excess stock could merely displace impact into obsolescence.
- **Monitoring evidence:** Part-level demand, shelf life, stock turns, emergency-production avoidance, obsolescence, route choice, lateness, and service-contract downtime outcome.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batch energy versus waiting and expedites:** Larger CNC or heat-treatment batches reduce kWh per item but may create 38-hour waits, raise heat-treatment lateness risk 1.7 times, and trigger later air shipment or emergency production. Transport increases can erase production gains.

2. **Batch efficiency versus obsolescence:** Forecast-driven large batches can leave 6% of items unshipped after 14 days. Energy per produced item may improve while energy and scrap per actually used item worsen.

3. **Ground/consolidation versus cancellation or emergency recovery:** Replacing air with ground saves transport emissions, but an inaccurate reliability prediction may produce late delivery, cancellation, or a subsequent dedicated recovery shipment.

4. **Shared emergency transport versus customer downtime:** Pooling vans reduces empty running, but waiting for another consignment can impose disproportionate downtime for a genuine Critical order. Avoided vehicle emissions must not be prioritized over contractual and operational failure.

5. **Lighter packaging versus returns:** Reducing reinforced packaging lowers material per outbound shipment, yet heavy MTO damage may rise. Replacement production, new packaging, and return transport can create substantially greater total impact.

6. **More reinforced packaging versus unnecessary material:** Enforcing reinforcement beyond Q1’s heavy/risk cases would suppress damage but double packaging material for parts that do not need it. Compatibility and risk certification are preferable to a universal rule.

7. **Additional inspection versus queues:** A quality gate can prevent rework, but indiscriminate inspection adds queue time. That can cause lateness, rushed inspections elsewhere, or express transport downstream.

8. **CNC_A preference versus bottleneck displacement:** Moving work from CNC_B may halve rework exposure directionally, but concentrating demand on CNC_A can increase queueing, batch waits, and resilience dependence on one machine group.

9. **Combining shipments versus urgent-line service:** Avoiding partial shipments saves packaging and transport, but holding a line needed for repair can prolong customer downtime or cause cancellation. The decision must be line- and criticality-specific.

10. **Longer green promises versus behavioral rebound:** Customers may overstate urgency, split orders, or choose expedited options if green promise windows are too long or unreliable. Conversely, very cheap green windows may stimulate additional orders or inventory at the customer.

11. **Critical-stock creation versus inventory waste:** Stocking critical parts can eliminate emergency production and vans, but slow-moving or engineering-change-prone parts may become obsolete. This displaces environmental impact from fulfillment to inventory write-off.

12. **Policy compliance versus hidden workarounds:** Rigid SP1/SP2 controls may cause users to misclassify orders as Critical or transact outside the system. A quick, auditable exception path is needed to keep real behavior visible.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a **segmented, constraint-based redesign**, rather than a universal lowest-emission route.

1. **At order receipt and availability check**
   - Separate verified true downtime from precautionary urgency.
   - Calculate promise slack and route reliability.
   - Decide line-level partial shipment using criticality and predicted complete-availability time.
   - Offer Regular MTO customers an optional lower-impact promise window.
   - Identify a narrow set of repeat critical parts for rotating safety stock.

2. **At production planning**
   - Use dynamic batching with maximum wait caps by segment and due-date slack.
   - Favor CNC_A for high-rework combinations when capacity permits, while retaining CNC_B as a fast and resilience alternative.
   - Protect inspection capacity for high-risk machine/part combinations.
   - Include projected obsolescence and likely downstream expedite emissions in the batching decision, not energy per produced item alone.

3. **At packaging**
   - Check part geometry, weight, damage risk, and planned route before material is consumed.
   - Enforce Q1 for heavy MTO products, with a documented low-risk certification path.
   - Freeze or revalidate route choice after packaging to prevent incompatibility-driven repacking.

4. **At dispatch**
   - Apply SP1 through the slack-aware recommender: ground or consolidated truck only where calibrated on-time probability remains above 85%.
   - Use priority pick plus ground for eligible Standard-stock Service-contract orders, addressing the observed 22% SP1 violation.
   - Preserve air for insufficient-slack, disrupted, or otherwise justified Service-contract cases.
   - Preserve dedicated vans for verified downtime where shared lanes cannot meet the service requirement. Use shared emergency lanes for precautionary or geographically poolable cases and maintain an alternate route under R1.

5. **Across the process**
   - Record every SP1/SP2/Q1 override in a structured exception event.
   - Optimize for **on-time, first-pass-good delivery impact**, not isolated energy per item or transport emissions per shipment.

This package targets the strongest avoidable impacts—unjustified stock air freight, empty emergency movements, CNC_B rework, packaging rework, second shipments, and inefficient small batches—while retaining explicit escape paths for true criticality, quality protection, and carrier or machine disruption.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

## Pilot design

Run staged pilots by lane, machine family, and customer segment:

- Standard-stock Service-contract: recommender plus priority pick.
- Non-urgent Regular stock: twice-daily consolidation.
- Selected MTO families: dynamic batching, CNC routing, and inspection gate.
- Selected emergency geographies: criticality triage plus shared courier lanes.
- Heavy MTO: packaging compatibility and Q1 enforcement.

Use concurrent matched controls or stepped-wedge rollout. Match on case type, customer type, promised lead time, destination, weight/geometry, disruption state, machine capability, and order time. Compare at least one full demand cycle to avoid mistaking seasonality for improvement.

## Event-log changes

Add events and attributes for:

- Route recommendation, predicted on-time probability, chosen route, and override.
- Promise slack at each decision, not only the original promised duration.
- Criticality classification and evidence of active downtime.
- SP2 dedicated-van justification and approver.
- Part weight, geometry, fragility, packaging decision, compatibility result, and Q1 certificate.
- Planned versus actual batch release, maximum-wait cap, and reason for early release.
- Machine-routing recommendation and override.
- Inspection risk score, rush status, outcome, and linked return.
- Shipment identifier and line items to distinguish partial and repeat shipments.
- Van load, shared-lane occupancy, and empty-return distance.
- Batch output subsequently shipped, stocked, scrapped, or obsolete.

## Outcome evaluation

Evaluate jointly:

- Energy per shipped item and per on-time first-pass-good order.
- Transport kgCO2e across all consignments and returns.
- Scrap and package material, including repackaging.
- Median and p90 cycle time, late rate, cancellation, partial shipments, and post-wait expedites.
- First-pass yield, production rework, packaging rework, damage, and returns.
- Batch wait, kWh/item, and unshipped-after-14-days rate.
- Route and machine alternative availability during disruption.

Report every result by segment. Set non-inferiority guardrails for lateness, p90 cycle time, cancellation, return/rework, and true Critical-downtime performance. Environmental improvements should not pass governance if these guardrails are breached.

## Conformance monitoring

- **SP1:** Replay every air, dedicated-van, or unconsolidated shipment against the route options and predicted on-time probability available at decision time. Report eligible lower-carbon choices not taken, overrides, and model calibration.
- **SP2:** Require a justification event before dedicated-van dispatch, except a controlled break-glass path. Reduce the current 31% missing-justification rate and audit whether justified cases were genuinely Critical.
- **Q1:** Check each MTO part over 8 kg for reinforced packaging or a valid low-damage-risk inspection certificate. Link violations to repackaging, damage, return, and rework outcomes.
- **R1:** Continuously verify at least one viable alternative route for each Service-contract lane; do not count a nominal carrier that cannot meet the contractual window.

Because emergency and partial-shipment variants have low discovered-model precision, conformance should combine declarative rules with observed variant monitoring rather than treating every uncommon path as noncompliant.

## Exception governance and review

- Use standardized reasons: verified downtime, carrier disruption, safety/quality requirement, customer-authorized partial shipment, machine outage, or model-confidence failure.
- Require named ownership, evidence, authorization level, and expiry.
- Review frequent overrides weekly during pilots and monthly after stabilization.
- Investigate clusters by planner, carrier, part family, destination, and machine group.
- Track both false positives—green recommendations that cause service failure—and false negatives—unnecessary air, vans, reinforcement, or small batches.
- Maintain manual emergency authority, but automatically trigger retrospective review.
- Recalibrate route reliability and batching models when lateness calibration drifts or the case mix changes.
- Approve scale-up only when lifecycle-proxy indicators improve and service, quality, and resilience remain within agreed segment-level guardrails.
