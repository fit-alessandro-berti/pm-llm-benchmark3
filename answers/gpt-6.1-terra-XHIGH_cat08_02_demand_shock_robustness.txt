# 1. Stress scenarios

Use the normal period as a reference, not a capacity promise. Weekly orders rose from 2,300 to 3,300 in the peak, about 43%; the Urgent share rose from 18% to 29% and Complex-panel from 13% to 20%. Complex panels require multiple assays, so equal case counts do not imply equal work. The scenarios can overlap; if they do, use the more restrictive capacity and escalation rules.

1. **Winter mix shift:** arrivals and mix resemble or exceed the observed peak, especially Urgent and Complex-panel orders. Repeat work also rises with heavy use and night shifts.
2. **Assay degradation or outage:** M2 becomes unreliable or M1 is unavailable for a shift while demand remains elevated. Repeats consume scarce run slots and an apparent machine recovery can release a wave to validation.
3. **Validator or specialist absence:** the medical validator is absent for an evening, or the specialist is unavailable on the recurring second Friday, while Complex-panel and Urgent demand remains high.
4. **Courier compression or delay:** both routes arrive close together, or a delayed route delivers a large, aged load near the end of a shift. Some urgent orders have already consumed much of their 18-hour allowance before laboratory intake.
5. **Pre-analytical quality shock:** a sending site or night shift produces more incomplete metadata, unsuitable tubes or mislabeled samples, increasing correction and recollection during an otherwise busy period.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The peak data show a chain of constraints, not a single permanent bottleneck. Pre-analytical utilization rises to 96%, assay to 98%, technical validation to 101%, medical validation to 93%, and specialist review to 87%. Their peak median queues are respectively 9.5, 18, 16, 22 and 36 hours. These are pool-level summaries; the performance DFG edges include elapsed time between events and should not be added to the queue medians as if they were independent. Peak Urgent median/p90 is 17/33 hours against an 18-hour limit, and even normal p90 is 19 hours. Routine peak p90 is 120 hours against its 72-hour target. The tails and deadline risk already matter before any redesign.

- **Winter mix:** registration and intake initially accumulate arrivals, then pre-check and preparation constrain flow. Peak Receive sample → Pre-analytical check is 9 hours versus 2 normally; Pre-analytical check → Test preparation is 13 versus 4. Batch wait → Assay run grows from 9 to 22 hours, while Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%. Faster pre-check or preparation feeds the 98%-utilized machines; faster assays feed technical validation at 101%, followed by medical validation and specialist review. Complex cases are 3.1 times as likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times as likely to enter specialist hold. The peak DFG has 610 Technical validation → Hold events versus 290 in the longer normal period, and Hold → Medical validation takes a median 48 hours versus 18. Strict Urgent-first dispatch can reduce urgent delay temporarily while Routine and Complex work ages and total work in process grows.
- **Machine degradation:** preparation and batch queues grow first, followed by repeat loops. Peak Assay run → Repeat assay occurs 1,260 times versus 1,140 in the longer normal period, and Repeat assay → Assay run takes 26 versus 10 median hours. M2 and night-shift clustering make indiscriminate routing to M2 hazardous. If repairs or rerouting clear the machine queue, the output surge hits technical validation, then medical validation for eligible cases; sending Complex work onward without review capacity merely moves the hold.
- **Validator or specialist absence:** medical-validation or specialist holds become the immediate constraint, even if upstream stations have spare capacity. Peak Technical validation → Medical validation is 24 versus 8 median hours; Hold → Medical validation is 48 versus 18. Continuing to run every Complex panel creates completed but unreleasable work. Diverting selected panels or adding review cover can expose transport, partner capacity, technical validation or medical release as the next constraint.
- **Courier compression:** registration and pre-check face an arrival burst; older urgent cases have less remaining time, while pre-check queues create the next choke point. Peak courier intake utilization is 88% and its median queue is 7.5 hours. Smoothing pickup or automating complete orders can relieve intake but send a burst to the 96%-utilized pre-check and then preparation and assay. Delayed recollections consume clerk time and compete with fresh arrivals.
- **Quality shock:** correction and recollection rise before assay demand does. Peak pre-check → recollection events are 780 versus 760 in the longer normal period; correction → recollection is 110 versus 70. Peak correction utilization is 91% with a 14-hour median queue, and recollection → receipt is 60 versus 42 hours. Early rejection prevents wasted assay and validation work but generates calls, courier trips and new intake. Moving technical validators to correction can unblock cases only to overload technical validation, already at 101% in peak.

DFG frequencies are event transitions, not necessarily distinct orders: loops, multiple assays and flattening can create repeated events. Several transition totals do not reconcile, including fewer recorded releases than registrations less cancellations and different counts around loops. Do not infer exact completion, failure or routing rates from these edges without case-level reconciliation, observation-window and event-completeness checks. Validate the proposed policies on linked case/tube and assay records, resource schedules and timestamps before setting production capacities.

# 3. Robust redesign package

- **Control flow by deadline and downstream capacity.** Maintain a shared board from receipt through release with case type, deadline, age, remaining steps, assay demand and required validation. Estimate remaining time using recent case-type and route-specific queue and service distributions, including transport and review; flag negative or low predicted slack. Reserve some preparation, assay and validation capacity for urgent work, but protect a minimum share for aging Routine and Complex work and allow scheduled urgent exceptions to batches. Limit releases into each stage using available downstream slots and work-in-process caps; escalate cases that cannot meet their deadline instead of silently accumulating them. Calibrate reservations and caps from actual capacity and demand, with an override for clinical urgency. A cap at assay can move the queue to preparation, and a cap at pre-check can move it to intake; track age and deterioration risk on both sides. This depends on reliable timestamps, workload estimates and safe clinical prioritization; it cannot make an infeasible 18-hour order feasible.
- **Prevent defects early and unblock corrections.** Triage incoming samples for high-impact defects promptly, aiming for a decision within two hours when staffing permits. Return actionable defect information to the sender, arrange recollection immediately and fast-track correctable metadata errors. Automate registration only for complete, verified digital orders; retain an exception path and audit matching. This reduces avoidable assay repeats and clerk work, but faster registration can overwhelm pre-check and early rejection can overload correction, courier and intake. Use the downstream caps and monitor defect and recollection workload by sender; do not reject samples on an unvalidated rule. The target depends on intake staffing and sender response.
- **Use bounded batching and quality-aware machine scheduling.** Keep Routine batching where it saves setup time, with a maximum batch age and deadline-based dispatch; permit urgent exceptions and protect some Complex-panel slots. Use 24 only when measured setup savings, machine quality and the age cap support it; otherwise use smaller batches. Route by validated assay compatibility, available time and current machine quality, rather than permanently assigning all Routine work to M2. Monitor repeats by machine and shift and act on a sustained quality signal. Faster preparation may overload machines, and a more efficient assay schedule can overload technical validation; use slot reservations and downstream caps. This depends on stable setups, validated routing and adequate assay-level visibility.
- **Build targeted flexibility at the scarce handoffs.** Cross-train two technical validators in accession correction and establish a rostered medical-validation reserve, including an evening shift during sustained peaks. Schedule specialist cover ahead of the second Friday, or prearrange a partner route for eligible Complex panels. Move cross-trained staff only against a minimum technical-validation staffing floor; the four-week training time means this must be prepared before winter. Correction relief can overload technical validation; an evening medical shift can expose specialist review or result-release capacity, and specialist cover can expose medical validation. Check the whole chain before activating cover. These measures depend on competency, clinical authorization, funding and real availability of cover; they are not a substitute for assay capacity.
- **Smooth intake with sending sites.** Stagger courier pickups and receiving appointments where feasible, reserve intake capacity for delayed or urgent shipments, and transmit manifests and defects ahead of arrival. This reduces arrival spikes but may increase time before pickup or deliver a steadier load into a saturated pre-check. Measure order-to-receipt time and pre-check backlog together, and preserve urgent transport arrangements.

Do not hold Routine releases merely to produce a batch report: it adds delay against a 72-hour target already missed in the peak tail. Streamline reporting only if release work is measured to be constraining and the change does not hold ready results; faster release can still expose downstream reporting or recipient-handling limits. Avoid a permanent strict Urgent-first rule and a permanent M1/M2 split. All changes require assay quality and clinical validation, chain-of-custody controls and review of case-level outcomes before wider rollout.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Thresholds below are initial operational triggers, not validated capacity limits. Calculate workload from actual staffed capacity and case-level remaining work; review them daily and adjust after observing performance. Shift-level changes require an accountable operations lead and clinical escalation for unsafe or infeasible cases.

| Scenario | Switch on | Policy and next constraint | Switch off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urgent/Complex mix surge | Forecast for the next shift exceeds staffed capacity at any required station, or urgent cases with predicted slack under 4 hours are increasing for two checks. | Increase the reserved urgent share at preparation, assay and required validation; preserve a minimum allocation for aging Routine and Complex work and escalate predicted misses. Limit assay starts to validation capacity. The resulting preparation queue may grow; alert on Routine age and reallocate if it threatens 72 hours. | After two consecutive shift reviews show forecast workload within staffed capacity, urgent slack stable and protected queues draining, return gradually to baseline reservations. |
| M2 degradation or machine outage | A machine is unavailable, fails quality control, or its shift-specific repeat rate exceeds its validated baseline and quality limit. | Quarantine affected runs as required, investigate the machine and night-shift cluster, and route only validated assays to remaining capacity. Pause elective batching increases, prioritize by clinical risk and age, and use an approved overflow route if available. Remaining machine capacity and then technical validation can saturate; cap starts and escalate unavoidable misses. | Restore normal routing only after repair, documented quality-control clearance and a stable repeat rate over the lab's approved observation period; then release backlog in controlled increments. |
| Medical or specialist absence | Roster shows uncovered required review, or predicted review workload exceeds staffed capacity for the next shift. | Activate the trained evening medical reserve or specialist cover. If inadequate, divert eligible Complex panels to the contracted partner only when predicted in-house completion exceeds partner completion including 12-hour transport, partner queue and return/release time. Confirm clinical eligibility, capacity, identifiers, custody and result reconciliation. Partner transport, its review capacity, or local medical release can become the next constraint. | Stop new diversion or reserve shifts when cover is restored and forecast review capacity exceeds incoming work with backlog draining for two shifts; continue tracking already diverted cases to final release. |
| Compressed or delayed courier arrivals | Route forecast shows two normal peaks converging, a missed pickup, or a delivery arriving too late for staffed intake; flag urgent orders with low remaining slack. | Stagger arrivals where possible, pre-register verified manifests and flex intake staffing within safe shift limits. Reserve pre-check slots for aged urgent shipments and pace the remaining intake into available pre-check capacity. Check for a growing upstream queue and sample stability; preparation and assay can become the next constraints. | Resume normal slots after arrivals and intake/pre-check queues return to the locally agreed operating band and forecast capacity covers the next shift. |
| Defect or correction surge | Defect/recollection rate or correction backlog exceeds its recent sender-specific baseline for two checks, or correction work threatens a case deadline. | Triage by clinical urgency and age, notify the sender promptly, and temporarily assign a trained validator to correction only if the technical-validation floor remains covered. Track recollection and courier demand; unblocked cases may overload preparation, assay and technical validation. | Return the validator when correction backlog is draining and technical-validation coverage or its queue reaches the warning band; continue sender follow-up until defects normalize. |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

A simultaneous machine outage and review absence can make demand exceed the capacity of the whole pathway despite reservations; some targets will then be missed. Escalate forecast misses to the clinical and contracting leads and communicate expected timing to affected ordering sites. Long recollection loops remain dependent on physicians and couriers. The single specialist, the two correction specialists, the four-week training lead time, machine-specific failure and partner capacity remain single points of fragility. Complex panels and repeat cases require assay-level workload measurement that the flattened order log does not provide.

The redesign itself can create fragility: reservations and WIP caps can hide an aging queue upstream, delay Routine cases, or allow samples to deteriorate; frequent urgent exceptions can fragment schedules and raise setup errors. Diversion adds 12 hours of transport and chain-of-custody and result-reconciliation risks. Digital registration and rapid rejection can propagate matching mistakes or reject usable material. Audit these effects by case type and sender, preserve clinical overrides, and revise or stop a policy if safety or tail performance worsens.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Review at least daily, and at shift changes during a declared surge. The warning bands below are provisional; compare against staffed, validated capacity, deadline risk and recent case-type baselines, not just overall averages. Make the owner and escalation action visible for each alert.

**Leading indicators**

- **Forecast demand versus staffed capacity for the next shift**, in assay runs and required technical, medical and specialist reviews as well as orders, split by case type. Amber when forecast approaches capacity; red when it exceeds capacity or required cover is absent. Include repeat work and confirmed partner slots.
- **Deadline slack and aging WIP by type and stage**, including work held upstream by a cap and in transport. Amber when urgent predicted slack falls below 4 hours or Routine cases approach 72 hours with substantial work remaining; red for predicted misses or rising aged backlogs across two checks. Escalate individual clinical cases without waiting for the daily review.
- **Queue workload and oldest age** at pre-check, correction, preparation/assay, technical validation, medical validation and specialist review. Amber when queue workload is growing for two checks; red when it exceeds what the next staffed shift can clear or threatens deadlines. Track both sides of each cap.
- **Machine availability and repeat signal by machine and shift.** Amber for a rising repeat rate versus the validated baseline; red for failed quality control, outage or a repeat rate beyond the approved quality limit. Investigate before routing additional work.
- **Incoming defects, recollections and courier forecast.** Amber for a sustained sender-specific defect increase or compressed arrivals; red for a missed route, correction backlog threatening deadlines, or compromised sample stability. Track partner transport and custody exceptions when diversion is active.

**Lagging outcomes:** report daily and trend by case type, sender and scenario: percentage of Urgent releases over 18 hours, Routine over 72 hours, median and p90 cycle time, Complex-panel and repeat-case tails, rework/repeat and recollection rates, cancellations, and partner turnaround and traceability incidents. Use the contractual clock consistently, include open overdue cases alongside completed-case results, and distinguish order counts from assay and tube counts. These outcomes test whether queue improvements actually produce timely, safe releases without hiding unfinished cases.
