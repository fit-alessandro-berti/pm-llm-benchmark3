## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1. Winter infection wave: mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel

**Likely bottleneck chain:**  
Courier/registration pressure  pre-analytical check and accession correction  preparation/batch wait  assay machines  technical validation  medical validation  specialist review  release.

The peak DFG shows why the bottleneck migrates:

- Pre-analytical defect rate increases: recollection rate rises from about 4.2% to 8.0%.
- Manual accession correction rate rises from about 2.8% to 4.3%, and the accession queue grows from 3h to 14h.
- Batch wait becomes much larger: 760 normal entries become 1,180 peak entries; `Batch wait -> Assay run` grows from 9h to 22h.
- Repeat assay increases: about 6.7% of assay outputs normally, about 14.8% at peak.
- Specialist hold increases sharply: 290 normal holds become 610 peak holds; `Hold -> Medical validation` grows from 18h to 48h.

**Migration effects from local fixes:**

- **I6 early defect triage** reduces bad samples entering later stages, but if good samples are released faster without a pull mechanism, preparation and assay queues grow.
- **I1 strict Urgent-first dispatch** helps Urgent p90 but can starve Routine and Complex-panel cases. Because Complex-panel cases need multiple assays and medical validation, delaying them increases total work-in-process and later specialist-review pressure.
- **I2 larger Routine batches** improves preparation efficiency but worsens Urgent latency if batch slots crowd out mini-batches or single urgent samples. Peak urgent bypass of batch wait falls from 74% to 58%, showing that batching pressure already harms Urgent during peaks.
- **I3 dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex and Routine to M2** protects critical cases but can increase repeats because Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after M2 usage and night shifts. More repeats feed back into assay capacity.
- **I5 evening medical validation** helps release Urgent cases but may simply move the queue into specialist review for Complex-panel cases if specialist capacity is unchanged.
- **I10 Complex-panel outsourcing** relieves specialist review but creates transport delay, traceability risk, and integration work at registration/result release.

### S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage

**Likely bottleneck chain:**  
Assay machine capacity  preparation/batch wait  pre-analytical check if upstream is not throttled  technical validation and medical validation in bursts when backlog clears.

The assay station is already near saturation at peak: 98% utilization and 18h median queue. If M2 degrades or fails:

- Preparation and batch wait queues grow first. `Batch wait -> Assay run` is already 22h at peak, so even a small capacity loss can create long Routine delays.
- If preparation continues pushing samples forward, WIP piles up before assay. If preparation is throttled, the bottleneck migrates backward to pre-analytical check and registration.
- Technical validators and medical validators may appear temporarily underloaded, then become overloaded when delayed assay output is released in bursts.
- Repeat-after-failure cases are dangerous here because they consume assay capacity twice. The peak `Repeat assay -> Assay run` time of 26h shows how quickly a repeat loop can dominate machine time.

**Migration effects from local fixes:**

- **Strict Urgent-first dispatch** protects contractual Urgent SLAs but can cause Routine and some Complex-panel cases to age severely. This creates hidden clinical risk and may increase cancellations.
- **Routing more Routine to M1** may preserve Routine flow but displaces Urgent and Complex-panel work and can overload M1’s schedule.
- **Adding assay overtime or outsourcing** can relieve assay, but if technical validation or medical validation capacity is not aligned, the next queue appears after assay.
- **Stopping Routine intake** protects the assay bottleneck but increases registration/receive backlog and may increase cancellations if communication is poor.

### S3. Medical validator or specialist-review absence

**Likely bottleneck chain:**  
Medical validation  specialist review  technical validation WIP  assay scheduling if no pull is applied.

The evidence shows that medical validation and specialist review are not just “last-mile” steps; they are major peak-period constraints:

- Medical validation queue rises from 2h to 22h.
- Specialist review queue rises from 5h to 36h.
- `Technical validation -> Medical validation` worsens from 8h to 24h.
- `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` worsens from 18h to 48h.
- Complex-panel cases are much more likely to need medical validation and specialist review.

**Migration effects from local fixes:**

- **I5 evening medical-validation shift** helps cases that only need medical validation, especially Urgent cases. But if many Complex-panel cases require specialist review, the evening shift may simply move cases into a longer specialist-hold queue.
- **Auto-releasing low-risk Routine cases** frees medical validators for Urgent and Complex-panel cases, but creates quality and governance risk if rules are too broad.
- **I4 cross-training technical validators for accession correction** can help accession, but if deployed during an assay or technical-validation surge, it can worsen technical validation capacity, which is already 101% utilized at peak.
- **I10 outsourcing Complex-panel overflow** reduces internal specialist-review load, but the 12h transport delay makes it unsuitable for many Urgent Complex-panel cases. It also creates a new bottleneck in transport, sample traceability, and result integration.

### S4. Courier arrival compression or delay

**Likely bottleneck chain:**  
Courier intake  registration/receive  pre-analytical check  accession correction  preparation/assay later in the day.

Courier intake is deceptively important because arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00 create batch arrivals. The peak metrics show:

- Courier intake queue rises from 1h to 7.5h.
- `Register order -> Receive sample` rises from 5h to 8h.
- `Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check` rises from 2h to 9h.
- Pre-analytical check utilization reaches 96%.
- Recollection loops become slower: `Request recollection -> Receive sample` rises from 42h to 60h.

**Migration effects from local fixes:**

- **I7 shifting courier pickup slots** smooths intake, but if couriers cannot change routes, the lab must still handle delayed bursts. Smoothing alone cannot be the only control.
- **I8 automated registration** can speed order intake, but if it releases more orders into receive/pre-analytical without capacity controls, it moves the queue from registration to pre-analytical check and assay.
- **I6 early defect triage** prevents poor samples from entering later stages, but it can increase immediate workload for clerks and couriers because rejected samples require communication, recollection requests, and possibly reverse logistics.
- **Prioritizing Urgent at receive** helps Urgent SLAs but can delay Routine samples enough that later batching becomes inefficient or Routine 72h targets are threatened.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign is built around five reinforcing bundles. It does not add capacity everywhere; it changes routing, quality gates, batching, validation policy, and pull control so that the process remains acceptable across scenarios.

### R1. SLA-based lane control with dynamic priority, not strict Urgent-first

**Interventions used:** modified I1; partial use of I3; explicit lane rules.

Create four operational lanes:

1. **Urgent/STAT lane**  
   - Single-piece or mini-batch flow.  
   - Priority at receive, pre-analytical check, preparation, assay, technical validation, and medical validation.  
   - Urgent bypass of batch wait is preserved unless a protected assay slot is available within a short threshold, e.g. 1–2h.

2. **Complex-panel lane**  
   - Early flag at registration.  
   - Reserved assay capacity and reserved medical-validation/specialist-review windows.  
   - Complex-panel cases should not be displaced indefinitely by Urgent cases because they already have long natural cycle times and high specialist-review probability.

3. **Routine batch lane**  
   - Batched preparation and assay.  
   - Managed by due-date and batch efficiency, not immediate priority.  
   - Routine cases are deliberately delayed only when Urgent/Complex pressure exceeds capacity, but anti-starvation rules prevent silent aging.

4. **Repeat-after-failure lane**  
   - Reserved assay slots or a repeat buffer so repeats do not consume fresh Urgent/Complex slots.  
   - Root-cause tagging by machine, shift, and case type, especially for M2 and night shifts.

**Priority rule:**  
Use a weighted earliest-due-date rule with clinical priority, not absolute Urgent-first. For example:

- Urgent cases approaching 12h since registration get immediate assay priority.
- Complex-panel cases approaching specialist-review cutoff get protected medical-validation priority.
- Routine cases older than 60h trigger an escalation or mini-batch release to protect the 72h target.

**Why robust:**  
This protects contractual Urgent SLAs during mix shifts while preventing Routine and Complex-panel starvation. It is useful in S1, S2, and S4, and partially in S3 when medical validation is scarce.

**Assumptions:**  
Accurate case-type classification, real-time lane WIP visibility, and scheduler discipline.

**Where it may fail / downstream risk:**  
If Urgent volume exceeds protected capacity, Routine p90 will worsen. If WIP caps are absent, Urgent priority can overload assay and medical validation. If Complex-panel is repeatedly displaced, specialist-review backlog and total WIP increase.

### R2. Front-end quality gate and arrival leveling

**Interventions used:** I6, I7, I8, plus accession-error prevention.

This bundle attacks the largest non-value-added loops:

- **I8 automated registration** for orders with complete digital metadata.
- Manual registration reserved for incomplete or high-risk orders, with separate SLA monitoring.
- **I6 early pre-analytical defect triage** to reject poor samples within 2h.
- Standardized rejection criteria to reduce subjective delay.
- Dedicated communication support for physicians so clerks are not trapped explaining errors while new samples arrive.
- **I7 courier slot smoothing** to reduce 10:00 and 17:00 spikes.
- Urgent samples exempt from smoothing; they receive a STAT intake path.

**Why robust:**  
It reduces rework before it consumes scarce assay and validation capacity. It helps in all scenarios, especially S1 and S4. The normal DFG shows 760 recollection requests; the peak DFG shows 780 despite far fewer cases. That is a strong signal that peak conditions amplify front-end defects.

**Assumptions:**  
Physicians accept standardized rejection criteria, couriers can partially adjust pickup slots, and digital metadata is reliable enough for automated registration.

**Where it may fail / downstream risk:**  
If early rejection increases courier reverse logistics or physician calls without extra communication support, registration becomes overloaded. If automation releases too many clean orders into pre-analytical check without pull control, the bottleneck moves to preparation and assay. If courier smoothing is impossible during weather events, the lab must still handle bursts.

### R3. Adaptive batching and risk-based machine allocation

**Interventions used:** modified I2, modified I3.

Do not simply increase batch size from 12 to 24. Use dynamic batch sizing:

- Normal stable period: Routine batch size around 12.
- Peak with low Urgent share: batch size may rise to 16–18 if Urgent queue is low and batch wait is acceptable.
- Peak with high Urgent/Complex share: reduce Routine batch size to 8–12 and use mini-batches or single-piece flow for Urgent.
- Batch cutoff every 2–4h for Urgent-eligible samples.
- Reserve 10–15% assay capacity for repeats and unexpected Urgent work.

Machine allocation should be risk-based, not absolute:

- **M1**: protected capacity for Urgent, Complex-panel, and repeat-sensitive cases.
- **M2**: Routine cases when M2 quality indicators are stable.
- Avoid sending high-risk or Complex-panel work to M2 during night shifts if M2 failure/repeat risk is high.
- Preventive maintenance for M2 before predicted peak periods.
- If M2 degrades, do not automatically flood M1 with Routine work; instead throttle Routine, extend batch windows, or outsource non-urgent work.

**Why robust:**  
It balances setup efficiency against Urgent latency and repeat risk. It is especially useful in S1 and S2.

**Assumptions:**  
Machine schedules can be adjusted, maintenance is actionable, and M1 capacity is sufficient for Urgent/Complex demand most of the time.

**Where it may fail / downstream risk:**  
If batch sizes are too large, Urgent and Complex-panel cases wait longer. If batch sizes are too small, setup overhead rises and assay capacity falls. If Routine is routed to M2 excessively, repeats may increase, consuming assay capacity and feeding technical validation with rework. If M1 is overprotected and M2 fails, Routine backlog can become severe.

### R4. Flexible validation, specialist coverage, and controlled overflow

**Interventions used:** I5, targeted I4, protocolized release, specialist backup, I10.

Medical validation and specialist review are peak-period constraints. The redesign should separate low-risk release work from scarce expert attention.

Actions:

- **I5 temporary evening medical-validation shift** during peaks, focused on Urgent and Complex-panel cases that are blocked at validation.
- **Protocolized auto-release** for low-risk Routine cases after technical validation, using predefined rules and audit sampling. This frees medical validators for Urgent and Complex-panel cases.
- **Targeted I4 cross-training**: cross-train two technical validators for accession correction, but deploy them only when technical-validation utilization is below a safe threshold or accession-correction queue exceeds a trigger. Do not permanently remove them from technical validation.
- Specialist-review coverage: remote backup specialist, delegated low-risk review, and explicit Friday-absence coverage.
- **I10 Complex-panel overflow** to partner lab when internal specialist-review queue exceeds threshold, but only for cases where 12h transport delay does not violate clinical urgency.

**Why robust:**  
It addresses the S3 constraint directly and helps S1 when Complex-panel volume rises. It also prevents medical validation from becoming the single point of failure after assay improvements.

**Assumptions:**  
Medical validators can be scheduled flexibly, auto-release rules are clinically safe, cross-trained validators do not weaken technical validation during assay surges, and the partner lab has reliable turnaround and traceability.

**Where it may fail / downstream risk:**  
Evening medical validation may create a next-morning release/courier spike. Auto-release may increase quality risk if rules are too broad. Cross-trained technical validators may reduce technical-validation capacity at the wrong time. Outsourcing may overload transport and result-integration steps, and it may not help Urgent Complex-panel cases because of the 12h delay.

### R5. Pull-based WIP caps, buffers, and rework-loop control

This is the control layer that prevents the other interventions from simply moving queues downstream.

**Pull/WIP rules:**

- Limit routine case release into pre-analytical check if pre-analytical queue exceeds threshold.
- Limit preparation release into assay if assay queue exceeds threshold.
- Limit assay output into validation if technical-validation or medical-validation queue exceeds threshold.
- Maintain small protected buffers before the active bottleneck:
  - Urgent buffer: always protected.
  - Complex-panel buffer: protected but monitored for specialist availability.
  - Routine buffer: adjustable and deliberately sacrificed only under explicit scenario policy.

**Rework-loop control:**

- Monitor recollection, manual accession correction, repeat assay, and specialist hold as first-class process-mining metrics.
- Trigger root-cause response when recollection rate, repeat rate, or accession-correction queue rises.
- Treat repeat-after-failure cases as capacity events, not just quality events, because they consume assay capacity twice.

**Why robust:**  
It prevents local optimization from becoming global overload. It is necessary whenever I6, I8, I3, or I5 accelerates one step.

**Where it may fail / downstream risk:**  
If WIP caps are too tight, the bottleneck may starve and throughput falls. If caps are too loose, WIP explodes and cycle times worsen. If Routine is held too long, registration backlog, cancellations, and 72h SLA breaches can increase. This is a redesign-induced vulnerability that must be monitored.

**Note on I9:**  
I9—holding Routine releases until a batch report is ready—is not a default redesign element. Release is not the main constraint in the logs; medical validation, specialist review, assay capacity, and pre-analytical defects are more binding. I9 should only be used as a guarded scenario policy when release or courier dispatch staffing is the active bottleneck and Routine cases are still safely within the 72h target.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

Even after the redesign, several fragilities remain.

1. **Assay capacity remains structurally tight.**  
   Peak assay utilization is 98%. The redesign can allocate that capacity better, but it cannot fully absorb a simultaneous Urgent surge, Complex-panel surge, and M2 outage. A compound scenario can still breach Routine and Complex-panel targets.

2. **Redesign-induced Routine starvation risk.**  
   Dynamic priority and WIP caps protect Urgent and Complex-panel cases, but they can deliberately delay Routine cases. If Urgent/Complex demand stays elevated for many days, Routine p90 can exceed the 72h target and cancellations may rise.

3. **Redesign-induced registration/courier burden from early rejection.**  
   Early pre-analytical defect triage reduces downstream waste but can increase immediate physician communication, recollection requests, and courier reverse logistics. If this communication is not staffed, registration becomes the new bottleneck.

4. **M2 reliability risk remains.**  
   Routing more Routine work to M2 can preserve M1 for Urgent/Complex cases, but M2 has a higher failure rate and is associated with repeat-after-failure clustering. If M2 degrades, Routine backlog and repeat assay demand can rise together.

5. **Evening medical validation may create a next-morning spike.**  
   Additional evening releases can shift work into next-day courier dispatch, result communication, or follow-up validation. If specialist coverage is still absent, Complex-panel cases may still stall despite extra medical-validation hours.

6. **Outsourcing Complex-panel work introduces external fragility.**  
   The partner lab may relieve specialist review, but the 12h transport delay, sample traceability requirements, and result-integration steps create new failure modes. Urgent Complex-panel cases may not be eligible for this overflow.

7. **Cross-trained technical validators can weaken technical validation.**  
   Using technical validators for accession correction helps a high-leverage queue, but if deployed during an assay or validation surge, it can worsen technical-validation capacity. This is especially dangerous because technical validation is already above 100% utilization in peak.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

During peak periods, monitor a small set of leading indicators daily. Lagging outcomes should be reviewed, but they should not be the only control mechanism.

### Leading indicators

| Indicator | Type | Warning bands | Why it is leading / action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected next-24h case mix: Urgent + Complex-panel share | Leading | Green < 35%; Amber 35–45%; Red > 45% | Predicts assay, medical-validation, and specialist pressure. Triggers surge lane protection. |
| Courier arrival adherence / slot load | Leading | Green < 85% of slot capacity; Amber 85–110%; Red > 110% or delayed burst | Predicts intake, registration, and pre-analytical queue spikes. Triggers STAT receive lane and smoothing. |
| Pre-analytical defect rate and accession-correction queue | Leading | Recollection: Green < 4%, Amber 4–6%, Red > 6%. Accession queue: Green < 4h, Amber 4–8h, Red > 8h | Predicts recollection loops and clerk overload. Triggers front-end containment and accession support. |
| Preparation queue and batch-wait age | Leading | Batch wait: Green < 6h, Amber 6–12h, Red > 12h | Predicts Routine p90 deterioration and Urgent batching conflicts. Triggers batch-size adjustment. |
| Assay machine status and repeat-assay rate | Leading | Green: M1/M2 stable, repeat < 7%; Amber: M2 degraded or repeat 7–10%; Red: M2 down or repeat > 10% | Predicts assay capacity loss and rework consumption. Triggers assay conservation policy. |
| Technical-validation and medical-validation queue | Leading | Medical queue: Green < 4h, Amber 4–8h, Red > 8h. Technical queue: Green < 6h, Amber 6–10h, Red > 10h | Predicts release delay after assay. Triggers validation flexibility and pull throttling. |
| Specialist-review hold age | Leading | Green < 12h; Amber 12–24h; Red > 24h | Predicts Complex-panel blockage. Triggers specialist backup or overflow. |
| Lane WIP versus caps | Leading | Green < 100% of cap; Amber 100–120%; Red > 120% | Prevents bottleneck migration. Triggers throttling, escalation, or buffer reallocation. |

### Lagging outcome metrics

These should be reviewed daily but interpreted as outcomes of the leading controls:

- Median and p90 cycle time by case type: Urgent, Routine, Complex-panel, Repeat-after-failure.
- Percentage of Urgent cases released within 18h.
- Percentage of Routine cases released within 72h.
- Overall rework rate and repeat-assay rate.
- Percentage of cases with at least one queue over 12h.
- Cancellation rate, especially cancellations after recollection requests.
- Complex-panel overflow turnaround and traceability exceptions.
