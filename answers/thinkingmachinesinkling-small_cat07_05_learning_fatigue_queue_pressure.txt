# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed trajectory—service time declining across sequence position while error rates stay low early, then spike late—is **not a single process** but an emergent interaction of scheduling rules, case assignment, adaptive speed, and hidden workarounds.

**Early shift (1–5 to 6–15):** The first events carry over backlog from prior shifts (mean queue 46, high-complexity share 48% in Early shifts). Managers and triage rules assign these complex, high-priority cases to senior resources. Service time is high (18.8 min) primarily because the tasks are difficult, not because workers are slow. Error rates are moderate (3.4%) because the work is careful and under moderate WIP (39–46). During this window, workers also warm up: under low-WIP conditions, later events show faster, stable-quality performance (Seniors 15.911.8 min, 2.1%2.2% error). Thus, part of the initial drop is genuine learning, but part is **case-mix improvement**—the remaining pool becomes simpler and lower priority.

**Mid-shift (16–30 to 31–50):** Complexity continues to fall (0.540.50), queue declines (3331), and workers are operating in a “stabilization” zone. Service time falls to 12.9 min with errors near baseline (2.7%). At this stage, **learning and selection are both active**: workers are faster, and they are also handling easier cases.

**Late shift (51–75 to 76+):** Two dynamics collide. First, **queue pressure rises sharply** (6288). In Day and Late shifts, demand peaks late (Day shift queue 3875). Second, workers adapt by rushing, batching, and deferring verification. Service time falls to 11.4 and then 10.8 min—not because they have “mastered” the job, but because they are trading speed for quality under high WIP. Errors jump to 4.6% and 7.9%; rework within 7 days climbs to 8.2% and 13.7%. This is especially severe for juniors (12.4% error under high WIP vs 4.4% under low WIP), who face both early complexity and later pressure.

The **batching mechanism** amplifies this: Junior resources batch Document Review into long sequences of very short events late in the Day shift, creating artificial throughput. Quality Check is deferred to the final 45 minutes, causing bursts of corrections that inflate both late-shift error flags and rework measures. Meanwhile, Bot_Risk handles Risk Classification instantly, but when queues exceed 70, human reviewers correct bot outputs more frequently, adding hidden workload that can lead to downstream errors.

**Shift context matters:** Night shifts have fewer cases but a higher share of urgent exceptions (56% high-complexity first hour) and different circadian conditions; speed does not improve as much (17.514.9 min) and high-WIP errors remain elevated (8.2%). This is not “fatigue” alone—it is a different operating regime with different assignment and urgency patterns.

**Why speed falls while errors rise only conditionally:** Under **low WIP**, faster work reflects warmth, simpler tasks, and efficient habits without quality loss. Under **high WIP**, faster work reflects pressure-induced rushing, batching, and deferred quality checks. The error rise is **conditional on WIP > 70 and on resource experience** (juniors and mids degrade more than seniors). Fatigue may contribute, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient: the signature of fatigue (slower work, rising errors at constant task difficulty) is absent. Instead, we see **pressure-driven acceleration with quality degradation**.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Selection bias makes raw comparisons misleading.** Early sequence events are not “the same work later.” They are more complex (0.72 vs 0.43), higher priority (41% vs 19%), and inherited from previous shifts (mean queue 61 at Early start). Comparing error rates across sequence bins without controlling for `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, and `wip_at_start` attributes system-level assignment rules to individuals.

**Measurement error is systematic.** `error_flag` captures observed errors at the event level, but some errors are undetected until `rework_flag` triggers days later. `service_time` based on `start_timestamp` does not include waiting or queue time, so “faster” may mean “skipped inspection,” not “more efficient.” Batching (e.g., 9–18 sequential Document Reviews) makes inter-event times artificially short, masking true cognitive load.

**Queue pressure is often a management variable, not a worker choice.** High WIP (>70) arises from staffing levels, shift handoverequality, batching policies, and demand peaks—not solely from individual speed. Penalizing workers for high-WIP errors ignores that the queue was created by scheduling and resource allocation.

**Automation interaction creates hidden workload.** Bot_Risk performs Risk Classification instantly, but when queues exceed 70, humans correct bot outputs more frequently. A worker’s “error rate” or service time may reflect **correction of automation failures**, not personal inattention. Attributing these to the human reviewer is an operational error.

**Punitive use of late-shift rates is unethical and counterproductive.** Because case-mix shifts, WIP rises, and quality checks are deferred, late-shift error rates are a **system indicator**, not a individual skill score. Penalties based on raw rates encourage hiding errors, rushing more, or avoiding high-complexity cases—worsening the very dynamics (batching, deferred checks, selection) that drive up errors.

**Quality-check deferral risks harm.** Removing or delaying quality checks to improve throughput (intervention 6) can reduce visible delays but increases downstream rework and claimant harm. Any throughput improvement must be measured against **downstream `rework_flag` and `error_flag` rates**, not just event speed.

**Shift caps and breaks must be protected.** If shift length is capped or breaks are mandated, they must not reduce pay or push workload onto fewer people, which could increase queue pressure for remaining staff.
