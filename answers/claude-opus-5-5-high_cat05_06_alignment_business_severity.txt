# Insurance-Claim Payments: Business Interpretation and Prioritisation of Alignment Deviations

Short answer: the three highest raw costs in the sample (CL-607 = 4, CL-612 = 4, CL-601 = 3) are business-benign. The two most serious control failures cost only 1 and 3 (CL-602, CL-604). Two real policy breaches cost 0 (CL-609, CL-610). If deviations are ranked by raw cost, the order is close to the reverse of the business risk.

---

## 1. Materiality Framework

### 1.1 Principle
An alignment tells us how many edit operations are needed to reconcile a trace with the control-flow model under a chosen cost function. Business severity asks a different question: "which control objective was compromised, how much could be lost or harmed, can it be undone, and how sure are we?" I therefore judge each case in three steps:

1. **Semantic translation.** Work out what each move means: a real activity out of place, a missing record, a renamed activity, telemetry, a scope artefact, or a silent routing step.
2. **Policy overlay.** Evaluate P1–P6 against the case data (amount, risk, bank change, timestamps, external evidence). The process tree has no guards, so it cannot do this.
3. **Materiality judgement.** Combine the dimensions below. This is a judgement, not a weighted sum.

### 1.2 Dimensions

| Dimension | Guiding question | Effect on severity |
|---|---|---|
| Control objective | Which control failed: payment authorisation (P2/P3), fraud prevention (P2, bank change), approval authority (P1), customer communication (P4), or record-keeping only? | Preventive payment controls rank above detective or record-keeping ones |
| Amount / exposure | How much money could be lost or misdirected? | Scales severity within a control class. It is not a trump card: a small payment with an active fraud signal can outrank a large, clean one |
| Reversibility | Can the effect be undone? A bank-accepted transfer to a changed account is close to irreversible. A late log entry can be fixed | Irreversible effects raise severity |
| Timing | Did the control happen too late to have any effect (payment before approval)? Was the P4 24-hour window met? | A control executed after the event it should gate counts as not executed |
| Customer / regulatory harm | Wrong payment, missed notification, wrongful denial, audit finding, regulatory reporting | Raises severity even when no money is lost |
| Recurrence | Is this a one-off or systemic (a rules-engine misconfiguration, a connector defect)? | Systemic issues raise the remediation priority of the pattern, even when each case is small |
| Evidence reliability | Is the deviation backed by independent, immutable evidence (bank timestamps) or by weak or self-serving evidence (a verbal claim, a mutable text link)? | Drives **confidence** mainly. It raises severity only when the control itself requires retained evidence |
| Model scope | Does the case belong to the population the model describes (P6)? | Out-of-scope cases get severity from their own norms (denial quality, withdrawal handling), not from the payment model |

### 1.3 Severity scale (business impact if the interpretation is correct)

| Level | Meaning | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| 5 – Critical | A preventive payment control was bypassed or ran after payment, with material or irreversible exposure and/or active fraud indicators | Payment before approval; missing fraud screen on a high-value, high-risk claim with a bank change |
| 4 – High | A mandatory control was bypassed with a fraud signal or clear authority breach, but exposure is moderate, or a systemic control defect exists | Auto-approval despite a bank change one hour earlier |
| 3 – Moderate | Approval-authority or policy breach without fraud indicators; recovery is plausible | Auto-approval of an 18k medium-risk claim |
| 2 – Low | The control was probably performed, but the audit trail is incomplete or weakly linked; mainly an evidence or compliance-documentation risk | External adjuster report not in the core log; notification evidenced only through a mutable CRM link |
| 1 – Negligible | No business deviation: technical noise, label or scope artefacts, silent moves | Telemetry, retry duplicates, tau moves, denied or withdrawn claims |

### 1.4 Confidence is a separate axis
- **Severity** answers: "if what we believe happened did happen, how bad is it?"
- **Confidence** answers: "how sure are we that it happened as described?" It depends on corroborating evidence, clock reliability, identifier stability, alignment ambiguity, and whether an activity could have been done outside the logged systems.
- Scale: **High** means independent, consistent evidence, and the interpretation would survive an audit. **Medium** means plausible, but one key fact (a link, a semantic equivalence, a document ID) is unverified. **Low** means the evidence is contradictory or mostly missing.
- The two axes interact when deciding what to do. High severity with lower confidence means "investigate first, because checking is cheap and being wrong is costly". Low severity with high confidence means "fix the pipeline in bulk".
- Evidence is treated in two ways. For **audit and compliance**, an unevidenced mandatory control is an ineffective control (CL-602 fails P2 whatever the verbal claim). For the **causal and operational narrative**, a missing event is never proof that the activity did not happen (see §4).

## 4. Causal Hypotheses and Evidence Tests

A missing event means "not evidenced in this log". It does not mean "did not happen". The tests below are designed to tell the hypotheses apart.

**4.1 CL-602: missing Fraud Screen on EUR 480k with a recent bank change**
- H1: No fraud check of any kind was done (bypass under time pressure or by habit).
- H2: An informal check was done (a call or chat with the fraud team) but not recorded. The approver's claim.
- H3: A screen was done in a separate fraud tool that is not extracted into the log (a correlation gap).
- H4: Deliberate fraud or collusion: the bank change was social-engineered or arranged internally, and the approval was steered.
- Tests:
  - Query the fraud tool by claim, policy, and beneficiary IBAN for screens around the approval time (confirms H3).
  - Look for a fraud-team ticket, phone or Teams logs, and emails between the approver and the fraud team (H2). Weigh self-serving testimony low unless independently corroborated.
  - Use UI telemetry (P5's exception) to see whether the approver opened the fraud or risk panel before approving.
  - Check how the bank change came in (channel, authentication, callback verification), whether the new account has links to other claims or employees, and the approver's history of approvals on accounts changed recently (H4).
  - Even if H2 or H3 is confirmed, P2 still counts as failed for audit purposes until evidence is retained; the severity of the fraud exposure would then fall.

**4.2 CL-604: payment 17 minutes before approval**
- H1: The payment system does not check approval status (systemic gating gap).
- H2: A manual override or emergency payment by a privileged user.
- H3: Approval was decided off-system before 10:03 and entered at 10:20 (a documentation delay).
- H4: A timestamp artefact: clock skew, or "bank accepted" versus "payment instructed" versus "value date" meaning different things.
- H5: A scheduled batch picked up the claim on status "assessed".
- Tests:
  - Payment-system audit trail: who or what released the payment, and whether an override flag or batch ID is set.
  - NTP and clock logs for both systems. A 17-minute drift is unlikely, and a timezone error would show as whole hours.
  - The approval screen's session telemetry (was the approver working on the case before 10:03?).
  - The other payments in the same file or batch.
  - For H3, look for contemporaneous written approval. Even if found, P3 as written is still breached, because a system-recorded approval is the control.

**4.3 Pattern D / CL-606: M(Assess Loss) with an external-adjuster document**
- H1: The assessment was done externally and not ingested (pure logging gap).
- H2: An internal Assess Loss event was also due (for example, an internal review of the adjuster report) and was skipped.
- H3: The document belongs to another case, or was produced or backdated afterwards (a risk for the 2 unlinked cases).
- Tests:
  - The e-signature provider's certificate timestamp against the Fraud Screen and approval times (the 10:05 order).
  - Claim, policy, and loss-date identifiers inside the document; the document-management ingest log; the adjuster licence registry.
  - Does the assessed amount reconcile with the paid amount?
  - Does the procedure require an internal acceptance step (H2)?

**4.4 Pattern C / CL-603: duplicate notification with the same message ID**
- H1: The first attempt succeeded and the retry was redundant; the claimant received one message (idempotent provider).
- H2: The claimant received two messages (non-idempotent delivery; minor harm).
- H3: The first attempt failed and only the retry succeeded, so the **synchronous** event is the wrong timestamp for measuring P4.
- Tests: per-attempt delivery receipts from the gateway, the provider's dedupe behaviour for repeated message IDs, connector timeout logs, and complaint records mentioning duplicates.

**4.5 Pattern B / CL-609 and CL-610: zero-cost auto-approval breaches**
- H1: Rules-engine misconfiguration (for example, a 50k ceiling entered instead of 5k, or MEDIUM treated as eligible).
- H2: Stale inputs: the risk score or bank-change flag had not arrived when the decision ran.
- H3: Manual override that forced the auto path.
- H4: The attributes changed after the decision (risk re-scored, amount supplemented, bank changed after approval). For CL-610, the P2 breach at payment time holds even in that case.
- Tests:
  - The rules-engine decision log with its input snapshot and rule version.
  - The timestamps of the bank-change record against the approval and payment.
  - Risk-score history.
  - Replay the four monthly cases through the current rule set to see whether the defect is still live.

**4.6 CL-611: Assess Damage versus Assess Loss**
- H1: A pure synonym (a source-system configuration name).
- H2: "Assess Damage" is a narrower sub-activity (a physical survey without quantifying the loss or coverage), so the full assessment may be missing.
- Tests:
  - Compare the data fields filled in by Assess Damage events with those of Assess Loss events in other cases (loss amount, reserve, assessor role).
  - Read the system release notes and the reason for the mapping change.
  - Get written confirmation from the procedure owner.
  - Check whether other cases contain **both** labels (which would argue against H1).

**4.7 CL-607 / CL-612: denial and withdrawal**
- H1: A legitimate terminal outcome that the model does not describe.
- H2: A problematic termination: a wrongful denial, a withdrawal the claimant was pressured into, or a claim later re-opened and paid outside the approval path.
- Tests: denial reason codes against the coverage data, a quality-assurance sample, complaints and ombudsman records, any later re-open or payment to the same claimant or policy, and the withdrawal request artefact (who initiated it and how) plus any acknowledgement.

**4.8 CL-608: notification only in the CRM**
- H1: Sent through the portal at +3h and delivered (compliant).
- H2: The text link points to a different or edited message.
- H3: The message was drafted but never delivered or published.
- Tests: portal publish and read logs keyed by immutable message ID, the field-level audit history of the CRM text field, and message content referencing the claim number.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

**What the number is.** An optimal alignment γ* minimises Σ c(m) over all move sequences that reconcile the trace with some complete run of the Petri net. Here c = 0 for synchronous and silent moves and c = 1 for every visible log or model move. So raw cost is the minimum number of insert and delete edits between the trace and the model's language. Business risk is roughly probability × impact of harm, given the evidence. The two share no inputs except the order of activity labels. Here is why, case by case.

1. **The cost function treats all moves alike.** Every visible move costs 1. CL-602's `M(Fraud Screen)` (a missing fraud control on EUR 480k with a recent bank change) weighs exactly the same as CL-603's `L(Notify Claimant)` (a transport retry). Uniform costs make the alignment answer "how many edits?", never "which edit matters?". Business weighting would help, but see points 4 and 8: some risks leave no moves to weight.

2. **Scope turns correct behaviour into cost.** CL-607 (denied) and CL-612 (withdrawn) have the highest cost, 4, because the payable-claims model forces the aligner to invent `M(Assess Loss)`, `M(Auto Approve)`, `M(Pay Claim)`, and even `M(Notify Claimant)`. Not paying these claims is correct. The invented `M(Auto Approve)` is chosen only because it is the cheapest branch of the XOR (1 move against 2 for Fraud Screen + Manual Approve), so it says nothing about the claim. Under P6 the question is which model applies, not whether the process deviated. Monthly evidence confirms this: denied and withdrawn cases make up most alignments with cost ≥4.

3. **Silent moves are free by design.** CL-605's `T(tau)` is a routing transition created by converting the tree to a Petri net. It costs 0, is fitting, and does not mean a business step was skipped. An analyst who counted "moves" rather than "visible deviations" would wrongly flag it. Cost is right here, but only because the cost function deliberately makes tau free.

4. **Data conditions are invisible.** CL-609 (EUR 18k, MEDIUM) and CL-610 (EUR 4.5k, bank change one hour before payment) both align perfectly, with cost 0, because the tree's XOR has no guards. P1 and P2 are predicates on amount, risk, and bank-change history. A control-flow alignment never reads them. No reweighting fixes this, since there are no moves to weight. Only a data-aware check can see these failures (four such cases this month).

5. **Granularity and labelling inflate cost.** CL-611 costs 2 for one real assessment event logged as `Assess Damage`: a log move plus a model move for the same activity. CL-601 costs 3 for three UI telemetry events (P5). Both costs come from how the log was extracted and labelled, not from how the claim was handled. Changing the mapping or the filter changes the cost with no change in behaviour, which shows the cost is not a property of the business process.

6. **Duplicated telemetry.** CL-603, like 74 cases this month, costs 1 because a connector retry was logged as a business event. Added up, retry duplicates would show "notification" as the most frequent deviation in the month, pure noise that hides real notification problems.

7. **Evidence outside the log.** CL-606's `M(Assess Loss)` and CL-608's `M(Notify Claimant)` each cost 1, but both activities very likely happened: a signed external adjuster report before the fraud screen, and a CRM-linked portal message at +3h. The alignment only sees the extracted core log. What remains is an evidence-integrity question (stable identifiers, an immutable link), which affects confidence and documentation compliance, not operational severity. The reverse also holds: missing evidence can matter a lot when the control *requires* retained evidence (CL-602's verbal check).

8. **Irreversibility and timing are not in the cost.** CL-604 costs 3, with the payment encoded as `L(Pay Claim)` + `M(Pay Claim)`. Swapping two events costs the same as two telemetry events, whether the swap is harmless or the money left the bank before anyone approved it. P3 states this explicitly: "severe even if the model alignment can repair it cheaply." The alignment "repairs" the trace on paper, but the funds are not repaired. Worse, under uniform costs a *cheaper* alignment exists for CL-604: `M(Auto Approve)`, `S(Pay Claim)`, `L(Manual Approve)` at cost 2. It would present the case as a small routing oddity and suggest an auto-approval path that P2 forbids for a EUR 90k claim. The optimiser minimises edits and has no idea what those edits mean for the business.

9. **Severity is not confidence.** Cost does not measure how reliable the evidence is. CL-602 (cost 1) is high-confidence, backed by the absence of any fraud-tool record plus self-serving testimony. CL-606 (cost 1) is medium-confidence, depending on document linkage. The same cost carries very different amounts of evidence.

**Consequence.** Cost ranks the sample roughly CL-607 = CL-612 > CL-601 = CL-604 > CL-611 > … > CL-609 = CL-610 = CL-605. Business severity ranks it CL-602 ≈ CL-604 > CL-610 > CL-609 > CL-606 ≈ CL-608 > the rest. The rank correlation is negative (§7). Raw cost is still useful as a **diagnostic locator**: it shows where the log and the model disagree. Treating it as a **risk score** would send investigators to denials and telemetry while zero-cost fraud exposure goes through unexamined. Normalised fitness (1 − cost / worst-case cost) inherits the same blind spots.
