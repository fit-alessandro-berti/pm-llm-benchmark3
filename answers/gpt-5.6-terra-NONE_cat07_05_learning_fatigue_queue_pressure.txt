# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is best understood as a mixture of changing work conditions and changing work behavior, not as a single “workers get tired” story.

Early in shifts, service times are high partly because the work is objectively harder. The first 1–5 events have the highest average complexity, the largest high-priority share, and substantial carryover backlog. Senior staff also appear to take difficult cases early, especially after night or weekend accumulation. Therefore, the early 18.8-minute service time should not be interpreted as lack of readiness or low productivity without adjustment.

From roughly positions 6–50, there is plausible warm-up or learning. Service time falls from 15.2 to 12.9 minutes, while error and rework rates initially decline or remain near their lowest levels. This is consistent with workers becoming oriented to the shift, retrieving task knowledge, settling into routines, and benefiting from fewer exceptional carryover cases. But part of this apparent learning is also compositional: complexity falls from 0.61 to 0.50 and high-priority share falls from 34% to 25%.

The late-shift pattern is qualitatively different. Service time continues to fall, reaching 10.8 minutes, but error and rework rise sharply. This is not the signature of uncomplicated learning. It is more compatible with queue pressure, expedited processing, batching, deferred quality work, and possibly fatigue interacting with those conditions.

The WIP evidence is especially important. Within both low- and high-complexity bands, high WIP is associated with shorter service times and much worse quality:

- Low-complexity cases: 12.7 minutes and 2.1% errors under low WIP, versus 9.4 minutes and 5.8% errors under high WIP.
- High-complexity cases: 22.4 minutes and 4.2% errors under low WIP, versus 16.8 minutes and 10.9% errors under high WIP.

That pattern suggests workers and the operating system are trading depth of processing for throughput under congestion. The shorter logged service times may represent faster decisions, reduced review, less documentation, task switching, deferred Quality Checks, or a change in which work is counted in the immediate activity duration. It should not automatically be interpreted as genuine efficiency improvement.

Fatigue remains plausible, especially because late-shift error and rework rates rise. However, fatigue is not isolated in this dataset. The final part of a shift is also when queue length is highest, Quality Checks are delayed, batch processing appears, demand may peak, and staffing or case ownership may change. A fatigue effect would be more credible if late-shift position predicts worse quality even among comparable workers handling comparable cases under similar WIP and queue conditions.

Circadian effects may also contribute, particularly for Night shift work, but they are difficult to separate from the unusual case mix. Night staff see fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, and the first-hour high-complexity share is highest on Night shift. A clock-time effect should be tested with workers who rotate across shifts or by comparing equivalent work conditions across time windows.

The resource-experience pattern reinforces the interaction view:

- Seniors speed up and retain relatively stable quality, though their error rate still rises under high WIP.
- Mid-level staff show larger quality degradation under pressure.
- Juniors have both early-shift complexity sensitivity and strong late/high-WIP risk.
- Night staff may face a combination of different demand, urgency, and timing conditions.

Thus, service time falls while errors rise only when speed is being produced under pressure, batching, or reduced checking—not when speed reflects harmless familiarization. A useful operational distinction is:

- **Early/mid-shift faster work with stable quality:** likely a mix of warm-up, reduced exception load, and routine familiarity.
- **Late/high-WIP faster work with rising errors and rework:** likely throughput pressure, delayed controls, workflow workarounds, and possibly fatigue layered on top.
- **Shift-specific differences:** potentially circadian, but heavily confounded by staffing, backlog inheritance, priorities, and assignment practices.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The analysis should estimate several mechanisms jointly rather than asking whether one simple correlation proves fatigue or learning.

Create derived fields that reflect mechanisms rather than relying on a raw sequence position alone:

```python
df["service_minutes"] = (
    pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"]) -
    pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"])
```

Important data-quality checks:

1. Confirm that `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp` describe actual start and completion rather than data-entry time.
2. Confirm whether `error_flag` is event-level, case-level, or detected later.
3. Define “rework within seven days” consistently and link it to the preceding activity.
4. Identify whether Bot_Risk has genuinely zero service time or whether human review is recorded elsewhere.
5. Verify that queue and WIP snapshots are measured consistently across shifts and activities.

## B. Within-resource comparisons

A core question is whether the same worker changes behavior over a shift after case mix and queue conditions are held constant.

For each resource, compare:

- early versus middle versus late shift position;
- matched activities;
- matched complexity and priority bands;
- comparable WIP/queue strata;
- comparable shift and clock-time conditions.

A fixed-effects model conceptually takes this form:

```text
log(service_time) ~ spline(minutes_since_shift_start)
                  + WIP + queue_length
                  + complexity + priority
                  + activity_family
                  + shift_id + clock_hour
                  + resource fixed effects
                  + resource experience
                  + interactions:
                    late_shift × high_WIP
                    experience × high_WIP
                    shift × clock_hour
```

For errors or rework, use logistic regression, mixed-effects logistic regression, or a generalized estimating equation:

```text
error_flag ~ spline(minutes_since_shift_start)
           + WIP + queue_length
           + complexity + priority
           + activity_family
           + shift + clock_hour
           + resource fixed effects
           + batch indicator
           + Bot_Risk interaction
           + late_shift × high_WIP
           + experience × high_WIP
```

Interpretation logic:

- A negative early-position service-time effect with stable error rates supports warm-up.
- A positive late-position error effect after queue and complexity adjustment is compatible with fatigue or end-of-shift effects.
- A strong high-WIP effect, especially with reduced service time and higher error/rework, supports pressure-induced speed-quality tradeoffs.
- Strong resource-specific slopes indicate that interventions should target workload design and support, not assume a uniform worker response.

Use flexible rather than linear shift-position modeling. The pattern is visibly nonlinear: early stabilization, mid-shift improvement, then late degradation in quality.

Suggested bins:

- 31–50

- 76+

Also use continuous splines for `minutes_since_shift_start` and `sequence_position_within_shift`.

## D. WIP and queue stratification

The existing coarse WIP-by-complexity table already points to a pressure effect. Extend it by:

- activity family;
- resource group;
- shift;
- priority;
- bot versus human decision path;
- early/middle/late shift position.

Use both WIP and queue length because they capture different aspects:

- WIP may reflect work already in progress.
- Queue length may reflect unstarted demand and perceived urgency.
- A worker may react more strongly to a visible queue than to the formal WIP metric.

```python
wip_summary = (
    df.groupby(["complexity_band", "high_wip", "resource_group", "shift_id"])

```

Assess threshold behavior around WIP 70. A queue alert is more credible if risk rises sharply near that threshold after adjustment, rather than merely increasing smoothly with workload.

## E. Complexity adjustment and case-mix/selection analysis

Raw resource performance is not comparable if seniors receive complex cases and juniors receive standardized work.

Control for:

- `case_complexity_score`;
- priority;
- activity family;
- whether the case is carryover/backlog;
- day of week and weekend-after effects;
- preceding path and prior rework;
- queue/WIP;
- shift and time of day;
- resource experience;
- whether the activity follows Bot_Risk.

Analyze assignment:

```python
assignment_summary = (
    df.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id"])
      .agg(
          events=("case:concept:name", "size"),
          complexity=("case_complexity_score", "mean"),
          high_priority=("priority", lambda s: (s == "high").mean()),
          mean_wip=("wip_at_start", "mean"),
          error_rate=("error_flag", "mean")

      .reset_index()

```

If assignment is non-random, consider propensity-score weighting or matching. For example, compare senior and junior events only within similar activity, complexity, priority, shift, and WIP cells. This will not fully establish causality, but it prevents misleading “senior versus junior” conclusions.

## F. Shift and time-of-day controls

Separate elapsed work time from clock time:

- `minutes_since_shift_start` captures shift progression;
- `shift_id` captures operational context;
- `clock_hour` captures time of day;
- day-of-week captures recurring demand patterns and post-weekend backlogs.

A worker rotating between Early and Late shifts is especially informative. If the same worker has elevated errors at the same clock hour across different shift positions, circadian/time-of-day effects become more plausible. If errors track elapsed time worked regardless of clock hour, fatigue or accumulated workload becomes more plausible.

# 4. Intervention options

|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May reduce excessive accumulated work time, overtime-related fatigue, and end-of-shift quality degradation. At most, review may identify training or system issues if conducted fairly and non-punitively. | High risk of unfairness, under-reporting, avoidance of complex cases, gaming, reduced psychological safety, and blaming workers for queue pressure or flawed automation. If individual review is necessary, use risk-adjusted metrics, confidence intervals, case-mix and assignment data, rework severity, audit consistency, and worker input. A practical priority order would be:

1. Validate and operationalize WIP/queue-pressure controls.
2. Add targeted senior support during Day peaks.
3. Improve Quality Check scheduling and handover design without eliminating controls.
4. Test micro-breaks and workload balancing using a controlled pilot.
5. Redesign assignment using constrained, skill-aware balancing rather than pure randomization.
6. Treat shift caps as a staffing-and-coverage policy, not as a standalone quality solution.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be misleading because event logs reflect an entire operating system, not just individual effort.

**Selection bias and case assignment.** Seniors appear to take more complex early work, especially backlog cases. Night staff handle more urgent exceptions. Juniors may receive different task types or be exposed to higher queue pressure. Comparing raw service times or raw error rates across these groups would unfairly penalize people assigned the hardest work.

**Queue pressure may be management-created.** High WIP can result from staffing levels, scheduling, demand forecasting, upstream release policies, system outages, approval bottlenecks, or deferred Quality Checks. It is inappropriate to attribute quality deterioration solely to workers when the organization has created a rush condition.

**Error measurement is not neutral.** An observed error rate depends on detection. If Quality Checks are delayed or inconsistently applied, some errors will be found later or not recorded. Teams with stronger auditing can appear worse simply because they detect more defects. Rework is also an imperfect proxy: some rework reflects legitimate customer changes, policy updates, or late-arriving documentation.

**Service time can be deceptive.** A short logged service time may reflect genuine competence, but it may also reflect batching, skipped verification, deferred documentation, handoffs, or the fact that cognitively demanding work occurred outside the recorded activity interval. Faster completion is not automatically better.

**Automation must be evaluated as part of the socio-technical system.** Bot_Risk may reduce apparent activity time while shifting work to human correction and Quality Check. Higher correction rates under high queues could indicate degraded human review under pressure, poor bot calibration for difficult cases, or both. Automation performance should be measured through downstream outcomes, not just instant completion time.

**Avoid punitive use of error rates.** Raw late-shift error rates must not be used to punish workers. Such a policy would encourage workers to avoid complex cases, under-report issues, delay case closure, or shift responsibility elsewhere. It would also undermine reporting and learning. Use error data for workload design, training, process improvement, safety review, and supportive coaching.

**Protect privacy and dignity.** Worker analytics should use purpose limitation, role-based access, aggregation where possible, transparent definitions, appeal mechanisms, and participation by workers and employee representatives. Individual-level analyses should be used only when statistically reliable, contextually reviewed, and connected to constructive support.

**Do not remove quality controls to improve superficial throughput.** Deferring selected low-priority checks may be reasonable if risk-based and tightly governed, but eliminating or systematically bypassing Quality Check would likely shift defects downstream and harm customers, staff, and compliance performance.

It should support drilling from aggregate trends into process paths and batches without turning into a punitive worker ranking system.

| Indicator | Definition | Key breakdowns | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Service time | Median, mean, and percentile service time from `start_timestamp` to `time:timestamp`. Show activity-level and case-level duration. | Activity, shift, sequence position, resource group, complexity, WIP band. | Detects warm-up, throughput mode, bottlenecks, and anomalous acceleration. Use medians and percentiles because durations are skewed. |
| 2. Waiting time | Time between prior relevant event/arrival and activity start; include case waiting and Quality Check waiting. | Shift, queue band, priority, activity, handover status. | Separates fast execution from long customer delay. Helps detect whether speed gains are achieved by deferring work. |
| 3. WIP and queue pressure | WIP at activity start, queue length at start, time above WIP > 70, and queue growth rate. | Shift, clock hour, activity family, day of week. | Identifies the high-risk congestion regime and whether intervention response occurs before quality deterioration. |
| 4. Error rate | Risk-adjusted and raw `error_flag` rate, with confidence intervals. | WIP band, complexity, shift position, activity, resource group. | Detects quality deterioration, but must always be interpreted with assignment and detection context. |
| 5. Rework rate and rework latency | Share of cases with `rework_flag`, Rework Correction frequency, and time from original activity to correction. | Original activity, WIP at original decision, bot/human path, shift. | Captures downstream quality consequences that immediate error flags can miss. |
| 6. Complexity and priority mix | Mean complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, carryover/backlog share. | Shift start versus end, resource group, assignment route. | Prevents misleading productivity comparisons and reveals whether difficult work is concentrated in particular windows. |
| 7. Batch/workaround indicator | Consecutive same-resource/same-activity runs, median run length, short inter-event gaps, batch-associated Quality Check delay. | Resource group, activity, shift, queue band. | Detects when apparent speed may come from batching or deferred controls rather than sustainable efficiency. |
| 8. Quality Check timeliness | Time from preceding decision to Quality Check; proportion completed in final 45 minutes; proportion deferred across shifts. | Shift, priority, complexity, activity path. | Detects end-of-shift checking bursts and protects against hidden quality debt. |
| 9. Handover and queue ownership changes | Number of resource changes per case, time between handover and next action, cases crossing shift boundaries, ownership changes under high WIP. | Shift transition, activity, complexity, priority. | Reveals whether backlog, deferral, and fragmented ownership are driving delay or errors. |
| 10. Worker-load fairness | Distribution of high-complexity, high-priority, high-WIP, and late-shift work across resources; workload concentration index. | Resource group and individual resource where governance permits. | Ensures difficult work and pressure exposure are not systematically concentrated on particular workers or groups. |
| 11. Experience-by-pressure interaction | Error/rework and service-time trends by experience group across WIP bands. | Seniors, mid-level, juniors, night staff; activity. | Supports targeted coaching, escalation rules, and staffing design rather than one-size-fits-all interventions. |
| 12. Automation quality and correction burden | Bot_Risk utilization, downstream correction rate, rework rate, human review lag, and bot × queue × complexity interaction. | Queue band, complexity, priority, shift. | Ensures automation is evaluated by end-to-end quality rather than instantaneous completion. |
| 13. Process-path performance | Performance DFG/path-specific duration, frequency, error and rework outcome for common paths. | High versus low WIP, bot versus non-bot, batch versus non-batch. | Identifies which routes become risky under pressure, such as Risk Classification  Quality Check  Rework Correction. |
| 14. Intervention impact panel | Before/after and pilot/control comparisons for alerts, staffing changes, breaks, assignment rules, and Quality Check scheduling. | Shift, site/team, activity, WIP regime. | Prevents judging interventions by throughput alone; requires simultaneous monitoring of quality, waiting, workload, and fairness. |

Recommended dashboard rules:

- Show raw and adjusted metrics side by side.
- Use control charts or confidence intervals rather than ranking workers by small differences.
- Alert on combinations, not just single thresholds: for example, **WIP > 70 + rising Quality Check delay + increased bot corrections**.
- Pair productivity measures with quality measures: service-time reduction should never be shown without error, rework, waiting-time, and complexity context.
- Treat late-shift error increases as an operational signal for support and redesign, not as automatic evidence of individual misconduct.
