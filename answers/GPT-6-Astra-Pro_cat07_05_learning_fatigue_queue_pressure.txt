# 2. Integrated explanation

## The beginning of a shift is not an unbiased baseline

The first events disproportionately involve difficult carryover: complexity averages 0.72, high-priority share is 41%, and seniors appear to absorb demanding backlog. Longer service at this point may represent appropriate attention to difficult claims, reconstruction of previous work, or handover effort—not poor worker performance.

By positions 31–50, mean service time is 12.9 minutes, approximately **31% below** the first-bin mean. However, complexity has also fallen to 0.50 and high-priority share to 25%. It would be unjustified to assign that whole improvement to learning.

A plausible account is that orientation and repeated practice improve fluency while the work being selected also becomes easier. The early decline in errors is consistent with warm-up, but does not distinguish it from those compositional changes.

## The later acceleration has a different quality signature

Between positions 31–50 and 76+, service time falls another **16%**, but errors rise from 2.7% to 7.9% and seven-day rework from 5.0% to 13.7%. Mean queue length rises from 31 to 88.

The WIP table makes this more than a simple comparison of early complex cases with later easy cases:

| Complexity band |   Low-WIP to high-WIP service change |                        Error-rate change |
| --------------- | -----------------------------------: | ---------------------------------------: |
| Low complexity  |  12.7 → 9.4 minutes: **26% shorter** |  2.1% → 5.8%: **+3.7 percentage points** |
| High complexity | 22.4 → 16.8 minutes: **25% shorter** | 4.2% → 10.9%: **+6.7 percentage points** |

These are descriptive contrasts, not causal estimates. Nevertheless, they support investigating a **speed–quality trade-off under pressure**, rather than treating every reduction in service time as productive learning.

Pressure may also alter which work gets selected. Evidence from another operational setting shows that easier-task selection can produce apparent throughput gains that look different after complexity adjustment; that establishes plausibility, not that the same mechanism has been proven here. ([PubsOnline][1])

## Why errors rise only under some conditions

Later low-WIP errors are 2.2% for seniors, 2.9% for mid-level staff, and 4.4% for juniors. Later high-WIP errors are 4.9%, 6.8%, and 12.4%, respectively.

This suggests that workers may become faster without substantial quality loss when work is manageable, but respond differently when demand exceeds available attention or support. Fatigue could make that response worse. It is not necessary to choose between “fatigue” and “pressure”: their interaction is a credible hypothesis.

However, experience groups also receive different work and support. The junior contrast does not establish that juniors are intrinsically less resilient. Moreover, **Night staff is a shift-assignment category, not an experience level**.

## Some apparent deterioration may be a recording or inspection effect

A Quality Check at 16:40 may identify an error in a decision made at 11:00. A Rework Correction at 16:50 may be performed by someone who did not create the error. Grouping either event by its own timestamp can misattribute the defect to late-shift work or to the correcting worker.

Likewise, short Document Review intervals may record only the final clicks of a batch whose preparation occurred earlier. Bot-related corrections can add work without revealing whether the bot deteriorated, its inputs became harder, or humans inspected it differently.

A plausible reinforcing cycle is:

**Demand or inherited backlog → pressure and changed work practices → additional defects or deferred checking → corrections and carryover → further pressure.**

The supplied evidence is compatible with this cycle, but does not establish every causal link. The operational objective should therefore be **timely, correct resolution with sustainable workload**, not minimum recorded service time.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Measurement and cohort construction

Treat each human activity execution as the event-level observational unit. Keep a separate, unfiltered case-history table containing human and bot events for downstream analysis.

Before modeling, establish the following measurement contract.

**Service time.** Calculate completion minus start for each execution. Validate timestamp precision, timezone, negative intervals, missing starts, duplicate source records, and overlapping activities. An open task window is not necessarily active labor. Examine human zero-duration events separately rather than mixing them with positive durations in a log-duration model.

**Position.** Define a worker-shift using resource, shift instance, and full shift-start timestamp. Preserve Night shifts across midnight. Determine whether sequence position means start order or completion order; simultaneous events need a documented tie policy. Do not reset sequence numbers after filtering activities.

**Exposure.** Confirm the scope of `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start`: worker, team, queue, or whole center. Reconstruct them from records available immediately before the event, not future case outcomes. Validate that complexity and priority are also their pre-event values.

**Labels and denominators.** Establish whether `error_flag` means a confirmed error, an error discovered during this execution, or an unresolved flag. Distinguish uninspected from inspected-and-correct. Clarify whether the WIP table’s “Cases/events” counts are events or unique claims; repeated events within a claim are not independent observations.

**Coverage.** Do not restrict the primary service analysis to cases that eventually close. Retain pending cases and inspect incomplete activity intervals near extraction. For seven-day outcomes, require seven days of complete downstream observation or explicitly model censoring.

The missing fields with greatest analytical value are stable event identifiers, queue and batch identifiers, readiness/queue-entry timestamps, assignment histories, break and staffing intervals, error-origin links, detection timestamps, inspection coverage, and bot versions.

## Statistical comparisons and identification

### Within-resource and within-shift comparisons

Fit event-level models separately for service time and quality, using activity-specific adjustment and resource effects. A suitable starting point is a positive-duration Gamma or lognormal model for service and a logistic model for adjudicated errors.

Estimate nonlinear associations with elapsed shift time, load, and complexity. Include priority, activity, calendar date or demand-period effects, shift type, and supported clock-time terms. Then estimate prespecified interactions:

* elapsed time × load;
* experience months × load;
* activity × load, with batching examined separately.

Use worker-shift fixed effects as a sensitivity analysis to compare events within the same shift. These absorb resource and shift-constant characteristics, so redundant fixed effects should not also be fitted.

**Event sequence is endogenous:** faster workers and shorter activities reach higher positions sooner. Workers appearing at position 76+ need not be representative of those appearing only in early bins. Use elapsed time as the primary clock, examine sequence and prior same-activity repetitions in alternative models, and report how many worker-shifts support each comparison. Do not interpret a high sequence position as an externally assigned treatment.

### Complexity and load adjustment

Use the continuous complexity score with nonlinear terms, not only the two coarse bands. Adjust for activity identity: a change from Eligibility Decision to Close Case can reduce mean duration without any within-activity improvement.

Estimate standardized service and error curves over a common distribution of pre-event complexity, activity, priority, and resource characteristics. Restrict comparisons to overlapping support; do not extrapolate, for example, to combinations of senior work, activity, and load that are never observed.

Model WIP and queue length separately before combining them. Strong correlation can make their individual coefficients unstable. Add capacity-normalized measures when staffing becomes available, such as estimated remaining work per available qualified worker-hour.

### Distinguishing the clocks

Clock time equals shift start plus elapsed time. With fixed schedules, highly flexible elapsed-time, shift, and clock-time effects may not be separately identifiable.

Useful comparisons include Early and Day staff during their overlapping hours, and Day and Late staff during theirs, while acknowledging worker and assignment differences. Naturally rotating workers provide stronger within-person contrasts. Where overlap is absent, retain uncertainty instead of labeling a fitted clock-time curve “circadian.”

Experimental research distinguishes time awake from circadian phase; this log contains neither direct biological phase nor time-awake measurements. A clock-time association here would therefore remain a broader operational association. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][2])

### Uncertainty and causal interpretation

Account for repeated events within cases, shared queue conditions, and dependence within worker-shifts. Use an appropriate multilevel model or cluster/block resampling, including common queue-day shocks. There are only thirteen named human resources: a large event count does not make worker-level comparisons highly precise.

Evaluate models on later calendar blocks, with linked cases kept together. Report effect sizes, uncertainty intervals, sample sizes, and support—not just statistical significance.

An adjusted WIP coefficient is still not automatically causal. Earlier processing and rework help create later WIP. Also, controlling for WIP when evaluating a staffing intervention would remove part of the intervention’s intended pathway. Separate **descriptive conditional associations** from **total effects of operational policies**.

## Process-mining and downstream analyses

**Interval and waiting semantics.** `get_service_time` supports activity-level summaries when the start key is supplied explicitly. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is different: its implementation sums event durations within a case and subtracts that sum from the case span. Overlapping intervals can therefore make its waiting result misleading or negative. Use interval unions for case non-processing time, and readiness-to-start timestamps for genuine queue waiting. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][3])

**Flow over time.** Use `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` for retrospective temporal summaries. Do not treat its full-period features as pre-event predictors. Despite its name, `insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` provides differences between case start/finish timestamps, not a directly observed arrival intensity. For operational demand, count actual arrivals and terminal completions in fixed time windows, retaining backlog and transfers. 

**Batching.** Run `discover_batches` within worker-shifts, with explicit start-timestamp metadata and sensitivity to the merge-distance setting. Its temporal categories identify candidates, not proof of shared queue membership or omitted work. Validate the reported Document Review runs against all intervening worker activities; filtering everything except Document Review first would manufacture apparent consecutiveness. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][4])

**Rework attribution.** Define, for example,

$$
R_7(e)=1
$$

when a confirmed correction linked to originating event \(e\) starts within seven days of that event’s completion. This requires an origin link. Without it, report a weaker **same-case subsequent-correction association**, not an error caused by a particular worker.

Separate defect creation, detection, correction, and final resolution. Examine longer follow-up as a sensitivity analysis: congestion could delay corrections beyond seven days without reducing the underlying defects. `get_rework_cases_per_activity` counts cases with repeated activities; legitimate repeated reviews can satisfy this definition, so it is not a replacement for attributed rework. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][5])

**Process paths and visualization.** Use dotted charts aligned both to clock time and shift start, with resource or worker-shift lanes. Compare complete case histories for cohorts exposed to different loads. A performance DFG describes timing between observed neighboring events, not necessarily queue waiting or causal dependencies. Event-level filtering before discovery can create artificial neighboring activities; preserve full histories or calculate transitions first and then stratify them. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][6])

### PM4Py-oriented implementation skeleton

This skeleton expects the raw interval log, not the supplied aggregate tables. Its outputs are descriptive; error attribution and audit eligibility require the additional business records described above.

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

C = "case:concept:name"
A = "concept:name"
T = "time:timestamp"
S = "start_timestamp"
R = "org:resource"

def build_analysis_views(
    raw: pd.DataFrame,
    complete_case_ids: set[str],
    followup_complete_through: pd.Timestamp,
    local_timezone: str,
) -> dict:
    """
    Preconditions:
      - Source duplicates have been investigated, not blindly removed.
      - error_flag is numeric/boolean; unknown labels remain missing.
      - complete_case_ids identifies fully observed case histories.
      - followup_complete_through is a verified coverage watermark,
        not simply the largest event timestamp.
    """
    needed = {
        C, A, T, S, R, "shift_id", "shift_start_time",
        "sequence_position_within_shift", "wip_at_start",
        "queue_length_at_start", "case_complexity_score", "error_flag",
    }
    missing = needed - set(raw.columns)
    if missing:
        raise ValueError(f"Missing columns: {sorted(missing)}")

    e = raw.copy()
    for col in [S, T, "shift_start_time"]:
        values = pd.to_datetime(e[col], errors="raise")
        if not isinstance(values.dtype, pd.DatetimeTZDtype):
            raise ValueError(f"{col} must be timezone-aware.")
        e[col] = values.dt.tz_convert("UTC")

    if e[[C, A, R, S, T]].isna().any().any():
        raise ValueError("Investigate missing identifiers or intervals.")
    if (e[T] < e[S]).any():
        raise ValueError("Investigate negative service intervals.")

    e = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        e, case_id=C, activity_key=A, timestamp_key=T
    )
    e.attrs["pm4py:param:start_timestamp_key"] = S
    e["service_min"] = (e[T] - e[S]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

    # Exclude bot executions only from human event-level analyses.
    h = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        e, R, ["Bot_Risk"], level="event", retain=False
    ).copy()

    if h[["shift_id", "shift_start_time"]].isna().any().any():
        raise ValueError("Human events require a known shift instance.")

    h["worker_shift"] = (
        h.groupby([R, "shift_id", "shift_start_time"])
         .ngroup().astype(str)
    )
    h["elapsed_min"] = (
        h[S] - h["shift_start_time"]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 60

    local_start = h[S].dt.tz_convert(local_timezone)
    h["clock_hour"] = local_start.dt.hour + local_start.dt.minute / 60

    # Preserve audited source sequence; never renumber after filtering.
    h["sequence_bin"] = pd.cut(
        h["sequence_position_within_shift"],
        bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, np.inf],
        labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
    )
    w = h["wip_at_start"]
    h["wip_band"] = np.select(
        [w.lt(30), w.between(30, 70), w.gt(70)],
        ["low", "medium", "high"],
        default="missing",
    )

    cutoff = pd.Timestamp(followup_complete_through)
    if cutoff.tzinfo is None:
        raise ValueError("Follow-up watermark must be timezone-aware.")
    h["window_available_7d"] = (
        h[T] + pd.Timedelta(days=7) <= cutoff
    )
    # Window availability is NOT itself an attributed rework label.

    panel = h.groupby(
        [R, "shift_id", A, "sequence_bin", "wip_band"],
        observed=True, dropna=False,
    ).agg(
        events=("service_min", "size"),
        cases=(C, "nunique"),
        service_median_min=("service_min", "median"),
        service_p90_min=("service_min", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)),
        known_error_labels=("error_flag", "count"),
        recorded_error_rate=("error_flag", "mean"),
        mean_complexity=("case_complexity_score", "mean"),
    )

    batch_candidates = {}
    for worker_shift, g in h.groupby("worker_shift", sort=False):
        g = g.copy()
        g.attrs["pm4py:param:start_timestamp_key"] = S
        for gap_seconds in [60, 120, 300]:
            batch_candidates[(worker_shift, gap_seconds)] = (
                pm4py.discover_batches(
                    g, merge_distance=gap_seconds, min_batch_size=3,
                    activity_key=A, timestamp_key=T,
                    case_id_key=C, resource_key=R,
                )
            )

    # Retain bots and all intervening events in complete case histories.
    full = e[e[C].isin(complete_case_ids)].copy()
    case_diagnostics = None
    performance_dfg = None
    repeated_activity_cases = {}

    if not full.empty:
        enriched = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
            full.copy(), start_timestamp_key=S
        )
        case_diagnostics = enriched[
            [C, "@@service_time", "@@sojourn_time", "@@waiting_time"]
        ].drop_duplicates(C)
        # Additive-duration diagnostic, not certified queue waiting.

        repeated_activity_cases = (
            pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(full)
        )

        # Explicit completion-to-completion timing, not queue waiting.
        dfg_input = full.drop(columns=[S]).copy()
        dfg_input.attrs = {}
        performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
            dfg_input, business_hours=False
        )

    return {
        "human_events": h,
        "descriptive_panel": panel,
        "batch_candidates": batch_candidates,
        "complete_case_diagnostics": case_diagnostics,
        "repeated_activity_cases": repeated_activity_cases,
        "performance_dfg": performance_dfg,
    }
```

Use `filter_case_performance` and `filter_paths_performance` to investigate slow-case and slow-transition cohorts, not to remove inconvenient long cases from the main analysis. `cluster_log` can help discover retrospective route differences, but clusters based on completed trajectories may incorporate downstream rework and should not automatically become baseline adjustment variables. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][5])

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker effects are not worker culpability.** Seniors receive difficult early cases; juniors may have less support; Night staff receive different exceptions. Even adjusted estimates can retain unmeasured differences. Individual conclusions require case review and an opportunity to challenge both the records and their interpretation.

**The person detecting or correcting an error may be protecting the process.** A correction attached to that person’s event is not evidence that they created the defect. Differences in inspection coverage also make “few recorded errors” ambiguous.

**Management creates part of the exposure.** Staffing, work allocation, performance targets, inherited backlog, and delayed quality checking shape queue pressure. Accountability for these conditions should not be transferred to workers through raw outcome rankings.

**Automation must remain visible.** Bot errors should not disappear into human correction statistics. Equally, human overrides should not automatically be classified as failures: some are successful safeguards.

**Measurement should not become intrusive surveillance.** Use the minimum information needed for workload and quality analysis. Restrict access to identifiable records, suppress unstable small-group comparisons, involve workers in metric definitions, and avoid public league tables. Break information should support coverage and recovery, not punishment for inactivity.

Finally, neither batching nor fast service establishes an intentional shortcut. Those are hypotheses requiring workflow validation. Rewarding transparent reporting and correct resolution is preferable to incentives that merely move defects, difficult claims, or unfinished work beyond the reporting boundary.

Use regular snapshots or reconstructed state intervals for time-weighted metrics. Include reopens and transfers in flow reconciliation. |
| **4. Error rate by origin period**                | Confirmed errors per eligible audited execution, severity breakdown, standardized rate, uncertainty interval, and audit coverage.                                                       | Show discovery-period counts separately. Distinguish uninspected records from confirmed negatives; use consistent or probability-weighted audit sampling.                                             |
| **5. Seven-day rework**                           | Share of eligible originating decisions with linked correction within seven days; correction effort and repeat-correction counts.                                                       | Use mature denominators and origin links. Show incomplete follow-up separately. Without attribution, label the result “same-case subsequent correction.”                                              |
| **6. Quality-check coverage and delay**           | Completed required checks / checks due; decision-to-check delay; overdue-check age; proportion occurring in the final 45 minutes.                                                       | Coverage comes from the applicable checking policy, not an assumption that every claim follows one route. Prevent apparent gains from deferred inspection.                                            |
| **7. Complexity and priority mix**                | Complexity distribution, high-complexity share, priority share, activity mix, and carryover age for both served and waiting work.                                                       | Makes early-shift selection and possible late easy-task selection visible. Monitor pre-event score availability and version changes.                                                                  |
| **8. Batching indicators**                        | Candidate and validated batch shares; batch-size distribution; same-activity run lengths; start/end timestamp concentration; batch-level quality and elapsed effort.                    | Validate with batch identifiers and full worker timelines. Distinguish efficient shared setup from recording artifacts or postponed effort.                                                           |
| **9. Automation quality and human recovery work** | Audited bot-error rate, correction rate per bot classification, verification coverage, and human correction minutes, split by bot version and load.                                     | Correction frequency alone cannot identify bot accuracy. Include audited uncorrected outputs to detect defects that escape human review.                                                              |
| **10. Handovers and queue ownership changes**     | Actual ownership transfers per case; transfer reasons; time waiting after transfer; cross-shift carryover.                                                                              | Consecutive events by different resources are only a handover proxy. Actual queue ownership needs assignment history and queue identifiers.                                                           |
| **11. Worker-load fairness**                      | Distribution of expected work minutes per available qualified worker-hour; difficult/urgent work exposure; high-pressure time; correction burden; supported-break coverage.             | Compare appropriately eligible peers. Use pre-event workload estimates rather than rewarding historically short recorded durations. Include mentoring and other support work where recorded.          |
| **12. End-to-end, quality-adjusted output**       | Unique correctly resolved cases per staffed hour; first-pass resolution; median and 90th-percentile case turnaround; downstream rework burden.                                          | Define resolution episodes consistently and allow outcomes to mature. Do not equate extra events or corrections with additional productive throughput.                                                |

Use the dashboard to trigger joint interpretation. A fall in service time alongside rising errors, rework, or overdue checks should initiate investigation—not a productivity celebration. Conversely, an initial rise in detected errors after adding senior review may indicate better inspection; independent audit, downstream outcomes, and stable denominators are needed to tell the difference.

**The decision rule should be to improve correct, timely resolution without exporting workload or defects to another worker, another shift, or an unobserved future period.**
