# 2. Integrated explanation

**The headline curves are at least five mechanisms superimposed, and the aggregates are not a clean function of time-on-task.** Two facts have to be reconciled: (a) mean service time falls monotonically from 18.8  10.8 min across the shift, while (b) error rate falls from 3.4%  2.5% and then rises sharply to 7.9%, with rework tripling from 4.8%  13.7%. A single-mechanism story fails on at least one of these.

**Phase 1 (bins 1–5): slow and error-prone because the work is hard and the worker is cold.** The first bin is not representative work. Complexity is 0.72 (the maximum observed), high-priority share 41%, and the queue is 46 — the notes call out "backlog from previous shift, complex carryover." So the 18.8 min is a *composition* effect to a substantial degree: a worker opening a shift inherits the hard, deferred, exception-laden residue the previous shift could not finish. On top of that sits a genuine warm-up cost (cognitive context reconstruction, tool/system startup, re-familiarization with cases) and, in some shifts, a circadian or sleep-inertia component. The elevated 3.4% error rate is therefore **not** evidence of a bad start in skill terms — it is the expected error rate for complexity 0.72 work done cold.

**Phase 2 (bins 6–30): the only region where "learning" is a defensible reading.** Service time falls 15.2  13.6 min while errors fall 2.8%  2.5%. Both metrics improving together is the signature of *either* genuine within-worker speed–skill gains *or* simply easier work arriving. The composition changes in exactly the direction that would produce this without any learning at all (complexity 0.61  0.54, priority 34%  28%, queue 39  33). Even the within-resource comparisons do not settle it, because Seniors *take* complex cases early — so a Senior's "first 20 events" are not exchangeable with their "later events." The honest reading: warm-up is real and plausible (and largest for Juniors, who start at 19.6 min vs Seniors' 15.9), but its magnitude is bounded above by the composition-adjusted slope, which has not yet been measured.

**Phase 3 (bins 31–50): the trough.** The worker is warm, the caseload is at its easiest (complexity 0.50), the queue is at its lowest (31), and errors are at their floor (2.7%). This is the operational "sweet spot" and it is *jointly produced* by rhythm and by workload mix — it is not a baseline to which later bins are a decay.

**Phase 4 (bins 51–76+): speed rises and quality collapses — and this is principally a pressure and artifact story.** Here the composition argument reverses against a fatigue reading. Complexity keeps *falling* (0.46  0.43) and high-priority share keeps falling (22%  19%). If these workers were doing easier work and merely tiring, errors should be flat-to-modestly-up. Instead they nearly double (2.7%  4.6%  7.9%) and rework nearly triples. What changed is the queue: 31  62  88. Within the stratified table, holding complexity constant, moving from low to high WIP takes error from 2.1%  5.8% (easy work) and 4.2%  10.9% (hard work), while service time *drops* by 20–25%. That is a textbook **speed–accuracy trade-off under workload**, i.e. per-event effort is being rationed: workers shorten verification, skip checks, and defer judgments. Crucially, the resource table shows this is **conditional**: later-in-shift under *low* WIP, errors are 2.2% / 2.9% / 4.4% for Seniors / Mids / Juniors — and for Juniors that is *lower* than their first-20 rate of 5.1%. Under *high* WIP the same workers hit 4.9% / 6.8% / 12.4%. **Fatigue is at most a vulnerability that pressure unlocks, not a clock that fires on its own.** The interaction is where the risk lives, and it is amplified inexperience (Juniors 12.4%  2.5× Seniors' 4.9%).

Two artifacts then exaggerate the Phase-4 signal. First, **batching**: Junior_H and Junior_I execute 9–18 consecutive Document Reviews with very short service times and a shared queue batch late in the Day shift — mechanically dragging the 76+ mean service time toward 10.8 min without any acceleration of true per-case effort. Second, **deferred quality checking**: Quality Check is pushed to end of shift, so corrections burst in the last 45 minutes. Errors are *detected* late even when they were *generated* earlier; attributing them to the late-shift worker's state conflates detection timing with production timing. The 7.9% is therefore an upper bound on "late-shift error generation."

**Circadian effects are present but secondary and cannot be read off the shift table.** Night is slowest (15.2 min) — consistent with reduced psychomotor speed at 22:00–06:00 — yet its last-two-hours error rate (5.1%) is *below* Day's (7.5%) and Late's (6.8%), despite Night handling 56% high-complexity work and urgent exceptions. If circadian fatigue dominated quality, Night should be worst on every metric. What actually differentiates shifts is their **queue trajectory and demand timing**: Early *clears* backlog (61  42, 4.2% late errors), while Day *accumulates* it (38  75, 7.5% late errors) because demand peaks at the end of the Day window. Late inherits Day's backlog (70  66). So the "late-shift" aggregate bins are a mixture across shifts in which Day and Late workers sit at peak queue while Early workers sit at 12:00–14:00 in a clearing queue. Averaging them is a Simpson's-paradox trap: the apparent universal late-shift error inflation is disproportionately a Day/Late-shift, peak-queue phenomenon, with a genuine but smaller circadian component (post-lunch dip in Early/Day at ~13:00–15:00; Night nadir 02:00–06:00) and a plausible residual time-on-task/vigilance decrement that has not yet been isolated.

Finally, **automation feeds the late-day error rate**. Bot_Risk returns instant Risk Classification, which does two things: it removes some easy volume from human queues (shifting residual human case mix toward harder judgments), and it emits classifications that humans must correct — correction probability rises when `queue_length_at_start > 70`, exactly the condition that dominates bins 51–76+. It is unresolved whether the bot's model degrades on the exception-heavy load it sees late, or whether human verification degrades under pressure (or both). Either way, some of the measured "human late-shift error" is upstream automation defect surfacing downstream.

**Why service time falls while errors rise — only in some conditions.** Because the two are governed by different terms. Service time is pulled down by four forces that all strengthen late in the shift: easier case mix, accumulated warm-up/skill gain, deliberate effort rationing under queue pressure, and batching artifacts. Error rate is pulled up by a *different* set: effort rationing under pressure, its interaction with inexperience and complexity, undetected-from-earlier errors surfacing at deferred QC, and automation corrections. **When WIP is low late in the shift, the error-inflating forces are switched off and only the service-time-deflating ones remain — producing exactly what we see (Juniors 13.7 min and 4.4%, better than their start). When WIP is high, effort rationing activates and the trade-off appears.** The correct summary is not "workers get worse as the shift goes on," but "workers get faster as the shift goes on for partly compositional and partly real reasons; whether they also get worse depends almost entirely on the load the organization has placed in front of them, and how much experience they have to absorb it."

import pandas as pd, numpy as np, statsmodels.formula.api as smf, statsmodels.api as sm
import pm4py

CASE, ACT, TS, ST, RES = ('case:concept:name', 'concept:name',
                          'time:timestamp', 'start_timestamp', 'org:resource')

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id_key=CASE, activity_key=ACT,
                            timestamp_key=TS, start_timestamp_key=ST, resource_key=RES)

# case-level service and waiting time decomposition
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df, start_timestamp_key=ST, timestamp_key=TS)

# exogenous-ish workload signal for the queue-pressure instrument

# clock-time features (hour of day, day of week, ...) to separate circadian from time-on-task
temp = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df, timestamp_key=TS)
# NOTE: return signature varies by PM4Py version -> may be (df, feature_names)
df = df.join(temp) if isinstance(temp, pd.DataFrame) and temp.index.equals(df.index) else temp[0].join(df)

# derived fields
df['service_time'] = (df[TS] - df[ST]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
df['hour_of_day']  = df[TS].dt.hour + df[TS].dt.minute / 60
df['pos_bin'] = pd.cut(df['sequence_position_within_shift'],

df['complexity_band'] = np.where(df['case_complexity_score'] >= df['case_complexity_score'].median(),
                                 'high', 'low')

                        labels=['low', 'medium', 'high'])
df['is_bot']  = df[RES].eq('Bot_Risk')
df['exp_group'] = np.select(
    [df[RES].str.startswith('Senior'), df[RES].str.startswith('Mid'),
     df[RES].str.startswith('Junior'), df[RES].str.startswith('Night')],
    ['senior', 'mid', 'junior', 'night'], default='other')

### 3.1 Within-resource comparisons (kills between-worker selection)

The headline risk is that Seniors take complex work early and Juniors take residual work — so "learning curves" measured across a worker's own shift are still contaminated by within-shift case-mix drift.

```python
# 1) entropy-balance / match EARLY (<=20 events in shift) to LATE (>20) WITHIN each worker
rows = []
for (res, sid), g in df_hum.groupby([RES, 'shift_id']):
    g = g.sort_values('minutes_since_shift_start')
    g = g.assign(period=np.where(g['sequence_position_within_shift'] <= 20, 'early', 'late'))
    for act, ga in g.groupby(ACT):
        for band in ['low', 'high']:
            s = ga[ga['complexity_band'] == band]
            if {'early','late'} <= set(s['period']):
                e, l = s[s.period=='early'], s[s.period=='late']
                rows.append(dict(resource=res, shift=sid, activity=act, complexity=band,
                                 n_early=len(e), n_late=len(l),
                                 st_early=e['service_time'].mean(), st_late=l['service_time'].mean(),
                                 err_early=e['error_flag'].mean(), err_late=l['error_flag'].mean()))
within = pd.DataFrame(rows)

# 2) mixed model: same worker, same activity, complexity + WIP + clock hour controlled
m = smf.mixedlm(
    "service_time ~ bs(minutes_since_shift_start, df=4) + complexity_band + C(priority) "
    "+ C(activity_family) + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start + C(hour_of_day) "
    "+ exp_group + complexity_band:wip_at_start + exp_group:wip_at_start "
    "+ minutes_since_shift_start:exp_group",
    data=df_hum, groups=df_hum[RES], re_formula="1").fit()

# 3) error outcome -> logistic mixed model (or GEE with resource clusters)
me = smf.glm(
    "error_flag ~ bs(minutes_since_shift_start, df=4) + complexity_band + C(priority) "
    "+ C(activity_family) + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start + C(hour_of_day) "
    "+ exp_group + wip_at_start:exp_group + minutes_since_shift_start:wip_at_start",
    data=df_hum, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(cov_type='cluster', cov_kwds={'groups': df_hum[RES]})
```

**Diagnostic that discriminates learning from fatigue from pressure:** after full adjustment,
- a significant negative spline on service time with a **non-significant** term on error, steeper for low `resource_experience_months`  **learning**;
- a significant **positive** term on error that is monotone in time-on-task *and persists within the low-WIP stratum*  **fatigue**;
- significance only through `minutes_since_shift_start:wip_at_start` and `wip_at_start`  **queue pressure**.

Placebo / negative control: repeat for the `Rework Correction` activity (a mechanical corrective task with little learning surface). If the "learning slope" appears there too, the slope is composition or warm-up system load, not skill acquisition. Weekend-gap placebo: include `days_since_last_shift`; learning predicts a discontinuity after gaps, pure time-on-task does not.

### 3.4 Complexity adjustment (composition vs rate)

# 4. Intervention options

| # | Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk | Monitoring with event-log indicators |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Strictly cap shift length for all workers** | Removes exposure to the 76+ bins where error is 7.9%; a hard cap is the only lever that guarantees bounded time-on-task. | Crude and possibly counterproductive: the 76+ bin is *not* where capacity is idle — queues are 88 there. Shortening shifts shrinks supply exactly when demand builds (Day ends at 75 queue), raising queue pressure on the remaining staff and likely **increasing** the error rate at WIP > 70 (10.9% zone). Also creates more handovers, and handovers already generate the complex carryover that makes bins 1–5 expensive. Does not address complexity, automation, or QC deferral. | `wip_at_start` distribution and share of events at WIP > 70; queue at shift end by `shift_id`; handover waiting time from `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`; error rate at fixed *time-on-task* (not fixed clock) to avoid shifting the problem; coverage gaps as idle-time holes in the dotted chart. **Compare pre/post at matched WIP**, not in aggregate. |
| **2** | **Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift** | Removes the composition confound and the cold-start penalty on hard work; expected drop in bins 1–5 service time (18.8  closer to 15) and error (3.4%  ~2.8%). | Deliberate deferral of complexity simply relocates it into the late bins — the very bins where WIP and errors already peak — and increases cycle time and SLA exposure for complex claims. Night handles 56% high-complexity in the first hour with **urgent exceptions** that cannot be deferred. Can also entrench the pattern that hard work is postponed rather than resourced. | Cycle time and `insert_case_service_waiting_time` waiting time **split by complexity band**; SLA/priority breach counts; complex-case share per `pos_bin` (target: flatten 0.72  0.43); complex-case error rate by position — the intervention succeeds only if it lowers errors somewhere without raising them elsewhere. Apply to **non-urgent** complexity only; verify with `pm4py.filter_paths_performance` that no slow edge appears for complex cases. |
| **3** | **Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70** | Directly targets the best-evidenced risk factor. May push risk onto the least experienced, since they are the most load-sensitive (12.4%). | Share of events with `wip_at_start > 70` and total minutes in that state; error and rework rate conditional on WIP band; `queue_length_at_start` vs `wip_at_start` divergence (gaming signal); bot-correction rate at `queue_length_at_start > 70`; time from alert to staffing/threshold response. **Success = the WIP-band error gap narrows**, not just that WIP falls. |

| **5** | **Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours** | Plausible vigilance restoration where measured error is highest; cheap; direct test of the fatigue hypothesis (see §1) which currently is *not* supported at low WIP. | The final two hours are when Quality Check bursts, corrections and handovers occur — breaks will collide with the closing workload and can inflate queue/WIP at exactly the worst moment. | **7** | **Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Attacks the actual bottleneck: Day queue 38  75 with 7.5% late errors. | **8** | **Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | *As stated, no defensible expected benefit.* Any apparent benefit is a measurement artifact of reporting suppression. | **Rejected.** Raw late-shift error rate is a product of queue pressure the organization created (WIP 88 at bin 76+), case mix and carryover selection, batching and deferred-QC detection timing, Bot_Risk defects surfacing as human corrections, shift-specific demand curves (Day 38  75), and experience. Punishing it punishes workers for system state. # 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Raw worker-level error rates are not worker competence.** The event log records an *outcome of a joint system*: the case that arrived, the priority stamp someone assigned, the queue that management's staffing policy created, the automation that pre-classified the claim, and the moment at which someone chose to look for the defect. Attributing `error_flag` to `org:resource` attributes all of that to one person. In these data, the single strongest predictor of error is `wip_at_start > 70` crossed with `case_complexity_score` — neither of which the worker chooses.

**Selection bias runs in both directions and is invisible in the aggregates.** Seniors take complex cases early (stated in the data notes), which makes seniors look slower and juniors look better on raw service time; equally, if routing sends residual hard cases to whoever is free, a worker's measured error rate reflects routing luck. Case complexity falls monotonically 0.72  0.43 across the shift, so "late-shift performance" is computed on systematically easier work — meaning the *true* late-shift quality degradation is **larger** than 7.9% implies relative to a matched baseline, while the *apparent* late speed-up is **smaller** than 10.8 min implies. Both naive conclusions (workers get slower, workers get faster) are wrong. Complexity adjustment and stratified assignment are prerequisites for any worker-level statement.

**Measurement error and detection timing.** `error_flag` is generated at an unknown point and *observed* when a Quality Check runs. Quality Checks are deferred to end of shift and burst in the last 45 minutes — so the "error rate last two hours" partly measures **when the organization inspected**, not **when the worker erred**. A worker whose shift ends before inspection is structurally assigned a lower error rate than one whose shift contains the inspection burst. Batching (Junior_H/I, 9–18 consecutive short Document Reviews) both deflates measured service time and compresses multiple judgments into one review window, so a single review lapse can be counted as one error or many depending on counting rules. Any performance metric must state its error-attribution rule explicitly (attributed to the generating event, the detecting event, or the case) and be robust across all three.

**Queue pressure is a management-created exposure.** WIP rises to 88 in the 76+ bin and Day-shift queue moves 38  75. That load is produced by demand forecasting, staffing ratios, arrival smoothing and automation policy. Under high WIP the organization gets 9.4–16.8 min service times *by trading away verification*; the 5.8–10.9% error rates are the **price of the throughput the organization is currently buying**. It is incoherent to celebrate the late-shift speed-up (10.8 min) and discipline the workers for the error rate that pays for it. Correct framing: the speed–accuracy trade-off point is set by workload policy, and the worker is implementing it.

**Automation quality must not be laundered into human error.** Bot_Risk returns instant Risk Classification and is corrected more often when `queue_length_at_start > 70`. Two very different failures are possible: the model degrades on the late-day exception load, or human verification degrades under pressure. Only the second is a worker-performance matter — and even then it is a workload matter. Counting bot corrections as human "errors" transfers an automation defect onto individuals. Bot-handled steps must be separately labelled in every metric, with a distinct `bot_correction_rate` and a separate accountability chain. Bot_Risk also removes easy work from human queues, so the human residual case mix hardens over time; automation savings should not be booked without re-measuring human case complexity.

**Over-adjustment risk.** `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` are partly mediators of staffing policy. "Controlling them away" can also hide the real causal effect of management decisions. Report both: the **total** effect of shift position (what actually happens) and the **direct** effect at fixed WIP (what the worker contributes). Presenting only one of these is itself a form of bias.

**Deferral and quality-check removal.** Suggesting that low-priority Quality Checks be dropped or indefinitely deferred to raise throughput converts a quality-control process into a throughput lever. The consequence is delayed detection, larger customer-exposure windows, and — as these data show — *recreated carryover complexity* for the next shift (bins 1–5 at 0.72 complexity), which then raises early-shift errors and invites further blame of the incoming worker. Quality checks are also the main independent source of defect data; weakening them degrades the evidence base on which all other conclusions rest.

**Fairness and voice.** Junior (12.4%) and Night staff (8.2%) carry the highest high-WIP error rates. Night staff also handle 56% high-complexity work and urgent exceptions with fewer events and less support. Penalizing these groups for the outcome of a workload and skill-matching problem punishes juniority and a scheduling assignment. Any metric used for personnel decisions must be risk-adjusted, volume-thresholded, uncertainty-quantified, and reviewed with workers; workers must be able to contest, annotate (e.g., "system outage", "batch requirement", "bot correction"), and see their own data. Finally, fine-grained sequence-level surveillance has dignity and privacy costs: dashboards should be aggregated and purpose-limited, not a per-event worker scoreboard.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Process-mining dashboard, refreshed daily, with a **global filter bar**: `shift_id`, `org:resource` / `exp_group`, `activity_family`, `concept:name`, `priority`, `complexity_band`, `wip_band`, clock-hour window, date range, and a toggle *raw vs risk-adjusted*. Every indicator below must render in both raw and complexity/WIP-standardized form, with sample-size and confidence-interval annotations.

(c) Errors are always shown as generation-position and detection-position. (d) The dashboard reports *system state* (WIP, queue, complexity mix, batching, automation health) with at least equal prominence to worker-level numbers — because the evidence says system state is the dominant driver. (e) Worker-level panels are aggregated and access-controlled; they are diagnostic for workload design and training, never a standalone input to discipline.
