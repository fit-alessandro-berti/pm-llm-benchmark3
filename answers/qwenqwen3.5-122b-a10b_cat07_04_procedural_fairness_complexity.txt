# 1. Fairness dimensions

To assess procedural fairness, we must look beyond raw approval rates and examine how the process treats different groups relative to their needs and the policy intent. The following dimensions are critical:

- **Outcome Parity:** Raw approval rates differ significantly (Group A: 78% vs. Group D: 58%). While raw parity is not expected due to differing risk profiles, large gaps persist even after adjustment, suggesting potential inequity in final decision-making.
- **Adjusted Outcome Disparity:** Even controlling for risk, documentation, and channel, Group B shows a -5.5 percentage point approval difference and Group D shows -3.1 percentage points. This indicates that case mix alone does not explain the outcome gap.
- **Procedural Burden:** Groups B and C face significantly more steps (variants V4 and V5) and handovers. Group B cases often route General  Bilingual  General, while Group C routes General  Specialist  Supervisor. This increases cognitive and administrative load on the applicant.
- **Timeliness:** Median cycle times are nearly double for Groups B (13.7d) and C (15.9d) compared to Group A (8.1d). Crucially, waiting times at specific support steps (Language Support: 3.8d; Accommodation Review: 4.7d) suggest resource bottlenecks rather than processing complexity.
- **Consistency of Routing:** Variants V1 (straight path) is dominant for Group A (42%) but rare for Groups B (19%) and D (18%). This suggests that access to the standard, efficient path is stratified by group characteristics.
- **Quality/Accessibility of Support:** While Language Support and Accommodation Review are rights-preserving, the data shows they function as bottlenecks. Only 7 bilingual caseworkers serve Group B, and 5 specialists serve Group C. Accessibility is legally mandated but operationally constrained.
- **Appeal/Rework Burden:** Group B has a 13% appeal rate and 22% rework rate (vs. 6% and 11% for Group A). Higher appeals may indicate lower confidence in the initial decision or dissatisfaction with the process experience.
- **Resource-Allocation Fairness:** Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers, while Group B relies on 7 bilingual staff. This concentration creates systemic delays for minority groups that are not present for the majority group.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish between legitimate complexity and unfair disparity, the following analysis strategy is proposed:

- **Matching or Stratification Variables:** Use propensity score matching to create comparable cohorts across groups based on `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, and `region`. This isolates the effect of `applicant_group` from observable case characteristics.
- **Path/Variant Comparison:** Compare cycle times and outcomes for identical variants (e.g., V1) across groups. If Group B takes longer than Group A on V1, the disparity is not due to the path itself but resource execution.
- **Resource-Assignment Comparison:** Analyze `org:resource` allocation. Calculate the average queue time per resource ID. If Group B cases wait longer on average for the *same* resource type as Group A, this indicates allocation bias or capacity mismatch.
- **Waiting-Time Decomposition:** Decompose total cycle time into `service_time` (processing) and `waiting_time` (queue). Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to isolate where delays occur (e.g., waiting for a bilingual staff member vs. processing the check).
- **Sensitivity Analysis for Possibly Biased Covariates:** Re-run adjusted models excluding `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`. If disparities increase, these variables may be encoding historical bias (e.g., risk scores penalizing Group D, documentation incompleteness penalizing Group B due to language).
- **Subgroup Checks by Region and Channel:** Stratify analysis by `region` and `channel`. If Group B disparities are concentrated in specific regions with fewer bilingual staff, this points to resource allocation rather than applicant behavior.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

The observed patterns can be generated by the following mechanisms, ranging from legitimate operational constraints to concerning systemic biases:

1.  **Resource Capacity Mismatch (Legitimate but Problematic):** The policy requires language and accommodation support, but the workforce (7 bilingual, 5 accommodation specialists) is insufficient for the volume (Group B: 1250 cases, Group C: 820 cases). This creates unavoidable waiting times that disproportionately affect these groups.
2.  **Proxy Discrimination via Documentation:** `documentation_completeness` is used as a routing trigger. Since language barriers often lead to incomplete forms, Group B is routed to "Request Missing Documents" more often. This mechanism penalizes applicants for a characteristic (language) that triggers a rights-preserving step, creating a double burden.
3.  **Historical Bias in Risk Scoring:** `risk_score_band` is used to route to Fraud Review. If the risk model was trained on historical data where Group D was over-policing or under-served, the risk score encodes historical disparity. This leads to Group D being routed to slower, more burdensome Fraud Review paths (V3) more frequently than warranted by actual fraud rates.
4.  **Supervisor Review Prioritization:** The data shows Group B has an 8% higher probability of Supervisor Review (adjusted). If supervisors prioritize Group A cases due to familiarity or implicit bias, Group B cases wait longer in the `Supervisor Review  Decision Draft` queue (1.9d vs 1.0d), extending cycle times and increasing frustration.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The current event log attributes are insufficient for a definitive fairness conclusion due to several measurement issues:

- **Measurement Bias in Covariates:** `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may not be neutral. Risk scores often correlate with demographic proxies, and documentation completeness often correlates with language proficiency. Controlling for these may inadvertently control for the protected attribute's impact (over-adjustment).
- **Missing Applicant Needs:** The log captures `disability_accommodation_requested` but not whether the accommodation was *effective*. A case may be marked "completed" even if the applicant struggled to understand the process, masking dignity violations.
- **Unobserved Resource Workload:** `org:resource` is recorded, but real-time workload (e.g., number of active cases per caseworker) is only observed at daily granularity. A bilingual caseworker might be 100% utilized while a generalist is 50%, causing the queue delays observed for Group B.
- **Appeal Quality:** `appeal_flag` indicates an appeal occurred but not the outcome (upheld/overturned). A high appeal rate for Group B could mean higher error rates in initial decisions or lower trust in the process, which are distinct fairness issues.
- **Historical Bias in Risk Scores:** There is no attribute indicating the source or validation of the `risk_score_band`. If the scoring algorithm reflects historical enforcement disparities, using it as a control variable validates the bias.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

To address identified disparities without removing necessary support:

- **Adding Capacity for Language Support or Accommodation Review:** Hire or train more bilingual staff and accommodation specialists.
    *   *Side Effect:* Increased operational costs. Risk of underutilization if demand fluctuates seasonally.
- **Service-Level Targets for Support Steps:** Define SLAs for `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` queues (e.g., max 24h wait).
    *   *Side Effect:* May incentivize rushing complex cases or transferring cases to generalists prematurely, risking quality.
- **Routing Redesign to Reduce Handovers:** Implement "case ownership" where a bilingual specialist owns the case from submission to decision if language support is needed, rather than handing back to a generalist.
    *   *Side Effect:* Reduces flexibility of generalist pool; requires higher skill levels for specialists.
- **Decision-Explanation Improvements:** Enhance `Decision Draft` templates to include plain-language explanations and specific reasons for denial, tailored to language preferences.
    *   *Side Effect:* Increases drafting time for caseworkers, potentially slowing initial decision speed.
- **Monitoring Adjusted Disparities:** Implement a dashboard tracking the adjusted outcome differences (e.g., -5.5pp for Group B) monthly.
    *   *Side Effect:* Requires ongoing data engineering resources to maintain the matching models.
- **Auditing Risk Score Band and Documentation Completeness:** Conduct periodic audits of the `risk_score_band` algorithm for demographic bias and review "incomplete documentation" flags for language-related patterns.
    *   *Side Effect:* May reveal need to retrain models, causing temporary disruption to routing logic.
