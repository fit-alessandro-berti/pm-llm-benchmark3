## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I treat the flag as part of an operational policy bundle, not as a directly manipulable variable. P0–P4 are policy contrasts. Where I discuss mediation, I use **interventional analogues** rather than natural direct/indirect effects, because natural effects require nested cross-world counterfactuals and are especially fragile when queues are shared and mediators affect later mediators.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P1 vs P0: suppress display of the flag, leaving risk score and human information unchanged | All arriving cases | Total cycle time, arrival  close | Total policy effect | Yes, as a policy contrast | Reviewers may infer the flag from risk score; no observed P1 period; display suppression can alter documentation |
| E2 | Priority-lane assignment on vs off for flagged cases | Flagged cases above the relevant risk threshold | Triage completion time, arrival  triage_done | Direct effect of the priority lane | Yes, if a lane override can be randomized | Lane assignment is currently determined by flag; no overlap in observed lane use; queue-state confounding |
| E3 | P2 vs P0: retain priority triage but remove automatic mandatory manual review | Flagged cases | Total cycle time | Interventional analogue of indirect effect through manual-review occurrence | Yes, as a policy contrast | Removing the automatic referral may change voluntary referral; queue mix and escalation also change |
| E4 | P3 vs P0: keep routing, add protected manual-review capacity for flagged cases | Flagged cases | Total cycle time | Interventional analogue of indirect effect through manual-review waiting | Yes, as a capacity contrast | Waiting time is not cleanly separable from service time; capacity changes may alter reviewer behavior |
| E5 | P4 vs P0: retain manual review, tighten escalation criteria or add senior capacity | Cases completing manual review | Total cycle time | Interventional analogue of indirect effect through escalation | Yes only after P4 is precisely specified | Escalation follows manual review; senior team is a shared scarce resource; quality effects are hard to measure |
| E6 | Natural indirect effect via manual-review occurrence | Flagged cases | Cycle time | Natural indirect effect | No | Requires no unmeasured exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding and no interference |
| E7 | P1 vs P0 | All arriving cases | Triage completion time | Total effect on triage stage | Yes | Flag suppression may leak; risk score remains visible |
| E8 | P1 vs P0 | All arriving cases | Administrative decision outcome, approve/reject | Total policy effect on decision outcome | Yes as an administrative outcome | True decision quality is unobserved; reviewers’ beliefs may affect the recorded outcome |

Time-ordered process-mechanism diagram:

```text
arrival -> Register -> triage_start -> triage_done -> manual_start -> manual_done -> escalation_start -> escalation_done -> close

Mechanisms in time order:

U_risk / risk_score / complexity -> flag -> priority_lane
priority_lane -> shorter triage_wait

flag -> automatic manual_review
manual_review -> manual_queue_wait -> manual_service

manual_service -> escalation
flag -> escalation criteria
escalation -> escalation_queue_wait -> senior_service

manual_queue_wait and escalation_queue_wait are shared across all cases.
Other cases' flags, reviews, and escalations feed these queues
and therefore affect the waiting times of every other case.
```

The shared queues create **interference**: one case’s manual review or escalation service time changes the waiting time of all later cases. **Exposure-induced confounding** arises because the flag causes manual review, and manual review then affects escalation; unmeasured risk may confound the manual-review–escalation relationship even if the flag itself were randomized. **Treatment-confounder feedback** occurs at the system level: queue state at time \(t\) is influenced by earlier cases’ treatments, and that queue state is a confounder for the current case’s manual queue wait.

## 3. Stage decomposition

Medians in minutes. Stage medians are calculated only over cases that actually execute the stage; absent stages are not treated as zero.

| subgroup | n | arrival-to-triage-start | triage service | triage-done-to-manual-start | manual service | manual-done-to-escalation-start | escalation service | residual-to-close | total cycle |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| flag 0 | 10 | 150 | 57 | 105 | 180 | 390 | 120 | 60 | 345 |
| flag 1 | 10 | 12 | 36 | 405 | 165 | 420 | 120 | 60 | 987 |
| complexity low | 4 | 138 | 48 | NA | NA | NA | NA | 60 | 267 |
| complexity medium | 8 | 24 | 45 | 270 | 120 | NA* | NA* | 60 | 483 |
| complexity high | 8 | 69 | 33 | 360 | 180 | 420 | 120 | 60 | 1167 |
| flagged high load | 6 | 12 | 33 | 420 | 165 | 420 | 120 | 60 | 1086 |
| flagged normal load | 4 | 15 | 36 | 270 | 150 | 450 | 120 | 60 | 735 |
| flagged with escalation | 6 | 12 | 30 | 450 | 180 | 420 | 120 | 60 | 1314 |
| flagged without escalation | 4 | 18 | 39 | 270 | 120 | NA | NA | 66 | 528 |

`NA` = stage structurally absent for the subgroup. `NA*` = only one case in the subgroup has that stage, so the median is not reported.

Residual-to-close is measured from the last completed stage: close minus escalation_done if escalation occurred; otherwise close minus manual_done if manual review occurred; otherwise close minus triage_done.

The contrast is clear: flagged cases reach triage much sooner and receive shorter triage service, but their downstream handoffs to manual review and escalation are much longer. This is consistent with acceleration at triage followed by queue-dominated delay. It is not automatically causal because flag assignment is correlated with risk score, complexity, arrival load, time of day, and resource assignment, and the manual/escalation queues are shared.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| flag  priority  triage timing | The priority lane preempts ordinary triage waiting | Flagged median arrival-to-triage-start is 12 min vs 150 min for unflagged | Lane assignment is exogenous conditional on load/time; no reverse causation | Flagged cases arrive during less congested periods; different triage resources are used | Randomize lane assignment for flagged cases | Unflagged waits increase; priority may be gamed |
| flag  manual-review occurrence | Automatic mandatory referral sends almost all flagged cases to manual review | 10/10 flagged cases have manual review vs 4/10 unflagged | Referral decision is separate from human judgment; manual_start marks true service start | Risk/complexity, not the flag, drives referral; the flag is just a proxy | P2: remove automatic referral; train staff | High-risk cases may be missed; workload falls |
| flag  manual queue | Flagged cases enter a shared manual queue and wait behind all other work | Flagged median triage_donemanual_start is 405 min vs 105 min for unflagged manual cases | Queue discipline is roughly FIFO; no unobserved batching or administrative handoff | Flagged cases are scheduled later because of complexity or reviewer availability | P3: protected manual-review capacity for flagged cases | Cost; protected slots may reduce capacity for unflagged cases |
| flag  escalation | Flagged cases are more likely to be escalated to the scarce senior team | 6/10 flagged escalate vs 2/10 unflagged; escalation waits are long | Escalation occurs after manual review; waiting time does not change the decision | High complexity or hidden risk, not the flag, drives escalation | P4: revise escalation criteria or add senior capacity | Senior team load changes; some complex cases may be under-escalated |
| complexity/risk confounding | Complexity and risk cause both flag assignment and slower processing | High-complexity cases appear in both flag groups; risk_score is high in flagged cases | Adjust for measured risk/complexity; no unmeasured risk confounder | Risk_score measurement error and unobserved risk drive the apparent flag effect | Collect richer case features; separate risk from complexity | Overadjustment if complexity is itself a mediator |
| shared-queue interference | One case’s manual review or escalation affects other cases’ waits | Manual and escalation queues are shared; high-load arrivals show long waits | SUTVA holds, or an explicit interference model is used | Time-of-day load, not the flag, drives queue congestion | Queue separation or randomized capacity blocks | Fairness and efficiency tradeoffs |
| reviewer/resource assignment | Different resources have different speeds or work schedules | Flagged cases are mostly assigned to T1/T2; unflagged to T3/T4 | Resource assignment is independent of risk conditional on measured variables | Senior resources are deliberately assigned to flagged/complex cases | Randomize resource assignment; account for resources | Possible quality loss if expertise matters |
| decision-quality tradeoffs | Faster triage and extra review may improve decision quality but add delay | Approve/reject rates are similar in this small sample; no ground truth | Recorded outcome reflects true decision quality | The flag changes documentation and reviewer expectations, not actual quality | Audit outcomes; use calibrated risk benchmarks | Over-rejection or under-rejection |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is the flag effect separate from a risk-score threshold? | Regression discontinuity or local randomization around the flag threshold, if a threshold exists | risk_score in a narrow band, complexity, arrival_load, calendar hour | Local continuity of potential outcomes; no precise manipulation of risk_score | Density test at the cutoff; covariate balance; placebo cutoffs | If density is discontinuous or covariates jump at the cutoff, the flag effect is not locally identified |
| Does queue load explain the long manual wait? | Multistate/queuing hazard model with time-varying queue length, recent arrivals, and available capacity | arrival_load, queue length, staffing, resource mix, hour | Conditional on queue state, case composition is as-if random | Compare model-predicted wait percentiles to observed waits; test residuals | If flag differences disappear after controlling for queue state, queueing is the dominant mechanism |
| What is the total policy effect? | Randomized encouragement: hide vs show the flag for eligible high-risk cases, while keeping risk_score visible | Randomization; stratify by complexity and load | No contamination; display rule is respected; no other policy changes | Check manual referral rates in the hidden-flag arm | If hidden-flag cases still get manual review/escalation at the same rate, the intervention did not change behavior |
| How much of the effect is mediated by manual review and escalation? | Interventional g-computation under simulated policies P2/P3/P4 | risk_score, complexity, arrival_load, queue state, resource | Sequential conditional independence; correct model specification; no unmeasured time-varying confounders | Cross-validate predicted stage transitions and times against holdout cases | If the process model predicts observed durations poorly, the mediated estimates are unreliable |
| Is the association spurious due to unobserved risk? | Negative-control outcome and placebo flag analysis: use triage service time as a negative-control outcome; also create a random placebo flag | Same adjustment set as main analysis | Negative control is unaffected by post-triage queues; placebo flag does not alter information | Compare observed flag effect on the negative control; examine placebo flag effects | If the negative control shows a flag effect, resource/severity confounding is present; if placebo flag affects outcomes, labeling effects matter |
| How sensitive is the conclusion to unobserved risk? | Sensitivity analysis using E-values or Rosenbaum-style bounds | Measured risk_score and complexity | Unobserved risk has bounded relationships with flag and outcome | Compute the strength of unobserved confounding required to nullify the effect | If a small unobserved confounder can explain the result, the causal interpretation is fragile |
| Does interference invalidate the estimates? | Cluster-randomized rollout by team/shift or use daily variation in flagged arrivals as an instrument for queue load | arrival_load, staffing, calendar, queue length | Interference occurs only through shared queue counts; cluster membership is stable | Estimate spillover from flagged volume to unflagged wait times | If spillover is substantial, individual-level direct/indirect effects may reverse |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Flagged cases triaged quickly; unflagged wait longer | Flagged cases wait long in manual queue; subset waits long for senior team | Long cycle for flagged; shorter for unflagged | Unknown; mandatory review gives some compliance | Flagged reviews crowd out unflagged manual/escalation | Observed data only; confounded | Keep as baseline; do not interpret as optimal |
| P1: suppress flag display | Loses priority lane; flagged triage wait may approach unflagged levels | Automatic manual-review triggers likely drop; queues may clear | Cycle time likely falls for flagged, but due to less review, not because of better design | High-risk cases may be under-reviewed; compliance failure | Frees manual capacity for other cases | Weak; no observed P1 data | Use only in a randomized study with audit safeguards |
| P2: priority triage, no automatic mandatory review | Same fast priority lane | Fewer flagged automatic reviews; manual queue less loaded; escalation may fall | Likely shorter cycle time for flagged | Missing a serious case if human judgment does not compensate | Relieves shared manual queue | Moderate; could be randomized | Pilot with a safety net and outcome audit |
| P3: keep routing, add protected manual-review capacity | Same | Flagged manual queue wait should drop; escalation unchanged unless upstream capacity changes | Shorter cycle time for flagged; low quality risk if capacity is additional | Lower; review still happens; less rushed review | Requires extra staff; may draw resources from other tasks if not protected | Strong; capacity can be randomized and measured | Best first pilot |
| P4: change escalation criteria/capacity | Same | Stricter criteria reduce escalation volume; extra senior capacity reduces escalation wait | Shorter if senior team is a bottleneck; worse if rejected escalations reenter | Risk of missing complex cases if criteria too strict; extra capacity may improve quality | Senior team is scarce; changes spill over to all escalated cases | Moderate; depends on precise rule | Pilot after P3; evaluate decision quality |

### Limited pilot: P3, protected manual-review capacity

I recommend a limited pilot of **P3**.

- **Eligibility:** Flagged cases whose manual review is mandatory and whose predicted manual queue wait exceeds a target threshold.
- **Randomization/assignment:** Randomize at the queue/team-day level. On protected days, two dedicated manual-review slots per hour are reserved for flagged cases; on control days, current routing remains unchanged.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** Flagged cases may use at most 40% of manual-review capacity. If suspected wait falls below 30 minutes, protected slots revert to general use.
- **Primary outcomes:** manual queue wait (triage_done  manual_start) and total cycle time.
- **Secondary outcomes:** escalation rate, decision outcome, reviewer workload, unflagged case waits.
- **Minimum process data:** complete timestamps, queue state at release, resource IDs, flag, risk score, complexity, arrival load, outcome.
- **Stopping conditions:** stop if flagged manual wait does not decrease with a lower confidence bound above zero; if unflagged wait increases more than 20%; if escalation rate changes unexpectedly; or if outcome audits show worse decision quality.

        "residual-to-close": median_min(sub["residual"]),
        "total cycle": median_min(sub["total_cycle"]),
    })

summary_df = pd.DataFrame(summary_rows)
print("\nStage summaries (median minutes; NA = structurally absent/unsupported):")
print(summary_df.fillna("NA").to_string(index=False))

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk.** If an unobserved component of true risk strongly predicts both the flag and slow downstream processing, then the apparent flag effect could reverse into a pure confounding story: the flag does not cause delay; it merely selects cases that would be delayed anyway.
2. **Queue interference.** If the long flagged waits are caused by systemic backlog created by earlier flagged cases, adding protected capacity for flagged cases may simply move the bottleneck elsewhere and could increase waits for unflagged cases.
3. **Decision quality.** If the manual review and escalation actually improve approval/rejection accuracy, then policies that shorten cycle time by removing review or escalation could harm decision quality; the recommendation would reverse.
4. **Timestamp semantics.** If `manual_start` or `escalation_start` denote when a case was pulled or passed to a worklist rather than when service begins, then the “queue wait” intervals are misattributed, and the waiting-time mechanism is not identified.
5. **Positivity/overlap.** If all high-risk cases are flagged and all low-risk cases are unflagged, there is no overlap in risk between flag groups; separating flag effects from risk effects requires extrapolation and is not credible.
6. **Reviewer behavior change.** If reviewers infer the flag from the risk score even when display is suppressed, P1 will not be a true “flag off” contrast; if adding protected capacity changes reviewer diligence, P3’s wait reductions may not generalize.

---

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed, the data show that flagged cases receive much faster triage than unflagged cases: median arrival-to-triage-start is 12 minutes versus 150 minutes. However, flagged cases are much more likely to receive manual review and escalation, and they experience far longer downstream waiting and service intervals. The total cycle time for flagged cases is therefore longer, not because triage is slow, but because the downstream queue and review stages dominate.

A plausible mechanism is that the flag policy bundle routes cases into a priority triage lane, then into a shared manual-review queue, and then sometimes into a scarce senior escalation team. The triage acceleration is real, but it is followed by queueing delays that more than offset it. The data are not sufficient to prove this mechanism causally: the flag is derived from a risk score, risk is imperfectly measured, complexity and arrival load are confounders, resources are not randomly assigned, and there is interference through shared queues. Natural direct and indirect effects are not identified in this setting.

The most useful next evidence would come from a limited pilot of P3: protected manual-review capacity for flagged cases. It preserves the review and escalation process, targets the largest observed delay, and can be randomized safely. The pilot should collect queue state at release, timestamps, resources, and outcome data so that subsequent analyses can separate waiting-time effects from service-time and decision-quality effects.
