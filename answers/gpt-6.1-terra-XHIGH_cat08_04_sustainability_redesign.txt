# 1. System boundary and assumptions

The decision boundary is the order-to-delivery process, including production and heat treatment where needed, inspection and rework, packaging, outbound transport, partial shipments, backorders, cancellations, and material that is produced but becomes obsolete. Include return transport and replacement production or repackaging when returns can be linked to the order. Assess customer downtime separately as a service and business consequence; do not assume that it can be converted reliably into environmental impact from this log.

Measure electricity in kWh, transport in kgCO2e, and scrap and packaging in kg. For a lifecycle comparison, supplement these with electricity factors by site and time, material and packaging production and disposal factors, carrier load and return-leg information, and end-of-life or recovery data where available. Avoid adding kWh, kg of material, and kgCO2e as if they were the same unit. Report a partial footprint when factors or downstream data are missing. Supplier manufacturing, upstream inventory production, warehousing, customer travel and use, and disposal are outside the directly observed log; bring material upstream and end-of-life impacts into a broader comparison if data become available. Do not claim a complete lifecycle assessment from the supplied attributes alone.

The unit of operational analysis is an order, but orders contain multiple line items and are flattened. Link line, item, batch, shipment, return and order IDs where possible; allocate shared batch energy and scrap to actual good items using a consistent documented rule, without charging a batch total to each order. Include unshipped and obsolete output in the denominator or allocate its burden to useful output. Compare both per fulfilled line or good item and per order, and report total impact and demand, so changed order splitting or volumes do not create a false improvement. Capture cancelled orders and their incurred work as well as completed orders.

The DFG is an aggregate of transitions, not a complete set of case paths or a flow-balanced ledger. For example, emergency and rework loops and some shipment counts do not reconcile, and 11,200 closes plus 530 cancellations do not account for all 12,000 starts. Open cases, extraction boundaries, missing events, flattening and mixed shipments may explain differences. Validate event completeness, timestamps, case/line linkage and the measurement window before estimating savings or compliance. The model's low precision means an inferred route is not proof that every observed combination is a normal or compliant path. The supplied associations and path averages are not causal estimates; segment, part complexity, urgency and disruption can confound comparisons.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Report distributions and totals by case_type, customer_type, promised_lead_time_hours, part and weight, machine_group, route_mode and disruption status; distinguish completed, cancelled and still-open cases. Use event timestamps to separate queue, production, transit and rework time. Baseline both the supplied segment summaries and the actual cohort to be changed.

- **Energy:** total and kWh per fulfilled good item and order, with CNC_A, CNC_B, heat_treat and inspection_cell separated where metering allows; include emergency production and rework loops. Track electricity-related CO2e separately when reliable factors exist.
- **Transport:** transport_kgco2e per delivered order and in total, by route_mode, lane and shipment count; include linked backorders, returns and van return legs where measured. Track air, dedicated-van and consolidated-truck shares and actual load factors when available.
- **Materials:** material_scrap_kg and package_material_kg per good item and order, and total; record packaging type, damage, returned goods, excess batch output and inventory not shipped within 14 days, then track eventual reuse, sale or disposal. The 6% figure is an early obsolescence signal, not proof that all such items are waste.
- **Rework and quality:** rework_flag and loop frequency and time for Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production and Package -> Rework packaging; distinguish production from packaging rework. Track damage-related returns, customer complaints, first-pass inspection yield and post-delivery failures, with sufficient follow-up for returns.
- **Batching:** batch_size and batch_id distributions, kWh per good item, Batch wait and heat-treatment wait, lateness, utilization and unshipped output by batch size, machine and segment. Pair batch-level totals with order due-date slack.
- **Service:** median and p90 order and line completion time, on-time delivery against the individual promise, cancellations due to delay, partial shipments, time to final line delivery and customer downtime where available. Follow open and delayed cases to completion to avoid excluding the worst outcomes.
- **Resilience and compliance:** successful alternative-route availability and use during carrier disruption for Service-contract customers, recovery time and disruption-specific late rates; SP1, SP2 and Q1 conformance and documented exceptions. Track forecast route reliability against actual outcomes and audit missing justification and certification events.

# 3. Intervention options

1. **S1 and S4: promise-aware routing with priority picking.** Target Standard-stock Service-contract orders on Pick from stock -> Package standard -> Ship air, especially the 22% of that segment flagged for SP1. At the availability check, predict end-to-end delivery probability for ground using pick, packing, cutoff and carrier-lane reliability, including uncertainty; prioritize picking when ground can meet the promise. Ground can avoid much of air's 4–6 times higher transport emissions. Retain air when ground cannot meet SP1's above-85% probability threshold or when service risk warrants it. Monitor route-specific predicted and actual on-time rates, cycle-time tails, picking queues displaced to other segments, transport_kgco2e and overrides. The 85% policy is a minimum routing condition, not a guarantee that the customer's contractual SLA is met.

2. **S5: scheduled consolidation.** For non-urgent stock orders with sufficient slack, feed Package standard -> Consolidated truck into twice-daily departures. Consolidation can reduce transport emissions by the observed 35–55% relative to individual ground parcels, conditional on comparable lanes and actual loading. The 12–24h wait can increase lateness and a missed departure can magnify it; dispatch individually when probability or service constraints fail. Monitor departure wait, load, actual route emissions, missed cutoffs, p90 and late rate by lane and segment, and the availability of a fallback carrier.

3. **S2: dynamic CNC and heat-treatment batching.** Target Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC and subsequent Heat treatment. Use due-date slack, predicted downstream queues and segment-specific maximum waits to decide when to release a batch; never wait solely to reach a target size. CNC_A energy per item falls from 2.8 to 1.3 kWh between size 1–3 and 12–20, while median wait rises from 4h to 38h. Heat batches above 10 are energy efficient, but heat-batch waiting is associated with 1.7 times the late risk. Cap release waits more tightly for service-contract and urgent orders, and validate heat-treatment quality requirements independently. Monitor batch_id, size, wait, kWh per good item, late rates, overtime, expedite_flag and surplus inventory by segment. The practical result may be smaller batches for urgent work and lower energy for flexible work, not a universal maximum batch.

4. **S3: route suitable work from CNC_B to CNC_A.** Target MTO -> CNC_B -> Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC, especially the 160h, 32%-late, 1.8 kg-scrap path. CNC_B's observed MTO rework rate is 18% versus 9% on CNC_A. For technically compatible part geometries, use CNC_A when its queue and remaining due-date slack support delivery, including heat treatment where required. Less rework can reduce energy and scrap; shifting work can also overload CNC_A or reduce machine diversity. Monitor mix-adjusted first-pass yield, scrap, energy, machine queues and p90/late performance on both machines. Verify capability and cause of defects before attributing the difference to the machine.

5. **S7: protect inspection capacity and add targeted quality gates.** Target Inspection -> Rework production loops for high-rework machine/part combinations and rushed inspection_cell queues. Reserve capacity, apply an appropriate inspection protocol before packaging, and feed defects back to machining. Better detection and prevention can avoid repeat CNC, packaging and returns; additional inspection consumes time and energy and can reveal defects that initially raise measured rework. Monitor queue duration, inspection energy, first-pass yield, defect type, downstream returns and eventual late rate, including false rejections and missed defects. Do not equate an initial increase in detected rework with worse final quality.

6. **S6: packaging and route compatibility at the packing decision.** Target Package standard/reinforced -> Rework packaging, observed 260 and 360 times respectively in the DFG, and prevent incompatible choices using part geometry, weight and route_mode. Apply Q1: MTO parts over 8 kg receive reinforced packaging unless inspection certifies low damage risk. Use the lighter option only when that condition is met. Reinforcement doubles package material but reduces damage-related returns by 40% for MTO heavy parts; account for avoided replacement and transport as well as packaging kg. Monitor rework_flag, package_material_kg, certification, damage returns and delivery times by part and route. Check whether the 40% estimate transfers to the affected mix.

7. **S8 and S9: classify urgency and provide shared emergency lanes.** At order receipt, distinguish verified Critical-downtime from precautionary urgency using customer-confirmed operational impact and promise. For verified downtime, retain emergency production and a dedicated van where needed; test reliable shared emergency courier lanes with appropriate capacity, pickup deadlines and disruption fallback. For precautionary cases, use stock or ground only when the promise and actual operational need allow. Shared loads and avoiding empty van returns can reduce transport emissions, but waiting for a shared lane or misclassifying a true outage can cause costly downtime. Monitor classification corrections, van justification, route capacity, empty return mileage, dispatch and delivery times, late rates and downtime by urgency category. Do not infer the critical stock/ground path's 9% late rate is acceptable for all critical orders.

8. **S10: negotiate flexible MTO promises.** For Regular MTO customers who can choose a later date, offer a credible date linked to a less urgent batch and ground shipment; preserve an expedited option when needed. More slack can reduce emergency production, air transport and energy per good item. Measure accepted versus offered dates, actual delivery against the agreed promise, Batch wait, batch energy, route choice, cancellations and surplus, and check whether customers switch to a competitor or place duplicate/earlier orders. Do not count a later promise itself as faster service or as evidence of emissions reduction.

9. **S11: line-aware availability and partial-shipment rules.** Target Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait, whose selected path has 79h median, 24% late and 7.2 kgCO2e per order. At availability check, estimate when all lines can ship and offer a combined shipment when it will satisfy the customer's need and promise; ship an urgent line separately when waiting would harm service. Improve stock accuracy and use demand evidence for frequently needed spares without blindly increasing inventory. Track line availability accuracy, number of shipments, final-line delivery, backorder wait, cancellations, transport and inventory aging. A lower shipment count is not a success if critical lines arrive too late.

10. **S12: structured exception decisions.** Add a timestamped decision event for SP1/SP2/Q1 with predicted route probability or downtime justification, part weight, certification where applicable, approver, reason, alternative considered and expiry/review. This exposes avoidable high-impact paths while preserving legitimate emergency and quality choices. It can slow urgent handling if designed as a blocking approval; provide a rapid emergency path and retrospective review where authorized. Monitor missing and late-recorded events, reason distributions, actual service, quality and transport outcomes, and audit whether exceptions reflect reality.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

- **Batching:** lower kWh per item can be offset by longer queues, late deliveries, overtime, air freight or emergency production. Cap waits using end-to-end slack, not machine utilization alone.
- **Obsolescence:** producing extra items to fill a batch may create the observed 14-day unshipped inventory and later disposal. Measure actual eventual disposition and allocate the production burden to fulfilled output.
- **Transport:** consolidating or switching air to ground may shift delay to the customer or provoke a subsequent express shipment; shared couriers may travel farther or return empty. Count all shipment legs and compare service outcomes.
- **Packaging:** reducing packaging mass can increase damage, returns and replacement production; stronger packaging can also be wasted on low-risk parts. Apply Q1 and compare material with actual damage and return outcomes.
- **Rework:** moving work to a seemingly efficient machine, rushing inspection or batching incompatible work can increase defects and hidden repeat machining, scrap and customer returns. Follow complete loops and delayed outcomes, not just first-pass energy.
- **Customer behavior:** later green promises may cause precautionary early orders, duplicate ordering, cancellations or supplier switching; separate actual demand changes from per-order efficiency and seek customer feedback.
- **Inventory and partial shipments:** holding stock or waiting for all lines can reduce trips but increase aged inventory or customer downtime. Measure both first critical-line and final-line delivery, plus inventory disposition.
- **Resource concentration:** loading CNC_A, a consolidated lane or an inspection gate can displace delays onto other customers and create a single point of failure. Track queues and preserve qualified alternatives.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Pilot an integrated decision at availability check and production planning: calculate credible end-to-end slack; prioritize eligible Standard-stock Service-contract picks for ground; consolidate flexible non-urgent stock orders only when the promised delivery remains likely; and release MTO CNC and heat batches under segment-specific wait caps. Route technically suitable MTO work to CNC_A when capacity and slack allow, while retaining CNC_B as a qualified alternative. Protect inspection capacity and use route/geometry packaging checks with Q1 certification. Coordinate the decisions so a batch or consolidation saving does not trigger downstream air freight or a missed promise.

Separately classify critical orders with customer input and trial shared emergency lanes only where their dispatch reliability and fallback support the actual downtime need. Keep dedicated vans and air available for genuine emergencies or insufficient slack, with SP2 justification. Offer flexible Regular MTO promises voluntarily, and combine partial shipments only when the customer's line-level need allows it. Use the exception log to expose avoidable overrides and learn from justified ones.

Prioritize the targeted stock air opportunity, packaging compatibility and rework reduction alongside a controlled batching pilot: the log flags substantial SP1 violations, 620 packaging-rework transitions from the two packaging states, and a particularly costly CNC_B rework path. These are leads for investigation, not proven savings. Maintain contract-specific service and quality baselines and carrier/machine alternatives; reject or revise an option if it improves footprint by creating unacceptable late deliveries, returns, downtime or disruption exposure.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

1. **Establish a trustworthy baseline.** Reconcile starts, closes, cancellations and open cases over a defined observation window; repair missing shipment, line, batch, return and decision links. Validate energy and emissions allocation and lane-specific reliability. Record pre-change segment and path distributions, contractual service levels, quality outcomes, disruption performance, total demand and environmental measures. Follow cohorts long enough to capture late deliveries, returns and inventory disposition.
2. **Run controlled pilots.** Where operationally feasible, randomize eligible comparable orders or lanes to the new decision versus current practice; otherwise use matched cohorts and pre/post comparisons adjusted for part mix, urgency, demand, season and disruption. Exclude genuinely ineligible emergencies from randomization, but monitor them. Test batching, routing and packaging interactions as a package as well as by intervention. Examine absolute totals and per-fulfilled-unit indicators; include cancelled work, extra shipments and returns. Report uncertainty and small samples rather than inferring savings from path averages.
3. **Set guardrails before rollout.** Agree with operations, customer service, quality and contract owners on segment-specific on-time and p90 limits, critical downtime response, damage/return and first-pass quality limits, and disruption recovery and alternative-route requirements. Use contractual obligations where applicable and existing performance as a baseline, not an assumption that it is adequate. Assign an owner to review guardrail breaches and revert or adjust the affected rule. Check Regular customers and lower-priority queues for displaced delays.
4. **Monitor conformance on complete decision data.** At each eligible routing decision, record the timestamped, calibrated probability of meeting the individual promise on ground and consolidated alternatives, available capacity and actual outcome. Test SP1 against the policy's above-85% condition and record why a higher-impact option was chosen; audit prediction calibration by segment and lane. Require explicit Critical-downtime justification for each dedicated van under SP2. For Q1, link each MTO line's weight, packaging and, where applicable, low-risk inspection certification. Report missing data separately from confirmed breaches, and investigate the existing 22% SP1 and 31% missing SP2 justification findings rather than treating them as causal proof of waste.
5. **Govern exceptions and resilience.** Give an operational owner authority to use an urgent exception without waiting for a slow approval, subject to prompt documentation and retrospective review. Review exception rates, reasons, outcomes and repeat patterns; audit Q1 overrides with quality staff. Regularly test that a genuinely usable alternative route remains available for Service-contract customers under R1, including carrier disruption scenarios, capacity and recovery time. Review models and wait caps as forecasts, carrier reliability, machine queues and demand change; expand only when environmental improvement and service, quality and resilience guardrails hold together.
