## 1. Stress scenarios

### S1 — Winter-wave mix shift (demand + acuity)
- **Demand pattern**: sustained 3–4 weeks at ~peak volume and mix, with further tilt toward high-acuity work (e.g., Urgent 30–35%, Complex-panel 20–25%).
- **Why plausible from log**: peak already shifts mix (Urgent 18%→29%, Complex 13%→20%) and increases rework (7.5%→11.8%) and long queues (cases with >12h queue: 22%→54%).
- **Operational consequence**: congestion simultaneously at pre-analytical (96% util, 9.5h median queue), assay (98% util, 18h queue), technical validation (101% util, 16h queue), and medical validation (22h queue), with a large rise in specialist-hold flow (Technical→Hold frequency share 1.8%→8.4%).

### S2 — Assay-machine degradation/outage (capacity + quality shock)
- **Disruption**: M1 down for 8–24h (maintenance/outage) and/or M2 failure rate rises (already implicated: repeat-after-failure clustered after night shifts and M2 usage).
- **Why plausible from log**: repeat loop intensifies in peak (Assay run→Repeat assay share ~6.9% normal to ~15.5% peak; Repeat→Assay median 10h→26h).
- **Operational consequence**: assay becomes the “drum” constraint; repeat loops amplify load, then downstream validators get hit by a release wave after recovery.

### S3 — Medical validator and/or specialist-review absence (human constraint shock)
- **Disruption**: 1–2 days reduced medical validation capacity (sickness) coinciding with the known specialist unavailability cadence (“every second Friday”), during high Complex-panel share.
- **Why plausible from log**: medical validation becomes much more common in peak (Technical→Medical share ~24.5% normal to ~47.2% peak) and specialist-hold waiting explodes (Hold→Medical median 18h→48h; specialist queue 36h peak).
- **Operational consequence**: WIP piles up between technical and medical validation (Technical→Medical median 24h peak), even if upstream steps are “sped up.”

### S4 — Courier arrival compression/delay (arrival variability shock)
- **Disruption**: snow/traffic compresses arrivals into one or two spikes (already at 10:00 and 17:00), or adds 6–12h delay to many samples.
- **Why plausible from log**: courier intake queue grows to 7.5h peak; Register→Receive slows (5h→8h median) and Receive→Pre-analytical slows sharply (2h→9h), consistent with intake/triage congestion.
- **Operational consequence**: intake and pre-analytical become first bottlenecks; delayed processing increases defects (recollection share ~4.2%→~8.0%), creating long recollection loops (60h peak) that consume clerk time (explaining errors) and re-inject load later.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1 — Winter-wave mix shift
**Likely bottleneck sequence**
1. **Pre-analytical check & accession correction**: utilization near 1 (96% / 91%) means any mix/arrival shift creates long queues; performance edges show Receive→Pre-analytical median 9h peak and Manual correction→Preparation 16h peak.
2. **Preparation & batching**: Preparation median queue 11h peak; batching behavior changes (Preparation→Batch wait share ~4.5%→~13.8%; Batch wait→Assay median 22h peak). Urgent bypass drops (74%→58%), so Urgent increasingly suffers the same batching waits it used to avoid.
3. **Assay machines + repeat loops**: Assay queue 18h peak plus increased repeats (repeat loop median 26h) consumes capacity twice for some cases (Repeat-after-failure).
4. **Validation bottlenecks**: Technical validators already overloaded (101% util, 16h queue; Assay→Technical median 15h). Then **medical validation** becomes dominant (22h queue; Technical→Medical median 24h). Specialist-hold cases add a further 48h median before medical validation.

**How “fixes” migrate the bottleneck**
- **If you only automate registration (I8)**: registration queue drops (2.8h peak), but arrivals to pre-analytical increase earlier in the day; pre-analytical (already 9.5h median queue) becomes the visible bottleneck sooner, and downstream assay/validation queues still dominate cycle time.
- **If you enforce strict Urgent-first dispatch (I1)**: Urgent improves short-term, but Routine/Complex starvation increases total WIP. More WIP arriving later to medical validation (already 22h queue) can *increase* total cycle time and create end-of-wave collapse (validators buried, urgent work still waiting on medical/specialist).
- **If you increase Routine batch size to 24 (I2) without guardrails**: preparation efficiency improves, but Batch wait and machine queues worsen for Urgent/Complex unless you separate streams; the data already show batching waits double/triple in peak.

### S2 — Assay degradation/outage
**Likely bottleneck sequence**
1. **Assay machines become the hard constraint**: with one machine down, WIP accumulates upstream (Preparation→Batch wait grows; Batch wait→Assay already 22h peak).
2. **Repeat loops amplify the constraint**: higher M2 failure pushes more Assay→Repeat events; each repeat consumes additional scarce assay slots and adds 26h median (peak) to the repeat cycle.
3. **Downstream “flood” after recovery**: once capacity returns, a surge of assay completions overloads technical validation (already 101% util), then medical validation (22h queue). This produces a second wave of delays even after the machine outage ends.

**How “fixes” migrate the bottleneck**
- **Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex (I3)** during degradation can protect high-acuity throughput, but if it forces most Routine onto M2 when M2 failure is high, repeats rise and the lab loses *net* assay capacity, which later overloads validators anyway.
- **Aggressively bypassing batches for Urgent (I1-like behavior)** fragments machine schedules; more changeovers/interruptions can reduce effective capacity and make the outage feel longer.

### S3 — Medical/specialist absence
**Likely bottleneck sequence**
1. **Medical validation becomes the primary queue**: even in peak without absence it has 22h median queue; removing capacity pushes it into multi-day backlog.
2. **Specialist review becomes the “serial gate”** for Complex/uncertain results: specialist queue is 36h peak and Hold→Medical median 48h peak; absence compounds both.
3. **Upstream stations may look “better”** (less crowded) only because they are blocked by downstream gating; cycle time worsens because cases wait between Technical→Medical (24h peak) and Hold→Medical (48h peak).

**How “fixes” migrate the bottleneck**
- **Cross-training technical validators into accession correction (I4)** may reduce the 14h accession queue, but it risks worsening technical validation (already 16h queue). Under a medical bottleneck, speeding accession/assay just increases WIP waiting for medical and makes SLA outcomes worse.
- **More batching/throughput upstream (I2)** is actively harmful here: it delivers larger bursts into an already constrained medical/specialist system.

### S4 — Courier compression/delay
**Likely bottleneck sequence**
1. **Courier intake and registration spike**: courier intake queue 7.5h peak indicates sensitivity to arrival spikes; Register→Receive median 8h peak further shows front-end delay.
2. **Pre-analytical becomes the first processing bottleneck**: Receive→Pre-analytical median 9h peak; pre-analytical queue 9.5h peak.
3. **Quality degradation increases rework**: delayed/handled samples raise defects; recollection share doubles (~8%) and each recollection loop costs ~60h peak, re-injecting work later and consuming clerk time for physician communication.
4. **Downstream bottlenecks reappear** once the compressed arrivals propagate: preparation batching and assay queues rise, then validators.

**How “fixes” migrate the bottleneck**
- **If you only add Urgent prioritization at prep/assay (I1)**: it does not fix the true first constraint (intake/pre-analytical) and can worsen fragmentation later in the day when late Urgent arrivals try to jump queues.
- **If you only smooth couriers (I7)** without early defect triage: defects are still discovered late (after pre-analytical congestion), so recollection loops still waste scarce downstream capacity.

## 3. Robust redesign package

### R1 — Make pre-analytical a fast quality gate (I6 + feedback loop)
- **What changes**: implement a **2-hour defect triage SLA** for newly received samples (especially Urgent/Complex) and standardize rejection reasons; trigger recollection immediately rather than after downstream waits.
- **Why robust**: rework grows in every stress condition (peak rework 11.8%; recollection share ~8%). Early rejection prevents wasting scarce preparation/assay/validator capacity on doomed cases and shortens the start of the 60h recollection loop.
- **Downstream overload risk**: more early recollection requests can temporarily increase clerk workload (physician calls) and courier traffic. Mitigation: scripted digital notifications + a “recollection concierge” window so clerks are not pulled from registration during spikes.
- **Assumptions**: defect criteria are clear; physicians can recollect quickly for Urgent; triage staffing covers arrival peaks.

### R2 — Level arrivals, not just processing (I7 + intake smoothing)
- **What changes**: renegotiate courier pickup/drop windows to **reduce the 10:00/17:00 spikes**, introduce staggered route cutoffs, and align internal staffing overlap to those windows.
- **Why robust**: variability drives queues when utilization is high (pre-analytical 96%, assay 98%). Reducing arrival compression directly attacks courier intake queue (7.5h peak) and the Receive→Pre-analytical delay (9h peak).
- **Downstream overload risk**: smoothing arrivals can move work earlier, exposing assay/validation bottlenecks sooner. Mitigation: pair with R3 (bottleneck-aware release) so earlier intake doesn’t simply inflate WIP.
- **Assumptions**: couriers accept slotting; lab can enforce cutoffs by case type (e.g., Urgent accepted later than Routine).

### R3 — Bottleneck-aware release + segmented dispatch (guarded version of I1/I2)
- **What changes**:
  - Treat **assay machines and medical validation as system constraints** and manage WIP before them (don’t “push” everything).
  - Use **three streams** with explicit capacity shares that adapt daily: Urgent, Complex-panel, Routine/Repeat.
  - Replace “strict Urgent-first” with **guardrails**: guarantee Urgent start within a short window, but preserve a minimum service rate for Complex and Routine to avoid starvation and WIP blow-ups.
  - Apply **separate batching rules**: micro-batches/short cadence for Urgent+Complex; larger batches for Routine when downstream is stable.
- **Why robust**: handles mix shifts (peak Urgent/Complex shares) without collapsing Routine/Complex; reduces the observed peak loss of Urgent bypass (74%→58%) by providing planned Urgent slots rather than ad hoc interruptions.
- **Downstream overload risk**: if you accelerate preparation too much, technical/medical validation queues (16h/22h) explode. The redesign therefore uses **release throttles** (cap WIP waiting for assay and waiting for medical) rather than only speeding upstream steps.
- **Assumptions**: reliable case-type labeling; real-time view of WIP/queues (even a simple board).

### R4 — Stabilize assay scheduling and reduce repeat amplification (quality-driven I3)
- **What changes**:
  - Implement **dynamic routing** between M1/M2 based on observed repeat rate and assay type risk, not a fixed “Routine to M2” rule.
  - Add a **night-shift/M2 quality sentinel** (calibration checks, second-person verification on borderline results) because repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 usage.
  - Keep a **small protected assay buffer for Urgent/Complex** so their bypass does not fragment the entire schedule.
- **Why robust**: repeat loops are a multiplicative load on the most constrained asset (assay capacity). Cutting repeats restores effective capacity without “adding machines.”
- **Downstream overload risk**: improving assay throughput will shift the bottleneck to technical/medical validation unless R3 is in place.
- **Assumptions**: repeat causes are measurable quickly (by machine/shift); rerouting is operationally feasible.

### R5 — Create a flex pool for accession correction without hollowing validation (thresholded I4)
- **What changes**: cross-train two technical validators for accession correction, but activate them **only under explicit thresholds** (e.g., accession queue high while technical queue manageable).
- **Why robust**: accession correction has only 2 specialists and reaches 14h median queue in peak; small relief unlocks many cases early and reduces downstream idle time caused by blocked orders.
- **Downstream overload risk**: stealing time from technical validation (already 101% util) can backfire. The flex pool must be *conditional* (Section 4) and reversible within the day.
- **Assumptions**: cross-trained staff maintain competence; queue metrics are visible.

### R6 — Automate registration selectively and pair it with downstream throttles (I8 + R3)
- **What changes**: auto-register orders with complete digital metadata, but couple it to **pre-analytical triage capacity and WIP caps** so the front-end doesn’t simply flood downstream.
- **Why robust**: reduces clerk queue (2.8h peak) and overtime-driven errors while preserving manual attention for incomplete/high-risk orders.
- **Downstream overload risk**: will expose pre-analytical/assay bottlenecks faster (explicitly hinted). Mitigation is the WIP-control in R3 and the fast quality gate in R1.
- **Assumptions**: digital completeness is strongly predictive of lower registration effort; exceptions are handled safely.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

### P1 — “Peak-mode validation” (activate I5 only when medical is the constraint)
- **Turn on when (any)**:
  - **Medical validation median queue > 8h** *or* **Technical→Medical median edge time > 16h** for 2 consecutive shifts.
  - **Urgent cases with projected slack < 4h** exceed 10% (slack-to-18h calculation using current WIP and median remaining step times).
- **What activates**: temporary evening medical-validation shift (remote if possible), plus a short daily specialist-review block reserved for Complex/urgent holds.
- **Turn off when**: medical median queue < 4h for 2 consecutive days and Urgent p90 < 20h.

### P2 — “Assay degradation/outage playbook” (machine shock)
- **Turn on when (any)**:
  - One machine unavailable > 2h, *or*
  - **Repeat rate (Assay→Repeat share) > 10%** and rising, *or*
  - **Repeat→Assay median wait > 18h** (approaching peak 26h).
- **What activates**:
  - Dynamic routing: protect Urgent/Complex on the more reliable machine; avoid pushing all Routine to a failing M2.
  - Temporarily **increase Routine batch sizes** (I2) *only for low-acuity lanes* to reduce setup waste, while keeping Urgent/Complex on short-cadence starts.
  - Throttle release from preparation (R3) to cap WIP before assay and prevent validator floods after recovery.
- **Turn off when**: repeat rate < 8% for 2 days and assay queue median < 8h.

### P3 — “Specialist scarcity” (absence / hold explosion)
- **Turn on when (any)**:
  - Specialist-review median queue > 18h (amber) or > 24h (red), *or*
  - Hold→Medical median edge time > 36h (approaching peak 48h), *or*
  - Scheduled specialist unavailability coincides with Complex-panel share > 18%.
- **What activates**:
  - Tighten criteria for Hold-for-specialist (require explicit trigger), and run a twice-daily “hold triage” so only true specialist cases wait.
  - Outsource Complex-panel overflow (I10) **only for non-urgent** complex panels or those already forecast to breach internal targets; account for 12h transport delay in due-date logic.
- **Turn off when**: specialist queue < 12h and hold volume returns to baseline share (<4% of technical validations).

### P4 — “Courier disruption” (compressed arrivals/delays)
- **Turn on when (any)**:
  - > 40% of daily arrivals occur within the single busiest hour, *or*
  - Courier intake median queue > 4h, *or*
  - Register→Receive median edge time > 10h (vs 8h peak baseline).
- **What activates**:
  - Emergency slotting: additional drop windows, reroute urgent pickups, and time-based cutoffs for Routine intake to protect Urgent/Complex.
  - Pre-analytical triage “first 2 hours” prioritization for Urgent/Complex, with immediate defect decision (R1) to avoid wasting assay capacity on compromised samples.
- **Turn off when**: arrival compression and intake queue return to green band for 2 days.

### P5 — “Urgent SLA rescue” (use strict Urgent-first only as a short-lived override)
- **Turn on when**: Urgent p50 > 16h or Urgent p90 > 24h, *and* the constraint is upstream of assay start (e.g., Urgent Batch wait median > 2h or assay queue rising).
- **What activates**: temporary Urgent-first dispatch at preparation/assay (I1) **with a hard time-box (e.g., 1 shift)** plus a minimum Complex/Routine throughput floor to avoid starvation.
- **Turn off when**: Urgent bypass rate returns > 70% and Urgent batch wait median < 1h.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Validation remains structurally fragile under high Complex share**: peak shows Technical→Medical 24h and Hold→Medical 48h medians; even with better flow, a simultaneous surge in Complex + specialist absence can still create multi-day backlogs.
- **Two-machine assay system is still outage-sensitive** at 98% peak utilization: any sustained loss of effective capacity (outage or repeats) can overwhelm the system; repeat suppression helps but does not eliminate single-point risk.
- **Recollection loops are inherently long (60h peak)** because they depend on external actors (physicians/patients). Early triage shortens wasted internal work but cannot fully protect SLA for those cases.
- **Policy complexity is itself a risk (redesign-caused vulnerability)**: segmented dispatch, dynamic routing, and thresholded flexing can fail if data are late/wrong or staff revert to ad hoc priorities; misclassification (marking Routine as Urgent) can recreate starvation and WIP explosions.
- **Outsourcing (when used) adds chain-of-custody and result harmonization risk**: transport delay is predictable (12h) but traceability errors or method differences can increase rework and client disputes.
- **Automation can “move errors downstream”**: I8 reduces clerk effort but can surface missing clinical context later, increasing medical/specialist queries unless completeness checks are strict.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

### Leading indicators (daily during peaks; act before SLA breaches)
- **Arrival variability**
  - **% of arrivals in busiest hour**: green < 25%, amber 25–40%, red > 40% (triggers P4).
  - **Courier intake median queue**: green < 2h, amber 2–4h, red > 4h.
- **Front-end quality**
  - **Recollection share (Pre-analytical→Request recollection)**: green < 5%, amber 5–7%, red > 7% (peak ~8%).
  - **Manual accession correction share** and **accession correction median queue**: green < 6h, amber 6–10h, red > 10h (peak 14h).
  - **Time-to-defect-decision** for new samples (R1): target < 2h for Urgent/Complex.
- **Batching and assay pressure**
  - **Preparation→Batch wait share**: green < 7%, amber 7–12%, red > 12% (peak ~13.8%).
  - **Batch wait→Assay median**: green < 10h, amber 10–16h, red > 16h (peak 22h).
  - **Assay repeat rate (Assay→Repeat share)** overall and by machine/shift: green < 8%, amber 8–10%, red > 10% (peak ~15.5%).
- **Validation queues (the usual “last mile” collapse)**
  - **Technical validation median queue**: green < 6h, amber 6–10h, red > 10h (peak 16h).
  - **Medical validation median queue**: green < 4h, amber 4–8h, red > 8h (peak 22h; triggers P1).
  - **Specialist review median queue**: green < 12h, amber 12–24h, red > 24h (peak 36h; triggers P3).
- **Class-specific flow health**
  - **Urgent bypass rate of Batch wait**: target > 70% (normal 74%, peak 58%); amber 60–70; red < 60 (triggers P5).
  - **WIP caps**: number of cases waiting-for-assay and waiting-for-medical; red when either exceeds a pre-set daily limit (set from staffing and median service rates).

### Lagging outcome metrics (track, but don’t steer with them alone)
- **Urgent SLA breach rate** (>18h) and **Urgent p90 cycle time** (peak 33h baseline).
- **Routine p90 cycle time** (peak 120h baseline) and **72h breach rate**.
- **Complex-panel p90 cycle time** (peak 210h baseline).
- **Overall rework rate** and **repeat-after-failure volume** by machine and shift.
