## 1. Control-Objective Decomposition

FEP-1 nominally requires that the human who performs Prepare_Payment and the human who performs Release_Payment be different, independent actors. The underlying control objective is broader than that rule. I decompose it into at least five threat scenarios and map the compensating controls against each.

**Threat T1 — Unilateral erroneous payment (honest mistake, wrong vendor, wrong amount, wrong account).**
A single person who both enters and releases a payment can press "send" on a transaction that no one else ever examined. The four-eyes split forces a second pair of eyes on the instruction at the release moment.
- AV-2 (preventive/detective): can block a duplicate invoice, an amount above the operator limit, a sanctioned vendor, and (for seven days) a recently changed bank account. It does not catch wrong-vendor errors when the vendor itself is valid, and it does not verify receipt of goods/services.
- MG-4 (preventive if before release, detective if after): an independent L3+ manager reviewing invoice, bank details, and supporting evidence before release prevents the error; after release it is detective only, with corrective value only if recall is still feasible.
- RA-7 (detective, partly corrective inside 24 h): retrospective audit catches the error; the bank-recall window determines whether audit is also corrective.
- ED-1 (permission with conditions): does not remove the four-eyes objective; it allows single-operator execution only if all seven conditions hold, and several of those conditions are themselves AV-2 and MG-4 tests.

**Threat T2 — Unilateral fraudulent payment by a single compromised operator (the operator chooses vendor, amount, and destination).**
- AV-2 (preventive): blocks sanctions hits, bank-account changes in the previous seven days, and amounts above the operator limit. A full pass is strong evidence only for the checks AV-2 actually performs; it does not establish bank-account ownership beyond what the system knows, does not detect a compromised account, and does not validate commercial reasonableness.
- MG-4 (preventive if timely and substantive): the strongest available deterrent against this threat when it precedes release, because an independent L3+ manager who is not the preparer's reporting line and who actually examines the file is hard to coerce or deceive.
- RA-7 (detective): if the operator acted alone, audit will find the transaction; recall is corrective only within roughly 24 hours.
- ED-1: does not address this threat on its own; the bank-change and MG-4 preconditions are the only relevant protections, and the operator-limit and 180-day-vendor conditions narrow the blast radius.

**Threat T3 — Collusion between two accounts that individually look legitimate (preparer and releaser are different people but act in concert, or "rubber-stamp" pairs).**
- AV-2: blind to collusion. A clean pass says nothing about whether the two humans are independent.
- MG-4: weak if the second human is itself the colluding partner, or if the manager is captured or merely a rubber stamp. MG-4 is only as strong as the manager's independence and the substance of their review.
- RA-7: the only compensating control that has any chance of detecting collusion, through behavioral analytics, repeated-pair detection, and shared-workstation signals — and even then only after the fact.
- ED-1: irrelevant; the nominal rule is already satisfied.

**Threat T4 — Manager captured or conflicted (manager is the colluding party, has a personal interest, or sets the operator's performance rating).**
- AV-2: not applicable; the control gate is before the manager.
- MG-4: fails outright. A manager who is the operator's direct supervisor, who shares an override credential with the operator, or who otherwise has a conflict cannot provide independence, even if the activity is logged.
- RA-7: the only realistic backstop, and again only detective; it is corrective only inside the recall window.

**Threat T5 — Process or system unavailability that forces degraded processing (AV-2 down, emergency token misused, no second human available, late manager).**
- AV-2: cannot function when the service is down; degraded operation must be treated as AV-2-not-performed, not AV-2-passed.
- MG-4: timeliness matters; approval after settlement is detective, not preventive. Approval using an override credential issued to a different person is an independence failure.
- RA-7: becomes the primary control and must run within the recall window to have any corrective value.
- ED-1: the seven-condition checklist is exactly designed to discipline this scenario, but the conditions are conjunctive; failure of any one demotes ED-1 from a permission to a policy violation.

**Threat T6 (extension) — Irrecoverable loss once settlement occurs.**
- None of AV-2, MG-4, RA-7, or ED-1 can correct after irrevocable settlement. Recoverability is a property of the timing of the controls, not of any single control's name. This is why timing must be evaluated against the bank-recall window and not just against "did the activity occur."

## 2. Equivalence and Sufficiency Test

I treat compensation as a structured test along seven dimensions, not as a checklist of activity names.

1. **Timing.** A control that runs after irrevocable settlement is at best detective; only controls that run before settlement (and ideally before release) carry preventive weight. Manager action after Release_Payment but before Bank_Settlement is hybrid: detective with respect to release, preventive with respect to recall, and corrective only insofar as it triggers a recall within 24 hours.

2. **Independence.** The actor performing the compensating control must be independent of the preparer, the releaser, and any party with a direct conflict of interest. Sharing a workstation, sharing an override credential, or being in a direct reporting relationship defeats independence even when activity is logged.

3. **Coverage.** The compensating control must address the specific failure mode the four-eyes split would have addressed. A clean AV-2 pass does not cover receipt of goods, commercial reasonableness, vendor legitimacy beyond sanctions, or collusion.

4. **Overrideability.** If a warning can be overridden by the same actor who is being controlled, the control is weakened. An override exercised by a person other than the controlled actor, and recorded with substantive justification, is acceptable; an override by the operator on their own transaction is not.

5. **Recoverability.** Detective controls only become corrective inside the recall window. I treat roughly 24 hours from settlement as the practical recall horizon; outside that, audit is detective with limited corrective value.

6. **Evidence quality.** A "manager approved" entry without evidence that invoice, bank details, and receipt were actually examined is a weak signal. The same applies to an audit entry without a substantive review trail.

7. **Collusion resistance.** The package must contain at least one control that does not depend on the two humans involved in the payment. AV-2 and RA-7 provide that independence; MG-4 only does so if the manager is genuinely independent.

I deliberately do not define equivalence as "same expected loss." Two payment processes can have the same expected monetary loss while having very different control-objective profiles — for example, a low-amount same-user case with AV-2 and a true four-eyes high-amount case with no controls. The control objective is about whether the process can be trusted to prevent, detect, and correct the threat it was designed to address, not only about whether the residual loss number looks similar. Conversely, a nonconforming nominal process can still meet the control objective if the compensating package is genuinely equivalent on all seven dimensions; the task is to judge that equivalence, not to count activities.
