# 1. System boundary and assumptions

**Decision boundary.** Use an operational, order-to-delivery boundary, extended where necessary to prevent burden shifting:

- From `Receive order` through delivery, cancellation, or closure.
- All production, heat-treatment, inspection, packaging, rework, and emergency-production events.
- All shipment legs, including partial shipments, backorders, replacement shipments, and empty dedicated-van returns where data can be obtained.
- Logged electricity use, production scrap, discarded packaging, and transport emissions.
- Batch output produced but not shipped within 14 days, as an obsolescence indicator.
- Returns, replacement production, and return transport should be linked back to the original order even when they occur after `Close`.

The primary functional units should be **per fulfilled order** and, after improving the log, **per good line item delivered**. Per-order indicators alone can distort comparisons because orders contain multiple lines and partial shipments.

**Lifecycle extensions.** Upstream burdens of raw material, packaging production, and replacement parts should be estimated when suitable factors are available. A cancellation is not assumed to have zero impact: the customer may obtain the part elsewhere. Likewise, a defect found after closure must not disappear from the assessment.

**Currently outside or incompletely measured.**

- Use-phase and end-of-life impacts of the spare part.
- Embodied impacts of machines, buildings, and carrier infrastructure.
- Facility energy not allocated to events, such as heating or energy consumed while orders wait.
- Electricity carbon intensity, material-specific embodied impacts, carrier load factors, and empty returns except where added later.
- The economic and wider environmental consequences of customer downtime.

**Important uncertainties.**

- The flattened order log cannot reliably allocate energy, scrap, packaging, or shipment legs to individual lines.
- It must be confirmed whether environmental values are event-level increments or order-level values repeated across events.
- Selected path comparisons are observational. Product weight, geometry, urgency, and disruption conditions can confound apparent machine or route effects.
- The low precision of the discovered model means unusual combinations of emergency production, partial shipment, and transport should not automatically be classified as nonconforming.
- Energy, kgCO2e, scrap, packaging, service, and resilience should remain separate criteria rather than being collapsed into an unsupported single score.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

All indicators should be reported by `case_type`, `customer_type`, path, machine group, route mode, and disruption status, with medians, p90 values, and totals to avoid hiding tail deterioration.

| Dimension | Indicators grounded in the log |
|---|---|
| **Energy** | Total `energy_kwh` per fulfilled order and per good line item; energy by `machine_group`; energy added by emergency production and inspection-to-rework loops; batch energy divided by good items actually shipped rather than nominal `batch_size`. |
| **Transport** | Sum of `transport_kgco2e` across every shipment leg; route-mode mix; emissions from partial and replacement shipments; air-to-ground and parcel-to-consolidated conversion rates. Add load factor and empty-return distance for dedicated vans and shared lanes. |
| **Policy-efficient transport** | SP1-eligible rate: proportion for which ground or consolidated truck has predicted on-time probability above 85%; avoidable high-carbon-route rate among those eligible; calibration of the on-time probability by segment. |
| **Materials** | `material_scrap_kg` per good item; `package_material_kg` including discarded packaging attempts; packaging material by part weight/geometry and route; percentage of produced items not shipped within 14 days. |
| **Rework and hidden burden** | Production first-pass yield; `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production` loop frequency and its incremental energy/scrap; packaging-rework frequency and discarded package mass; return, replacement, and damage rates after delivery. |
| **Batching** | `batch_size`, batch-wait duration, kWh per shipped item, heat-treatment wait, release-at-cap rate, late-delivery rate by batch size, and 14-day nonshipment/obsolescence rate. |
| **Service** | Median and p90 order cycle time; on-time delivery against the accepted promise; time to first critical line and time to complete order; partial-shipment, backorder, expedite, and cancellation rates. Preserve the original requested date when promises are renegotiated. |
| **Quality** | Rework and return rates by machine/part combination; rushed-inspection rate; packaging damage rate; Q1 compliance for made-to-order parts over 8 kg; low-damage-risk certification outcomes. |
| **Resilience** | Percentage of Service-contract orders with a qualified alternative route available; route/carrier concentration; service performance during disruptions; justified versus unjustified dedicated-van use; time to switch carriers or modes. |
| **Combined outcome control** | Environmental burden per fulfilled demand, including cancellations followed by reorder, rather than only per closed order. Track environmental gains alongside segment-specific service and quality guardrails. |

# 3. Intervention options

| Option | Target path and environmental mechanism | Service, quality, and resilience handling | Process-mining evidence required |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Slack-aware shipping and priority picking — S1/S4** | Target `Stock -> Package standard -> Ship air`, especially Standard-stock Service-contract orders. Choose ground when its calibrated on-time probability exceeds 85%, and use priority picking to recover warehouse time. This addresses the 22% SP1 violation rate without treating every air shipment as avoidable. | Retain air when slack is insufficient, ground reliability is degraded, or a justified critical condition exists. Do not remove air as an R1 alternative route. | Store decision time, available modes, predicted on-time probability, model version, remaining slack, recommendation, selected route, `expedite_flag`, override reason, actual delivery, and transport kgCO2e. Monitor calibration and lateness for the converted cohort. |
| **2. Twice-daily consolidation — S5** | Move eligible non-urgent stock orders from individual ground parcels to the existing consolidated-truck path. The evidence indicates 35–55% lower transport emissions for eligible shipments. | Only admit orders with at least the 12–24 hour consolidation wait plus a reliability buffer. Use tighter rules for Service-contract customers and bypass consolidation during carrier disruption. | Measure arrival-to-cutoff time, consolidation wait, load/utilization, missed departure, actual route, promise slack, late delivery, and fallback to air or parcel. Compare with eligible parcel-ground controls. |
| **3. Due-date-capped dynamic batching — S2** | Replace fixed or “largest possible” batches on `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC/heat treatment` with batches optimized for compatible demand, energy, and due-date slack. Release underfilled batches when a segment-specific wait cap is reached, and avoid speculative overproduction. | This captures CNC_A and heat-treatment efficiency without accepting unlimited waits. Service-contract and true-downtime orders need shorter caps; Regular MTO orders can use longer caps only after an accepted promise. | Link `batch_id`, good output, actual `batch_size`, batch-wait duration, machine, heat-treatment wait, energy, due-date slack, late delivery, expedite after waiting, and output not shipped within 14 days. |
| **4. Conditional CNC_A routing — S3** | Route Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A when A has sufficient capacity and due-date slack. The observed MTO rework rate is 9% on A versus 18% on B, and the CNC_B rework path has high energy and scrap. | Do not make CNC_A a single point of failure. Keep CNC_B qualified for urgent work, A outages, and products for which B has demonstrated quality. Account for A queue growth before rerouting. | Compare like-for-like machine/part combinations using queue length, slack at routing, batch size, energy, scrap, inspection outcome, rework loops, and lateness. Use matched or randomized routing where operationally safe. |
| **5. Reserved inspection quality gate — S7** | For high-rework machine/part combinations, reserve inspection capacity when production is scheduled and require a completed result before packaging. This targets the 470 `Inspection -> Rework production` transitions and the loops caused by rushed inspections. | A gate without inspection capacity could merely create a new bottleneck. Use risk-based inspection, adequate staffing, and escalation when the queue threatens the promise. | Capture inspection start/end, queue duration, inspection intensity, defect result, rushed/override status, machine/part, later rework or return, and incremental energy/scrap. Monitor whether defects move outside the logged process rather than disappear. |
| **6. Packaging-route compatibility and risk-based packaging — S6** | Add route reservation and part-geometry/weight checks before the packaging event. Prevent incompatible packaging that currently causes `Package -> Rework packaging` loops. Right-size low-risk parts while maintaining reinforced protection for heavy MTO parts. | Enforce Q1: parts over 8 kg retain reinforced packaging unless inspection certifies low damage risk. Reinforced packaging doubles material but reduces damage-related returns by 40%, so indiscriminate lightweighting would be counterproductive. | Link package type, package material, discarded package, part weight/geometry, planned and actual `route_mode`, Q1 certificate, packaging rework, damage, return, and replacement shipment. |
| **7. Conditional suppression of partial shipments — S11** | At `Availability check`, hold an order for complete shipment when all lines can be available within the remaining slack. This avoids the second package and shipment associated with the 1,010 partial-shipment cases and the 7% path with 79-hour median cycle time and 24% lateness. | Retain partial shipment when an available line prevents real downtime, when the missing line is highly uncertain, or when the customer accepts the split. A blanket ban could worsen service and resilience. | An object-centric line-item log is required: line availability dates, customer consent, first-line and complete-order delivery, number of packages/legs, emissions, cancellation, and reason for partial shipment. |
| **8. Critical-downtime triage — S9** | At receipt, distinguish verified current downtime from precautionary urgent demand. Reserve `Emergency production` and the fastest transport for verified downtime; process precautionary cases through priority stock, bounded production, ground, or shared emergency lanes when feasible. | Misclassification has a high service consequence. Require customer/service-desk confirmation, allow rapid escalation, and audit any commercial incentive to label orders critical. | Add urgency category, evidence of downtime, approval, decision time, escalation, actual route, production path, customer downtime outcome, and delivery performance. Compare true and precautionary subsegments separately. |
| **9. Shared emergency courier lanes — S8** | Replace suitable dedicated-van trips with pooled emergency lanes, backhauls, or shared couriers. This targets the high-emission `Critical -> Emergency production -> Dedicated van` path and empty returns. | Keep dedicated vans as a justified fallback when pooling cannot meet the true-downtime promise. Maintain more than one qualified carrier or route under R1. | Add vehicle/load identifiers, departure wait, stops, occupancy, empty-return distance, alternative-route availability, quoted and actual arrival, critical justification, and failed pooling attempts. |
| **10. Carbon-aware promise negotiation — S10** | Offer Regular MTO customers a realistic low-impact window that enables an efficient CNC/heat batch and ground or consolidated transport, alongside a faster higher-impact option. | Participation should be explicit rather than achieved by silently extending promises. Preserve the requested date so improved on-time performance cannot be created merely by relaxing the denominator. | Record original requested date, options shown, predicted path/impact band, accepted promise, actual path, batch size, transport mode, changes, cancellation, and later expedite. Monitor order-size and ordering-frequency changes. |
| **11. Sustainability exception log — S12** | Add auditable decision events for SP1, SP2, and Q1 exceptions. This has little direct environmental effect but enables the other interventions and distinguishes legitimate air/van/reinforced-pack choices from avoidable ones. | Use controlled reason codes, accountable approval, model confidence, and disruption status. Do not create a slow approval step for true downtime; emergency approval can be rapid but must not be retroactively fabricated. | Conformance checking of recommendation, decision, justification, approval, and route/package execution. Track exception frequency, reason, approver, segment, outcome, and repeated overrides. |
| **12. Selective critical-stock trial** | For a small number of repeatedly demanded, stable true-downtime parts, test strategic stock that can replace emergency production and dedicated transport with `Pick from stock` and a lower-impact feasible route. | Apply only where avoided emergency burden exceeds inventory and obsolescence risk. Do not create large speculative batches; preserve geographic and supplier diversity. | SKU-level demand stability, stock age, service criticality, replenishment batch, items not shipped within 14 days, emergency orders avoided, route used, write-offs, and disruption performance. |

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

| Risk | How the burden can move | Required guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| **Batch-efficiency rebound** | Larger batches reduce kWh/item but increase the observed batch wait toward 38 hours. Late orders may then require immediate CNC production, air transport, or emergency couriers, erasing the saving. | Use due-date caps and count subsequent `expedite_flag`, air, and emergency events against the batching decision. |
| **Batch obsolescence** | Energy intensity looks favorable if divided by nominal batch size even though 6% of batch-produced items are not shipped within 14 days when forecasts are wrong. | Divide by good items actually shipped, prohibit unnecessary speculative output, and include write-offs and inventory age. |
| **Transport-mode displacement** | A blanket air restriction can create multiple ground parcels, partial shipments, dedicated vans, or last-minute air after a missed consolidation departure. | Sum every shipment leg and evaluate the full path, not only the initially selected route. |
| **Consolidation service rebound** | The 12–24 hour consolidation wait may cause lateness, cancellation, or external customer sourcing. A cancellation can make the distributor’s footprint appear lower while moving impact to another supplier. | Restrict admission by calibrated slack, retain parcel fallback, and follow cancellations and reorders where possible. |
| **Packaging burden shift** | Lightweighting reduces package material but can increase damage, return transport, replacement production, and customer downtime. Conversely, universal reinforced packaging doubles package material unnecessarily. | Apply Q1 and geometry-based risk assessment; compare total packaging plus return/replacement burden. |
| **Rework displaced beyond closure** | A faster or less thorough inspection can reduce logged rework while defects are found during packaging or by the customer. The impact has not disappeared; it has moved outside the case. | Link returns and replacement cases to original production and retain a post-delivery observation window. |
| **Inspection bottleneck rebound** | Additional quality gates can lengthen the inspection queue, causing rushed inspections, late delivery, or rescue air shipments. | Reserve inspection capacity with production schedules and monitor queue time, overrides, and downstream expedites. |
| **Machine concentration** | Moving work to lower-rework CNC_A may overload it or reduce resilience to an A outage, pushing urgent work back to CNC_B or emergency production. | Apply queue-aware routing and keep CNC_B as a qualified alternative with targeted quality improvement. |
| **Critical-route displacement** | Shared emergency lanes reduce empty travel but can add detours. Incorrectly downgrading a true-downtime case can create large service and economic harm. | Retain dedicated fallback, rapid escalation, and explicit verified-downtime evidence. |
| **Customer-behavior rebound** | Customers offered greener dates may order earlier or in larger quantities, creating inventory and obsolescence at either the distributor or customer. Others may select a long promise and later request an expedite. | Monitor order frequency, quantity, changes, cancellations, and expedite-after-negotiation behavior. |
| **Inventory displacement** | Strategic stock converts visible emergency transport into upstream production, warehousing, and obsolete inventory. | Limit trials to stable demand and evaluate stock age, write-offs, replenishment energy, and avoided emergencies together. |
| **Metric gaming** | Service can appear improved by extending promises, and environmental intensity can appear improved by excluding cancellations, rework, or unshipped output. | Preserve original requested dates and report outcomes per fulfilled demand, alongside absolute totals and exception rates. |

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Adopt a **constrained optimization design** rather than a universal low-carbon rule:

1. **Apply hard feasibility constraints first.**
   - Q1 packaging protection for heavy MTO parts.
   - R1 alternative-route availability for Service-contract customers.
   - Verified true-downtime service requirements.
   - Machine, inspection, and carrier capability.

2. **Protect service using calibrated slack.**
   - Apply SP1 only where ground or consolidation has more than an 85% probability of meeting the promise.
   - Keep air and dedicated vans for insufficient slack, verified downtime, and disruptions.
   - Do not decommission those capabilities merely because their average emissions are high.

3. **Among feasible alternatives, minimize expected end-to-end burden.**
   - Include production energy, all transport legs, scrap, packaging attempts, expected rework/returns, and obsolescence.
   - Do not optimize energy per nominal batch item or emissions per shipment in isolation.

The recommended package is:

- **First, remove avoidable quality loss:** packaging-route compatibility, Q1 enforcement, reserved inspection capacity, and conditional CNC_A routing. This addresses the MTO energy and scrap hot spot while also improving quality. Inspection capacity must be added or rescheduled so the quality gate does not become another queue.
- **Second, introduce slack-aware logistics:** priority pick plus ground for eligible Standard-stock Service-contract orders, twice-daily consolidation for genuinely non-urgent stock orders, and conditional—not blanket—suppression of partial shipments.
- **Third, use dynamic production batching:** cap CNC and heat-treatment waiting by segment and due-date slack, release underfilled batches at the cap, and avoid forecast-driven excess production. Regular MTO customers can voluntarily accept a wider low-impact promise window.
- **Fourth, redesign critical fulfillment:** separate true downtime from precautionary urgency, use shared emergency lanes where they can meet the verified need, and retain justified dedicated vans. Selective critical stock should remain a narrow pilot because of obsolescence risk.
- **Finally, make exceptions visible:** add the SP1/SP2/Q1 decision and justification events before scaling route automation.

This package does not select ground, consolidation, large batches, or standard packaging merely because each can be locally greener. It uses those options only when their expected end-to-end impact is lower **and** service, quality, and resilience constraints remain satisfied.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

### Log and data improvements

Move toward an object-centric log linking orders, line items, batches, packages, shipment legs, vehicles, returns, and replacements. Add:

- Part/SKU, weight, geometry, quantity, and criticality.
- Original requested date, offered dates, and accepted promise.
- Route alternatives, predicted on-time probability, model version, and decision slack.
- Package identifier, package attempt, discarded material, and Q1 certificate.
- Vehicle/load identifier, carrier, utilization, empty-return distance, and disruption status.
- Inspection result, queue duration, rushed/override status, and downstream return cause.
- Batch good output, items shipped, inventory age, and write-offs.
- Explicit exception reason, approver, timestamp, and expiry.

Validate whether `energy_kwh`, scrap, package material, and transport values are incremental or duplicated before aggregation.

### Pilot design

- Establish pre-intervention baselines by segment, path, machine/part, route, weight, and disruption status.
- Use randomized assignment within safe eligibility bands where possible—for example, eligible ground versus usual route or alternative batch caps.
- Where randomization is unsafe, use matched controls or phased/stepped-wedge implementation. Control for due-date slack, weight, geometry, queue length, urgency, and disruption.
- Analyze both **intention to treat** and actual conformance. Otherwise operators who override difficult cases can make the intervention appear better than it is.
- Do not infer causal effects from raw CNC_A/CNC_B or route averages alone.

### Service, quality, and resilience guardrails

Track median, p90, late-delivery, cancellation, and rework against the current segment baselines:

- Standard-stock Regular: 7% late, p90 62h.
- Standard-stock Service-contract: 5% late, p90 41h.
- MTO Regular: 18% late, p90 180h.
- MTO Service-contract: 13% late, p90 156h.
- Critical-downtime Service-contract: 4% late, p90 30h.

For negotiated MTO promises, report performance against both the original requested date and the accepted date. For partial shipments, report time to first required line and complete-order delivery separately.

Before the pilot, service owners should specify acceptable non-inferiority margins and immediate-stop conditions. True-downtime cases should have stricter stopping rules than Regular orders. Quality gates should cover first-pass yield, packaging rework, damage returns, replacement production, and defects found after `Close`.

For resilience, monitor alternative-route availability and periodically test carrier switching. A lower-emission primary route does not satisfy R1 if the alternative exists only on paper.

### Conformance monitoring

- **SP1:** At the route decision, check whether ground or consolidation had a predicted on-time probability above 85%. A higher-carbon choice requires a contemporaneous approved exception. Monitor prediction calibration as well as apparent violations.
- **SP2:** Every dedicated-van dispatch must be preceded by a verified Critical-downtime justification or disruption exception. The current 31% missing-justification rate should be treated as a data and governance defect, not proof that every trip was unnecessary.
- **Q1:** Every MTO part over 8 kg must have reinforced packaging or a prior low-damage-risk inspection certificate. Link violations to packaging rework, returns, and replacement shipments.
- Use conformance checking against the intended decision rules, but manually review unusual emergency/partial-shipment combinations because the discovered model currently has low precision.

### Environmental outcome validation

For each intervention, compare:

- Total and per-fulfilled-demand energy.
- Total transport kgCO2e across all legs.
- Scrap, packaging attempts, and obsolescence.
- Incremental burden from production rework, packaging rework, returns, and replacements.
- Absolute totals as well as intensity, so volume rebound is visible.

Use sensitivity ranges for unmeasured electricity, material, package, and empty-return factors rather than reporting false precision.

### Exception governance and rollout

- Use controlled exception reasons: verified downtime, insufficient slack, carrier disruption, quality protection, alternate-route failure, and model uncertainty.
- Require pre-dispatch approval where practical, with a rapid emergency path for true downtime.
- Review recurring overrides by segment, operator, carrier, and part. Repeated exceptions indicate a flawed rule, promise, inventory policy, or model—not merely operator noncompliance.
- Review results at least monthly during pilots and after major demand or carrier changes.
- Scale only when environmental indicators improve and segment-specific service, quality, and resilience remain within the pre-agreed guardrails. Pause or roll back the affected eligibility band rather than abandoning justified emergency options system-wide.
