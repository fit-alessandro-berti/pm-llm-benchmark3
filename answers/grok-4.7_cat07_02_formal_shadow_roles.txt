# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

**Automated intake boundary (Nora).** Core work is the whole observed profile: register and entitlement precheck, 760 and 610 events. There is no peripheral human judgment in the log. Complexity stops at medium, and high and critical counts are zero. She has no substitution episode and no organizational authority; a later human validate still appears on the technical path (fragment 2), while fragment 1 shows her precheck standing in front of a standard response. Frequency is capacity of a system, and authority stays with entitlement-certified agents when the case leaves the low band.

**Routine front-office service (Amira, Ben).** Core is validate, standard response, close, and register. For Amira that block is about 1,180 of 1,375 events. Peripheral diagnosis is real but secondary (Amira 75, Ben 60 of about 815 diagnoses). Negotiation is occasional, and pricing is occasional for Amira (18) and rare for Ben (5). Neither has approval or security events. Authority matches the entitlement certification: they can complete the simple path and the human validation step. They also absorb temp rework (fragment 6) even though reopen counts look small (8 and 4). Amira’s higher median (0.34 vs 0.29), higher high-band count (58 vs 31), and higher negotiation count mark a seniority gradient inside one role, not a second post. No evidence in this window says either of them covered Fatima or Hana.

**Bounded temporary front-office support (Kai, Lina).** Core is the same four activities, and it is almost the entire job (about 98%). Peripheral diagnosis and negotiation are traces (3–12 events) and should stay traces. They handle the lowest complexity in the human workforce and no critical cases. They are not substitution for permanent agents; they are seasonal load on the simple path (contract, tenure under half a year, no certification). The authority boundary is the important one: they already perform large volumes of A2 (270 and 250) without an entitlement certification, and weeks 8–10 tie 17 reopens to incomplete validation notes. They do not perform A13, so the failure returns to permanent staff. Similarity of activity mix is a weak argument for giving them diagnosis, pricing, or unsupervised entitlement decisions.

**Technical solution analysis (Chen, Dina).** Core is diagnose, configure, and implement: 240–260 diagnoses each, 235–240 configurations, 215–220 implementations, together about three-quarters of technical A5 and A10. Peripheral standard responses and closes are the simple residue of technical cases. KB updates (14 and 11) and quality touches are occasional. Chen’s 24 security reviews are peripheral in his own frequency (about 2.7%) and material in the security activity (about 16% of A6). Dina’s 6 security events are occasional exposure, consistent with 1.2 years’ tenure. Complexity is the split inside the role: Chen’s median 0.71 and 42 critical cases against Dina’s 0.48 and 9. Chen’s late shift also means the harder technical work continues when Erik, Fatima, Hana, and Mateo are off shift. Neither technician has approval authority. Chen is the daytime security specialist’s technical partner, not a second security authority.

**Security control specialist (Erik).** Core is A6, 96 events, 56% of his own work and about two-thirds of the activity. Everything else is peripheral execution around the same rare cases: implement, diagnose, configure, KB, quality, reopen. That periphery is why a frequency profile looks semi-technical. Complexity says the periphery is the critical case itself (median 0.86, 55 critical). Authority comes from the security certification and from where fragment 3 places him, upstream of Mateo’s coordination. Weeks 7–8 show that his observed frequency is partly Mateo’s triage policy: fewer A6 events, fewer false-escalation complaints, and two critical waits over two days. A temporary drop in A6 is weak evidence that security demand fell. His own implement events (18) mean some cases may be reviewed and executed by the same person; that is a segregation question the activity count cannot settle.

**Commercial exception specialist (Fatima).** Core is pricing review and negotiation, 122 and 90 events, about 73% of her work. She is the majority owner of A7. Peripheral validate, close, and the 16 approval events are the edges of commercial cases. Those A9 events are 7% of all approvals and 5.5% of her events; her certification is pricing, and Hana holds approval. Treating A9 frequency as delegated authority overreaches the log. Median complexity 0.67 and 25 critical cases fit exception work. The week 3–4 absence is the substitution test: Giorgio kept the activity alive, and median pricing duration moved from 38 minutes to 74. That supports Fatima as the fluent owner and Giorgio as backup, which is a different claim from “either person is the role.”

**Hybrid senior agent with commercial shadow membership (Giorgio).** He has two cores, and they have different bases. The frequency core is front office: validate 190, standard response 150, close 130, register 90. The organizationally significant core is commercial: negotiation 88 (about 26% of all A8, level with Fatima) and pricing 72 (about 31% of all A7). Removing the recorded absence cover still leaves on the order of 41 pricing reviews and 70 negotiations, so the shadow pattern is steady-state work, with the absence as a spike on top. Peripheral diagnosis (40) fits a senior agent on harder front-office cases. The 20 approval events are the ambiguous edge: enough to matter, short of Hana’s certification and volume. His case mix (median 0.58, 36 critical) sits between front office and commercial. He is certified for pricing, so the shadow is an informal placement relative to the Commercial home team, and the log still shows a fluency gap under full cover (38 minutes to 74). One person carrying both cores is also a combined absence risk.

**Final approval authority (Hana).** Core is A9, 138 events, 56% of her work and 63% of the activity. Peripheral reopen (24), negotiation (22), and quality spot-checks (12) are how an approver supervises hard cases rather than a second job. She has the highest median among managers and coordinators after Erik (0.79) and 62 critical cases. Authority is the certification plus the week-5 recheck behavior: Mateo could log preliminary approvals, and 13 of 21 came back to her. Other people’s A9 counts (Mateo 44, Giorgio 20, Fatima 16) are peripheral to this role. Frequency of the word “approval” in the activity label is weaker evidence than certification, recheck, and escalation level.

**Quality gate and knowledge stewardship (Ivan, Julia).** Core is quality check and KB update: roughly 300 checks each and about 100 KB updates each, together near 90% of A11 and near 80% of A14. Peripheral implement (38 and 35) and configure (26 and 22) are large enough to be a real tail. They may be defect correction after the check or help during the build; the order of A10 and A11 inside the case decides that, and the aggregate matrix does not. Complexity is medium and similar, which matches a gate applied to other people’s hard work rather than ownership of the critical residue (11 and 7 critical cases). They are not substitutes for Chen or Erik. Shift is the operational difference: Ivan late beside Chen, Julia day beside Dina. KB volume is the resilience mechanism for Erik’s and Chen’s scarce knowledge (Erik himself has only 9 KB events).

**Cross-functional escalation broker (Mateo).** A core defined by share of personal volume does not fit. His largest activities are each about 10–12% (close, validate, standard response, approval, reopen). The core is visible in sequences and in disruptions: he is on the security path after Erik, on the reopen path before specialists and Hana, and in the middle of both the approval workshop and the security surge. A13 leadership (42) and critical-case contact (76) describe the post better than any activity mode. Peripheral work is “touching” specialist activities (A6 16, A7 10, A5 18, A11 20). Those counts are weak evidence of specialist competence. Week 5 is the authority boundary: 21 preliminary approvals, 13 rechecked, during a six-day-scale absence of Hana, on top of a baseline of roughly two dozen other A9 events across the remaining weeks. Weeks 7–8 are the triage boundary: filtering low-risk security referrals reduced noise and delayed two critical cases. Brokerage has organizational authority to route and to prepare, and the log keeps final security judgment and final approval elsewhere.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**Giorgio as commercial shadow.** His formal post is Senior Service Agent in Front Office. His actual membership includes the commercial exception path. Negotiation volume is effectively tied with the commercial specialist (88 vs 90), and pricing volume is second in the organization (72 vs Fatima’s 122). The pricing certification makes this a credentialed shadow: the home team and the formal title lag the work, and the work is still outside the Commercial role that Fatima occupies alone on a normal week. Benefit: complex front-office relationships keep a negotiator who already knows the case; during six absent days the pricing queue continued (31 A7 and 18 A8) instead of stopping. Benefit also shows up in ordinary weeks, because most of his A8 volume is outside that episode. Risk: under that cover, median pricing duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes, so backup is slower than the owner. Risk also includes 20 approval-labeled events without an approval certification, and a single absence that would hit both front-office capacity and commercial backup. The shadow is a resilience asset when it is explicit, volume-capped, and sampled by Fatima. It is a control weakness when staffing models treat him as another Amira (cosine 0.84) and commercial capacity is planned as one person.

**Mateo as escalation broker.** He bridges technical, security, commercial, quality, and approval work without owning any of those specialties. The flat profile, the low cosines with Hana (0.54) and Erik (0.39), the highest reopen count, and the highest critical-case contact are one pattern: cases that have already left the routine path pass through him. Benefit: one coordinator reduces false security escalations, keeps a preliminary approval stream moving while Hana is out, and gives reopens a named route (fragments 3 and 5). Benefit is exactly what a specialist cluster cannot see, because the value is betweenness in the handover network. Risk: he is a single day-shift bottleneck on the critical slice (76 critical cases). Preliminary approvals were often redone (13 of 21). The security filter that protected Erik also left two critical Enterprise Security cases waiting more than two days. His modest A6 and A7 counts can be misread as growing specialist skill. Brokerage stays valuable when triage rules, wait limits, and “preliminary versus final” are written down.

**Erik as rare critical specialist.** He bridges the technical path to a security decision that almost nobody else is allowed to finish. Chen contributes a real adjacent tail (24 A6), Mateo contributes routing (16 A6 and a filter that may not even be logged as A6), and Erik contributes the majority of the reviews at the highest complexity. Benefit: critical exposure is concentrated in a certified specialist, and a small KB trail (9 events) plus Ivan and Julia’s KB role can spread that judgment. The rareness is the point of the control, not a sign of spare capacity. About 170 events against Nora’s 1,370 is a poor importance ranking. Risk: one day-shift person is the control; the late shift has Chen and Ivan only. Risk includes review and implementation on the same small caseload (18 A10). Risk also includes reading the weeks 7–8 dip in A6 as lower demand, when the dip is consistent with Mateo holding cases back. A capacity plan that clones high-volume roles and trims residual clusters removes this control first.

**Nora as the automation boundary.** She bridges channels into the human process and stops at low-complexity precheck. Benefit: 760 registrations and 610 validations absorb the bulk of simple intake and run outside human shifts. Risk: counting those events as full-time-equivalent agent work overstates human capacity and understates the human validate that still follows her on technical cases. A bot failure moves that volume onto the same front-office and temp pools that already create entitlement rework.

**Kai and Lina as a temporary-workforce boundary.** They bridge seasonal volume into the simple fragment (register or validate → standard response → close). Benefit: several hundred low-complexity closures without occupying Chen, Fatima, Erik, or Hana. Risk: activity vectors look like Amira and Ben, while certification, complexity, and reopen ownership do not. The 17 entitlement-note failures are the cost of stretching that boundary into A2 decisions.

**Ivan and Julia as the quality bridge.** They sit between every completing path and closure, and between specialist judgment and the knowledge base. Benefit: a day/late pair catches both Dina’s and Chen’s work, and KB updates are the organization’s memory for security and configuration. Risk: their 35–38 implement events each can quietly become a second technical team, and a frequency match of 0.97 can make the two shifts look redundant.

**Chen as a security-adjacent bridge inside the technical role.** Twenty-four security events are peripheral to Chen and material to A6. Benefit: the late shift has someone who can prepare, and sometimes perform, security-adjacent work while Erik is off. Risk: those events are not a second security specialist. Dina’s much smaller A6 count, at similar overall activity mix, is the contrast that keeps this as a bridge facet of the senior technician.

# 4. Ambiguous assignments and alternative interpretations

**R07_Giorgio.** Interpretation A: a senior front-office agent whose commercial events are mostly absence cover and coaching. Interpretation B: a standing commercial shadow, and the normal negotiator on complex front-office cases, with Fatima owning the harder pricing residue. The absence-adjusted counts lean to B for negotiation (on the order of 70 A8 events outside the episode) and to a dual reading for pricing (31 of 72 A7 events sit in the absence). Deciding evidence: A7 and A8 counts split by `temporary_substitution`; outcome parity with Fatima on discount level, override rate, and rework; whether cycle time is elevated only in weeks 3–4 or on his ordinary commercial events too; and whether his 20 A9 events are recommendations, preparations, or binding decisions. His pricing certification is compatible with both interpretations and does not by itself choose the home-team question.

**R13_Mateo.** Interpretation A: a router and case manager with no decision rights. Interpretation B: a deputy approver whose A9 events are accepted unless Hana happens to be away. Interpretation C: a security triage officer whose filter is an intentional control. The week-5 recheck rate (13 of 21) leans against B for that episode. The weeks 7–8 pattern supports C as behavior and leaves the design question open, because two critical waits cut against an unconstrained filter. His baseline of A9 events outside week 5 (44 minus 21) still needs a label. Deciding evidence: `escalation_level` and outcome of his A9 events, the share later followed by an A9 from Hana, whether his A6 events contain a security decision or only a referral, and the wait-time distribution of Enterprise Security cases he diverted. Working-together and subcontracting networks decide A better than any activity count: a broker returns work to specialists and to Hana; a deputy ends the case.

**R03_Chen and R04_Dina.** Interpretation A: one role, with Chen assigned the harder queue because of tenure, late shift, and product mix. Interpretation B: Chen is already a senior technical lead and security liaison, and Dina is a junior analyst whose activity mix has caught up before her case mix has. Similar totals and cosine 0.94 fit A. The complexity gap (median 0.71 vs 0.48; critical 42 vs 9) and the A6 gap (24 vs 6) fit a seniority gradient that B makes explicit. Deciding evidence: complexity and `product_line` stratified by shift, so the late-shift effect is separated from skill; whether Chen’s A6 events are independent reviews or preparation for Erik; reopen and defect rates on similar product lines; and whether Dina’s high-band share is rising with tenure. A staffing model can keep one role and still refuse interchangeability.

**R01_Amira.** Interpretation A: the same routine front-office role as Ben, with ordinary person-to-person variation. Interpretation B: a developing generalist bridge, already taking more diagnosis, negotiation, pricing, and medium/high cases, on the same path Giorgio is further along. The entitlement certification and the shared core activities fit A. The gap in medians (0.34 vs 0.29), high-band cases (58 vs 31), A8 (42 vs 25), and A7 (18 vs 5) fits a mild form of B, still far from Giorgio’s commercial share (Amira’s A7+A8 are about 4% of her events; Giorgio’s are about 19.5%). Deciding evidence: customer segment and product line, whether her A7 events are owned decisions or assisted Fatima/Giorgio, and defect rates on the medium band.

**R06_Fatima’s approval-labeled events, shared ambiguity with Giorgio.** Interpretation A: A9 rows are mislabeled negotiation outcomes or system status changes. Interpretation B: commercial staff and the senior agent issue a defined subclass of approvals. Only Hana carries the approval certification; Fatima has 16 A9 events and Giorgio 20, against Hana’s 138. Deciding evidence: the `escalation_level` on those events, whether a later Hana event rechecks them, and the monetary threshold in policy. Until that join exists, these events stay peripheral to the approval role.

**R14_Nora’s validations.** Interpretation A: every Nora A2 is the same entitlement decision a certified agent records. Interpretation B: Nora performs a precheck, and human A2 remains the decision except on the simplest fragment. Fragment 1 is compatible with A on the simple path. Fragment 2, where a person validates after Nora registers, is compatible with B on everything technical. Deciding evidence: share of cases with both a Nora A2 and a later human A2, disagreement rate between them, and complexity of cases where Nora’s validate is the last entitlement event. High/critical counts of zero already say she is outside the hard entitlement decision.

**R11_Kai and R12_Lina.** Interpretation A: junior members of the routine front-office role, safe to schedule as Amira/Ben capacity. Interpretation B: a bounded seasonal role whose quality system is the permanent staff who reopen their cases. Activity mix supports A. Certification, complexity, contract type, zero reopen handling, and 17 entitlement-note failures support B. Deciding evidence: reopen rate per closure versus Amira and Ben on low-complexity cases only, note-completeness audits, and whether their A2 events are restricted by segment and product line. The late/day split (Lina late, Kai day) is coverage, not two roles.

**R09_Ivan and R10_Julia on implement and configure.** Interpretation A: one quality role, and the A10/A5 tail is rework they own after the gate. Interpretation B: they are part-time members of the technical role, especially on the shift they share with Chen or Dina. The 0.97 cosine and the dominance of A11 and A14 support A as the role. About 70 implement events between them are enough that B should be tested, not assumed away. Deciding evidence: whether A10 occurs after A11 in the same case, and whether those cases already contain a Chen or Dina implement event. Shift pairing alone is not evidence that Ivan is Chen’s backup technician.

**R05_Erik’s non-review events.** Interpretation A: peripheral wrap-up on cases he has already reviewed (document, small fix, KB). Interpretation B: he is a security engineer who builds the solution he approves. A6 dominance and the criticality profile fit A as the role definition. Eighteen implementations keep B open as a control design issue. Deciding evidence: same-case ordering of A6 versus A10, and whether a different resource implements when the case is critical. Either interpretation keeps him out of the technical analyst cluster.

**R08_Hana’s non-approval events.** Interpretation A: an approver who samples negotiation, reopen, and quality on the cases she must decide. def activity_cosine(frame):
    counts = pd.crosstab(frame["org:resource"], frame["concept:name"])

    norms = np.linalg.norm(vectors, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    unit = vectors / np.clip(norms, 1e-12, None)

    out = pd.DataFrame(sim, index=share.index, columns=share.index)

def sna_edges(metric):
    if hasattr(metric, "edges"):
        rows = [{"source": u, "target": v, "weight": d.get("weight", 1.0)}

    else:

                for (a, b), w in dict(metric).items()]

    edges = edges[edges["source"] != edges["target"]]

The similarity call and the manual cosine answer the same question: who looks alike when every event counts as one. The ownership threshold, the commercial submatrix, the complexity-weighted cosine, the handover degree, and the certification join answer the questions that similarity leaves open. A resource who changes role between threshold 0.05 and 0.20, or who is core on one activity family and a major owner of another, is a hybrid or a shadow, and a single cluster label will discard one of those memberships.

# 6. Operational interpretation

Use the ten roles as a RACI overlay on the formal chart. Core activities are the work a role is staffed and measured on. Peripheral activities stay visible in training and in audit samples. Shadow membership is written down as backup with a volume cap, a reviewer, and a cycle-time comparison to the owner. Brokerage is staffed as a post, because Mateo’s value is handover position and critical-case contact, not a modal activity.

Staff the simple path with Amira and Ben as the entitled permanent core, and with Kai and Lina as extra low-complexity capacity on day and late shift. Give the temps a completed-entitlement checklist and keep reopen ownership with permanent agents, matching fragment 6 and the 17 failed notes. Route ordinary diagnosis, configuration, and implementation to Chen and Dina together, and route high, critical, and security-adjacent technical cases to Chen while Dina’s high-band share grows. Keep Fatima as the pricing owner. Keep Giorgio as the credentialed negotiator and the named pricing backup, and review his exception outcomes against Fatima, using the 38-to-74-minute shift as the fluency gap to close before his commercial volume grows further. Keep Hana as the final approver. Define Mateo’s A9 events as preliminary unless policy names a subclass he may finish; the 13 rechecks out of 21 are the control to retain. Protect Erik’s time for A6. Publish the security filter Mateo already operates, with a maximum wait for critical Enterprise Security cases, so the drop in false escalations and the two long critical waits are both designed for. Staff quality as a day/late pair, Julia with the day technical path and Ivan with Chen’s late path, and feed Erik’s and Chen’s closures into their KB queue.

The late shift is the resilience gap: Chen, Ivan, Lina, and Nora are on, and Erik, Fatima, Hana, Mateo, and Giorgio are day resources. Complex late cases need an explicit “prepare overnight, decide next day” rule for security, price, and approval. Nora’s 1,370 events stay in the capacity model as automation. A bot outage plan assigns registration and precheck to entitled permanent agents first, not to the temp pool that is already failing entitlement notes. Cross-training that matches the evidence is entitlement quality for the temps, pricing speed and criteria for Giorgio, approval boundaries for Mateo, and supervised security exposure for Dina with Chen and Erik. A segregation check on cases where Erik both reviews and implements belongs in the same pass.

Three cautions apply before any of that becomes a roster change.

Mined activity clusters will move headcount in the wrong direction if they are used alone. They merge temps with permanent front office, merge Giorgio with Amira and Ben, treat Chen and Dina as interchangeable despite the critical-case gap (42 versus 9), and rank Erik’s 170 events as the first place to cut. Mateo either vanishes at a strict threshold or appears as a second supervisor. Those errors line up with the cosines and with a single ownership threshold; they are properties of the method.

Temporary substitution is evidence of who kept a queue moving, and it is weak evidence of permanent competence or authority. Giorgio’s cover coincided with a doubling of median pricing duration. Mateo’s cover coincided with a 13-of-21 recheck. His security filter coincided with two critical waits over two days. Promoting any of those episodes into a standing delegation needs outcome parity, not event counts.

These ten weeks also contain seasonal contracts and three disruption windows. A target operating model copied from the raw totals will institutionalize absence behavior and temporary staff. Recompute the profiles with substitution events held out, and decide authority from certification, recheck, escalation level, and cycle time.
