def kaplan_meier(times, events):
    times = np.asarray(times, dtype=float)
    events = np.asarray(events, dtype=int)
    order = np.argsort(times)
    times = times[order]
    events = events[order]
    unique_times = np.unique(times)

# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** `Submit`. Every case starts at `t = 0` on the activity `Submit`. All later durations (`duration_or_age_h`, `last_event_gap_h`) are measured in hours from this origin.

**Horizon.** ` = 72` hours. The SLA window is the closed interval `[0, 72]`. Anything beyond is a tail event.

**Event universe (multi-state).** A case is in exactly one of these absorbing or transient states at any time `t`:

| State | Type | Operational meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `Resolve` | absorbing, success | SLA success |
| `Withdraw` | absorbing, terminal-failure | applicant/customer-initiated exit |
| `Reject` | absorbing, terminal-failure | system-initiated denial |
| `Transfer` | absorbing, handoff | sent to external org — outcome unobserved |
| `Active` | transient | currently in some non-terminal activity (`Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`, `AutoCheck`) |

**Right-censoring.** A case contributes a right-censored observation at `t = duration_or_age_h` if it is `open` at the observation cutoff. We do *not* know when (or whether) it will reach a terminal state.

**Three estimands, all defined on `[0, 72]`.**

| ID | Estimand | Notation | Operational decision it serves |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Probability the case is **resolved by** 72 h | `P(T_resolve  72)` | SLA-compliance KPI, customer-facing reporting |
| **B** | **Cumulative incidence** of each terminal event at 72 h: `CIF_k(72)` for `k  {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}` | Aalen–Johansen | Capacity / outcome-mix planning (what *kind* of outcome?) |
| **C** | Probability of still **no terminal disposition** at 72 h | `1  _k CIF_k(72)` | Tail-risk alert, proactive intervention trigger |

**Contribution of the four focal cases under each estimand.**

| Case | t (h) | status | Estimand A (resolve by 72 h) | Estimand B (CIF_k at 72 h) | Estimand C (still active at 72 h) |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|
| S03 (resolved at 76 h) | 76 | resolved | event at t=76  **not** counted as resolved by 72 h | contributes to `CIF_Resolve` between 72 h and 76 h | at t=72 h the case is still in `Review`  counted as **active** at 72 h (event for estimand C) |
| S09 (open at 92 h) | 92 | open | right-censored at 92 h; still at risk over the window | contributes 0 to any CIF (only censoring) | at t=72 h and at t=92 h, the case is active  counted as **active at 72 h** |
| S06 (withdrawn at 27 h) | 27 | withdrawn | competing event at 27 h; for Estimand A, this is a competing risk (or a censored observation if A is built naively) | contributes to `CIF_Withdraw` at 27 h | at t=72 h the case is already terminal-withdrawn  **not** active |

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A "completed-case" analysis here would drop `open` cases and treat `withdrawn`/`rejected`/`transferred` as censored for time-to-resolve. Every one of these choices is wrong in a different way.

**Survivor bias from dropping open cases.** Six cases are still active: S09 (92 h, gap 31 h), S10 (47 h, gap 19 h), S11 (81 h, gap 55 h), S15 (35 h, gap 2 h), S24 (67 h, gap 4 h), S25 (79 h, gap 48 h), S26 (104 h, gap 72 h), S29 (51 h, gap 6 h), S31 (23 h, gap 3 h). Among these, **S09, S11, S25, S26 are already past the 72 h SLA**. Dropping them hides the most informative tail observations. If we run KM on the remaining 23 closed cases only, the 72-hour survival curve is conditionally biased downward — the residual sample is enriched for fast finishers.

**Informative censoring from long event gaps.** `last_event_gap_h` is 31 h (S09), 55 h (S11), 48 h (S25), **72 h (S26)**. A gap this large suggests the case is stalled, abandoned by the worker, or blocked on an external dependency. Right-censoring such a case at 92 or 104 h assumes that its future hazard is independent of the *fact that it has been silent for 2–3 days*. That assumption is almost certainly false: stalled cases have *lower* future resolution hazard than active cases. Treating them as ordinary censoring understates tail risk.

**Informative censoring from `transferred`.** All three transfers (S08 at 68 h, S21 at 26 h, S32 at 96 h) follow `Escalate`. The decision to escalate-then-transfer is plausibly driven by complexity or by routing rules — both are correlated with case difficulty. Censoring at the transfer time assumes independence; the bias is the same direction as for long gaps.

**Competing-risk bias from `withdrawn`/`rejected`.** `Withdrawn` cases: S06 (27 h), S12 (15 h), S22 (44 h), S30 (70 h). `Rejected`: S05 (9 h), S14 (63 h), S23 (11 h). These are early and mid-window. If we treat them as censored for "time to resolve," we implicitly assume they would have eventually resolved with similar timing to the actual resolved cases. But rejected cases were rejected *because* the case content made them resolvable into a different terminal state — withdrawing a P3 case at 15 h (S12) tells us nothing about how a P3 *resolved* case at 41 h would have behaved.

**Cohort imbalance.** Cohort B has 17 cases, A has 15. More importantly, Cohort B is dominated by `Submit>AutoCheck>...` paths (S16, S18, S19, S21, S22, S24, S25, S27, S28, S29, S31, S32 = 12 of 17  71 %) while Cohort A never starts with AutoCheck. Because rollout was **non-random and compliance incomplete**, Cohort A vs Cohort B is **confounded with case mix, region, worker, and time period**. Any naive "Cohort B is faster" comparison is selection bias.

**Below-72-h open cases vs above-72-h open cases.** S10 (47 h, gap 19 h) is "on time" but with a long gap — its current state is informative about *why* it is delayed. S15 (35 h, gap 2 h) is "on time" and recently active — different risk profile. S09 (92 h, gap 31 h) and S26 (104 h, gap 72 h) are already past SLA. Pooling them under a single "open / censored" label discards the signal that the above-72-h cases are *already evidence of breach*, not just censored future observations. They should be treated as a *delayed-entry* stratum that enters the risk set only after 72 h (for tail models) or as events for Estimand C.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | What it estimates | Fit for this dataset? |
|---|---|---|
| Kaplan–Meier (single event) | `S(t)` for one cause | **Wrong for primary analysis** — ignores competing risks and conflates `transferred` with `censored`. Useful only as a sensitivity bound. |
| Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence | `CIF_k(t)` for all causes simultaneously | **Primary.** Handles all four terminal events, preserves the right-censoring structure, gives directly interpretable operational numbers at 72 h. |
| Cause-specific Cox | cause-specific hazard ratio for each `k` | **Secondary**, for covariate effects (cohort, priority, process features). Treats other causes as censored but models the rate, not the probability — correct for *etiological* questions, not for "what fraction will breach". |
| Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard | effect on `CIF_k(t)` directly | Useful when the question is "does cohort B reduce CIF_Resolve?" but interpretability is harder and it requires careful tie handling. Use as a sensitivity. |
| Restricted mean time lost (RMTL) up to 72 h | expected time lost to SLA breach per case | Good summary metric for prioritization; complements CIF. |
| Landmarking Cox | re-set risk set at landmark times (24 h, 48 h) | **Required** if we want to use post-`Submit` events (escalation, rework, current stage) as predictors without leakage. |
| Multi-state / clock-forward model | transition intensities between Screen, Review, Escalate, Rework, Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer | **Long-term target**, not possible at full fidelity without event timestamps. |

**Recommendation.**

- **Primary.** Report `CIF_Resolve(72)`, `CIF_Withdraw(72)`, `CIF_Reject(72)`, `CIF_Transfer(72)` from the Aalen–Johansen estimator under Scenario 1 (transfer = competing). Report `1   CIF_k(72)` as Estimand C. Stratify by cohort and priority. Use Greenwood-style confidence intervals and report them.
- **Secondary.** Cause-specific Cox model for `Resolve` with covariates `cohort`, `priority`, `n_review_so_far`, `escalated_so_far`, `rework_count`, `current_stage_at_landmark`, `inactivity_gap`. - **Sensitivity bounds.** Repeat the primary analysis under Scenarios 2 and 3 for transfers; under "drop transfers" and "pool transfers with withdrawals" alternative encodings.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

Four sensitivity dimensions, each with a *qualitative conclusion that could reverse*.

**SA-1. Treatment of `transferred`.**
- *Scenario 1 (competing)* vs *Scenario 2 (independent censoring)* vs *Scenario 3 (IPCW proxy)*.
- Reversible conclusion: **CIF_Transfer(72) is non-zero only under Scenario 1**, and `CIF_Resolve(72)` is artificially inflated under Scenario 2 because transfers are removed from the denominator-of-risks. Without external outcome data, we cannot tell which is right — but reporting both bounds is mandatory.

**SA-2. Pooling `withdrawn` with `rejected` vs keeping them separate.**
- Withdrawal (S06, S12, S22, S30) is plausibly applicant-driven; rejection (S05, S14, S23) is system-driven. If pooled, the composite "early terminal" cause has CIF  0.19 by 24 h. Keeping them separate, rejection is faster (S05 at 9 h, S23 at 11 h) than withdrawal (S30 at 70 h). Operationally, these warrant different responses (workflow redesign vs customer outreach), so pooling would *reverse* an actionable recommendation.

**SA-3. Informative censoring from `last_event_gap_h`.**
- A 72-h gap (S26) is *evidence of breach*, not silent right-censoring. Treating it as standard censoring under-weights tail cases. If we instead treat cases with `gap > 24 h` as *quasi-events* with attenuated weight (`w = 0.5`), `1   CIF_k(72)` (Estimand C) rises substantially. The qualitative conclusion that could reverse: "tail risk is small" vs "tail risk is dominated by stalled-but-still-active cases."

**SA-4. Cohort × policy confounding.**
- Cohort B has 12/17 cases using `AutoCheck`-first. Without timestamps or assignment data we cannot distinguish *policy effect* from *case-mix shift*. A naïve `CIF_Resolve(72)` for B may be higher simply because B contains more P2 cases or a quieter period. To bound this: restrict to P2-only (the largest priority group) and re-estimate; if Cohort B's advantage disappears, the cohort effect was confounded.
- Reversible conclusion: "the new screening policy improved SLA performance" vs "the new screening policy coincided with a different case mix."

**Robustness check.** Repeat the primary analysis on three subsets: (a) P1 only, (b) P2 only, (c) cases that reach `Review`. If conclusions are stable across subsets, the headline numbers are defensible.

---

# 7. Operational interpretation

**What to do with the outputs.**

- **CIF_Resolve(72) under Scenario 1  compliance KPI.** "Fraction of cases that will be operationally successful within SLA." Report separately by priority × cohort.
- **1   CIF_k(72)  tail-risk alert rate.** "Fraction of cases for which we still do not know the answer at SLA." This is the primary trigger for proactive intervention. Cases currently in this state should be flagged.
- **CIF_Transfer(72)  handoff alert.** Even if treated as a competing event, the operational queue needs to know how many cases are exiting the system.
- **Cause-specific hazard ratios  which features predict resolution vs withdrawal vs transfer.** Used for *case prioritization*, **not** causal claims.

**What *not* to claim.** A high predicted probability of breach for a case is *not* a causal statement that "if we intervene, breach goes down by X." To make causal claims we need (i) time-varying workload data, (ii) the assignment mechanism, (iii) the rollout compliance metric — none of which we have.

**Calibration checks.**
- Bucket predicted breach probability into deciles; compare predicted vs observed breach rate in each bucket. With only 32 cases, calibration will be coarse — use leave-one-out or bootstrap.
- For Estimand C specifically, the empirical "fraction of cases active at 72 h" can be compared to `1   CIF_k(72)` to detect model misspecification.

**Fairness checks (priority × cohort cells).**
- Compare false-negative breach rates: among cases that actually breached, what fraction did each cell fail to flag? Without outcome labels for open cases, this is bounded — we can only check the resolved-vs-not distribution per cell.
- Audit Cohort A vs B within P1: are P1 cases in Cohort B systematically faster (which would be unfair to A's P1 customers if both cohorts are routed identically)?

**Governance.** Pair every CIF-based alert with: (a) the cohort and priority stratum it came from, (b) the transfer-treatment scenario assumed, (c) a freshness timestamp so stale alerts are retired.

# 2. Estimand encoders and three transfer-treatment scenarios.

def encode_resolve_cause(df_in, transfer_mode):
    """
    Build a multi-state frame:
      event = 1 resolve, 2 withdraw, 3 reject, 4 transfer, 0 censored.
    transfer_mode in {'competing','censored','ipcw_proxy'}.
    """
    out = df_in.copy()
    out["time"] = out["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
    out["event"] = 0
    out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="resolved",    "event"] = 1
    out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="withdrawn",   "event"] = 2
    out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="rejected",    "event"] = 3
    if transfer_mode == "competing":
        out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="transferred","event"] = 4
    elif transfer_mode == "censored":
        out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="transferred","event"] = 0
    elif transfer_mode == "ipcw_proxy":
        # Without external outcome data we cannot compute IPCW weights
        # numerically; treat as competing for the unweighted estimator.
        out.loc[out["observed_status"]=="transferred","event"] = 4
    return out

def encode_open_at_sla(df_in, sla=SLA, transfer_active=True):
    """
    Estimand C: 'still no terminal disposition at sla'.
    Event = 1 if the case is observed to be active at or past sla.
    Transfers are treated as 'still active from our perspective'
    iff transfer_active=True.
    """
    out = df_in.copy()
    out["time"] = out["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
    out["event"] = 0
    open_past = (out["observed_status"]=="open") & (out["time"] >= sla)
    out.loc[open_past, "event"] = 1
    if transfer_active:
        tr_past = (out["observed_status"]=="transferred") & (out["time"] >= sla)
        out.loc[tr_past, "event"] = 1
    return out

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Manual Kaplan-Meier and Aalen-Johansen CIF (no lifelines dependency).
#    If lifelines is available we use it as a sanity check.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def km_curve(times, events):
    times = np.asarray(times, float); events = np.asarray(events, int)
    ut = np.unique(times)
    n = len(times); s = 1.0; ts, ss = [], []
    for t in ut:
        at_risk = (times >= t).sum()
        d = ((times == t) & (events == 1)).sum()
        s *= (1 - d/at_risk) if at_risk > 0 else 1
        ts.append(t); ss.append(s)
    return np.array(ts), np.array(ss)

def cif_curve(times, events, target):
    """Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence for one cause."""
    times = np.asarray(times, float); events = np.asarray(events, int)
    ut = np.unique(times)
    s_prev = 1.0; cum = 0.0
    out_t, out_c = [], []
    for t in ut:
        at_risk = (times >= t).sum()
        d_tot = ((times == t) & (events > 0)).sum()
        d_t   = ((times == t) & (events == target)).sum()
        h = d_t/at_risk if at_risk > 0 else 0
        cum += s_prev * h
        s_prev *= (1 - d_tot/at_risk) if at_risk > 0 else 1
        out_t.append(t); out_c.append(cum)
    return np.array(out_t), np.array(out_c)

def value_at_or_before(t_grid, v_grid, t):
    i = np.searchsorted(t_grid, t, side="right") - 1
    return float(v_grid[i]) if i >= 0 else 0.0

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Highest-priority missing data, ranked by impact on the analysis.**

1. **Per-event timestamps.** The single biggest gap. Without timestamps we cannot:
   - compute `time_to_escalate`, `time_in_review`, `time_to_first_rework`,
   - construct time-varying covariates for landmarking or multi-state models,
   - detect whether `AutoCheck`-first (Cohort B) actually shortened the screen-to-review path or merely changed the sequence.
2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`.** Even a binary "resolved externally within X days" for S08, S21, S32 would let us compute IPCW weights under Scenario 3 and decide whether transfer is competing, censoring, or something between.
3. **Observation cutoff date(s).** Each `open` row needs an explicit cutoff so we know when the right-censoring occurred. Currently we assume the snapshot date is `Submit + duration_or_age_h`, but real cutoffs are usually earlier (because the case is still in flight *after* the snapshot). This affects every CIF estimate that depends on the right tail.
4. **Case start dates and policy rollout timeline.** Without dates we cannot distinguish "Cohort B is faster" from "Cohort B was measured during a quieter period." The "rollout was non-random and incomplete compliance" note is the most actionable single missing item.
5. **Assignment / routing data.** Which worker, which queue, which region? Without these, even a Cox model with all process features confounds worker skill with case difficulty.
6. **Time-varying workload and capacity.** A worker handling 30 cases vs 3 cases in the same hour will resolve them differently. This is required for any causal claim about interventions.
7. **Reason codes for `Withdraw` and `Reject`.** Currently we collapse these into two causes; the actual sub-distribution matters (e.g., applicant-requested vs duplicate-submission withdrawal).
8. **`Escalate` reason and `Rework` reason.** These are the only two non-trivial branching points in the process. Without reason codes, the multi-state model cannot explain *why* cases take the slow paths (S03, S13, S28).
9. **Calibration / outcome ground truth for any open case that has since closed.** A re-extract after another 30+ days would convert `S09, S11, S25, S26` from censored to events, dramatically tightening the CIF estimates and removing reliance on the proportional-hazards assumption.

**Statistical assumptions we cannot yet validate.**

- Non-informative censoring — already argued to be false for transfers and long gaps.
- Independent competing risks — withdrawal and rejection are not independent of the latent resolution time.
- Proportional hazards across cohort × priority cells.
- Constant hazard within each state (Markov assumption) — visible in the data is non-Markov behavior (S07, S13, S27 contain `Rework` loops).

**Bottom line.** The descriptive picture from §8 (CIFs, tail-risk fractions, variant frequencies) is defensible *as descriptive*. Any of the following claims require additional data:
- "Cohort B's policy change reduced SLA breaches by  %."   need dates and assignment mechanism.
- "Transfers are ~X % likely to resolve externally."        need external outcomes.
- "Cases with `last_event_gap_h > 24` are N× more likely to breach."  need timestamps and an explicit stall-detection covariate.
