# 1. Objectives and constraints

The policy should minimize expected end-to-end case cost, rather than maximize the number of assignments completed in the next few hours. A useful objective is a weighted, monitored combination of:

- SLA performance: reduce predicted breach probability and waiting time, with urgent and high-priority cases receiving the strongest protection.
- Quality and throughput: match work to a competent resource, reduce rework, and avoid preventable handovers so that completed work does not return to the queue.
- Continuity: keep the current owner where that owner is eligible and has enough capacity, especially for complex cases and high-cost handovers.
- Fairness: distribute projected work, hours, difficult cases, and overtime exposure rather than merely assigning to the resource with the fewest open cases.
- Development: give safe, documented training opportunities and supervised shadowing, without making a trainee absorb the risk of an urgent or complex case.
- Resilience: maintain usable capability outside a single specialist and avoid creating a new bottleneck in Security, Approval, or any other activity.

The objectives are intentionally competing. For example, moving a case from an overloaded current owner may improve fairness but create a costly handover; using a specialist may reduce immediate error risk but make the specialist queue longer. The weights should therefore be calibrated against observed SLA, rework, waiting-time, and worker-feedback outcomes, not treated as a proof of a single optimum.

Safety and compliance constraints are hard constraints:

1. A resource must have a positive skill score for the activity. A score of 0 is not operational eligibility.
2. Security Review is performed by skill 3, or by skill 1 or 2 under an explicitly recorded, available supervisor who is R7_Gita or R8_Hugo. A skill-0 person may observe or shadow but may not perform the review.
3. Final Approval is finalized only by skill 2 or 3. A skill-1 person can prepare material but cannot make the final approval decision.
4. A high- or urgent-priority case cannot be performed by a resource below skill 2 without an explicit supervisor and a recorded supervision plan. The supervisor is accountable for the result.
5. Capacity, access, segregation-of-duties, and team/security rules must be checked before scoring. A nominally skilled person who cannot access the case is not eligible.
6. No resource receives more than four new primary assignments in one decision batch. Supervised observation is not a new primary ownership assignment, but it consumes a separately tracked supervision budget.
7. An assignment is not made if the forecasted completion cannot meet the SLA when an eligible safer alternative exists. If no feasible alternative exists, the case is escalated rather than silently violating a hard rule.

The following are soft preferences, applied after hard filtering: urgency and predicted breach risk, skill margin, continuity and handover cost, projected workload and overtime risk, training value, bottleneck avoidance, and stable team collaboration. Overtime risk marked `high` receives a strong negative score. It can be overridden only for an imminent SLA breach when no safer eligible resource or supervisor is available, with an explicit exception reason.

# 2. Assignment policy

At each dispatch interval, the policy evaluates the whole queue together. This matters because assigning the first case changes capacity, bottleneck pressure, and the fairness score for every later case.

For a queued case `c`, first create candidate assignments of two types:

- a single primary resource `r`; or
- a supervised pair `(trainee, supervisor)`, where the trainee performs only what their skill and the rule allow and the supervisor has explicit accountability.

An illustrative policy is:

```text
dispatch(queue, resources, now):
    estimate service time and breach probability from historical cases
    calculate each resource's projected load, available hours, and supervision load
    calculate activity queue pressure and the number of qualified alternatives

    for case c in queue, ordered by predicted breach risk then priority:
        candidates = []
        for resource r and permitted supervised pair p:
            if not hard_eligible(c, r, p):
                continue
            if primary_assignments(r) >= 4:
                continue
            candidates.append((r, p))

        if candidates is empty:
            escalate(c, reason="no safe eligible assignment")
            continue

        for candidate in candidates:
            score[candidate] = (
                35 * sla_urgency(c, candidate)
              + 25 * skill_match(c, candidate)
              + 20 * continuity_and_handover(c, candidate)
              + 15 * workload_fairness(c, candidate)
              + 10 * safe_training_value(c, candidate)
              + 10 * resilience_value(c, candidate)
            )

        choose the highest-scoring feasible candidate
        record assignment_reason with the component scores and any exception
        update projected loads, primary counts, queue pressure, and supervision load
```

The numbers are starting weights, not immutable constants. The SLA term is also checked lexicographically: an assignment that is forecast to breach an urgent deadline is not rejected in favor of a slightly better fairness or training score. A production implementation should compare a few feasible whole-batch schedules rather than greedily dispatching when the queue is large.

The score components are explainable as follows.

- `sla_urgency` combines priority, remaining slack, predicted service time, predicted waiting time, complexity, and the probability of breach. A simple normalized form is `priority_weight + max(0, 1 - slack / planning_horizon)`, adjusted upward when the candidate's forecasted completion is after `sla_deadline`. An urgent case with three hours left therefore outranks a normal case with 30 hours left even if the latter is easier.
- `skill_match` rewards an eligible competent or specialist resource and records the skill margin above the minimum. It does not allow a skill-0 resource to obtain a positive score by being cheap or available. A supervised trainee can receive a development score, but not a false specialist score.
- `continuity_and_handover` gives a bonus for retaining `current_owner` when that owner is eligible and has capacity. If ownership changes, it subtracts a penalty based on the handover band, case complexity, and reassignment count. The penalty increases sharply when the move would take the case above two reassignments. Continuity is not allowed to defeat a safety rule or an unavoidable SLA failure.
- `workload_fairness` uses projected open cases and hours, not only the current open-case count. It penalizes an assignment that creates an outlier, penalizes `overtime risk="high"` strongly, and includes difficult-case or supervision load. It can include a team-level balancing term, while retaining the right skill and access requirements.
- `safe_training_value` is positive only when the resource's documented training priority matches the activity and the case is normal or low priority with acceptable slack and complexity. It is also positive for a scheduled shadowing session with a qualified performer. The dispatcher tracks the 20% normal/low target, but suspends training credit when it would materially increase SLA or quality risk.
- `resilience_value` rewards preserving qualified alternatives, avoiding unnecessary use of a sole specialist, and reducing concentration in a bottleneck. It should not penalize using the only qualified specialist for a security-critical case; instead it should expose the bottleneck and trigger capacity planning.

Tie-breaking is deterministic: first lower predicted breach risk, then lower handover cost, then continuity, then lower projected overtime exposure, then lower projected workload, and finally a stable resource identifier. The chosen reason records both positive and negative factors, for example `urgent SLA; current owner skill 3; retained ownership; high workload accepted because no second Approval resource meets the two-hour forecast`.

Exceptions are human-visible. A dispatcher may override a recommendation only by selecting an allowed alternative and recording a reason such as a live absence, access restriction, or customer-language requirement. An override cannot authorize skill 0 on Security or a skill-1/0 person to finalize Approval. If the only way to protect an SLA violates a safety rule, escalate to the service manager or an approved emergency process rather than conceal the violation.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

| Dimension | First-available routing | Specialist-only routing | Proposed dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected throughput | Often good for simple work because it minimizes immediate queue waiting; throughput can fall later when mismatches cause rework | Good first-pass quality on specialist tasks, but specialist queues can reduce end-to-end throughput | Usually a balanced result: competent resources are used where safe, continuity avoids avoidable setup time, and bottlenecks are exposed rather than hidden |
| SLA risk | May be low for the next dispatch but high when skill mismatch or a handover adds work; it does not prioritize deadline slack explicitly | Can be high in Security and Approval while the best specialist waits; it may also move cases from their current owner | Explicit breach-risk ordering protects urgent cases and permits a competent supervised path when safe; normal cases can wait for training or batching |
| Error and rework risk | Highest of the three when the lowest-load resource is only marginally skilled | Generally lowest immediate mismatch risk, but queue pressure and rushed specialist work can still create errors | Controls minimum skill, adds supervision, uses continuity and complexity, and treats rework as a measured outcome rather than assuming skill alone solves it |
| Workload fairness | Fair on a narrow open-case count, but can repeatedly send work to the apparently least-loaded resource or ignore hours and overtime risk | Often unfair: a few specialists receive most of the work | Uses projected cases, hours, difficulty, overtime exposure, and supervision load, with a four-assignment cap |
| Skill development | Accidental and unsafe for urgent work; no reliable training target | Poor: trainees are bypassed and specialist dependence increases | Deliberate training share for safe normal/low cases and shadowing; urgent and complex cases remain protected |
| Continuity | Often breaks ownership because it ignores the current owner | Breaks ownership whenever the owner is not the top specialist | Retains eligible owners, with a cost-sensitive penalty for handovers and exceptions when fairness or SLA risk justifies a move |
| Resilience and bottlenecks | Can use a superficially available but unsuitable person and does not plan redundancy | Fragile dependence on the highest-skilled resource and vulnerable specialist queues | Maintains qualified alternatives where possible, tracks sole-specialist queues, and makes unavoidable specialist dependence visible |

First-available may be preferable for genuinely routine, low-risk work when all eligible resources are similarly competent. Specialist-only may be preferable for a rare safety-critical activity when the specialist queue is comfortably inside SLA. The dynamic policy is preferable when the queue mixes urgent cases, different skill levels, handover costs, workload imbalance, and development goals, but its weights should be re-evaluated from live outcomes.

# 5. Explanation to workers

The assignment process looks first at safety and the deadline. A case is sent only to someone permitted and able to do the work. Urgent work is protected from unsafe trainee assignments; where training is appropriate, it is supervised and clearly marked.

After that, the process considers whether keeping the current owner will avoid a costly handover, whether the person has enough time left, and whether the assignment would create an unreasonable workload or overtime risk. It also considers safe development opportunities and whether relying on one specialist is making the queue fragile. These factors can point in different directions, so the displayed reason will show the main factors and the trade-off accepted.

For example, keeping Celine on C001 preserves the customer and case context, but R7 must supervise the Security Review. Moving C010 from Bruno to Eva protects a high-overtime-risk colleague because the handover is low-cost and Eva is competent in Approval. Jonas can shadow C015 to build Security capability, but he cannot perform or sign that review until he meets the required skill level.

This is workload coordination, not a performance ranking. A high load can reflect difficult cases, coaching, or urgent service, so supervisors review the context. Workers can report an access problem, an unsafe pairing, a missing handover detail, or a changed customer need. A human dispatcher can change an allowed assignment and must record why; workers should not be expected to work around a safety rule to make a metric look better.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

The deployment should preserve the decision-time snapshot and the final decision, including candidate scores, selected resource or pair, supervisor, `assignment_reason`, override flag, and policy version. Audit indicators should be sliced by activity, priority, complexity band, team, resource, and policy version; overall averages can hide unsafe concentration.

- **Handover frequency:** count resource changes between consecutive events in a case, divided by cases and by activity transitions. Report the distribution by `handover_cost_band` and complexity, not only the mean.
- **Reassignment count:** mean, median, p90, and share of cases above two reassignments. Compare before and after deployment and inspect the association with rework and appeals.
- **Skill mismatch:** share of performed events where the resource's skill is below the required threshold, plus separate counts for allowed supervised exceptions, unrecorded supervision, Security violations, and Approval finalization by skill 1 or 0. A supervised trainee must not be mislabeled as an unsupervised mismatch or as a specialist.
- **Workload distribution:** assignments, open cases, estimated hours, complexity-weighted work, and supervision hours per resource. Report max/min, p90-to-median, Gini or another agreed inequality measure, and the share exceeding four new assignments.
- **Overtime-risk exposure:** proportion of assignments and estimated hours sent to `overtime risk="high"`, split into ordinary and documented urgent exceptions. Also measure whether the policy reduced exposure without increasing SLA misses elsewhere.
- **SLA misses:** share and count of cases completed after `sla_deadline`, time-to-deadline at assignment, predicted versus realized breach rate, and urgent/high-priority slices. Include waiting time separately from service time.
- **Rework and appeal after assignment:** rework loops, corrective activities, appeals, and repeat contacts after the assignment, stratified by skill match, supervision, handover band, and reassignment count. This is an outcome association, not by itself proof that a resource caused the error.
- **Training assignment share:** among normal/low assignments with acceptable slack, the share matching a documented training priority or a recorded shadowing plan. Report the numerator, denominator, supervisor, completion quality, and whether training displaced an SLA-safe alternative.
- **Specialist bottlenecks:** queue age and waiting time by activity and skill, utilization of skill-3 resources, fraction of work sent to the top one or two specialists, concentration of assignments, number of qualified alternatives, and SLA risk while a specialist is unavailable.
- **Deviations from policy:** missing or contradictory `assignment_reason`, policy-version mismatch, assignments outside the candidate set, unrecorded supervision, override rate, override reasons, and the difference between recommended and final resource. Review overrides for fairness and recurring data-quality problems.

Useful fairness slices include team, resource tenure/skill level, complexity, priority, and current-owner status. A policy passes audit only if aggregate performance improves without hiding a rise in unsafe mismatches, concentration, or adverse workload exposure for a subgroup.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

The following is an audit-oriented sketch. The historical resource matrix and assignment decisions are joined to the event log before calculating the indicators. Exact parameter names can be pinned to the installed PM4Py version; the important point is to retain both the event log and the decision-time assignment snapshot.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# events contains the columns in the question plus any assignment/supervision fields.
events = pd.read_parquet("events.parquet")
events["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(events["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(events)

# The matrix is a controlled reference table, not inferred from a single event.
skill = {
    # resource: {activity: score}
    "R1_Amal": {"Technical Diagnosis": 1, "Eligibility Review": 3},
    # ...remaining resource/activity scores...
}

def required_score(row):
    return skill.get(row["org:resource"], {}).get(row["concept:name"], 0)

events["observed_skill"] = events.apply(required_score, axis=1)
events["skill_mismatch"] = (
    events["observed_skill"] < events["required_skill_score"]
)

# Reconstruct the policy's case-level output and compare it with the event log.
# These fields should have been written at dispatch time, not inferred afterward.
events["policy_deviation"] = (
    events["org:resource"] != events["policy_selected_resource"]
) & (~events["policy_override"].fillna(False))
events["unrecorded_supervision"] = (
    events["supervisor"].isna()
    & (events["required_skill_score"] < 2)
    & events["case_priority"].isin(["high", "urgent"])
)

# Resource-interaction views for handovers, collaboration, and role structure.
handover_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
activity_resource_similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)

# These can be visualized with pm4py.view_sna(...) or saved with pm4py.save_vis_sna(...).
# Use them to find isolated resources, over-central specialists, and potential mentors.

# Batch and performance views.
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)
service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(log)
log_with_wait = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log_with_wait)
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)

# Slice the same indicators by case attributes. The exact level argument may be
# named differently in a pinned PM4Py release.
urgent_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, "case_priority", ["urgent"], level="case"
)
complex_log = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    log, 0, 10**9, parameters={"case_id_key": "case:concept:name"}
)

# Simple event-log indicators; deadline and assignment snapshots must be aligned
# to the case or dispatch event before these calculations.
ordered = events.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
handover_events = ordered.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].diff().notna()
handover_rate = handover_events.groupby(
    ordered["case:concept:name"]
).sum().mean()

sla_miss = (
    events.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].max()
    > events.groupby("case:concept:name")["sla_deadline"].max()
)
training_cases = events["training_flag"].fillna(False)
training_share = (
    training_cases[events["case_priority"].isin(["normal", "low"])]
    .groupby(events.loc[training_cases.index, "case:concept:name"])
    .any()
    .mean()
)

audit = {
    "handover_rate": float(handover_rate),
    "skill_mismatch_rate": float(events["skill_mismatch"].mean()),
    "policy_deviation_rate": float(events["policy_deviation"].mean()),
    "sla_miss_rate": float(sla_miss.mean()),
    "training_share": float(training_share),
}
print(audit)
```

For a production audit, the policy simulator should replay each dispatch snapshot: filter hard-ineligible candidates, calculate the six score components, and compare the recommended candidate with `org:resource`. PM4Py then supplies the evidence about what happened after the decision—handover networks, collaboration, service and waiting time, variants, batches, performance DFGs, and attribute-filtered slices. The simulator and the log should be versioned together so a later change in weights does not make an old assignment appear to have followed a policy that did not exist at the time.
