# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ substantially: 78% for Group A versus 63%, 69%, and 58% for Groups B, C, and D. These figures identify disparities but do not establish discrimination because risk, documentation, channel, prior cases, and required routing differ.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity.** After adjustment, approval remains 5.5 percentage points lower for B, 2.2 points lower for C, and 3.1 points lower for D. The B difference is especially notable, but causal interpretation requires uncertainty estimates, model diagnostics, overlap checks, and scrutiny of whether controls such as risk and documentation are biased or affected by language barriers.

- **Procedural burden.** B and C experience support-related detours, more handovers, more document requests, and more supervisor review. Required support is legitimate and rights-preserving; the concern is that exercising those rights appears to impose additional queues and coordination costs.

- **Timeliness.** Adjusted cycle time is 2.6 days longer for B and 4.1 days longer for C. Specific waits—3.8 days after Language Support Contact and 4.7 days after Accommodation Review—point to capacity constraints rather than case complexity alone. Tail delays are also large: raw 90th-percentile times are 34.2 days for B and 38.8 days for C, versus 21.4 days for A.

- **Consistency of routing.** Fraud Review for high-risk cases, Accommodation Review when requested, and Language Support Contact when needed are policy-consistent. Fairness nevertheless requires that similarly situated cases receive these steps consistently and that unnecessary document requests, supervisor reviews, or region-specific handovers are not concentrated by group.

- **Quality and accessibility of support.** The log records whether support occurred, not whether it was timely, understandable, culturally and linguistically appropriate, or effective. A completed support event is therefore not proof of equitable access.

- **Appeal and rework burden.** B and D retain adjusted appeal differences of +4.4 and +5.7 points. B and C also have high raw reopen/rework rates. Appeals may reflect lower-quality decisions or explanations, inconsistent initial adjudication, or applicant disagreement; they can also reflect better access to appeal. The available measures do not distinguish these possibilities.

- **Resource-allocation fairness.** B is concentrated among seven bilingual workers and three overloaded regional officers; C among five specialists, two of whom also conduct supervisor review. Scarce specialist capacity and extra handovers create structurally unequal service conditions even if assignment rules contain no explicit group preference.

Overall, procedural fairness requires comparable burden, timeliness, consistency, access, and decision quality among cases with comparable relevant needs—not identical paths or outcomes for every group.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

1. **Define case-level treatment and outcomes.**
   - Group indicators are comparison attributes, not automatically causal “treatments.”
   - Outcomes should include approval, total and stage-specific delay, tail delay, document requests, supervisor/fraud review, handover count, rework, appeal, and activation after approval.
   - Separately assess whether required support was provided and whether it introduced excess delay.

2. **Match or stratify comparable cases.**
   Match or weight on:
   - risk-score band;
   - documentation completeness;
   - channel;
   - language preference/support need;
   - accommodation request;
   - household-size band;
   - prior-case count;
   - region and month;
   - relevant initial process state and, where appropriate, outcome-neutral complexity indicators.

   Use exact matching for policy-determinative variables where feasible, followed by propensity-score weighting or coarsened exact matching. Report common support and avoid extrapolation where, for example, few A cases resemble language-support cases.

3. **Handle support needs carefully.**
   Produce two estimands:
   - a **total disparity**, without controlling away language or accommodation needs and their consequences;
   - a **within-need procedural disparity**, comparing cases with similar needs, regions, channels, and complexity.

   Do not simply control for post-group mediators such as a potentially barrier-induced “incomplete documentation” status and call the remainder the full disparity.

4. **Compare paths and variants.**
   Within matched strata, compare:
   - probability of each variant;
   - extra activities and repetitions;
   - handover count;
   - time within the same variant;
   - outcomes within the same variant.

   Variant adjustment can reveal execution differences but may also condition on mediators. Present both pre-routing and within-path analyses.

5. **Compare resource assignment.**
   Estimate the probability of assignment to each worker, specialist team, overloaded officer, or region-specific queue conditional on need and complexity. Compare workload, experience, decision rates, service times, handovers, and queue delay. Use multilevel models with worker, region, and month effects rather than interpreting worker-level differences as misconduct.

6. **Decompose waiting time.**
   Split case duration into:
   - active service time;
   - queue/waiting time;
   - support-step delay;
   - fraud/supervisor delay;
   - rework and document-response time;
   - appeal time.

   Apply decomposition within matched strata and variants. Separate applicant-controlled response intervals, where reliably measured, from agency-controlled queue time.

7. **Estimate uncertainty.**
   Report confidence intervals, adjusted sample sizes, overlap, balance, multiple-testing corrections, and sensitivity to model form. Cluster uncertainty by worker or region where assignment and performance are correlated.

8. **Sensitivity analysis for suspect covariates.**
   - Re-estimate with and without `risk_score_band`.
   - Replace it, where possible, with its transparent inputs.
   - Reclassify or omit `documentation_completeness`.
   - Model documentation requests as a possible mediator.
   - Test plausible misclassification and unmeasured-confounding scenarios.
   - Examine whether risk predicts validated fraud equally across groups.

9. **Subgroup checks.**
   Report region-by-group and channel-by-group results, especially:
   - B in the two high-handover regions;
   - online versus in-person B cases;
   - C cases handled by dual-role versus other specialists;
   - D cases within identical risk bands.

Evidence of residual disparity is not by itself proof of discrimination. “Discrimination” requires stronger evidence that protected status, a proxy, or unjustified group-linked practices affect treatment. Legitimate routing explains some path differences, but does not justify avoidable delay produced by inadequate implementation.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Group B has the clearest language- and documentation-linked burden.** Only 19% follow the fast V1 path, compared with 42% of A. B is overrepresented in V2, involving missing documents, at 34% versus 24%, and in V4, involving language support and document requests, at 24% versus 1%. Some difference is expected because 57% need language support and 46% have incomplete documentation. However:

- adjusted Request Missing Documents probability remains 9.5 points higher;
- adjusted Supervisor Review remains 8 points higher;
- adjusted cycle time remains 2.6 days longer;
- the language-to-eligibility wait is 3.8 days;
- B cases are concentrated among seven bilingual workers and overloaded regional officers;
- two regions add general-to-bilingual-to-general handovers.

Thus, support routing itself is legitimate, but the added queues, repeat transfers, and residual document-request disparity remain concerning. Documentation status may also reflect inaccessible intake rather than applicant complexity.

**Group C bears the largest adjusted time penalty.** C has a 31% share in V5, compared with 2% for A, consistent with its 62% accommodation-request rate. The Accommodation Review is required and should not be removed. The concern is its implementation:

- V5 has a 21.6-day median;
- the Accommodation Review-to-Eligibility wait is 4.7 days;
- adjusted cycle time remains 4.1 days longer;
- five specialists handle these cases, and two also perform supervisor review;
- extra specialist-to-supervisor handovers may create queues or conflicts in capacity.

The routing difference is largely explicable by need; the waiting penalty and capacity design are not thereby justified.

**Group D’s fraud routing is more plausibly complexity-related but still requires audit.** D has 51% high-risk cases and 28% in V3, versus 9% for A. Its adjusted cycle-time difference is only 0.9 days, and the Risk Screening-to-Fraud Review wait is 2.8 days versus 2.2 days for A. This is broadly consistent with risk-based routing. Nevertheless, D retains a 3.1-point adjusted approval gap and a 5.7-point adjusted appeal gap. Because the risk score may encode historical disparities, policy consistency does not establish fairness.

**Appeal paths indicate downstream burden.** D has 13% in V6 and B 8%, versus A’s 4%; V6 has a 30.7-day median. Appeals naturally extend cycle time, so primary-processing time and appeal-inclusive time must be reported separately. The adjusted appeal gaps for B and D may indicate unclear explanations, initial-decision quality issues, or inconsistent application of criteria, but may also reflect differences in appeal access or case contestability.

**Regional resource concentration is an independent fairness concern.** The Supervisor Review-to-Decision wait is 1.9 days for B and 1.6 for C, versus 1.0 for A, with backlogs in two regions. If matched cases assigned to overloaded staff wait longer because support needs constrain assignment, the inequality arises from resource design even without biased individual decisions.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate complexity-based routing.** Higher measured risk sends more D cases to Fraud Review; accommodation requests send C cases to specialists; language needs send B cases to bilingual staff. These mechanisms can legitimately produce different paths.

2. **Specialist capacity bottlenecks.** Seven bilingual workers and five accommodation specialists create queues. Dual-role specialists may compound C’s delay when accommodation and supervisor workloads peak.

3. **Handover and coordination costs.** Generalist-to-specialist-to-generalist routing creates waiting, information loss, repeated checks, and possible document re-requests. This is especially plausible for B and C in the identified regions.

4. **Inaccessible intake and documentation processes.** Forms, instructions, or evidence standards may be harder to navigate in minority languages or with disabilities. Recorded incompleteness could then be partly caused by process design, not merely pre-existing case complexity.

5. **Regional or worker-level inconsistency.** Overloaded offices may use supervisor review, document requests, or cautious decisions more often. Concentrating B and C cases in these units can create group disparities without an explicit group-based rule.

6. **Historically biased risk measurement.** Prior enforcement, reporting, or case histories may raise D’s risk scores even when current underlying fraud propensity is comparable. A formally risk-based route can therefore reproduce historical inequity.

7. **Decision-explanation or communication deficiencies.** Higher adjusted appeal rates for B and D may result from poorly translated, inaccessible, or insufficiently reasoned notices, as well as from questionable decisions.

8. **Applicant response time.** Some delay after a document request may reflect time needed to obtain records. Even so, fairness questions remain if instructions, assistance, or deadlines are not equally accessible.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias.** Documentation completeness may be recorded inconsistently and may reflect language access, disability accessibility, channel design, or staff judgment. Treating it as an entirely pre-existing neutral control can hide process-created burden.

- **Historical bias in risk scores.** `risk_score_band` may incorporate prior-case count, historical investigations, regional enforcement patterns, or proxy variables. Its predictive validity and calibration must be tested by group; controlling for it can absorb rather than explain inequity.

- **Missing applicant needs.** The data do not capture disability type, requested accommodation, literacy, digital access, urgency, availability of records, interpreter dialect, communication preference, or whether support was offered versus requested.

- **Limited workload information.** Daily resource workload cannot identify intra-day queue order, concurrent duties, absence, case difficulty, or whether cases were actively worked. Apparent worker effects may therefore reflect unobserved load.

- **Timestamp semantics.** Event logs may not distinguish start from completion, agency waiting from applicant response time, batching, reopened work, or administrative timestamp delays.

- **Support quality is unobserved.** A Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review event says little about adequacy, timeliness, accessibility, continuity, dignity, or applicant comprehension.

- **Appeal quality is missing.** `appeal_flag` does not show grounds, representation, timeliness, withdrawal, reversal, remand, explanation quality, or whether applicants understood appeal rights. Higher appeal rates can indicate either worse initial decisions or better appeal access.

- **Outcome quality is narrow.** Approval does not capture benefit amount, partial approval, time to activation, accuracy, reasons, or respectful treatment.

- **Residual confounding and selection.** Region, channel, and support requests may themselves be shaped by access barriers. Some groups may be less able to request support, creating selection among observed support users.

- **Sparse comparisons.** Only 2% of A need language support and 4% request accommodation, limiting direct cross-group overlap for some comparisons.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation | Intended effect | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add bilingual and accommodation capacity, including cross-trained backup staff | Reduce specialist queues and dependence on a few workers | Hiring and training costs; low-volume languages may remain underserved; cross-training can reduce specialization quality if superficial |
| Introduce service-level targets for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review | Make rights-preserving steps subject to explicit timeliness accountability | Targets may encourage rushed or token support, gaming of timestamps, or prioritization of easy cases; quality measures must accompany speed |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers, such as specialist consultation without full reassignment or persistent case ownership | Reduce queue transfers, duplication, and information loss | Generalists may lack expertise; persistent ownership may create bottlenecks unless specialists remain readily available |
| Improve multilingual and accessible intake, document checklists, reminders, and proactive assistance | Reduce avoidable incompleteness and repeated document requests | Upfront processing may take longer; translation and accessibility maintenance require continuing review |
| Improve decision notices with plain language, accurate translations, accessible formats, reasons, and appeal guidance | Increase comprehension and potentially reduce avoidable appeals | Better notices may initially increase appeals by making rights understandable; this can be fairness-enhancing rather than a failure |
| Monitor adjusted disparities in paths, waits, handovers, document requests, appeals, and outcomes | Detect persistent inequity beyond raw outcome parity | Models may legitimize biased controls or generate false alarms; dashboards need uncertainty, overlap, and governance |
| Audit `risk_score_band` for calibration, false positives, historical proxies, and group-specific validity | Determine whether fraud routing is substantively justified | Altering thresholds may increase missed fraud or review volume; auditing requires reliable ground truth |
| Audit `documentation_completeness` definitions and staff consistency | Identify whether incompleteness is process-created or inconsistently coded | More standardized checks can become rigid and increase requests unless discretion and assistance are preserved |
| Rebalance caseloads across regions and protect specialist time | Reduce overload-driven disparities | Transfers can weaken local knowledge, create new handovers, or shift delays to other groups |
| Conduct structured review of appealed and reworked cases | Identify recurring errors and improve first-instance decisions | Review consumes capacity and may slow current cases unless separately resourced |

Support steps should be improved and adequately resourced, not removed to make cycle-time statistics appear equal.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **not demonstrably fair, but the evidence is insufficient to conclude intentional discrimination or that every disparity is unjustified**. Much of D’s fraud routing and B/C’s specialist routing is consistent with documented policy needs. However, B and C appear to bear avoidable burdens from scarce specialist capacity, extra handovers, and support-step queues; B also retains notable adjusted differences in document requests, review, approval, and appeal.

The most defensible conclusion is that procedural fairness is **indeterminate with material warning signs**, particularly for language-support and accommodation users. Stronger causal conclusions require matched within-need comparisons, regional and resource-level analysis, uncertainty estimates, and audits of risk and documentation measures.
