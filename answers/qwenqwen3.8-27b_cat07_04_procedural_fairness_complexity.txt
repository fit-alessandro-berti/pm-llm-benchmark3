# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A defensible analysis should distinguish four concepts:

| Concept | Meaning in this context |
|---|---|
| **Disparity** | An adjusted difference in outcomes, cycle time, waits, handovers, appeals, or rework between applicant groups. |
| **Legitimate routing** | A difference explained by policy-required steps or case complexity, such as fraud review for high risk, language support for minority-language applicants, or accommodation review for disability-related requests. |
| **Discrimination** | A difference that remains after accounting for legitimate routing and complexity, that is not operationally justified, and that is associated with group membership or a proxy for group membership. |
| **Uncertainty** | Ambiguity caused by small cells, missing data, biased covariates, observational design, or lack of quality measures. |

## Matching or stratification variables

Use a combination of exact stratification, propensity-score matching, or inverse-probability weighting.

Base covariates:

- `risk_score_band`
- `documentation_completeness`
- `channel`
- `household_size_band`
- `prior_case_count`
- `region`
- `month`

Additional stratification variables for legitimate routing:

- `language_preference` or “requires language support”
- `disability_accommodation_requested`
- high-risk flag derived from `risk_score_band`
- in-person vs online channel
- presence of Request Missing Documents
- presence of Supervisor Review, Fraud Review, Language Support Contact, or Accommodation Review

Important nuance: do not simply control away language or accommodation needs as if they were ordinary confounders. Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving steps. The fairness question is not whether those steps should exist, but whether they are delivered with comparable timeliness, continuity, quality, and burden.

For Group B, compare language-support cases to comparable language-support cases where possible, or to expected performance for comparable complexity. For Group C, compare accommodation-review cases to comparable accommodation-review cases. For Group D, compare high-risk cases to comparable high-risk cases.

- V1: standard fast path
- V2: Request Missing Documents path
- V3: Risk/Fraud/Supervisor path
- V4: Language Support path
- V5: Accommodation Review path
- V6: Appeal path

For each variant, compare:

- case share by group

- transition durations
- resource paths
- handover counts
- appeal and rework rates

This helps determine whether Group B’s slower experience is mainly due to the legitimate language-support path or due to avoidable delays within that path.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Compare resource assignment across groups using:

- handover-of-work networks
- working-together networks
- activity-based resource similarity
- organizational roles
- resource concentration metrics
- service time by resource
- handover count per case

Decompose total cycle time into:

4. Service time for Language Support Contact.

This decomposition is essential because a longer total cycle time may be caused by applicant document collection, legitimate review time, or system bottlenecks. The provided waiting-time summary suggests that support-step queues are a major source of delay for Groups B and C.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

`risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may be biased.

Run sensitivity analyses:

- Remove `risk_score_band` from the adjustment set.
- Remove `documentation_completeness` from the adjustment set.

The adjusted approval gap and high appeal rate suggest possible over-screening, poor explanation, or decision-quality issues. Fraud-review throughput should be checked for fairness. |
| **Cross-group** | V2 Request Missing Documents is common for Groups B, C, and D. Group B has the highest adjusted increase in Request Missing Documents. Group D has the highest appeal path share. | Document requests may be necessary when applications are incomplete. | If incomplete documentation is partly caused by inaccessible forms, language barriers, or lack of assistance, then document requests become a system-imposed burden rather than a neutral complexity factor. |

The most important procedural disparities are:

1. Group B language-support bottleneck and handover pattern.
2. Group C accommodation-review queue and specialist concentration.
3. Group D risk/fraud routing combined with high appeals.
4. Higher adjusted document-request and supervisor-review probabilities for Group B and Group C.

Some of these disparities may be justified by legitimate routing. However, the remaining adjusted gaps, especially for Group B, and the resource concentration for Groups B and C are concerning and should not be dismissed as mere case-mix differences.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate risk-based routing for Group D**  
   Higher measured risk scores route Group D more often to Fraud Review and Supervisor Review. This can explain part of the longer path and lower approval rate. However, this mechanism is only legitimate if the risk score is accurate, calibrated, and not a proxy for protected characteristics or historical disadvantage.

2. **Legitimate rights-based support for Groups B and C**  
   Minority-language applicants require Language Support Contact, and disability-accommodation applicants require Accommodation Review. These steps can legitimately increase process length and require specialized staff. The fairness question is whether the steps are timely, accessible, and not excessively burdensome.

3. **Documentation and channel complexity**  
   Group B has a higher share of incomplete documentation and in-person applications. This can increase Identity Verification waits, Request Missing Documents steps, and overall cycle time. This mechanism may be partly legitimate, but it becomes a fairness concern if incomplete documentation is caused by inaccessible forms, language barriers, or insufficient assistance.

4. **Capacity bottleneck and resource concentration**  
   Group B depends on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. Group C depends on 5 accommodation specialists. This can create long waits, handovers, and inconsistent treatment. This mechanism is concerning because it imposes delay and burden on applicants using rights-preserving support, even when the support itself is legitimate.

5. **Measurement bias in risk score or documentation completeness**  
   If `risk_score_band` or `documentation_completeness` is biased against certain groups, then the process may over-screen or over-request documents from those groups. This can generate lower approval rates, more supervisor review, more fraud review, and more appeals. This mechanism is concerning because it can make biased inputs look like neutral complexity.

6. **Cascading delays and appeal burden**  
   Early delays in language support, accommodation review, or fraud review can push cases into supervisor backlog, later decisions, and more appeals. This can make the process feel slower and less predictable for Groups B, C, and D. This mechanism may be operational rather than intentional, but it still affects procedural fairness.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available attributes are useful but insufficient for a definitive fairness conclusion.

- **Measurement bias in risk score**  
  `risk_score_band` may encode historical disparities, prior system contact, proxy variables for race, language, disability, or socioeconomic status. A high risk band may reflect prior adverse interactions rather than current fraud risk.

- **Measurement bias in documentation completeness**  
  `documentation_completeness` may be lower for applicants who face language barriers, digital access problems, disability-related barriers, or unclear forms. If the system does not provide accessible assistance, incomplete documentation may be a system-caused burden rather than an applicant-caused one.

- **Missing applicant needs**  
  The log does not capture actual language proficiency, preferred dialect, disability type, severity of accommodation need, interpreter quality, digital access, trust in the process, prior adverse experiences, or quality of communication.

- **Unobserved resource workload**  
  Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. The log may not capture task complexity, concurrent case load, breaks, training, regional backlog, queue position, or whether a caseworker is newly assigned to a task.

- **Appeal quality is not measured**  
  `appeal_flag` shows that an appeal occurred, but not why, whether the appeal succeeded, whether the original decision was poorly explained, or whether the applicant had access to legal or advocacy support.

- **Outcome quality is not measured**  
  `case_outcome` captures approval or denial, but not decision accuracy, quality of explanation, dignity of treatment, timeliness of benefit activation, or whether the applicant understood the decision.

- **Process data limitations**  
  Variants show activity sequences but not all timing, resource, or attribute detail. Small cells, especially for Group A language-support or accommodation cases, may make comparisons unstable. Observational adjustment cannot fully rule out omitted confounding.

- **No direct measure of discrimination**  
  The data can show disparity and possible procedural burden, but they cannot directly prove intent, bias, or discrimination without additional qualitative evidence, audits, and causal analysis.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

The goal should be to preserve rights-preserving support steps while reducing avoidable delay, handovers, and unequal burden.

| Mitigation option | Recommended action | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add capacity for language support or accommodation review** | Hire or cross-train additional bilingual caseworkers, accommodation specialists, and interpreters. Increase fraud-review capacity if Group D delays are substantial. | Higher cost and hiring time. May create new silos if not integrated with general casework. Cross-trained staff may have less expertise initially. Capacity alone may not fix routing or handover problems. |
| **Service-level targets for support steps** | Set separate timeliness targets for Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, Fraud Review, Request Missing Documents, and Supervisor Review. Monitor median and 90th percentile waits. | Targets may incentivize speed over quality if not paired with quality metrics. Complex cases may need exceptions. Poorly designed targets may create gaming or pressure to rush rights-preserving steps. |
| **Routing redesign to reduce handovers** | Use single-point-of-contact caseworkers with language or accommodation competence where possible. Use parallel processing, embedded specialists, or case conferences instead of repeated handovers. | May reduce specialization. General caseworkers may need more training and may face higher workload. Poorly designed redesign may weaken fraud detection or accommodation quality. Must preserve required support steps. |
| **Decision-explanation improvements** | Provide standardized, plain-language, multilingual, and accessible explanations. Explain missing-document requests, risk-based routing, supervisor review, appeal rights, and expected timelines. | Takes additional time and privacy care. May increase appeals if applicants expect changes. Explanations must be accurate and not reveal sensitive model details inappropriately. |
| **Monitoring adjusted disparities** | Build dashboards for adjusted approval, cycle time, support-step waits, handovers, appeals, rework, and resource concentration by group, region, channel, and variant. | Administrative burden. Small cells may create unstable estimates. Staff may game metrics if monitoring is not paired with quality and fairness audits. Numeric monitoring may miss dignity or explanation quality. |
| **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness** | Audit risk score calibration by group, region, channel, and outcome. Test documentation forms for language and accessibility barriers. Use human review for high-risk or high-disparity cases. | Cost and governance complexity. May reduce fraud detection if risk thresholds are changed without evidence. May face resistance from operational teams. Requires ongoing oversight and transparent reporting. |

Additional useful options include applicant communication improvements, parallel processing of support steps and eligibility assessment, and fast-track appeal review for cases with long prior delays. These should complement, not replace, the core mitigations above.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does not appear to be clearly procedurally fair, but the evidence is not strong enough to conclude that it is discriminatory. Some raw differences are plausibly explained by legitimate routing: Group D’s higher fraud-review share is consistent with higher measured risk, Group B’s language-support path is consistent with minority-language need, and Group C’s accommodation-review path is consistent with disability-accommodation requests.

However, the adjusted comparison still shows residual disparities, especially for Group B. Group B has a larger adjusted approval gap, higher adjusted document-request and supervisor-review probabilities, longer language-support waits, more handovers, and concentration on a small number of bilingual and regional staff. Group C shows a smaller adjusted approval gap but a larger adjusted cycle-time gap, long accommodation-review waits, and concentration on a small specialist team. Group D’s high appeal rate and adjusted approval gap are concerning, but they may partly reflect legitimate risk-based scrutiny; the risk score itself must be audited before that explanation can be accepted.

The most defensible conclusion is that the process is **indeterminate with material procedural fairness concerns**. The evidence supports targeted investigation of language-support capacity, accommodation-review capacity, handover patterns, risk-score bias, documentation-completeness bias, and decision-explanation quality. It does not support treating the process as fair merely because the raw differences are associated with case mix, nor does it support removing rights-preserving support steps. The priority should be to make those support steps timely, accessible, consistently resourced, and transparent.
