# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The evidence supports interpreting Mira_Coordinator as a high-leverage exception-routing actor, but not necessarily as a conventional team lead or sustained case worker. The role is ambiguous because different organizational networks measure different relationships: event succession, co-presence on cases, delegation loops, activity similarity, and time impact. 

## Handover-of-work network

A handover-of-work network connects resource A to resource B when A performs an event in a case and B performs a later event in the same case, often immediately afterward. For Mira_Coordinator, this network implies that she sits at a major routing point, especially between automation, specialists, and adjusters.

The strongest supporting observations are:

* Bot_Rules → Mira_Coordinator has 920 handovers, making her the main manual destination after automated exception routing.
* Mira_Coordinator hands work to Omar_Medical, Priya_Legal, Carla_Adjuster, Diego_Adjuster, Elise_Adjuster, and Farid_Adjuster.
* Her handover out-degree centrality is 0.82, in-degree centrality is 0.51, and handover betweenness is 0.77, all much higher than ordinary adjusters and specialists.
* During her absence, ordinary low-value claims were unaffected, but exception cases accumulated and their 90th percentile cycle time rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days.

This implies that Mira is structurally important for moving exception cases between automated routing, human review, specialists, and reassignment. It does **not** by itself prove that she is overloaded, causing delays, making final decisions, or exercising inappropriate discretion. Handover centrality can reflect useful coordination as well as bottleneck risk.

The automated resources matter here. Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules create a high-volume automated front end. Bot_Rules has high handover betweenness at 0.41, but minimal cycle-time contribution. Mira appears to absorb the part of the process that automation cannot resolve: exception desk routing, medical/legal routing, reopen handling, complaints, and clarification work.

## Working-together network

A working-together network connects two resources when they co-occur on the same case, usually without regard to direct event order. For Mira_Coordinator, this network implies that she is not embedded in stable case teams in the same way as Alice_TeamLead with Carla/Elise or Bruno_TeamLead with Diego/Farid.

The apparent contradiction is mostly measurement-based. Mira can be central in handovers while peripheral in working-together because she often performs short routing, assignment, reopen, clarification, or exception events and then disappears from the case for several later events. The prompt explicitly notes that her working-together co-occurrence is low because many of her events are short coordination events.

Her co-case rates with adjusters are only 6–8%, and with Alice/Bruno only 4–5%, despite many handovers. That means she often touches cases at transfer points rather than staying involved as a persistent case partner.

This does **not** imply that Mira is unimportant. It means her organizational role is episodic and cross-boundary rather than team-based. By contrast, Alice—Carla at 27%, Bruno—Diego at 26%, Carla—Elise at 20%, and Diego—Farid at 19% look like stable team or work-sharing structures.

## Subcontracting network

A subcontracting network with `n=2` captures patterns where resource A performs an event, resource B performs an intervening event, and resource A returns within two later events. This is useful for identifying delegation, consultation, review loops, or temporary outsourcing of work.

For Mira_Coordinator, the subcontracting evidence strongly suggests brokered consultation loops:

* Mira → Omar_Medical → Mira occurs 126 times.
* Mira → Priya_Legal → Mira occurs 98 times.
* Typical fragments include “Request Missing Information → Medical Review → Assign Specialist/Reopen” and “Escalate Complaint → Legal Review → Reopen/Assign Specialist.”
* Her subcontracting betweenness is 0.69, much higher than team leads, adjusters, temps, vendors, and specialists.

This implies that Mira may be dispatching hard cases to specialists and then receiving them back for the next routing or case-state decision. It does **not** prove she supervises Omar or Priya, nor that the specialists are formally subordinate to her. The same pattern could arise from an exception desk workflow, a specialist scheduling protocol, or a data-capture artifact where “Assign Specialist” is logged under Mira.

The vendor relationships should also be separated from Mira’s specialist-broker pattern. Temp_1 → Vendor_Verify → Temp_1 appears 68 times and suggests a document-verification loop. Vendor_Pay is a high-volume sink for Issue Payment, with high waiting-time contribution but no handover out-degree. These vendor/automation nodes form organizational boundaries distinct from Mira’s human exception brokerage.

## Performance/time statistics

Performance statistics imply that Mira’s role affects exception-case flow more than ordinary throughput. She touches only 22% of all claims but 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints. Her average cycle-time contribution is described as “high variance,” not simply high. During her six-day absence, low-value ordinary claims were stable, while exception cases accumulated and the tail of cycle time worsened.

This supports a targeted key-person-dependency interpretation: Mira is probably not the main driver of all process performance, but she is important for the long-tail cases that require exception handling, specialist routing, reopen management, or complaint escalation.

However, time statistics do not identify causality on their own. Exception cases are inherently more complex. Omar_Medical has a median service time of 37 minutes and high exception share of 0.62; Priya_Legal has 51 minutes and exception share of 0.71. Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay have high waiting-time components. Therefore, delays around Mira may reflect the complexity of cases she receives, downstream specialist capacity, vendor queues, or control requirements, not just Mira herself.

## Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

Activity-profile and role-discovery methods would likely separate Mira from standard adjusters, team leads, specialists, bots, temps, and vendors. Her activity mix includes Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Assign Specialist. This is unlike Omar_Medical and Priya_Legal, who perform narrow specialist reviews; unlike Vendor_Pay, which issues payments; unlike Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules, which automate ingestion/routing; and unlike Carla/Diego/Elise/Farid, who mainly assess damage, negotiate settlement, or request missing information.

Role discovery would probably identify Mira as an exception-handling coordinator or routing broker. But that conclusion would depend on the event labels. If “Assign Specialist” is logged under the person who clicks a queue action, role discovery may overstate her decision authority. If automated decisions are recorded under Bot_Rules and manual corrections under Mira, activity similarity could also exaggerate the contrast between automation and human exception handling.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

## Hypothesis 1: Formal coordinator / exception desk

### Mechanism

Mira_Coordinator acts as the formal manual exception desk after Bot_Rules. Routine cases go from Bot_Ingest to Bot_Rules and then to Alice/Bruno or vendors, while cases with ambiguity, missing information, complaints, reopenings, medical/legal complexity, or failed automation checks are routed to Mira.

### Evidence supporting it

Bot_Rules → Mira_Coordinator is a high-frequency handover with 920 cases and a short mean elapsed time of 3.5 minutes, consistent with automated exception routing. Mira’s activities include Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, and Assign Specialist. She touches 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, far above her 22% share of all claims. The process owner’s “queue cleaner” description also fits a formal exception-desk role, even if phrased dismissively.

### Evidence contradicting or limiting it

She is not merely a passive queue cleaner. Her high handover betweenness, high subcontracting betweenness, and loops with Omar_Medical and Priya_Legal suggest she may influence where hard cases go next. Adjusters describe her as “the person who knows where hard cases should go,” which implies expertise and judgment beyond simple queue cleanup.

### Observable predictions in future event logs

Future logs should show Mira’s activity concentrated immediately after Bot_Rules exceptions, reopen events, complaint escalations, and missing-information loops. Her cases should have higher exception_flag rates than ordinary team-lead cases. If this hypothesis is correct, her routing decisions should follow relatively stable rules based on case_type, priority, amount_band, region, channel, and exception_flag.

## Hypothesis 2: Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

### Mechanism

Mira may be the only or primary person able to resolve ambiguous routing and exception decisions. Even if her events are short, many exception cases may wait for her judgment, creating hidden dependency and queue accumulation when she is absent.

### Evidence supporting it

Her handover out-degree centrality is 0.82, handover betweenness is 0.77, and subcontracting betweenness is 0.69. During her six-day absence, ordinary low-value claims did not change, but exception cases accumulated and the 90th percentile cycle time for exception_flag=True cases increased from 8.4 to 13.9 days. That pattern is consistent with a key-person dependency concentrated in exception handling.

### Evidence contradicting or limiting it

Her median service time is only 4.2 minutes, which suggests the work she records is brief. High tail cycle times may come from downstream specialist capacity, vendor delay, missing claimant information, legal/medical complexity, or compliance controls. Also, she touches only 22% of all claims, so she is not a system-wide bottleneck for ordinary claims.

### Observable predictions in future event logs

If Mira is a bottleneck, periods with high Mira queue length or absence should predict longer waiting times before specialist assignment, reopen resolution, or complaint progression. Work-in-progress before Mira should correlate with exception-case cycle time. Substitution by another coordinator should reduce tail delays if the dependency is organizational rather than case-inherent.

## Hypothesis 3: Organizational boundary spanner or triage broker

### Mechanism

Mira links otherwise separate organizational zones: automation, team leads, adjusters, medical specialist, legal specialist, temporary staff, and exception handling. She does not “work together” with them in stable teams; instead, she routes cases across boundaries when the normal path is insufficient.

### Evidence supporting it

Mira receives work from Bot_Rules, Temp_1, Temp_2, Omar_Medical, and Priya_Legal, then sends work to specialists and adjusters. Her co-case rates with adjusters and team leads are low, yet direct handovers are frequent. This is exactly the signature of a boundary spanner: many transitions, low durable co-working. The adjusters’ statement that she knows where hard cases should go also supports a broker role.

### Evidence contradicting or limiting it

Boundary-spanning may be partly an artifact of how events are logged. “Assign Specialist” could be a system activity attributed to Mira even when the true routing rule is standardized or automated. Also, Alice and Bruno also perform routing to Carla/Diego and approvals, so Mira is not the only broker in the process; she may be a broker specifically for exceptions.

### Observable predictions in future event logs

Future logs should show Mira connecting cases that move between activity families: fraud, missing information, medical review, legal review, complaint escalation, and reopen. Network community detection should place her between clusters rather than inside the North or South team communities.

## Hypothesis 4: Quality gatekeeper or control point

### Mechanism

Mira may detect incomplete, inconsistent, or risky cases and ensure they go to the appropriate specialist before settlement or closure. Her role may protect quality and compliance by preventing hard cases from being processed through routine adjuster paths.

### Evidence supporting it

She is involved in complaints, reopenings, specialist assignment, and missing-information work. Exception_flag=True cases are overrepresented in her work, with a 0.48 exception share. Her absence led to accumulation of exception cases rather than ordinary cases, suggesting that her work is tied to quality-sensitive deviations.

### Evidence contradicting or limiting it

The evidence does not show final outcomes, error rates, audit results, or improper payments. We cannot conclude that Mira improves quality, only that she is associated with exception flow. The compliance reviewer’s concern also means the same gatekeeping role could create uncontrolled discretion if not governed.

### Observable predictions in future event logs

If Mira is a quality gatekeeper, cases routed through her should have fewer downstream reopenings, fewer complaints after closure, or fewer compliance defects than comparable exception cases not routed through her. Conversely, if she merely shifts work, those quality improvements may not appear after adjustment for case complexity.

## Hypothesis 5: Data artifact or logging convention

### Mechanism

Mira’s centrality may partly result from event attribution. Short system actions such as “Assign Specialist,” “Reopen Claim,” or “Request Missing Information” may be logged under Mira because she owns a queue or user account, even when the actual decision is made elsewhere or follows a standard rule.

### Evidence supporting it

Her median service time is short, her working-together co-occurrence is low, and she often appears immediately after Bot_Rules and then disappears for several events. These patterns could arise from queue operations rather than substantive case handling.

### Evidence contradicting or limiting it

The absence-period evidence argues against a pure artifact. If she were only a logging placeholder, her absence should not cause exception queues to accumulate unless no substitute performed the same logged actions. Adjuster testimony also suggests she has real practical knowledge.

### Observable predictions in future event logs

If centrality is a logging artifact, replacing the logged user or changing the assignment mechanism would transfer the same network pattern to another account without changing performance. Event comments, system audit logs, or assignment-rule metadata would show standardized decisions rather than discretionary judgment.

# 3. Risk assessment

## Bottleneck risk

There is a meaningful bottleneck risk for exception cases, not for all claims. The strongest evidence is the six-day absence: ordinary low-value claims were stable, but exception queues accumulated and the 90th percentile cycle time for exception cases rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days. This suggests that Mira’s function is capacity-sensitive in the process tail.

The risk should not be overstated. Her events are short, and the specialist/vendor nodes also have substantial time contributions. Priya_Legal, Omar_Medical, Vendor_Verify, and Vendor_Pay may all contribute to delays. Mira may be where delayed exception cases pass through, rather than the root cause of delay.

## Coordination risk

Mira appears to coordinate across automation, adjusters, specialists, complaints, reopenings, and missing-information loops. This creates value but also fragility. If her routing logic is informal or undocumented, other staff may not know how to continue the exception process consistently.

Coordination risk is especially high because working-together centrality is low. She may not be embedded enough in stable teams for knowledge transfer to happen naturally.

## Key-person risk

The absence evidence strongly suggests a key-person risk. Even if she is not a formal team lead, the process may depend on her tacit knowledge of where hard cases should go. The process owner’s “queue cleaner” description and adjusters’ “person who knows where hard cases should go” description are not contradictory; together, they suggest an undervalued operational role.

The key-person risk is not that Mira is doing something wrong. The risk is that the organization may not have a backup, documented routing criteria, or cross-trained coordinator pool.

## Compliance/control risk

The compliance concern is plausible but unproven. Mira may be both detecting problems and selecting the next specialist. In a regulated claims process, that can create uncontrolled discretion if there are no clear decision rules, audit trails, segregation of duties, or review thresholds.

The evidence does not show misconduct, bias, or policy violation. It only shows that one person is central in exception routing and specialist selection. That is enough to justify control review, not enough to accuse her of inappropriate behavior.

## Workload fairness risk

Mira handles a disproportionate share of reopened claims, complaints, and exception cases. That may create stress, invisible labor, or career unfairness if her work is treated as low-status queue cleaning while requiring expert judgment. There is also fairness risk for adjusters and specialists if Mira’s routing criteria unintentionally allocate difficult cases unevenly across Carla, Diego, Elise, Farid, Omar, and Priya.

The current evidence does not prove unfair allocation. Future analysis should compare complexity-adjusted assignment patterns by resource, region, case type, channel, priority, and amount_band.

## Data-quality risk

Several risks are possible:

* Short routing events may understate actual cognitive work.
* “Assign Specialist” may conflate administrative clicking with substantive judgment.
* Working-together co-occurrence may understate coordination that happens through queues, messages, or phone calls outside the event log.
* Automated and vendor resources may distort human-resource centrality if bot/vendor events are included without interpretation.
* exception_flag may be both a routing driver and an outcome of later case handling, so analyses must avoid assuming it is purely exogenous.

These data-quality risks mean the networks should be treated as diagnostic evidence, not direct proof of organizational reality.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

Management should not remove Mira_Coordinator. The absence-period evidence suggests that the coordination function is operationally necessary for exception cases. Removing her without replacement would likely increase formal escalations, specialist overload, and exception-case tail delays.

Immediate changes should focus on making the function safer and more resilient:

* Document the main routing criteria for medical, legal, adjuster, reopen, and complaint paths.
* Create a backup exception-coordinator rota so that Mira is not a single point of failure.
* Add lightweight audit logging for specialist assignment decisions, especially when cases involve high amount bands, complaints, reopenings, or exception_flag=True.
* Clarify whether Mira is acting as queue administrator, triage broker, quality gatekeeper, or delegated decision-maker in each activity.
* Ensure Bot_Rules exception reasons are visible to Mira and auditable after routing.

These changes preserve the coordination function while reducing dependency and control risk.

## What should be monitored

Management should monitor exception-case queues, not only average cycle time. The ordinary low-value median did not change during Mira’s absence, so global averages may hide the problem.

Recommended monitoring indicators:

* Queue length and waiting time for exception_flag=True cases.
* 90th and 95th percentile cycle time for reopened claims and complaints.
* Bot_Rules → Mira volume and elapsed time.
* Mira → Omar/Priya handover volume, waiting time, and return loops.
* Distribution of Mira-routed cases across Carla, Diego, Elise, Farid, Omar, and Priya.
* Specialist overload after Mira routing.
* Reopen and complaint rates after different routing paths.
* Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay waiting-time contributions, so Mira is not blamed for vendor delays.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

Management should not demote, bypass, automate away, or remove Mira’s role based only on high network centrality. High centrality is not automatically bad; in this case it may reflect useful exception coordination.

Management should also avoid immediately converting all Mira decisions into rigid automation. Some exception handling may require human judgment, especially for complaints, reopenings, legal review, and medical review. Automation should be considered only after routing criteria and outcome impacts are better understood.

Finally, management should not assume that Mira is the root cause of delays. The evidence also points to specialist complexity, vendor waiting time, automated exception routing, and case-mix effects.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator appears to be a central exception-routing actor, not a conventional team lead. She is highly central in handover and subcontracting networks because cases often pass through her when automation, missing information, complaints, reopenings, medical review, or legal review require routing. She is peripheral in the working-together network because she usually performs short coordination actions and then leaves the case, rather than staying as a stable case partner.

The evidence supports several interpretations at once: formal exception desk, boundary-spanning triage broker, quality gatekeeper, and possible key-person dependency. Her six-day absence is especially important: ordinary low-value claims were unaffected, but exception cases accumulated and high-percentile cycle times worsened.

Management should not remove her role. Instead, it should document routing rules, create backups, add audit trails for specialist assignment, and monitor exception-case queues and tail cycle times. The goal is to preserve useful coordination while reducing bottleneck, compliance, and key-person risks.
