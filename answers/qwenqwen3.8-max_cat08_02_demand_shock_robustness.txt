# 1. Stress scenarios

The normal-period averages are not a safe design basis. In the peak period, weekly volume rises from about 2,300 cases/week to 3,300 cases/week, but the mix becomes much more hostile: Urgent + Complex-panel rises from about 31% to 49% of cases, rework rises from 7.5% to 11.8%, and the share of cases experiencing at least one queue over 12 hours rises from 22% to 54%. The p90 cycle times also show that the tail, not the median, is the operational risk: Urgent p90 is already 19h in normal periods, above the 18h contractual threshold, and reaches 33h in peak.

I define four stress scenarios that vary by mix and disruption type, not just total volume.

| Scenario | Main stress | Plausible shape | Evidence from logs / metrics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1. Winter infection wave: demand mix shift** | Higher volume plus shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases | Volume +40% to +60%; Urgent + Complex-panel = 45% to 55%; repeat rate 9% to 12%; pre-analytical defect rate 10% to 14% | Peak period already shows 49% Urgent + Complex-panel, pre-analytical defect rate about 12.3%, medical validation queue 22h, specialist review queue 36h |
| **S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage** | Loss or slowdown of assay capacity, especially M2, with repeat amplification | One machine down, or throughput reduced 20% to 40%; repeat-after-failure rate rises, especially after night shifts; urgent and complex still arrive | Assay utilization reaches 98% in peak; assay queue rises to 18h; repeat assay loop exists in DFG; Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and cluster after M2 usage |
| **S3. Medical validator or specialist-review absence** | Validation tail capacity collapses while Urgent and Complex-panel demand remains high | One or two medical validators absent; specialist unavailable, e.g. every second Friday; Urgent and Complex-panel demand at peak level | Medical validators reach 93% peak utilization and 22h peak median queue; specialist review reaches 87% peak utilization and 36h peak median queue; Complex-panel cases are much more likely to need medical validation and specialist review |
| **S4. Courier arrival compression or delay** | Samples arrive late or in compressed waves, creating front-end shock and uneven downstream loading | Couriers delayed by weather, traffic, or infection-wave volume; 60% to 70% of daily samples arrive in two compressed windows; urgent samples arrive with part of their SLA clock already consumed | Courier intake spikes at 10:00 and 17:00; courier intake queue rises from 1.0h to 7.5h; Register order -> Receive sample rises from 5h to 8h; Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check rises from 2h to 9h |

A robust redesign must therefore survive: high urgent/complex mix, assay instability, validation-tail absence, and arrival variability. Optimizing the average normal-period flow would miss all four failure modes.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## S1. Demand mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases

Likely bottleneck sequence:

1. **Courier intake and registration**  
   Peak registration utilization rises to 85% and queue to 2.8h. If registration is automated or staffed up without controlling the next step, more orders move quickly into sample receipt and pre-analytical check.

2. **Pre-analytical check**  
   Pre-analytical check is already at 96% peak utilization with a 9.5h median queue. The peak DFG shows a higher defect rate: direct recollection plus manual accession correction is about 12.3% of received samples, versus about 7.0% in normal periods. Recollection loops are long: Request recollection -> Receive sample is 42h normal and 60h peak. These loops consume clerical time and reduce effective intake capacity.

3. **Accession correction and test preparation**  
   Only two accession correction specialists exist. Their queue rises from 3.0h to 14.0h. Manual accession correction -> Test preparation rises from 5h to 16h. Preparation queue rises to 11.0h. If accession correction is relieved without controlling preparation, more cases pile up at preparation benches.

4. **Assay machines and batch wait**  
   Assay utilization reaches 98%, queue reaches 18h. Batch wait becomes more common: Test preparation -> Batch wait increases from 760 to 1,180 frequency units, and Batch wait -> Assay run median rises from 9h to 22h. Urgent bypass of batch wait falls from 74% to 58%, meaning urgent cases increasingly compete with batch scheduling and fragment machine use.

5. **Technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review**  
   Technical validators reach 101% peak utilization and 16h queue. Medical validation queue reaches 22h. Specialist review queue reaches 36h, and Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation rises from 18h to 48h. Complex-panel cases are disproportionately affected because they are more likely to need medical validation and specialist review.

Migration risk:

- Automating registration only, I8, can push more work into pre-analytical check and accession correction.
- Early defect triage, I6, can reduce late loops but may increase immediate recollection or cancellation workload if not paired with physician/courier feedback.
- Larger routine batches, I2, can improve preparation efficiency but create larger waves into assay machines and then technical validation.
- Strict Urgent-first dispatch, I1, can improve Urgent medians but starve Routine and Complex-panel cases, increasing total WIP and eventually overloading medical validation with urgent cases while complex cases sit in specialist review.
- Adding evening medical validation, I5, helps urgent release but may simply move the queue to specialist review for Complex-panel cases.

## S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage

Likely bottleneck sequence:

1. **Assay capacity becomes the constraint**  
   If M2 degrades or fails, Routine volume may be pushed toward M1. If M1 degrades, the Urgent/Complex fast lane is directly damaged. Assay queue, already 18h in peak, can rise sharply.

2. **Upstream WIP accumulates at preparation and batch wait**  
   Test preparation and batch wait become holding areas. Batch wait -> Assay run is already 22h peak. If assay capacity drops, this queue lengthens quickly. Larger batches may look efficient at preparation but increase batch delay.

3. **Repeat-after-failure amplifies the loss**  
   Repeat assay cases consume assay capacity twice. The DFG shows a repeat loop: Assay run -> Repeat assay and Repeat assay -> Assay run. Peak repeat activity is higher than normal despite fewer total cases. Repeat -> Assay run median rises from 10h to 26h. Repeats clustered after night shifts and M2 usage mean that a fragile M2 can create a repeat spiral.

4. **Irregular output hits technical validation**  
   When machines recover or partial batches complete, technical validation receives uneven waves. Technical validators are already overloaded in peak, at 101% utilization and 16h queue.

5. **Medical validation remains the final constraint for Urgent and Complex-panel**  
   Even if assay output is restored, Urgent and Complex-panel cases still need medical validation. Medical validation queue can remain high, especially if specialist review is also constrained.

Migration risk:

- Increasing batch size, I2, during an assay constraint can worsen batch wait and create lumpy output.
- Strict Urgent-first, I1, may protect Urgent cases temporarily but causes Routine and some Complex-panel cases to age, increasing total WIP and later creating a recovery wave.
- Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex, I3, protects critical cases, but if M2 receives all Routine volume while unstable, repeats may increase. If M2 fails completely, Routine volume stops.
- Adding preparation capacity without assay capacity simply moves queue from preparation to batch wait.
- When assay capacity recovers, technical validation can become the next bottleneck unless validation capacity is flexed.

## S3. Medical validator or specialist-review absence

Likely bottleneck sequence:

1. **Technical validation may still produce output**  
   Technical validation can continue for a while, especially if assay output is stable. But the downstream destination is constrained.

2. **Medical validation queue grows**  
   Technical validation -> Medical validation rises from 8h to 24h peak. Medical validation queue reaches 22h. Urgent cases, which require medical validation, become exposed to 18h contractual penalties.

3. **Complex-panel cases accumulate in specialist review**  
   Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation rises from 18h to 48h peak. Specialist review queue reaches 36h. Because Complex-panel cases are 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist review, a specialist absence creates a disproportionate tail delay.

4. **Upstream effects appear later**  
   If medical validation and specialist review remain blocked, WIP accumulates after technical validation. Eventually, lab staff may slow technical validation or preparation because there is no place to move completed work.

Migration risk:

- Moving technical validators to accession correction, I4, during a medical-validation shortage can reduce technical validation capacity without solving the medical bottleneck.
- Adding medical validation capacity, I5, helps Urgent cases but may not help Complex-panel cases if the constraint is specialist review.
- Outsourcing Complex-panel overflow, I10, can relieve specialist review but introduces 12h transport delay, traceability risk, and result reconciliation work.
- Prioritizing Urgent cases at medical validation can protect contractual SLAs but starve Complex-panel and Routine cases, increasing their p90 delays.

## S4. Courier arrival compression or delay

Likely bottleneck sequence:

1. **Arrival spike or delayed arrival consumes SLA clock**  
   Courier intake queue rises to 7.5h peak. If Urgent samples are delayed in transit, the lab has less remaining time to process them.

2. **Registration and sample receipt become unstable**  
   Register order -> Receive sample rises from 5h to 8h peak. Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check rises from 2h to 9h. If registration is automated, more orders flow into receipt and pre-analytical check.

3. **Pre-analytical defects increase and loops amplify**  
   Compressed arrivals make pre-analytical check more error-prone. Recollection loops already take 60h peak and cancellations after recollection rise from 290 to 370 in the peak DFG. Clerks spend time explaining errors to physicians, reducing available intake capacity.

4. **Downstream scheduling becomes lumpy**  
   Preparation and assay machines receive uneven supply. Batch wait increases, and urgent bypass becomes harder to maintain. Assay schedules fragment.

5. **Validation tail receives waves**  
   Once the compressed intake is processed, technical and medical validation receive clustered output. If the arrival spike occurs late in the day, evening validation coverage becomes critical.

Migration risk:

- Smoothing courier slots, I7, helps but may be outside lab control during severe weather or infection-wave logistics failures.
- Automating registration, I8, can expose pre-analytical check and accession correction.
- Early defect triage, I6, can prevent downstream loops but may increase immediate recollection workload and physician callbacks.
- Expediting late Urgent samples can displace Routine and Complex-panel cases, increasing total WIP unless priority rules are capped.

# 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign should not be “add capacity everywhere.” It should stabilize the front door, separate case classes, protect the validation tail, reduce repeat demand, and use conditional overflow. The package below is intended to be robust across multiple scenarios.

## 3.1 Front-door stabilization: I6 + I7 + I8

**Intervention**

- Use I8 to automate registration for orders with complete digital metadata.
- Use I7 to shift courier pickup slots and smooth the 10:00 and 17:00 arrival peaks.
- Use I6 for early pre-analytical defect triage so poor samples are rejected or flagged within 2 hours rather than discovered late.
- Add a feedback loop to couriers and physicians for recurring defect causes.

**Why robust**

This reduces variability before it propagates. The DFG shows that recollection loops are long and consume capacity: Request recollection -> Receive sample is 42h normal and 60h peak. Reducing late defects protects pre-analytical check, accession correction, preparation, and assay capacity.

**Assumptions**

- Digital metadata is complete enough for automated registration to be safe.
- Couriers can adjust pickup slots at least partially.
- Early defect triage criteria are clinically safe and not overly aggressive.

**Where it may fail / downstream overload**

- If only registration is automated, pre-analytical check becomes the next bottleneck.
- If early rejection is too aggressive, recollection and cancellation workload may rise, and physician dissatisfaction may increase.
- If courier smoothing fails due to external logistics, the lab still receives compressed late batches.

## 3.2 Case-class lanes with SLA-based dynamic dispatch, modified I1

**Intervention**

Do not use a blunt strict Urgent-first rule. Instead, create four operational lanes:

1. **Urgent lane**  
   No routine batch wait. Target single-piece flow. Reserved assay slots and reserved medical validation slots.

2. **Complex-panel lane**  
   Grouped panel scheduling to reduce setup and specialist-review fragmentation. Reserved specialist-review windows. Overflow triage for outsourcing when needed.

3. **Routine lane**  
   Batched for efficiency, but with maximum batch age and SLA aging. Routine cases should not be allowed to silently exceed the 72h target.

4. **Repeat-after-failure lane**  
   Protected rerun windows and root-cause review, especially for M2 and night-shift repeats.

Dispatch priority should be based on remaining SLA time, case type, setup cost, and downstream availability. Urgent cases get priority, but not unlimited priority. WIP caps should prevent urgent cases from flooding medical validation.

**Why robust**

This avoids the failure of optimizing only one case type. Strict Urgent-first can improve Urgent median time but increase total WIP and harm Complex-panel cases. Dynamic dispatch protects Urgent SLAs while preventing Routine and Complex-panel starvation.

**Assumptions**

- Real-time SLA clocks are available.
- Case classification is reliable and not gamed.
- Dispatch rules can be enforced at preparation, assay, and validation.

**Where it may fail / downstream overload**

- If Urgent cases are pushed into assay without reserved medical validation capacity, medical validation becomes overloaded.
- If WIP caps are too tight, throughput may fall.
- If Urgent classification is inflated, the priority system loses meaning.

## 3.3 Adaptive batching and machine allocation, guarded I2 + I3

**Intervention**

- Keep default Routine batch size at 12 during peak instability.
- Increase batch size to 18 or 24 only when Urgent queue is low, M2 is stable, and batch wait is below threshold.
- Reserve M1 primarily for Urgent, Complex-panel, repeats, and high-risk assays.
- Route Routine bulk to M2 only when M2 quality metrics are stable.
- Maintain a high Urgent bypass rate for batch wait, targeting at least the normal-period level of about 74% and avoiding the peak drop to 58%.
- Set maximum batch wait targets, for example 6h normal and 10h peak for Routine batches, with exceptions only under declared emergencies.

**Why robust**

This captures some batching efficiency without allowing Routine batches to delay Urgent cases. It also protects critical cases from M2 instability. Because Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after M2 and night shifts, M2 should not blindly absorb all Routine volume during high-load periods.

**Assumptions**

- M1 has enough capacity to handle Urgent, Complex-panel, and repeats when M2 is unstable.
- M2 can be stabilized with better QC and preventive maintenance.
- Scheduling can enforce batch age limits.

**Where it may fail / downstream overload**

- Larger batches create larger output waves into technical validation.
- If M2 becomes unstable, Routine volume can generate more repeats.
- If M1 fails, Urgent and Complex-panel capacity is directly impaired.
- If batch age limits are ignored, Routine p90 can breach the 72h target badly, as seen in peak where Routine p90 reaches 120h.

## 3.4 Flexible validation tail: I5 + guarded I4 + specialist coverage + I10

**Intervention**

- Use I5 to add a temporary evening medical-validation shift during peaks.
- Use I4 to cross-train two technical validators for accession correction, but only under guardrails: they should be deployed to accession correction only when technical validation queue is low enough and medical validation is not the active constraint.
- Provide remote or on-call specialist review coverage for known absence periods, such as every second Friday.
- Use I10 selectively for Complex-panel overflow when specialist review queue is high and the case has enough slack to tolerate the 12h transport delay.

**Why robust**

The peak data shows that the validation tail is often the real constraint: medical validation queue 22h, specialist review queue 36h, Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation 48h. A front-door-only redesign would simply move cases faster into this tail.

**Assumptions**

- Cross-trained technical validators can perform accession correction without causing validation errors.
- Evening medical validators can maintain quality despite fatigue risk.
- Partner lab outsourcing has acceptable traceability and result reconciliation.

**Where it may fail / downstream overload**

- I4 can create a technical validation shortage if used during assay recovery waves.
- I5 can increase release volume but may not solve Complex-panel delay if specialist review is missing.
- I10 can relieve internal specialist review but creates transport delay, chain-of-custody complexity, and integration work.

## 3.5 Repeat suppression and machine reliability program

**Intervention**

- Investigate Repeat-after-failure clustering after night shifts and M2 usage.
- Add targeted QC checks, calibration checks, and preventive maintenance for M2.
- Create rerun rules that distinguish true repeats from avoidable repeats.
- Reserve some repeat capacity so repeats do not constantly displace new Urgent or Complex-panel work.

**Why robust**

Repeats consume assay capacity twice and increase technical validation workload. Reducing avoidable repeats is equivalent to adding assay capacity without buying machines.

**Assumptions**

- The repeat problem has identifiable causes, e.g. M2 instability, night-shift staffing, sample quality, or assay setup.
- QC changes do not excessively increase assay time.

**Where it may fail / downstream overload**

- Stricter QC may initially increase detected repeats.
- If repeats fall sharply, more cases may move into technical and medical validation, requiring dispatch control.

## 3.6 Controlled use of routine release batching, conditional I9

I9 should not be a blanket policy. Holding Routine releases until a batch report is ready can reduce release work, but it adds waiting and can hide WIP. It should only be used when Routine cases have sufficient slack to the 72h target and when release/administrative load is truly constraining. Urgent and Complex-panel cases should never be included in routine release holds.

**Where it may fail**

- Routine p90 is already 120h in peak, so blanket release holds can cause severe SLA breaches.
- It can make the process look better at release while increasing hidden queue time.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

Even after the redesign, several fragilities remain.

1. **Medical validation and specialist review remain the deepest tail risk.**  
   The redesign reduces pressure on the tail but does not eliminate the structural scarcity of medical validators and specialist reviewers. If S1 and S3 occur together, a winter wave plus specialist absence, Urgent penalties and Complex-panel delays can still occur.

2. **M1 becomes a protected but critical single point.**  
   Reserving M1 for Urgent, Complex-panel, and repeats is necessary, but if M1 fails, the most time-critical work is directly affected. The redesign reduces this risk but does not remove it.

3. **M2 dependence for Routine volume remains risky.**  
   Routing Routine cases to M2 improves M1 availability, but M2 has a higher failure rate and is associated with repeat clusters. If M2 stability does not improve, Routine volume can generate more repeats and rework.

4. **Outsourcing Complex-panel cases creates external dependency.**  
   I10 can relieve specialist review, but it introduces transport delay, traceability risk, result reconciliation work, and partner-lab capacity risk. It is not a safe default for urgent complex cases because of the 12h transport delay.

5. **Dynamic priority can be gamed or misapplied.**  
   If clinicians or order entry systems inflate Urgent classification, the priority system degrades. If dispatchers override WIP caps too often, Urgent cases can overload medical validation.

6. **Redesign-induced vulnerability: batch holds and release batching can hide WIP.**  
   Larger Routine batches and conditional I9 release holds can make the lab look operationally smooth while Routine cases age silently. This can cause sudden 72h SLA breaches if batch age limits are not enforced.

7. **Redesign-induced vulnerability: cross-training can rob one bottleneck to feed another.**  
   Using technical validators for accession correction, I4, can relieve accession correction but create a technical validation shortage. This is especially dangerous during assay recovery waves or medical validation surges.

8. **Front-door controls depend on external partners.**  
   Courier slot smoothing and early defect rejection depend on courier behavior and physician response. In severe winter conditions, arrivals may still be compressed and late.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

During peak periods, the daily dashboard should focus on leading indicators, not only lagging cycle-time averages. Lagging metrics tell us what already happened; leading metrics tell us where the next bottleneck will form.

| **Courier arrival concentration / intake queue** | Detects front-door compression before pre-analytical overload | Green: < 35% of samples in top 2-hour window and intake queue < 2h; Amber: 35% to 50% or queue 2h to 4h; Red: > 50% or queue > 4h |
| **Pre-analytical defect and recollection rate** | Early signal of recollection loops and clerical capacity loss | Green: < 8%; Amber: 8% to 12%; Red: > 12% |
| **Accession correction queue** | Two specialists can block many downstream cases | Green: < 6h; Amber: 6h to 10h; Red: > 10h |
| **Urgent bypass rate and Urgent batch-wait time** | Shows whether Urgent cases are being fragmented by batching | Green: bypass >= 70% and Urgent batch wait < 2h; Amber: bypass 60% to 70% or wait 2h to 4h; Red: bypass < 60% or wait > 4h |
| **Assay availability, assay queue, and repeat rate** | Detects machine constraint and repeat amplification before validation overload | Green: availability > 90%, assay queue < 8h, repeat rate < 8%; Amber: availability 80% to 90%, queue 8h to 14h, repeat rate 8% to 12%; Red: availability < 80%, queue > 14h, repeat rate > 12% |
| **Technical validation queue** | Detects wave formation after assay output | Green: < 8h; Amber: 8h to 14h; Red: > 14h |
| **Medical validation queue for Urgent/Complex and specialist review queue** | Direct leading indicator of SLA risk at the tail | Medical queue: Green < 4h, Amber 4h to 8h, Red > 8h. Specialist queue: Green < 12h, Amber 12h to 24h, Red > 24h |

## Lagging outcome metrics

These should still be reviewed daily, but they should not be the primary control metrics.

| Lagging metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Urgent median and p90 cycle time | Detect contractual 18h performance, especially p90 |
| Routine median and p90 cycle time | Detect 72h target erosion and batch-delay abuse |
| Complex-panel median and p90 cycle time | Detect specialist-review and panel-scheduling problems |
| Overall rework / repeat rate | Confirm whether repeat suppression is working |
| Percentage of cases with at least one queue > 12h | Detect hidden queue migration and WIP accumulation |
| Cancellations after recollection | Detect front-door quality failures and physician/courier issues |

The key operating rule is: when leading indicators turn amber or red, activate the corresponding scenario policy before the lagging SLA metrics fail. This avoids the mistake of managing to historical averages while the process tail silently breaches Urgent, Routine, and Complex-panel commitments.
