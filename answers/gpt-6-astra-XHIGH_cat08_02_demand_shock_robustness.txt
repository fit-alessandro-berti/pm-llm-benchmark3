# 1. Stress scenarios

The redesign should protect deadlines, tail performance, quality, and a stable backlog under changing workloads. Faster average completion is insufficient: even normally, Urgent p90 is 19h against an 18h contract. At peak it reaches 33h, while Routine p90 reaches 120h against a 72h target.

Normalize the unequal observation periods before interpreting demand:

| Order class | Normal orders/week | Peak orders/week | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Routine | 1,403 | 1,386 | −1% |
| Urgent | 414 | 957 | +131% |
| Complex-panel | 299 | 660 | +121% |
| Repeat-after-failure | 184 | 297 | +61% |
| Total | 2,300 | 3,300 | +43% |

Routine volume barely changes; the peak disproportionately adds medical-validation demand, multiple assays, and failure-related work. Capacity planning must therefore use assay, preparation, and validation workload as well as order counts.

Use the following stress tests. The disruption magnitudes are planning assumptions, not measurements from the supplied logs.

| Scenario | Demand and disruption assumptions | Main exposure |
|---|---|---|
| S1: Winter mix shift | Sustain 3,300 orders/week at the observed peak mix: 42% Routine, 29% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel, 9% Repeat-after-failure. Preserve day/night and courier arrival patterns. | Assay and technical-validation overload, followed by medical and specialist queues. |
| S2: Machine impairment | Test both normal and peak mixes with M2 losing 25% of usable capacity and having twice its baseline repeat probability; separately test an 8h outage of either machine. | Reduced successful assay output, repeat feedback, and a validation surge after recovery. |
| S3: Validator absence | At peak mix, remove the scheduled specialist every second Friday; separately add one medical validator absent for a shift. Test a Complex-panel-heavy arrival wave during the absence. | Completed assays awaiting an appropriately qualified decision; orders blocked until every required component is reviewed. |
| S4: Courier compression or delay | Keep daily volume unchanged, but combine the 10:00 and 17:00 deliveries into a late 3h arrival window; separately delay an Urgent-heavy route by 6h. | Intake bursts, sample deterioration, and little contractual time remaining after receipt. |
| S5: Compound disruption | Combine the peak mix, M2 impairment, a Friday specialist absence, and late delivery. Continue the test through recovery. | Several simultaneous constraints, repeated work, deadline conflicts, and prolonged backlog clearance. |

Define acceptability before comparing packages: measure the fraction exceeding Urgent 18h and Routine 72h, excess hours beyond each deadline, p90/p95 and oldest age by class, rework, and backlog growth/recovery. Confirm the contractual clock start; do not reset it at receipt, reassignment, or outsourcing. Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure orders need clinically agreed due dates; their normal p90 values, 118h and 132h, are useful recovery references, not established service promises. Required medical validation and analytical quality remain mandatory.

A package is sustainable only if effective capacity exceeds incoming work over the relevant operating horizon and disruption backlog clears within an agreed recovery window. The summaries cannot establish that all targets are attainable, especially in S5. Infeasible demand must trigger explicit clinical prioritization and overflow arrangements rather than an invisible accumulation of overdue work.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Interpret the DFG as evidence of congestion and repeated transitions, not a complete resource model. Orders flatten multiple tubes and assays; branches can hide parallel work and joins. Counts are not flow-conserving, and the incoming cohort need not equal the completed cohort. For example, normal releases plus cancellations total 16,440 versus 18,400 starts; the difference is not a defensible backlog estimate without cohort and boundary information. The stated requirement that Urgent and Complex-panel orders need medical validation must govern routing even though aggregate DFG counts do not cleanly reconcile with those class totals. Audit that discrepancy rather than inferring that these orders can bypass review.

Likewise, do not add edge medians or station queue medians to reconstruct a typical case: they describe different populations and can overlap. Treat technical utilization of 101% as offered work exceeding nominal capacity, not literal productive occupancy above 100%.

**S1 — Mix shift: pre-analytics and correction → assay → technical validation → medical validation/specialist review.** Pre-analytics rises from 74% to 96% utilization. Its listed exception exits—recollection plus correction—rise from approximately 7.0% to 12.3% of listed pre-analytical exits. These are transition shares, not independently established unique-order defect rates. Correction queues rise from 3h to 14h with only two specialists, while the correction-to-preparation edge grows from 5h to 16h.

Fixing intake and correction releases work toward machines already at 98% and technical validators at 101%. The assay queue rises from 4h to 18h and the assay-to-technical-validation edge from 5h to 15h. Expanding machine output alone therefore increases unvalidated inventory. Expanding technical validation then feeds medical validation: its queue rises from 2h to 22h and the technical-to-medical edge from 8h to 24h. Medical-to-release transitions rise from about 521/week to 1,330/week, approximately 2.55 times normal.

Complex panels amplify the final constraint: they are 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist hold. Technical-to-hold transitions rise from about 36/week to 203/week, approximately 5.6 times normal; hold-to-medical time rises from 18h to 48h. Specialist utilization of 87% does not imply accessible capacity when skills and calendars create localized gaps. Strict Urgent-first scheduling may lower Urgent waiting initially while making Routine and Complex-panel inventory older; it can also merely relocate Urgent waiting to medical validation.

**S2 — Machine impairment: assay/repeat loop → preparation blocking → validation surge during recovery.** Assay-to-repeat transitions rise from 143/week normally to 420/week at peak. Among the two listed assay exits, the repeat share rises from about 6.7% to 14.8%; this is not the overall order-level rework measure of 7.5%/11.8%. The repeat-to-assay edge stretches from 10h to 26h. M2/night-shift clustering suggests a targeted investigation, but does not prove that M2 itself causes failures: assay mix, operators, maintenance, and specimen condition may confound the association.

An outage makes compatible assay capacity the immediate constraint. Preparation can then accumulate unstable or unusable prepared material. Routing everything to the surviving machine risks incompatible tests and priority conflicts. Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex-panel work can also force Routine onto an unreliable M2, generating extra repeats that return to the same scarce assay capacity. After recovery, clearing the machine queue quickly pushes a surge into technical validation, then medical/specialist review. Previously completed orders may remain review-constrained throughout the outage.

**S3 — Absence: specialist/medical review → incomplete panel inventory → catch-up and release congestion.** A missing specialist can block the relevant order subset even if average medical capacity appears adequate. The 36h peak specialist queue and 48h hold-to-medical edge reveal the calendar-sensitive tail. More assays will not release a panel whose decisive review remains unavailable. An evening general medical shift helps only if its staff have the required authorization; it cannot substitute for an absent specialist automatically. On the specialist's return, completed reviews can flood medical sign-off and release; the medical-to-release edge already grows from 2h to 5h. That edge signals a handoff worth investigating, not proof of a dedicated release-resource shortage.

**S4 — Courier shock: intake/registration → pre-analytics/correction → preparation/assay → evening validation.** Daily average courier utilization of 88% hides concentrated arrivals; its queue increases from 1h to 7.5h. Registration-to-receipt rises from 5h to 8h and receipt-to-pre-analytics from 2h to 9h. A late route uses up an Urgent order's remaining deadline before laboratory processing starts. Delayed samples may also cause more recollections; the recollection-to-receipt edge is already 60h at peak. Those loops pull clerks into physician communication and further reduce intake capacity.

Automation can drain registration faster but intensify the pre-analytical burst. Extra preparation capacity can then shift the burst to assay and validation after scheduled reviewers leave. Larger batches may improve setup efficiency while worsening the arrival-to-first-assay delay for urgent samples embedded in the wave. Courier smoothing must reduce bunching without increasing transport age.

**S5 — Compound disruption: simultaneous constraints and repeated migration.** Early defect detection exposes replacement demand; the impaired machine becomes limiting; prioritizing critical assays increases medical/specialist backlog; specialist recovery then exposes release delays. Repeat loops and recollections consume capacity more than once, so a temporary arrival reduction does not immediately restore stability. This scenario needs coordinated limits on work released between stages, selective overflow, and a controlled recovery plan. A single permanent priority rule cannot resolve it.

# 3. Robust redesign package

Use a small common operating package, with additional measures activated by scenario. Its purpose is to remove avoidable work and coordinate scarce capacity across the entire order path.

**A. Establish an order-level control queue with assay-level workload and deadline-aware dispatch.** At receipt, record clinical priority, original deadline, sample stability, required assays, machine compatibility, and required reviewer qualifications. Link all tubes, assay attempts, panels, recollections, external work, and final release to the parent order. A panel completes only when all required components and reviews are satisfied. Repeat-after-failure is a workload characteristic; a repeat retains the parent order's clinical urgency and deadline.

Dispatch using remaining slack: deadline minus current time minus an estimated remaining completion time for the actual route, including downstream queues and staffing calendars. Start with conservative estimates, then calibrate their prediction intervals. Give clinically critical orders immediate escalation, reserve a rolling amount of assay and medical-review capacity for forecast Urgent work, and give overdue-risk Routine and Complex-panel orders explicit protected service. Allocate these protections in resource minutes, not equal numbers of orders; Complex-panel orders require several assays. Release unused reservations to the oldest feasible work and maintain aging escalation.

Reservations must sum to available capacity. If protected non-Urgent service and critical deadlines conflict, escalate to the clinical lead and activate available overflow; do not claim that every class can be first. Limiting concurrent starts can protect prepared-sample stability and downstream queues, but held work remains visible in total WIP and its clock continues. Time-sensitive specimens may require immediate processing even when review capacity is constrained. The migration risk is accumulation before preparation or in validation; monitor both and adjust reservations across stages together.

**B. Improve first-pass intake with I6, selective I8, and I7.** Screen all received samples for defects within 2h, with Urgent orders screened first and sooner when their remaining slack requires it. Distinguish a metadata correction from a specimen requiring recollection; authorized staff make quality decisions using existing acceptance criteria. Initiate necessary recollection immediately, with a tracked owner, pickup plan, and deadline escalation. A faster rejection must not be counted as a successful result or used to make release statistics look better.

Automate registration only for validated, complete digital metadata; send exceptions to a visible correction queue. Pre-check manifests where available. Smooth courier pickups jointly with collection sites, preserving transport and specimen-stability limits rather than delaying Urgent pickups merely to flatten laboratory arrivals. Schedule a qualified intake/pre-analytic flex team around unavoidable delivery windows, avoiding clerk shifts beyond 9h because the stated error risk would recreate demand.

These changes help every scenario by reducing avoidable re-entry and identifying poor samples before scarce assay work. They depend on reliable metadata, collection-site cooperation, and adequate triage skill. Triage itself consumes pre-analytical capacity; start with a brief screen and measure net workload reduction. Successful intake feeds preparation, assay, and validation faster, so couple it to bundle A's workload forecast. Recollection also creates fresh courier/intake work that must be included in that forecast.

**C. Use bounded batching and reliability-aware routing, modifying I1–I3 and rejecting I9.** Keep the current batch size of 12 as a starting default. Permit batches up to 24 only for compatible Routine work that can fill promptly and complete within its deadline margin; enforce a maximum formation wait. Provide scheduled small Urgent runs or bounded exceptions, coordinated with medical coverage, rather than unrestricted interruptions of every batch. At peak only 58% of Urgent orders bypass Batch wait, versus 74% normally, but the right objective is timely release, not maximizing bypass percentage. Preparation-to-Batch-wait transitions rise from 95/week to 393/week, and Batch-wait-to-assay time reaches 22h, so batch formation needs explicit control.

Route eligible work across M1/M2 using expected successful output, including reruns, setup costs, and current QC status. Give M1 preference to time-critical work or assays with demonstrated poor M2 performance, while allowing other compatible work onto M1 when capacity is available. Do not permanently assign all Routine work to M2. Investigate the M2/night-shift repeat clusters and correct supported causes before relying on nominal machine speed.

This protects Routine setup efficiency while limiting Urgent fragmentation and repeat demand. It requires assay compatibility data and dependable QC; small runs can reduce total throughput, and conservative M2 use can overload M1. Larger or faster assay batches can overwhelm technical and medical validation, so size and time runs against their receiving capacity. Do not implement I9: deliberately holding an already releasable Routine result adds delay, and the supplied evidence does not establish enough release-work savings to justify it.

**D. Protect constrained skills with selective flexibility, modifying I4 and supporting I5.** Establish cross-cover competencies before winter, allowing for the stated four-week training lead time and competency sign-off. Do not routinely move two technical validators into accession correction when technical validation is already at 101%. Cross-trained staff may cover brief correction bursts only when the technical work forecast has verified headroom or qualified replacement coverage. Judge the move by total order completion, including the newly unblocked work that will return to technical validation.

Prepare a temporary evening medical shift aligned to forecast completed assays, and a qualified specialist backup arrangement for the known Friday absence. Include final release coverage in that plan. Cross-cover fails if the underlying skill is not substitutable, and extra evening hours fail if upstream work arrives later or staff fatigue creates errors. Correction relief feeds assay/technical work; medical relief feeds specialist exceptions and release. Activate the relevant coverage together only where the workload justifies it, rather than increasing every station's staffing.

**Validate the package across scenarios before claiming robustness.** Reconstruct class-specific traces with activity start/completion, resource calendars, queue entry, machine, assay, tube, attempt, and original deadline. Include open cases to avoid optimistic tails from completed orders alone. Check routing conformance, required medical review, batch membership, and panel joins. Estimate service and waiting distributions separately and stratify repeat risk by machine, assay, shift, and specimen condition.

Use a discrete-event model with correlated courier waves, shift calendars, priority aging, batch formation, multi-assay joins, and repeat/recollection feedback. Reproduce the supplied normal and peak class tails and station queues as calibration checks; those aggregate matches alone do not validate the model. Compare baseline, individual interventions, and bundles under S1–S5, varying unknown capacities, disruption durations, and failure rates. Report deadline violations, worst-served class, quality, WIP, recovery time, and cost with uncertainty. Choose a package with acceptable performance across scenarios and limited worst-case deterioration, not the lowest average cycle time. Pilot with shadow dispatch and staged routing changes, and roll back changes that worsen quality or protected-class service.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

The following are provisional operational thresholds to test and calibrate. Forecast utilization means required compatible work divided by available staffed or qualified capacity, including expected repeat work. Review it at least every 2h during disruptions; use the daily dashboard for oversight. Except for safety events, require sustained evidence before activating or relaxing policies so one unusual hour does not repeatedly reverse decisions.

| Policy and scenario | Switch on | Operating rule and downstream safeguard | Switch off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coordinated surge dispatch and I5 evening medical coverage — S1, also S5 | Next-shift assay, technical, or medical workload exceeds 90% of effective capacity for two reviews; or an Urgent order's conservative completion forecast exceeds its deadline. | Increase Urgent reservations at both assay and medical review, retain explicit aging-based Routine/Complex service, and activate evening medical coverage where review is forecast to constrain releases. Cap upstream releases against technical capacity and synchronize specialist/release coverage. | Relevant next-shift loads remain below 85% for two consecutive shifts, queues decline, and no unaddressed deadline risk remains. Keep the normal protected-service rules. |
| Temporary larger Routine batches — S1 or recovery, only with preparation setup pressure | Compatible Routine work is ready or forecast to form a batch within 2h, no affected order loses its deadline/stability margin, and assay/validation can accept the completion wave. | Allow up to 24; otherwise run smaller. Provisionally limit Urgent batch-formation wait to 1h, or less when slack requires. Schedule exceptions to limit machine fragmentation. | Immediately cancel larger batching when wait/slack limits are violated or receiving stations cannot absorb the batch. Reduce batch size during machine impairment. |
| Degraded-machine mode — S2, S5 | Machine outage or QC stop immediately; otherwise a sustained, volume-sufficient, assay-adjusted M2 repeat increase above its control limit. | Use the validated contingency route; restrict M2 for affected assays, use M1 for compatible deadline-critical work, and activate partner capacity if feasible. Reduce unnecessary preparation starts. During recovery meter completions into technical/medical review instead of creating an unrestricted catch-up wave. | Authorized maintenance/QC clearance plus a supervised run demonstrating acceptable quality and sustainable successful output. Return routing gradually as downstream backlog clears. |
| Review-absence coverage and selective I10 — S3, S5 | Known Friday gap enters the planning horizon, or qualified review capacity before an order's due time is insufficient. Evaluate outsourcing as soon as a suitable panel is received. | Book qualified backup or timed remote review where authorized. Outsource eligible panels only when the confirmed partner completion estimate beats local completion and meets clinical timing. Reserve local medical/release capacity for returned results. | Local qualified coverage returns and its forecast queue meets due dates for two reviews; stop new transfers while completing and reconciling those already external. |
| Courier compression mode — S4, S5 | Manifest/ETA predicts arrivals above staffed intake capacity in the next 2h, or transit delay consumes an Urgent order's remaining slack. | Pre-register valid manifests, move available qualified intake/triage coverage to the arrival window, and process by clinical need and remaining deadline. Coordinate downstream assay and evening review capacity before the wave; expedite feasible recollections. | Arrival backlog clears, two successive review windows are within staffed capacity, and affected downstream queues are falling. |
| Short accession-correction deployment — S4 or S1 with local imbalance | Oldest correction waits over 6h or blocks an imminent deadline, while technical load remains below 85% after accounting for the staff transfer and the corrected orders' downstream work. | Move one cross-trained person for a bounded interval; move the second only if the same calculation still passes. If technical capacity is constrained, use qualified alternative coverage or retain the current allocation. | Correction pressure resolves, technical load reaches 90%, or technical completion forecasts deteriorate. Reassess after each interval. |

For I10, calculate remaining time as **handoff + 12h transport + partner accession/assays/review + any return or data-transfer delay + local reconciliation/release**. Confirm whether the stated 12h covers all required transport. Compare a conservative partner estimate with the remaining deadline and local estimate; a shorter specialist queue alone is insufficient. Twelve hours of transport consumes two-thirds of an Urgent order's entire 18h allowance, so ordinary Urgent outsourcing is usually unsuitable without a verified faster complete path. Require specimen compatibility/stability, accepted partner capacity, shared identifiers, chain of custody, and ownership of missing or discrepant results. Avoid unplanned duplicate local and partner assays. Outsourcing relieves local assay/specialist demand but can overload transport, partner capacity, accession reconciliation, and local sign-off.

If S5 makes deadlines infeasible, the clinical lead sets explicit priority among affected orders, activates feasible overflow and replacement collection routes, and communicates expected delays through established channels. Defer eligible Routine starts only with visible age limits and clinical agreement; do not reset targets or cancel orders to improve metrics. Maintain recovery controls until the oldest Routine, Complex-panel, and Repeat orders are addressed, not merely until new Urgent arrivals are on time.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Insufficient total or compatible capacity.** Neither scheduling nor automation can sustainably absorb demand above effective machine or qualified-review capacity. A long outage or sustained peak may still require targeted temporary capacity, accepted overflow, or clinically governed postponement.
- **Long, partly external loops.** Recollection takes a median 60h at peak. Early detection starts recovery sooner but cannot guarantee an 18h Urgent result when a usable specimen is unavailable. Physician response and courier replacement capacity remain dependencies.
- **Non-substitutable expertise and panel joins.** One missing specialist or one failed assay can hold an otherwise complete panel. Generic medical staffing cannot resolve every specialist absence, and the order-level DFG understates component-level blocking.
- **Redesign-induced fragmentation and displacement.** Reserved Urgent slots and small batches can reduce setup efficiency, while limits on new assay starts can shift waiting to intake or preparation. Excessively generous reservations can recreate Routine starvation; overly rigid Routine protection can delay a clinically critical order. Monitor total WIP, sample stability, and delivered service across classes.
- **Redesign-induced routing and partner risks.** Restricting M2 may overload M1; outsourcing introduces transport, traceability, and reconciliation failure modes. The partner may experience the same regional winter surge, making its apparent spare capacity unavailable.
- **Staff fatigue and fragile cross-cover.** Overtime and evening work can increase mistakes, particularly with the stated clerk error rise beyond 9h. Cross-training does not add labor hours, and moving validators may amplify a later bottleneck.
- **Imperfect evidence.** Flattened events, unbalanced counts, censored open cases, unknown service-time distributions, and confounded repeat clusters prevent defensible numerical guarantees. Scenario results must remain conditional until detailed logs and live pilots support them.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Use one compact dashboard with the following leading indicators. Refresh operational queues at least every 2h during peaks, immediately for outages/QC events, and review trends daily. Thresholds are initial control settings, not values statistically established by these summaries.

| Leading indicator | Warning bands and operational response |
|---|---|
| **Demand translated into resource work** | Show next-shift hours required at preparation, each compatible machine, technical, medical, and specialist review, including active WIP and expected repeats. Green below 85% of available capacity; amber 85–95%; red above 95% or any interval exceeding 100%. Use the 90% sustained trigger for preventive action before red. Include class mix and courier ETAs so unchanged order counts cannot hide heavier work. |
| **Remaining deadline slack and oldest work** | By class and current station, show orders forecast to finish late and those with ≤2h buffer before the Urgent deadline or ≤12h before the Routine target. These are provisional amber bands; negative slack is red. For Complex/Repeat use their assigned clinical due dates. Show unreleased open orders and downstream reviewer availability. Escalate any missed protected service window while eligible Routine/Complex work waits. |
| **Arrival and early-exception pressure** | Amber when predicted 2h arrivals exceed staffed intake capacity or correction oldest age exceeds 6h; red when any sample misses the 2h triage target, specimen stability is threatened, or a correction blocks an imminent deadline. Track recollection work awaiting contact or pickup separately so rejected orders do not disappear. |
| **Assay reliability and forward repeat workload** | Report successful assays per available machine-hour, machine status, oldest batch wait, and pending rerun hours. Amber for a sustained assay/shift-adjusted repeat-rate rise above its baseline control band; red for QC failure/outage or repeat demand that pushes machine work beyond available capacity. Treat small denominators cautiously and retain immediate QC stops. |
| **Qualified review and release readiness** | Compare medical/specialist work due before the next coverage gap with qualified staffed hours. Amber when due work approaches the available hours or a known absence lacks confirmed cover; red when required review cannot occur before the deadline. Show specialist-blocked orders, confirmed partner ETAs where used, and ready-to-release orders approaching their remaining deadline. |
| **Backlog direction and recovery forecast** | Track total open orders plus remaining assay/review hours, including held and external work. Amber if residual WIP grows across two comparable operating windows despite intervention; red if conservative recovery extends beyond affected deadlines or the agreed recovery window. Compare like times of day to avoid mistaking a routine courier wave for sustained instability. |

Keep lagging outcomes in a separate strip: actual Urgent >18h and Routine >72h rates and excess hours; class-specific p50/p90/p95 and oldest unresolved age; rework/recollection/cancellation rates by reason; percentage of cases with a queue >12h; and penalties where available. Show raw and case-mix-adjusted quality measures, open-case counts, and completion denominators. Use the normal 7.5% versus peak 11.8% rework and 22% versus 54% long-queue incidence as reference levels, not acceptable peak targets. These outcomes judge whether the redesign works; leading workload, slack, reliability, and coverage indicators determine when to act.
