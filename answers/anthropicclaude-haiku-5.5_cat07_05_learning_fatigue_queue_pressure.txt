# 1. Competing mechanism table

No single mechanism explains the pattern. Several are plausibly active at once, and some of them produce opposite signs on service time and error rate. The table lists what each mechanism predicts, what the data show for and against it, and how to test it.

**Key takeaways from the table.** Several mechanisms predict opposite signs for service time (learning, complexity, and queue pressure all reduce or raise it depending on context). Errors are the more discriminating outcome, but they are measured with lag and depend on QC timing. Testing has to separate mechanisms by stratifying on the others, not by looking at marginal trends.

# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is best understood as a sequence of interacting regimes rather than a single slope.

**Early shift (positions 1–30).** Service time is high (18.8  13.6 min) and error is moderate (3.4%  2.5%). Complexity is high (0.72  0.54), so a large share of the early slowdown is case mix from backlog carryover. Warm-up may add to it, particularly for Juniors, who show the largest first-20 penalty. Errors are not high despite the complexity, which suggests that early work is handled with care while queue pressure is still moderate (46  33). This is the phase where learning or warm-up is most plausible as a contributor, and it is also the phase where complexity explains the most.

**Mid shift (positions 31–50).** This is the most stable period: lowest complexity (0.50), moderate queue (31), and the lowest error rate (2.7%). Service time continues to fall (12.9 min). The period is a useful reference point, because it combines low complexity, moderate queue, and presumably settled work rhythm.

**Late shift (positions 51+).** Queue jumps (62  88) and error rises sharply (4.6%  7.9%), while service time keeps falling (11.4  10.8 min). The speed gain here is not best explained by learning. Learning would predict lower error as well as faster service, and it would not cause errors to rise with queue. The more coherent account is that workers shorten or skip steps under pressure. That reduces time per case and raises error, and the effect is strongest when the case is complex. The WIP table supports this: for high-complexity work, service time drops from 22.4 to 16.8 min as WIP rises, while error rises from 4.2% to 10.9%. Pressure buys speed by reducing verification, and it does so most where verification matters most.

**Why errors rise only in some conditions.** Errors are not a monotone function of time or workload. They rise when pressure coincides with (a) high complexity, (b) less experienced staff who lack the margin to absorb complexity, and (c) reduced checking capacity, such as deferred Quality Check. Under low WIP, Seniors show no error increase later in the shift (2.1%  2.2%), and fatigue alone would predict some degradation. Under high WIP, Seniors' error rises to 4.9%, and Juniors' to 12.4%. This interaction is the central finding: time-on-task matters less than the load at which the work is done.

**The role of detection.** Errors are recorded when they are found, and Quality Check is often delayed to the end of the shift. Late-shift error and rework rates therefore partly reflect when verification happens, not only when work is done. Some of the late-shift error burden may originate in the middle of the shift and be discovered at the end. This artifact makes the late-shift error rate an unreliable measure of late-shift performance.

**Shift-level contrast.** The Night shift has the highest complexity in its first hour (56%), the highest service time in its last two hours (15.2 min), and fewer events but more urgent exceptions. The Day shift has the highest late-shift error rate (7.5%) and the largest queue growth (38  75), consistent with demand peaking late and passing pressure to the Late shift. The Late shift inherits this backlog (70  66). Queue is therefore a transferable state across shifts, and shift-level comparisons conflate the shift's own behavior with what it inherits.

**Bottom line.** Late-shift speed is mostly pressure-driven throughput, partly offset by a lower-complexity mix. Late-shift error is pressure interacting with complexity, staffing mix, deferred checking, and detection timing. Learning and warm-up explain part of the early-shift slowdown but are hard to separate from complexity carryover. Fatigue plausibly contributes to degradation for mid-level and junior staff under pressure, but it does not explain the speed gain. Circadian effects are plausible for the night shift but are confounded with staffing and case mix. Batching and automation interactions are plausible mechanisms for specific subgroups and need direct tests.

**A rough sense of scale.** Within-band contrasts suggest that complexity differences account for several minutes of the service-time decline. The full 8-minute drop from position 1–5 to 76+ is therefore not explained by complexity alone, but a meaningful part of it is. This must be estimated with a regression that includes complexity, priority, and WIP jointly, not by hand from the aggregate table.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The analyses below move from descriptive to causal. Each one addresses a specific confound.

### 3.1 Data preparation and derived variables

Derive event-level service time, waiting time, and lagged queue. Service time is the duration between `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp`. Waiting time is the gap between the previous event's completion in the same case or the same resource, depending on the question.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

# 1. Load and format
df = pd.read_csv("claims_events.csv")
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)
df = df.sort_values(["org:resource", "start_timestamp"]).reset_index(drop=True)

# 2. Derived measures
df["service_min"] = (df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
df["log_service"] = np.log(df["service_min"].clip(lower=0.5))

# Resource-level waiting: time since the same resource's previous completion
df["prev_end_res"] = df.groupby("org:resource")["time:timestamp"].shift(1)
df["idle_gap_min"] = (df["start_timestamp"] - df["prev_end_res"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Case-level waiting: time since the case's previous completion
df = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "start_timestamp"])
df["prev_end_case"] = df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].shift(1)
df["case_wait_min"] = (df["start_timestamp"] - df["prev_end_case"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Lagged queue: queue observed at the previous event, to reduce simultaneity
df = df.sort_values(["org:resource", "start_timestamp"])
df["queue_lag"] = df.groupby("org:resource")["queue_length_at_start"].shift(1)

# Temporal features (clock hour, weekday)
df = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df, timestamp_column="start_timestamp")
df["hour_of_day"] = df["start_timestamp"].dt.hour
```

Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)` if you need case-level service and waiting time already inserted into an event log object. Verify the signature for your PM4Py version, because the input type (DataFrame or EventLog) has changed across releases.

### 3.2 Within-resource comparisons

Compare each resource against itself across positions, days, and WIP states. This removes stable differences in skill and assignment. Use a fixed-effects specification with resource-level clustering.

```python
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# Service time model with resource fixed effects
m_service = smf.ols(
    "log_service ~ C(pos_bin) + case_complexity_score + C(priority) + C(activity_family)"
    " + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start + C(shift_id) + resource_experience_months"
    " + C(\"org:resource\")",
    data=df,
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["org:resource"].astype("category").cat.codes})
print(m_service.summary())
```

The key test is whether position effects survive when the same resource is compared to itself. If they disappear, the pattern reflects selection of which resource handles which cases.

### 3.3 Within-shift position effects

Separate the position-in-shift effect from the clock-time effect, since they are collinear in fixed-length shifts.

```python
df["pos_bin"] = pd.cut(
    df["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, 1e9],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
)

# Errors as a function of position, controlling for complexity, WIP, queue, and resource
m_error = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ C(pos_bin) + case_complexity_score + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start"
    " + C(priority) + C(activity_family) + C(\"org:resource\")",
    data=df,
).fit(disp=False)
print(m_error.summary())
```

To separate position from clock time, add `minutes_since_shift_start` as a continuous term, then compare models with `hour_of_day` and with `shift_id` as alternatives. The model that fits best shows which timing variable carries the signal, though the variables remain partly collinear.

### 3.4 WIP and queue stratification

Test whether queue effects are nonlinear and whether position effects vanish once queue is held fixed.

```python
# Queue strata
df["queue_band"] = pd.cut(df["queue_length_at_start"], [0, 30, 50, 70, 1e9],
                          labels=["<30", "30-50", "50-70", ">70"])

# Stratified position effects: are early vs late differences present within each queue band?
for band, sub in df.groupby("queue_band"):
    m = smf.logit("error_flag ~ C(pos_bin) + case_complexity_score + C(priority)", data=sub).fit(disp=False)
    print(band, m.params.filter(like="pos_bin").round(3).to_dict())

# Spline or piecewise queue effect (knot at 70)
df["queue_over70"] = np.maximum(df["queue_length_at_start"] - 70, 0)
m_knee = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ queue_length_at_start + queue_over70 + case_complexity_score + C(priority)",
    data=df,
).fit(disp=False)
```

A significant `queue_over70` term supports a threshold. Test the threshold location by refitting with different knots. Note that a threshold claim requires care, because queue is endogenous (see Section 5).

### 3.5 Complexity adjustment

Complexity is a coarse score. Check whether position effects depend on it by testing interactions and using matched comparisons.

```python
# Interaction: does the queue effect on errors differ by complexity?
m_int = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ queue_length_at_start * case_complexity_score + C(pos_bin) + C(priority)"
    " + C(\"org:resource\")",
    data=df,
).fit(disp=False)

# Within-complexity-band comparisons: high-complexity events only
hi = df[df["case_complexity_score"] >= df["case_complexity_score"].quantile(0.75)]
m_hi = smf.logit("error_flag ~ C(pos_bin) + queue_length_at_start + C(\"org:resource\")", data=hi).fit(disp=False)
```

Also estimate the share of the 8-minute service-time decline explained by complexity: compare the position coefficient in a model with and without the complexity term. The reduction in the coefficient is the mix-explained share.

### 3.6 Shift and time-of-day controls

Hold clock time and shift identity apart, and use rosters where the same person works different shifts.

```python
m_clock = smf.ols(
    "log_service ~ C(hour_of_day) + C(shift_id) + C(pos_bin) + case_complexity_score"
    " + queue_length_at_start + C(\"org:resource\")",
    data=df,
).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["org:resource"].astype("category").cat.codes})

# Identification: resources observed in multiple shifts
multi = df.groupby("org:resource")["shift_id"].nunique()
df_multi = df[df["org:resource"].isin(multi[multi > 1].index)]
```

Use only the subset of resources who work multiple shift types for identifying circadian effects. Otherwise shift and staff composition are confounded.

### 3.7 Batching detection

Detect runs of consecutive same-activity events by the same resource with very short service times and identical queue batches.

```python
def flag_batches(d, min_run=5, short_quantile=0.25):
    d = d.sort_values("start_timestamp").copy()
    short_thr = d["service_min"].quantile(short_quantile)
    same_act = d["concept:name"].eq(d["concept:name"].shift())
    same_queue = d["queue_length_at_start"].eq(d["queue_length_at_start"].shift())
    short = d["service_min"] <= short_thr
    run_ok = same_act & same_queue & short
    # run-length grouping
    grp = (~run_ok).cumsum()
    d["run_id"] = grp.where(run_ok)
    run_len = d.groupby("run_id")["run_id"].transform("size")
    d["batch_flag"] = (run_len >= min_run) & d["run_id"].notna()
    return d

df_b = (df.groupby("org:resource", group_keys=False)
          .apply(flag_batches)
          .reset_index(drop=True))

print(df_b.groupby(["org:resource", "batch_flag"])["error_flag"].mean())
```

Then use PM4Py's batch discovery to cross-check the heuristic:

```python
# Verify parameters in your PM4Py version
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)  # or the DataFrame-compatible variant
```

Compare batched and non-batched events on error, rework, and complexity. Check whether batched runs share a queue value at the same moment (suggesting the batch was formed by the queue, not by the worker).

### 3.8 Resource-experience interactions

Test whether the pressure-error relationship depends on experience, since the Junior result is the strongest.

```python
m_exp = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ queue_length_at_start * resource_experience_months"
    " + case_complexity_score * resource_experience_months + C(pos_bin) + C(priority)",

```

Experience should be measured as the resource's cumulative events or months at the time of the event, not as a static attribute, to separate learning from selection.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker-level conclusions are often unfair.** Error rates and service times reflect the cases a worker receives, the queue they face, the time at which they work, and the detection process that finds their errors. A raw late-shift error rate for an individual mixes all of these. A worker who receives the most complex late-shift cases, during the highest queue, with QC deferred, will look worse than one who receives simple cases at low queue, even if they perform identically.

**Selection bias.** Seniors take complex cases early, and complex cases are riskier. Case assignment is not random, so any comparison of workers is confounded by assignment. Random or stratified assignment is the cleanest fix, and at minimum any worker-level metric must be adjusted for case mix and queue state before it is reported.

**Measurement error.** Errors are detected when found. Quality Check timing shifts detection into the end of shift, so late-shift error counts partly reflect when verification happens. Rework within 7 days depends on whether a rework event was logged at all. Service time depends on how start and end timestamps are recorded; batched work may have timestamps that do not reflect actual effort. Waiting time may be recorded as service time when a worker is blocked.

**Queue pressure created by management.** Shift-level queue patterns are partly produced by staffing, demand scheduling, and handover rules. When management measures workers on throughput while queue is high, workers are incentivized to shortcut. The late-shift speed gain may therefore be a managerial design outcome, not a worker characteristic. Interventions that increase throughput targets without changing capacity are likely to increase error.

**Automation quality.** Bot_Risk outputs are instantaneous, and human corrections are concentrated in high-queue periods. Attributing those corrections to human error is wrong if the bot's classification is weak under certain conditions. Bot error rates by queue state must be measured and reported separately from human error. Workers who correct bot output should not be penalized for the bot's mistakes.

**Punitive use of error rates.** Using error rates as a basis for penalties creates several harms. Workers avoid late-shift and complex work, which shifts burden onto others. Workers may under-report errors or manipulate flags. Juniors and night staff, who face the most structural pressure, are most exposed. Error rates should be used for process diagnosis, with support and training as the response, not for individual discipline unless the case-mix and queue adjustments show a robust, individual-specific effect.

**Small samples and noise.** Night staff and some resources have small event counts in some strata. Worker-level estimates are noisy, and small differences can look large. Report confidence intervals and avoid ranking workers on point estimates.

**Privacy and transparency.** Worker-level dashboards must be designed so that they support process improvement, not surveillance. Share the methodology with workers. Allow workers to see and contest their adjusted metrics.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard is organized around process state, not individual ranking. Each indicator has a definition, a data source, and a suggested alert condition. Worker-level views should be restricted to complexity- and queue-adjusted metrics with confidence intervals.

| # | Indicator | Definition | Source columns | Suggested alert or use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Service time, complexity-adjusted** | Median service time by activity and position, adjusted for complexity, priority, and activity mix (expected value from a reference model) | `start_timestamp`, `time:timestamp`, `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, `activity_family` | Alert when adjusted service falls more than 15% below baseline while queue is above 70. This pattern indicates shortcutting, not improvement. |
| 2 | **Waiting time** | Median and 90th percentile of case waiting time (time between a case's previous event and the start of the current event) | `case_wait_min` (derived), `time:timestamp` | Alert on 90th percentile above target. Track by shift and by complexity band. |
| 3 | **WIP and queue state** | Share of events with WIP above 70 and queue above 70; time spent in each band per shift | `wip_at_start`, `queue_length_at_start`, `shift_id` | Alert when share above 70 exceeds 20% of a shift. Track shift-boundary queue inheritance (end-of-shift queue vs next shift start). |
| 4 | **Error rate, stratified** | Error rate by queue band, complexity band, and activity; reported with 95% CI | `error_flag`, `queue_length_at_start`, `case_complexity_score` | Alert when error rate in the high-queue, high-complexity stratum rises above baseline. Do not aggregate across strata for alerts. |
| 5 | **Rework within 7 days** | Share of cases with rework events within 7 days of first closure, by origin position and detection lag | `rework_flag`, `concept:name`, `time:timestamp` | Alert on rework rate rise in cases originating in high-queue windows. Track detection lag (QC time minus error time). |
| 6 | **Quality Check lag and burst index** | Median time from work completion to QC; share of QC events in the last 45 minutes of a shift | `concept:name` (Quality Check), `time:timestamp`, `shift_id` | Alert when QC share in the last 45 minutes exceeds 35%, or when median lag exceeds the service-level target. |
| 7 | **Complexity mix by window** | Share of high-complexity cases in the first hour and last two hours of each shift; mean complexity by position | `case_complexity_score`, `sequence_position_within_shift`, `minutes_since_shift_start` | Use to contextualize other metrics. Alert on large mix shifts between consecutive shifts. |
| 8 | **Batching index** | Share of events in runs of 5 or more consecutive same-activity, same-queue, short-service events, by resource and shift | `concept:name`, `queue_length_at_start`, `service_min` | Alert on batch runs above threshold for a resource or activity. Review batch error and rework rates at the same time. |
| 9 | **Handover and queue ownership changes** | Number of resource changes per case; number of cases whose queue ownership changes at shift boundaries; time between handover and next event | `case:concept:name`, `org:resource`, `shift_id`, `start_timestamp` | Alert on rising handovers per case. Track error rate in the first 30 minutes after a handover. |
| 10 | **Automation override rate** | Share of Bot_Risk classifications corrected by a human; time from bot output to human action; correction rate by queue band | `org:resource` (Bot_Risk), `concept:name` (Risk Classification), `queue_length_at_start` | Alert when correction rate rises above queue 70 or when time-to-accept falls sharply (possible uncritical acceptance). |
| 11 | **Worker-load fairness** | Gini coefficient of events per resource per shift; share of high-complexity and high-priority cases per resource; errors per resource normalized by complexity-matched expectation | `org:resource`, `shift_id`, `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, `error_flag` | Alert on load concentration (e.g., a senior receiving a disproportionate share of complex early-shift cases). Use to inform assignment rules, not for discipline. |
| 12 | **Aging of complex cases** | Age distribution of high-complexity cases at processing start; share exceeding SLA | `case_complexity_score`, `case:concept:name`, `start_timestamp` | Alert when complex-case aging rises, which can indicate the effect of rules that move complex work away from specific windows (see Intervention 2). |

**Dashboard design notes.**

- Show all process indicators at shift, queue-band, and complexity-band level before showing any resource-level view.
- Resource-level views should show adjusted, not raw, metrics, with confidence intervals and minimum event thresholds.
- Include a "change log" panel recording interventions, staffing changes, and bot model changes, so that dashboard changes can be attributed.
- Use the dashboard to evaluate interventions with pre-specified indicators (e.g., error in high-queue high-complexity stratum), not with whichever indicator moves in the desired direction.
