# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is best interpreted as several mechanisms operating at once.

In the first part of shifts, service time falls from 18.8 minutes to 12.9 minutes and error rates fall modestly. Some of that is plausibly operational warm-up: workers settle into systems, recover context, and establish a rhythm. The resource table is consistent with a real speed-up after the first 20 events, including for seniors.

However, the early improvement cannot be labeled “learning” without qualification. The first events are disproportionately difficult carryover work: complexity is 0.72 in positions 1–5, high-priority share is 41%, and queues are elevated. By positions 31–50, complexity is only 0.50 and queue length has fallen. Seniors also appear to select or receive complex cases early. Thus, part of the early service-time decline is likely a change in work composition and backlog clearance, not just workers becoming more proficient.

The late-shift pattern is different. Service time continues to decline, reaching 10.8 minutes at position 76+, but errors rise to 7.9% and rework within seven days rises to 13.7%. This is not the classic simple fatigue signature of “slower and more error-prone.” Instead, it is a speed-quality tradeoff under queue pressure:

- WIP rises from 31 in positions 31–50 to 88 in positions 76+.
- High-WIP events are faster in both complexity bands.
- High-WIP events are also much more error-prone in both complexity bands.
- The risk increase is strongest for high-complexity work and less experienced workers.

That pattern is compatible with workers shortening checks, postponing documentation, reducing consultation, or prioritizing throughput when queues become salient. It may also reflect management-created pressure: staffing levels, demand peaks, backlog inheritance, and queue targets can make “work faster” the locally rational behavior.

Fatigue may still contribute, especially where quality declines after long exposure and under pressure. But the available data cannot distinguish fatigue from pressure because late sequence position, elapsed shift time, queue growth, batching, and end-of-shift quality-control timing occur together. A worker may be tired, but may also be processing easier cases faster while under stronger pressure to clear the queue.

Circadian effects are plausible but not established. Night operations are slower and have a distinct error pattern, but Night staff handle fewer cases, more urgent exceptions, and more high-complexity cases. Differences between night and day therefore reflect staffing, case mix, and operating conditions as well as possible circadian effects.

Batching and delayed quality control can further distort the apparent relationship between service time and quality. Sequential Document Review batches can legitimately reduce setup time, but may also shift documentation or checking work elsewhere. Similarly, delayed Quality Checks can create an end-of-shift burst of detected corrections. A late error flag may indicate an error made earlier but detected later. Therefore, the analysis should distinguish:

1. when the original decision was made;
2. when the issue was detected;
3. when the correction was performed; and
4. whether the case reworked within seven days.

The strongest current conclusion is not that workers inevitably degrade over time. It is that high queue pressure, combined with changing case mix, selection, batching, and possibly fatigue, creates conditions in which recorded service time falls while downstream quality worsens.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Statistical design

Use event-level models for service time and errors, but retain case-level outcomes for rework and end-to-end performance.

Recommended outcomes:

- **Event service time:** `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`; model log service time because durations are usually right-skewed.
- **Error flag:** binary event-level outcome.
- **Rework within seven days:** case-level or decision-level outcome; account for cases without a full seven-day follow-up window.
- **Correction delay:** time between original decision, Quality Check, and Rework Correction.
- **End-to-end case performance:** total service time, waiting time, cycle time, and number of handovers.

For service time, estimate a mixed-effects or fixed-effects model such as:

```text
log(service_time) ~
    activity_family
  + case_complexity_score
  + priority
  + spline(wip_at_start)
  + spline(queue_length_at_start)
  + spline(minutes_since_shift_start)
  + spline(sequence_position_within_shift)
  + shift_id
  + cyclic_clock_time
  + day_of_week
  + resource_experience_months
  + high_wip * case_complexity_score
  + high_wip * resource_experience_months
  + high_wip * activity_family
  + batch_indicator
  + bot_risk_path_indicator
  + resource fixed effects
  + calendar-date or shift-date effects
```

For errors and rework, use a logistic mixed model or generalized estimating equation with clustered standard errors by case, resource, and shift-date.

Important modeling cautions:

- Do not interpret a raw sequence-position coefficient as fatigue.
- Sequence position and minutes since shift start are highly correlated. Estimate alternative specifications, use splines, or residualize one against the other rather than claiming each is independently causal.
- WIP and queue length are also related. Choose one as the primary exposure or use carefully regularized nonlinear models.
- Use **WIP at event start**. Do not use a queue measure affected by the event’s completion.
- Include activity family because a “service time” comparison across Risk Classification, Customer Callback, and Quality Check is otherwise misleading.
- Analyze Bot_Risk separately from human work because zero-duration automated events can distort averages.
- Avoid controlling for post-treatment variables such as a Quality Check that occurred because an earlier decision was suspicious.

## Within-resource and within-shift comparisons

1. **Within-resource comparison**
   - Compare each worker against their own performance across similar activities, complexity bands, priorities, and WIP levels.
   - Include resource fixed effects so that Senior_A is not directly compared with Junior_J as if they receive the same work.
   - Compare workers’ low-WIP and high-WIP performance separately.

2. **Within-shift position effects**
   - Divide each shift into position and elapsed-time bins.
   - Estimate early, middle, and late effects after adjustment for complexity, priority, activity, and WIP.
   - Test whether late-position quality deterioration remains at low WIP. If it does, fatigue or time-of-day becomes more plausible; if it mostly disappears, pressure is the stronger explanation.

3. **WIP and queue stratification**
   - Use the existing low/medium/high WIP bands for communication.
   - Also use continuous nonlinear curves because risk may rise sharply around 70 rather than smoothly.
   - Estimate WIP-by-complexity and WIP-by-experience interactions.

4. **Complexity adjustment and assignment analysis**
   - Stratify by complexity and priority before comparing resources or sequence positions.
   - Model assignment to senior, mid-level, junior, or night staff as an outcome. If seniors systematically receive difficult early work, raw senior-versus-junior comparisons are invalid.
   - Consider constrained randomization only among workers eligible to perform a task, rather than unrestricted random assignment.

5. **Shift and clock-time controls**
   - Include shift identity, local clock hour, weekday/weekend, month, and holiday effects.
   - Compare overlapping periods, such as Early and Day coverage, to separate workload from clock time where possible.
   - For workers who rotate shifts, compare the same worker across shift types.

6. **Batching and delayed-quality analysis**
   - Identify sequential runs by the same resource and activity.
   - Detect whether Quality Checks and Rework Corrections occur in bursts near handover or shift end.
   - Compare batch versus non-batch cases on later errors, rework, correction delay, and end-to-end cycle time.

7. **Downstream rework analysis**
   - Link original decisions to later Quality Check, Rework Correction, and Close Case events.
   - Separate errors detected at Quality Check from errors that escaped closure and returned within seven days.
   - Use a time-to-rework model when follow-up periods vary.

## PM4Py-oriented pseudocode

The exact parameter names can vary slightly by PM4Py version, but the analysis flow is as follows.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

df = raw_events.copy()

# Ensure timestamps are timezone-aware and consistently recorded.
df["start_timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"], utc=True)
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)

# Event-level service time; retain invalid or negative durations for data-quality review.
df["event_service_seconds"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds()

df["high_wip"] = (df["wip_at_start"] > 70).astype(int)
df["late_shift"] = (df["minutes_since_shift_start"] >= 360).astype(int)
df["clock_hour"] = df["start_timestamp"].dt.hour
df["weekday"] = df["start_timestamp"].dt.dayofweek

# PM4Py formatting for process-mining operations.
elog = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

# Case-level performance enrichments. These should complement, not replace,
# the event-level service-time calculation above.
elog = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
    elog,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

elog = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(
    elog,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

temporal_features = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
    elog,
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

case_service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(
    elog,
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

rework_by_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(
    elog,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name"
)
```

Use PM4Py filters to create reproducible operational cohorts:


# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Main risk or limitation | How to monitor impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May reduce excess overtime exposure, support recovery, and limit true fatigue where shifts routinely extend beyond schedule. | The observed pressure pattern occurs within normal shifts as well. A rigid cap can create handovers, raise WIP, move work to fewer staff, and worsen backlog. | Actual duty duration, overtime, uncompleted work at handover, WIP, queue age, handover count, risk-adjusted errors, rework, and staffing coverage. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour | Could reduce early overload for workers receiving inherited backlog and make the first hour more stable. | Delays may harm urgent cases, violate priority obligations, and simply transfer complexity and errors later. Seniors may appropriately be handling complex work early. | Complexity and priority age by hour, SLA compliance, first-hour and later-hour error/rework, queue age, high-priority waiting time, and complex-case assignment by experience level. |
| 3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP exceeds 70 | Strongly supported as an operational warning threshold. It can trigger staffing support, senior review, reprioritization, or protected quality checks. | An alert alone can cause alarm fatigue or be used to pressure workers further. WIP may be manipulated or shifted between queues. | Time above WIP 70, response time to alerts, queue age, risk-adjusted service time, error rate, rework, Quality Check completion, and workload distribution after alerts. |
| 4. Force random assignment of cases | Can reveal selection effects and reduce arbitrary allocation patterns. A limited experiment could improve fairness and identification. | Full randomization can remove skill-based routing, continuity, language matching, specialist access, and priority safeguards. It may worsen outcomes for complex cases. | Use constrained random assignment among eligible staff only. Monitor allocation balance, complexity by worker group, service time, error/rework, handovers, escalations, and priority SLA outcomes. |
| 5. Mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours | May improve attention and reduce fatigue-related or pressure-amplified errors, especially during sustained high-WIP periods. | If breaks are not staggered, they can increase queues and pressure; mandatory breaks without staffing relief may merely defer work. | Break uptake and timing, WIP during break windows, staffing coverage, post-break error/rework, service time, high-priority waiting time, and employee-reported workload. |
| 6. Defer low-priority Quality Checks to the next shift | May smooth end-of-shift bursts and prevent rushed checking if low-risk cases are explicitly identified. | Deferral can delay detection, increase handovers, obscure accountability, and allow errors to reach closure. Quality checks must not be removed merely to improve throughput. | Quality Check backlog and age, completion SLA, correction delay, missed-error rate, rework within seven days, cross-shift handovers, and risk-adjusted case outcomes. |
| 7. Add an extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks | Likely useful if Day demand peaks create high WIP and if seniors can handle complex cases, escalation, and targeted review. It may also reduce pressure on junior and mid-level staff. | Could shift bottlenecks elsewhere, overload the senior, or produce misleading improvement if the senior selectively handles only difficult cases. | Day-shift time above WIP 70, complex-case queue age, junior/mid-level high-WIP exposure, senior workload, risk-adjusted errors, rework, escalation rate, and end-to-end cycle time. |
| 8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates | No reliable quality benefit should be expected. At most, it may draw attention to a systemic risk pattern. | High risk of unfair punishment, under-reporting, gaming, avoidance of difficult cases, reluctance to document corrections, and worsened queue pressure. Raw late-shift error rates are confounded by case mix and workload. | Do not implement as a punishment metric. If monitored, use confidential, risk-adjusted team-level learning indicators; track reporting rates, correction detection, case allocation, rework, workload, and psychological-safety feedback. |

The most defensible near-term package is a non-punitive WIP alert coupled with staffing escalation, targeted senior support during Day peaks, staggered break experiments, and improved timing of Quality Checks. These should be evaluated as controlled operational pilots with risk-adjusted quality outcomes, not only throughput metrics.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair because workers do not receive identical work.

- **Selection bias:** Seniors may receive difficult early backlog, urgent cases, or exception work. A worker with a high raw error rate may be the worker assigned the hardest cases or the worker who detects and documents problems others miss.
- **Case complexity measurement is incomplete:** The supplied complexity score may not capture missing documents, customer responsiveness, legal ambiguity, language needs, handovers, or emotional escalation.
- **Event-level denominators can mislead:** A worker who performs many short activities will have a different denominator from a worker handling fewer complex end-to-end cases. Compare like activity families and case types.
- **Error detection is not error occurrence:** End-of-shift Quality Check bursts can increase recorded late errors even if the mistakes were made earlier. Rework may be detected days later. Attribute origin, detection, and correction separately.
- **Timestamp measurement error matters:** A short recorded service time may represent genuine efficiency, batch processing, delayed documentation, auto-completed events, or missing start timestamps.
- **Queue pressure may be management-created:** Staffing, demand forecasting, queue policies, service-level targets, handover rules, and deferred quality work can create the high-WIP conditions under which errors increase. It is inappropriate to treat WIP-driven quality failures solely as individual shortcomings.
- **Automation must be evaluated end-to-end:** Bot_Risk’s instantaneous service time is not evidence of superior performance if human correction and rework increase downstream. Bot accuracy, calibration, override rates, and differential impacts across case groups require separate monitoring.
- **Punitive error metrics create perverse incentives:** Penalties can suppress error reporting, discourage Quality Checks, encourage case avoidance, and shift work toward easier cases. They can make measured performance look better while actual risk worsens.
- **Fairness and privacy:** Resource-level analysis should use role-appropriate access controls, transparent methods, worker input, and aggregated reporting where possible. It should support staffing, training, tooling, and safe workflow design rather than ranking workers on raw metrics.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should show both raw and risk-adjusted indicators, with drill-down by shift, activity family, complexity band, priority, WIP band, experience group, and calendar period. It should emphasize team and process conditions rather than worker league tables.

| Indicator | Definition and key slices | Operational use |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Event service time | Median, 75th, 90th percentile, and risk-adjusted service time by activity, shift position, WIP band, resource group, and batch status. | Detect apparent speed-ups that may be caused by pressure, batching, automation, or timestamp anomalies. |
| 2. Case waiting time and queue age | Time from arrival to first work, time between activities, age of oldest queue items, and waiting time by priority and complexity. | Detect backlog harm that service-time averages hide; protect urgent and complex cases. |
| 3. WIP and queue pressure | WIP at event start, queue length, time above 70, queue growth rate, and WIP by shift and activity family. | Trigger staffing or workflow escalation before high-pressure quality degradation occurs. |
| 4. Error rate | Raw and risk-adjusted error rate, with separate views for original-decision errors, Quality Check detections, and correction events. | Avoid treating late detection as identical to late error creation. |
| 5. Rework and downstream correction | Rework within 7 days, correction-loop frequency, time to correction, and Bot_Risk-to-correction paths. | Measure end-to-end quality rather than relying only on immediate error flags. |
| 6. Complexity and priority mix | Mean complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, and complexity by worker group, shift hour, and queue state. | Detect selection effects and ensure difficult work is not concentrated unfairly or delayed inappropriately. |
| 7. Batching indicators | Consecutive same-activity run length, batch size, inter-event gap, queue-batch reuse, and post-batch error/rework rate. | Identify efficient batching versus batching associated with deferred work or quality risk. |
| 8. Quality Check timeliness | Time from decision to Quality Check, end-of-shift Quality Check concentration, Quality Check backlog, and correction burst size. | Prevent quality work from being silently deferred merely to improve throughput. |
| 9. Handover and queue ownership changes | Number of resource changes per case, cross-shift handovers, queue transfers, ownership duration, and error/rework after handover. | Identify loss of context, shift-end dumping, and workload transfer between teams. |
| 10. Automation quality | Bot_Risk volume, override rate, correction rate, rework after bot classification, and bot performance by WIP, complexity, and priority. | Ensure automation reduces total work and risk rather than only reducing recorded service time. |
| 11. Worker-load fairness | Events per staffed hour, complexity-adjusted workload, high-WIP exposure, high-priority exposure, overtime, break access, and handover burden by role group. | Identify uneven exposure to risky operating conditions without creating punitive individual rankings. |
| 12. Demand-capacity balance | Arrival rate, finish rate, backlog change, staffing level, absenteeism, and queue growth by hour and shift. | Distinguish worker performance issues from demand spikes or insufficient staffing. |

Dashboard alerts should be based on combinations rather than one metric alone. For example:

- **High-risk queue state:** WIP above 70, rising queue age, high-complexity share above baseline, and increasing risk-adjusted error rate.
- **Potential batching risk:** unusually long Document Review runs plus delayed Quality Checks and elevated later correction rates.
- **Automation concern:** Bot_Risk correction rate rises under high WIP while end-to-end rework increases.
- **Workload fairness concern:** one resource group receives disproportionately high-complexity or high-WIP work without corresponding staffing, review support, or recovery time.
