# 1. System boundary and assumptions

Use a **fulfilled, usable spare part** as the main functional unit, while also reporting totals per order and for all orders received. The boundary runs from availability check through picking or production, batching, inspection, packaging, outbound delivery, packaging or production rework, returns, and disposal or eventual shipment of surplus batch output. Count energy used by the recorded machine groups, material scrap and packaging, and transport including second shipments and empty van returns. Where defensible emission factors and bills of material exist, add upstream impacts of electricity, materials, packaging, and fuel; keep these estimates separate from directly observed kWh, kg, and transport kgCO2e. Customer equipment operation and the emissions consequences of downtime are outside the measured boundary, but downtime, service promises, and route redundancy are binding decision constraints.

The log is flattened by order although orders can have multiple lines, and the listed variants do not cover every path. Link lines, batches, packages, shipments, returns, and disposal events before attributing impact. The available data do not give electricity carbon intensity, full material composition, carrier load factors or empty mileage, return rates by path, or the ultimate fate of the 6% of batch-produced items still unshipped after 14 days. Do not sum energy kWh, scrap kg, and transport kgCO2e as if they shared a unit, or claim precise lifecycle savings from these data. Compare like-for-like part mix, size, destination, promise, and customer segment; count cancellations and later replacement orders so apparently cleaner completed orders do not hide displaced impact.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

| Dimension | Indicators from linked events and paths |
|---|---|
| Energy | Total `energy_kwh` and kWh per usable delivered item, by `machine_group`, case type, and production path; energy from rework loops separately. Track total energy as well as intensity. |
| Transport | Sum `transport_kgco2e` across *all* legs per order and delivered item, including partial/backorder shipments and van returns; air, ground, consolidated-truck, and dedicated-van shares by segment and promise slack. Record carrier load and empty distance when available. |
| Materials | `material_scrap_kg` and `package_material_kg` per usable item; damaged returns, replacement packaging, and batch units unshipped after 14 days and ultimately scrapped. Separate heavy-part reinforced packs from avoidable overpacking. |
| Rework and quality | Rate and count of Inspection → Rework production → CNC loops, packaging-rework loops, damage returns, and first-pass yield, by machine, part geometry, weight, pack type, and route. |
| Batching | `batch_size`, kWh per item, Batch wait and heat-treatment wait distributions, due-date slack at release, late risk, and eventual obsolescence by batch. |
| Service | Original-promise and revised-promise late rates, median and p90 order-to-delivery time, first-item and complete-order delivery time, partial shipments, and delay cancellations by `case_type` × `customer_type`. Record customer downtime where available. |
| Resilience | Availability and tested delivery time of an alternative route for each Service-contract lane, carrier concentration, recovery during disruption, and justified emergency-capacity use. |

Report both absolute monthly impact and impact per usable delivered item, with case mix and the number of received, cancelled, returned, and replaced orders. Otherwise lower intensity can coexist with more total waste or emissions.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batch efficiency can become surplus waste.** A larger CNC_A or heat batch saves kWh per produced item but adds up to 34 h of median batch wait in the stated CNC_A comparison, threatens promises, and may leave forecast-driven parts obsolete. Measure kWh and scrap per *shipped usable* item and final fate of unshipped units, not just kWh per machine output.
2. **Waiting can trigger a dirtier recovery path.** A batch or truck-consolidation hold can cause late orders, followed by air, emergency CNC, dedicated vans, or cancellation and reorder. Include these downstream paths when evaluating the initial saving.
3. **Transport savings can shift burden to service and resilience.** Replacing all critical vans with ground would expose the observed 9% late critical stock/ground exception rather than the 3% dedicated-van path. A single consolidated carrier can also fail during disruption. Keep eligibility rules, capacity reserves, and alternative routes.
4. **Packaging reduction can multiply returns.** Reinforced packaging doubles packaging material yet cuts damage-related MTO heavy-part returns by 40%. Using standard packs outside Q1 can create replacement material, rework, and transport that exceed pack savings.
5. **Extra inspection can simply move the queue.** A quality gate lowers rework only if it catches defects early and has enough capacity; an overloaded inspection cell raises lateness and can induce rushed checks or express shipping. Monitor queue time and first-pass yield together.
6. **Machine switching can displace impact.** Sending all CNC_B work to CNC_A may lower rework but congest CNC_A, increase waiting or heat-batch pressure, and make the operation dependent on one machine. Retain CNC_B as backup and compare part-adjusted outcomes.
7. **Combining shipments can delay the needed part.** A full-kit rule lowers parcel count while a missing noncritical line holds a critical available line. Track first-item delivery, complete-order delivery, and customer downtime separately.
8. **A changed promise can mask worse service or change demand.** A later MTO promise may prompt customers to stockpile, split orders, select precautionary urgent status, or buy elsewhere. Track original requested dates, accepted dates, cancellations, duplicate orders, and total demand as well as on-time percentage.
9. **Relative efficiency can hide volume rebound.** Lower transport kgCO2e per delivered item may accompany more shipments or more obsolete production. Maintain absolute impact, per-order impact, and per-usable-item impact side by side.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a connected decision path. At receipt, identify actual downtime, customer contract, line-level availability, original requested date, and feasible routes. At availability check, apply a full-kit rule to non-urgent orders where it preserves the required date; reserve partial shipment for a critical line or a clear service benefit. For stock orders, priority-pick before selecting ground or a twice-daily consolidated truck when calibrated on-time probability and segment service targets are met. Preserve air for short slack and disruption; preserve at least one independent Service-contract route.

For MTO, schedule CNC_A and heat treatment with due-date-based maximum waits, minimum sensible load, and explicit release when a wait cap is reached. Choose CNC_B when CNC_A's queue would break a promise or as a failover. Add a targeted, adequately staffed inspection gate for the machine/part combinations causing rework loops, followed by a geometry-, weight-, and route-aware packaging check. Keep reinforced packaging for heavy MTO parts unless Q1's inspection exception is documented. This attacks hidden rework and material loss before trying to save packaging mass.

For Critical-downtime, distinguish verified downtime from precautionary urgency. Test shared emergency courier lanes against actual critical deadlines, while retaining dedicated vans for cases where pooled or ground service cannot reliably protect downtime. Require an SP2 justification event, including for an appropriate van use. The aim is to cut avoidable emergency trips without weakening response or route redundancy.

Prioritize packaging compatibility, targeted quality checks, priority-pick/ground, and promise-aware route selection because they address documented waste with limited required waiting. Pilot dynamic batching and consolidation under explicit wait caps and obsolescence checks. Offer Regular MTO customers a genuine choice of promise dates. Do not score a redesign as successful if it reduces a transport metric while increasing late deliveries, damage, cancellations, or unshipped stock.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

First establish a baseline at order-line, batch, shipment, and return level, linking repeated events to the same order. Stratify by the five stated segments, part weight/geometry, destination, lane, machine, promise slack, and disruption period. Compare total impact and per-usable-item impact, median and p90 times, original- and accepted-promise lateness, first and complete delivery, rework, returns, cancellations, and obsolescence. The selected paths are descriptive associations; use matched or staggered pilots with comparable controls, and report uncertainty rather than attributing every difference to an intervention.

Run the recommender in shadow mode to calibrate on-time probabilities, then pilot eligible stock lanes and MTO cells in stages. Set stop or rollback triggers for material increases in segment-level late rate, p90, damage returns, cancellations, critical downtime, or loss of alternative route; retain the current 5% Standard-stock Service-contract and 4% Critical-downtime late rates as particularly important reference points. Assess the whole chain after a suitable return and obsolescence window, not just shipment day. Use the observed 35–55% consolidation benefit and 4–6× air-versus-ground emissions only as hypotheses to check on the actual switched lanes.

Monitor SP1 at each shipment decision: if ground or consolidation had a calibrated >85% probability of meeting the promise, record whether a higher-emission route was chosen and why. Also enforce the stricter applicable service guardrail, since 85% alone could allow deterioration in Service-contract service. For SP2, require a timestamped Critical-downtime justification for every dedicated van and audit the current 31% missing-event gap. For Q1, flag each MTO part over 8 kg sent without reinforced packaging unless a low-damage-risk inspection certificate exists; link violations to returns and packaging rework. Maintain R1 with periodic disruption drills and measured failover times.

Give operations a short, standardized override set—true downtime, low route reliability, carrier disruption, part-specific damage risk, and customer-approved promise change—with responsible owner and later review. Review override frequency and outcomes weekly by segment and lane; investigate systematic exceptions as a rule or capacity problem, not merely operator noncompliance. Because the discovered model has low precision around emergency paths and partial shipments, validate cases through their event attributes and linked line histories before treating unusual variants as violations. Update route probabilities, batching caps, inspection capacity, and exception rules from measured outcomes.
