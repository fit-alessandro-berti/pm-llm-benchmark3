# 1. Fairness dimensions

Evaluating public-benefits administration requires looking beyond raw outcome parity to assess whether the operational process treats applicants equitably.

```
                     
                               EVALUATING FAIRNESS            
                     
                                          
                  
                                                                 
                           
         OUTCOME PARITY                              PROCEDURAL FAIRNESS   
      Unadjusted percentages                        Adjusted process flow  
                           
                                                                 
                     
                                                                              
Approval                    Raw Appeal       Timeliness       Procedural       Resource
 Rates                        Rates          & Queues          Burden        Allocation
```

* **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates vary significantly across applicant groups (Group A: 78%, Group C: 69%, Group B: 63%, Group D: 58%). While raw disparities highlight potential inequities, they combine baseline case-mix differences (e.g., risk scores, document completeness) with operational process effects.
* **Adjusted outcome disparity:** Controlling for documented complexity (risk band, document completeness, channel, region, household size, prior cases), unexplained approval gaps persist: Group B is -5.5 percentage points lower than Group A, Group D is -3.1 percentage points lower, and Group C is -2.2 percentage points lower.
* **Procedural burden:** Applicants requiring support encounter higher administrative friction. Group B faces a +9.5 percentage point adjusted increase in receiving a `Request Missing Documents` step, and Group B and C cases suffer frequent multi-touch handovers across caseworkers, regional officers, and support specialists.
* **Timeliness:** Adjusted cycle times reveal structural operational delay: Group C cases take +4.1 days longer and Group B cases take +2.6 days longer, even after controlling for risk and completeness.
* **Consistency of routing:** Similarly situated cases follow different pathways depending on group membership. Group B (+8.0 percentage points) and Group C (+5.5 percentage points) have significantly higher adjusted probabilities of being routed to `Supervisor Review`, indicating potential double-handling or risk-averse caseworker routing.
* **Quality and accessibility of support:** Utilizing rights-preserving services introduces large queue delays. Accessing `Language Support Contact` adds a median queue of 3.8 days (Group B), while `Accommodation Review` adds 4.7 days (Group C). This acts as a time penalty for exercising statutory accommodation rights.
* **Appeal and rework burden:** Rework rates (reopen/rework: Group C 24%, Group B 22%, Group D 20%, Group A 11%) and appeal rates (Group D 16%, Group B 13%) show that protected and complex groups encounter higher decision dissatisfaction and post-decision administrative load.
* **Resource-allocation fairness:** Capacity distribution creates operational bottlenecks: 1,250 Group B cases rely heavily on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, while 820 Group C cases rely on 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom dual-hat as supervisors, creating queue conflicts).

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish legitimate routing (driven by complexity) from procedural discrimination or operational bottlenecks, the analysis must isolate process friction from applicant attributes.

```
                      
                          EVENT LOG RAW DATASET        
                      
                                       
                                       
                      
                       Stratification & Propensity     
                       Matching (Risk, Region, Channel)
                      
                                       
        
                                                                    
                              
 Variant &                    Waiting Time                 Resource &   
 Path Alignment               Decomposition                Handover Net 
                              
                                                                    
        
                                       
                                       
                      
                       Sensitivity & Bias Audits       
                       (Covariate & Risk Score Testing)
                      
```

### Matching and Stratification Variables
Construct propensity scores or exact-matched sub-cohorts using:
* `risk_score_band`
* `documentation_completeness`
* `channel` (online vs. in-person)
* `household_size_band`
* `prior_case_count`
* `region`
* Intake month (to control for seasonality)

### Path and Variant Comparison
* Restrict comparison to cases following identical structural paths. For instance, evaluate outcome and timing differences within **Variant V2** (`Submit  Verify  Completeness  Request Docs  Eligibility  Decision  Notify`) across groups to measure if Group B/C cases experience longer processing given identical activity sequences.
* Compare standard low-risk cases (**Variant V1**) between Group A and minority groups to detect baseline disparity in the absence of support steps.

### Resource-Assignment Comparison
* Measure caseworker case-load density, resource availability, and handover complexity.
* Quantify how often Group B and C cases are handed off between generalists, specialists, and supervisors compared to Group A cases with similar risk profiles.

### Waiting-Time Decomposition
* Deconstruct total duration into **active service time** (e.g., executing `Eligibility Assessment`) versus **queue/waiting time** (e.g., waiting in queue between `Accommodation Review` and `Eligibility Assessment`).
* Isolate queue delays caused by resource capacity shortages from delay caused by applicant document delivery lag.

### Sensitivity Analysis for Biased Covariates
* Conduct sensitivity checks by removing `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` from the matching model. Because documentation incompleteness may stem from poor form translation and risk scores may reflect historical bias, controlling for them directly could mask underlying systemic discrimination.

### Subgroup Checks by Region and Channel
* Stratify analyses by regional office and application channel (`online` vs. `in-person`). Compare Group B vs. Group A strictly within in-person channels in identical regions to isolate regional operational inefficiency from group-level discrimination.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Analyzing variant distributions, waiting times, and resource assignments highlights key operational friction points:

```
                  TYPICAL QUEUE & HANDOVER BOTTLENECK

  Group B:
  [Submit] 1.4d [Verify] 1.6d [Lang Support] 3.8d [Eligibility] 1.9d [Supervisor]
   (In-person queue)       (Bilingual staff wait)     (7 Caseworkers queue)      (Overloaded regional)

  Group C:
  [Submit] 1.2d [Verify]  [Accom Review] 4.7d [Eligibility] 1.6d [Supervisor]
                                           (5 Specialists queue)                 (Dual-role bottleneck)
```

### Key Procedural Disparities

1. **Language Support Queue Penalty (Group B):**
   * **Data:** Variant V4 accounts for 24% of Group B cases (median cycle time: 19.2 days). Moving from `Language Support Contact` to `Eligibility Assessment` incurs a median waiting time of **3.8 days**.
   * **Assessment:** *Concerning.* While providing language support is rights-preserving, restricting this workload to 7 bilingual caseworkers creates an operational bottleneck that delays application processing.

2. **Accommodation Queue & Dual-Role Bottleneck (Group C):**
   * **Data:** Variant V5 represents 31% of Group C cases (median cycle time: 21.6 days). The step `Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment` adds a **4.7-day median wait time**. Group C cases are assigned to 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also handle `Supervisor Review`.
   * **Assessment:** *Concerning.* The dual role creates a structural conflict of interest and workflow backlog, penalizing disability accommodation requests with the longest cycle time across non-appealed paths (21.6 days vs. 5.8 days for V1).

3. **Excess Document Requests and Document Incompleteness Wait (Group B & C):**
   * **Data:** Group B exhibits 46% incomplete documentation, and Group C exhibits 36%. Waiting between `Completeness Check` and `Request Missing Documents` takes **1.6 days for Group B** versus **0.4 days for Group A**. Adjusted probability of `Request Missing Documents` is +9.5 percentage points for Group B.
   * **Assessment:** *Mixed / Concerning.* High incomplete documentation rates partly reflect language and accessibility barriers in the initial application forms. The 1.6-day delay to issue a document request indicates lagging caseworker processing for minority-language queues.

4. **Supervisor Review Disparity (Group B & C):**
   * **Data:** Controlling for complexity, Group B (+8.0 pp) and Group C (+5.5 pp) undergo `Supervisor Review` far more often than Group A baseline cases. Wait times from `Supervisor Review` to `Decision Draft` are 1.9 days (Group B) and 1.6 days (Group C) versus 1.0 day (Group A).
   * **Assessment:** *Concerning.* Higher supervisor routing for non-high-risk cases suggests risk-averse caseworker behavior or implicit bias regarding language-assisted or accommodation-adjusted applications.

5. **Fraud Review and Risk Routing (Group D):**
   * **Data:** Group D cases are heavily routed through Variant V3 (28%), which includes `Risk Screening  Fraud Review  Supervisor Review` (18.4 days median duration). Risk Screening  Fraud Review queue is 2.8 days for Group D.
   * **Assessment:** *Partially Justified / Conditionally Concerning.* High fraud routing aligns with Group D’s 51% high `risk_score_band`. However, Group D exhibits the highest appeal rate (16%, adjusted +5.7 pp), indicating that decisions coming out of this high-scrutiny pipeline may have higher error rates or poorer explanations.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

The observed disparities across groups may be generated by several operational mechanisms:

```

                           PLAUSIBLE MECHANISMS                              

                LEGITIMATE                              CONCERNING          

 1. Objective Risk-Based Fraud Routing         3. Specialized Resource      
    High risk scores legitimately trigger         Capacity Bottlenecks      
    Fraud Review checks.                          Severe understaffing in    
                                                  support roles creates     
 2. Complex Document Verification Needs           queue penalties.          
    Legitimate delays when processing multi-                                
    step document reviews.                     4. Differential Risk Aversion
                                                  Unnecessary routing to    
                                                  supervisors for support   
                                                  cases.                    

```

1. **Specialized Resource Capacity Bottlenecks (Structural Operational Friction - Concerning):**
   * *Mechanism:* Group B and Group C are funneled into dedicated pools (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists). Because these teams are understaffed relative to demand and lack service-level targets, structural queues form before eligibility processing can begin.
2. **Differential Risk Aversion & Double-Handling (Implicit Bias / Procedural Discrimination - Concerning):**
   * *Mechanism:* General caseworkers may feel less confident assessing applications with non-standard document formats, translated forms, or accommodation modifications. To mitigate perceived error risk, caseworkers route Group B (+8.0 pp) and Group C (+5.5 pp) cases to `Supervisor Review` even when risk scores are low, increasing processing time and touchpoints.
3. **Upstream Application Design Barriers (Measurement & Access Barrier - Concerning):**
   * *Mechanism:* Initial application portals and forms may lack clear translated guidance or accessible form controls. This causes higher initial document incompleteness (Group B: 46%, Group C: 36%), which forces cases into `Request Missing Documents` loops (V2/V4) and extends cycle times.
4. **Legitimate Objective Risk-Based Routing (Workflow Design - Legitimate):**
   * *Mechanism:* Cases with `risk_score_band = High` (Group D: 51%) automatically trigger `Fraud Review` and `Supervisor Review` per program integrity guidelines. This accounts for Group D's higher usage of Variant V3 (28%) and higher baseline processing duration.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The event log attributes provide process-level visibility, but key data limitations constrain definitive conclusions:

* **Measurement Bias in `documentation_completeness`:** A flag of "incomplete" treats all missing items equally. It does not distinguish whether an application missed a critical eligibility document or an auxiliary form, nor does it capture whether incompleteness was caused by unclear translated form instructions.
* **Historical Bias in `risk_score_band`:** The risk score model may rely on historical administrative data containing structural biases (e.g., higher past audit rates or prior case counts in specific zip codes/regions). If the risk score over-classifies Group D or Group B as high risk, downstream fraud routing is systematically biased.
* **Unobserved Applicant Needs and Urgency:** The dataset lacks measures of applicant vulnerability, digital literacy, emergency financial need, or severe medical conditions, preventing an assessment of whether processing speed aligns with applicant urgency.
* **Unobserved Resource Workload and Intraday Capacity:** Resource utilization is captured via discrete event logs without showing total active concurrent caseloads, off-log administrative tasks, or regional office staffing disruptions.
* **Lack of Qualitative Outcome Quality Metrics:** `case_outcome` records binary decisions (approved/denied) but omits decision justification quality, accuracy, applicant dignity during interactions, or whether appeals stemmed from notification errors versus incorrect eligibility determinations.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

```

                          MITIGATION STRATEGIES                              

 Strategy                       Primary Objective                           

 Capacity Addition              Eliminate queue bottlenecks in support      
 Target SLAs for Support        Enforce timeliness equity for support steps 
 Concurrent Routing Redesign    Remove multi-touch sequential handovers     
 Decision Explanation Redesign  Reduce post-decision appeal/rework rate     
 Algorithmic Risk Audits        Correct upstream scoring & form barriers    

```

### 1. Adding Capacity for Language Support and Accommodation Review
* **Mitigation:** Expand the pool of bilingual caseworkers from 7 to 20 and accommodation specialists from 5 to 15 through hiring and cross-training general staff.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Increases operational personnel costs. Cross-training general staff without rigorous quality control may lead to initial assessment errors or inconsistent application of accommodation rules.

### 2. Implementing Service-Level Targets (SLAs) for Support Steps
* **Mitigation:** Mandate maximum queue time SLAs (e.g., <24 hours) for `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` to prevent rights-preserving steps from delaying processing.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Staff may rush support reviews to avoid SLA breaches, potentially decreasing support quality or closing support tickets prematurely without fully assisting the applicant.

### 3. Routing Redesign to Reduce Multi-Touch Handovers
* **Mitigation:** Replace sequential handovers (`General  Support Specialist  General  Supervisor`) with integrated single-touch handling or parallel processing where support specialists complete eligibility assessments directly.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Requires broader job descriptions and system permission updates; support specialists may face higher overall workload per case, reducing throughput per specialist.

### 4. Decision-Explanation Improvements and Standardized Forms
* **Mitigation:** Redesign decision notices and missing document requests into plain language, offering pre-translated templates and explicit denial rationale.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Requires initial capital outlay for IT portal redesign and translation vendor contracting; may slightly increase drafting time during `Decision Draft`.

### 5. Monitoring Adjusted Disparities
* **Mitigation:** Establish automated process-mining dashboards that monitor adjusted cycle times, supervisor routing rates, and outcome gaps in real time across applicant groups.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Operational teams might optimize specifically for monitored indicators (Goodhart's Law), such as reducing supervisor reviews for complex cases where review is genuinely warranted.

### 6. Auditing `risk_score_band` and Application Forms
* **Mitigation:** Commission an independent algorithmic audit of the risk-scoring model to eliminate biased proxies, and perform usability testing on translated and accessible intake forms.
* **Side Effects / Trade-offs:** Recalibrating risk models may temporarily increase false-negative fraud risk or require temporary manual review during model transition.

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The following Python script utilizes `pm4py` to filter logs, extract process variants, compute performance DFGs, construct handover networks, analyze queue times, and verify procedural constraints across applicant groups.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

def run_procedural_fairness_analysis(event_log_path: str):
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 1. Load and Format Event Log
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    df = pd.read_csv(event_log_path)
    df['time:timestamp'] = pd.to_datetime(df['time:timestamp'])
    
    # Format dataframe to PM4Py standards
    log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id='case:concept:name',
        activity_key='concept:name',
        timestamp_key='time:timestamp'
    )

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 2. Filter Sub-Logs by Applicant Group
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    log_a = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', ['Group A'], level='case')
    log_b = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', ['Group B'], level='case')
    log_c = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', ['Group C'], level='case')
    log_d = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'applicant_group', ['Group D'], level='case')

    print(f"Case Counts - A: {len(log_a['case:concept:name'].unique())}, "
          f"B: {len(log_b['case:concept:name'].unique())}, "
          f"C: {len(log_c['case:concept:name'].unique())}, "
          f"D: {len(log_d['case:concept:name'].unique())}")

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 3. Variant Analysis & Distribution
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    variants_a = pm4py.get_variants(log_a)
    variants_b = pm4py.get_variants(log_b)
    variants_c = pm4py.get_variants(log_c)
    
    print("\nTop Variants Group B:")
    for v, cases in list(variants_b.items())[:3]:
        print(f"Variant: {v} | Count: {len(cases)}")

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 4. Performance Directly-Follows Graph (DFG) & Waiting Time Insertion
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Calculate performance DFG for Group B and Group C to detect queues
    perf_dfg_b, start_b, end_b = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log_b)
    perf_dfg_c, start_c, end_c = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log_c)

    # Insert waiting and service time attributes into log
    log_with_times = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
    
    # Extract overall case durations across groups
    durations_a = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(log_a)
    durations_b = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(log_b)
    durations_c = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(log_c)

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 5. Handover of Work & Resource Networks
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Handover network for Group B to isolate generalist <-> bilingual transfers
    handover_b = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log_b)
    
    # Working together network for Group C accommodation specialists
    work_together_c = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log_c)
    
    # Organizational roles discovery
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
    print("\nDiscovered Roles:", roles)

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 6. Filter Specific Path Performance (e.g., Support Queues)
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Filter paths specifically through Language Support Contact -> Eligibility Assessment
    lang_support_paths = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
        log_b, 
        [('Language Support Contact', 'Eligibility Assessment')]
    )
    
    # Filter paths through Accommodation Review -> Eligibility Assessment
    accom_paths = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
        log_c, 
        [('Accommodation Review', 'Eligibility Assessment')]
    )

    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # 7. Procedural Rules Verification (Log Skeleton / DECLARE)
    # -------------------------------------------------------------------------
    # Check rule compliance: e.g., 'Accommodation Review' must always lead to 'Eligibility Assessment'
    skeleton = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log_c, noise_threshold=0.1)
    print("\nLog Skeleton Constraints for Group C:")
    print(skeleton['directly_follows'])

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Path to event log CSV
    run_procedural_fairness_analysis("public_benefits_event_log.csv")
```

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

### Assessment: Procedurally Unfair

The public-benefits case-management process exhibits **procedural unfairness** toward applicants requiring minority language support (Group B) and disability accommodations (Group C). 

While raw outcome gaps are partially driven by case mix (such as higher document incompleteness and higher risk scores in Group D), controlling for these variables reveals systemic operational penalties linked directly to protected attributes and rights-preserving requests:

1. **Structural Time Penalties for Rights-Preserving Support:** Applicants requesting language support or disability accommodations incur mandatory queue penalties of 3.8 and 4.7 median days respectively, driven by severe staffing shortages (7 bilingual staff, 5 accommodation specialists).
2. **Unexplained Differential Scrutiny:** Even after controlling for risk scores and completeness, Group B (+8.0 percentage points) and Group C (+5.5 percentage points) face significantly higher probabilities of being routed to `Supervisor Review`, resulting in extra handovers and processing delays (+2.6 to +4.1 days).
3. **Upstream Administrative Friction:** High initial rates of incomplete documentation among Group B (46%) and Group C (36%) indicate that application interfaces and forms lack sufficient accessibility and plain-language guidance, creating avoidable rework cycles.

### Conclusion
The process fails procedural fairness standards by penalizing applicants who exercise statutory rights to language support and disability accommodations with prolonged processing times, additional administrative reviews, and higher handover burdens. Operational interventions must focus on expanding specialist capacity, eliminating unnecessary supervisor routing, and redesigning accessible intake tools.
