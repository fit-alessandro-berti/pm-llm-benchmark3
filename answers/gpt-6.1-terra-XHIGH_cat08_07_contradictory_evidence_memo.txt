# 1. Executive memo

**Decision: choose E, a guarded segment-specific deployment, combined with a controlled experiment and better measurement before any wider rollout.** Keep the redesigned path for verified simple, low-risk houses, where all reported segment outcomes improved. For apartments, older buildings and other uncertain-risk cases, restore the field survey and permit controls pending validation. Require a successful activation test before billing starts, subject to verified legal and contractual requirements; route any exception for explicit review. Retain coordinator ownership with a named backup and shared case context, but do not assume it can be scaled safely at current staffing.

The pilot delivered a faster typical case: median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, and cases with at least three handovers fell from 34% to 12%. Yet the p95 rose from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, and complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%. The estimated full cost rose from €198 to €207 even as measured pre-complaint cost fell from €184 to €161. That estimate is incomplete. These outcomes make national scaling premature, but a blanket rollback would sacrifice the strong gains reported for simple houses.

The evidence is consistent with a fast lane for straightforward orders and a slower, rework-heavy exception path for difficult ones. Survey skipping, activation after billing, and overloaded coordinators are plausible contributors, not proven causes. Apartments and older buildings were more common in pilot regions than in the baseline; early pilot risk attributes are incomplete; and incomplete downstream costs and case maturity may distort comparisons. We should not attribute the entire change to R, nor assume that its simple-house benefit will transfer nationally.

Act now on the visible exposure: enforce activation-before-billing and verify permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings; correct any affected customer billing or service issues through the normal operational process. Review exceptions using the underlying events and case records: alignment deviations are diagnostic signals, not themselves proof of a violation. Add backup coverage and monitor coordinator and revisit-team queues in the two regions at 93% and 92% utilization respectively. Use the available survey capacity to restore checks, subject to local staffing and routing feasibility.

Run a prospectively assigned, preferably randomized regional comparison against the appropriate baseline workflow, stratified by verified building risk and region. Compare end-to-end outcomes, including tails, first-time-right, complaints and 60-day downstream cost, as well as median speed. Validate the risk rule against surveyed outcomes and capture reason codes, activation/billing and permit conformance, handover/absence effects, and the customer experience of working service. Restrict any survey-skip experiment to cases for which skipping is permitted and operationally safe; keep permit and activation controls in both arms. A staged test can isolate coordinator ownership from survey skipping and activation ordering rather than treating R as one indivisible package.

Broader rollout requires evidence that the gains persist in each intended segment without unacceptable degradation in tail duration, rework, complaints, complete cost or activation and permit compliance. Pre-register thresholds and sample sizes, review mature cohorts and uncertainty, and stop or narrow exposure if guardrails fail. Until then, retain the simple-house benefit while containing exposure in higher-risk cases.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness, low precision:** Fitness measures how well recorded traces can be replayed by the model; precision concerns how much additional behavior the model permits beyond what was observed. A permissive pilot model can replay many observed cases yet allow numerous combinations of survey skipping, manual activation, repeat visits and billing. It need not mean that all those combinations happened. Lower generalization adds concern about how well the model will represent other cases. Moves-on-log denote recorded events not matched by the modeled path; moves-on-model denote expected modeled events absent from the observed trace. Check the underlying log and model before labeling any case a breach.
- **Faster median, slower tail:** Merging validation and scheduling and bypassing surveys can speed the many straightforward cases, while failed visits, manual activation, missing permit handling and rescheduling can delay a smaller group substantially. Temporal deviations and segment results are consistent with that mechanism, but changing case mix and incomplete follow-up can also change the distribution. A median improvement does not imply improvement for every customer.
- **Fewer handovers, less resilience:** A coordinator can reduce transfers and provide accountability, while concentrating knowledge and decisions in one person. The simpler handover network and more centralized working-together network are compatible. High utilization and absence-related stalls suggest a capacity and continuity risk, though they do not isolate its causal effect.
- **Lower visible cost, higher downstream cost:** Skipping or deferring work can reduce measured initial cost while shifting work into second visits, manual activation and complaints. The €207 figure is an incomplete estimate, so the net cost increase is a concern rather than a settled result. Measure consistent costs over a mature follow-up period.
- **Different segments, different outcomes:** Simple houses may benefit from a short path because their installation is predictable. Apartments and older buildings may need surveys, permits or specialist coordination; bypassing checks may generate rework. The pilot also overrepresents such buildings relative to baseline, and early risk attributes are missing. Stratified comparisons with consistent definitions are needed to distinguish intervention effects from composition and recording differences.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | When reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures median speed, lower visible cost and fewer handovers at scale. | Exposes more customers to the observed tail, rework, complaints, possible billing/activation and permit problems, and concentrated workload. Pilot may not transfer. | Only after controlled, segment-specific evidence meets rollout guardrails, operational capacity is adequate, and activation and permit controls are verified. Not justified now. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Restores familiar surveys and sequencing while containing exposure to unresolved pilot risks. | Gives up the clear observed simple-house gains and may restore unnecessary handovers and waits; baseline also had breaches and rework. | Appropriate if immediate controls cannot be enforced, customer harm is severe, or a segment fails guardrails and cannot be safely isolated. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation** | Preserves accountability while removing two suspected sources of risk. | Coordinator overload, absence and centralized knowledge can still create delays; ownership's independent effect is not established. | Reasonable where capacity, backup and shared context are in place and controlled evidence shows ownership helps without harming tails or quality. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Estimates effects and tests mechanisms with more comparable groups. | Delays some benefits, costs time and needs adequate sample sizes and mature outcomes; experimentation cannot override required controls. | Appropriate for uncertain segments or components after safeguards and measurement are in place. Combine with E. |
| **E. Simple-house deployment, controls for apartments and old buildings, measurement before wider scale** | Keeps the strongest observed segment benefit while containing higher-risk exposure and generating evidence. | Risk classification errors, local capacity limits and observational segment comparisons could undermine the result; other segments remain uncertain. | Best current choice if eligibility can be verified, exceptions can be routed to a controlled path, and guardrails are actively monitored. |

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by expected ability to change an imminent decision, considering urgency, actionability and the limits of current evidence. Several measures should run in parallel; a lower rank does not mean optional.

| Rank | Measurement or experiment | Uncertainty resolved and decision it could change |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M7: Conformance monitoring for billing before activation and permit-check absence** | Establishes the frequency and context of recorded deviations and identifies cases for review. Confirm whether events are missing, checks occurred outside the log, and rules apply. Verified failures would trigger correction, tighter controls or a pause; reliable compliance is a prerequisite for expansion. |
| 2 | **M6: Validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes** | Measures classification errors, especially risky addresses labeled safe to skip. Poor sensitivity or unstable performance argues for restoring surveys and revising eligibility; strong external validation could support a bounded test. Include surveyed cases across risk levels to avoid selective verification bias. |
| 3 | **M4: Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing** | Tests whether apparent savings survive complaints, revisits and other downstream work. A higher complete cost could block or narrow expansion even if median speed improves; a sustained reduction would strengthen the case. Compare mature cohorts using consistent cost definitions. |
| 4 | **M3: Structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, complaints and second visits** | Identifies which failure modes drive rework and where controls or capacity might help. Distinct permit, survey, activation and scheduling causes would direct targeted fixes and experiment design; unknown or inconsistent coding limits attribution. |
| 5 | **M1: Randomized survey skipping by building-risk stratum** | Estimates the causal effect of skipping on speed, first-time-right, tails and downstream cost, after risk validation. A favorable effect within a verified stratum could justify selective skipping; a harmful effect would support continued surveys. Restrict randomization to permissible, safe cases, retain required permit and activation controls, and monitor early harm. |
| 6 | **M5: Coordinator versus shared-team ownership under absence** | Estimates whether continuity and backup improve resilience and whether single ownership adds value independently. Evidence of fewer stalls without loss of accountability could justify shared coverage or change the ownership design before scale. |
| 7 | **M2: Start timestamps for installation, activation and manual activation** | Separates waiting from active work and clarifies where queues form; completion timestamps alone cannot do so. Long waits would support staffing or routing changes; long processing times might support different technical fixes. |
| 8 | **M8: Separate experience of appointment speed and working service** | Reveals whether customers value the faster appointment enough to offset service failures, and reduces reliance on a single post-close NPS. Poor working-service experience would strengthen restrictions even if speed is popular; improved scores across both dimensions would support expansion. |

For causal comparisons, assign cases prospectively within region and verified risk strata, record allocation and exclusions, and compare like-for-like outcomes. Plan sample sizes for the slower-to-observe tail and complaint outcomes; report effect estimates and uncertainty, not just point estimates. Track in-flight cases as well as completed cohorts to avoid excluding the longest cases. Follow cases through 30 days for complaints and 60 days after billing for cost, allowing enough time for those windows to mature.

# 6. Final recommendation

- **Immediate action:** Adopt E. Verify risk classification, require successful activation before billing subject to applicable requirements, verify permit handling, review flagged cases, and provide coordinator backup and shared case context. Monitor revisit queues and customer impact.
- **Pause:** Pause national rollout, survey skipping for medium-risk or uncertain addresses, and routine post-billing activation checks. Do not treat an alignment deviation alone as a confirmed violation.
- **Scale:** Continue or expand the simplified path only for verified simple low-risk houses, in controlled stages with local capacity and monitoring. Preserve coordinator ownership where backup is functioning. Route unclassified cases to the controlled survey and permit path.
- **Measure next:** Start M7, M6, M4 and M3 immediately; prepare the safeguarded M1 experiment and M5 ownership comparison, alongside M2 and M8. Use comparable, mature cohorts and stratify by region and risk.
- **Evidence for broader rollout:** Before testing, agree and register thresholds with operations and customer owners. As an initial decision rule, require each proposed segment to show a meaningful median improvement versus a concurrent comparator, with no material deterioration in p90 or p95, first-time-right, 30-day complaints, SLA breaches or complete 60-day cost. For example, set non-inferiority margins of at most 2 days for p90, 3 days for p95, and 2 percentage points each for first-time-right, complaints and SLA breaches; require complete mean cost to be no more than 5% higher unless an explicit business decision accepts that trade-off. Require confidence intervals to exclude deterioration beyond those margins, sufficient mature cases in each segment, acceptable staffing and backup, and verified adherence to applicable activation and permit requirements. These are proposed management thresholds, not established statistical or legal standards; size and feasibility should be confirmed before the experiment. If a guardrail fails or evidence is too imprecise, do not expand that segment; investigate, correct and retest, or revert it to the controlled baseline path.
