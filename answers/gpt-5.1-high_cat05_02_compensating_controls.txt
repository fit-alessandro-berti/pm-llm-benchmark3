## 1. Control-Objective Decomposition

FEP-1 (“different human for Prepare_Payment and Release_Payment”) bundles several underlying objectives and threat scenarios. At least the following apply:

1. **O1 – Catch ordinary data-entry and process errors by a second human**
   - Scenario: Preparer mistypes amount, vendor, or bank details; second person should notice before an erroneous payment is completed.
   - **AV-2**: Preventive for some structured errors (duplicate invoice, bank-change proximity, operator limit, basic PO mismatch). It does **not** check receipt of goods or nuanced commercial sense, so it only partially substitutes for a human second look.
   - **MG-4**: Preventive/detective when performed before release or settlement; the manager can catch incorrect details that AV-2 does not cover. If done after settlement, it is only detective.
   - **RA-7**: Detective; partly corrective only if errors are found while recall or cancellation is still possible.
   - **ED-1**: Indirect; it does not itself detect errors, but forces a structured combination of AV-2, MG-4, and RA-7 under stricter conditions, improving error-detection coverage when four-eyes is relaxed.

2. **O2 – Prevent unilateral fraudulent payments by a single operator**
   - Scenario: Operator intentionally pays a related party or fictitious vendor, acting alone.
   - **AV-2**: May prevent some frauds (duplicate invoices, risky bank changes, sanctions hits, account not owned by vendor, amount outside limits), but cannot detect all self-dealing or fabricated services.
   - **MG-4**: Strong preventive/detective control **if** the manager is independent, actually reviews documents and bank details, and acts before settlement. If the manager is conflicted or rubber-stamps, it becomes weak and largely ineffective against intentional fraud.
   - **RA-7**: Detective; partly corrective if within recall window. It can identify and sometimes recover fraudulent transfers but does not stop the initial outflow.
   - **ED-1**: Constrains single-operator emergencies so that unilateral action is still subject to automated checks, independent approval, and rapid audit. It reduces the attack surface but relies on independence and timely execution of the other controls.

3. **O3 – Mitigate collusion between preparer and releaser (or between operator and manager)**
   - Scenario: Two people agree to push through an illegitimate payment; mere role separation on paper is not enough.
   - **AV-2**: Preventive for certain objective anomalies regardless of collusion (duplicate invoice, recent bank change, sanctions, operator limit). It is agnostic to collusion but cannot detect commercial sham arrangements.
   - **MG-4**: If the manager is colluding, MG-4 fails almost completely. If independent, it helps detect collusion between preparer and releaser by re-evaluating evidence and patterns (e.g., unusual vendor, weak receipt).
   - **RA-7**: Detective; audits can detect patterns of collusion (e.g., fabricated receipts, repeated suspicious pairs) and support corrective actions (discipline, legal action, system reconfiguration). Corrective only if losses are still recoverable.
   - **ED-1**: Does not directly address collusion; it assumes the manager and auditor remain independent. Under collusion with the manager, ED-1’s conditions could be falsely satisfied.

4. **O4 – Control payments involving high-risk bank-account changes and new vendors**
   - Scenario: Account takeover or fake-vendor scheme using recently changed bank details or new payees.
   - **AV-2**: Strong preventive coverage for recent bank changes and basic bank-ownership checks (when operational). It generates warnings or blocks in risky situations.
   - **MG-4**: Preventive/detective if the manager explicitly checks bank-change documentation and vendor onboarding quality before release/settlement.
   - **RA-7**: Detective; can find defects in vendor onboarding or bank-change approvals, but mostly after funds are at risk. Corrective only within recall window.
   - **ED-1**: Explicitly forbids emergency single-operator payments when there has been a recent bank-account change or short vendor tenure, thus preventing the riskiest emergency scenarios.

5. **O5 – Limit exposure window and enable effective recall or stoppage**
   - Scenario: Even if an error or fraud passes initial controls, the organization wants time-bounded ability to stop or recall the payment before it is irreversible.
   - **AV-2**: Preventive; by blocking or warning before release, it reduces the number of problematic payments that ever reach the bank.
   - **MG-4**: Preventive when before release; hybrid preventive/corrective when after release but before settlement (detects a defect and can still cancel). Purely detective when after settlement.
   - **RA-7**: Detective with some corrective capacity only if carried out before bank recall becomes infeasible (roughly 24 hours). After that, it mainly supports learning and accountability, not correction.
   - **ED-1**: Forces RA-7 to occur within one business day and MG-4 before settlement, thus structurally increasing the chance that defects are found within the recoverable window.

6. **O6 – Control emergency same-operator behaviour so that “emergency” does not become a loophole**
   - Scenario: Staff routinely bypass four-eyes principles by calling things emergencies.
   - **AV-2**: Still applies and must be operational under ED-1; it prevents some emergency abuses but cannot decide what is a true emergency.
   - **MG-4**: Under ED-1, MG-4 must be independent and before settlement; this constrains abuse of emergency flags by requiring another human gatekeeper.
   - **RA-7**: 100% audit of emergency same-user payments is required; this detective (and partly corrective) control discourages abuse by increasing the chance of swift scrutiny.
   - **ED-1**: Preventive at the policy layer; it enumerates strict preconditions (token timing, vendor tenure, no recent bank change, etc.) so that not all “emergencies” can bypass four-eyes. If ED-1 conditions are not satisfied, emergency same-operator handling is unjustified.

7. **O7 – Detect structural weaknesses such as repeated high-risk pairs and control outages**
   - Scenario: Independence is nominally present, but in practice the same people, workstations, or paths are always used, or automated controls are frequently down.
   - **AV-2**: When offline, it cannot address this objective at all; its outages must themselves be monitored.
   - **MG-4**: If always the same manager or strongly dependent relationships approve, independence degrades; MG-4 can be either a strong structural check or part of the weakness.
   - **RA-7**: Detective; through retrospective reviews and data analytics, audit can identify repeated pairs, misuse of emergency tokens, override patterns, or control outages.
   - **ED-1**: Encourages monitoring by mandating RA-7 for emergency cases; however, ED-1 itself does not detect repeated non-emergency pairings or AV-2 outages.

In sum, AV-2 is a mainly **preventive automated filter** on a subset of threats; MG-4, when truly independent and timely, is a **human preventive/detective gate**; RA-7 is primarily **detective with time-limited corrective power**; and ED-1 is a **policy framework** that combines the other controls in emergencies to approximate (but not automatically equal) four-eyes protection.

## 2. Equivalence and Sufficiency Test

To judge compensating-control packages relative to FEP-1, I use the following structured test across dimensions of timing, independence, coverage, overrideability, recoverability, evidence quality, and collusion resistance.

1. **Dimensions**
   - **Timing**:
     - *Pre-release*: Before Release_Payment is executed in the system.
     - *Post-release / pre-settlement*: After Release_Payment but before bank settlement; can still cancel.
     - *Post-settlement, within recall window (≈24h)*: Some chance to recall, but lower assurance.
     - *Post-recall-window*: No realistic corrective power; only learning/accountability.
   - **Independence**:
     - Separation of duties between preparer, releaser, manager, and auditor.
     - Absence of close supervisory or personal conflicts of interest.
     - Avoidance of shared credentials and shared workstations undermining independence.
   - **Coverage**:
     - Which threat scenarios from Section 1 are meaningfully addressed (e.g., errors, self-dealing, collusion, bank changes, new vendors, emergency misuse).
     - For AV-2, only the explicitly checked conditions should be credited.
   - **Overrideability and control strength**:
     - Whether warnings can be overridden, by whom, and under what logging/approval.
     - Whether blocks can be bypassed without higher-level approval.
   - **Recoverability**:
     - Whether the control acts early enough that a defect can be stopped, cancelled, or recalled with high probability.
   - **Evidence quality and audit trail**:
     - Quality of documentation showing what was reviewed (invoice, bank details, receipts).
     - Reliability of system logs (e.g., token timestamps, override attribution).
   - **Collusion resistance and pattern detection**:
     - Whether the package can still detect or deter collusion (e.g., independent manager, RA-7 analytics).
     - Whether structural patterns (repeated pairs, shared workstations, override clusters) are monitored.

2. **Equivalence categories**
   - **Fully equivalent (or stronger than four-eyes)**
     - For each major objective (O1–O7), the combination of AV-2, MG-4, RA-7, and ED-1 provides protection that is at least as strong as two genuinely independent humans at Prepare and Release_Payment.
     - Key conditions:
       - Independent manager approval with real review occurs before release or at least before settlement.
       - AV-2 is operational and passed without same-operator override for high-risk flags.
       - RA-7 covers 100% of relevant exceptions with audit starting before the recall window closes.
       - Emergency criteria (ED-1) are fully satisfied and evidenced (token timing, vendor tenure, bank-change constraints).
       - No new single-person failure mode is introduced (e.g., a single manager who can both override AV-2 and approve release without oversight).
     - Example pattern: A well-implemented ED-1 case with fully satisfied conditions and high-quality documentation (such as AP-203 or AP-207) can be treated as *effectively equivalent* for those specific emergencies.

   - **Partially equivalent**
     - Most, but not all, key objectives are met. Protective strength is close to four-eyes, but there are known weaknesses (e.g., slightly late audit or manager approval after release but still before settlement).
     - Typical characteristics:
       - Independence is good (no conflicts of interest), and AV-2 coverage is intact.
       - Manager approval may be post-release but pre-settlement, giving hybrid preventive/corrective protection.
       - RA-7 audits may be slightly late but still within a broadly acceptable business tolerance for the case risk.
       - Residual risk is low-to-moderate and judged tolerable given amount and context.

   - **Merely mitigating**
     - Controls reduce risk but fall materially short of four-eyes strength on one or more major threat scenarios.
     - Example features:
       - Manager approval after settlement, or by a conflicted manager.
       - AV-2 warnings overridden by the same person who benefits from the payment.
       - RA-7 outside the recall window, making it purely detective.
       - Independence weakened by repeated pairs or shared workstations with no compensating monitoring.
     - These packages may justify treating a case as *technically nonconforming but tolerable* for low-risk amounts, but not as fully equivalent to FEP-1.

   - **Ineffective (relative to FEP-1)**
     - Controls either do not operate, are structurally compromised (e.g., collusion, conflicts of interest), or are so late that they cannot prevent or correct loss.
     - Examples:
       - AV-2 unavailable with no robust manual alternative.
       - Manager ratification well after settlement and outside recall window, especially with conflicts of interest.
       - RA-7 audits long after exposure, functioning only as historical review.
       - Control paths that permit a single operator to override both AV-2 and managerial checks.
     - These cases should be treated as **OBJECTIVE_FAILED** unless evidence is fundamentally missing.

3. **Why “same expected loss” is not the only standard**
   - Internal control standards care about more than expected loss:
     - **Tail risk and concentration**: A control that yields the same expected loss but allows rare catastrophic failures (e.g., EUR 900,000 with weak controls) is worse than one that yields frequent tiny, easily corrected issues.
     - **Regulatory, reputational, and governance obligations**: Certain failures (e.g., related-party fraud) are unacceptable even if the monetary expectation is small.
     - **Detectability and attribution**: Controls that leave clear evidence for investigation and discipline can be preferable even if average loss is similar, because they deter misconduct.
     - **Fairness and accountability**: Independence between supervisor and subordinate reduces moral hazard, even if raw loss statistics happen to match in a given sample.
   - Therefore, the sufficiency test must weigh structure, independence, and recoverability—not just ex post financial outcomes.

## 4. Preventive–Detective–Corrective Comparison

The controls can be compared as follows, with explicit positioning of manager actions around Release_Payment and settlement:

Control | Typical timing relative to Release_Payment / settlement | Preventive role | Detective role | Corrective role | Comments
--------|---------------------------------------------------------|-----------------|----------------|-----------------|---------
AV-2 (Automated validation) | Before or at attempted Release_Payment | Strong: blocks high-risk patterns (duplicate invoice, sanctions, operator limit breaches, recent bank changes) or issues warnings | Limited: warning logs and blocks provide signals for later review of user behaviour and configuration gaps | None directly; it prevents errors rather than correcting completed payments | Highly effective when operational; coverage is limited to codified rules and cannot judge receipt of goods or commercial reasonableness; overrides must be tightly governed.
MG-4 before Release_Payment | Pre-release | Strong: independent human can challenge or deny payment before it hits the bank; close analogue to second releaser | Yes: manager reviews documentation and may detect issues preparer missed | Yes, in the sense of requiring correction before a valid release can occur | This is closest to classic four-eyes: if independence and quality of review are high, it can be fully equivalent or stronger than FEP-1 for many threats.
MG-4 after Release_Payment but before settlement | Post-release / pre-settlement | Partly preventive with respect to *completed bank settlement*; cannot prevent internal “Release_Payment” action, but can still stop the external transfer | Yes: manager examines executed but not settled payments and may detect anomalies | Yes: can trigger cancellation or recall before settlement, thus correcting defects | Best described as a **hybrid**: detective with strong corrective capacity that prevents final loss, but structurally weaker than a pre-release check because the system has already committed a payment instruction.
MG-4 after settlement | Post-settlement (usually > recall window) | None; by the time of review, financial risk is already realised | Yes: may detect fraud, process failure, or collusion | Very limited; occasionally legal recovery is possible but not controllable | This is essentially an ex post oversight or ratification, offering governance and learning value but not equivalent to preventive four-eyes.
RA-7 audit within recall window (≈24h) | Post-release but within recall horizon | Indirect: its existence deters misconduct but does not prevent execution | Yes: focused review of high-risk cases (e.g., emergency same-user payments) can quickly surface defects | Yes, partially: if issues are caught early enough, bank recall or stoppage may still succeed | When consistently timely for emergency and exception cases, RA-7 significantly strengthens overall control but still cannot substitute for independent, pre-release human review.
RA-7 audit after recall window | Post-settlement, no realistic recall | None | Yes | Very limited; mainly supports discipline, process improvement, and regulatory evidence | Valuable for monitoring and deterrence, but not for achieving FEP-1’s prevention objective in the specific case.
ED-1 framework | Policy layer spanning before prepare, pre-release, and post-release | Preventive by constraining when single-operator processing is allowed and requiring AV-2, MG-4, and RA-7 in combination | Indirect: mandatory audits and approvals create a high likelihood that misuse will eventually be detected | Corrective via enforced RA-7 timeliness and manager timing, if those sub-controls are actually followed | ED-1’s effectiveness depends entirely on strict adherence to its conditions; if timing or independence conditions slip, ED-1 collapses from “equivalent package” to “mere justification for bypassing four-eyes.”

Under this semantics, **manager action before settlement but after Release_Payment is best treated as a hybrid detective–corrective control**. It is not truly preventive against system-level “release” activity, but it can still prevent the *final outcome* FEP-1 cares about (an erroneous or fraudulent completed payment) by cancelling or recalling before settlement. However, because the action occurs later in the chain and often under time pressure, it is structurally weaker than a pre-release second human and should not be given full equivalence by default.

## 5. Counterfactual and Residual-Risk Analysis

For at least five cases, the following counterfactuals illustrate how controls break causal chains and what residual risk remains, including the effect of an independent second releaser.

1. **AP-201 (small office-supply payment, convenience same-user)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Ordinary data-entry error or minor vendor fraud; no strong evidence of collusion.  
   - **Control breaking the chain**: AV-2 reduced structured risks (duplicate, sanctions, bank-change issues); independent MG-4 review pre-settlement provided a human check on documentation and bank details.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Low; main concerns are a superficial manager review or undiscovered minor fraud that AV-2 cannot see (e.g., overbilling for goods). RA-7 was too late to enable correction.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: A second releaser instead of same-user release would almost certainly have made the case conforming to FEP-1 outright; risk reduction relative to the observed compensating package is modest given the low amount and independent management review, but it would improve assurance on O1 and O2.

2. **AP-202 (new vendor, bank change, large fraud)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Deliberate fraud benefiting an employee’s relative, exploiting new-vendor onboarding and recent bank change; possibly collusion between u23 and m09 or at least serious governance failure.  
   - **Control breaking the chain (or failing to)**: AV-2 generated a warning but allowed an override by the same operator, so it did **not** successfully break the causal chain. MG-4 and RA-7 acted too late and with poor independence, leaving the chain intact until loss occurred.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Essentially total at time of release; no effective pre-settlement brake was present.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: A truly independent second releaser, barred from using the supervisor’s override credential and sensitive to new-vendor plus recent-bank-change flags, would likely have blocked or escalated the payment. Even if expected loss under some statistical model were similar, the risk of catastrophic individual cases like AP-202 would be greatly reduced.

3. **AP-203 (well-structured emergency repair under ED-1)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Misclassification of emergency or overpayment for repair services, but vendor age, lack of bank change, and AV-2 pass reduce typical fraud patterns.  
   - **Control breaking the chain**: ED-1’s conditions—token pre-prepare, AV-2 pass, independent MG-4 before settlement, and same-day RA-7—combine to create multiple overlapping gates. Any attempt to overpay or misdirect funds would need to bypass both automated checks and independent human review.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Low; remaining risks are subtle valuation disputes (e.g., price of urgent repair) or collusion between u31 and m02, for which there is no evidence.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: A separate releaser would add little additional protection compared with the existing independent manager and audit; the marginal risk reduction is small and mainly relates to edge cases of manager oversight failure.

4. **AP-204 (collusion or structural weakness despite four-eyes form)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Collusion or at least unhealthy dependence between u12 and u13, enabling a fabricated service receipt to pass; possibly aided by weak management oversight.  
   - **Control breaking the chain (or failing to)**: AV-2 could not detect fabricated services; the preventive four-eyes structure was undermined because the two users functioned as a de facto joint cell with shared workstation and repeated approvals. RA-7 eventually detected the issue but likely too late to ensure recovery.  
   - **Residual exposure**: High; even after audit detection, recovery may be uncertain, and the pattern of 37 joint approvals suggests a broader risk across other payments.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: If independence had been enforced in practice—e.g., mandatory rotation of releasers, prohibition on shared workstations, or requirement that the manager, not a paired colleague, release high-value consulting payments—the fabricated receipt might have been challenged earlier. Merely adding a nominal second releaser with the same close relationship would not help; the key is structural independence.

5. **AP-205 (duplicate invoice blocked by AV-2)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Accidental duplicate processing by u44 or opportunistic attempt to pay the same invoice twice.  
   - **Control breaking the chain**: AV-2’s duplicate-invoice block directly stopped the attempted loss; subsequent Cancel_Payment ensured no completion.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Close to zero for this specific invoice; remaining risk is behavioural (e.g., repeated attempts by the same user or vendor) that needs monitoring.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: A second releaser might also have spotted the duplicate, but the automated block is faster, more consistent, and effective; four-eyes is not necessary to achieve the specific control objective in this scenario, though monitoring patterns of blocks remains important.

6. **AP-206 (borderline ED-1 emergency with timing ambiguity)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Misuse of emergency status to bypass normal four-eyes, possibly for convenience rather than necessity, and potential overpayment under time pressure.  
   - **Control breaking the chain**: Assuming the emergency is genuine, the combination of AV-2 pass, independent manager approval before release, and next-morning audit likely blocks most serious abuse patterns. However, if the emergency token truly followed Prepare_Payment, ED-1’s safeguard against casual reclassification of ordinary cases into emergencies was weakened.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Moderate; there is still a time window between prepare and manager approval where a non-independent user could have acted, and an organisational culture of back-dating tokens would be risky.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: A strict four-eyes process, even under emergency, would require another human at the moment of release, reducing dependence on token-handling discipline. This would mainly reduce O6-type risks (emergency loophole abuse).

7. **AP-208 (high-value payment during AV-2 outage)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Fraud or error exploiting an AV-2 outage and manual checklist failure, especially given newish vendor and recent bank change; in this instance the payment happened to be legitimate.  
   - **Control breaking the chain (or failing to)**: No effective chain-breaking occurred: AV-2 was offline, the manual checklist was unsigned, and manager ratification plus audit were both post-settlement. The only “control” was ex post confirmation of legitimacy, which is not acceptable for risk in this range.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Extremely high at execution time; a different factual outcome could easily have been a large unrecoverable fraud.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: An independent releaser, combined with a robust manual bank-ownership check in the absence of AV-2, could have reintroduced a strong gate. Without AV-2, four-eyes becomes more important; in this case, it was effectively absent.

8. **AP-209 (repeated u12/u13 pair, no defect found)**  
   - **Plausible loss mechanism**: Either no loss (as observed) or concealed collusion similar to AP-204 but undetected here.  
   - **Control breaking the chain**: AV-2 and timely RA-7 likely sufficed to prevent obvious anomalies; however, if collusion exists, these may not detect fabricated services or subtle overbilling.  
   - **Residual exposure**: Moderate; single-case risk appears low, but pattern-level risk from repeated pairing and shared workstation remains.  
   - **Effect of independent second releaser**: Enforcing rotation or independent releasers (not always u12/u13) and monitoring workstation-sharing would better realise FEP-1’s intent; in this specific case, that might not change the outcome, but it would reduce system-level residual risk.

## 6. Evidence and Monitoring Recommendations

To manage residual risk, the organisation should implement structured evidence collection and monitoring, focusing on alerts rather than treating any single signal as proof of misconduct.

1. **Same-user prepare–release rate**
   - Track the proportion of completed payments where the same person performed Prepare_Payment and Release_Payment, segmented by presence of an emergency token.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - For ordinary (non-emergency) cases, same-user executions should be extremely rare and individually justified.
     - For emergency cases, verify that ED-1 conditions (token timing, amount, vendor age, bank-change rules) are met and logged.
   - **Alert vs proof**: A high same-user rate is a strong *indicator* of control weakness or emergency abuse, not direct evidence of fraud.

2. **Override and warning rate (AV-2)**
   - Log all AV-2 warnings and blocks, including the actor who overrode or responded and the reason code.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Monitor the rate of overrides per user, vendor, and manager; spikes or concentration on particular individuals or vendors (as in AP-202) should trigger review.
     - Distinguish between justified overrides (documented, manager-approved) and self-approved overrides by the same operator.
   - **Alert vs proof**: Frequent overrides are red flags for either poor AV-2 rule design (too many false positives) or risk-taking behaviour; they require investigation, not automatic accusation.

3. **Late manager approval**
   - Track the timing of MG-4 events relative to Release_Payment and Bank_Settlement.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Classify approvals as pre-release, post-release/pre-settlement, or post-settlement.
     - Require root-cause analysis for post-settlement approvals on high-risk payments (e.g., AP-208) and for approvals by conflicted managers (e.g., direct supervisors like m09 in AP-202).
   - **Alert vs proof**: Late or conflicted approvals indicate control design or execution problems; they do not by themselves prove collusion but warrant targeted audit.

4. **Audit-before-recall-window performance**
   - For RA-7, measure the delay from Release_Payment to Audit_Review, especially for emergency same-user and high-risk cases.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Aim for 100% of emergency same-user cases audited within 24 hours (or one business day) as specified.
     - Treat audits starting after the recall window as structural weaknesses in corrective capability.
   - **Alert vs proof**: A low proportion of timely audits indicates a systemic gap; it does not reveal which specific payments are fraudulent, but it increases residual risk across the portfolio.

5. **Repeated resource pairs and shared workstations**
   - Use analytics to detect frequently recurring (preparer, releaser) pairs, especially when combined with shared workstations or same department.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Flag pairs whose frequency or value concentration exceeds defined thresholds (e.g., top 1% of pair counts or amounts).
     - Investigate pairs like u12/u13 to ensure that any “two-person cell” design is explicitly approved, rotated where possible, and subject to enhanced oversight.
   - **Alert vs proof**: A repeated pair is not proof of collusion—it may reflect legitimate role design—but it weakens practical independence and should trigger targeted review.

6. **Bank-change proximity and vendor age monitoring**
   - Combine payment data with vendor master data on account changes and tenure.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Flag payments to vendors with recent bank-account changes (e.g., <7 days) or very short vendor history (e.g., <180 days), especially at higher amounts.
     - Ensure AV-2 rules and manual checks are stricter for these cases; treat any same-user or emergency handling here as high-risk exceptions.
   - **Alert vs proof**: Bank-change proximity and new vendors indicate heightened exposure but are not inherently suspicious; context and documentation quality matter.

7. **Control outages (e.g., AV-2 unavailable)**
   - Log all periods where AV-2 or similar services are unavailable, and record every payment processed during these windows.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Require documented manual alternative checks (e.g., bank-ownership confirmation) for high-risk payments during outages.
     - Post-incident, audit a sample (or all high-value cases) from outage periods, as AP-208 shows the danger of proceeding without strong substitutes.
   - **Alert vs proof**: Payments during control outages, especially large ones with recent bank changes, are high-priority for review but not automatically improper.

8. **Emergency-token and ED-1 compliance monitoring**
   - Maintain a structured registry of emergency tokens and their associated cases.
   - **Evidence rules**:
     - Check that tokens are activated before Prepare_Payment, meet value and vendor-tenure thresholds, and are rare enough not to become the default path.
     - Cross-check token timing against system logs to avoid back-dating or misuse, as raised in AP-206.
   - **Alert vs proof**: High emergency-token usage or systematic timing anomalies warrant investigation but must be interpreted in operational context (e.g., real outages).

Overall, these monitoring rules produce **risk flags** that feed RA-7 and management oversight. None of them alone should be interpreted as proof of misconduct; instead, they prioritise cases for deeper investigation and help evaluate whether compensating controls remain within acceptable risk tolerances.

The exact behaviour and availability of `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `pm4py.convert_to_event_log`, and `pm4py.filter_four_eyes_principle` can vary slightly by pm4py version (for example, some versions expose these functions at different import paths, or require explicit parameter names). The code above assumes that these helpers are available from the top-level `pm4py` namespace, that the four-eyes filter interprets `Prepare_Payment` and `Release_Payment` as activity keys using the `org:resource` column for resources, and that timestamp parsing with `utc=True` yields timezone-aware datetimes compatible with pm4py. If a particular pm4py installation organises APIs differently, minor adjustments to import paths or parameter names may be needed, but the overall evidence-generation logic remains valid.
