## 1. Conformance semantics

I distinguish findings by the normative force of the relation and by the evidential quality of the observation.

- **Forbidden behavior**: an observed relation that falls within an explicitly forbidden norm and its stated scope. Forbidden norms are closed-world: one in-scope occurrence is enough to raise a finding. If the observation is plausibly impossible, it may also be a data-quality error, but it still requires containment until disproved.
- **Missing mandatory behavior**: a mandatory relation is absent for an object or event that is clearly inside the norm’s scope. For mandatory rules expressed as “eventually”, absence in a directly-follows summary is not sufficient proof of noncompliance.
- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior**: an observed relation not covered by the norm. Because the norm is open-world outside forbidden and mandatory scoped relations, this is not a violation.
- **Typical-pattern absence**: a typical relation is missing for some cases. This is not noncompliance by itself; it may indicate legitimate exceptions, delayed activity, or residual risk.
- **Likely data-quality error**: the observation is implausible, contradicts known system behavior, or is explained by join, key, labeling, or extraction defects.
- **Abstraction mismatch**: the conformance model uses a stricter or different representation than the business rule, for example directly-follows instead of eventual precedence.
- **Insufficient evidence**: the summary lacks timestamps, scope flags, lifecycle history, or exception attributes needed for a definitive judgment.

The norm is **closed-world only** for explicitly forbidden relations and for mandatory relations inside their stated scope. It is otherwise **open-world**. Therefore, absence from the normative edge set does not imply noncompliance. Typical norms create expectations, not obligations. Unspecified relations are allowed unless another scoped norm applies.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

An OC-DFG summary can establish that certain event types frequently follow one another on a given object type. It is useful for detecting obvious forbidden sequences and for measuring common process patterns. Here it shows the N3 forbidden sequence for 2 Orders, the N1 direct sequence for 98 of 100 Orders, the N4 direct sequence for 198 of 200 externally shipped Packages, and the N5 typical payment pattern for 92 of 110 Invoices.

It cannot establish eventual compliance when intermediate events exist. It also cannot reliably distinguish a missing business event from an unrecorded event. The two Orders missing direct `Create Order -> Validate Order` may still have been validated later, especially because 3 Orders show `Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review`. N1 explicitly says the business expectation is eventual validation, while the summary approximates it with directly-follows logic. Treating that approximation as the rule would create false positives.

Counts and scopes matter. N4 applies only to externally shipped Packages. If the 200 denominator is correct, the two missing `Pack Package -> Dispatch Package` cases are mandatory gaps. If the external-shipment flag is unreliable, the finding becomes a scope-quality issue. For N5, the 18 unpaid invoices include 4 write-offs and 14 open successors; because N5 is typical and exceptions are legitimate, absence is not automatically noncompliance.

The main danger is interpreting an “eventually expected” business rule as a directly-follows rule. Directly-follows conformance punishes legitimate intermediate behavior, such as risk review, invoicing, or fulfilment steps. It can also hide eventual compliance when the required event occurs later in the trace. Therefore, N1 should be investigated as an eventual-relation question, not treated as a confirmed violation based only on the direct-follows count.

### OTG

An OTG can establish object-object relationships and their weights. It shows mandatory T1 Order-Item interaction at 1,450, mandatory T2 Package-Delivery interaction at 200, typical T3 Invoice-Payment interaction at 92, unspecified Order-Customer and Order-RiskCase edges, forbidden T6 Order-Payment cobirth at 1, and unregulated Package-Delivery codeath at 7.

It cannot establish when a relationship became relevant, whether it was present at the required event, or whether the relationship satisfies an event-level mandatory rule. For example, the OTG shows 200 Package-Delivery interactions, but the ET-OT summary shows only 198 `Dispatch Package -> Delivery` links. Thus the object graph may be conforming at the object level while the event-to-object linkage is suspicious at the event level. Similarly, Order-Item weight 1,450 appears complete, yet two `Add Item` events lack Order links in the ET-OT view.

The forbidden T6 cobirth edge is serious if genuine: Orders and Payments cannot be born in the same business event. However, the graph alone cannot prove whether this was a real business event or a data-integration artifact. The seven Package-Delivery codeath edges are not violations because codeath is outside the norm.

### ET-OT

ET-OT relations establish whether event types are linked to required object types. They provide the most direct evidence for mandatory event-object coverage. In this snapshot, E1, E3, E4, E5, and E7 appear satisfied. Gaps exist for E2, E6, and E8. E10 is forbidden and observed once.

ET-OT cannot establish business exceptions unless those exceptions are explicitly typed. The one `Record Payment` without Invoice may be unapplied cash, but the event is not labeled as such. Because E8 allows the exception only for explicitly typed unapplied cash, the observation remains a mandatory gap pending labeling or correction.

ET-OT also reveals abstraction mismatches. Two `Dispatch Package` events lack Delivery links but have carrier references. The business may consider the carrier reference sufficient evidence of delivery, while the normative object model requires a Delivery object. Likewise, two `Add Item` events have Item links but missing Order links, suggesting an extraction or foreign-key problem rather than a process choice. A relation can therefore be conforming at one abstraction and suspicious at another: Package-Delivery exists in the OTG but is missing for two dispatch events in ET-OT; validation may eventually exist for Orders even though the OC-DFG direct-follows check misses it.

## 7. Investigation and remediation plan

### Immediate containment

Prioritize the two highest-severity findings: N3 and E10.

1. **N3, Cancel Order -> Dispatch Package, 2 Orders**
   - Identify the two Orders and their Packages.
   - Check whether dispatch occurred after effective cancellation.
   - If yes, hold or recall shipments where possible, notify fulfilment and customer service, and block dispatch when an Order has an active cancellation flag.

2. **E10, Sensor Heartbeat -> Invoice, 1 edge**
   - Quarantine the pipeline or join that produced the Sensor Heartbeat-Invoice link.
   - Remove or suppress the edge in analytical and conformance views.
   - Verify whether other sensor events have been joined to financial objects using reused numeric identifiers.

T6, the Order-Payment cobirth edge, is also high severity and should be investigated promptly, but it ranks below the two explicit forbidden findings with clearer operational exposure.

### Data repair

- Replace reused numeric identifiers with stable natural keys or globally unique identifiers.
- Add explicit `unapplied_cash` typing for the one Payment without Invoice, or link it to the correct Invoice.
- Backfill missing Order links for the two `Add Item` events.
- Materialize Delivery objects from carrier references or document carrier reference as an accepted alternative.
- Investigate and backfill missing Pack events for the two externally shipped Packages if warehouse records confirm packing.

### Process investigation

- For N3, determine whether the cause is a race condition between cancellation and dispatch, a user workaround, a cancellation reversal, or a timestamp recording error.
- For T6, determine whether the system intentionally creates Order and Payment together, or whether an integration layer incorrectly merges two events.
- For N4, determine whether packing is genuinely skipped, recorded late, or not extracted.
- For N1, inspect the two Orders without direct validation to determine whether validation eventually occurred.

### Norm revision

- Formalize eventual semantics for N1.
- Add unapplied-cash exception typing for E8.
- Add effective-time scope to N3.
- Add external-shipment scope attribute for N4.
- Clarify Delivery object requirements for E6/T2.
- Clarify governance of risk-review and customer-related interactions.
- Extend E10 to all financial objects if intended.

### Monitoring

- Zero-tolerance alerts for observed forbidden edges: N3, T6, and E10.
- Eventual-compliance dashboards for N1, N4, E2, E6, and E8.
- Aging reports for N5 typical-pattern absences, including open invoices and write-offs.
- Reconciliation checks between OTG and ET-OT counts, especially Add Item Order/Item counts and Dispatch Package/Delivery counts.
- Data-quality monitors for reused identifiers, missing foreign keys, and unlabeled exception events.

Typical absences under N5 and unregulated rare relations are not violations by themselves. Several conclusions depend on the norm’s incompleteness: whether the two Orders were eventually validated under N1, whether the unlabeled Payment is legitimate unapplied cash under E8, whether write-offs and open invoices are legitimate N5 exceptions, and whether risk-review or codeath relations should later be governed. Other conclusions depend on suspected data-quality issues: the Sensor Heartbeat-Invoice edge, missing Delivery links with carrier references, missing Add Item Order links, and possibly the cobirth edge. Graph summaries alone cannot decide effective timestamps, cancellation validity, physical dispatch, payment application, business intent, fraud, or whether mandatory eventual relations were ultimately satisfied.
