## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Framework choice. I do **not** use natural direct/indirect effects (NDE/NIE) as the primary target. The mediators here (manual_review occurrence, manual_queue_wait, escalation, escalation_queue_wait) are (a) exposure-induced-confounded — the flag changes the priority lane, which changes queue position and reviewer assignment, which then confound the mediator→outcome relation — and (b) *interfering*: one case's mediator value changes another case's waiting time, so "the mediator value that would have occurred under flag=0" is not even well-defined for a single case in isolation. Cross-world quantities are therefore both untestable and, under interference, arguably meaningless. I use instead:

- **Interventional (in)direct effects** (VanderWeele/Vansteelandt randomised-analogue) where a stochastic mediator draw is enough;
- **Controlled direct effects (CDE)** where the mediator is an actual policy lever (mandatory review on/off, capacity level) — these correspond to real, implementable regimes;
- **Separable effects** (Robins–Richardson) for the flag itself: split `flag` into `flag_lane` (the component acting on triage prioritisation) and `flag_route` (the component acting on the review/escalation routing rule). P1–P4 are literally interventions on separable components, which is why they do not share a "direct effect";
- **Cluster/policy-level estimands** (system-time-window comparisons) wherever shared queues make individual-level SUTVA fail.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Interference is the dominant threat. Review and senior servers are shared, so flagging case *i* lengthens case *j*'s waits: SUTVA fails, and the observed flag-0 baseline is itself contaminated by flag-1 congestion (the flag-0 group's waits under P1 would likely *fall*, so the P0 flag effect on flag-1 cases is overstated relative to a true no-flag world). Priority triage is likewise zero-sum: flagged cases' 0.2 h arrival→triage-start is partly *purchased* from unflagged cases' 2.5 h. Exposure-induced confounding: `flag` → `priority_lane` → earlier triage release time → different queue state at review entry, and `flag` → `manual_review` → review findings, which then confound `escalation`→`cycle_time`. Treatment-confounder feedback appears longitudinally: congestion at time *t* changes routing and escalation thresholds, which changes congestion at *t+1*, which changes the next case's exposure to the priority lane's benefit. Any static, case-level mediation adjustment for queue wait therefore conditions on a post-treatment, feedback-generated variable.

## 3. Stage decomposition

Handling of absent stages: a stage that did not occur is `NA`, never 0. Stage medians are computed only over cases where the stage occurred (support in parentheses/`n` column context), so subgroup stage medians are **not** additive to the total-cycle median. `residual-to-close` = close minus the last completed stage timestamp (escalation_done, else manual_done, else triage_done); this is the only way to compare cases with different path lengths on a common last segment. The pattern is exactly the one described: flagged cases start triage in ~0.2 h versus ~2.5 h and are triaged faster in service (0.60 vs 0.95 h), yet their median cycle time is ~2.9× longer. The gap is not in service times — manual service (2.75 vs 3.00 h) and escalation service (2.00 vs 2.00 h) are indistinguishable — it sits in *occurrence* and *waiting*: every flagged case is reviewed, waits a median 6.75 h for a review slot (vs 1.75 h for the few discretionary flag-0 reviews), and 60% then wait ~7 h for a senior. None of this is automatically causal. Flag is a deterministic threshold on risk_score, and the two groups have **zero overlap** (flag 0: 0.28–0.61; flag 1: 0.64–0.93), and complexity is perfectly confounded at the tails (no Low case is flagged, no High-complexity flagged/unflagged pair is well balanced). Within High complexity the total gap shrinks to 24.1 vs 18.4 h. The long review wait may equally reflect flagged cases being scheduled behind each other in a congested shared queue, or reviewers reserving longer slots for genuinely harder work.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| How much of the delay is congestion vs case difficulty? | Queue-state-aware regression / queueing decomposition: model manual_queue_wait as f(number in M-queue at triage_done, servers busy, position in priority order) with case attributes | Reconstructed queue length at each stage entry, server-busy indicator, hour-of-day, day-of-week | Timestamps reconstruct the true queue; work-conserving servers; no hidden off-log work | Fit an M/M/c-style prediction and compare predicted vs observed waits; check that residual flag coefficient shrinks toward 0 once queue state is included | If the flag coefficient survives full queue-state adjustment, the delay is scheduling/behaviour, not congestion — P3 (capacity) would then fail and P2/P4a become preferred |

**Pilot: P3, protected review capacity for flagged cases.**

Eligibility: all arrivals with risk_score ≥ 0.62 (the flag rule), during weekday day and evening shifts, excluding cases requiring external third-party input (their waits are not queue-driven).

Assignment rule: switchback randomisation at the **shift-block** level (8-hour blocks), not at case level, because the queue is shared and case-level randomisation would violate SUTVA. Blocks are randomised 1:1 to P3 or P0 within each day-of-week stratum, with a 1-hour washout at each boundary so the review queue drains. Both arms keep the priority triage lane and the mandatory-review rule; only the reserved reviewer slot differs.

Queue-capacity guardrail: P3 adds one dedicated reviewer-equivalent for flagged cases during that block rather than ring-fencing existing capacity. Pre-specified stop: if unflagged cases' median triage_done→manual_start exceeds 1.5× its baseline for two consecutive P3 blocks, the reservation is released — this detects crowd-out.

Primary outcome: median triage_done→manual_start for flagged cases (the pathway the policy targets). Secondary: total cycle time; escalation rate and escalation queue wait; unflagged cases' review wait (spillover); reviewer utilisation; audited decision-quality on a 15% random sample scored blind against a gold standard.

Minimum data: per-case start *and* completion timestamps at every stage, reviewer and senior identity, queue length and servers-busy at each stage entry, block assignment, and reasons recorded for each escalation decision.

Stopping conditions: harm — spillover guardrail breached twice, or audited false-approve rate rising above a pre-set margin; futility — after 8 weeks the flagged review wait reduction's confidence interval excludes 1.5 h; success — pre-specified block count reached with the primary effect confirmed and no guardrail breach.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk.** If audit shows `risk_score` omits a severity dimension that reviewers can see but the log cannot, then long review and escalation waits reflect genuinely harder work queued deliberately, not congestion; the flag would be a near-innocent label, P3 would buy little, and the correct conclusion would be that flagged cycle time is appropriate.
2. **Queue interference.** If block-level analysis shows the flagged fraction has no effect on unflagged cases' waits (slack capacity), then priority triage is not zero-sum, P1's supposed benefit to unflagged cases vanishes, and reserving capacity (P3) becomes nearly free — strengthening P3; conversely, if reserving capacity simply relocates the backlog with no net system gain, P3 should be dropped in favour of P4b.
3. **Decision quality.** If a blind audit shows that reviewed-and-escalated cases catch materially more true bad cases than discretionary routing does, then P2 becomes actively harmful and the ranking flips to P3/P4b (capacity, not routing). If instead the audit shows review adds no accuracy over triage alone, P2 dominates everything here.
4. **Timestamp semantics.** If `manual_start` records when a case was *assigned* to a reviewer rather than when work began, the 6.75 h "queue wait" is mislabelled and the true wait sits inside `manual_service`; the bottleneck then looks like reviewer throughput, not queue access, and P3 (more access) would not help. Similarly, local clock time crossing a shift boundary or DST could manufacture apparent overnight waits.
5. **Positivity / overlap.** The current data has **no overlap** in risk_score between flag groups (0.28–0.61 vs 0.64–0.93), no flagged Low-complexity case, and no flagged case without review. If overlap does not improve with more data near the threshold, every case-level flag contrast here is pure extrapolation, and only regime-level (switchback/stepped-wedge) evidence is admissible — the mediation story must be withdrawn as unidentified rather than merely imprecise.
6. **Behaviour change by reviewers.** If, under P3's protected slots, reviewers expand review depth to fill the reserved capacity (Parkinson-style), or triagers begin flagging more cases to access the fast lane (score gaming / flag inflation), the waiting-time gain evaporates and the policy becomes a net capacity loss; the recommendation would shift to P4a with explicit criteria plus flag-rate monitoring.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** Flagged cases enter triage in ~0.2 h versus ~2.5 h for unflagged, and are triaged slightly faster (0.60 vs 0.95 h), yet their median cycle time is ~16.5 h against ~5.8 h. The gap is not in service: manual service (2.75 vs 3.00 h) and escalation service (2.00 vs 2.00 h) are essentially identical across groups. It is in **occurrence and waiting** — review 100% vs 40%, escalation 60% vs 20%, review wait 6.75 vs 1.75 h, and a senior queue where 7 h of waiting buys 2 h of work.

**Plausible mechanism.** The flag is a policy bundle, not a cause: it simultaneously buys triage priority (probably zero-sum, taken from unflagged cases) and imposes mandatory review, which loads a shared, capacity-limited queue whose congestion the flagged cases largely inflict on each other. The 3.5:1 wait-to-service ratio at escalation is a textbook bottleneck signature.

**Unidentified.** Everything case-level. Flag is a deterministic threshold with zero score overlap and no Low-complexity flagged case, so the raw contrast is unadjustable extrapolation confounded by complexity and `U_risk`. Natural direct/indirect effects are unavailable — mediators are exposure-induced-confounded and interfering — and no flagged case lacks review, so the occurrence mediator has no positivity. Decision quality is unmeasured entirely: Approve/Reject is not accuracy.

**Most useful next evidence.** A shift-level switchback of **P3** (added, not ring-fenced, review capacity for flagged cases) with a spillover guardrail. It is the only lever that tests the congestion mechanism directly, cannot degrade decision quality, and — by measuring unflagged cases' waits under varying flagged intensity — simultaneously measures the interference that makes every other estimate here fragile.
