## 1. Materiality Framework

To evaluate process deviations effectively, technical conformance results must be translated through a business-contextualized materiality framework. A uniform Levenshtein-style edit distance treats a harmless UI telemetry event as equivalent to an unapproved EUR 480,000 disbursement. Our materiality framework scores **Business Severity (1–5)** across eight operational and compliance dimensions, while tracking **Evidence Confidence (Low / Medium / High)** as an orthogonal dimension.

### Dimensions of Business Severity
1. **Control Objective & Preventative Gate Integrity:** Does the deviation bypass a critical fiduciary or anti-fraud control (e.g., *Fraud Screen*, *Manual Approve*, or *Assess Loss*) before value leaves the organization (Policies P1, P2, P3)?
2. **Financial Exposure & Risk Tier:** What is the monetary value at stake (e.g., <= EUR 5,000 vs. > EUR 50,000) and the intrinsic claim risk profile (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH)?
3. **Reversibility:** Has an irreversible external action occurred—specifically, *Pay Claim* via banking settlement rails—before mandatory control gates were satisfied? Once funds are disbursed (especially following a recent beneficiary-bank change), recovery is legally and operationally difficult.
4. **Sequence & Timing Integrity:** Were activities executed in the mandated causal order (P3: approval *before* payment) and within regulatory SLAs (P4: notification within 24 hours)? Retroactive approvals ("rubber-stamping" after payment) represent severe control failures.
5. **Customer & Regulatory Harm:** Does the deviation degrade the claimant experience (e.g., duplicate notifications, delayed payouts) or breach statutory insurance regulations and audit trail mandates?
6. **Systemic Recurrence:** Is the case an isolated human override or part of a systemic pattern (e.g., 74 monthly retry duplicates, 9 external-adjuster logging gaps, 4 data-aware auto-approval bypasses)?
7. **Evidence Reliability & Auditability:** When core event logs omit an activity, does immutable, cryptographically or systemically linked secondary evidence exist (e.g., signed adjuster reports, CRM logs), or is the link fragile/mutable (e.g., free-text fields, unrecorded verbal assertions)?
8. **Model Scope & Lifecycle Fit:** Is the trace an actual operational failure, or is it a non-payable lifecycle path (denied pre-screens, claimant withdrawals under P6) forced against a normative model designed solely for payable claims?

### Severity Scale (1 to 5)
* **5 – Critical (Catastrophic Control Failure):** Irreversible payment executed without mandatory approval or fraud screening on high-exposure or high-risk claims (e.g., > EUR 50k, recent bank change, pre-approval payment). Immediate financial loss or regulatory sanction exposure.
* **4 – High (Major Policy / Control Breach):** Substantive bypass of business rules (P1/P2) on moderate exposure or elevated risk indicators without compensating controls, or systemic breakdown in high-value audit trail integrity.
* **3 – Medium (Audit Trail & Evidentiary Deficiency):** Substantive business controls were executed in reality, but core system logging failed or relies on unverified/mutable cross-system links (P4/external documents). No direct financial leakage, but high audit failure risk.
* **2 – Low (Model Scope Mismatch / Semantic Drift):** Valid business execution (e.g., denied or withdrawn claims, taxonomy renames) that conflicts with an overly narrow process model or outdated ETL mapping table.
* **1 – Negligible (Harmless Technical Noise / Fully Compliant):** Fully compliant execution, silent routing transitions (`tau`), or benign technical noise (UI telemetry, idempotent transport retries) with zero control or customer impact (P5).

### How Confidence Differs from Severity
**Severity** measures the *potential or realized business impact* of an event pattern assuming our interpretation of the trace is correct. **Confidence** measures the *epistemic certainty and evidentiary reliability* that our reconstruction of reality matches what actually occurred on the ground. 

An analyst must never conflate the two:
* **High Severity + High Confidence:** In **CL-604**, bank timestamps (10:03) and approval timestamps (10:20) prove beyond doubt that EUR 90,000 left the bank before approval and without a fraud screen.
* **High Severity + Low/Medium Confidence:** In **CL-602**, a EUR 480,000 claim shows `M(Fraud Screen)`. The approver claims a verbal check occurred. Because a missing log event (`M`) is not absolute proof of non-occurrence in poorly integrated systems, our confidence in whether a check *informally* happened is low-to-medium, but the **severity remains 5 (Critical)** because a verbal assertion for a EUR 480k claim with a 1-day-old bank change is a catastrophic governance failure.
* **Medium Severity + Medium Confidence:** In **CL-606** and **CL-608**, substantive actions (external assessment, CRM notification) occurred outside the core log. Severity is moderated (3) because the work happened, while confidence is governed by the quality of the cross-system identifier (signed timestamped report vs. mutable text field).

## 4. Causal Hypotheses and Evidence Tests

A missing event in an alignment (`M(A)`) proves only that *no matching event label was recorded in the extracted core log*—it is never automatic proof that the business activity failed to occur. Conversely, a synchronous move (`S(A)`) proves only that a timestamped record was logged, not that the underlying control was substantively valid. Below are competing hypotheses and definitive forensic tests for six critical cases and patterns.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

In process mining, standard trace alignment algorithms (such as the $A^*$ search over a synchronous product net) compute the minimum edit distance required to transform a recorded event trace into a valid execution sequence of a normative model. When configured with a uniform unit cost function ($c(L) = 1, c(M) = 1$), **`raw_cost` measures syntactic edit distance, not business risk, compliance exposure, or operational harm.** Relying on raw alignment cost to prioritize audit or remediation resources leads to catastrophic misallocation—investigating benign UI clicks and withdrawn claims while missing six-figure fraud and unauthorized payouts.

Below is a rigorous deconstruction of eight structural reasons why alignment cost diverges from business risk, demonstrated across the supplied cases:

### 1. Blindness to Data Guards and Multi-Perspective Business Rules (`raw_cost = 0` vs. Critical Risk)
A process tree or Petri net encodes only control-flow ordering (who can follow whom). In our model, the choice between `->('Fraud Screen', 'Manual Approve')` and `'Auto Approve'` is represented as an unconstrained exclusive choice (`X`). The alignment algorithm has no visibility into case attributes (`amount`, `risk_tier`, `bank_change_timestamp`).
* **Evidence:** In **CL-609** (EUR 18,000, MEDIUM risk) and **CL-610** (EUR 4,500, beneficiary bank changed 1 hour before payment), the system executed `Auto Approve`. Because `Auto Approve` is a valid branch in the process tree, both cases achieve a perfect **`raw_cost = 0`** (fully synchronous). Yet **CL-609** violates Policy P1 (exceeding the EUR 5,000 and LOW-risk ceiling), and **CL-610** violates P1 and P2 in a textbook Account Takeover (ATO) pattern.

### 2. Cost-Function Symmetry and Insensitivity to Control Criticality (`raw_cost = 1` vs. EUR 480,000 Exposure)
Under a uniform cost function, every model move $M(A)$ and log move $L(A)$ costs exactly $1$, regardless of whether activity $A$ is a preventative anti-fraud gate on a half-million-euro payout or a duplicate email retry on a small claim.
* **Evidence:** **CL-602** involves a EUR 480,000 HIGH-risk claim with a 1-day-old beneficiary bank change where `Fraud Screen` was skipped (`M(Fraud Screen, c=1)`). Its technical cost is **`1`**. **CL-603** is a fully compliant EUR 3,200 LOW-risk claim where a network connector retried the notification (`L(Notify Claimant, c=1)`), also yielding a technical cost of **`1`**. Treating these two cases as equally deviant because both have `raw_cost = 1` equates a trivial transport retry with a EUR 480,000 unscreened payout.

### 3. Irreversibility and Sequence Repair Illusion (`raw_cost = 3` in CL-604 vs. CL-601)
When an irreversible action like `Pay Claim` occurs *before* `Manual Approve`, the business consequence is severe (Policy P3): funds have already exited the bank before fiduciary authorization. However, the alignment algorithm simply looks for the cheapest mathematical repair: it treats the early payment as an extraneous log move `L(Pay Claim, c=1)` and inserts a missing model move `M(Pay Claim, c=1)` after `Manual Approve` (plus `M(Fraud Screen, c=1)`), yielding **`raw_cost = 3`**.
* **Evidence:** In **CL-604** (EUR 90,000 paid at 10:03, manually approved at 10:20 without a fraud screen), this severe control failure receives a `raw_cost` of **3**—the **exact same `raw_cost` (3)** as **CL-601**, a completely compliant EUR 1,200 claim that logged three harmless UI events (`Open Workbench`, `Autosave`, `Close Workbench`).

### 4. Normative Model Scope Limitations and Early-Termination Penalties (`raw_cost = 4` on Zero-Risk Cases)
When a normative model represents only the "payable happy path" (Policy P6), any legitimate early termination (such as denying an invalid claim or honoring a customer withdrawal) forces the alignment algorithm to pay for the termination event (`L(Deny Claim, c=1)` or `L(Withdraw Claim, c=1)`) **plus** pay a penalty for every subsequent downstream payable step that was rightly skipped (`M(Assess Loss)`, `M(Auto Approve)`, `M(Pay Claim)`, `M(Notify Claimant)`).
* **Evidence:** **CL-607** (denied pre-screen) and **CL-612** (withdrawn EUR 2,000 claim) incur the **highest raw alignment cost in the entire dataset (`raw_cost = 4`)**. In business terms, *not paying* a denied or withdrawn claim is 100% correct; paying them to achieve `raw_cost = 0` would be catastrophic fraud or waste.

### 5. Activity Granularity, UI Telemetry, and Idempotent Retries
Event logs frequently mix business milestone events with low-level technical telemetry (Policy P5). Because the normative model only includes business activities, every unmapped technical event is penalized as $L(\text{telemetry}, c=1)$.
* **Evidence:** **CL-601** accumulates `raw_cost = 3` purely from `Open Workbench`, `Autosave`, and `Close Workbench`. **CL-603** (and 73 other monthly cases) accumulates `raw_cost = 1` from a transport-layer retry sharing the same `message_id`. Neither represents a business deviation.

### 6. Event-Label Synonymy and Taxonomy Drift (`raw_cost = 2` for a Substitution)
Standard alignment algorithms match events by exact string equality. If an IT system logs a synonymous label (`Assess Damage` instead of `Assess Loss`), the algorithm cannot recognize semantic equivalence. It penalizes the trace *twice*: once for the "unexpected" log label $L(\text{Assess Damage}, c=1)$ and once for the "missing" model label $M(\text{Assess Loss}, c=1)$.
* **Evidence:** **CL-611** (EUR 62,000 HIGH-risk claim) followed every single policy and control step in the exact right order, yet receives **`raw_cost = 2`**—twice the penalty of **CL-602**'s skipped fraud screen—purely due to a lag in updating an ETL mapping table.

### 7. Missing Log Events vs. External / Distributed Evidence (`M(A)` $\neq$ Non-Occurrence)
In a distributed enterprise architecture, an `M(A)` move means only that activity $A$ was not recorded in the *extracted core event log*. The activity may have been properly executed in an external partner system or adjacent enterprise platform.
* **Evidence:** In **CL-606** (EUR 75,000 HIGH-risk), `M(Assess Loss, c=1)` flags a missing core event, but a licensed external adjuster signed the assessment report at 10:05 prior to fraud screening and approval. In **CL-608** (EUR 250,000 HIGH-risk), `M(Notify Claimant, c=1)` flags a missing core notification, but CRM sent a secure-portal message at +3 hours (satisfying P4's 24h rule, albeit with a mutable link). Both require **audit-trail integration remediation (Severity 3)**, not fraud recovery.

### 8. Silent Model Transitions (`T(tau, c=0)`)
When a process tree is converted into an accepting Petri net, structural routing transitions (`tau`) are introduced to represent XOR splits/joins without visible labels.
* **Evidence:** In **CL-605** (EUR 800 LOW-risk), the alignment includes `T(tau, c=0)` before `Auto Approve`. A naive reader unfamiliar with Petri net semantics might mistake `T(tau)` for an anomaly, whereas it is a completely fitting (`c=0`) structural transition on a 100% compliant trace.
