# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

## Time Origin, Horizon, and Events
*   **Time Origin ($t = 0$):** Case submission (`Submit`), which represents the exact moment when the 72-hour elapsed-time Service Level Agreement (SLA) clock begins.
*   **Time Horizon ($t = 72$ hours):** The operational SLA threshold. Cases that have not achieved operational success by this mark are considered SLA breaches for compliance and monitoring purposes.
*   **Target Event (Operational Success):** Reaching `Resolve`.
*   **Competing / Terminal Internal Outcomes:** `Withdraw` and `Reject`. These terminate the internal process flow without achieving operational success, but they are distinct from failure-to-resolve because they preclude further internal processing.
*   **Censoring:** Right censoring occurs for cases that are still `open` at the observation cutoff (whose final resolution or termination happens after the observation window closes) and for cases that are `transferred` out of the internal system.

## Definition of Estimands
To avoid ambiguity regarding the phrase "probability of breaching the SLA," we define two distinct, operationally actionable estimands:

1.  **Estimand 1: Probability of Failing Operational SLA (Standard 1-Survival / Cumulative Failure)**
    *   *Definition:* The marginal probability that a case has **not** achieved `Resolve` by $t = 72$ hours, treating all terminal non-resolutions (`Withdraw`, `Reject`, and potentially `Transfer`) as either competing risks or right-censoring depending on the analytical stance. 
    *   *Target Population Metric:* $1 - S(72)$, where $S(t)$ is the survival probability of remaining unresolved in the absence of informative censoring.

2.  **Estimand 2: Cumulative Incidence of Resolution by 72 Hours (Competing-Risks Framework)**
    *   *Definition:* The probability that a case achieves `Resolve` by $t = 72$ hours in the presence of competing terminal events (`Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`). This prevents the overestimation of failure risk caused by treating withdrawals or rejections as independent censoring.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis that filters the dataset to include *only* completed, resolved cases (e.g., S01, S02, S03, S04, S07, S13 in Cohort A) suffers from multiple structural biases:

1.  **Survivor Bias:** 
    *   Restricting the analysis to resolved cases strips out cases that are still open, rejected, withdrawn, or transferred. 
    *   For example, case **S13** took 110 hours to resolve. Including *only* completed cases skews the mean duration downward if long-running cases are truncated, but more importantly, looking *only* at resolved cases makes it impossible to calculate the proportion of cases that breach the 72-hour SLA among all initiated cases. Cases S09, S10, and S11 are currently open past 72 hours (ages 92h, 47h, 81h respectively); omitting them ignores active breaches or pending long-running cases.
2.  **Informative Censoring & Truncation:**
    *   Cases like **S09** (open, age 92h, last event gap 31h) and **S26** (open, age 104h, last event gap 72h) are stalled. A completed-case analysis ignores them entirely, even though their large `last_event_gap_h` indicates high probability of SLA breach.
3.  **Competing Risks:**
    *   Treating `rejected` (e.g., **S05**, 9h) or `withdrawn` (e.g., **S06**, 27h) as if they were unobserved or independent right-censoring distorts the risk set. Withdrawals and rejections remove cases from the pool of potential resolutions. Treating them as ordinary censoring overestimates the cumulative probability of resolution.
4.  **Cohort Imbalance & Policy Shifts:**
    *   Cohort B introduced automated screening. Naive aggregation mixes two different underlying data-generating processes without adjusting for assignment non-randomization and incomplete rollout compliance.
5.  **Active Open Cases Below vs. Above 72 Hours:**
    *   Cases like **S15** (open, age 35h, gap 2h) are right-censored *before* the SLA horizon; they have not breached yet and may resolve within the window. In contrast, cases like **S11** (open, age 81h, gap 55h) have already *exceeded* the 72-hour threshold without resolving. Treating them identically as "open" ignores their exact position relative to the SLA deadline.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## Methodological Comparison
*   **Kaplan-Meier (KM):** Standard for estimating overall survival (unresolved probability). *Limitation:* Biased in the presence of competing risks (`Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`), treating them as independent censoring.
*   **Aalen-Johansen / Cumulative Incidence Functions (CIF):** Essential for competing risks. Estimates the probability of resolution over time while accounting for competing terminal states. *Recommended for primary descriptive analysis.*
*   **Cause-Specific Hazards (Cox Proportional Hazards):** Models the instantaneous rate of resolution among cases still at risk. Good for assessing covariate effects on resolution speed.
*   **Subdistribution Hazards (Fine-Gray Model):** Directly models the effect of covariates on the cumulative incidence of resolution in the presence of competing risks. *Recommended for operational policy evaluation.*
*   **Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST):** Measures the expected time to resolution restricted to the 72-hour window. Robust against proportional hazards violations.
*   **Landmarking:** Addresses time-varying covariates and immortal-time bias by resetting the time origin at a fixed landmark time (e.g., $t = 24$ hours).
*   **Multi-State Models:** Captures transitions between states (`Submit` $\to$ `Review` $\to$ `Rework` $\to$ `Escalate` $\to$ `Resolve`). *Recommended for process-aware structural analysis.*

## Recommended Approach
*   **Primary Analysis:** **Aalen-Johansen Estimator / Fine-Gray Competing Risks Regression**, treating `Withdraw` and `Reject` as competing events, and `Transfer` as a sensitivity dimension (either censored or competing).
*   **Secondary Analysis:** **Landmark Cox Proportional Hazards Model** evaluated at $t = 24$ hours using process-aware covariates (e.g., whether a case has escalated or experienced rework by 24 hours) to predict subsequent SLA breach risk.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

## Feature Derivation from Prefixes
Using the sequence prefixes, we extract the following process features:
*   **Current Stage / Path Pattern:** Presence of `AutoCheck` (Cohort B feature), `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`.
*   **Repeated Review / Rework Count:** Explicitly captured in `rework_count` and visible in prefixes (e.g., `Review>Rework>Review` in **S07**).
*   **Escalation Timing:** `escalated` (yes/no) and whether escalation occurred early or late.
*   **Inactivity Gap (`last_event_gap_h`):** Time elapsed since the last recorded event. High gaps indicate stagnation.

## Methodological Guardrails: Immortal-Time & Leakage Risks
*   **Immortal Time Bias:** An event like `Escalate` or `Rework` happens *after* `Submit`. If we use "ever escalated" as a baseline covariate in a standard Cox model from $t=0$ without time-varying structuring or landmarking, cases must survive long enough to experience escalation. This grants them "immortal time" prior to escalation, biasing hazard ratios.
*   **Solution:** Use **Time-Varying Covariates** or **Landmark Analysis** (conditioning on status at a fixed time $t_0$, e.g., 24h).

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

To ensure robustness, we evaluate three distinct sensitivity scenarios regarding terminal states and policy effects:

1.  **Scenario A (Optimistic / Default Censoring):** 
    *   `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are treated as independent right-censoring.
    *   *Risk:* Overestimates resolution probability if withdrawals/rejections are correlated with slow processing.
2.  **Scenario B (Pessimistic / Transfer-as-Breach):** 
    *   `Transfer` to external organizations is treated as an SLA breach (failure), under the hypothesis that transfer stems from internal delays.
    *   *Qualitative Reversal:* Cohort B's apparent performance could degrade if transfers are heavily penalized.
3.  **Scenario C (Competing Risks Framework):** 
    *   `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are modeled as competing events using Aalen-Johansen estimators.
    *   *Qualitative Reversal:* Provides the true unbiased probability of resolution by accounting for cases removed by withdrawal.

# 7. Operational interpretation

*   **Converting Outputs to Alerts:** Cases whose predicted probability of breaching the 72-hour SLA exceeds 0.70 at landmark $t = 24h$ or $48h$ are flagged for managerial intervention.
*   **Workload Actions:** Focus queue reassignments on open cases with high `last_event_gap_h` and presence of `Rework` (e.g., **S13**, **S27**).
*   **Avoiding Causal Fallacies:** Risk scores (e.g., "Escalation increases breach risk") reflect *associational* warning signals, not causal effects. `Escalate` is triggered by troubled cases; escalation does not *cause* the failure, but identifies cases already at risk.
*   **Calibration & Fairness Checks:** Evaluate predicted vs. actual breach rates across Priority tiers (P1 vs. P3) and Cohorts (A vs. B) to ensure automated screening in Cohort B does not introduce disparate impact or miscalibration for high-priority cases.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

To achieve stronger causal and predictive inference, future data collection and process extracts should incorporate:

1.  **Exact Event Timestamps:** Granular timestamps (date-time strings) for every transition rather than aggregated durations (`duration_or_age_h`) and gaps (`last_event_gap_h`). This enables precise continuous-time hazard modeling.
2.  **External Outcomes After Transfer:** Follow-up tracking for cases sent to external organizations (`Transfer`), allowing evaluation of whether transfers successfully resolve externally or result in failure.
3.  **Calendar Start and Cutoff Dates:** Absolute calendar timestamps for case submission and data freeze to properly model seasonal workload and staffing fluctuations.
4.  **Time-Varying Workload & Staffing Metrics:** Active queue depth, caseworker caseload, and resource availability at the time of each review step.
5.  **Assignment Mechanism and Policy Compliance:** Explicit audit logs detailing why Cohort B cases were or were not routed through automated screening, enabling propensity score weighting or instrumental variable analysis to isolate true policy causal effects from selection bias.
