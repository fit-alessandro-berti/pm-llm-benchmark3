# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Use `Submit` as elapsed time zero and 72 hours as the inclusive deadline: resolution at or before 72 hours is timely. These are elapsed clock hours, not business hours. Let C be age at the observation cutoff. The table supplies either the time of an observed outcome or C, but not absolute start dates or intermediate event times.

**Primary interpretation: successful internal resolution.** Assume Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer are mutually exclusive absorbing internal outcomes, with no reopening. Let T be time to the first such outcome, J its cause, F_k(t) = P(T ≤ t, J = k), and U(t) = P(T > t). Under this interpretation, transfer ends internal responsibility but is not successful resolution. This is a definition of the internal endpoint, not an assertion about external success.

Three useful estimands are:

1. **Non-resolution by the deadline:** N72 = 1 − F_Resolve(72). This includes cases still open and cases ending unsuccessfully. It answers whether an incoming case will achieve the specified successful outcome on time. It fits service-success governance, but includes early withdrawal and rejection even though those cases need no further internal work.
2. **Late successful resolution by a specified later horizon:** L(72, τ) = F_Resolve(τ) − F_Resolve(72), for τ > 72. Use τ = 96 hours as an illustrative finite horizon here. This measures successful recovery after a missed deadline and helps plan recovery work. It excludes unsuccessful endings and cases still unresolved at τ; therefore it is not the overall breach probability. “Ever resolves late” is not identifiable from this short follow-up without tail assumptions.
3. **Still awaiting internal disposition at the deadline:** U72 = U(72). This measures unresolved internal workload, treating all four internal outcomes as exits. It fits queue capacity and overdue-work management. A timely rejection can improve this measure without improving successful service delivery.

The relationship is N72 = U72 + F_Withdraw(72) + F_Reject(72) + F_Transfer(72). Thus a low pending-work rate does not establish a high successful-resolution rate.

For a case still awaiting internal disposition at 72 hours, the probability of resolution between 72 and τ is [F_Resolve(τ) − F_Resolve(72)] / U72, provided U72 > 0. Conditioning instead on all cases not resolved by 72 uses denominator N72 and includes already closed unsuccessful cases. These are different populations.

An **end-to-end resolution estimand** would count eventual external resolution as success. Its 72-hour risk cannot be identified for transfers before 72 from this extract. It requires external follow-up or explicit sensitivity assumptions. Likewise, a cause-specific resolution hazard describes the instantaneous resolution rate among cases with no previous competing outcome; it is not itself a breach probability.

The following contributions use the primary internal interpretation. An individual event contributes to an estimator through its risk set, not necessarily a weight of 1/32.

| Supplied example | N72: not internally resolved | L(72, τ): late resolution | U72: no internal disposition | Cause-specific interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76 h | Known breach: 1 | Resolve event at 76 contributes if τ ≥ 76 | Known pending at 72: 1 | Remains at risk through 76, then Resolve |
| S09, open at 92 h | Known breach: 1 | No observed Resolve; right-censored at 92 for future resolution | Known pending at 72: 1 | Remains at risk through 92; future outcome unknown |
| S06, withdrawn at 27 h | Known non-resolution: 1 | No late Resolve under the absorbing-outcome assumption | Already disposed at 72: 0 | Withdraw competing event at 27; exits the resolution risk set |
| S08, transferred at 68 h | Known internal non-resolution: 1 | No later internal Resolve under the primary definition | Internal disposition occurred: 0 | Transfer competing event at 68 |

For S08 under an end-to-end endpoint, censoring at transfer leaves its 72-hour resolution status unknown; assuming immediate external resolution makes it timely; assuming no external resolution makes it unsuccessful. These alternatives must not be mixed into one unlabelled “SLA breach” measure.

**Analysis assumptions:** the extract contains an inception sample rather than a selectively chosen set of completed or surviving cases; the supplied statuses and elapsed times are correct; priority is measured at submission and remains fixed for the baseline-stratified illustration. These assumptions are needed for the proposed risk sets and baseline comparisons. If priority changes, use its timestamped history instead. Independent censoring is a separate estimation assumption, assessed below rather than presumed from an `open` label.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Primary competing-risk codes are 0 = right-censored, 1 = Resolve, 2 = Withdraw, 3 = Reject, and 4 = Transfer. Define e_R as an observed resolution indicator and e_D as an indicator of any observed internal terminal disposition.

| Observed status | Analysis time | e_R | e_D | Primary cause code | Censoring rule and sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| resolved | Duration to Resolve | 1 | 1 | 1 | Observed event, even after 72. Its lateness is known; do not censor it at the SLA threshold. |
| withdrawn | Duration to Withdraw | 0 | 1 | 2 | Observed competing outcome. Alternative disposition SLA counts it as a successful closure. Treating it as censoring for a latent-resolution question requires an explicit hypothetical intervention and assumptions. |
| rejected | Duration to Reject | 0 | 1 | 3 | Observed competing outcome. Same distinction between successful service and timely closure; investigate eligibility before defining an alternative SLA denominator. |
| transferred | Duration to Transfer | 0 | 1 | 4 | Primary: observed internal competing outcome. Alternatives are described below. |
| open | Age at cutoff | 0 | 0 | 0 | Administrative right-censoring at age C for future outcomes. If C ≥ 72, its missed resolution deadline and pending status at 72 are already observed. If C < 72, those horizon labels are unknown. |

Use at least these three transfer treatments:

1. **Internal competing outcome:** retain code 4 at transfer. This directly answers the internal responsibility question without assuming anything about external resolution. Transfer is observed, so it is not missing follow-up for this endpoint.
2. **Loss of end-to-end follow-up:** use code 0 at transfer while retaining withdrawal and rejection as competing outcomes. Estimating an end-to-end resolution CIF then assumes that, conditional on measured history, transferred cases have the same subsequent outcome distribution as comparable cases whose outcomes remain observable. Transfers may alter service delivery as well as observation; this assumption is strong and untestable here.
3. **Optimistic immediate external resolution:** impute code 1 at the transfer time. S21 and S08 are then timely successes; S32 is a late success at 96 hours. This is an optimistic sensitivity proxy, not an observed Resolve. Assuming external resolution cannot precede transfer, it gives the earliest possible external resolution time, but its fitted CIF is not a distribution-free bound on the population CIF.

A pessimistic assumption of no eventual external resolution preserves transfers as unsuccessful absorbing outcomes for the resolution endpoint, yielding the primary internal non-resolution classification. It does not establish when an external workload ends.

In a cause-specific resolution regression, competing outcomes leave the risk set at their observed times and have e_R = 0. That encoding estimates a cause-specific hazard without assuming independent competing causes. Interpreting a resolution-only Kaplan–Meier curve as actual resolution probability is a different step and generally invalid: it removes the probability mass of competing outcomes.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

There are 32 cases: 13 resolved, 4 withdrawn, 3 rejected, 3 transferred, and 9 open. Nine resolutions were timely; four were late: S03, S13, S20, and S28. Restricting to completed resolutions reports 4/13 = 30.8% late, including 2/6 in A and 2/7 in B. This estimates lateness conditional on having reached an observed successful resolution before extraction, not the incoming-case probability of missing the SLA.

**Completion selection and survivor bias:** S09, S11, S25, and S26 are still open at ages 92, 81, 79, and 104. They have already missed the deadline, yet completed-resolution analysis discards all four. S13's 110-hour resolution shows that long durations exist, but similarly long unfinished cases have no opportunity to enter a completed-resolution summary. Selection depends on both outcome and follow-up length; omitting early unsuccessful endings creates a second selection effect.

**Competing risks:** S05's rejection at 9 hours and S06's withdrawal at 27 hours remove internal work but do not deliver Resolve. Excluding them hides unsuccessful service outcomes. Conversely, coding them as 72-hour pending cases inflates workload. S08 and S21 are observed transfers before the deadline, with unknown external success. S32 transfers at 96 hours, so it was already unresolved internally at 72 even though its external future is unknown.

**Already observed versus uncertain deadlines:** the four overdue open cases above have known 72-hour failures. The five younger open cases—S10 at 47, S15 at 35, S24 at 67, S29 at 51, and S31 at 23—have unknown deadline outcomes. S24 is close to the deadline but is not yet an observed breach. All nine contribute observed time to survival risk sets; none should simply be dropped.

**Informative observation:** S11 has a 55-hour event gap and S26 a 72-hour gap. Such silence might represent stalled work, external coordination, or an unrecorded completion. These are hypotheses, not supplied outcomes. Reliable open status makes their overdue state observable despite silence. If status is stale, missingness and true outcomes may be associated. Transfers are also selected: all three transferred cases are P1 and escalated, so they are not a random subset of comparable internal cases.

**Cohort imbalance:** A has 15 cases and B 17. Their priority distributions are A: P1/P2/P3 = 3/7/5 and B: 5/7/5. AutoCheck appears in 2/15 A prefixes and 13/17 B prefixes. This is an observed exposure proxy, not verified policy compliance; some prefixes are unfinished. B has a larger P1 share, begins after a calendar-time policy change, and was not randomized. Different case complexity, arrival dates, workload, adherence, and follow-up maturity could explain differences. Conditioning on eventual AutoCheck, escalation, or completion would introduce additional selection.

Without any independent-censoring assumption, the actual finite extract supplies useful **logical bounds** for internal N72: 9 known timely successes, 18 known failures, and 5 unknowns imply 18/32 ≤ N72 ≤ 23/32, or 56.25%–71.875%. For U72, 18 cases have a known terminal disposition by 72, 9 are known pending then, and 5 are unknown, giving 28.125%–43.75%. These are bounds on this extract's eventual horizon labels, not confidence intervals for a population parameter. End-to-end N72 has two additional unknowns, S08 and S21, so its bounds become 16/32–23/32 if withdrawal and rejection remain unsuccessful absorbing outcomes.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Primary analysis:** estimate all four CIFs with Aalen–Johansen under the internal competing-outcome definition. Report F_Resolve(72), N72, U72, and late resolution incidence to a supported later horizon, with numbers at risk and the logical bounds above. Use every case from Submit through its outcome or censoring age. First show pooled estimates, then descriptive cohort and cohort-by-priority estimates; standardize cohort comparisons to the same pooled priority distribution. With only 32 cases, these are exploratory summaries, not a basis for a large regression model.

For this single initial-state competing-risk model, at each event time t, let n(t) be the number still observed and event-free just before t and d_k(t) the events of cause k. Update U(t) by multiplying by 1 − Σ_k d_k(t)/n(t), and update F_k(t) by adding U(t−)d_k(t)/n(t). At a tied time, include same-time censorings in the event risk set. No exact stage timestamps are required for this case-level calculation.

Under independent administrative censoring for the relevant population or strata, the supplied data give these **estimates**, rounded:

| Quantity | Pooled primary estimate |
|---|---:|
| Resolution by 72, F_Resolve(72) | 30.4% |
| Non-resolution by 72, N72 | 69.6% |
| No internal disposition at 72, U72 | 38.0% |
| Withdrawal CIF at 72 | 14.0% |
| Rejection CIF at 72 | 10.1% |
| Transfer CIF at 72 | 7.5% |
| Resolution between 72 and 96 | 14.3% |

Nine cases have observed follow-up reaching 72 hours; only three remain in the event-free observed risk set immediately before 96 hours. Late-tail estimates therefore deserve substantial uncertainty. A last observed resolution at 110 hours does not establish that the population's remaining unresolved cases will all resolve.

The methods answer different questions:

| Method | Appropriate role and practical limitation here |
|---|---|
| Kaplan–Meier | With every internal terminal outcome treated as an event, estimates U(t); it gives U72 = 38.0%, matching the primary AJ survivor function. With only Resolve as an event and competing outcomes censored, gives a net non-resolution estimate of 63.9% at 72, rather than actual-world N72 = 69.6%. The latter net curve concerns a hypothetical removal of competing outcomes, not the primary service-success endpoint. |
| Aalen–Johansen/CIF | Primary method: preserves resolution, withdrawal, rejection, and transfer probabilities and separates success from workload. Requires independent censoring, possibly conditional on strata/history; it does not require independent competing causes. |
| Cause-specific hazards | Model transition rates among currently event-free cases. Useful later for stage-specific staffing and dynamic prediction, but reconstruct CIFs using all relevant cause hazards. Thirteen resolution events cannot support a rich model with priority, cohort, multiple path features, and interactions. |
| Subdistribution hazards | A baseline Fine–Gray model can relate covariates to resolution CIFs. Its modified risk set retains competing-outcome cases; its hazard ratio is not the resolution-rate multiplier among active cases. Internal time-varying stages become problematic after terminal outcomes. It adds little over direct CIF summaries at this sample size. |
| Restricted mean time | Integrate U(t) to 72 or 96 for expected hours awaiting internal disposition. Integrate 1 − F_Resolve(t) for restricted hours without successful resolution, keeping unsuccessful closed cases unresolved to the chosen horizon. These are different burdens. Integrating U(t) from 72 to τ measures expected overdue internal work time. Restrict horizons to supported follow-up. |
| Landmarking | Predict resolution or disposition before 72 conditional on being active at a fixed age, such as 24 or 48 hours, using only history available then. At age 72, predict late recovery among active cases. Prefixes observed at different final ages do not supply complete historical landmark covariates. |
| Multi-state model | With timestamped logs, model process stages, revisits, escalation, and absorbing outcomes. It can connect queue dwell to transition-specific hazards and forecast active-state occupancy. Ordered prefixes alone identify observed transitions, not their dwell times or stage-specific hourly hazards. |

**Secondary analysis:** use all-terminal KM and restricted mean pending time to answer capacity questions, alongside transfer and observation sensitivities. With timestamped histories and a larger sample, add landmark predictions and a parsimonious semi-Markov multi-state model. Time since entering a stage is likely more useful than a memoryless state-only assumption for stalled queues.

Report case-bootstrap uncertainty or suitable nonparametric confidence intervals, explicitly flagging tiny cells and bootstrap replicates lacking horizon support. Preserve cohort/priority structure when estimating standardized contrasts. Cases may share workload shocks, so case independence also needs checking. Do not claim a significant or causal cohort benefit from these point estimates; no numerical confidence intervals are supplied here.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Baseline candidates are cohort and submission priority, subject to the stated priority assumption. Case identifiers are identifiers, not predictors. Arrival calendar time, initial complexity, and eligibility would be valuable baseline features, but are missing.

| Feature from the supplied prefixes | Type and useful interpretation | Prediction constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Latest recorded stage, such as Screen, Review, or Escalate | Time-varying state for currently active cases; supports queue-specific intervention | A final stage at cutoff cannot be assigned retrospectively to Submit or an earlier landmark. A terminal activity identifies the outcome. |
| AutoCheck occurrence and its position relative to Screen | Time-varying exposure and possible mediator of the policy; describes observed execution patterns | Eventual occurrence is post-treatment and depends on follow-up. Use an indicator that switches only when the event occurs. It is not equivalent to policy assignment or verified compliance. |
| Escalation flag and elapsed time to escalation | Time-varying intervention/severity marker, potentially confounded by priority and deteriorating performance | Define E(t) = 1 only after escalation. The supplied `escalated` column is an eventual observed flag, unsafe as a baseline exposure. |
| Number of Review visits and repeated Review | Cumulative time-varying history; S03, S07, and S13 have repeated Review | Longer observation creates more opportunity for repetition. Final visit counts are post-outcome information for a baseline prediction. |
| Rework count and previous rework | Time-varying burden; present in S07, S13, and S27 | Rework may mediate policy effects or mark complexity. Final counts cannot be adjusted for automatically in a total policy-effect analysis. |
| Ordered path/variant and observed variant frequency | Descriptive control flow; can identify commonly stalled prefixes | Closed full paths include their terminal outcome, and open paths are truncated. Learn an outcome-free prefix representation from history available at the prediction time. |
| Time since the latest recorded event | Time-varying inactivity signal; helps distinguish active progress from long silence | Final gap depends on observation time and is not a baseline variable. Silence is not proof of censoring or of an unobserved resolution. |

AutoCheck can occur before Screen, after Screen, or before an Escalate without an observed Screen, as S16, S17, and S18 show. A single compulsory linear screening sequence would misrepresent these prefixes. S27 reaches Resolve directly after Rework, whereas S07 revisits Review. Rework must not be equated with a repeated Review visit.

**Proposed transition diagram**, using the union of supplied immediate adjacencies, expressed without assigning event times:

- Submit → Screen or AutoCheck.
- Screen → AutoCheck, Review, Escalate, Resolve, Reject, or Withdraw.
- AutoCheck → Screen, Review, Escalate, or Resolve.
- Review → Rework, Escalate, Resolve, Withdraw, or Reject.
- Rework → Review or Resolve.
- Escalate → Review, Resolve, or Transfer.
- Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer are absorbing internal states in the primary model.

For an end-to-end extension, add a hypothesized external-active state reached by Transfer, followed by external outcomes requiring new data. The observed adjacency union is descriptive; combinations of its edges are not evidence that every resulting path is possible. An escalation-history flag and cumulative rework count can accompany the current stage rather than forcing a separate state for every history.

With timestamps, estimate transition-specific hazards as functions of case age, current stage, time in stage, priority, prior escalation/rework, and contemporaneous workload. S11 and S25 suggest an escalated queue deserving investigation, while S09 and S26 suggest a review queue with prolonged silence. These are observed stalled prefixes, not proof that escalation or review caused delays.

Some elapsed times can legitimately be derived: for an open case, age minus last-event gap gives the latest recorded event's elapsed time. Thus S11's latest Escalate is at 26 hours and S25's at 31 hours. This does not recover earlier activity timestamps or all cases' escalation times. The code deliberately keeps event order without inventing timestamps.

**Immortal-time risk:** classifying S03 as “escalated” from Submit incorrectly attributes its pre-escalation time to the exposed group. To receive escalation, it must remain eligible and unresolved until that event. Selection by this future event can generate a spurious association even without a treatment effect. Use time-dependent exposure or landmark history; address confounding by indication separately. For a total cohort-policy effect, AutoCheck, escalation, and rework may be mediators, so adjusting for their final occurrence changes the estimand and can introduce bias.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

The executable analysis below supplies the first three transfer scenarios, retaining withdrawal and rejection as competing outcomes. Estimates are conditional on each scenario's endpoint and observation assumptions.

| Transfer assumption | Pooled non-resolution at 72 | Resolution between 72 and 96 | Meaning |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Internal competing outcome | 69.6% | 14.3% | Primary internal service-success endpoint |
| Censored at transfer | 68.7% | 16.4% | Hypothetical end-to-end analysis under conditional independent transfer censoring |
| Imputed resolution at transfer | 62.1% | 22.2% | Optimistic external-success proxy; includes imputed late success for S32 |

The optimistic increase in late resolution partly comes from assigning success at 96 to S32, not from observing additional internal resolution. A higher late-resolution CIF can mean more eventual recovery, not necessarily worse service.

Run the following additional comparisons:

1. **Withdrawal/rejection interpretation:** retain them as unsuccessful competing events for the primary SLA, then analyze timely disposition as a separate endpoint. Under the primary analysis, cohort A has estimated N72 = 70.8% and B 68.4%, slightly favoring B. For pending work, U72 is 33.9% in A and 41.6% in B, favoring A. The operational ranking already reverses when the target changes. Do not exclude withdrawal/rejection retrospectively merely because their outcomes are inconvenient. A eligibility-based denominator needs rules defined before the outcome.
2. **Informative transfer:** obtain external outcomes where possible; otherwise vary the probability of external resolution by 72 for S08 and S21 and post-transfer delay distributions. Assume resolution cannot precede transfer. S32's known internal miss cannot be repaired by any later external success. If difficult P1 cases tend to transfer, censoring them may make service look better than it is; successful fast external handling could instead make the internal endpoint pessimistic for end-to-end service.
3. **Informative administrative observation:** maintain open cases at their stated cutoff age as primary. With calendar starts and complete histories, model remaining observable follow-up conditional on priority, cohort, start date, stages, workload, and inactivity; use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights when conditional exchangeability and positivity are plausible. Check unstable weights and compare trimmed versus untrimmed analyses. These assumptions cannot be verified or fitted adequately with the present sample.
4. **Stale-status sensitivity:** audit long-gap cases and use a tipping analysis allowing specified fractions of their outcomes to have gone unrecorded. As a deliberately different observation model, one may censor at the last verified event, rather than the extract age. For S09, S11, S25, and S26, those ages are 61, 26, 31, and 32, so this alternative removes their known-overdue classification only by rejecting the reliability of the supplied open status. It is not the primary treatment and should not silently erase breaches. Consider possible terminal cause as well as completion time when imputing missing events.
5. **Cohort and policy:** compare crude CIFs, common-priority standardized CIFs, and, when available, comparisons adjusted for baseline severity and calendar workload. Pooled priority weights are P1/P2/P3 = 8/32, 14/32, and 10/32. Under the primary definition, standardized N72 is 68.75% for A and 67.50% for B. Under immediate-resolution transfer imputation, it is 60.42% for A and 62.50% for B: the cohort ranking reverses. The differences are small relative to sample uncertainty. Separate the cohort-assignment contrast from an actual policy-exposure contrast; neither is a causal effect here.

The robust observations are the four late internal resolutions and four overdue cases labelled open, assuming the recorded status is reliable. Less robust conclusions include the amount of end-to-end failure, whether B performs better, whether escalation is helpful, and how much silence reflects a real bottleneck. Restricting horizon or dropping the longest-gap cases can change conclusions by changing the target or selection, not by resolving uncertainty.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Use **two dashboards**: successful resolution by the deadline and pending internal work. Show withdrawal, rejection, and transfer CIFs alongside them. This prevents faster unsuccessful closure or transfer from appearing to improve successful resolution without explanation. SLA governance must specify whether the organization owns internal or end-to-end completion and which unsuccessful outcomes are legitimate exemptions, using rules set before outcomes are observed.

For an active case at age a < 72, alert on P(no resolution by 72 | still active at a, history through a), with remaining time and predicted competing outcomes displayed separately. Select alert thresholds using capacity, the cost of a miss, and the benefit of a feasible intervention. S24 has five hours remaining and a recent event gap of four hours; it warrants deadline attention but is not yet a demonstrated breach. S31 has 49 hours remaining and a three-hour gap, a different operational situation. A risk probability for either case is not identifiable reliably from these two rows alone.

For a ≥ 72, the deadline miss is already observed: route S09, S11, S25, and S26 to recovery or status-verification work. Predict eventual resolution and time to disposition rather than repeatedly labelling their breach as uncertain. Long-gap escalated P1 cases S11 and S25 warrant checking ownership and stalled dependencies; the review prefixes of S09 and S26 warrant review-queue investigation. None of these patterns proves that adding escalation will accelerate resolution.

Forecast active workload from state-occupancy probabilities and restricted mean pending time, then allocate review and escalation capacity according to forecasted arrivals, age, and priority. Track transferred cases through an external ownership register if end-to-end success is the governed endpoint. Report transfer closure separately while external completion remains unknown.

Validate future models at fixed prediction ages and horizons using later calendar cohorts, retaining cases whose outcomes become observable after prediction. Use competing-risk or censoring-adjusted calibration and Brier scores with their assumptions stated; compare predicted versus observed resolution and disposition CIFs. Check calibration, missed-alert rates, alert burden, and time to action by priority and cohort. P1 is highest priority, so evaluate whether the action policy respects urgency rather than optimizing only pooled accuracy. Tiny strata cannot certify fairness or calibration; use uncertainty intervals and gather more data. No protected-attribute fairness conclusion is possible from these fields.

Risk predictions summarize prognosis under observed practice. They do not estimate the benefit of escalation, automation, or extra staffing. All eight P1 cases in this sample are escalated, leaving no observed P1 non-escalated comparison. Estimating intervention effects needs stronger assignment information and a design addressing time-varying confounding; a prediction-driven workflow should subsequently be evaluated for actual operational benefit.

def encode(data, scenario):
    out = data.copy()
    out["time"] = out["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
    out["code"] = out["observed_status"].map(
        {"open": 0, "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2,
         "rejected": 3, "transferred": 4}).astype(int)
    transferred = out["observed_status"].eq("transferred")
    if scenario == "transfer_censored":
        out.loc[transferred, "code"] = 0
    elif scenario == "transfer_immediate_resolution":
        out.loc[transferred, "code"] = 1  # Sensitivity imputation, not observed Resolve.
    elif scenario != "internal_competing":
        raise ValueError(scenario)
    out["resolve_event"] = out["code"].eq(1).astype(int)
    out["any_event"] = out["code"].gt(0).astype(int)
    # Exactly known horizon labels; early censored observations remain unknown.
    nr = pd.Series(pd.NA, index=out.index, dtype="Int64")
    nr.loc[out["time"].ge(H) | out["code"].gt(1)] = 1
    nr.loc[out["code"].eq(1) & out["time"].le(H)] = 0
    pending = pd.Series(pd.NA, index=out.index, dtype="Int64")
    pending.loc[out["time"].ge(H)] = 1
    pending.loc[out["code"].gt(0) & out["time"].le(H)] = 0
    out["not_resolved_72_known"] = nr
    out["no_modeled_terminal_72_known"] = pending
    return out

def aj_table(data):
    """Aalen-Johansen for one initial state and mutually exclusive causes."""
    survival = 1.0
    cif = {k: 0.0 for k in CAUSES}
    rows = []
    for t in sorted(data["time"].unique()):
        n = int(data["time"].ge(t).sum())
        at_t = data.loc[data["time"].eq(t), "code"]
        deaths = {k: int(at_t.eq(k).sum()) for k in CAUSES}
        before = survival
        for k in CAUSES:
            cif[k] += before * deaths[k] / n
        survival *= 1.0 - sum(deaths.values()) / n
        rows.append({"time": t, "risk_set": n, "S": survival,
                     "censored": int(at_t.eq(0).sum()),
                     **{f"d{k}": deaths[k] for k in CAUSES},
                     **{f"F{k}": cif[k] for k in CAUSES}})
    # At tied times, events use the risk set including same-time censorings.
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def at_horizon(table, horizon):
    if horizon > table["time"].max() and table.iloc[-1]["S"] > 1e-12:
        return None  # Do not extrapolate a censored tail without support.
    past = table.loc[table["time"].le(horizon)]
    if past.empty:
        return {"S": 1.0, **{f"F{k}": 0.0 for k in CAUSES}}
    return past.iloc[-1].to_dict()

def km_ready(data, event):
    return data[["case", "cohort", "priority", "time", event]].rename(
        columns={event: "event"})

def km_at(ready, horizon):
    s = 1.0
    for t in sorted(ready.loc[ready["time"].le(horizon), "time"].unique()):
        n = int(ready["time"].ge(t).sum())
        d = int(ready.loc[ready["time"].eq(t), "event"].sum())
        s *= 1.0 - d / n
    if horizon > ready["time"].max() and s > 1e-12:
        return float("nan")
    return s

def label_bounds(series):
    yes = int(series.eq(1).sum())
    unknown = int(series.isna().sum())
    return yes / len(series), (yes + unknown) / len(series), unknown

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The 32 cases provide only 13 resolution events, sparse priority/cohort cells, unknown future outcomes for five cases censored before the deadline, and no externally observed resolutions. Detailed hazard regressions, process-state duration estimates, strong calibration claims, and causal policy conclusions would exceed the evidence. The policy contrast also mixes calendar change, nonrandom assignment, and incomplete adherence. An apparent smaller late-resolution incidence can reflect less eventual success rather than less tail risk.

Prioritize the following additions:

1. **Exact Submit and activity timestamps**, event lifecycle information, and terminal/reopening rules. These allow time-varying escalation and AutoCheck exposure, stage dwell estimates, valid landmark features, and detection of clock or logging problems. Preserve elapsed-clock semantics and confirm whether priority changes over time.
2. **Linked external outcomes after Transfer**, external resolution timestamps, responsibility boundaries, and transfer reasons. These directly address the unidentifiable end-to-end SLA and informative transfer mechanism.
3. **Absolute cutoff/start dates and extract inclusion rules**, including whether cases entered observation after Submit. These establish follow-up maturity, calendar confounding, independent-censoring plausibility, and any left-truncation or case-ascertainment correction.
4. **Time-varying workload and ownership:** queue size, arrivals, staffing, team/worker allocation, handoffs, and outage periods. These help explain simultaneous stalled prefixes and support meaningful resource forecasts.
5. **Assignment mechanism and policy rollout compliance:** eligibility, activation dates, intended assignment, actual automated-screening use, escalation triggers/timestamps, exception reasons, and baseline complexity. These distinguish assignment from exposure and support a defensible evaluation design. Additional pre-policy periods would be needed for a credible comparative time-series or difference-in-differences study.
6. **Status verification and logging-completeness audits**, especially for long event gaps, plus reasons for withdrawal and rejection. These distinguish legitimate absorbing outcomes, stalled live work, unrecorded completion, and possible reopening.
7. **Larger prospectively followed cohorts and repeated freezes** extending beyond the reporting horizon. These provide supported late-tail estimates, independent model validation, and sufficiently sized priority/cohort calibration checks.

Until those data exist, report the endpoint definition, observed deadline labels and bounds, competing-outcome probabilities, risk-set sizes, and sensitivity-dependent cohort comparisons together.
