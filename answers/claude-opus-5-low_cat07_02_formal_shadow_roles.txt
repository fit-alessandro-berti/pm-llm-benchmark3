# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

The operating rule used throughout: an activity is **core** for a role when (a) the role holds a large share of the process-wide volume of that activity, (b) the activity appears in that role's position in the frequent fragments, and (c) removing the role degrades that activity's throughput or quality. Everything else is peripheral, and peripheral activities come in three distinct flavours that must not be conflated: *overflow* (helping under load), *substitution* (covering an absence), and *rework* (touching an activity because something went wrong upstream).

**R-A Automated Intake Layer (Nora).** Core: A1, A2 precheck. Peripheral: none — and that is the point. Nora's 1,370 events are entirely low-complexity (median 0.11, zero high/critical cases). She has no substitution capability in either direction: she cannot cover for humans on anything non-trivial, and when she fails, work silently reverts to humans without an explicit handover event. Authority: none; she executes a rule set. The automation boundary is therefore a *risk* boundary: everything she validates is validated to a fixed rule, and cases whose entitlement is genuinely ambiguous are exactly the ones her precheck will misclassify.

**R-B Front-Office Generalists (Amira, Ben).** Core: A2 Validate, A4 Standard Response, A12 Close, plus human A1. Peripheral: A3 Diagnose (75/60) is *triage-depth* diagnosis, not technical diagnosis — it precedes the handover in fragment 2 rather than replacing it. A8 Negotiate and A7 Pricing (Amira 42/18, Ben 25/5) are overflow into commercial territory on simple exceptions. A10 Implement and A11 Quality (single digits) are almost certainly small corrective touches. Complexity: both are low-median, but Amira's 58 high + 9 critical against Ben's 31 + 3, plus her 3.6× pricing edge, mark her as the informal senior of the pair. Authority: none formal — they hold no A9. Substitution: they absorb temp-generated rework (fragment 6), which is an unbudgeted load.

**R-C Temporary Pool (Kai, Lina).** Core: A1, A2, A4, A12 on low-complexity cases only. Peripheral: A3 (8/12) and A8 (3/4) are marginal and should be audited — a temp with no certification recording a Diagnose event is either a labelling artefact or unsupervised scope creep. Complexity: capped by construction (0 critical, 2–4 high between them). Authority: none. The crucial finding is that their *core* work is not actually safe: weeks 8–10 produced 17 reopens traced to incomplete A2 notes. Their A2 events are nominally identical to Amira's and Ben's but carry a materially higher downstream failure rate. Volume equivalence is not quality equivalence.

**R-D Technical Resolution (Chen, Dina).** Core: A3 Diagnose, A5 Configure, A10 Implement — the trio moves together for both. Peripheral: A2/A4 (front-office backflow, ~20–35 events), A11 Quality (10–12, self-checks), A14 KB (11–14). The internal split is the substantive point: Chen carries 42 critical cases and 24 A6 security-adjacent events on a late shift; Dina carries 9 critical and 6 A6. Same core, different depth. Authority: technical decision rights over configuration, no approval rights. Substitution: Dina can cover Chen's *routine* load but the 0.94 similarity gives no evidence she can cover his critical/late-shift load — and the log contains no episode where she did.

**R-E Quality Gatekeeping (Ivan, Julia).** Core: A11 (310/298) and A14 (96/104). Peripheral: A10 Implement (38/35), A5 Configure (26/22), A3 Diagnose (20/16), A13 Reopen (12/8). Read these peripherals as the *fix-forward* pattern — a quality analyst who fails a check and then repairs the item themselves. That is efficient but it compromises independence: the checker becomes the doer. Complexity: medium (0.48–0.52) because quality checks follow the technical stream, not the critical stream. Authority: real but non-obvious — the right to fail work and force rework is a control right that never appears as an event count. Shift split (Ivan late, Julia day) means quality coverage is continuous, which is a resilience asset worth protecting.

**R-F Commercial Exception Handling (Fatima).** Core: A7 (122 of ~235 process-wide) and A8 (90). Peripheral: A2 Validate (22, entitlement checks feeding her own pricing decisions), A9 (16 — most plausibly preliminary or delegated approvals later confirmed by Hana), A12 Close (18). Complexity: 0.67, with 105 high and 25 critical — a small-volume, high-stakes role. Authority: pricing-exception decision rights, bounded above by A9. Substitution: covered in weeks 3–4 by Giorgio, at roughly double the cycle time.

**R-G Security Specialist (Erik).** Core: A6 only. Peripheral: A3, A5, A10, A11, A14 — these are follow-through on cases he has already reviewed, not a second role. Complexity: 0.86, only 5 low-complexity cases in ten weeks; his work is definitionally the tail of the distribution. Authority: veto/gate authority over security-sensitive changes — again invisible in counts. Substitution: none exists. Weeks 7–8 show the system's response to load was not to substitute Erik but to *reduce his input queue* via Mateo's filtering, which is a different and riskier control.

**R-H Commercial Shadow (Giorgio).** Core (formal): A2 (190), A4 (150), A12 (130), A1 (90) — a front-office profile. Core (de facto, second role): A7 (72) and A8 (88). Note that Giorgio's A8 volume nearly equals Fatima's (88 vs 90) and his A7 is 59% of hers, and this is his *steady-state* level, not just the 31 events from her absence. Peripheral: A9 (20), A3 (40). Complexity: bimodal — median 0.58 understates him; he has 120 high and 36 critical cases, more critical cases than Fatima. Authority: ambiguous; the 20 A9 events are the sharpest question in the log. Substitution: proven capable but demonstrably slower on pricing (38→74 min).

**R-I Escalation Broker (Mateo).** Core: routing and coordination, which the log expresses as A13 Reopen (42), preliminary A9 (44), and thin presence everywhere else. Peripheral: essentially every individual activity — his 10–52 counts per column are each unremarkable in isolation. Complexity: 0.77 median, 76 critical cases, the highest critical count after Hana. Authority: coordination authority, explicitly *not* final approval authority — week 5 established that 62% of his approvals required Hana's recheck. Substitution: he substitutes for others rather than being substituted for.

**R-J Approval Authority (Hana).** Core: A9 (138) and reopen adjudication (24). Peripheral: A8 (22), A11 (12), and scattered A2/A3/A7 — these read as supervisory sampling rather than production work. Complexity: 0.79, 62 critical. Authority: the only unambiguous final authority in the process. Substitution: attempted in week 5 and only partially successful.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

### 3.1 R07_Giorgio — commercial shadow role

Giorgio's formal title is Senior Service Agent and his largest activity counts are front-office. But he holds `pricing` certification alongside `entitlement`, and his ongoing A7/A8 volume (72/88) is at the same order of magnitude as the designated Commercial Specialist. His similarity signature is the tell: 0.84 to Amira and 0.71 to Fatima simultaneously. He is not a front-office agent who occasionally helps commercial; he is a *second, undeclared commercial capacity* embedded in the front office, most likely because it is faster to price a complex front-office case in place than to hand it off.

*Benefits.* He shortens the path for complex front-office cases that would otherwise queue for Fatima; he provides real absence cover; he is a natural training bridge between two teams; and his 36 critical cases suggest management already routes hard work to him deliberately.

*Risks.* Substitution performance was measurably worse (median pricing exception 38 → 74 min), so his competence is real but not equivalent. His work is invisible in the commercial team's capacity plan and probably in its quality assurance, meaning pricing decisions are being made outside the controls designed for pricing decisions. His 20 A9 approval events are unexplained and potentially a segregation-of-duties issue — an agent who both negotiates and approves. And because the shadow role is informal, it is unrewarded and undocumented; if Giorgio leaves, the process loses commercial capacity that no org chart records.

*Recommendation.* Formalize rather than suppress: make the hybrid role explicit, define which pricing exception bands he may decide, and route the rest to Fatima.

### 3.2 R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker

Mateo has non-zero counts in all fourteen activities and a dominant count in none. This is the classic signature of a broker, and it is precisely the signature that activity-frequency clustering handles worst. His value is structural: in the handover network he will show high betweenness and a low clustering coefficient, connecting front office, technical, commercial, security and management components that otherwise touch each other rarely. Fragments 3 and 5 both pivot on him — every reopen path and every security-escalation path passes through his coordination.

He performs three distinguishable functions. First, *routing*: reopened and cross-functional cases (42 A13 events) are triaged to the right specialist. Second, *preliminary approval*: 44 A9 events, but week 5 shows these are provisional — 13 of 21 were rechecked. Third, and most consequentially, *demand shaping*: in weeks 7–8 he filtered low-risk security referrals away from Erik.

*Benefits.* Reduced false escalations, protection of scarce specialist capacity, and faster cross-team resolution.

*Risks.* Demand shaping made him an unaccountable gatekeeper on the critical path — two critical cases waited more than two days. Filtering that reduces false positives will, at some threshold, produce false negatives, and no one had authority or visibility over where he set that threshold. Separately, his preliminary approvals create genuine authority ambiguity: downstream actors may not know whether an A9 is final.

*Recommendation.* Keep the broker; make the filter explicit. Publish the risk criteria for security referral, log filtered-out referrals as events so the false-negative rate is measurable, and mark preliminary versus final approvals distinctly.

### 3.3 R05_Erik — rare but critical specialist

Erik is the clearest demonstration that frequency and importance are unrelated. He generated ~161 events in ten weeks — fewer than Kai produces in a week — yet 55 of his cases are critical and only 5 are low-complexity, giving him the highest median complexity in the log (0.86). He performs roughly two-thirds of all Security Review events, and fragment 3 places him squarely on the critical path for security-sensitive resolution.

*Structural fragility.* There is no second security-certified resource. Chen's 24 A6 events are the only partial substitute in the data, and they are best read as security-adjacent technical work rather than independent review. When load spiked in weeks 7–8, the organization did not add security capacity; it throttled Erik's input queue. That is a coping mechanism, not resilience.

*Benefits of the current arrangement.* Deep expertise, consistent risk judgment, low false-positive rate, and a genuinely low cost of maintaining the role.

*Risks.* Single point of failure with no tested backup; his absence during a security incident would be unrecoverable. His A6 work also correlates with a small A14 KB contribution (9 events), suggesting his knowledge is largely tacit.

*Recommendation.* Do not measure Erik on throughput. Build a second security-certified resource — Chen is the obvious candidate given his existing A6 exposure — and require KB capture on critical reviews.

### 3.4 Additional bridging roles

**Automation boundary role (R14_Nora + the humans downstream of her).** Nora's 1,370 events set the entry condition for everything else, but her median complexity is 0.11 and she handles zero high or critical cases. The interesting role is not Nora but the *exception-handling boundary*: who catches the cases her precheck gets wrong, and is that catch logged? Fragment 1 shows her registrations flowing straight into temp/front-office standard responses with no explicit re-validation. Combined with the weeks 8–10 reopen finding, this suggests a validate-quality gap that spans the bot–temp handoff.

**Temporary workforce role (R11_Kai, R12_Lina).** Their 0.98 similarity and near-subset activity profile make them look like cheap front-office agents. They are not: they are a *load buffer* with a hard complexity ceiling and a measurable quality cost (17 reopens from incomplete A2 notes). The reopens land on Amira, Ben and Mateo — so temp capacity partially converts into senior rework, and the true cost of the buffer is understated by headcount.

**Quality gatekeeping role (R09_Ivan, R10_Julia).** They hold near-monopoly control over A11 and A14, which gives them real veto power over the technical stream despite unremarkable complexity figures. Their own A10/A5 activity means the checker sometimes becomes the fixer, weakening independence. Their day/late shift split is an underrecognized resilience asset.

None of these shadow arrangements is improper. Every one of them exists because it solves a real routing, capacity or expertise problem faster than the formal structure does. The issue is that each is invisible to planning and to control, so its benefits are unbudgeted and its risks are unmonitored.

# 4. Ambiguous assignments and alternative interpretations

**R07_Giorgio.**
*Interpretation A — front-office senior with commercial overflow duties.* His largest mass is A1/A2/A4/A12 and his home team is Front Office.
*Interpretation B — de facto second commercial specialist stationed in the front office.* His A8 volume nearly matches Fatima's and his critical-case count exceeds hers.
*Deciding evidence:* Segment his A7/A8 events by whether they occurred during Fatima's absence. If ongoing weeks look like the absence weeks, B holds. Also inspect whether his pricing cases arrive by handover from others (B: he is a destination) or are cases he already owned (A: opportunistic in-place handling). Finally, examine the exception value/discount bands he decides versus Fatima's — same bands means an unrecognized peer; strictly smaller bands means a bounded overflow role.

**R13_Mateo.**
*Interpretation A — coordinator without decision rights;* his 44 A9 events are administrative staging.
*Interpretation B — de facto deputy supervisor* exercising real, if provisional, authority.
*Deciding evidence:* The week-5 recheck rate is the key statistic — 13 of 21 rechecked argues for A. Check whether his A9 events outside week 5 are also followed by a Hana A9 on the same case; if most stand alone, B gains ground. Also check whether any case *closed* on his approval alone.

**R05_Erik.**
*Interpretation A — pure security gate,* with A3/A5/A10 as follow-through on his own cases.
*Interpretation B — senior technical resource who also does security,* i.e. partially substitutable with Chen.
*Deciding evidence:* Check whether his A3/A5/A10 events occur on cases where he also logged A6. If yes, A. If he appears on technical cases with no security review, B. Cross-check whether Chen's 24 A6 events are independent reviews or assists on Erik's cases.

**R03_Chen versus R04_Dina.**
*Interpretation A — one homogeneous Technical Analyst role* (cosine 0.94).
*Interpretation B — a two-tier role: senior/complex and mid-level/routine.*
*Deciding evidence:* Complexity already favours B (0.71 vs 0.48; 42 vs 9 critical). Confirm with service times on high-complexity A5/A10 and with rework rates: if Dina's high-complexity cases show elevated A11 failures or A13 reopens, B is decisive. Also test whether Dina ever handles critical cases on the late shift unaided.

**R01_Amira versus R02_Ben.**
*Interpretation A — identical front-office agents.*
*Interpretation B — Amira is an informal senior/complex-case handler.*
*Deciding evidence:* 58 vs 31 high-complexity cases and 18 vs 5 A7 events already lean B. Decide by checking whether complex cases are routed to Amira by handover (deliberate assignment) or arrive at random, and whether her A8 events cluster on medium/high cases while Ben's cluster on low.

**R06_Fatima's 16 A9 approval events.**
*Interpretation A — she holds delegated approval authority within pricing limits.*
*Interpretation B — logging artefact / preliminary sign-off later confirmed by Hana.*
*Deciding evidence:* Check whether a Hana A9 follows on the same case. If not, she has real authority and the approval hierarchy in fragment 4 is incomplete. Same test applies to Giorgio's 20 A9 events, where a positive result would raise a segregation-of-duties concern since he also negotiates.

**R09_Ivan and R10_Julia's A10/A5 events.**
*Interpretation A — quality analysts performing minor fixes after failed checks.*
*Interpretation B — a genuine secondary technical capacity.*
*Deciding evidence:* Check event order within the case. A11 → A10 by the same resource supports A; A10 preceding any A11, or on cases with no quality check, supports B. Under A, note the independence concern; under B, quality capacity is being consumed by production work.

**R11_Kai and R12_Lina's A3 Diagnose events (8 and 12).**
*Interpretation A — mislabelled triage notes.*
*Interpretation B — unsupervised scope creep beyond certification.*
*Deciding evidence:* Check the duration of those events and whether the cases were later reopened or re-diagnosed by R03/R04. Very short durations plus immediate technical re-diagnosis indicates A; substantive durations followed by direct Configure indicates B, which is a control finding.

**R14_Nora's A2 Validate events.**
*Interpretation A — full entitlement validation equivalent to a human's.*
*Interpretation B — a rule-based precheck that is not equivalent and needs human confirmation on non-trivial cases.*
*Deciding evidence:* Compare reopen rates for cases whose only A2 was Nora's against cases with a subsequent human A2. Given the weeks 8–10 finding on incomplete validation notes, this comparison is worth running before extending automation.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
df = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution.csv")
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)
# keep org:resource and the contextual attributes; format_dataframe
# only normalizes the three mandatory columns.
log["org:resource"] = log["org:resource"].astype(str)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Baseline role discovery (activity-frequency based)
#    NOTE: this is a HYPOTHESIS GENERATOR, not an authority model.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)
# roles :: list of (activity_set, {resource: count})

role_rows = []
for idx, role in enumerate(roles):
    activities, res_counts = role[0], role[1]
    for res, cnt in res_counts.items():
        role_rows.append({
            "mined_role_id": f"MR{idx:02d}",
            "mined_role_activities": "|".join(sorted(activities)),
            "org:resource": res,
            "role_event_count": cnt,
        })
roles_df = pd.DataFrame(role_rows)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Activity-based resource similarity
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
# sim :: {(r1, r2): cosine_similarity}
sim_df = (pd.Series(sim).rename("similarity").rename_axis(["r1", "r2"])
            .reset_index())

# Similar profile != similar authority or risk. Always pair similarity
# with a complexity delta before concluding "interchangeable".
med_cx = log.groupby("org:resource")["case_complexity"].median()
crit = (log[log["case_complexity"] >= 0.8]
          .groupby("org:resource")["case:concept:name"].nunique())

sim_df["cx_gap"] = (sim_df["r1"].map(med_cx) - sim_df["r2"].map(med_cx)).abs()
sim_df["crit_ratio"] = (
    sim_df[["r1", "r2"]].apply(lambda s: s.map(crit).fillna(0), axis=0)
      .pipe(lambda d: (d.max(axis=1) + 1) / (d.min(axis=1) + 1))
)
# High similarity + high cx_gap/crit_ratio  ==>  TIERED role, not one role.
tiered = sim_df[(sim_df.similarity > 0.85) &
                ((sim_df.cx_gap > 0.15) | (sim_df.crit_ratio > 2.5))]

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Social networks: structure that activity counts cannot show
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
ho   = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log, beta=0)
wt   = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
subc = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(log, n=2)

def net_to_df(net, name):
    return (pd.Series(net.connections).rename(name)
              .rename_axis(["source", "target"]).reset_index())

ho_df, wt_df, sc_df = (net_to_df(ho, "handover"),
                       net_to_df(wt, "working_together"),
                       net_to_df(subc, "subcontracting"))

# Broker detection: normalized betweenness on the handover graph.
import networkx as nx
G = nx.DiGraph()
for _, r in ho_df.iterrows():
    G.add_edge(r["source"], r["target"], weight=r["handover"])
btw = pd.Series(nx.betweenness_centrality(G, weight="weight", normalized=True))
# Expectation: R13_Mateo ranks top on betweenness while ranking low on
# every single-activity share  ->  bridging role, not an outlier.

# Rare-specialist detection: activity share x criticality, ignoring volume.
act_share = (log.groupby(["concept:name", "org:resource"]).size()
               .groupby(level=0).apply(lambda s: s / s.sum())
               .rename("activity_share").reset_index())
rare_spec = act_share.merge(med_cx.rename("median_cx"), on="org:resource")
rare_spec = rare_spec[(rare_spec.activity_share > 0.5) &
                      (rare_spec.median_cx > 0.7)]
# Expectation: R05_Erik on A6 despite a tiny absolute event count.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Substitution-aware profiles: exclude cover work from "core"
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
log["temporary_substitution"] = log["temporary_substitution"].fillna(False).astype(bool)

steady = log[~log["temporary_substitution"]]
subst  = log[log["temporary_substitution"]]

def profile(frame, tag):
    return (frame.groupby(["org:resource", "concept:name"]).size()
                 .rename(f"n_{tag}").reset_index())

prof = profile(steady, "steady").merge(profile(subst, "sub"),
                                       on=["org:resource", "concept:name"],
                                       how="outer").fillna(0)
prof["sub_ratio"] = prof["n_sub"] / (prof["n_sub"] + prof["n_steady"])
# sub_ratio near 1.0  -> capability observed ONLY under substitution.
# Never promote such an (activity, resource) pair to "core".
shadow_candidates = prof[(prof.sub_ratio < 0.5) & (prof.n_steady >= 20)]
# R07_Giorgio on A7/A8 should appear here: substantial STEADY-STATE
# pricing work, i.e. a standing shadow role rather than pure cover.

# Substitution quality: was cover as good as the incumbent?
svc = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")
sub_perf = (log.groupby(["org:resource", "concept:name",
                         "temporary_substitution"])
              .apply(lambda g: g["duration_s"].median()
                     if "duration_s" in g else np.nan))
# Expected: Giorgio/A7 median rises ~38min -> ~74min under substitution.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Join role output with context, and separate authority from volume
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
ctx = (log.groupby("org:resource")
          .agg(events=("concept:name", "size"),
               cases=("case:concept:name", "nunique"),
               median_cx=("case_complexity", "median"),
               p90_cx=("case_complexity", lambda s: s.quantile(0.90)),
               n_critical=("case_complexity", lambda s: (s >= 0.8).sum()),
               max_escalation=("escalation_level", "max"),
               n_substitution=("temporary_substitution", "sum"),
               n_segments=("customer_segment", "nunique"),
               n_product_lines=("product_line", "nunique"))
          .reset_index())

# Authority proxy: FINAL decision events, i.e. an approval with no
# subsequent approval on the same case. Counting A9 events alone would
# wrongly rank Mateo alongside Hana.
appr = log[log["concept:name"] == "A9 Supervisor Approval"].sort_values("time:timestamp")
appr["is_final"] = ~appr.duplicated(subset="case:concept:name", keep="last")
authority = (appr.groupby("org:resource")["is_final"]
                 .agg(final_approvals="sum", approval_events="size")
                 .reset_index())
authority["finality_rate"] = authority.final_approvals / authority.approval_events
# Expected: Hana ~1.0; Mateo materially below 1.0 (week-5 recheck effect).

enriched = (roles_df.merge(ctx, on="org:resource", how="left")
                    .merge(authority, on="org:resource", how="left")
                    .merge(btw.rename("betweenness"), left_on="org:resource",
                           right_index=True, how="left"))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------
# 7. Guardrails against over-interpreting frequency clusters
# ---------------------------------------------------------------
def flag_role(row):
    flags = []
    if row.get("betweenness", 0) > 0.20 and row["role_event_count"] < 0.1 * row["events"]:
        flags.append("BRIDGING: role membership understates network position")
    if row["median_cx"] > 0.70 and row["events"] < 250:
        flags.append("RARE-CRITICAL: low volume, high stakes - do not prune")
    if row["n_substitution"] > 0.4 * row["events"]:
        flags.append("SUBSTITUTION-DRIVEN: capability not proven in steady state")
    if pd.notna(row.get("finality_rate")) and row["finality_rate"] < 0.6:
        flags.append("PRELIMINARY AUTHORITY: approvals routinely rechecked")
    if row["n_product_lines"] <= 1 and row["median_cx"] < 0.25:
        flags.append("CAPPED SCOPE: buffer capacity, not full role competence")
    return "; ".join(flags) or "-"

enriched["interpretation_flags"] = enriched.apply(flag_role, axis=1)

# Complexity-stratified re-run: roles mined on the easy tail of the log
# are NOT the same roles that run the hard tail.
for lo, hi, tag in [(0.0, 0.4, "low"), (0.4, 0.7, "med"), (0.7, 1.01, "high")]:
    sub_log = log[(log.case_complexity >= lo) & (log.case_complexity < hi)]
    print(tag, pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(sub_log))
# Expected: temps and the bot vanish entirely from the "high" stratum,
# while Erik, Mateo, Hana and Chen dominate it - which is exactly the
# distinction a single global clustering pass destroys.

# Variant/segment sanity checks
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
sec_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, "product_line", ["Enterprise Security"], level="case", retain=True)
log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
```

**How the guardrails avoid the frequency trap.** Four moves do the work. (1) Never rank by raw counts — always pair a count with `median_cx` and `n_critical`, which is what keeps Erik and Fatima visible and keeps Nora and the temps from dominating. (2) Compute authority from *finality* of approvals rather than from approval counts, which is what separates Hana from Mateo. (3) Compute bridging from network betweenness rather than from the activity vector, which is what rescues Mateo from being an outlier. (4) Split steady-state from substitution events before defining core activities, which is what distinguishes Giorgio's standing shadow role from mere absence cover. The complexity-stratified re-run is the single cheapest check: if a resource's role membership changes across strata, that resource does not have one role.

# 6. Operational interpretation

**Staffing.** Treat the ten roles as three different kinds of capacity. *Elastic capacity* (Nora, Kai, Lina) scales with low-complexity volume and nothing else; adding temps buys throughput on cases below roughly 0.25 complexity and buys rework above it. *Substitutable capacity* (Amira/Ben, Chen/Dina, Ivan/Julia) can be planned as pools, but only within tier — Ben cannot simply replace Amira on complex work, and Dina cannot replace Chen on critical late-shift work. *Non-substitutable capacity* (Erik, Fatima, Hana, Mateo) must be planned as individuals with named backups. The most actionable staffing finding is that Fatima and Erik are each single points of failure whose formal headcount cost is trivial relative to the exposure. Give Fatima a second commercial resource, and note that one is already half-built in Giorgio.

**Training.** Three concrete programmes follow from the evidence. First, close the temp validation gap: the 17 reopens traced to incomplete A2 notes are a training and checklist problem, and fixing it converts temp capacity from partly-illusory to real. Second, build a security deputy — Chen already has 24 A6 events and the tenure to take it, and pairing him with Erik on critical reviews both develops the backup and forces knowledge out of Erik's head into A14. Third, close the pricing speed gap: Giorgio's 38→74 minute degradation is a specific, measurable training target, and reaching parity turns an informal shadow into a validated backup.

**Risk control.** Four items deserve attention. The A9 approval picture needs cleaning up: Mateo's, Fatima's and especially Giorgio's approval events should be explicitly typed as preliminary or final, and a negotiator approving his own exceptions is a segregation-of-duties question that should be answered rather than assumed benign. Mateo's security-referral filtering should be made an explicit, auditable rule with logged rejections — the two critical cases that waited over two days are the observed cost of an unmonitored filter. The quality function's independence should be protected: Ivan and Julia fixing what they check is convenient but weakens the control. And the automation boundary needs an exception path — Nora's precheck has no visible mechanism for flagging cases it cannot properly judge.

**Resilience.** Current resilience rests on informal behaviour: Giorgio covers commercial, Mateo covers coordination and partially covers approval, Chen covers the security-adjacent edge. This works and should not be dismantled, but it is untracked and therefore unmanaged. The correct response is to make each informal path explicit — name the backup, define the boundary of what they may decide, measure the performance gap under cover, and monitor it. Note also that shift coverage is thinner than headcount suggests: Chen, Ivan and Lina are the only late-shift resources, so a late-shift critical security or approval case has no on-shift authority at all.

**Cautions about changing staffing on mined roles alone.**

1. *Mined similarity is not interchangeability.* Chen and Dina sit at 0.94 and Ivan and Julia at 0.97, but only the second pair is genuinely symmetric in complexity. Concluding from cosine similarity that one member of a pair is redundant would remove the organization's deepest technical resource while the metric said nothing was lost. Always cross-check a similarity claim against complexity distribution, critical-case count, shift, and certification before acting.

2. *Demonstrated substitution is not proven competence.* Giorgio handled 31 pricing exceptions during Fatima's absence — and cycle time doubled. Mateo issued 21 approvals — and 62% needed rechecking. Reading either episode as "we already have a backup, so we don't need one" would formalize a degradation that was only tolerable because it lasted days. Substitution evidence establishes an upper bound on disruption, not an equivalence.

3. *Low volume is not low value.* Erik generates fewer events in ten weeks than a temp generates in a week. Any productivity-driven staffing review that ranks by event count will identify him as the least productive resource in the process, when he in fact carries the highest median complexity and is unbacked. Rare-but-critical roles must be evaluated on exposure avoided, not on throughput.

4. *Removing a shadow role removes the compensation without removing the underlying problem.* Giorgio's pricing work and Mateo's filtering exist because the formal structure is too slow or too coarse in specific places. Prohibiting them without fixing routing and referral criteria would simply relocate the delay into the queues those shadows were bypassing.

5. *Mined roles reflect ten weeks with two absences, a seasonal load spike and a security incident.* That window is not steady state. Anchor any permanent structural change to a longer baseline, and re-run the analysis on the substitution-free periods separately before committing headcount.
