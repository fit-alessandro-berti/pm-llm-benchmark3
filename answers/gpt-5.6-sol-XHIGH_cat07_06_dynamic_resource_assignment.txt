# 1. Objectives and constraints

## Objective set

The policy should treat assignment as a multi-objective decision rather than maximize a single score. It should:

1. Reduce expected SLA lateness, especially for urgent and high-priority cases.
2. Maintain end-to-end throughput by reducing both queueing time and avoidable service time.
3. Reduce error, rework, and appeal risk through appropriate skill matching.
4. Preserve case ownership where handovers are likely to create delay or loss of context.
5. Distribute projected effort fairly, considering remaining hours, complexity, supervision, and overtime risk—not merely open-case counts.
6. Create safe, documented development opportunities.
7. Avoid concentrating all work on a few specialists, preserving capacity for emergencies and absences.
8. Keep decisions understandable and open to human correction.

These objectives conflict. For example, retaining C003 protects context but leaves work with an overloaded resource; moving it protects workload fairness but creates a third reassignment. The policy therefore produces a defensible compromise, not a unique optimum.

## Safety and compliance constraints

These are applied before any preference score:

- Skill level 0 cannot perform the activity. A level-0 worker may only observe an approved development session; they cannot prepare, decide, sign, or finalize.
- Urgent and high-priority work requires skill level 2–3 unless a valid supervised arrangement is explicitly recorded.
- Security Review:
  - Level 3 may perform independently.
  - Level 1–2 may perform only with explicit supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo.
  - The supervisor must be available and accountable for review/sign-off.
- Final Approval:
  - The finalizer must have level 2–3.
  - Level 1 may prepare under supervision but cannot finalize.
- No resource receives more than four substantive commitments in the decision batch. Performing, supervising, coaching, and structured shadowing all consume capacity and count toward this limit.
- Quality Check may be batched only for normal/low-priority cases with more than 24 hours remaining.
- An assignment must fit forecast capacity. If predicted service and supervisory time cannot fit the available window, the option is ineligible.
- High-overtime-risk resources should receive no new work unless the case is urgent and no safer eligible option exists.

## Portfolio guardrails and preference trade-offs

- At least `ceil(20% × normal/low cases)` should have a documented development component when SLA and quality risk remain acceptable.
- For high-complexity, high-handover-cost cases, retain the current owner unless the owner has skill 0–1 and safe supervision cannot be arranged.
- A reassignment should occur only if its expected benefit exceeds the handover delay, context-loss risk, and reassignment-history penalty.
- Specialist capacity should be reserved for work that genuinely requires it. Routine cases should use competent resources when doing so is safe.
- A development assignment must include a learning objective, permitted task boundary, supervisor or coach where needed, and time allowance. Passive observation should not be counted unless it is structured and recorded.

For this queue, a provisional high-complexity threshold of 0.70 is used. That threshold should be governed, versioned, and audited rather than silently embedded in code.

# 2. Assignment policy

## Candidate generation and eligibility filtering

For each case, generate individual and, where allowed, supervised-pair options.

```text
INPUT:
    queue, skill matrix, workload snapshot, hours remaining,
    overtime risk, current ownership, historical duration and handover models

1. Freeze the decision snapshot and policy version.

2. Estimate for each case-option:
       queue delay
       service time
       supervision time
       handover delay
       predicted completion time
       probability of meeting SLA
       expected rework/error risk

3. Generate performer candidates:
       reject skill 0
       reject unavailable resources
       reject options exceeding four batch commitments
       reject options that do not fit forecast capacity

4. Apply activity rules:
       Security:
           independent performer must be level 3
           level 1–2 requires R7 or R8 as an available supervisor
       Final Approval:
           finalizer must be level 2–3
           level 1 can only be recorded in a preparation role
       High/urgent:
           level 1 requires explicit valid supervision

5. Apply workload guardrails:
       exclude high-overtime-risk resources unless urgent and no safer
       eligible alternative exists

6. Add optional development roles:
       only if the lead performer remains fully compliant
       level-0 observers perform no controlled activity
       count coaching/observation against capacity

7. Score the remaining options and allocate the batch collectively,
   preserving specialist capacity and the training-share constraint.

8. Reassign an owned case only when the score improvement exceeds a
   stability margin based on handover cost and reassignment history.
```

## SLA urgency and completion score

For option \(x\) on case \(c\):

- Priority weight: urgent `1.00`, high `0.75`, normal `0.40`, low `0.20`.
- Predicted slack:

\[
\text{slack}_{c,x}
= \text{SLA time remaining}
- \text{queue delay}_{x}
- \text{service time}_{c,x}
- \text{handover delay}_{c,x}
- \text{supervision wait}_{x}
\]

The SLA score combines priority, probability of on-time completion, and expected tardiness. Negative predicted slack receives a substantial penalty. Historical distributions should be used rather than a single average duration.

The known evidence is incorporated into the estimates:

- Add a handover-delay prior of about 2.7 hours for high-complexity owner changes.
- Add a weak-skill service-time and rework penalty when retaining an unsupported level-1 owner.
- Increase outcome-risk estimates after the third reassignment.

## Skill-match score

A possible normalized score is:

- Level 3: `1.00`
- Level 2: `0.75`
- Level 1 in a permitted, supervised arrangement: `0.35`
- Level 0: ineligible as performer

Complexity reduces the score for marginally qualified options. Specialist status should have diminishing value for routine work; otherwise every case will be routed to specialists and recreate the Security and Approval queues.

## Continuity and handover score

Continuity receives a positive score for:

- Retaining the current owner.
- Retaining the owner while adding a specialist supervisor.
- Avoiding a third or later reassignment.

Owner changes receive penalties based on:

- Handover band: none, low, medium, or high.
- Reassignment count.
- Complexity.
- Whether the case is in a loop.
- Estimated context-transfer time.

Continuity is not absolute. Keeping an owner with weak skill can be worse than a controlled handover, particularly when supervision is unavailable.

## Workload and overtime fairness score

Use projected effort rather than raw case count:

\[
\text{projected utilization}
=
\frac{\text{estimated remaining work}+\text{new work}+\text{supervision}}
{\text{hours available}}
\]

The score favors resources below the team’s weighted-utilization median and penalizes:

- High projected utilization.
- Four concurrent batch commitments.
- High overtime risk.
- Concentration of urgent work on one person.

High-overtime-risk candidates are normally filtered out, not merely given a small penalty.

## Training and development score

A development bonus is applied only when:

- The activity matches a documented training priority.
- The case is normal/low priority, or training creates no material delay.
- The task boundary is permitted at the trainee’s current level.
- A coach, supervisor, or accountable lead is named where needed.
- The expected SLA buffer remains acceptable.

Level-0 workers may receive structured observation but cannot be used to make an otherwise illegal assignment appear compliant.

## Resilience and bottleneck avoidance

The resilience score:

- Penalizes assigning routine work to a scarce specialist when a competent option is safe.
- Penalizes concentration of Security work on one specialist.
- Penalizes concentration of Approval work on R6.
- Rewards supervised development that creates future coverage.
- Reserves some specialist capacity for urgent arrivals.
- Uses working-together history to identify viable coaching pairs, but never treats historical collaboration as authorization.

## Combined decision score

After hard filtering, an illustrative score is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Score} ={}&
0.27(\text{SLA fit}) +
0.17(\text{skill match}) +
0.17(\text{continuity}) \\
&+0.14(\text{workload fairness}) +
0.10(\text{throughput}) +
0.08(\text{development}) +
0.07(\text{resilience})
\end{aligned}
\]

Weights should be governed with worker and operational input. For urgent cases, SLA and skill weights increase; for normal/low cases, training and resilience may increase.

## Tie-breaking and exceptions

Tie-break in this order:

1. Lower safety or SLA risk.
2. Current owner or lower handover cost.
3. Lower projected weighted utilization.
4. Lower use of scarce specialist capacity.
5. Fair rotation among equivalent resources.
6. Stable deterministic identifier for audit reproducibility.

If no compliant candidate exists:

- Do not make an unsafe automatic assignment.
- Escalate to a manager for capacity borrowing, deadline negotiation, or explicit supervised handling.
- Record the candidate set, rejected constraints, decision reason, and approver.

Assignments already in progress should normally be frozen. Re-scoring should occur on meaningful events—absence, SLA deterioration, completion, or major workload change—not continuously, because excessive re-optimization itself causes handovers.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

The raw first-available baseline is not deployable without a safety wrapper. For example, a static lowest-load choice could give C002 to R10, whose Approval level is 1 and who cannot finalize, or leave C001 with a level-1 Security performer without explicit supervision. For a fair operational comparison, all policies should be evaluated with the same compliance filters.

| Dimension | First-available routing | Specialist-only routing | Proposed dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected throughput | Fast initial dispatch and often low queue time, but weak matches can increase service time, rework, and later handovers | Fast service once a specialist starts, but specialist queues can reduce end-to-end throughput | Usually moderate-to-high: uses competent resources, batching, and forecasts while accepting some coaching overhead |
| SLA risk | May reduce average waiting but can endanger individual urgent cases through mismatch or illegal assignment | Low when specialist capacity is free; higher when Security or Approval queues form | Prioritizes predicted completion and reserves specialists for urgent or regulated work |
| Error/rework risk | Highest of the three because it ignores skill strength beyond skill 0 | Usually lowest immediate error rate | Low-to-moderate: specialists for high-risk work, competent staff for routine work, supervised stretches only |
| Fairness of workload | Superficially fair by case count, but ignores complexity, hours, supervision, and overtime | Poor; work accumulates on R3, R6, R7/R8, and Quality specialists | Uses weighted effort and avoids all three high-overtime-risk resources in this batch |
| Skill development | Accidental and potentially unsafe; not tied to learning plans | Very limited because work stays with existing experts | Explicit, supervised, and targeted to documented priorities |
| Continuity | Often poor because the least-loaded person may not be the owner | Often poor when the owner is competent but not the highest-skilled person | Retains 11 of the 15 existing owners and uses supervision rather than transfer for C001 |
| Resilience | Broad distribution may look resilient, but workers do not necessarily build valid capability | Low “bus factor”; absences among Security or Approval specialists create immediate bottlenecks | Protects specialist capacity and creates controlled exposure for future coverage |
| Coordination cost | Low at assignment time, higher later through reassignment and correction | Low per case, high at queues | Higher up front because some cases require coaching, explanation, and audit records |

For the current queue, specialist-only routing would likely:

- Concentrate Diagnosis on R3.
- Concentrate Approval on R6.
- Send most Security work to R8 because R8 has fewer open cases than R7.
- Move C016 away from its competent current owner despite high handover cost.
- Potentially exceed the four-assignment cap for R3.

First-available routing remains useful for homogeneous, low-risk work where all candidates are equally qualified. Specialist-only routing can be appropriate during a severe safety incident. The proposed policy is preferable for the mixed queue, but it incurs more coordination overhead and depends on reliable duration, workload, and skill data.

# 5. Explanation to workers

> Assignments are based on five visible considerations: customer deadline, required qualification, current case ownership, available capacity, and planned development.
>
> Urgent and regulated work is given only to people who are qualified or to an explicitly supervised pair. Where changing ownership would lose important case context, we try to keep the current owner and add help instead. That is why C001 stays with Celine but has Hugo’s Security supervision.
>
> Open-case counts are not treated as a performance ranking. We also consider complexity, remaining hours, urgent commitments, supervision time, and overtime risk. Bruno, David, and Iris receive no new work in this batch to protect their capacity; this is not a judgment about their performance.
>
> Training is placed on cases with enough time and a named coach or accountable lead. A trainee or observer is never expected to work outside their permission level, and coaching time is counted as real workload.
>
> We also avoid sending every suitable case to the same specialist. Specialists need capacity for difficult cases and unexpected arrivals, while competent colleagues should be able to retain appropriate work.
>
> Every assignment has a reason code, and workers can challenge incorrect information about skills, workload, deadlines, ownership, or availability. A manager remains accountable for overrides. Once work has started, it will not be moved merely because a slightly higher score appears.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Assignments should be logged as distinct decision events or otherwise have a unique decision identifier. Supervised work also needs structured roles such as performer, finalizer, supervisor, coach, and observer. If schema changes are impossible, these details should be encoded consistently in `assignment_reason`, though separate fields are preferable.

| Indicator | Definition and interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Handover frequency** | Percentage of consecutive case events where `org:resource` or recorded owner changes. Report by activity, priority, complexity, team, and `handover_cost_band`. Separate ownership transfers from supervision or collaboration. |
| **Reassignment count** | Mean, median, 90th percentile, and share of cases exceeding two reassignments. Compare rework and appeal rates for 0–2 versus more than 2. Validate the event-derived count against `reassignment_count`. |
| **Skill mismatch** | Join each assignment to the versioned skill matrix. Report skill-0 violations, high/urgent assignments below level 2 without supervision, invalid Security supervision, and Approval finalizers below level 2. Report supervised stretches separately from violations. |
| **Workload distribution** | Assignment count and projected weighted hours by resource/team; Gini coefficient, coefficient of variation, maximum-to-median ratio, and breaches of four commitments. Use `workload_snapshot`, complexity, and supervisory work rather than raw case count alone. |
| **Overtime risk exposure** | Share of assignments and projected hours given to high-overtime-risk resources. Every such assignment should have an urgent/no-safer-alternative reason and an override record if applicable. |
| **SLA misses** | Percentage completed after `sla_deadline`, total tardy hours, and on-time rate by priority, activity, policy, and assignment reason. Also measure calibration of predicted versus actual completion. |
| **Rework/appeal after assignment** | Share of cases with a later `Rework Correction` or `Appeal` event after an assignment, split by mismatch, handover, training status, and reassignment count. Control for complexity and priority; treat this as association, not automatic proof of individual causation. |
| **Training assignment share** | Normal/low decision IDs tagged with a valid development plan divided by all normal/low decision IDs. Target at least 20%. Track whether the planned supervision occurred and whether the learner later gained competence. |
| **Specialist bottleneck indicators** | Queue waiting time, service time, backlog age, specialist utilization, supervision demand, and workload concentration for Security and Approval. Include HHI or share handled by the top one/two resources and the waiting-to-service-time ratio. |
| **Continuity protection** | Owner-retention rate, high-complexity/high-cost owner-retention rate, and cases where an owner change occurred despite a feasible continuity option. |
| **Batching compliance** | For each detected Quality batch, verify normal/low priority and more than 24 hours of SLA time at batch formation. Measure waiting introduced versus service-time savings. |
| **Deviations from policy** | Reconstruct the eligible set from the frozen snapshot. Count hard-rule violations, four-assignment breaches, invalid overtime exceptions, and selected options outside the permitted score/stability range. Separate explained human overrides from unexplained deviations. |
| **Assignment stability** | Number of re-optimizations and reassignment events caused by the policy itself. A dynamic policy that repeatedly changes its mind is not operationally successful. |

Audit results should be used for process improvement, not as simplistic worker league tables. Role, network, and performance indicators can reflect historical opportunity and staffing patterns; they are not pure measures of individual ability.

def gini(values):
    x = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    x = x[np.isfinite(x)]
    if len(x) == 0 or np.sum(x) == 0:
        return 0.0
    x = np.sort(np.maximum(x, 0))
    n = len(x)
    return (
        2 * np.sum((np.arange(1, n + 1)) * x) / (n * np.sum(x))
        - (n + 1) / n
    )
