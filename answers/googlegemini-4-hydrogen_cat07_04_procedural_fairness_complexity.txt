# 1. Fairness dimensions

Assessing procedural fairness in a public-benefits process requires looking beyond raw approval disparities to evaluate how applicants are treated, routed, supported, and burdened throughout the case lifecycle:

- **Outcome parity (raw approval differences):** Approval rates vary substantially—78% for Group A, 69% for Group C, 63% for Group B, and 58% for Group D. Raw parity alone cannot establish discrimination because groups differ markedly in measured risk scores (51% high in Group D vs. 18% in Group A), incomplete documentation (46% in Group B vs. 21% in Group A), and support needs.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** After adjusting for `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and `month`, approval gaps shrink substantially but do not vanish: Group B remains 5.5 percentage points (pp) lower, Group D 3.1 pp lower, and Group C 2.2 pp lower than Group A. Residual gaps could reflect unmeasured case complexity, biased covariates, or unequal treatment.
- **Procedural burden:** Even when cases end in approval, applicants may face extra friction—more document requests, supervisor escalations, handovers, and rework. Adjusted probabilities of `Request Missing Documents` (+9.5 pp for Group B, +4.2 pp for Group C) and `Supervisor Review` (+8.0 pp for Group B, +5.5 pp for Group C) show substantial extra procedural hurdles not explained by documented covariates. Reopen/rework rates are roughly double Group A's (11%) for Groups B (22%), C (24%), and D (20%).
- **Timeliness:** Median cycle times are 5.6 days longer for Group B (13.7d) and 7.8 days longer for Group C (15.9d) than Group A (8.1d), with 90th-percentile durations reaching 34.2d and 38.8d respectively. Adjusted cycle-time penalties remain large for Group C (+4.1d) and Group B (+2.6d), whereas Group D’s adjusted delay is modest (+0.9d). In public benefits, multi-week delays impose material hardship regardless of final eligibility.
- **Consistency of routing:** Legitimate routing rules send high-risk cases to `Fraud Review` and rights-preserving requests to `Language Support Contact` or `Accommodation Review`. Procedural fairness requires that similar cases follow equivalent paths and that protective steps do not trigger unmotivated downstream escalations (e.g., `Supervisor Review` or repeated completeness loops).
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** Support steps exist to guarantee equal access, yet Group B experiences a 3.8-day wait after `Language Support Contact` and Group C a 4.7-day wait after `Accommodation Review`. Moreover, `case_outcome` does not measure whether communications were understandable, culturally/linguistically accessible, or respectful.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Group D has a 16% raw appeal rate and a +5.7 pp adjusted appeal disparity; Group B has a 13% raw rate and +4.4 pp adjusted disparity. High appeal and reopen rates indicate either noisy/error-prone initial decisions, inadequate explanations, or adversarial friction.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Group A cases are distributed across 52 general caseworkers, whereas Group B relies on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and Group C relies on 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom also perform supervisor reviews). Structural under-staffing of specialized queues concentrates delays on protected or disadvantaged groups.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To separate legitimate complexity-driven routing from unwarranted procedural disparity, an observational process-mining and statistical design should combine covariate adjustment with path- and resource-level decomposition:

- **Matching or stratification variables:**
  - Exact stratification or propensity-score/coarsened-exact matching on `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and `month`.
  - Crucially, run *two* specifications: (a) conditioning on `language_preference` and `disability_accommodation_requested` to test whether groups differ *within* the same mandated support tier, and (b) excluding or decomposing those steps to measure the total procedural penalty borne by applicants who legitimately need them.
- **Path and variant comparison:**
  - Compare variant distributions (e.g., V1–V6) and transition probabilities within matched strata. Specifically, test whether applicants with identical `documentation_completeness` and `risk_score_band` enter `Request Missing Documents` or `Supervisor Review` at different rates across groups, and whether `Accommodation Review` or `Language Support Contact` mechanically trigger `Supervisor Review` (as seen in V5) without policy justification.
- **Resource-assignment comparison:**
  - Compare caseworker pools, queue sizes, handover counts, and bounce-back loops (`general -> specialist -> general`) across groups within the same `region` and `channel`. Measure whether disparities vanish when controlling for assigned resource or region—distinguishing individual bias from capacity bottlenecks.
- **Waiting-time decomposition:**
  - Partition total cycle time into *service time* (active processing) and *waiting time* (queue delay) across every edge `(a -> b)`. Separate unavoidable waiting in mandated support steps from downstream delays on shared steps (e.g., `Completeness Check -> Request Missing Documents` or `Supervisor Review -> Decision Draft`).
- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates:**
  - Because `risk_score_band` may encode historical bias and `documentation_completeness` may reflect inaccessible forms or language barriers, treat them as potentially post-treatment or noisy proxies rather than ground truth. Conduct tipping-point / omitted-variable sensitivity analyses (e.g., Rosenbaum bounds or Cinelli-Hazlett sensitivity) and re-estimate disparities using subsets where risk scores are low/medium or where initial submissions were verified in person.
- **Subgroup checks by region and channel:**
  - Stratify all analyses by `region` (especially the two regions with supervisor backlogs and extra handovers) and `channel` (online vs. in-person, where Group B is 38% in-person vs. 9% for Group A). If disparities concentrate in specific regions or in-person queues, interventions can target local capacity and workflow rather than assuming system-wide bias.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Multiple mechanisms—both benign/structural and concerning—could produce the observed patterns:

1. **Structural capacity under-investment in specialized queues (concerning institutional design):** Bilingual staff (7 caseworkers) and accommodation specialists (5 staff) are too few for demand, and service-level targets ignore those steps. Even without individual caseworker bias, queuing dynamics create multi-day waits (3.8d–4.7d), regional backlogs, and +2.6d to +4.1d adjusted cycle-time penalties for Groups B and C.
2. **Ping-pong handovers and role-coupling friction (concerning workflow design):** Routing Group B cases back and forth between general and bilingual workers increases context loss, duplicated completeness checks, and `Request Missing Documents` loops (+9.5 pp adjusted). Similarly, having 2 of 5 accommodation specialists double as supervisors may conflate accommodation review with supervisory sign-off, inflating Group C’s `Supervisor Review` rate (+5.5 pp adjusted) and rework (24%).
3. **Communication barriers disguised as "incomplete documentation" (measurement & accessibility mechanism):** If notices and intake forms are poorly translated or inaccessible, minority-language applicants (Group B) may submit technically incomplete files or fail to respond accurately to document requests. This depresses approvals (-5.5 pp adjusted), increases appeals (+4.4 pp adjusted), and inflates rework (22%), even within the same recorded `documentation_completeness` band.
4. **Legitimate unmeasured complexity and specialized verification (benign/legitimate mechanism):** Coarse covariates (`risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`) may mask genuine within-band heterogeneity—for instance, multi-jurisdiction income records or complex medical-accommodation documentation that legitimately requires extra review time or supervisor sign-off, accounting for part of the residual cycle-time and approval gaps.
5. **Biased risk scoring or inconsistent discretion (concerning decision/algorithmic mechanism):** For Group D, higher `prior_case_count` (49% high) and `risk_score_band` (51% high) drive V3 fraud routing. If prior rejections or appeals feed back into higher risk scores, applicants enter a self-reinforcing loop of scrutiny, leading to contested denials and high appeals (+5.7 pp adjusted, 16% raw). Caseworker discretion in borderline `Eligibility Assessment` or `Supervisor Review` could also disproportionately disfavor Groups B and D.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The event log alone cannot definitively prove or rule out unlawful discrimination due to critical data gaps:

- **Measurement bias in `documentation_completeness`:** Recorded completeness is an institutional judgment made *within* the process, not an objective exogenous trait. If intake staff misclassify foreign/non-standard documents or if applicants cannot understand instructions due to language barriers, adjusting for `documentation_completeness` controls away part of the accessibility barrier ("controlling for a mediator").
- **Historical bias and endogeneity in `risk_score_band`:** `risk_score_band` may encode past enforcement disparities, geographic correlations, or prior administrative errors (`prior_case_count`). Treating it as a neutral control risks laundering historical inequity into "legitimate risk routing."
- **Missing applicant needs and complexity granularity:** Coarse bands (`household_size_band`, binary `disability_accommodation_requested`, `language_preference`) omit severity of disability, type of accommodation needed (e.g., ASL interpreter vs. extended deadline), literacy, digital access, and true legal eligibility under statutory rules. Without ground-truth eligibility audits, residual approval gaps (-2.2 pp to -5.5 pp) cannot be conclusively attributed to bias vs. unobserved eligibility differences.
- **Unobserved resource workload and capacity constraints:** Workload is observed only at daily granularity. We lack exact queue depths, concurrent caseload complexity, leave schedules, and shift assignments, making it hard to separate caseworker-level behavior from instantaneous congestion—especially for the 3 overloaded regional officers and 5 accommodation specialists.
- **Missing appeal outcomes and decision-quality metrics:** The log records `appeal_flag` and `Appeal Review`, but lacks *appeal reversal rates* (whether initial denials were overturned), quality-assurance scores, clarity/translation quality of `Applicant Notification`, and applicant-reported dignity or comprehension. A high appeal rate with high reversal rates strongly implies initial decision error; high appeal rates with low reversals may reflect poor explanation of valid denials.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Any intervention must improve timeliness, consistency, and support quality *without* removing or weakening rights-preserving steps:

- **Add dedicated capacity for language support and accommodation review:**
  - *Action:* Hire or cross-train additional bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; separate accommodation specialist duties from supervisor-review duties.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Higher staffing and training costs; rapid onboarding without quality assurance could reduce consistency of support; drawing experienced staff from general pools could temporarily lengthen Group A/D queues.
- **Establish explicit service-level targets (SLAs) for support steps:**
  - *Action:* Introduce separate timeliness thresholds for `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` and track p50/p90 waits by group and region.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Goodhart’s Law risks—staff under time pressure might rush complex accommodations, rubber-stamp reviews, or shift delays onto adjacent unmonitored steps (e.g., delaying `Eligibility Assessment` or prematurely issuing `Request Missing Documents` to "stop the SLA clock").
- **Redesign routing to reduce handovers (end-to-end case ownership):**
  - *Action:* Assign Group B cases directly to bilingual caseworkers from intake through decision where possible, eliminating `general -> bilingual -> general` handovers; decouple `Accommodation Review` from mandatory `Supervisor Review` unless objective risk/complexity criteria apply.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Bilingual or specialist staff may become siloed and lose peer calibration with general teams; if general caseworkers lose exposure to diverse cases, organizational capability narrows; concentrating specialized cases could create single-point-of-failure bottlenecks during staff absences.
- **Improve decision explanations and accessible notifications:**
  - *Action:* Provide plain-language, translated, and disability-accessible `Applicant Notification` templates detailing exact reasons for missing-document requests, denials, and appeal rights; add a pre-denial clarification call for borderline cases.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Increases active service time per case (`Decision Draft` / `Applicant Notification`) and may initially increase workload; clearer appeal instructions could temporarily raise appeal volume even as erroneous denials drop.
- **Monitor adjusted procedural disparities continuously:**
  - *Action:* Build recurring dashboards tracking adjusted approval gaps, cycle-time decomposition, handover counts, rework rates, and appeal reversal rates by group, region, and channel.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Risk of overfitting metrics or encouraging defensive case-handling (e.g., approving ineligible cases or avoiding necessary documentation checks purely to equalize dashboard numbers).
- **Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` criteria:**
  - *Action:* Conduct disparate-impact and calibration audits on the risk model (checking whether `prior_case_count` unfairly penalizes prior appeals/system errors) and blind quality audits on completeness determinations across languages and channels.
  - *Side effects / trade-offs:* Recalibrating risk thresholds may temporarily increase fraud-review false negatives or require manual QA sampling costs; changing completeness standards requires retraining staff and updating IT systems.
