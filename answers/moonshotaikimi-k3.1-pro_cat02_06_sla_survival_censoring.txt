# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin and observation.** The time origin is `Submit`, with \(t=0\), and the SLA horizon is \(H=72\) elapsed hours. Let:

- \(T_R\): time of `Resolve`;
- \(T_W,T_J,T_T\): times of `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`;
- \(C\): age at the common observation cutoff for an `open` case;
- \(D\): first internal disposition among `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, and `Reject`.

For a terminal row, `duration_or_age_h` is the relevant event time. For an `open` row, it is a right-censoring time, not an event time. Intermediate activities have an ordering but no timestamps.

**Assumptions needed for estimation, not verified facts:**

1. **A1—complete internal ascertainment:** until the recorded terminal event, transfer, or cutoff, the internal system would have recorded any internal terminal outcome.
2. **A2—baseline cohort:** the 32 rows represent the intended target cohort from `Submit`, without unobserved left truncation or selective inclusion.
3. **A3—elapsed SLA clock:** there are no pauses or business-hour adjustments.
4. **A4—primary censoring assumption:** administrative censoring of open cases is independent of future outcomes, possibly conditional on submission time, cohort, priority, and observed history. This is questionable enough to require sensitivity analysis.
5. **A5—baseline priority:** priority is known at `Submit` if used as a baseline covariate. If it can change later, it must instead be time-varying.

The word *resolved* must also be scoped. **Internal resolution** means an observed internal `Resolve`. **All-location resolution** additionally allows resolution after transfer, which is not observed here.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use competing-risk codes **0 = censored, R = Resolve, W = Withdraw, J = Reject, T = Transfer**. For the primary internal analysis, the event time is the first of these endpoints, with open cases administratively censored.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | R event at \(u=\)`duration_or_age_h`. | R is also a disposition and ends active backlog. | Code R at \(u\); no censoring. | If \(u\leq72\), resolved by the SLA. | `open` | No R event. | No disposition or active-backlog event. | Code 0 and right-censor at age \(u\). | If \(u>72\), unresolved and active at 72; censoring occurs after the horizon. **Three explicit transfer scenarios**

1. **T1—transfer is a competing internal route, primary for internal resolution.**  
   Internal follow-up ends because the case has left the internal process; subsequent internal `Resolve` is not possible. S08 and S21 therefore count as not internally resolved by 72. A fourth, policy-based convention—**any handoff automatically fails the internal service standard**—may be useful for governance, but it is a definition of accountability rather than new statistical evidence.

**Important distinctions:**

- **Right censoring:** open cases have no observed terminal event by their cutoff age.
- **Competing events:** withdrawal, rejection, and, under T1, transfer change which internal outcomes can subsequently occur.
- **Already-observed breaches:** S03, S13, S20, and S28 resolved after 72; S09, S11, S25, and S26 were still open after 72; S32 was still unresolved at 72 and transferred later.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Descriptive observations, not valid SLA estimates:**

- There are 13 observed resolved cases. Their durations have median **41 hours**, and four—S03, S13, S20, and S28—resolved after 72 hours.
- Thus the resolved-only late fraction is \(4/13=30.8\%\). It is not the probability that a submitted case fails the resolution SLA.
- A resolved-only analysis excludes **19 cases**: nine open, seven withdrawn/rejected, and three transferred.

**Survivor/completion bias.** Conditioning on observed resolution conditions on part of the future. Fast resolutions such as S16 at 16 hours and S18 at 12 hours are certain to appear in the resolved sample. Long unresolved cases such as S09 at 92 hours and S26 at 104 hours are excluded, while S13 is included only because it happened to resolve at 110 hours. At a fixed cutoff, the resolved sample therefore overrepresents cases whose resolution happened soon enough to be observed.

**Competing risks are not ordinary censoring.** S05 was rejected at 9 hours, S12 withdrawn at 15, S06 withdrawn at 27, and S23 rejected at 11. Dropping them or censoring them and interpreting \(1-S_{KM}\) as real-world resolution probability removes cases that actually failed to reach `Resolve`. Conversely, for an active-backlog estimand, treating these cases as still open would overstate workload.

**Open cases below and above 72 hours require different treatment.**

- Below 72 and not yet breached at cutoff: **S10 (47), S15 (35), S24 (67), S29 (51), and S31 (23)**. Their 72-hour outcomes are not yet observed. Calling all of them breaches is wrong; dropping them can also bias survival estimates.
- Above 72 and already known to have breached: **S09 (92), S11 (81), S25 (79), and S26 (104)**. They contribute failure information at 72, but their later resolution times remain censored. They must not be treated as permanently unresolved.

**Potentially informative observation.** The gaps are substantial for several post-72 open cases:

- S11: \(81-55=26\) hours since `Submit` at its last recorded activity;
- S25: last activity at \(79-48=31\) hours;
- S26: last activity at \(104-72=32\) hours;
- S09: last activity at \(92-31=61\) hours.

These calculations identify stale cases for investigation; they do not establish that resolution has or has not occurred. If stale cases have a different post-cutoff resolution hazard, ordinary censoring assumptions can distort the late-resolution tail.

Transfers present a stronger observation problem: all transferred cases—S08, S21, and S32—are P1 and escalated. Transfer is therefore associated with observed process history and priority, and may also be associated with unobserved complexity.

**Cohort imbalance and policy confounding.** Cohort A has 15 cases and B has 17; B contains five P1 cases versus three in A. B also has two transfers compared with one in A. Cohort was not randomized, and calendar time, priority, case mix, and incomplete rollout compliance can all affect a raw A–B comparison. Prefixes do not supply a clean compliance instrument: some B cases, such as S20, S23, S26, and S30, have no `AutoCheck`, while A also contains `AutoCheck` paths such as S01 and S15. Actual compliance cannot be inferred reliably from cohort or prefix alone.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

For each case, construct a first-endpoint dataset \((Y_i,\varepsilon_i)\), where \(Y_i\) is the terminal event time or open-case censoring age. Open cases remain in the data. At each event time, the risk set includes every case whose event/censoring time is at least that value.

For cause \(k\), the Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence is

\[
\widehat F_k(t)=\sum_{t_j\leq t}
\widehat S(t_j-)\frac{d_{kj}}{n_j},
\]

where \(n_j\) is the number at risk, \(d_{kj}\) the number of cause-\(k\) events, and \(\widehat S\) survival free of every first internal endpoint. Administrative censoring reduces later risk sets but does not create an event.

**Primary recommendation: Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence under T1.**

Report, overall and descriptively by cohort and priority:

- \(F_R(72)\) and \(q_{72}=1-F_R(72)\);
- \(F_W(72),F_J(72),F_T(72)\);
- active internal probability \(a_{72}=\widehat S(72)\);
- late-resolution increments \(F_R(\tau)-F_R(72)\) only at horizons with adequate risk-set support.

This directly matches the operational resolution estimand while retaining competing dispositions. It does not treat withdrawal, rejection, or transfer as generic administrative censoring.

The tail is sparse. Under the primary encoding, only **nine cases** are in the 72-hour risk set: S03, S09, S11, S13, S20, S25, S26, S28, and S32. After 72 hours, four have observed late resolutions, S32 transfers, and four open cases are censored. By 110 hours, the risk set has shrunk to S13 alone. Consequently, late-tail percentages and cohort/priority-stratified curves should be presented with risk-set counts and strong uncertainty warnings, not as precise performance rankings.

**Comparison of candidate methods**

| Method | Appropriate role here | Why it is not sufficient by itself |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan–Meier** | Suitable for the single composite endpoint “any first internal endpoint,” yielding active-internal survival. Also useful as a clearly labelled net-resolution sensitivity. | Censoring W/J/T and interpreting \(1-KM\) as actual resolution probability estimates a hypothetical quantity. KM alone does not provide competing cumulative incidences. |
| **Aalen–Johansen/CIF** | Best primary match for absolute resolution, non-resolution, transfer, and active-state probabilities. | Still depends on valid censoring of open cases and on the chosen transfer interpretation. It does not make cohort comparisons causal. |
| **Cause-specific hazards** | Useful secondary mechanism analysis: instantaneous resolution or transfer rates among cases still active and observed. With more data, stage and escalation can be time-varying. | A resolution hazard does not itself give resolution CIF; all cause-specific hazards are needed. With 13 resolutions and very few W/J/T events, multivariable models here would be unstable. |
| **Subdistribution hazards/Fine–Gray** | Potential future method when the goal is a direct covariate association with resolution CIF or late-resolution CIF. | Not needed for the primary descriptive estimand, poorly supported by 32 cases, and not a causal remedy for nonrandom policy assignment. Time-varying process features also require careful landmark or time-dependent design. |
| **Restricted mean time** | Useful secondary summary through 72 hours. A customer-centered resolution-delay burden is \(\int_0^{72}[1-F_R(u)]\,du\); an internal workload measure is \(\int_0^{72}S(u)\,du\). | These are different quantities: withdrawal can increase the first while reducing the second. Restriction avoids extrapolated mean lifetimes but does not repair informative censoring. |
| **Landmarking** | Preferred future design for dynamic alerts at 24 or 48 hours, using only history available at the landmark. At 72 hours, predict later recovery rather than “risk of breach,” which is already known. | Cannot be constructed from the current prefixes because intermediate activity times are unavailable. |
| **Multi-state model** | Best long-term process-aware representation for stage-specific hazards, repeated review, escalation, transfer, and state occupancy. | Too sparse for a richly parameterized fitted model here. Current data support transition counts and state definitions, not reliable transition-specific duration models. |

**Secondary analyses actually justified for this extract** are:

1. late-resolution CIF with explicit risk-set truncation;
2. the two restricted-mean summaries above, computed from the primary AJ curve;
3. cohort- and priority-stratified descriptive estimates, without multivariable significance testing.

Cause-specific, subdistribution, landmark, and multi-state regression should be treated as prospective designs once timestamps and more observations are available.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

| Feature | Derivation | Timing classification | Safe interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Priority | Supplied `priority` | Baseline only under A5 | Stratification or adjustment; if reassigned later, use its value at each landmark. |
| Cohort/policy era | Supplied `cohort` | Baseline calendar/policy proxy | Describes an era association, not a randomized treatment effect. |
| Current stage | Last activity observed by prediction time | Time-varying | Appropriate for current-state hazards and dynamic alerts. For open cases, the last prefix activity is the stage at cutoff. |
| Review visits so far | Count of `Review` activities observed by time \(t\) | Time-varying | Captures repeated review without using future reviews. |
| Repeat-review indicator | At least two reviews so far | Time-varying | S03 and S13 illustrate why the full-path version cannot be known at `Submit`. |
| Rework so far / rework loop | Rework count so far; observed `Review>Rework>Review` pattern | Time-varying | S07 and S13 contain a return-to-review loop; S27 instead has `Review>Rework>Resolve`. These should not be collapsed without considering the operational distinction. |
| Escalation so far | Indicator after an observed `Escalate` | Time-varying | Use escalation timestamp in a time-dependent model. |
| Escalation timing | Time from `Submit` or stage entry to `Escalate` | Time-varying/landmark | Proposed only; exact timing is absent and must not be imputed from sequence position. |
| Screening path | `AutoCheck` before/after `Screen`, screening activities completed so far | Time-varying or landmark | Possible policy/process marker, not verified rollout compliance and not automatically baseline. |
| Path length and variant-so-far | Activities and exact variant observed by a landmark | Time-varying | Useful for descriptive process comparison. Full variants are too sparse to be separate regression strata here. |
| Inactivity gap | \(g(t)=t-\max\{\text{activity time}\leq t\}\) | Time-varying | Useful for stage hazards and stale-case review. It may also predict observation problems. |
| Full `escalated`, full `rework_count`, full `prefix` | Computed using the entire observed case | Post-baseline; often post-outcome | Not safe as baseline predictors. A full prefix can include terminal activities and possibly activities after 72 hours. |
| Supplied `last_event_gap_h` | Measured at cutoff for open rows and zero for terminal rows | Cutoff-dependent and leakage-prone | Useful for reconciling currently open cases. It is not comparable as a baseline predictor across open and terminal rows; zero on terminal rows can reveal terminal status. |

Treating `escalated=yes` as a baseline covariate creates **immortal-time and selection problems**: an escalated case must remain unresolved and observed long enough to reach escalation, while the comparison group contains early resolutions and dispositions. Because resolution is the event of interest, the direction of bias should not be assumed. Rework and full-path features have analogous problems. Proper alternatives are time-dependent indicators, multi-state transitions, or landmarks with prefixes truncated to information available at that time.

**Proposed state-transition diagram in text:**

- `Submit  Screening`, where Screening contains `Screen` and/or `AutoCheck` in their observed orders.
- `Screening  Review`, `Escalation`, `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, or `Reject`.
- `Review  Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Rework`, or `Escalation`.
- `Rework  Review` or `Resolve`.
- `Escalation  Review`, `Resolve`, or `Transfer`.
- Any active internal state may lead to administrative censoring at cutoff; **open is not an absorbing process state**.
- `Transfer  External/unobserved`, with possible external resolution or other outcomes currently unknown.

These edges describe observed transitions, not proof that other transitions are impossible. A future semi-Markov multi-state model should use both time since `Submit`, for the SLA clock, and time since entry into the current state, for waiting and inactivity hazards.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

| Analysis | Transfer and W/J treatment | Censoring/cohort treatment | What it tests |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S0: primary internal AJ** | T competing; W and J separate competing causes. | Open cases administratively censored; unadjusted overall analysis plus descriptive cohort/priority strata. | Internal resolution SLA, active backlog, and cause composition without dropping open cases. |
| **S1: independent transfer censoring** | Censor at transfer; W/J remain competing. | Compare with S0; history-dependent IPCW could be considered only with much more data. | Whether absolute resolution and late-tail estimates depend materially on treating handoff as an internal endpoint versus independent loss of observation. |
| **S2: all-location transfer bounds/pattern mixture** | Pre-72 transfers range from external resolution by 72 to no resolution by 72; S32 remains a 72-hour failure. | Early open cases also require bounds or an explicit censoring model. | The accountability question that includes external resolution. No single post-transfer CIF should be asserted without external data. |
| **S3: resolution versus disposition governance** | Primary: W/J are non-resolution but disposition events. Alternative estimand: any R/W/J disposition is timely closure. | Keep the same censoring rules so that only the estimand changes. | Whether a policy appears beneficial merely because it closes cases through withdrawal/rejection or removes them through transfer. |
| **S4: informative open-case observation** | Retain S0 endpoint definitions. | Compare administrative censoring with elicited post-censoring hazard multipliers, best/worst late-outcome scenarios, or measured-history IPCW. Stale cases such as S11, S25, and S26 receive particular attention. | Whether late-resolution conclusions depend on assuming that stale open cases resemble continuously active cases. IPCW addresses measured censoring predictors, not unmeasured informativeness. |
| **S5: cohort-policy analysis** | Use the same endpoint definitions across cohorts. | Compare raw cohort estimates with priority-stratified or directly standardized estimates; separately describe actual compliance if it becomes available. | Whether an apparent policy-era difference is explained by priority/case mix or changes under alternative compliance definitions. None of these alone establishes causation. |

**Assumption-light bounds, not confidence intervals.** Under complete internal ascertainment:

- Nine cases definitely resolved by 72.
- Sixteen definitely did not resolve by 72: four late resolutions, seven withdrawals/rejections, four open cases older than 72, and S32.
- Seven are ambiguous for **all-location** resolution: five open cases censored before 72 plus S08 and S21.

Thus the extract alone bounds all-location non-resolution at 72 between \(16/32=50.0\%\) and \(23/32=71.9\%\), allowing every ambiguous case to move from success to failure. For **internal** resolution, S08 and S21 are known non-resolutions, leaving only five early open cases ambiguous; the corresponding bounds are \(18/32=56.3\%\) to \(23/32=71.9\%\). AJ estimates use additional censoring assumptions to smooth within these bounds.

**Qualitative conclusions that could reverse:**

- An apparent A–B or priority ranking could reverse between T1, T2, and external-outcome scenarios, particularly because all transfers are P1 and B contains the very early transfer S21.
- A policy could appear to reduce active backlog or time to disposition while worsening resolution if it increases rejection, withdrawal, or transfer.
- Late-resolution rankings can change under different assumptions for S09, S11, S25, and S26, although their known 72-hour breach status does not change.
- A naive association suggesting that escalation “causes” delay could weaken or reverse after using escalation timing, baseline complexity, and priority correctly; conversely, a high escalation hazard need not imply a high eventual resolution CIF.
- Any apparent benefit of the B policy era could disappear or reverse after priority standardization, calendar adjustment, or valid compliance classification.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Alerts and recovery actions**

- Before 72 hours, use landmark predictions such as \(P(\text{no Resolve by 72}\mid\text{active at }s,\mathcal H(s))\), for example at \(s=24\) or 48 hours. Thresholds should reflect intervention capacity, alert lead time, and the cost of missed P1 cases.
- Do not alert every open case identically. S24 at 67 hours is close to the horizon but not yet breached; S31 at 23 hours has much more time. Both require conditional risk assessment rather than classification from current age alone.
- At or after 72 hours, breach status is observed, not predicted. S09, S11, S25, and S26 belong in recovery/reconciliation queues. S26’s 72-hour inactivity gap additionally warrants a data-quality and ownership check.
- Convert case-level probabilities into expected workload by summing predicted probabilities of remaining active, entering review, or requiring specialist follow-up over specified future windows. Keep transferred external workload separate from the internal active queue.
- Use stage-specific outputs for action selection: repeated review may indicate reviewer capacity needs, while long escalation-stage waits may indicate specialist queue problems. These are operational hypotheses, not demonstrated causal effects.

A high risk score associated with escalation does **not** imply that avoiding escalation will improve outcomes. Evaluating an alert-triggered action requires a separate intervention design, such as a randomized rollout or a defensible quasi-experimental analysis.

**SLA governance**

Report a small dashboard rather than one ambiguous “breach probability”:

1. all-case internal resolution failure by 72;
2. all-location resolution failure, with transfer-outcome missingness or bounds;
3. active internal backlog at 72;
4. withdrawal, rejection, and transfer cumulative incidences;
5. late-resolution incidence and restricted resolution-delay burden;
6. risk-set sizes, early censoring, stale-case frequency, and external follow-up completeness.

Reporting disposition speed alone creates an incentive to withdraw, reject, or transfer difficult cases to improve the metric.

**Calibration and fairness**

- Validate prospectively by submission period, not by randomly mixing future and past cases.
- At 72 hours, assess calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope or reliability plots when sample size permits, and time-dependent Brier score using appropriate censoring weights. Calibration of all-location resolution cannot be completed without external outcomes.
- Evaluate late-resolution and active-backlog predictions separately; good calibration for one does not imply calibration for the other.
- Stratify calibration, false-negative rates, alert rates, and workload allocation by priority and cohort. In particular, check whether sparse P1 tail cases are systematically under-alerted.
- Priority and cohort are operational strata, not a complete fairness assessment. Protected-group fairness would require additional appropriate demographic and access data.
- With only 32 cases, these should be validation plans rather than fitted claims. Pooling over future validation windows and preserving temporal separation will be more credible than complex internal cross-validation.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

The following code uses pandas and implements Aalen–Johansen directly, so no survival library is required; this is also the fallback if packages such as `lifelines` are unavailable. PM4Py is used only for sequence-based control-flow variants. No intermediate timestamps are fabricated.

```python

MAX_OBSERVED_TIME = 110.0

# Three transfer scenarios:
#   competing:       transfer is an observed competing internal endpoint.
#   censor:          transfer is right censoring for subsequent resolution.
#   external_bounds: preserve internal T history, but do not assign any external
#                    resolution time; a separate all-location bound is computed.
TRANSFER_SCENARIOS = ("competing", "censor", "external_bounds")

def make_aj_ready(data, transfer_scenario):
    if transfer_scenario not in TRANSFER_SCENARIOS:
        raise ValueError("Unknown transfer scenario")

    x = data.copy()
    x["time"] = x["duration_or_age_h"]
    causes = []

    for r in x.itertuples(index=False):
        status = r.observed_status
        if status == "open":
            cause = "0"
        elif status == "resolved":
            cause = "R"

            cause = "W"
        elif status == "rejected":

        elif status == "transferred":

            else:

                cause = "T"
        else:

        (x["cause"] == "R") & (x["time"] > H)
    ).astype(int)
    x["disposition_event"] = x["cause"].isin(["R", "W", "J"]).astype(int)
    x["any_internal_endpoint"] = (x["cause"] != "0").astype(int)

# Direct Aalen-Johansen implementation. Censoring affects later risk sets.
def aalen_johansen(x):
    cause_names = ["R", "W", "J", "T"]
    survival = 1.0
    cif = {k: 0.0 for k in cause_names}

    initial = {
        "time": 0.0,
        "n_risk": len(x),
        "n_endpoint_events": 0,
        "S_any": 1.0
    }
    initial.update({"CIF_" + k: 0.0 for k in cause_names})
    curve_rows = [initial]

    event_times = sorted(x.loc[x["cause"] != "0", "time"].unique())

    for t in event_times:
        at_risk = x.loc[x["time"] >= t]
        n_risk = len(at_risk)
        events = x.loc[(x["time"] == t) & (x["cause"] != "0")]
        counts = events["cause"].value_counts()
        d_total = len(events)

        if n_risk == 0:
            raise RuntimeError("Event with empty risk set")

        # Use survival immediately before this event time.
        for k in cause_names:
            d_k = int(counts.get(k, 0))
            cif[k] += survival * d_k / n_risk

        survival *= (1.0 - d_total / n_risk)

        row = {
            "time": float(t),
            "n_risk": int(n_risk),
            "n_endpoint_events": int(d_total),
            "S_any": survival
        }
        row.update({"CIF_" + k: cif[k] for k in cause_names})
        curve_rows.append(row)

    return pd.DataFrame(curve_rows)

def curve_value(curve, column, t):
    return float(curve.loc[curve["time"] <= t, column].iloc[-1])

def summarize_72(x):
    curve = aalen_johansen(x)
    resolve_72 = curve_value(curve, "CIF_R", H)
    return {
        "n": len(x),
        "risk_set_72": int((x["time"] >= H).sum()),
        "resolve_CIF_72": resolve_72,
        "q_not_resolved_72": 1.0 - resolve_72,
        "active_internal_72": curve_value(curve, "S_any", H),
    }

# Overall primary estimate and transfer-censoring sensitivity.
for scenario in ["competing", "censor"]:
    x = ready_by_scenario[scenario]
    curve = aalen_johansen(x)
    summary = summarize_72(x)
    late_cif = (
        curve_value(curve, "CIF_R", MAX_OBSERVED_TIME)
        - curve_value(curve, "CIF_R", H)
    )
    print(
        f"\n{scenario}: n={summary['n']}, "
        f"risk set at 72={summary['risk_set_72']}, "
        f"resolution CIF(72)={summary['resolve_CIF_72']:.4f}, "
        f"model-based q(72)={summary['q_not_resolved_72']:.4f}, "
        f"active probability(72)={summary['active_internal_72']:.4f}"
    )
    print(
        f"Illustrative late-resolution CIF increment from 72 to 110 h: "
        f"{late_cif:.4f}. Tail risk sets are tiny; this is not a precise tail estimate."
    )

primary = ready_by_scenario["competing"]
print("\nAJ-ready primary data retain all rows and all open cases:")
print("rows:", len(primary), "open rows:", int(df["observed_status"].eq("open").sum()))
print(primary["cause"].value_counts().sort_index().to_string())

risk_72_ids = primary.loc[primary["time"] >= H, "case"].tolist()
print("Primary 72-hour risk-set case IDs:", ", ".join(risk_72_ids))
print("Risk set at 110 h:", int((primary["time"] >= MAX_OBSERVED_TIME).sum()))

def stratified_summary(x, keys):
    records = []
    for key, group in x.groupby(keys, sort=True):
        if not isinstance(key, tuple):
            key = (key,)
        record = dict(zip(keys, key))
        record.update(summarize_72(group))
        records.append(record)
    return pd.DataFrame(records)

print("\nCohort-stratified primary AJ summaries:")
print(stratified_summary(primary, ["cohort"]).round(4).to_string(index=False))

print("\nPriority-stratified primary AJ summaries:")

print("\nCohort-by-priority summaries; small risk sets dominate interpretation:")
print(
    stratified_summary(primary, ["cohort", "priority"])
    .round(4).to_string(index=False)

# All-location 72-hour bounds. No outcome or event time is imputed.
def all_location_h72_class(r):
    u = float(r.duration_or_age_h)
    status = r.observed_status

    if status == "resolved":
        return "resolved" if u <= H else "known_not_resolved"
    if status in ["withdrawn", "rejected"]:
        return "known_not_resolved"
    if status == "open":
        return "known_not_resolved" if u >= H else "unknown_early_censor"
    if status == "transferred":
        # A transfer after H cannot undo the known absence of resolution at H.
        return "known_not_resolved" if u >= H else "unknown_external_transfer"
    raise ValueError(status)

h72_class = df.apply(all_location_h72_class, axis=1)
n_success = int((h72_class == "resolved").sum())
n_failure = int((h72_class == "known_not_resolved").sum())
n_unknown = int(h72_class.isin(
    ["unknown_early_censor", "unknown_external_transfer"]
).sum())

assert n_success + n_failure + n_unknown == len(df)
lower_bound = n_failure / len(df)
upper_bound = (n_failure + n_unknown) / len(df)

print("\nAssumption-light all-location 72-hour bound:")
print(
    f"known resolved={n_success}, known not resolved={n_failure}, "
    f"ambiguous={n_unknown}"
)
print(
    f"q_any(72) is bounded descriptively between {lower_bound:.4f} "
    f"and {upper_bound:.4f}; this is not a confidence interval."
)

# Full-prefix features below are descriptive only, not baseline predictors.
df["review_visits_full_prefix"] = df["prefix"].apply(
    lambda p: p.split(">").count("Review")

df["repeat_review_full_prefix"] = df["review_visits_full_prefix"] > 1
df["rework_loop_full_prefix"] = df["prefix"].str.contains(

# PM4Py-compatible EventLog construction with activity order only.
# No time:timestamp attributes are created.

    log = EventLog()

    print("\nPM4Py get_variants control-flow summary:")
    print("number of exact variants:", len(variant_sizes))

except ImportError:
    # Fallback only for control-flow counting; it does not replace PM4Py mining.
    variant_sizes = df.groupby("prefix").size().to_dict()
    df["variant_frequency"] = df["prefix"].map(variant_sizes).astype(int)
    print(
        "\nPM4Py is not installed. Install pm4py to construct the EventLog and "
        "call get_variants; pandas variant counts are used as a fallback."
    )

variant_table = (
    pd.DataFrame({
        "prefix": list(variant_sizes.keys()),
        "frequency": list(variant_sizes.values())
    })
    .sort_values(["frequency", "prefix"], ascending=[False, True])

print(variant_table.head(8).to_string(index=False))

print(

    int(df["repeat_review_full_prefix"].sum()),
    "; Review>Rework>Review loops=",
    int(df["rework_loop_full_prefix"].sum())

# Current-stage and staleness features are legitimate only for open cases now.
open_view = df.loc[df["observed_status"].eq("open")].copy()
open_view["current_stage_at_cutoff"] = open_view["prefix"].apply(

open_view["last_activity_age_h"] = (

)
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The highest-priority additions are:

1. **Exact timestamps for every activity and terminal event.** These are necessary for escalation timing, time-dependent covariates, landmark histories, stage-entry durations, transition hazards, and determining which activities occurred before 72 hours. The current prefixes support control-flow descriptions only.
2. **External outcomes after transfer.** Obtain linked outcome type and timestamp, external ownership, and whether the SLA clock continues. Without these, all-location resolution and post-transfer late resolution are not identifiable.
3. **Submission dates and the exact observation cutoff.** These are needed to verify administrative censoring, compare calendar-period case mix, detect staggered follow-up, and construct correct cohort-era analyses.
4. **Observation-completeness and inactivity data.** Determine whether an `open` status guarantees terminal ascertainment, and obtain synchronization logs, case ownership, last successful system contact, and reasons for long gaps. This distinguishes genuine inactivity from unrecorded disposition.
5. **Assignment and case-severity variables.** Collect priority-assessment time and rules, complexity, request type, customer/channel characteristics, escalation and transfer reasons, and screening scores. These are needed for confounding control and for assessing whether transfer censoring is plausibly conditionally independent.
6. **Time-varying workload and staffing.** Queue length, reviewer/specialist capacity, backlog, and staffing by stage would support meaningful stage hazards and workload interventions. The SLA remains elapsed-time even if staffing varies.
7. **Policy rollout compliance.** Record the actual screening and escalation-policy version applied to each case, activation time, exceptions, and staff adherence. Cohort B alone is neither randomized assignment nor verified compliance.
8. **A larger, prospectively validated cohort.** Thirty-two cases, nine cases in the primary 72-hour risk set, and very few non-resolution causes cannot support stable multivariable, subdistribution, or richly parameterized multi-state models.

Finally, cohort differences remain associational even after adjustment unless the assignment mechanism and unmeasured-confounding assumptions are addressed. The most defensible current output is therefore a clearly scoped estimand, transparent competing-risk/censoring encoding, small-sample descriptive estimates with risk sets, and explicit transfer and informative-censoring sensitivity ranges—not a single causal policy effect or a precise tail-risk score.
