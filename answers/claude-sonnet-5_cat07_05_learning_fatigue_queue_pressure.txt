# 2. Integrated explanation

No single mechanism accounts for the full pattern; the data are explicitly constructed as an overlapping mixture, and the topline aggregate table (by sequence position) is a composite of at least five simultaneous, partially offsetting processes.

Early in a shift (positions 1–5, 6–15), service times are long and complexity is high — but this is not evidence of "un-learned" workers being slow. It is largely **case-mix/selection**: shifts inherit complex, high-priority carryover from the previous shift (explicitly noted: "backlog from previous shift, complex carryover"; seniors self-select into these complex early cases). As the shift progresses through mid-positions (16–50), complexity keeps falling, queue length falls, and service time falls in step — this looks like "learning" only if you ignore that the *cases themselves* are getting easier and the *queue* is getting shorter. A genuine warm-up effect may exist on top of this, but it cannot be identified from the aggregate table alone because complexity and queue length are strong confounders moving in the same direction.

The critical inflection is at positions 51–75 and 76+, where service time keeps falling (11.4, 10.8 min) but error rate and rework rate rise sharply (4.6%7.9%, 8.2%13.7%) even though mean complexity continues to *fall* (0.46, 0.43). This decoupling — errors rising while complexity is dropping — cannot be explained by case-mix (which should reduce errors) or by simple learning (which should keep improving both metrics). It aligns almost exactly with the rise in queue length (62, then 88) in the same bins. This is the signature of **queue pressure**: workers (and possibly local workarounds/batching) trade accuracy for throughput as WIP climbs, producing simultaneously faster and less accurate work. The WIP × complexity table confirms this directly: at fixed complexity, error rate roughly doubles or triples from low to high WIP, while service time drops — the "speed-quality tradeoff" is real and independent of shift position per se.

Fatigue and circadian effects are plausible *contributors* but are not separable from queue pressure and shift-position confounding in this data: the resource-experience table shows error rates rising for all groups under high WIP, but staying essentially flat under low WIP even late in the shift (seniors 2.1%2.2%, mid 2.8%2.9%). If pure fatigue were the dominant driver, error rates should rise late in the shift even when the queue is short — they do not, in this table. That said, night staff and the "last two hours" shift-level error rates (4.2–7.5%) suggest some additional circadian/end-of-shift effect (possibly compounded by Quality Check batching bursts and micro-fatigue), which is not fully absorbed by the WIP control alone; the two mechanisms likely coexist and partially reinforce each other near the end of a shift, when queues also happen to be largest.

Batching and automation interaction add further distortion that must not be conflated with individual human performance: sequential Document Review batching produces artificially short service times that pull down the "late shift" average independent of any individual's true capability, and Bot_Risk misclassifications surface as human "errors" during high-WIP periods in a way that has nothing to do with the correcting resource's fatigue or skill.

In short: the fall in service time across a shift is driven jointly by case-mix easing, plausible learning, and batching; the rise in error rate late in the shift is driven predominantly by queue pressure (and secondarily by end-of-shift batching bursts and possible circadian/fatigue effects), not by the same mechanism that produces the speed gain. Attributing both observations to one cause — "workers learn to go faster" or "workers get tired and sloppy" — is not supported once complexity, WIP, and batching are examined jointly.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Within-resource comparisons** — control for individual skill by using resource fixed effects; compare each resource's own performance across position/time/WIP rather than pooling across the workforce.

```python
import pandas as pd, statsmodels.formula.api as smf

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id='case:concept:name',
                             activity_key='concept:name', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')

model = smf.mixedlm(
    "service_time ~ sequence_position_within_shift + case_complexity_score "
    "+ wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start + C(shift_id)",
    data=df, groups=df["org:resource"]
).fit()
print(model.summary())
```

**Within-shift position effects, adjusted** — bin sequence position as in the report, but stratify by complexity and WIP tertile before comparing bins, so "learning" is tested net of composition changes.

```python
df['wip_band'] = pd.qcut(df['wip_at_start'], 3, labels=['low','med','high'])
df['cx_band']  = pd.qcut(df['case_complexity_score'], 3, labels=['low','med','high'])
df.groupby(['sequence_position_bin','wip_band','cx_band'])[['service_time','error_flag']].mean()
```

**WIP/queue stratification** — use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` to attach waiting-time and arrival-rate covariates, then interact with WIP bands.

```python
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df)
df.groupby('wip_band')[['service_time','error_flag','service_waiting_time']].mean()
```

**Complexity adjustment** — regression-adjust or exact-match on `case_complexity_score` before comparing time-of-shift effects; also run `pm4py.filter_case_performance` to isolate the complexity-service_time relationship independent of shift stage.

**Shift and time-of-day controls** — model `minutes_since_shift_start` and `shift_id` (categorical: Early/Day/Late/Night) as separate covariates, plus clock-hour to separate circadian effect from elapsed-time-in-shift effect (they are collinear within a fixed shift but not across shift types).

```python
df = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df)  # hour-of-day, day-of-week, etc.
model2 = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ minutes_since_shift_start + C(shift_id) + case_complexity_score + wip_at_start",
    data=df).fit()
```

**Batching detection**

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(df, activity_key='concept:name',
                                  resource_key='org:resource', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')
# inspect batch size, duration, and whether batch membership predicts error_flag

batches_df.groupby('activity')[['batch_size','mean_duration']].describe()
```
Cross-reference batch runs with `sequence_position_within_shift` to see whether "late-shift speed-up" is really batch-driven for Document Review/Quality Check.

**Resource-experience interactions** — interact `resource_experience_months` (or seniority group) with WIP band and sequence position; test whether juniors show a steeper WIP-error slope than seniors (resource_experience_months × wip_at_start).

```python
model3 = smf.logit(
    "error_flag ~ resource_experience_months * wip_at_start + case_complexity_score + C(shift_id)",
    data=df).fit()
```

**Rework/error downstream analysis** — use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` and `pm4py.get_service_time` to see whether errors made late in a shift/under high WIP generate rework that lands on a *different* shift or resource (hidden cost transfer), and `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` to see where rework loops concentrate.


**Visual diagnostics** — `pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart` (x=time, y=sequence_position, color=error_flag) and `pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph` (hour-of-day histograms) to visually separate queue-driven vs time-driven clustering of errors; `pm4py.cluster_log` to see if a "high-WIP fast-and-error-prone" cluster of traces emerges independent of shift/resource labels.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level error-rate comparisons in this dataset are confounded in ways that make punitive or even purely evaluative use unsafe. First, **selection bias**: seniors deliberately take complex, carryover cases early in the shift; juniors and mid-level staff are more likely to be working under high-WIP conditions later in the shift by virtue of scheduling and case routing, not choice or skill. Comparing raw error rates across resource groups without controlling for the WIP and complexity they were actually handed conflates "who got the hard/high-pressure work" with "who is more error-prone."

Second, **measurement error and log artifacts**: service-time compression from batching (Document Review, Quality Check bursts) can make a worker look artificially fast or artificially error-prone depending on how the batch is logged and how errors are attributed within a batch; Bot_Risk misclassifications surface as human "errors" upon correction, penalizing the human who caught and fixed an automation failure rather than the automation itself.

Third, **queue pressure is management-created, not worker-created**: WIP and queue length are downstream of staffing levels, shift scheduling, and triage/routing policy — decisions made by management, not by the individual resource. Rising error rates under WIP > 70 reflect a systemic capacity mismatch; treating this as an individual performance problem (intervention 8) misattributes a structural failure to the people absorbing it, and risks punishing exactly the staff (often juniors, night staff) who are structurally exposed to the worst queue conditions.

Fourth, **automation quality is invisible in naive error attribution**: because Bot_Risk errors surface as human "corrections," any scorecard that counts corrections against the correcting resource inverts the actual quality signal — the human is doing quality control on the machine, not making an independent error.

Finally, **punitive use of error rates** risks perverse incentives: if workers are penalized for late-shift/high-WIP errors, they may respond by silently deferring or mis-classifying difficult cases (gaming rework_flag, avoiding Rework Correction logging, or pushing cases to Specialist Referral inappropriately) rather than improving actual quality — degrading the very event log used to monitor the system. Any intervention involving individual accountability must be based on WIP-adjusted, complexity-adjusted, batch-excluded, automation-excluded metrics at minimum, and ideally validated against independent quality audit rather than self-reported error_flag alone.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

A process-mining dashboard should combine time series, distributional, and case-mix views, refreshed per shift and rolled up weekly:

1. **Service time trend** — median/mean `service_time` per activity, per shift, per sequence-position bin, with complexity-adjusted overlay (residual service time after regressing out `case_complexity_score`).
2. **Waiting time / queue time** — case service waiting time (`pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`) distribution per shift and per hour, to separate resource-side speed from queue-side delay.
3. **WIP and queue length over time** — `wip_at_start` / `queue_length_at_start` time series per shift with the WIP>70 threshold marked, plus queue-at-shift-start vs queue-at-shift-end delta per shift (backlog handoff indicator).
4. **Error rate, WIP-stratified** — error rate broken out by WIP tertile and complexity tertile simultaneously (a 3×3 heatmap), refreshed continuously, so raw error-rate trend is never viewed unstratified.
5. **Rework rate and rework lag** — `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` output plus time-to-detection (gap between original activity and Rework Correction), by activity and by originating shift.
6. **Complexity/case-mix indicator** — mean and distribution of `case_complexity_score` and high-priority share per shift and per sequence-position bin, so any service-time/error trend is always shown alongside the composition that could explain it.
7. **Batching indicator** — `pm4py.discover_batches` output: batch frequency, batch size, and mean intra-batch service time per activity/resource/shift, flagged separately from non-batched events in all other metrics.
8. **Handover / queue-ownership changes** — count and performance impact of cases crossing shift boundaries (case open at shift end, continued next shift), and DFG-based handover frequency between resource groups (`pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` filtered on handover edges).
9. **Worker-load fairness indicator** — distribution (not just mean) of case complexity and WIP exposure actually assigned to each resource/resource-group over time, to detect systematic routing of hard/high-pressure work to specific groups (juniors, night staff) — reported as an equity metric, not a performance metric.
10. **Automation-correction indicator** — rate of human corrections following Bot_Risk output, stratified by queue_length_at_start, tracked separately from general human error rate.

Each indicator should be viewable filtered by shift, resource group, and WIP band (`pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values`, `pm4py.filter_time_range`) so that any single-number alert (e.g., "late-shift error rate rose") is always paired with the WIP, complexity, and batching context needed to interpret it correctly before any operational or personnel action is taken.
