# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

The apparent contradiction is mainly a construct mismatch: handover and subcontracting networks measure control-flow movement, while working-together measures case co-occurrence. Mira_Coordinator is central in the first two because many exception traces pass through her, but peripheral in the third because she usually touches a case briefly, routes it onward, and then leaves the case to another resource.

**Handover-of-work network**

- What it does: captures directed transitions between resources within the same case, usually using consecutive events and possibly lifecycle transitions.
- What it suggests about Mira_Coordinator:
  - She is a major routing point: high handover out-degree centrality, high in-degree, and high betweenness.
  - She receives work from automated triage and temporary staff, then dispatches it to adjusters and specialists.

**Working-together network**

- What it does: captures undirected co-occurrence of resources within the same case, often as a proxy for shared work, team membership, or collaboration.

# 5. Recommendation

**What should be changed immediately**

- Establish formal backup coverage for the exception desk. This does not mean removing Mira_Coordinator; it means ensuring that exception routing does not stop when she is absent.
- Create a documented routing matrix for exception cases, specifying when Bot_Rules, team leads, adjusters, medical specialists, legal specialists, or Mira_Coordinator should act.
- Require audit-friendly reason codes for `Assign Specialist`, `Reopen Claim`, and `Escalate Complaint`. This addresses compliance concerns without assuming misconduct.
- Build an operational dashboard for exception aging, specialist waiting time, complaint backlog, reopened-claim backlog, and queue depth before and after Mira_Coordinator events.
- Validate the event log’s resource attribution, lifecycle transitions, activity taxonomy, and working-together normalization before using network centrality for staffing or control decisions.

**What should be monitored**

- Handover and subcontracting loops involving Mira_Coordinator, Omar_Medical, Priya_Legal, and Bot_Rules.
- Exception-flagged cycle time, especially p90, during absences, peaks, or routing-rule changes.
- Specialist overload if exception routing is shifted away from Mira_Coordinator.
- Workload fairness across adjusters, normalized by priority, amount band, region, channel, and case type.
- Compliance samples of high-priority, high-amount, complaint, fraud, legal, and medical cases to check whether routing decisions are consistent and documented.
- Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay waiting times, because these may dominate cycle time and should not be confused with coordinator performance.

**What should not be changed until more evidence is available**

- Do not remove Mira_Coordinator or redistribute all exception work solely because her working-together centrality is low. The evidence suggests she is operationally important for exceptions, even if she is not embedded in routine team collaboration.
- Do not automate all specialist selection or shift all exception routing to Bot_Rules without a pilot. The current logs suggest the bot routes exceptions to her for a reason; removing that step could increase specialist overload or uncontrolled routing.
- Do not treat high handover betweenness as automatically inefficient. It may be a necessary control point in a regulated process.
- Do not treat the absence test as proof of personal failure. It shows dependency, but not whether the dependency is optimal, avoidable, or required by current process design.
- If a future redesign removes or reduces her role, the lost coordination function must be replaced with a documented exception queue, routing rules, specialist intake controls, audit logging, and trained backup coverage.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira_Coordinator behaves like the exception desk: automated routing and temporary staff send hard or unclear claims to her, she sends them to medical/legal specialists or adjusters, and specialists return answers through her. This explains high handover and subcontracting centrality: many cases pass through her, often repeatedly. Her low working-together score is not evidence of low value; it reflects short, cross-team routing touches rather than shared case work with one team. The absence test shows routine claims are stable, but exception cases slow sharply, indicating a real key-person and bottleneck risk for complex work. The main risks are undocumented triage discretion, specialist overload, queue aging, and compliance concerns about one person both detecting problems and choosing next steps. Recommended actions are to formalize routing rules, create backup coverage and audit logs, and monitor exception waiting times before redesigning the organization.
