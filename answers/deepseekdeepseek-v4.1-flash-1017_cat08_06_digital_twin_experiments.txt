# 1. Model scope and state variables

## 1.1 Digital-twin boundary and level of detail

The twin is a discrete-event/agent-based simulation of the municipal building-permit process, extending from applicant preparation and portal submission through completeness checks, reviews, inspections, public notice, legal check, and final decision. For interventions such as D1 and D7, the boundary may start before submission, modeling applicant preparation/appointment behavior and the effect of a pre-submission checker.

The model should be individual-case and individual-resource level, with continuous simulated time and business-calendar time. It should also include a case-dependency layer because applications from the same architect, the same district, or related neighborhoods can interact through objections, inspection batching, resource contention, and policy routing.

The twin should support both:
- calibrated baseline replication of historical aggregate behavior, not merely case-by-case replay;
- counterfactual intervention simulation under non-stationary demand and policy changes.

## 1.2 Entities

- **Case**: building-permit application, with type, district, architect, value, required reviews, public-notice status, inspection status, rework history, deadlines/SLA, and final decision.
- **Applicant/architect**: source of submissions, missing-document responses, and appointment behavior; top architects may create bursts and related applications.
- **Resource**: clerk, planner, fire reviewer, environmental reviewer, inspector, legal officer, scheduler, system task, applicant/third-party responder.
- **Resource pool/queue**: e.g., planners, fire reviewers, inspectors, legal officers, grouped by skills and calendars.
- **District/geography**: affects travel time, environmentally sensitive areas, batching, and objection risk.
- **Related-case bundle**: set of applications linked by architect, district, parcel/neighborhood, or timing.
- **Objection/neighborhood process**: latent event affecting public-notice duration and legal/planning workload.
- **Calendar event**: weekday, holiday, council meeting week, peak permit month, fire-reviewer schedule, inspector field days.

## 1.3 Case state variables

Each case should maintain at minimum:

- identifiers: `case_id`, `case_type`, `district`, `architect_id`, `application_value`;
- routing attributes: `requires_fire`, `requires_environment`, `requires_public_notice`, estimated missing thresholds such as building area and prior-violation flags;
- process state: current activity, waiting/service/blocked/appeal/public-notice/rework stage, completed required reviews, outstanding joins, rework count by reason;
- document state: completeness status, missing-document request count, applicant response pending;
- inspection state: scheduling status, batched group, assigned inspector, travel requirement, no-show/readiness status;
- public-notice state: required, start/end, latent objection probability/status, objection extension;
- legal/decision state: legal precheck status, decision drafted, legal review pending, final issue/reject;
- timing state: submission time, enablement times, queue-entry times, activity start/completion times, SLA due date;
- cross-case links: related architect group, related district batch, objection neighborhood group.

## 1.4 Resources, queues, and calendars

Resource pools and calendars should be explicit:

- Clerks: 5 FTE, Mon-Fri 08:00-16:00; one Monday portal-support clerk.
- Planners: 8 FTE, Mon-Fri 09:00-17:00; two specialize in Simple renovation.
- Fire reviewers: 2 FTE, Tue-Thu only.
- Environmental reviewers: 3 FTE, Mon-Fri, with only 60% effective permit capacity.
- Inspectors: 4 FTE, Tue-Fri field visits, travel by district, batching possible.
- Legal officers: 2 FTE, no work during council meeting weeks except urgent cases.
- System tasks: effectively unlimited capacity.
- Applicant/third-party responses: modeled separately from municipal service capacity, with heavy-tailed and calendar-sensitive delays.

Queues should be activity- and resource-pool-specific, with priority rules for urgent/legal cases, aging cases (D6), batching windows (D3), and appointment smoothing (D7).

## 1.5 Cross-case dependencies

The twin must not treat cases as independent. It should model:

- **Architect-related bursts**: top architects generate correlated submissions in the same week; related applications may share review context and inspection bundling.
- **District batching**: inspections in the same district can be grouped, reducing travel time but increasing waiting for the first case in a batch.
- **Neighborhood objections**: objections can affect multiple related cases and extend public notice. Because objection events are missing from the log, they must be represented as latent variables inferred from public-notice duration and calibrated through sensitivity analysis.
- **Resource contention**: all cases compete for planners, fire/environmental reviewers, inspectors, and legal officers.
- **Decision joins**: decision drafting waits for all applicable reviews, public-notice completion, and inspection outcomes.

## 2.2 Arrivals and demand

Estimate case arrivals from unique case start times:

- overall arrival rate and case mix by month, weekday, and case type;
- seasonal multipliers, using March-May and September-November 1.7x pattern;
- Monday portal submission multiplier of 2.2x versus Wednesday;
- architect burst process: top 20 architect IDs account for 31% of cases and often submit multiple related cases within short windows;
- district and case-type mix by season and weekday.

Recommended arrival model: a non-homogeneous Poisson or negative-binomial process with seasonal and weekday intensity, case-type multinomial sampling, and a compound burst process for top architects. ## 2.3 Service times and waiting times

Where start and completion timestamps exist, estimate service times per activity/resource pool and fit positive skewed distributions such as lognormal, gamma, or Weibull. Include resource, case type, district, and calendar effects where sample size permits.

Where only completion timestamps exist:

- do not simply treat inter-event elapsed time as pure service time;
- infer the waiting/service split using resource calendars, queue assumptions, priors from the empirical duration summaries, and sensitivity analysis;
- represent the uncertainty explicitly, e.g., through hierarchical Bayesian priors or scenario bounds for the fraction of elapsed time attributable to waiting.

Waiting should be modeled as emergent from resources, calendars, priorities, and queues. External waiting—applicant missing documents, consultant reports, objection responses—should be modeled separately from municipal service times. Use heavy-tailed distributions such as lognormal or Pareto mixtures for applicant and third-party response delays.

## 2.4 Branching, required reviews, rework, and decisions

- final decision probabilities by case type and conflict indicators.

Use PM4Py variants and conformance to identify actual loop frequencies. Because rework reasons are unstructured, use categorical latent reasons only if supported by text mining; otherwise model rework by stage and case attributes and perform sensitivity analysis.

## 2.5 Resource calendars and capacity

Calendars come from HR data, but they should be validated against event-log timestamp patterns:

- activity frequency by hour/day/resource;
- lower activity during fire-reviewer non-work days;
- reduced legal work during council weeks;
- Monday portal spikes;
- inspector field-day patterns.

Calibrate effective capacities, non-permit duties, and reserved time. Do not assume full FTE availability if the log suggests otherwise.

## 2.6 Cross-case dependencies and missing objections

Estimate:

- architect burst statistics: inter-submission times, number of related applications, district overlap;
- inspection bundling opportunity: same district and overlapping scheduling windows;
- objection process: although objection events are missing, public-notice duration is much longer with objections. Use a mixture model or latent objection state calibrated from public-notice duration, case type, district, architect history, and public-notice age. Treat this as an assumption with uncertainty, not as observed fact;
- related-case influence: objections and reviews on one case may influence another through shared architect/neighborhood state.

Use train/validation/test periods or hold-out districts/architects for out-of-sample checks.

# 3. Assumptions

- Human service times are positive, right-skewed, and may vary by resource, case type, district, and calendar context.
- Where start timestamps are missing, the service/waiting split is uncertain; the twin must represent this uncertainty rather than overstate precision.
- Specialized reviewers and inspectors have effectively different service capacity due to part-time calendars and travel.

## Routing

- The supplied process tree is a simplified schematic, not the full ground truth.
- Fire, environmental, public-notice, and legal requirements depend on observable flags plus unobserved thresholds/sensitivity data.

## Resources/calendars

- HR calendars are approximately correct but may need adjustment based on observed activity patterns.
- Legal capacity is materially affected by council meeting weeks.
- Inspector travel and district batching are significant.
- System tasks have unlimited capacity.

## Rework

- Rework loops occur after Planning, Inspection, Legal, and appeal.
- Rework reasons are not reliably coded; stage-level rework probabilities are modeled with uncertainty.
- Rework may be correlated with case complexity, architect, district, and missing-document history.

- Applicant response time is heavy-tailed and separate from municipal service time.
- Missing-document loops are driven by applicant behavior and can be reduced by D1, but D1 may shift effort before submission.
- Consultants and objectors may have their own delays not fully visible in the log.

## Cross-case dependencies

- Related architect cases and district inspection batching create meaningful interactions.
- Neighborhood objections can affect multiple cases but are only partially observed.
- Top architects may face different arrival patterns and service dynamics than small applicants.

## Non-stationary demand

- Seasonal peaks and Monday submission spikes repeat in the simulation horizon.
- Stress levels beyond historical range will be simulated rather than assumed to be exactly estimated.

## Missing data

- Missing start timestamps, objection events, environmental sensitivity, building-area thresholds, and rare emergency decisions create parameter uncertainty.
- The baseline simulation should be calibrated to aggregate distributions and known constraints, not to a single exact trace.
- No simulation result should be claimed as exact optimality; decisions should use uncertainty intervals and robustness.

# 5. Warm-up, replications, and uncertainty treatment

## Warm-up

Run a warm-up of at least 6 months and preferably 12 months because of strong seasonality and council calendars. Initialize the warm-up with a plausible historical backlog/WIP snapshot or with a burn-in period from empty state. Discard warm-up statistics for steady/seasonal comparison. For non-stationary demand, use a 36-month simulated horizon and discard the first 12 months, then report over 24 months spanning complete seasonal cycles. Randomize or stratify starting month across replications to avoid phase-of-season artifacts.

## Replications and run length

Use at least 200 independent replications per main scenario and 500+ for tail metrics such as p95/p99. Use common random numbers across interventions to compare paired differences: same arrival stream, same applicant-response random draws, same objection latent draws, and same no-show draws where logically valid. Store seeds and scenario identifiers. For stress scenarios, use at least 100-200 replications but report wider uncertainty.

## Uncertainty treatment

Report means, medians, p90, p95, SLA compliance, backlog, utilization, and differences versus baseline with 95% confidence or credible intervals. Use batch means for correlated output and bootstrap quantile intervals for medians and tails. Propagate parameter uncertainty by sampling from hierarchical/Bayesian posteriors or by Latin-hypercube/global sensitivity analysis over key uncertain parameters:

- service/waiting split for activities with missing start timestamps;
- objection probability and duration;
- fire/environment/public-notice requirement thresholds;
- rework probabilities;
- top-architect burst intensity;
- calendar interruption frequency;
- inspector travel/no-show distributions.

For heavy-tailed durations, avoid arbitrary truncation. Use mixtures and empirical tails where data support them. If data are sparse, use conservative tail priors and report a tail-sensitivity scenario. Do not claim exact optimality; select interventions robust over plausible parameter draws and stress scenarios.

- waiting and service-time distributions where start timestamps permit;
- public-notice duration distribution, especially the long tail;

Train parameters on an earlier period and validate on a later hold-out period, or hold out districts/architects/months. Evaluate aggregate and tail predictions, not just point forecasts. Re-calibrate or explicitly widen uncertainty if out-of-sample performance is poor. Use intervention results only after the baseline has passed agreed validation tolerances.

# 7. Decision criteria

Decision metrics should be computed by case type, district, architect-size class, and stress scenario.

## Primary performance metrics

- **Median case duration** overall and by case type.
- **Tail duration**: p90 and p95 by case type, especially Complex variance and Commercial.
- **SLA compliance**: define calibrated thresholds, e.g., Simple renovation 30 days, New residential 90 days, Commercial 120 days, Complex variance 180 days, or use business-approved values. Report percentage of cases meeting SLA and the change versus baseline.
- **Backlog and aged cases**: mean/peak backlog and number of cases older than 60/90/120 days.

## Fairness and distributional metrics

- Median and SLA compliance by district, especially D3/D5.
- Median and SLA compliance by architect-size class; monitor top-20 architects.
- Difference in SLA between best and worst districts/architect groups. Interventions should not increase this gap by more than about 2 percentage points or a documented relative tolerance.

## Resource and quality metrics

- Resource utilization by pool: target roughly 65-85% for human pools, with no sustained >90% utilization unless explicitly justified.
- Inspector travel time, overtime, and batching efficiency.
- Rework rate, missing-document loop rate, inspection rework rate, appeal rate, and objection-extension rate.
- For D2/D8/D9, false-negative proxies such as later rework, appeals, or legal conflicts.

## Decision rule

Interventions should first satisfy hard constraints:

- no material degradation in SLA for any case type or vulnerable district/architect group;
- no sustained resource utilization above acceptable limits;
- no excessive risk from D2/D8/D9 false negatives;
- cost/capacity within municipal limits.

Then rank remaining interventions using a multi-criteria rule, for example:

- 35-40% tail/SLA improvement;
- 20% median duration improvement;
- 15% fairness and equity;
- 15% resource utilization/cost;
- 10-15% robustness under stress and parameter uncertainty.

Select an intervention only if it is plausibly better than baseline with a high probability under uncertainty, e.g., 80% posterior/CI probability of improving overall SLA and no case-type degradation beyond tolerance. Prefer robust interventions that perform acceptably across stress scenarios over interventions that are best only in the calibrated baseline. Do not claim exact optimality.

def load_and_format_log(csv_text: str = LOG_CSV) -> Tuple[pd.DataFrame, pd.DataFrame]:
    """Load log with pandas; never call a nonexistent pm4py.read_csv."""
    raw = pd.read_csv(StringIO(csv_text))
    raw["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(raw["time:timestamp"])

    # PM4Py convention: case:concept:name, concept:name, time:timestamp.
    formatted = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        raw,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    return raw, formatted


def parse_simplified_process_tree(s: str):
    """
    Parse the simplified tree if possible. PM4Py process-tree syntax may not
    capture all constructs in the supplied abstraction. Use the parsed tree as
    a schematic; use discovered DFG/variants/conformance for calibration.
    """
    try:
        return pm4py.parse_process_tree(s)
    except Exception as exc:
        print(f"Simplified process tree could not be parsed exactly: {exc}")
        return {"raw_tree": s, "parse_error": str(exc)}


def compute_variants(df: pd.DataFrame):
    """Use PM4Py variants where available; fallback to a pandas implementation."""
    try:
        return pm4py.get_variants(df)
    except Exception:
        tmp = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
        return {
            cid: tuple(grp["concept:name"].tolist())
            for cid, grp in tmp.groupby("case:concept:name")
        }


def analyze_log_with_pm4py(df: pd.DataFrame) -> Dict[str, object]:
    variants = compute_variants(df)
    dfg, start_activities, end_activities = pm4py.discover_dfg(df)
    perf_dfg, perf_start, perf_end = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)

    # Case arrival statistics.
    case_starts = (
        df.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].min().sort_index()
    )
    span_days = max(
        (case_starts.max() - case_starts.min()).total_seconds() / 86400.0,
        1.0,
    )
    avg_arrivals_per_day = len(case_starts) / span_days
    interarrival_days = case_starts.diff().dt.total_seconds().div(86400).dropna()

    # Approximate service/waiting columns. With only completion timestamps,
    # elapsed time since the previous completion is a mixed waiting+service proxy.
    work = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).copy()
    work["prev_completion_ts"] = work.groupby("case:concept:name")[
        "time:timestamp"
    ].shift(1)
    work["elapsed_since_prev_completion_days"] = (
        work["time:timestamp"] - work["prev_completion_ts"]
    ).dt.total_seconds().div(86400)

    if {"start_timestamp", "time:timestamp"}.issubset(work.columns):
        work["service_days"] = (
            work["time:timestamp"] - pd.to_datetime(work["start_timestamp"])
        ).dt.total_seconds().div(86400)
        work["waiting_before_activity_days_approx"] = (
            work["elapsed_since_prev_completion_days"] - work["service_days"]
        )
    else:
        work["service_days"] = np.nan
        work["waiting_before_activity_days_approx"] = np.nan
        # Use elapsed_since_prev_completion_days as a mixed proxy only.

    return {
        "variants": variants,
        "dfg": dfg,
        "start_activities": start_activities,
        "end_activities": end_activities,
        "performance_dfg": perf_dfg,
        "avg_arrivals_per_day": avg_arrivals_per_day,
        "mean_interarrival_days": interarrival_days.mean(),
        "event_table_with_timing": work,
    }

@dataclass
class Calendar:
    name: str
    working_weekdays: set = field(default_factory=lambda: {0, 1, 2, 3, 4})
    start_hour: int = 8
    end_hour: int = 16
    holidays: set = field(default_factory=set)

    def is_working(self, ts: pd.Timestamp) -> bool:
        return ts.weekday() in self.working_weekdays and ts.date() not in self.holidays

    def next_working_time(self, ts: pd.Timestamp) -> pd.Timestamp:
        """Placeholder: advance to the next working day/hour."""
        cur = ts
        for _ in range(14):
            if self.is_working(cur):
                return cur
            cur = (cur + pd.Timedelta(days=1)).normalize() + pd.Timedelta(hours=self.start_hour)
        return cur


class ResourcePool:
    def __init__(self, name: str, capacity: int, calendar: Calendar):
        self.name = name
        self.capacity = capacity
        self.calendar = calendar
        self.busy = 0
        self.queue: deque = deque()

    def request(self, case_id: str, activity: str, priority: float, now: pd.Timestamp):
        """
        Placeholder queueing method. A real implementation would use a heap
        keyed by priority/aging, reserve a resource during working time, and
        return an available time and resource identifier.
        """
        if self.busy < self.capacity and self.calendar.is_working(now):
            self.busy += 1
            return now, f"{self.name}_res_{self.busy}"
        self.queue.append((priority, case_id, activity, now))
        return now, None

    def release(self, resource_id: str, now: pd.Timestamp):
        self.busy = max(0, self.busy - 1)

class RoutingRules:
    def __init__(self, params: Dict, policies: Dict):
        self.params = params
        self.policies = policies

    def required_reviews(self, case: Case) -> List[str]:
        acts = ["Completeness check", "Planning review"]
        if case.requires_fire:
            acts.append("Fire-safety review")
        if case.requires_environment:
            acts.append("Environmental review")
        if case.requires_public_notice:
            acts.append("Public-notice period")
        acts += ["Inspection scheduling", "Site inspection", "Decision drafting"]
        if case.case_type in {"Commercial", "Complex variance"}:
            acts.append("Legal check")
        return acts

    def should_rework(self, case: Case, activity: str, rng: np.random.Generator) -> bool:
        """Placeholder empirical rework probability lookup."""
        p = self.params.get("rework_probability", {}).get(activity, 0.0)
        return rng.random() < p

    def choose_decision(self, case: Case, rng: np.random.Generator) -> str:
        p_issue = self.params.get("issue_probability_by_type", {}).get(case.case_type, 0.9)
        return "Issue permit" if rng.random() < p_issue else "Reject application"
