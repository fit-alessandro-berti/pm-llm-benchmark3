## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this case, the difference is between visible, bounded, compensated deviations and invisible, open-ended, uncompensated control loss.

**True resilience** preserves critical outcomes while making deviations explicit, controlled, and recoverable. Examples:

- **B. Manual authorization check** preserved authorization control using offline lists.
- **I. Defer noncritical order** protected hospital capacity, although it created visible service loss for pharmacies.
- **J. Backlog prioritization** restored order by criticality and product risk.
- **K. Reconcile manual reservation** restored data integrity after recovery.
- **A. Hospital-critical fast lane** is partly resilient because it protected hospital fill rate, but only if it did not silently bypass authorization.
- **F. Temporary release control** was resilient for controlled-substance orders because all controlled-substance orders were checked, but it was incomplete for cold-chain documentation.

**Risky shortcuts or concealed noncompliance** preserve throughput or cycle time by dropping critical controls and hiding the gap until later. Examples:

- **C. Skip system authorization check** created possible unauthorized shipments. The missing authorization was not immediately visible in cycle-time metrics.
- **G. Dispatch without scan** created the largest traceability gap. Orders physically moved, but chain-of-custody evidence disappeared.
- **D. Manual inventory snapshot check** became a shortcut as the snapshot aged. Early use was reasonable; later use concealed inventory inaccuracy.
- **H. Phone proof of delivery** is weak for controlled products because it cannot provide the same evidence as a signature, ID, or chain-of-custody record.
- **L. Audit remediation after close** can conceal noncompliance if it becomes the standard way to close known gaps. It creates the illusion of recovery because invoices close while unresolved compliance issues remain.

The conformance findings support this: baseline fitness was 0.96 with 91% fitting traces, but manual workaround fitness dropped to 0.71 with only 44% fitting traces. This does not mean every nonconforming trace was bad. It means the process became highly variable. The dangerous part is not the deviation itself, but whether the deviation was authorized, documented, compensated, and later resolved.

The elevated traceability exceptions in T2 and T4 are the strongest signal of concealed noncompliance:

- Traceability exceptions: baseline 0.8%  T2 9.5%  T4 2.2%
- Cold-chain temperature documentation missing: baseline 0.4%  T2 4.8%  T4 0.9%
- Controlled-substance dual-control missing: baseline 0.2%  T2 2.9%  T4 0.5%

Even in stabilization, these metrics remained above baseline, and an audit-remediation queue persisted after operational recovery. That indicates hidden workload and residual compliance risk.

**Ambiguous adaptations** depend on risk appetite and product type:

- **A. Hospital-critical fast lane** is acceptable for ambient hospital orders if there is a fast, documented emergency authorization. It is not acceptable for controlled substances or cold-chain products without full control.
- **D. Manual inventory snapshot check** is acceptable for a short time for low-risk ambient items. It is not acceptable for stale inventory or recall-sensitive products.
- **H. Phone proof of delivery** is acceptable for low-risk ambient deliveries. It is too weak for controlled substances or high-risk cold-chain items.
- **L. Audit remediation** is acceptable for minor missing documentation. It is not acceptable as a substitute for real-time control of high-risk products.

In short: resilience means degrading gracefully with visible controls. Concealed noncompliance means keeping the line moving while losing the evidence or authorization needed to prove that the shipment was safe, legal, and traceable.

## 4. Recovery process model

This is a structured model for future disruptions. It is organized as a trigger-and-phase process: trigger, manual mode, backlog prioritization, reconciliation, and audit-remediation.

### 4.1 Trigger conditions

Manual recovery mode should be activated when any of the following occur:

- WMS or order-management system is unavailable for more than 4 hours.
- Inventory synchronization or scanning is unreliable.
- Cyber incident declared with unknown data integrity.
- Normal dispatch or proof-of-delivery scanning cannot be performed.
- Critical hospital supply cannot wait for system restoration.

Activation should be explicit and visible, with an incident commander and a daily control review.

### 4.2 Triage rules

Orders should be assigned to one of four lanes:

| Lane | Customer segment | Product risk | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1: Protect first | Hospital-critical | Any | Process first; apply enhanced manual controls for cold-chain/controlled/recall. |
| P2: Process with controls | Pharmacy-standard | Ambient | Normal manual flow; may be deferred if capacity is constrained but must be communicated. |
| P3: Defer by default | Wholesale-bulk | Ambient | Defer unless otherwise critical; maximum deferral age must be set. |
| P4: High-risk hold | Any | Controlled/cold/recall | Do not dispatch unless all minimum high-risk controls are met, regardless of customer pressure. |

Customer criticality and product risk should be combined explicitly. A hospital-critical cold-chain order is high priority but also high control; it should not be rushed without temperature and cold-chain verification.

### 4.3 Minimum controls by product/customer risk

| Risk cell | Minimum control before dispatch | Manual evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital-critical + ambient | Emergency authorization, manual pick/pack, courier-signed manifest | Order ID, authorized by, pick list, courier signature |
| Hospital-critical + controlled/cold/recall | Dual manual authorization, physical lot/location confirmation, temperature/dual-control check | Authorization signatures, lot/batch, temp logger reading, chain-of-custody |
| Pharmacy-standard + ambient | Offline authorization check, manual pick/pack, courier-signed manifest, phone/email POD | Order ID, authorization, pick list, POD confirmation |
| Pharmacy-standard + controlled/cold/recall | Same as hospital-critical high-risk controls | Same as high-risk evidence |
| Wholesale-bulk + ambient | Defer by default; if processed, standard manual flow | Order ID, authorization, pick list, POD |
| Wholesale-bulk + high-risk | If processed, same high-risk controls; otherwise defer with customer communication | Same as high-risk evidence |

### 4.4 Manual evidence capture

Manual processes should use a single incident ledger. Each order or container should have a unique manual record containing:

- Order ID and customer segment.
- Product type and risk class.
- Authorization decision and who made it.
- Inventory snapshot version or physical count.
- Pick list number and picker.
- Lot/batch/expiry for recall-sensitive items.
- Temperature logger ID and reading for cold-chain items.
- Controlled-substance dual-control partners and counts.
- Courier handoff time and courier signature.
- POD evidence type: phone, photo, email, or signed paper.

Evidence should be captured at the moment of the control, not reconstructed days later. If a system is down, capture paper, photo, or mobile-device records and upload them during recovery.

### 4.5 Backlog prioritization

Backlog priority should be explicit and age-sensitive. A simple scoring model:

- Customer criticality weight: Hospital-critical = 3, Pharmacy-standard = 2, Wholesale-bulk = 1.
- Product risk weight: Controlled/cold/recall = 3, Ambient = 1.
- Age escalator: add points as an order ages beyond its segment threshold.

Example priority score:

```text
Priority score = (customer weight * 10) + (product risk weight * 5) + age points
```

Age thresholds:

- Hospital-critical: escalate after 8 hours.
- Pharmacy-standard: escalate after 24 hours.
- Wholesale-bulk: escalate after 48 hours.

The backlog should be recalculated every 4 hours. Lower-priority orders cannot be postponed indefinitely; an age-based escalator must force action even for bulk orders.

### 4.6 Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

During manual mode, all manual records go into the incident ledger. On system recovery:

1. **Reconciliation**
   - Match manual reservations against WMS inventory and physical counts.
   - For controlled/cold/recall items, reconcile before dispatch or before invoice close.
   - For ambient items, reconciliation may proceed after dispatch but before final close.
   - Any mismatch creates a reconciliation hold.

2. **Audit remediation**
   - Classify missing evidence into critical, moderate, and low risk.
   - Critical gaps: controlled-substance dual-control, cold-chain temperature, recall-sensitive lot traceability, dispatch chain-of-custody, or authorization bypass.
   - Critical gaps must be resolved before invoice close. No exceptions.
   - Moderate gaps: missing POD scan for ambient items, incomplete manual notes, minor spreadsheet errors.
   - Moderate gaps may close provisionally but must be resolved within 48–72 hours.
   - Low gaps: formatting, duplicate entries, delayed data entry.
   - Low gaps may be batch-fixed, but must be tracked.

3. **Invoice close**
   - Invoice close should require completion of critical remediation.
   - If audit remediation is pending, the order should remain in a “provisional close” state, not a final closed state.

4. **Return to normal**
   - Normal process may be declared restored only when:
     - System availability and scanning are stable.
     - Backlog is below threshold.
     - Reconciliation queue is clearing.
     - Control metrics return to within pre-defined tolerance of baseline.
     - Audit remediation queue is below SLA.

This prevents a false return-to-normal while hidden remediation work remains.

| 8 | Make audit remediation a first-class recovery process with SLAs and high-risk hold-before-close rules. | Medium | Prevents after-the-fact remediation from becoming concealed noncompliance | Some low-risk exceptions may still extend after close |

---

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept that during a major system outage, non-critical cycle times will degrade, some pharmacy and wholesale orders may be deferred or cancelled, manual controls will be slower than normal, and a visible exception backlog will exist. This is acceptable if the deviations are explicit, bounded, and risk-based. Management should not accept missing authorization for controlled-substance, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive shipments; scan-less dispatch of high-risk products; temperature documentation gaps; or the routine use of after-the-fact audit remediation to close orders before critical evidence is verified. A controlled emergency deviation for hospital-critical ambient supply may be acceptable if authorized by a responsible person and reconciled quickly. The recommendation would change if offline authorization lists and manual evidence capture were shown to maintain baseline-level traceability for high-risk orders and if hospital-critical fill rates remained protected without a hidden audit backlog. Conversely, if hospital fill rates fell below roughly 85%, or if high-risk control exceptions remained elevated beyond a small tolerance, stricter controls or temporary shutdown of affected high-risk shipments would be required.
