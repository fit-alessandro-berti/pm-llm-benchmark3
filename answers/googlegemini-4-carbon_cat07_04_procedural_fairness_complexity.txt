# 1. Fairness dimensions

Assessing fairness in public-benefits administration requires looking beyond raw approval rates to evaluate how applicants experience the process, how administrative burdens are distributed, and whether institutional resources match legal commitments. At least eight distinct dimensions apply here:

* **Outcome parity (unconditional):** Measures whether raw approval rates are equal across groups (Group A: 78% vs. Group B: 63%, Group C: 69%, Group D: 58%). Outcome parity alone **cannot** establish discrimination because underlying eligibility, documentation status, and risk profiles vary across groups. Conversely, raw differences remain socially consequential if administrative barriers depress approvals for vulnerable populations.
* **Adjusted outcome disparity (conditional):** Measures differences in approval probability after controlling for observable case-mix factors. Even after adjustment, Group B (-5.5 percentage points [pp]), Group D (-3.1 pp), and Group C (-2.2 pp) have lower approval probabilities than Group A. While closer to testing "equal treatment of equals," adjusted estimates remain vulnerable to omitted variables and biased controls.
* **Procedural burden (compliance and psychological costs):** Captures the effort imposed on applicants to prove eligibility—such as repeated document requests, multi-touch interactions, and opaque handoffs. Group B faces a +9.5 pp adjusted increase in `Request Missing Documents` even *after* controlling for baseline `documentation_completeness`, signaling heightened friction rather than baseline omissions alone.
* **Timeliness (speed and tail delays):** Evaluates how long applicants wait for subsistence benefits, both at the median and in the upper tail. Group B (13.7d median; 34.2d p90) and Group C (15.9d median; 38.8d p90) endure far longer delays than Group A (8.1d median; 21.4d p90), retaining adjusted penalties of +2.6 days and +4.1 days, respectively. For public benefits, a two-to-three-week tail gap represents material deprivation.
* **Consistency of routing:** Assesses whether process rules are applied uniformly across comparable cases. Policy dictates specific routing for high risk, language needs, and accommodations, yet Group B (+8.0 pp) and Group C (+5.5 pp) face substantially higher adjusted rates of `Supervisor Review` without an explicit policy mandate requiring supervisory sign-off for minority-language or accommodated applicants.
* **Quality and accessibility of support:** Examines whether rights-preserving steps (`Language Support Contact`, `Accommodation Review`) genuinely equalize access or degrade into second-class bottlenecks. Because service-level targets omit these steps, exercising a statutory right to language or disability support triggers severe queuing penalties (3.8d and 4.7d median waits just to reach `Eligibility Assessment`).
* **Appeal and rework burden:** Reflects process error, poor communication, and administrative churn. Reopen/rework rates for Groups B (22%), C (24%), and D (20%) are roughly double Group A's (11%), and adjusted appeal probabilities are elevated for Group D (+5.7 pp) and Group B (+4.4 pp). High rework and appeals force vulnerable applicants to expend extra time and advocacy to correct agency mistakes or contest opaque denials.
* **Resource-allocation fairness:** Evaluates whether staffing capacity and specialization are proportional to case demand and complexity. Group A is served by a broad pool of 52 generalists, whereas Group B relies on 7 bilingual workers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and Group C relies on 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom double as supervisors). Under-provisioning specialized queues structurally disadvantages the groups legally entitled to use them.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To separate **raw disparity** (unadjusted gaps), **legitimate routing** (policy-mandated steps for valid complexity), **procedural/substantive unfairness** (unjustified hurdles or capacity starvation), and **statistical uncertainty** (omitted variables or measurement error), the evaluation should follow a six-part strategy:

1. **Tiered matching and stratification:**
   * *Tier 1 (Exogenous/structural covariates):* Use Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) or inverse-probability weighting on `channel`, `household_size_band`, `region`, and intake month. These capture operational environment without conditioning on potentially tainted agency assessments.
   * *Tier 2 (Observed complexity covariates):* Add `documentation_completeness`, `prior_case_count`, `risk_score_band`, `language_preference`, and `disability_accommodation_requested` sequentially. Comparing Tier 1 and Tier 2 isolates how much of the raw disparity is absorbed by recorded complexity versus unexplained group effects.
2. **Path and variant comparison (within-variant vs. between-variant):**
   * *Between-variant routing audit:* Model the conditional probability of entering specific variants (V1–V6) or discretionary steps (`Request Missing Documents`, `Supervisor Review`) given identical Tier 2 covariates. Legitimate routing explains why high-risk cases enter V3 (`Fraud Review`), but cannot explain why Group B with complete documentation still receives extra document requests.
   * *Within-variant performance comparison:* Compare Group A, B, C, and D cases that traverse the *exact same variant* (e.g., comparing only cases on clean path V1 or doc-request path V2). If Group B still experiences slower cycle times or lower approvals within V1, the disparity stems from caseworker execution or regional queuing rather than path structure.
3. **Resource-assignment and network comparison:**
   * Quantify resource concentration (e.g., Herfindahl-Hirschman Index of case assignments per group), handover counts per case, and "ping-pong" loops (`generalist  specialist  generalist`).
   * Test whether outcomes and delays are driven by specific overloaded individuals (e.g., the 3 regional officers or the 2 dual-role accommodation/supervisor staff) using multilevel models with random effects for `org:resource`.
4. **Waiting-time decomposition:**
   * Partition total cycle time into: (a) **active service time** (caseworker processing), (b) **internal queue waiting time** (waiting for bilingual, accommodation, fraud, or supervisor pickup), and (c) **external clock-stop/applicant waiting time** (`Request Missing Documents  Eligibility Assessment`).
   * Isolate the "support-step time tax" by computing the net queue wait added by `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` relative to direct transitions into `Eligibility Assessment`.
5. **Sensitivity analysis for potentially biased covariates:**
   * `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may act as **contaminated controls** (mediators of discrimination or proxies for language/socioeconomic barriers). Estimate models *excluding* `risk_score_band` and *excluding* `documentation_completeness` to bound total disparity.
   * Conduct formal sensitivity analyses (e.g., Oster’s $\delta$ or Rosenbaum bounds) to test how strong an unobserved confounder (such as unrecorded income volatility or complex medical proof) would need to be to fully explain Group B’s -5.5 pp approval gap or Group C’s +4.1d cycle-time penalty.
6. **Subgroup checks by region and channel:**
   * Estimate interaction effects across `region × applicant_group` and `channel × applicant_group`. Because two regions suffer supervisor backlogs and Group B has a 38% in-person share (vs. 9% for Group A), subgroup slicing distinguishes localized operational failures and physical-mail digitization lags from system-wide disparate treatment.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Synthesizing the variant, waiting-time, and staffing evidence reveals a clear distinction between complexity-driven routing and structurally unfair procedural bottlenecks:

### Disparities potentially justified by case complexity and policy

Because 51% of Group D falls into the high `risk_score_band` (vs. 18% of Group A) and policy mandates Fraud Review for high-risk bands, this routing follows formal rules—*conditional on the risk score itself being valid* (a critical caveat addressed below). Notably, once controlling for covariates, Group D's cycle time penalty shrinks to +0.9 days, indicating the fraud queue itself is relatively consistently paced across groups (2.2d–2.8d wait).
* **Entry into rights-preserving pathways (V4 and V5):** Group B’s concentration in V4 (24%) and Group C’s concentration in V5 (31%) reflect legitimate activation of `Language Support Contact` (57% need in Group B) and `Accommodation Review` (62% requested in Group C). Similarly, longer initial waits from `Submit  Identity Verification` for Groups B (1.4d) and C (1.2d) vs. Group A (0.8d) partially reflect higher in-person submission shares (38% and 24% vs. 9%), which require manual intake handling.

### Concerning procedural and resource disparities
* **Capacity starvation of rights-preserving queues ("time tax" on legal rights):** Policy designates language and accommodation steps as rights-preserving, yet management omits them from SLA governance and staffs them thinly (7 bilingual workers for ~712 Group B cases needing support; 5 specialists for ~508 Group C accommodation cases, vs. 52 caseworkers serving Group A). Consequently, Group B waits a median of **3.8 days** after `Language Support Contact` to reach `Eligibility Assessment` and **1.6 days** (4x Group A's 0.4d) between `Completeness Check` and `Request Missing Documents`. Group C waits **4.7 days** from `Accommodation Review` to `Eligibility Assessment`. Applicants are penalized with multi-day queue delays simply for needing accessible service.
* **Unexplained discretionary escalations (`Supervisor Review` and `Request Missing Documents`):** Controlling for documented completeness, risk, channel, and region, Group B still faces a **+9.5 pp** higher rate of missing-document requests and a **+8.0 pp** higher rate of `Supervisor Review`; Group C faces **+5.5 pp** more `Supervisor Review` and **+4.2 pp** more document requests. Moreover, Variant V5 shows `Accommodation Review` routinely funneling Group C into `Supervisor Review`, while two of the five accommodation specialists *also* perform supervisor reviews—creating a structural bottleneck and potential role conflict where specialists review or queue behind their own team's caseload.
* **Excessive handovers and rework loops:** In two regions, Group B endures `generalist  bilingual  generalist` ping-ponging, and Group C undergoes `generalist  specialist  supervisor` chains. Multi-handoff chains cause context loss and inconsistent interpretations, aligning with doubled reopen/rework rates for Groups B (22%) and C (24%) compared to Group A (11%).
* **Truncation/attrition risk in Variant V4:** While V1 terminates in `Notify  Activate` (42% of Group A, 5.8d), top path V4 (`Submit  Verify  Language Support  Completeness  Request Docs  Eligibility  Decision`, 24% of Group B, 19.2d) notably lacks `Activate` (and even `Notify` in the top-path trace summary), suggesting higher denial rates, delayed notification, or procedural stalling along the bilingual doc-request path.
* **Elevated post-decision contestation for Groups B and D:** Adjusted appeal probabilities remain significantly higher for Group D (+5.7 pp) and Group B (+4.4 pp), indicating that denials in Fraud Review and bilingual pathways are frequently disputed by applicants.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least five distinct mechanisms—spanning legitimate administration, structural under-resourcing, and systemic bias—could generate these empirical patterns:

1. **Legitimate substantive and evidentiary complexity (Legitimate):** Applicants with complex household arrangements, multi-source informal income, or specialized medical/disability verification genuinely require more verification steps (`Accommodation Review`, `Request Missing Documents`) and careful deliberation, naturally extending processing times relative to straightforward online renewals (V1).
2. **"Rights without resources" / Structural rationing (Concerning):** The agency instituted formal compliance steps (`Language Support Contact`, `Accommodation Review`) without matching staffing capacity or SLA accountability. Combined with segmented generalist/specialist workflows, this creates severe queuing bottlenecks and handover friction (`generalist  specialist  generalist`), turning protective rights into an unintended procedural penalty (+2.6d for Group B, +4.1d for Group C).
3. **Endogenous communication barriers driving procedural denials (Concerning):** If initial intake forms, completeness checklists, or follow-up notices are confusing or poorly translated, minority-language and disabled applicants are more likely to submit technically incomplete packets—and even when initially complete, generalist workers encountering non-English documents may reflexively trigger `Request Missing Documents` (+9.5 pp adjusted for Group B) while waiting for a bilingual worker. Resulting delays or missed deadlines can produce **procedural denials** (failing administrative compliance) rather than substantive ineligibility, explaining Group B's -5.5 pp adjusted approval gap.
4. **Defensive escalation and role coupling (Concerning):** Caseworkers unfamiliar with minority-language documents or disability accommodations may escalate cases to `Supervisor Review` (+8.0 pp Group B, +5.5 pp Group C) out of uncertainty or liability aversion. For Group C, having two of five accommodation specialists double as supervisors compounds queue waits and blurs independent oversight.
5. **Historical feedback loops in algorithmic risk scoring (Concerning):** Group D has high `prior_case_count` (49%) and high `risk_score_band` (51%). In public-benefits systems, frequent prior applications often reflect poverty-induced income churn or *past wrongful procedural closures/reopens* rather than fraud. If `prior_case_count` or historically over-audited demographics feed `risk_score_band`, Group D is mechanically funneled into adversarial `Fraud Review` (V3), depressing approvals (-3.1 pp adjusted) and provoking high appeal rates (+5.7 pp adjusted).

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Even a rigorous regression or matching model built on the provided event log cannot definitively prove or rule out discrimination due to five fundamental data limitations:

* **Measurement bias and endogeneity in `documentation_completeness`:** Completeness is recorded by staff *during* the process, not as an objective pre-treatment fact. Whether a document is deemed "complete" depends on form clarity, language accessibility, and caseworker discretion. Conditioning on `documentation_completeness` risks understating unfairness against Group B if language barriers or stricter scrutiny caused the "incomplete" label in the first place.
* **Historical and proxy bias in `risk_score_band`:** Treating `risk_score_band` as an objective control assumes equal risk scores represent equal true fraud probability across groups. If the scoring rule weights `prior_case_count`, neighborhood/region, or historical investigation outcomes tainted by selective enforcement, controlling for `risk_score_band` masks institutional bias as "legitimate risk routing."
* **Missing applicant needs and unrecorded vulnerability:** Binary flags (`disability_accommodation_requested`, `language_preference`) only capture *disclosed and recorded* requests. Applicants unaware of their right to accommodations, deterred by stigma, or experiencing low literacy, cognitive burden, or housing instability go unmeasured. Unobserved socioeconomic fragility could confound both cycle times and approval rates.
* **Coarse resource-workload granularity:** Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity, hiding intra-day multitasking, non-casework duties (e.g., specialists pulled into supervisory admin), unlogged phone consultations, and true case difficulty. Furthermore, logs often blur *active service time* with *passive calendar time* when cases sit open on an overloaded worker's desk.
* **Absence of appeal outcomes and interaction quality:** `appeal_flag` records whether an applicant appealed, not whether the **appeal succeeded (reversal rate)**. High appeal reversals for Groups B and D would strongly confirm initial agency error; conversely, *low* appeal filing in Group C (only +1.1 pp adjusted despite +4.1d delays and 24% rework) might reflect applicant exhaustion rather than satisfaction. Finally, `case_outcome` omits benefit adequacy, clarity of written explanations, and dignity of interpersonal treatment.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Note: **Removing `Language Support Contact` or `Accommodation Review` to shorten cycle times is unacceptable**, as doing so would strip legally protected, rights-preserving safeguards and likely worsen erroneous denials. Instead, mitigations should eliminate the structural penalties attached to those steps:

| Mitigation option | Mechanism of improvement | Possible side effects and trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** | Hiring additional bilingual caseworkers and dedicated accommodation specialists (plus rebalancing the 3 overloaded regional officers) directly attacks the 3.8d and 4.7d specialist queue bottlenecks. | Budgetary cost; recruitment delays for certified bilingual/disability staff; temporary dip in consistency or quality during onboarding if training isn't standardized. |
| **2. Establish service-level targets (SLAs) for support steps** | Setting explicit wait-time thresholds for `Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review` makes hidden bottlenecks visible to management and prioritizes resource deployment. | **Goodhart's Law risk:** Staff pressured by strict timers may rush interpreter calls or provide superficial, cookie-cutter disability accommodations rather than thorough individualized support. Pair SLAs with quality audits. |
| **3. Redesign routing to reduce handovers and role conflicts** | Assign bilingual and accommodation-certified workers **end-to-end case ownership** (eliminating `generalist  specialist  generalist` ping-pong), decouple routine `Accommodation Review` from automatic `Supervisor Review`, and separate the 2 dual-hatted specialists from supervisory sign-off on their own queue. | Risk of siloing or burning out specialized workers if total caseload weights aren't reduced; removing automatic supervisor review could increase undetected errors unless replaced by targeted quality sampling. |
| **4. Improve intake accessibility and decision explanations** | Co-design translated, plain-language, screen-reader-accessible forms, missing-document checklists, and denial notices explaining exact evidentiary gaps and appeal rights. | Upfront translation and IT redesign costs; clearer denial notices will likely **increase `appeal_flag` rates in the short term** as previously confused applicants exercise their rights, temporarily increasing `Appeal Review` workload. |
| **5. Institute continuous monitoring of adjusted and unadjusted disparities** | Track raw and Tier-1/Tier-2 adjusted disparities in approvals, queue waits, missing-doc requests, and reopens across groups and regions on routine operational dashboards. | Risk of **"adjustment washing"**—leadership may dismiss real problems if contaminated controls shrink the adjusted metric, or staff may game intermediate flags (e.g., avoiding logging formal doc requests while asking informally). |
| **6. Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` criteria** | Test `risk_score_band` for predictive parity and false-positive rates across groups, decouple benign `prior_case_count`/rework history from fraud triggers, and standardize what constitutes a "missing document." | Narrowing fraud triggers could temporarily lower fraud detection sensitivity during recalibration or shift volume back into standard eligibility queues, requiring workload rebalancing. |

slow_accomm_support = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(

# -------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Organizational Mining: Handovers, Collaboration, Similarity & Roles

for grp in ["Group A", "Group B", "Group C"]:

    # Detect ping-pong handovers (generalist -> specialist -> generalist)
