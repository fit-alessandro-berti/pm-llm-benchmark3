# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Board  
**From:** Operations and Process Analytics  
**Subject:** Recommendation on Redesign R — conditional hybrid rollout, not national scaling

**Recommendation:** Approve **Alternative E — hybrid deployment** for the next 8–10 weeks. Do not scale Redesign R nationally (A), and do not fully roll it back (B). Keep the coordinator and merged validate/schedule steps only for simple, low-risk houses, suspend the other two Redesign R rules for higher-risk segments, and run a controlled experiment before any wider rollout.

The trade-off is segment-specific. Median cycle time improved from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. For simple low-risk houses — 34% of pilot cases — median duration fell 6.0 days, p90 fell 8.0 days, and first-time-right rose 3 percentage points. That gain should be preserved.

But averages conceal concentrated harm. Overall p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days; first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%; complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%; second visits and manual activation roughly doubled in share. Apartments and old, permit-sensitive buildings drive the tail, with p90 increases of 12 and 25 days respectively. Process-mining aligns with this mechanism: pilot fitness is 0.94 but precision only 0.51, and conformance shows frequent billing before successful activation, repeat installation without survey, and missing permit checks. The apparent cost saving, EUR 184 to 161, excludes downstream complaint handling after week 4; the fuller estimate already reverses to EUR 198 to 207.

Coordinator ownership also creates fragility: utilization reached 93% in two regions, the working-together network centralised around coordinators and activation specialists, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent.

Therefore: **pause** predictive survey-skipping for medium/high-risk, apartment and old buildings, and **pause** billing before successful activation; **scale** coordination only where benefit is proven; add permit, survey and activation gates for other segments; and measure full 60-day cost and segment-specific tail outcomes under controlled conditions. Broader rollout should require explicit gates on tail duration, first-time-right, complaints, activation compliance and full cost — not median speed alone.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision:** Fitness asks whether observed pilot traces can be replayed by the discovered model; precision asks whether the model also allows too many unobserved behaviours. The pilot majority follows a simplified coordinator path, so fitness rises to 0.94. Simultaneously, skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, billing start and complaint can combine in many ways, so the model over-permits. A loose model can therefore fit the log well while being poor at constraining unsafe variants — exactly the conformance concern. Lower generalisation (0.56) further warns that this model may not represent unseen or wider-scale behaviour.

**Improved median but worse tail:** The median describes the typical case, increasingly a simple house benefiting from merged validation/scheduling and direct schedulinginstallation. The p90/p95 describe the worst decile. Those are disproportionately apartments, old buildings and other complex cases where skipped survey, missing permit check, failed visit or manual activation creates repeats and long waits. Polarisation — faster simple cases, slower complex cases — plus higher coordinator/second-visit queueing and a pilot mix with more complex buildings can produce both patterns at once.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Handover count measures transfers, not redundancy. A single coordinator reduces transfers and ambiguity, but concentrates tacit context in one person and one queue. At 93% utilisation there is little slack. The handover network looks simpler, while the working-together network becomes centralised around coordinators and activation specialists. Hence accountability improves in normal conditions, but absence, overload or specialist bottlenecks stall cases.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost:** Removing or deferring survey and activation work reduces cost counted before complaint handling. If the skipped work was needed, cost reappears later as failed visits, second visits, manual activation, complaint handling and billing disputes — some after the measurement cut-off. Resource data fit this: survey-team load falls while second-visit load rises to 92%. Visible saving and downstream increase are therefore compatible; the net effect is unresolved until full follow-up cost is measured.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** Redesign R’s rules fit simple, low-risk cases: validation/scheduling can be merged, survey risk is genuinely low, and direct installation succeeds. For apartments and old buildings, hidden complexity is higher: access, structure, permits and signal quality make the predictive skip rule more likely to be wrong and permit checks more important. The same coordinator model also imposes extra coordination load without the survey/permit information needed for those cases. Segment, temporal and alignment findings all point to heterogeneous treatment effects rather than a uniform process improvement.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally**  
Advantages: fastest capture of median and handover gains; one process; simple communication.  
Risks: scales tail delays, repeat visits, complaints and billing-before-activation to all segments; coordinator capacity and activation-specialist bottlenecks would be nationalised; low-precision variants become routine; full cost may exceed baseline.  
Reasonable only if: tail, FTR, complaint and activation-compliance gates are fixed first, coordinator capacity is proven below safe utilisation, and full downstream cost is shown to be no worse than baseline. Those conditions are not met.

**B. Roll back to baseline process**  
Advantages: restores the higher-precision baseline model, survey/permit discipline, activation-before-billing sequence and known FTR/complaint levels.  
Risks: discards proven simple-house gains (6d median, 8d p90), reintroduces handovers and accountability problems, and loses learning from the pilot.  
Reasonable if: harm were universal, activation/permit violations were uncontrollable, or simple-house benefit disappeared after mix adjustment. Current segment evidence does not support full rollback.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**  
Advantages: preserves accountability and merged scheduling while removing the two riskiest rules; simpler than segment-specific routing; directly addresses billing-before-activation and wrong survey skips.  
Risks: coordinator bottleneck and absence fragility remain; benefit for medium/high-risk segments uncertain; merged task may still need gating; second-visit capacity may stay high.  
Reasonable if: coordinator caseload caps, backup ownership and shared case context are implemented, and complex segments retain mandatory survey/permit/activation gates. This is a strong core option, but needs segment controls and measurement to become safe.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**  
Advantages: best causal identification of survey-skip, coordinator-model and segment effects; can test thresholds before irreversible scaling; improves risk-rule validity.  
Risks: delays benefits; experimental cost and complexity; if risky arms continue, customers in pilot regions continue to experience billing/permit harm; results may arrive too late for operational pressure.  
Reasonable if: the experiment excludes known unsafe practices (billing before activation, missing permit checks), is time-boxed, and is paired with immediate guardrails for non-experimental cases.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and measure before wider scale**  
Advantages: captures proven benefit where it exists, confines tail/complaint risk, preserves option value, and allows segment-by-segment learning. It combines C’s removal of risky rules with D’s measurement discipline.  
Risks: two process variants; misclassification if building-risk data/attributes are incomplete; routing complexity; possible perceived inequity if simple houses get faster service.  
Reasonable now because: segment effects are strong and consistent, the risky rules are pausable without losing all pilot gains, and controls can be implemented as workflow gates. It is the most defensible option under uncertainty.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision:** Approve **Alternative E (hybrid)**, implemented with the core safeguards of C and the measurement discipline of D. Reject national scaling A at this time; reject full rollback B.

**Immediate action:**  
Within the next 8–10 weeks, freeze national rollout, route only validated simple low-risk houses to Redesign R, and place workflow hard-stops: no billing start before successful activation test, and no installation for permit-sensitive buildings without a recorded permit check. Cap coordinator caseloads, introduce a named backup owner and shared case-context notes, and review weekly segment-level p90, FTR, complaints and conformance violations.

**What to pause:**  
Pause predictive field-survey skipping for apartments, medium/high building risk and old/permit-sensitive buildings. Pause moving activation checks after billing start for all segments except, if legally/operationally permitted, a tightly monitored simple-house exception with zero tolerance for billing before successful activation. Pause any expansion of manual activation as a routine path.

**What to scale:**  
Scale only the proven elements for simple low-risk houses: single coordinator after address validation and merged validate/schedule, with mandatory survey/permit/activation gates retained for all other segments. Preserve coordinator accountability, but add backup coverage to address the 93% utilisation fragility.

**What to measure next:**  
Start M7 immediately; run M4 for a full 60-day downstream cost; validate the risk rule with M6 before any further survey-skip testing; then run M1 by risk stratum. Add M3 reason codes and M8 split customer-experience measures. Instrument M2 start timestamps for installation/activation/manual activation, and test M5 absence resilience before increasing coordinator caseloads. Report results by segment, not only overall medians.

**Threshold / evidence that would justify broader rollout:**  
Broader rollout should proceed segment-by-segment only if, over a controlled period of at least 6–8 weeks with segment completeness 95%: (1) billing before successful activation is effectively zero and permit-check compliance for old buildings is 100%; (2) segment p90 is no worse than mix-adjusted baseline and p95 does not deteriorate; (3) FTR is 82% overall with no segment drop >2pp; (4) 30-day complaints are 6.5% and NPS is at least baseline; (5) full 60-day cost including revisits/complaints is  baseline EUR 198; (6) manual activation and second-visit rates return close to baseline; and (7) coordinator utilisation is sustained 85% with a passed absence/backup test. Randomised survey-skip evidence of no tail/FTR harm in a stratum is additionally required before reinstating skipping there. If these gates fail for a segment, keep the pause or roll that segment back to baseline while retaining R for simple low-risk houses that continue to pass.
