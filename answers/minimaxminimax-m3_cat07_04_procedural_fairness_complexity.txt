# Procedural Fairness Assessment: Public-Benefits Case Management

## Key Insight
The adjusted model reveals disparities that persist *after* controlling for documented complexity. These residuals—particularly around language support and accommodation review—warrant close procedural-fairness scrutiny, as they may reflect how the system handles rights-preserving steps rather than applicant qualifications themselves.

---

## 1. Fairness dimensions

**Outcome parity (raw)**
Raw approval rates differ by 20 percentage points (78% vs. 58%). This figure alone is uninformative because the groups also differ in measured risk, documentation completeness, prior-case history, and accommodation needs. Raw parity is the wrong target when the populations are not comparable.

**Adjusted outcome disparity**
After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, all three minority groups still show lower approval probability (2.2 to 5.5 pp) and longer cycle times (+0.9 to +4.1 days). The adjusted gap is smaller than the raw gap but not eliminated, which is the more relevant signal.

**Procedural burden**
Groups B, C, and D experience more handovers, more frequent Request Missing Documents loops, and more frequent Supervisor Review. Burden-asymmetry is itself a fairness concern: even if the eventual decision is correct, the applicant's path through the process is materially harder.

**Timeliness**
Median cycle times are 8.1, 13.7, 15.9, and 12.1 days. After adjustment, Group C still carries +4.1 days and Group B +2.6 days. Importantly, the slow segments sit *inside* support steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review), so the delay is co-located with rights-preserving activity.

**Consistency of routing**
Routing differs by group in ways that are partially explained by legitimate rules (high risk  Fraud Review; accommodation requested  Accommodation Review; minority language  Language Support Contact). However, *how* those routes are executed (queue depth, handover count, who picks up the work) is inconsistent. A rule is fair; an inconsistent implementation may not be.

**Quality/accessibility of support**
Only 7 bilingual caseworkers serve Group B, and only 5 accommodation specialists serve Group C (two of whom also perform Supervisor Review). Capacity is thin relative to demand: 57% of Group B needs language support, 62% of Group C needs accommodation review. Thin capacity is a structural access barrier.

**Appeal and rework burden**
Appeal rates (6% / 13% / 10% / 16%) and reopen/rework rates (11% / 22% / 24% / 20%) are higher for Groups B, C, D. This can reflect either worse decisions or a more rigorous (or more adversarial) initial review; the data do not by themselves distinguish those interpretations.

**Resource-allocation fairness**
Workload is concentrated: Group B leans on 7 bilingual + 3 overloaded officers; Group C leans on 5 specialists with overlapping roles. Group A is distributed across 52 caseworkers. Concentration in under-resourced pools is a fairness risk because wait times inherit queue depth, and queue depth is not the applicant's fault.

---

## 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate four things: (a) legitimate complexity-driven disparity, (b) disparate treatment unrelated to need, (c) disparate *impact* from neutral-sounding rules, and (d) uncertainty from measurement error.

**Matching or stratification variables**
- Stratifiers: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month, language_preference, disability_accommodation_requested.
- Use propensity-score stratification or exact matching on (region × channel × month) before computing within-stratum group gaps. This removes local-policy confounds.

**Path/variant comparison**
- Compute the share of each variant V1–V6 *within* each matched stratum, not in aggregate. If Group B's share of V4 (Language Support path) is higher in every stratum, that is structural, not compositional.
- Compare the *duration of each edge* (e.g., Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment) for matched cases only.

**Resource-assignment comparison**
- For each group, build the handover-of-work network and the working-together network. Compare median handover count per case, not just activity counts.
- Compute resource-similarity and role-discovery outputs to test whether the same individuals perform the same role across groups (they should, by policy).

**Waiting-time decomposition**
- Use `insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `get_service_waiting_time` to separate *queue wait* from *active processing*.
- Decompose total cycle time into (i) wait before Language Support / Accommodation Review, (ii) active support time, (iii) wait after, (iv) downstream processing. The question is *which segment* drives the residual gap.

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates**
- Re-run adjusted models treating risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as suspect:
  - Replace risk_score_band with a blinded subset (only non-derived inputs).
  - Re-define documentation_completeness as "complete on second request" to absorb language-barrier effects.
  - Report how much of the residual gap changes. If it shrinks, the covariate was a proxy for group status.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel**
- Run the adjusted model *within* each region and *within* each channel. Look for region-channel cells where the gap is unusually large or where Group B or C experiences longer Supervisor Review queues. Two regions already show Group B handover inflation; that is the place to look first.

## 3. Path and resource disparities

**Variant V4 (Language Support path)** is the most striking signal. 24% of Group B follows it; only 1–3% of other groups do. Its 19.2-day median is more than three times V1's 5.8 days. The fact that this path exists at all is policy-compliant; the fact that it is so much slower is the concern. With only 7 bilingual caseworkers and a 3.8-day wait inside this step, the bottleneck is capacity, not applicant behavior.

**Variant V5 (Accommodation path)** is the Group C equivalent: 31% of Group C, 21.6-day median, 4.7-day Accommodation Review queue. Two of the five specialists also perform Supervisor Review, creating a structural conflict between speed and review.

**Variant V3 (Fraud Review path)** is 28% of Group D versus 11% elsewhere. The 18.4-day median is partially explained by Group D's 51% high-risk share, which is a legitimate trigger. However, the elevated appeal rate (16% adjusted +5.7 pp) raises the question of whether Fraud Review is over-triggered or whether decisions in that branch are less stable.

**Variant V2 (Request Missing Documents path)** is elevated for Groups B, C, and D (34%, 29%, 26% vs. 24%). The +9.5 pp adjusted probability for Group B in "Request Missing Documents" is the largest process-step disparity in the table. If the request is legitimate, it is fine; if incomplete documentation is partly a language-barrier artifact, the request is a procedural tax on Group B.

**Handover count** matters: Group B cases bounce general  bilingual  general; Group C cases bounce general  specialist  supervisor. Each handover is a queue join and a potential information-loss point.

**Justified by complexity:** Fraud Review for high-risk applicants, Accommodation Review for accommodation requests, and Language Support Contact for minority-language applicants are policy-required. Their *existence* is legitimate.

**Concerning after adjustment:** The persistent +2.0 to +8.0 pp elevation in Supervisor Review, the +9.5 pp elevation in Request Missing Documents for Group B, the cycle-time penalty concentrated inside support steps, and the thin specialist capacity are not explained by case mix. They point to implementation-level unfairness, not policy-level unfairness.

---

## 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Legitimate mechanisms**
1. **Higher measured risk  Fraud Review routing.** Group D's 51% high-risk share triggers the longest mandatory branch. This is rule-based and likely appropriate, though see Section 5 on score bias.
2. **Higher documentation gaps  Request Missing Documents.** Group B's 46% incomplete-documentation share creates more loops. Some of this is genuine missing paperwork.
3. **Required support steps add wall-clock time.** Language Support and Accommodation Review are real activities with real processing time.

**Concerning mechanisms**
4. **Thin capacity for rights-preserving steps.** 7 bilingual workers, 5 accommodation specialists, and 2 of those 5 also doing supervisor review means the system structurally under-serves the populations that need it most. This converts a right into a delay.
5. **Documentation requests as procedural tax.** If "incomplete documentation" partly reflects language barriers (forms in majority language, instructions not translated), then requesting more documents is asking the applicant to overcome a system-side obstacle. Group B's +9.5 pp is consistent with this.
6. **Specialist overload creating cross-subsidy.** When two accommodation specialists also perform Supervisor Review, the queue for Accommodation Review grows because their time is contested. This is a *resource allocation* problem, not an applicant problem.
7. **Supervisor Review as a probabilistic second look.** The +8.0 pp for Group B and +5.5 pp for Group C could reflect either prudent oversight (legitimate) or a less-trusting default toward minority or accommodation-flagged cases (concerning). The data alone cannot distinguish these; an audit of review reasons is needed.
8. **Score feedback loop.** If risk_score_band is informed by prior-case count, and prior-case count is higher for groups that have been re-assessed more often, the "complexity control" partially absorbs the very disparity it is meant to remove.

---

## 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in covariates**
- `documentation_completeness` is observed as the caseworker's assessment. If the assessor shares a language with Group A but not Group B, the standard of "complete" may be applied unevenly.
- `risk_score_band` is described as "measured," which usually means model-derived. If the model uses prior-case counts or zip-code proxies, it is encoding historical access patterns, not applicant risk per se.
- `prior_case_count` is itself an outcome of past processing, not a fixed trait. Using it as a complexity control is partially circular.

**Missing applicant needs**
- The log captures *accommodation requested* and *language preference* but not, for example, literacy level, digital access, caregiver availability, or whether the applicant understood the initial notification. These unobserved needs plausibly correlate with group membership and affect both effort and outcome.

**Unobserved resource workload**
- Resource data is daily granularity, so within-day queue depth, idle time, and escalation pressure are invisible. A specialist who appears "available" may be saturated.
- The handover networks show *who* hands to *whom*, not *why* (escalation, leave coverage, specialization). Without the "why," handover counts are ambiguous signals.

**Appeal quality, not just appeal count**
- Appeal rate and reopen rate are observed; appeal merit and explanation quality are not. A higher appeal rate can mean either worse decisions *or* a more usable appeal process. The data do not tell us which.
- `case_outcome` captures approval/denial, not partial approvals, time-bounded benefits, or the clarity of the decision rationale.

**Historical bias in risk scores**
- If `risk_score_band` was trained on historical data in which Group D was over-surveilled (because they were over-routed to Fraud Review), the score is partly self-confirming. Adjusting for it can mask rather than reveal disparate impact.

**Selection into channels**
- Channel (online vs. in-person) is endogenous: applicants choose the channel that is accessible to them. Treating channel as a pure complexity control risks absorbing accessibility disparities into "case mix."

## 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**Add capacity for language support and accommodation review**
Hire more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; reduce or eliminate dual-role assignments (specialist + supervisor).
- *Side effects:* hiring takes time and budget; if volume is seasonal, capacity may sit idle in low months. A cost-sharing arrangement across regions can reduce per-region idle capacity.

**Service-level targets for support steps**
Add separate timeliness thresholds for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review (e.g., 80% within 2 days), with public reporting.
- *Side effects:* gaming risk (caseworkers may mark "support started" prematurely to hit the clock). Mitigate by measuring *completion*, not start, of the support step.

**Routing redesign to reduce handovers**
Allow the bilingual caseworker to complete the case end-to-end rather than handing back to a generalist. Same for accommodation specialists.
- *Side effects:* creates single points of failure if a specialist is absent; may require cross-training so generalists can also serve these cases. Address with cross-training and clear escalation paths.

**Decision-explanation improvements**
Standardize decision letters: include the specific eligibility criteria, the evidence considered, the steps to appeal, and the contact for language/accommodation support.
- *Side effects:* may increase appeal volume short-term (more people understand they can appeal) before reducing it (fewer "I didn't understand" appeals). Net effect on cycle time is ambiguous; quality-of-treatment effect is likely positive.

**Monitoring adjusted disparities**
Publish quarterly adjusted gap reports by group, with confidence intervals, alongside raw outcomes.
- *Side effects:* may be gamed (caseworkers avoid borderline Group B/C cases to protect their unit's numbers). Mitigate by tying metrics to *cycle time* and *explanation quality*, not approval rates.

**Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**
Periodic fairness audit of the risk model: test for disparate error rates across groups. Re-define documentation completeness with a language-adjusted standard (e.g., "complete once translated instructions were provided").
- *Side effects:* audit findings may force model retraining or replacement, which has its own validation burden. Replacing the documentation standard is politically sensitive; communicate it as "we are removing a barrier," not "we are lowering the bar."

## 7. PM4Py analysis plan

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# 1. Load and format
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    pd.read_csv("events.csv"),
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"

# 2. Add service/waiting time
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df, timestamp_key="time:timestamp")
service = pm4py.get_service_time(df)        # active processing per activity

# 3. Variant discovery, stratified by applicant group
variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)
for group in ["A", "B", "C", "D"]:
    sub = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        df, "applicant_group", [group]
    )
    v_sub = pm4py.get_variants(sub)
    # compare top variants across groups; flag V4/V5 concentration

# 4. Filter to the slow support paths for forensic analysis
lang_path = pm4py.filter_variants(
    df, ["Submit Application", "Identity Verification",
         "Language Support Contact", "Completeness Check",
         "Request Missing Documents", "Eligibility Assessment",
         "Decision Draft"],
    activity_key="concept:name",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    positive=True
)
acc_path = pm4py.filter_variants(
    df, ["Submit Application", "Identity Verification",
         "Accommodation Review", "Eligibility Assessment",
         "Supervisor Review", "Decision Draft",
         "Applicant Notification"],
    positive=True
)

# 5. Performance DFG to see where time is spent
perf_dfg, sa, ea = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)
# inspect edges: 'Language Support Contact' -> 'Eligibility Assessment',
# 'Accommodation Review' -> 'Eligibility Assessment',
# 'Supervisor Review' -> 'Decision Draft'

# 6. Path performance for the bottleneck edges
slow_edges = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    df, min_performance=2.0, max_performance=10.0
)

# 7. Handover and collaboration networks
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df, resource_key="org:resource")
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df)
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df)
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df)

# 8. Per-group and per-resource waiting time
for group in ["A", "B", "C", "D"]:
    g = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(df, "applicant_group", [group])
    pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(g, timestamp_key="time:timestamp")

# 9. Filter slow cases (right tail) and inspect
slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    df, max_performance=30.0
)

# 10. Declarative / log-skeleton to test procedural rules
skel = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(df)
# 'Accommodation Review' should imply a prior 'disability_accommodation_requested'
declare_model = pm4py.discover_declare(df)
```

**What each step answers:**
- Variants  which paths are group-concentrated.
- Performance DFG  which edges carry the wait.
- Handover network  whether Group B/C cases have more hops.
- Service vs. waiting decomposition  whether the delay is queue or active work.
- Declarative model  whether support steps are skipped when required (a procedural violation) or imposed when not needed.

## 8. Bottom-line assessment

**The process is procedurally unfair in its current implementation, though not in its stated policy.** The policy requires language support and accommodation review; the implementation under-resources both, so that exercising a right imposes a procedural cost. After adjustment for documented complexity, Groups B, C, and D still face longer cycle times, more handovers, more missing-document requests, and more supervisor reviews than Group A. The largest residuals cluster inside the rights-preserving support steps, which is the strongest signal that the unfairness is structural rather than applicant-driven.

**Caveats that prevent a stronger conclusion:**
- The adjusted model controls for variables that may themselves be biased (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, prior_case_count). If those proxies absorb real disparities, the true adjusted gap is larger than reported.
- The data do not capture appeal quality, decision-explanation quality, or applicant-side experience. A process that is procedurally fair on paper can still be experienced as unfair.
- Some of the elevated rework and appeal rates for Groups B, C, D may reflect prudent extra scrutiny rather than worse decisions; the data do not distinguish these interpretations.

**The right next step** is not to remove support steps, nor to declare the system discriminatory, but to (a) re-run the adjusted analysis with suspect covariates softened, (b) audit the support-step queues and the specialist dual-role assignments, and (c) publish adjusted gap metrics alongside capacity and timeliness metrics. The evidence supports saying that *the system as currently resourced* imposes uneven procedural burden; it does not yet support saying the system *as designed* is discriminatory. The distinction matters for what you fix.
