## 1. Stress scenarios

The redesign should not be sized to the historical average. Normal demand is approximately **2,300 cases/week**, versus **3,300/week at peak**, but the mix changes substantially:

- Routine: approximately **1,403  1,386 cases/week**, essentially unchanged.
- Urgent: approximately **414  957/week**, a **2.3-fold increase**.
- Complex-panel: approximately **299  660/week**, a **2.2-fold increase**.
- Repeat-after-failure: approximately **184  297/week**, about **1.6-fold**, with disproportionately high assay consumption.

The following are stress tests, not forecasts. Where I specify a severity, it is an explicit planning assumption rather than an observed estimate.

| Scenario | Demand and disruption assumptions | Main stress and plausible failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| **S1. Winter infection wave: Urgent and Complex-panel growth** | Retain approximately 3,300 cases/week and the peak mix: 42% Routine, 29% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel, 9% Repeat-after-failure. No additional machine outage. | Preparation batching delays Urgent work; strict Urgent-first dispatch moves queues into technical and medical validation. Complex-panel growth disproportionately increases specialist and medical-validator demand. |
| **S2. M2 degradation or outage during the winter wave** | S1 demand, plus a sustained 20% loss of M2 effective throughput—for example, downtime or performance degradation. This is a planning assumption, not a measured capacity estimate. | M1 absorbs displaced work; routine batching fragments further; increased M2 use if work is simply moved there can raise repeats; technical validation becomes the next saturated resource. |
| **S3. Medical-validator or specialist absence** | S1 demand, with one qualified medical validator absent, or the specialist unavailable on the recurring Friday. The absence is known sufficiently far in advance to adjust the roster. | Technical validation cannot simply transfer all medical work elsewhere. Medical queues, specialist holds, and then technical queues grow; routing around medical validation may produce complete technical work that cannot be released. |
| **S4. Courier compression with delayed collections** | S1 demand, with 50% of daily arrivals compressed into two two-hour windows, around the existing 10:00/17:00 peaks, plus a recurring late collection of up to four hours. The compression percentage is illustrative. | Registration and receipt bunching overload pre-analytical checking. If an Urgent specimen is delayed or requires recollection, an 18-hour end-to-end target becomes difficult; registration automation does not remove the physical sample-handling constraint. |
| **S5. Post-night repeat-failure wave** | Illustrative mix: 35% Routine, 25% Urgent, 25% Complex-panel, 15% Repeat-after-failure, with 20% of first-run assay exits entering repeat rather than the observed peak proportion of about 15.5%. **Acceptance should be case-specific.** The agreed Urgent release target is 18 hours and the Routine target is 72 hours. The data already show an Urgent p90 of **19 hours normally and 33 hours at peak**, so the current process is not reliably meeting the Urgent requirement even before a severe outage. Complex-panel and repeat cases require clinically agreed limits; their normal medians of 64 and 70 hours are reference points, not invented contractual targets.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### Evidence cautions before interpreting the logs

- The DFGs are flattened by order and do not identify case type, tube count, parallel panel assays, or resource assignment. Consequently, the stated **3.1-fold likelihood of medical validation** and **2.4-fold likelihood of specialist review** cannot be converted directly into exact resource-hour requirements. They do establish that panel work is disproportionately clinical.
- The Urgent bypass percentages—74% normal and 58% peak—do not have a stated denominator in the event log. They are evidence of changed routing behavior, not a reliable estimate of each Urgent case’s probability of bypassing batching.
- The performance edges are local delay signatures, not additive case-cycle predictions. For example, a 5-hour Medical validation  Release edge does not imply that five hours of additional validator capacity fixes a 22-hour queue.
- There is cohort incompleteness. At peak, 9,900 orders enter and 8,040 reach Release, while only 540 follow the shown cancellation paths. Thus, **1,320 orders are not accounted for by the displayed terminal paths**. They may be open, outside the observation window, or otherwise censored; they should not automatically be classified as lost.

### S1. Urgent and Complex-panel growth: bottlenecks migrate downstream

**Likely sequence**

1. Courier intake and pre-analytical checking are already near saturation: **88% and 96% peak utilization**, with **7.5-hour and 9.5-hour median queues**.
2. Preparation reaches **89% utilization and an 11-hour queue**. Batching accounts for approximately **12.7% of preparation exits**, versus 4.6% normally, and Batch wait  Assay run rises from **9 to 22 hours**.
3. Urgent exceptions compete with large batches and parallel panel assays at the machines. Pooled machine utilization reaches **98%**, with an **18-hour queue**.
4. Work accumulates at technical and medical validation, especially for panels and Urgent cases. Technical validators are already at **101% utilization**; medical validators rise from **58% to 93%**, with the queue increasing from **2 to 22 hours**.

**Case-type and routing evidence**

Technical-validation outputs to medical validation increase from **24.5% to 47.2%** of technical-validation entries, while Hold for specialist review increases from **1.8% to 8.4%**. More than half of peak technical-validation entries now take one of those clinical routes. Complex-panel orders are much more likely to need both.

**Where relief moves the bottleneck**

- **I1, if implemented as strict Urgent-first**, can empty Urgent machine queues while increasing the age of Routine and panel work. The resulting work-in-process may reappear at technical validation or specialist review.
- **I2, larger Routine batches**, improves setup efficiency but lengthens the wait for an Urgent sample that enters a batch just before processing. Urgent bypass behavior also becomes less dependable during disruption: the observed association falls from 74% to 58%.
- Adding preparation or machine capacity alone would expose the already committed technical and medical pools as the next constraint.

### S2. M2 degradation: apparent machine relief can increase rework

**Likely sequence**

1. M2’s reduced effective capacity lengthens the combined machine queue. Urgent exceptions and M1’s reserved work become harder to insert.
2. Displaced work is batched upstream at preparation, where queues were already 11 hours, rather than immediately disappearing.
3. If Routine work is automatically shifted onto degraded M2, first-run failures can rise. Repeat-assay entries are already approximately **155 per 1,000 first-assay exits at peak**, versus 69 normally.
4. Additional successful repeats then arrive at technical validation, adding both repeat and post-repeat review work.

**Why static I3 is risky**

I3 would leave M2 to absorb Routine volume despite its higher failure rate. The supplied evidence does not establish either machine’s individual capacity or failure-adjusted throughput, so a permanent M1/M2 split is not justified. The aggregate 98% utilization cannot tell us which machine has usable reserve.

**Migration risk**

Relieving M2 through a temporary M1 fallback can move congestion into preparation, technical validation, or M1 itself. Bypassing batch wait more frequently can also fragment schedules; machine availability alone does not measure the cost of that fragmentation.

### S3. Medical or specialist absence: clinical work becomes the dominant constraint

**Likely sequence**

1. A medical-validator absence directly affects the pool already at **93% utilization**, with a **22-hour median queue**.
2. Specialist absence affects the pool at **87% utilization with a 36-hour queue**; the Hold for specialist review  Medical validation edge rises from **18 to 48 hours**.
3. Some technical work is diverted toward direct release to preserve urgent capacity. This can reduce immediate medical intake, but at peak **56% of technical-validation entries already go directly to Release**, and 47% go to medical validation. Diverting additional work is therefore limited.
4. If assays continue to finish, their results wait as complete technical packets. Technical validation can become the next visible bottleneck.

**Why I4 and I5 are conditional rather than complete solutions**

- I4 does not replace medical or specialist qualifications. Moving two technical validators into accession correction while technical validation is already observed at 101% utilization can worsen the downstream clinical queue.
- I5 provides useful hours only when appropriately validated results are ready. It cannot release an assay that has not run or a technical packet that has not been reviewed. An evening shift can overload technical validation if downstream readiness is not checked first.

### S4. Courier compression and delay: upstream relief exposes pre-analytical work

**Likely sequence**

1. The intake pools are already at **85–88% utilization**. Two arrival windows create short registration and receipt surges.
2. Register order  Receive sample rises from **5 to 8 hours**, and Receive sample  Pre-analytical check rises from **2 to 9 hours**.
3. A delayed collection adds elapsed time before any laboratory work begins. If a recollection is required, Request recollection  Receive sample is already **42–60 hours**.
4. Clerks handling recollection explanations and exceptions can be pulled away from routine receipt, extending the intake loop.

**Where relief moves the bottleneck**

- **I7** can smooth the physical arrival profile, but simply renaming the 10:00 and 17:00 slots without reducing their concentration will not help.
- **I8** removes registration effort only for orders whose metadata is complete and correct. The sample still has to be physically received, checked, and prepared. With pre-analytical checking at 96%, automation can expose that constraint.
- **I6** can stop invalid work earlier, but false or premature triage decisions generate additional recollection work. Recollection is not a free capacity release.

### S5. Repeat-failure wave: the same case consumes more machine capacity

**Likely sequence**

1. Failure-driven repeats compete with new Urgent and panel assays.
2. Preparation enters Batch wait, and the Repeat assay  Assay run edge rises from **10 to 26 hours**. The work does not disappear after the first failure; it returns with additional technical-review requirements.
3. A policy that gives every repeat immediate priority can starve new Urgent cases. A policy that gives every new Urgent case priority indefinitely can build a repeat backlog.
4. A dedicated repeat lane can therefore transfer the queue to new urgent, complex, or technical-review work.

**Cross-scenario conclusion**

Peak rework rises from **7.5% to 11.8%**, while overall median cycle time deteriorates across every case type. These are different denominators from the assay-repeat proportions above, so they should not be added or treated as interchangeable. The redesign must control the **first-time-right rate and resource consumption by case type**, not merely the overall rework percentage.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The package below contains both always-on controls and capabilities that can be activated under defined conditions. It does **not** mean activating every proposed intervention simultaneously.

### A. Front-door quality and arrival control: I6, I7, and selective I8

- Establish an early pre-analytical triage that identifies specimen defects, missing items, or accession discrepancies within a short, controlled interval. “Triage within two hours” should mean a documented disposition, not an unsafe definitive rejection based on incomplete information.
- Provide a rapid correction/recollection path and notify the ordering source electronically. Track specimen adequacy and first-pass collection success so faster rejection is not mistaken for improved quality.
- Smooth courier arrivals across additional windows, with intake appointments or volume caps where sample stability permits.
- Automate registration only for verifiably complete digital orders. Incomplete records go to a small exception lane rather than creating silent errors.

**Why robust:** These measures improve first-time-right processing and reduce arrival bunching across demand peaks, machine outages, and courier disruption.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** Digital metadata is reliable; specimens can be handled safely when arrivals are smoothed; staff are competent to distinguish a correctable defect from an analytically valid specimen. Otherwise, I8 can move work to pre-analytical checking, and I6 can increase recollection and clerk workload.

### B. Class-aware, age-protected dispatch: revised I1, conditional I2, and no unconditional I3

Use a shared dispatch policy that considers:

- contractual or clinical due time;
- case type and expected downstream resource demand;
- current age and longest waiting work;
- machine condition and failure-adjusted throughput;
- progress of individual tubes and assays within a panel.

Provide **limited protected Urgent and panel capacity**, not an unlimited Urgent-first rule. Within each class, use oldest-ready-first and maximum-age safeguards, with a minimum Routine service floor. Unused reserved capacity should be released rather than left idle.

Increase batches from 12 to 24 **only off-peak or when no time-critical work is awaiting preparation and the batch has a known completion time**. A routine batch must not absorb an Urgent sample that is about to breach its release requirement.

M1/M2 routing should be dynamic and failure-aware. Test a limited M1 allocation for Urgent and panels, but do not permanently send all Routine work to the higher-failure machine.

**Why robust:** The same policy works during demand-mix changes and equipment degradation without depending on a historically average machine.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** It requires reliable case/tube status and workload forecasts. Too many protected Urgent slots can strand capacity; too few can breach the 18-hour target. A whole-order priority flag can also delay one assay in a partially completed panel unnecessarily.

### C. Flexible skills with protected backfill: I4

Cross-train the two technical validators for accession correction, but rotate them only with named technical-validation backfill. Maintain a skills matrix, minimum staffing by pool, and a four-week training lead time.

**Why robust:** This helps the 91%-utilized, two-person correction pool during intake and pre-analytical surges without assuming that technical validation has spare capacity.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** The correction work must be sufficiently standardized to cross-train safely. Without backfill, I4 simply exports the bottleneck into technical validation.

### D. Protected clinical capacity and validated direct release: I5 plus selective I9 replacement

Forecast Urgent and panel demand in **assay units, panel work, and medical-review units**, not just orders. Staff evening medical validation when the next-shift ready-work forecast exceeds available qualified capacity.

Use validated autovalation/direct-release rules for eligible results. Continue mandatory medical review for applicable Urgent, Complex-panel, failed, or otherwise excluded results.

**I9 should not be adopted as a blanket hold for Routine releases.** The log does not demonstrate that report batching is a major constraint, and holding clinically available results increases staleness and can mask downstream bottlenecks.

**Why robust:** Direct release removes unnecessary manual work while protected clinical capacity handles the cases that genuinely require it.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** Evening staffing is sustainable, and assays and technical packets will be ready. Otherwise, I5 creates an idle medical shift or increases queues elsewhere. Autovalidation must be case-specific and auditable, not a way to bypass required review.

### E. Repeat prevention and bounded failure capacity

Use a standard maintenance and quality-control response for M2, with explicit escalation after failures. Investigate night-shift clusters by instrument, assay, specimen source, and handling step.

Reserve a controlled share of machine capacity for failure recovery when repeat rates rise, rather than allowing either new work or repeats to wait indefinitely. Prebook repeat slots and return them to the normal pool when the repeat signal subsides.

**Why robust:** This addresses the actual repeat variant—night-shift and M2 clustering—without assuming that more machine capacity eliminates defects.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** A reserved lane can be misused as a general priority queue. The reservation must be time-limited and governed by a repeat-rate signal.

### F. Pre-qualified, narrow Complex-panel overflow: I10

Pre-validate a partner’s test menu, result-review arrangements, transport conditions, traceability, and return process. Activate overflow for appropriate Complex-panel work, not as a routine alternative to the local process.

If local medical validation remains mandatory, include that step in the partner’s expected turnaround and local workload. Treat transport, partner queue, and result return as separate process steps.

**Why robust:** Pre-qualification avoids improvising an external route during an outage.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** A 12-hour transport time alone does not establish total external turnaround. Referral is generally unsuitable for Urgent cases because the contractual window leaves too little time for testing and validation. Result or specimen discrepancies may create local rework and additional specialist work.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

The following are **initial operating thresholds**, not mathematically optimal cutoffs. They should be calibrated using case-level data from additional peak episodes. Policies should remain active until their backlog is cleared rather than toggling every few minutes.

| Policy | Turn-on trigger | Actions while active | Turn-off or safety condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1. Demand/mix surge: revised I1, I2, I3, and I5** | Next-day Urgent/panel workload exceeds **85% of qualified staffed capacity**; or Urgent/panel ready work exceeds available capacity for two consecutive periods; or Urgent and panel queue ages are rising together. | Protect limited Urgent/panel machine windows; prioritize ready panel assays rather than entire incomplete orders; activate evening medical validation only if downstream work will be ready; enlarge Routine batches only when no time-critical work is waiting. | Reduce temporary protections only when forecast load is below approximately **70% of capacity for two stable periods** and the class-specific backlog is aging down. Always retain a Routine age floor. |
| **P2. M2 degradation: failure-aware routing and recovery** | M2 effective throughput falls at least **20% for two hours** relative to a same-mix reference; a defined QC failure occurs; or M2’s machine queue exceeds 12 hours together with a rising failure-adjusted repeat rate. | Stop discretionary Routine allocation to M2; use tested M1 fallback capacity; prioritize valid Urgent and panel work within protected capacity; transfer suitable Routine or repeat assays only to a pre-qualified alternative. | Require stable machine performance and repeat behavior for two shifts, plus an M1 queue below the locally chosen recovery threshold. |
| **P3. Medical/specialist absence: protected roster and overflow** | Known absence; specialist hold arriving faster than available review hours; or sustained medical queue **8 hours** and specialist queue **12 hours**. | Publish ready-case age; use the evening shift only for ready packets; invoke eligible autovalidation; pre-book qualified panel overflow; do not transfer unqualified technical staff into medical review. | Continue until the queue is below the recovery band and the oldest ready work is progressing. A staffing change alone is insufficient if the ready-work backlog remains. |
| **P4. Courier bunching or delay: smoothing and intake triage** | A two-hour arrival forecast exceeds **1.5 times staffed receipt capacity for two windows**, or receipt/check median exceeds approximately four hours. | Activate additional courier windows with capacity caps; use selective I8; route complete registrations straight to receipt; triage Urgent and defective specimens; defer nonessential Routine batching rather than dumping work into pre-analytical checking. | Require arrivals to remain within approximately **1.2 times capacity across at least three windows for two days**, with receipt backlog cleared. Moving the old peaks by one hour is not sufficient. |
| **P5. Repeat-failure wave: bounded repeat lane and quality response** | First-run repeat entries exceed their case-mix-adjusted control limit, especially with a night-shift/M2 cluster. | Open controlled recovery slots; suspend unnecessary large batches; perform instrument and specimen-level cause analysis; route overflow only to validated alternatives. | Keep active until repeat rate returns below the control limit for two stable periods. Do not permanently reserve the lane if machines recover. |

These policies explicitly prevent all case classes from being prioritized simultaneously: Urgent work receives contractual protection, panel work receives clinical-age protection, and Routine work receives an age/service floor.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **The redesign can create its own Routine starvation.** Protected Urgent and panel slots may remain underused, or become excessive during demand shifts. Minimum-age rules and release of unused reservations are therefore essential.

2. **Cross-training can export the bottleneck.** If the two cross-trained validators are absent from technical validation, the clinical queue may remain short temporarily while complete technical packets accumulate.

3. **Machine redundancy remains limited.** Losing M1 as well as M2 would invalidate the protected-dispatch plan. A pre-qualified external route helps only work that fits its transport and turnaround envelope.

4. **Triage and automation can misclassify work.** Incomplete digital metadata can become an expensive front-end error; aggressive defect triage can increase the 42–60-hour recollection loop. Neither should be evaluated solely by cases processed per hour.

5. **WIP limits can conceal rather than solve congestion.** Work can move to couriers, uncontrolled staging areas, or partner queues. Upstream WIP and unresolved recollection requests must remain visible.

6. **Evening staffing can become an empty intervention.** Medical-validation hours have little value if assays, consumables, or technical review are unavailable. Sustainable scheduling and readiness gates are necessary.

7. **External overflow weakens end-to-end visibility.** The 12-hour transport delay, partner queue, local follow-up, and missing-result handling must all be measured. Dispatching a panel is not equivalent to releasing its result.

8. **The current evidence cannot support precise resource coefficients.** Because the log is flattened, changes in tubes, parallel assays, or case-specific routing may invalidate staffing estimates calibrated only on the two aggregate periods.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

The dashboard should use **case-entry cohorts**, distinguish orders from tubes/assays, and include unfinished cases. Completed-case cycle times alone will hide a growing backlog.

| Type | Leading indicator | Initial warning / critical band |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** | **Next-24-hour workload versus qualified staffed capacity**, split into Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure; convert panels to expected assay and medical-review units | **Warning:** >85% of capacity. **Critical:** >100%, or incoming ready work exceeds completable staffed work for two periods. The historical 29% Urgent and 20% panel mix are reference points, not universal capacity thresholds. |
| **Leading** | **Courier and pre-analytical flow**: arrivals per two-hour window, registration-to-receipt time, receipt-to-check time, and defect/recollection rate | **Warning:** arrivals >1.5 times staffed window capacity or receipt-to-check >2 hours. **Critical:** sustained arrival compression or receipt-to-check >6 hours. Investigate abnormal-specimen rates against case-mix-adjusted baselines, not a fixed historical average. |

A daily view is not sufficient for learning: periodically replay the winter-mix, M2-loss, clinical-absence, and courier-compression scenarios against the event log. This tests whether relieving one station genuinely reduces end-to-end congestion or merely moves queues and work-in-process downstream.
