Raw approval rates differ substantially (78% for Group A vs. 58% for Group D), but after adjusting for case complexity (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, etc.), disparities shrink to 2–5.5 percentage points. This suggests some—but not all—of the raw disparity reflects legitimate case-mix differences.

Even after adjustment, Groups B and D have lower approval probabilities than Group A. The adjusted differences (–5.5 pp for Group B, –3.1 pp for Group D) may reflect residual bias in covariates (e.g., risk_score_band encoding historical disparities) or unmeasured complexity (e.g., quality of documentation). The smaller gap for Group C (–2.2 pp) suggests accommodation needs are partially accounted for in the model.

Groups B and C experience higher rates of **Request Missing Documents** (+9.5 pp and +4.2 pp, respectively) and **Supervisor Review** (+8.0 pp and +5.5 pp). These steps add delays and administrative friction, even after controlling for documentation completeness. Group C’s **Accommodation Review** (31% of paths) and Group B’s **Language Support Contact** (24% of paths) introduce additional handovers and waiting times, creating procedural burden beyond what complexity alone would justify.

- Group B waits **3.8 days** for Language Support Contact  Eligibility (vs. no wait for Group A).

Routing appears **legitimate** for:

Groups B (13% appeal rate), C (10%), and D (16%) have higher appeal rates than Group A (6%), even after adjustment. This suggests:
- Group D’s appeals may reflect legitimate fraud concerns.

- Rework rates (20–24% for Groups B–D vs. 11% for Group A) indicate higher administrative burden, possibly due to handovers or documentation issues.

## Resource-allocation fairness
- **Group A** cases are evenly distributed across 52 caseworkers, minimizing individual workload.
- **Group B** cases are concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers + 3 overloaded regional officers, creating **asymmetric capacity**.
- **Group C** cases are funneled to 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also handle Supervisor Review, risking **role conflicts**.
- **Group D** cases are routed to Fraud Review as intended, but the **2.8-day wait** (vs. 2.2 for Group A) may reflect understaffing in the fraud queue.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

## Legitimate mechanisms
1. **Case complexity**: Group D’s higher risk_score_band legitimately triggers Fraud Review, increasing cycle time.
2. **Rights-preserving routing**: Groups B and C require Language/Accommodation Support, which introduces waits but is procedurally necessary.

## Concerning mechanisms
1. **Over-scrutiny of minority-language applicants**: Group B’s higher Request Missing Documents rate persists after controlling for documentation completeness, suggesting **implicit bias** in completeness checks.
2. **Bottlenecks in support steps**: Limited bilingual caseworkers (7) and accommodation specialists (5) create **unfair waits** for Groups B and C, even though these steps are rights-preserving.
3. **Role conflicts in accommodation review**: Specialists also performing Supervisor Review may **prioritize efficiency over accommodation needs**, delaying responses.
4. **Historical bias in risk_score_band**: If risk_score_band encodes past discrimination (e.g., higher scores for historically marginalized groups), Group D’s lower approval rates may reflect **legacy bias** rather than current risk.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

## Measurement bias
- **risk_score_band**: May encode historical disparities (e.g., if past denials disproportionately affected certain groups).
- **documentation_completeness**: Likely influenced by language barriers (Group B’s 46% incomplete vs. Group A’s 21%), but the metric may not distinguish between **genuine incompleteness** and **applicant effort**.
- **case_outcome**: Does not capture **quality of decision explanations** or **dignity of treatment**, which are critical for procedural fairness.

## Missing applicant needs
- **Unobserved disabilities**: Some applicants may need accommodations but not request them.
- **Digital literacy**: Online applicants (Group A) may face fewer barriers than in-person applicants (Group B), but this is not measured.
- **Mental health**: Complex cases may require additional support not captured in the log.

## Unobserved resource workload
- **Daily workload granularity** hides spikes in demand (e.g., a single overloaded supervisor causing delays).
- **Informal communication**: Handovers or decisions made outside the log (e.g., phone calls) are invisible.
- **Resource motivation**: Caseworkers may prioritize certain groups based on implicit biases.

## Appeal quality
- **Appeal_flag** does not indicate whether appeals are **legitimate** (e.g., due to procedural errors) or **frivolous**.
- **Reopen/rework rate** may reflect **applicant confusion** (e.g., unclear instructions) rather than fair process.

## Historical bias
- **risk_score_band** and **prior_case_count** may reflect **systemic barriers** (e.g., past denials due to language discrimination) rather than current merit.
- **Region effects**: Disparities may vary by local policies or cultural biases not captured in the log.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

## Option 1: Add capacity for language/accommodation support
- **Action**: Hire more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; cross-train general caseworkers.
- **Side effects**:
  - Short-term cost increase.
  - May require retraining existing staff.
  - Risk of **over-specialization** if roles become too rigid.

## Option 2: Service-level targets for support steps
- **Action**: Set **maximum wait times** (e.g., 24 hours for Language Support Contact, 48 hours for Accommodation Review) with penalties for violations.
- **Side effects**:
  - May require **hiring more staff** to meet targets.
  - Could lead to **rushed decisions** if targets are unrealistic.
  - May increase **administrative burden** on caseworkers.

## Option 3: Routing redesign to reduce handovers
- **Action**:
  - Assign **dedicated caseworkers** to minority-language or accommodation cases to minimize handovers.
  - Use **automated routing** to reduce delays in Language/Accommodation steps.
- **Side effects**:
  - May **reduce flexibility** in workload distribution.
  - Could **increase silos** if caseworkers specialize too narrowly.

## Option 4: Decision-explanation improvements
- **Action**:
  - Standardize **decision letters** to include clear explanations for denials.
  - Provide **multilingual explanations** and **plain-language summaries**.
  - Offer **appeal guidance** upfront to reduce rework.
- **Side effects**:
  - **Higher operational costs** for translation and training.
  - May **increase appeal volume** if applicants feel explanations are insufficient.

## Option 5: Monitor adjusted disparities
- **Action**:
  - Track **approval rates, cycle times, and appeal rates** by group after controlling for complexity.
  - Publish **transparency reports** on disparities.
- **Side effects**:
  - **Gaming the system**: Caseworkers may avoid high-complexity cases to improve metrics.
  - **Over-reliance on metrics**: May distract from qualitative fairness concerns.

## Option 6: Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness
- **Action**:
  - **Re-calibrate risk_score_band** to remove historical bias (e.g., using fairness-aware ML).
  - **Audit documentation checks** for minority-language applicants to reduce over-scrutiny.
- **Side effects**:
  - **False positives/negatives**: Risk of misclassifying legitimate fraud or genuine need.
  - **Resistance from caseworkers** if they perceive audits as micromanagement.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process exhibits **mixed procedural fairness**, with **some disparities justified by case complexity** and **others concerning**. Key findings:

1. **Legitimate routing** for Groups B (language support), C (accommodation), and D (fraud review) is generally appropriate, but **capacity constraints** (e.g., 7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists) create **unfair waits** (3.8–4.7 days) that disproportionately burden these groups.
2. **Over-scrutiny concerns** emerge for Group B, whose **Request Missing Documents rate** remains elevated (+9.5 pp) even after controlling for documentation completeness, suggesting **potential bias in completeness checks**.
3. **Handover burden** for Groups B and C introduces **administrative friction** and **delays**, increasing appeal/rework rates. The policy’s **rights-preserving steps** (language/accommodation support) are **necessary but under-resourced**.
4. **Residual disparities** in approval rates (–2.2 to –5.5 pp after adjustment) may reflect **unmeasured complexity** or **historical bias in risk_score_band**, warranting further audit.

**Strengths**:
- Routing aligns with legitimate needs (language, accommodation, risk).
- Adjustments for case complexity reduce but do not eliminate disparities.

**Concerns**:
- **Bottlenecks in support steps** create procedural burden beyond what complexity justifies.
- **Over-scrutiny of minority-language applicants** may reflect implicit bias.
- **Lack of service-level targets** for rights-preserving steps allows delays to accumulate.

**Conclusion**:
The process is **not procedurally unfair in all respects**, but **targeted disparities**—particularly for Groups B and C—suggest **systemic inefficiencies** and **potential bias** that warrant mitigation. **Further investigation** into risk_score_band, documentation checks, and regional practices is needed before concluding fairness. **Mitigation should focus on capacity expansion for support steps, routing redesign, and audits of biased covariates**.
