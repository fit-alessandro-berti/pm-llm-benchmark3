## 1. Interaction-graph description

The appropriate representation is a **typed, temporal interaction graph**, not six independent Job traces. Jobs become coupled through shared batches, tools, lots, machines, and shipments. Some relations are persistent object-to-object (O2O) links; others arise because multiple objects participate in the same event.

### Important nodes and edges

- **Jobs:** J1–J6
- **Components/lots:** C1–C6, L7, L8
- **Resources:** CNC-2, OVEN-1, TEST-1, FIX-A, RACK-7, CAL-3
- **Synchronization objects:** B1, B2, S1–S3

Important edge types include:

- `Job --requires--> Component`
- `ComponentLot --contains--> Component`
- `Batch --batches--> Job`
- `Shipment --consolidates--> Job`
- `Job --uses-at-event--> Machine/Tool`
- `Batch --uses-at-event--> OVEN-1/RACK-7`
- inferred `Job --waits-for--> Batch/Tool/Lot/Shipment partner`

The last class is not directly logged and must not be treated as proven causality.

### Higher-order motifs

1. **Batch synchronization motif:**  
   `J1/J2/J3  B1  OVEN-1 + RACK-7`  
   Three jobs and their components converge on one batch event. B1 cannot start under the apparent batching rule until J3 is ready; early jobs accumulate synchronization waiting even though OVEN-1 need not be highly utilized.

2. **Tool-mediated machine contention motif:**  
   `J3  FIX-A  J4  CNC-2`  
   J3 holds FIX-A until 11:40; J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 but starts at 11:45 with FIX-A. CNC-2 can therefore be idle while J4 is infeasible to process because a complementary tool is unavailable.

3. **Lot-policy-batch motif:**  
   `L7  {C2,C5}  {J2,J5}  {B1,B2}`  
   One lot-level quarantine couples otherwise unrelated jobs and potentially propagates into two batches. J2 bypasses the release through an override; J5 does not, producing asymmetric effects from the same lot event.

4. **Calibration-mediated testing motif:**  
   `{J5,J6}  TEST-1 + CAL-3`  
   TEST-1 has spare nominal capacity but cannot test J6 while CAL-3 is recalibrated. The effective service station is the resource bundle `(TEST-1, CAL-3)`.

5. **Shipment join motif:**  
   `{J1,J4}  S1`  
   J1’s dispatch is governed by J4’s completion, not by J1’s own processing. S1 acts as a downstream synchronization barrier.

### Compact edge list

```text
requires:       J1>C1, J2>C2, J3>C3, J4>C4, J5>C5, J6>C6
contains:       L7>{C2,C5}, L8>{C1,C3}
batches:        B1>{J1,J2,J3}, B2>{J4,J5}
consolidates:   S1>{J1,J4}, S2>{J2,J3}, S3>{J5,J6}

event-observed:
B1 --Batch Cure@12:05--> {OVEN-1,RACK-7,J1,J2,J3,C1,C2,C3}
J4 --Machine Process@11:45--> {CNC-2,FIX-A,C4}
L7 --Quarantine@10:20/Release@13:50--> {C2,C5,J2,J5}
J5/J6 --Final Test--> {TEST-1,CAL-3}

inferred, conditional:
J1,J2 --wait-for--> J3/B1
J4 --wait-for--> FIX-A
J5 --wait-for--> L7 release
J6 --wait-for--> CAL-3 recalibration
J1 --wait-for--> J4/S1
B2 --wait-for--> RACK-7 availability
```

O2O membership and listed event-object relations are **direct observations**. Readiness-based waiting and resource-blocking edges are **inferences** based on timestamps plus assumptions about policies and feasibility.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

These are counterfactual estimates, not causal proofs.

1. **J1’s B1 synchronization waiting**

   \[
   12{:}05-08{:}10=3\text{ h }55\text{ min}
   \]

   **Counterfactual:** J1 could have entered an immediate individual cure, or an earlier admissible batch, at 08:10 with equivalent processing capability. Under that assumption, up to **235 minutes** are synchronization-induced.

2. **J2’s B1 synchronization waiting**

   \[
   12{:}05-08{:}25=3\text{ h }40\text{ min}=220\text{ min}
   \]

   **Counterfactual:** J2 was genuinely process-ready at 08:25 despite L7’s later quarantine/release issue, and could legally be cured using the supervisor override. Otherwise part of this interval is lot-policy waiting rather than batch waiting.

3. **Delay due specifically to waiting for the last B1 member**

   J3 becomes ready at 11:55 and B1 starts at 12:05. If B1 required all three jobs:

   - J1 waiting for J3’s readiness:  
     \[
     11{:}55-08{:}10=3\text{ h }45\text{ min}
     \]
   - J2 waiting for J3’s readiness:  
     \[
     11{:}55-08{:}25=3\text{ h }30\text{ min}
     \]
   - post-readiness batch launch overhead:  
     \[
     12{:}05-11{:}55=10\text{ min}
     \]

   **Counterfactual:** J3 was the final binding requirement, with OVEN-1 and RACK-7 otherwise available and no minimum-start-time policy.

4. **FIX-A-associated delay for J4**

   J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50; FIX-A is released by J3 at 11:40; J4 starts at 11:45:

   \[
   11{:}45-09{:}50=1\text{ h }55\text{ min}
   \]

   Of this, the interval until fixture release is:

   \[
   11{:}40-09{:}50=1\text{ h }50\text{ min}
   \]

   and release-to-start is five minutes. Thus up to **110 minutes** are plausibly fixture-associated.

   **Counterfactual:** J4 was fully ready at 09:50, required FIX-A, no substitute existed, and CNC-2/operator availability was not independently binding. The CNC-2 idle interval 10:05–10:47 supports, but does not prove, this explanation.

5. **RACK-7-associated delay for B2**

   J5 becomes ready at 14:40, RACK-7 leaves cleaning/quarantine at 14:35, and B2 starts at 14:50. Since the rack is available before the last job:

   \[
   14{:}50-14{:}40=10\text{ min}
   \]

   On the supplied timestamps, RACK-7 imposes **no demonstrated delay after all B2 members are ready**. J4’s total wait is:

   \[
   14{:}50-13{:}10=1\text{ h }40\text{ min}
   \]

   of which at least:

   \[
   14{:}40-13{:}10=1\text{ h }30\text{ min}
   \]

   is explained by waiting for J5 under an all-members-ready rule.

   **Counterfactual:** Readiness times are valid and B2 requires both jobs. Rack quarantine could still have prevented an earlier partial batch, but that policy is not established.

6. **Shipment consolidation delay imposed on J1**

   J1 is packed at 13:30 and S1 dispatches at 19:25:

   \[
   19{:}25-13{:}30=5\text{ h }55\text{ min}=355\text{ min}
   \]

   J4 is packed at 19:10, leaving:

   \[
   19{:}25-19{:}10=15\text{ min}
   \]

   for post-last-pack loading/dispatch. Relative to dispatching J1 with the same 15-minute handling delay:

   \[
   19{:}10-13{:}30=5\text{ h }40\text{ min}
   \]

   Hence **340 minutes** are plausibly partner-consolidation waiting.

   **Counterfactual:** A separate shipment was permitted and would incur only the observed 15-minute post-pack handling interval.

7. **CAL-3-associated delay for J6**

   J6 is ready at 16:05 and tests at 17:25:

   \[
   17{:}25-16{:}05=80\text{ min}
   \]

   J5 occupies testing from 16:10 to 16:35. If J6 follows J5, its earliest resource-feasible start without recalibration is 16:35:

   \[
   17{:}25-16{:}35=50\text{ min}
   \]

   CAL-3 recalibration lasts 16:35–17:20; the additional five minutes may be handoff/setup. Thus **45 minutes are directly coincident with recalibration**, and **50 minutes** are plausibly recalibration-associated.

   **Counterfactual:** J5 had priority, TEST-1 was available, J6 required CAL-3, and no other staffing or setup constraint applied.

8. **L7 exposure for J5**

   Quarantine occurs at 10:20 and release at 13:50:

   \[
   13{:}50-10{:}20=3\text{ h }30\text{ min}
   \]

   **Counterfactual:** J5/C5 would otherwise have been ready to proceed throughout this interval. The evidence only says J5 did not proceed until after release; therefore **210 minutes is an exposure window, not a demonstrated job delay**.

There is no single proved bottleneck. The strongest structure is a family of **synchronization and complementary-resource constraints** whose binding member changes over time.

## 4. What flattening misses

### Job viewpoint

- **Visible:** each job’s activity order, rework, and elapsed stage-to-stage times.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** batch events may be copied into several traces; lot events may disappear or be inconsistently copied; partner identities and tool ownership are lost.
- **Misleading conclusion:** the broadly normal durations and median `Stage Component  Machine Process` of 1.1 hours suggest independent variation, despite J1/J2 sharing one batch barrier and J1 later waiting for J4.

### Machine viewpoint

- **Visible:** setup, processing, release, utilization, and machine-event gaps.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** jobs waiting while a tool, rack, lot release, batch partner, or operator is unavailable.
- **Misleading conclusion:** idle OVEN-1 or TEST-1 implies spare effective capacity. In fact, the executable resource bundles are `(OVEN-1,RACK-7,ready batch)` and `(TEST-1,CAL-3)`.

Low utilization can coexist with long waiting because utilization measures occupancy of one object, whereas work requires the **simultaneous availability of several objects**. A machine may be idle precisely because a fixture, calibration tool, batch partner, quality release, or policy authorization is missing.

### Batch viewpoint

- **Visible:** convergence of jobs, final-member readiness, batch start/cure/unload, and shared oven/rack use.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** upstream reasons why members are late and downstream shipment effects; one batch event may be duplicated across job traces.
- **Misleading conclusion:** low frequency—only two Batch–Machine interactions—means low impact. B1 alone creates hundreds of job-minutes of waiting.

### Tool viewpoint

- **Visible:** acquire/release, holding intervals, recalibration, cleaning, and contention.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** unlogged demand before acquisition and whether a job was otherwise ready.
- **Misleading conclusion:** tool activity is short or infrequent, so the tool is unimportant. FIX-A and CAL-3 can block idle machines without any `Wait for Tool` event.

### ComponentLot viewpoint

- **Visible:** quarantine/release lifecycle and propagation to C2/C5.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** job-level effect if events are linked only through components; alternatively, events can be duplicated into every affected job.
- **Misleading conclusion:** J2 and J5 experienced identical lot delays. J2 used an override, while J5 waited, and the integration pipeline changes whether either event appears in a Job trace.

### Shipment viewpoint

- **Visible:** shipment creation, loading, dispatch, partner membership.
- **Hidden/duplicated:** time from each job’s pack completion to the shipment becoming ready.
- **Misleading conclusion:** `Load  Dispatch` median 0.3 hours indicates fast fulfillment. For J1, the dominant delay is the 5 hours 40 minutes waiting for J4 before the final 15-minute dispatch interval.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

### Narrative A: J3–FIX-A–J4–CNC-2–B2–S1

**Temporal precedence:** J3 holds FIX-A from 09:00 to 11:40. J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and begins with FIX-A at 11:45. J4 is ready for B2 at 13:10, B2 starts at 14:50, J4 packs at 19:10, and S1 dispatches J1 and J4 at 19:25.

**Resource feasibility:** J4’s machining event explicitly requires CNC-2 and FIX-A. If FIX-A is unique, CNC-2 alone is insufficient.

**Policy constraints:** B2 apparently synchronizes J4 with J5; S1 consolidates J4 with J1.

**Causal speculation:** J3’s fixture hold may have delayed J4, which may then have affected B2 and ultimately J1’s shipment. The timestamps support a propagation path across **Job, Tool, Machine, Batch, and Shipment**, but do not prove every downstream event would have moved earlier one-for-one.

### Narrative B: L7–C5–J5–B2–RACK-7/OVEN-1

**Temporal precedence:** L7 is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50. J5 does not proceed until after release and is ready for B2 at 14:40. RACK-7 remains in cleaning/quarantine until 14:35; B2 starts on OVEN-1 at 14:50.

**Resource feasibility:** B2 needs OVEN-1 and RACK-7; J5 needs acceptable C5 from L7.

**Policy constraints:** Quality release is required for J5, and B2 apparently requires both J4 and J5.

**Causal speculation:** L7 release likely contributes to J5’s late readiness and hence J4’s synchronization wait. RACK-7 does not appear binding after 14:40, although it may have prevented a hypothetical earlier partial run. This narrative spans **ComponentLot, Component, Job, Batch, Tool, and Machine**.

### Narrative C: J5–CAL-3–TEST-1–J6–S3

**Temporal precedence:** J5 and J6 are test-ready at 16:00 and 16:05. J5 tests on TEST-1 with CAL-3 from 16:10 to 16:35. CAL-3 recalibrates until 17:20; J6 starts at 17:25. S3 consolidates J5 and J6.

**Resource feasibility:** Final test requires both TEST-1 and CAL-3. TEST-1’s idle time cannot serve J6 while CAL-3 is unavailable.

**Policy constraints:** J5 receives first service; recalibration occurs before J6; S3 waits for both jobs.

**Causal speculation:** Recalibration plausibly delays J6 by about 50 minutes and may delay S3 and J5’s dispatch through consolidation. Testing order or an external shipment cutoff could weaken this chain. It spans **Jobs, Tool, Machine, and Shipment**.

### Narrative D: L7–J2–B1–OVEN-1–RACK-7

**Temporal precedence:** J2 is batch-ready at 08:25; L7 is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50; B1 cures at 12:05.

**Resource feasibility:** J2 needs C2, which belongs to L7, plus the batch resource bundle.

**Policy constraints:** A supervisor override allows J2 to enter B1 before formal lot release.

**Causal speculation:** The override may prevent L7 from delaying the whole B1 batch. Without it, J1 and J3 could have been held until at least 13:50. This is a plausible avoided cascade, not an observed counterfactual.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

| Intervention | Target mechanism | Expected benefit | Helped | Potentially harmed | New cost/risk | Perverse metric | Object-centric evaluation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dynamic batch release: maximum wait or partial-batch rule | Batch synchronization | Reduce J1/J2/J4 early-member waiting | Early-ready jobs, B1/B2 | Oven efficiency, energy use, later jobs needing capacity | More cures, setups, energy | “Batch fill rate” may encourage excessive waiting | Compare matched Batch objects on member-ready spread, job waiting, energy, and downstream shipments |
| Add/duplicate FIX-A or shorten controlled handoff | Fixture contention | Make CNC-2 executable when jobs arrive | J4 and future fixture users | Maintenance team; jobs if duplicate quality differs | Capital, validation, calibration | Tool utilization target may discourage redundancy | Track Job–Tool demand intervals, simultaneous requests, CNC idle-with-demand, and quality outcomes |
| Add CAL-3 or schedule recalibration outside queued demand | Calibration constraint | Remove J6-like test gaps | J6, TEST-1 queue, S3 partners | Calibration workload; jobs if calibration is deferred too far | Compliance and drift risk | TEST-1 utilization may reward unsafe deferral | Compare `(TEST-1,CAL-3)` bundle availability, queue delay, recalibration compliance, failures |
| Pre-clean or rotate RACK-7; add validated spare rack | Batch/tool availability | Reduce chance that rack quarantine blocks oven batches | B2 and future batches | Cleaning capacity and storage | Cross-contamination, validation | Rack turnaround metric could encourage inadequate cleaning | Measure rack states, ready-batch intervals, oven idle-with-ready-batch, contamination incidents |
| Shipment max-hold rule or optional split for early jobs | Shipment consolidation | Reduce J1’s 340-minute partner wait | J1 and urgent early jobs | J4/S1 consolidation economics; carrier workload | More shipments, freight, emissions | On-time dispatch may drive costly fragmentation | Randomize/phase policy by Shipment; measure pack-to-dispatch per Job, cost, fill, service, emissions |
| Risk-based lot release with explicit override workflow | Lot coupling | Reduce unnecessary propagation while preserving quality control | J5-like jobs and batch partners | Quality system if risk underestimated | Compliance and defect escape | Release-time KPI may pressure premature release | Link Lot, Component, Job, override, test and defect outcomes; compare approved policy strata |
| Log explicit readiness, queue-entry, wait reason, reservation, and policy events | Data artifact/causal ambiguity | Distinguish batch, tool, lot, operator, and policy delays | Analysts and all improvement programs | Operational users entering data | Instrumentation burden, inconsistent coding | “Low unclassified wait” may encourage miscoding | Validate event-object completeness across source systems and reconcile direct versus propagated links |
| Constraint-aware scheduling across jobs, tools, batches, and shipments | Cascading coordination | Avoid schedules feasible for machines but not resource bundles | J4, J6, batch and shipment partners | Lower-priority jobs displaced | Complexity and unstable priorities | Mean flow time may sacrifice tail/urgent jobs | Simulate and pilot using full object graph; report effects by Job, Tool, Batch, Lot, and Shipment |

Interventions can move rather than eliminate queues. Partial batches may increase oven utilization and delay later batches; an extra fixture may shift congestion to OVEN-1; faster J6 testing may only move its wait to S3; split shipments reduce waiting but increase freight. Evaluation must therefore follow all related objects downstream.

def edge_metric(model, object_type, metric_name):
    """
    Defensive accessor for common OCDFG layouts.
    Inspect model.keys() for the installed PM4Py version.

    candidates = [
        model.get(metric_name, {}),
        model.get("edges", {}).get(metric_name, {}),

# 6) Waiting analysis must join explicit readiness, acquire/release,
# maintenance, quarantine/release, and partner completion timestamps.
# Do NOT infer waiting solely from gaps between machine events.
#

#   job_ready -> batch_start, conditional on batch membership

The two frequency notions should be interpreted differently: event-couple frequency can become large when the same objects co-occur repeatedly, while unique-object frequency emphasizes how many distinct objects traverse a relation. Neither measures unrecorded waiting.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

### Observations that would weaken batch synchronization as the top explanation

1. Readiness audits show J1 and J2 were not actually process-ready at 08:10 and 08:25.
2. OVEN-1 was unavailable or reserved continuously until near 12:05 despite the recorded utilization summary.
3. RACK-7 or an operator was unavailable before 12:05, independently of J3.
4. B1 had a fixed, exogenous 12:05 scheduled start that would not change with earlier J3 readiness.
5. Quality rules prohibited curing J1 or J2 separately, making “immediate individual cure” infeasible.
6. Earlier partial batches would have missed a carrier, increased rework, or caused equivalent downstream waiting.
7. A larger sample shows member-ready spreads are usually near zero and B1 is exceptional.
8. Job waiting remains unchanged after a controlled relaxation of batch-size or maximum-wait policy.
9. Batch start timestamps represent delayed data entry rather than physical starts.
10. OVEN-1 processing capacity, not synchronization, becomes saturated when readiness is measured correctly.

### Additional discriminating fields or event types

1. Explicit `Job Ready for Operation` events with operation and timestamp.
2. Queue-entry, reservation, dispatch-to-resource, and actual service-start events.
3. `Wait Start/End` with reason codes: tool, operator, quality, batch partner, machine, material, shipment.
4. Tool demand, reservation, acquire, release, location, condition, and substitute-tool eligibility.
5. Machine state events distinguishing idle, blocked, starved, setup, breakdown, and reserved.
6. Batch policy fields: minimum/maximum size, allowed partial run, maximum wait, recipe, and scheduled start.
7. Lot status effective times, release scope, affected serials/components, override approval, and override validity.
8. Operator/skill availability and shift calendars.
9. Shipment cutoff, route, carrier booking, consolidation rule, split eligibility, freight cost, and promised date.
10. Component physical-availability and staging timestamps, separate from administrative release.
11. Calibration due time, reason, duration, certificate, tool alternatives, and whether testing can proceed under grace rules.
12. Cleaning/quarantine reason and planned versus actual availability for RACK-7.
13. Event provenance and linkage method: directly recorded Job relation versus relation propagated through Component/Lot.
14. Stable clock source, timezone, ingestion time, and correction history to detect timestamp artifacts.
15. Priority changes and scheduler decisions, including why J5 tested before J6.
16. Counterfactual-relevant outcomes: energy per cure, defect/rework rates, shipment cost, service level, and emissions.

These additions would permit separation of temporal association, physical feasibility, policy enforcement, and data-construction effects—without assuming that whichever event occurs first caused the next delay.
