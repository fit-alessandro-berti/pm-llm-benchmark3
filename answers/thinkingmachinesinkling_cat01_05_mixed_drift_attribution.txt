**Primary monitoring population:** The primary segment should be the monthly cohort of **post-migration (Mar onward), standard-complexity, non-Supplier-Z cases with complete resource identifiers**. This segment minimizes three major confounders: (1) logging artifacts are stabilized by post-migration mapping; (2) supplier contamination is removed; (3) extreme seasonal case-mix and Senior Assessment routing are reduced. By holding supplier and complexity relatively constant, changes in queue wait, service time, variant distribution, and resource workload in this segment provide the cleanest signal for organizational (staffing, priority) and persistent behavioral drift. All other segments (Z-cases, high-complexity cases, missing-resource cases) are monitored as deviation diagnostics relative to this baseline.

## 9. Provisional attribution

The strongest conclusion is that March 2026 represents a **logging and semantic migration event**, not a business-process failure. Evidence is overwhelming: raw conformance collapses by ~0.40, mapped conformance recovers almost fully (~0.90), the label “Eligibility Screen” replaces “Validate Eligibility,” duplicate technical events appear (~4–5%), and resource missingness jumps (~4.8%). Crucially, the normalized DFG distances between January and March are minimal (~3 L1), service times are unchanged, and cycle-time metrics are stable. I assign this ~95% confidence. The main revision condition would be discovery that “Eligibility Screen” introduces materially different entry logic or durations that mapping conceals; current data do not support that.

The supplier disruption beginning **May 5** is the next strongest mechanism (~85% confidence). Supplier Z share jumps from ~18% to ~43%, and the normalized DFG shows AssessRequestInfo doubling (12  24 per 100) alongside a fall in direct decision rate (82  76). The rework variant share tracks this closely (13  24%). I attribute this primarily to **supplier-driven behavioral drift**—incomplete bundles force additional “Request Info” loops—rather than pure population drift, because supplier Z serves a non-random subset, but the loop rate increases within the overall flow, not just within Z cases (implied by aggregate DFG change). A confounding issue is the June staffing cut, which likely amplifies the performance impact of the same rework loops by reducing the capacity available to process them. If supplier share recovered fully by September and loop rates returned to baseline, attribution would be cleaner; instead, loop rates only fall to ~20, suggesting either residual supplier issues or organizational adaptation.

**Staffing cuts on June 15** generate a **performance/queueing drift** with high confidence (~85%). The critical evidence is the divergence between service time (median Assess service ~4.8  ~5.4 h, a ~13% increase explaining only ~1–2 h of cycle-time growth) and reconstructed queue waiting, which “increased much more.” Meanwhile, volume rises toward seasonal peaks and headcount falls by four assessors with no full replacement. The median cycle time climbs from ~32 (April) to ~45 (August) despite partial supplier recovery in September. This is classic capacity-driven queueing. The mediating mechanism is important: supplier-induced rework consumes assessor time, so the staffing loss interacts with the supplier disruption—the same staff must now handle both higher loop frequency and unchanged (or rising) volume. Revision would be required if service times had also spiked sharply, which would indicate skill degradation or measurement change rather than pure queue accumulation.

The **priority rule of July 1** is plausible but less certain (~65% confidence). The policy should reduce waiting for high-complexity cases and increase it for standard cases, yet aggregate median and p90 continue rising through July and August. Without segmented queue-wait data by priority class, I cannot confirm redistribution. The aggregate stability of high-complexity share (~28–29%) in July–August suggests that any priority effect is masked by concurrent staffing loss and supplier backlog. I treat this as a **behavioral/control-flow mechanism** whose signature is expected in segmented temporal profiles, not in overall aggregates.

**Seasonal renewal (October 1 onward)** explains the October–November peak in volume (760–790), high-complexity share (41–45%), and Senior Assessment rate (22 per 100 in October DFG fragment) with high confidence (~80%). This is a **recurring population/case-mix drift** with known historical precedent. It also drives performance degradation because the seasonal population is both larger and more complex, encountering a system already depleted by staff losses and chronic rework. The interaction is non-additive: the November median cycle time (~49 h) represents the worst performance despite supplier Z share falling to ~27%. Confounding includes the December overtime and quality campaign, which coincides with seasonal easing and volume reduction (690 cases, 34% high complexity).

Finally, there is evidence of **persistent organizational or behavioral adaptation** (~65% confidence). Even in December, after supplier recovery, seasonal easing, and active interventions, mapped fitness (~0.90) remains below the pre-migration baseline (~0.93), rework rate (~21%) is nearly double the baseline (~12%), and the direct decision rate (~78) is below the baseline (~83). This sustained divergence suggests that the organization has not returned to its pre-2026 operating mode—possibly due to permanent staff reduction, changed assessment rigor, or residual supplier-process changes. The interaction of mechanisms prevents clean attribution for the November peak: supplier loops, reduced staff, priority queuing, and seasonal complexity all contribute simultaneously.

In summary, the data support **multiple mechanism-specific change points** rather than a single drift event. Monitoring should therefore track logging artifacts via mapped fitness and duplicate/missing rates; supplier quality via supplier-stratified rework rates; organizational capacity via service-time-stable, queue-wait-rising patterns; and seasonality via year-over-year complexity and volume comparisons. I would revise this account if segmented event-level data showed that priority-class wait did not redistribute in July, if supplier-stratified analysis showed identical loop rates across supplier groups (indicating population-only effects), or if service times spiked sharply (indicating skill or measurement change rather than queueing).

## 10. Monitoring decision rules

1. `IF` mapped fitness drops >0.05 and raw fitness drops >0.30 simultaneously with a new dominant activity label, `THEN` treat the shift as a logging/semantic migration artifact and apply label mapping and duplicate removal before any behavioral diagnosis, `BECAUSE` vocabulary and technical provenance changes create artifactual non-conformance that can fully explain large fitness collapses.

2. `IF` supplier Z share increases >10 percentage points and AssessRequestInfo rate increases >5 points per 100 cases within the same month, `THEN` trigger a supplier-document-quality audit and compute supplier-stratified rework variants, `BECAUSE` supplier-induced incomplete bundles directly generate information-request loops.

3. `IF` median service time for Assess changes <10% but median/p90 cycle time increases >20% and organizational records show staff reduction, `THEN` attribute deterioration primarily to queueing/resource drift rather than service-quality degradation, `BECAUSE` flat processing speed combined with rising throughput delay indicates capacity loss.

4. `IF` high-complexity share spikes >10 percentage points above the rolling seasonal baseline and Senior Assessment rate in the normalized DFG doubles, `THEN` apply seasonal case-mix adjustments before diagnosing behavioral change, `BECAUSE` renewal seasons introduce legitimate population shifts that extend aggregate cycle times independent of process failure.

5. `IF` a priority-queue policy is implemented and aggregate delay continues rising, `THEN` compute segmented queue waiting by complexity class; `IF` standard-case wait increases disproportionately relative to high-complexity wait, `THEN` confirm priority-induced redistribution of delay, `BECAUSE` queue-order policies redistribute waiting rather than eliminate it.

6. `IF` rework rate remains >50% above baseline for >3 months after supplier recovery reports and staff-restoration attempts, `THEN` investigate persistent behavioral or organizational adaptation (e.g., changed assessment rigor or residual process changes), `BECAUSE` transient mechanisms should decay; sustained elevation implies structural change.

7. `IF` resource missingness exceeds 3% overall or is concentrated (>10%) in specific migrated activities, `THEN` exclude affected cases from organizational/network analysis and flag provenance repair, `BECAUSE` non-random missing identifiers bias workload attribution and can falsely suggest staff redistribution or role changes.
