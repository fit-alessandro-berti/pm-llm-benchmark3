# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

“Core” should mean a stable organizational responsibility, not merely the largest cell in an activity-frequency matrix. Peripheral work can still be operationally critical, and events performed during substitution periods should be evaluated separately.

1. **Automated intake and entitlement precheck**
   - **Core:** A1 and A2 are exclusive and structurally positioned at the beginning of routine variants.
   - **Peripheral:** No downstream resolution work is observed. Any routing performed by the bot is not separately logged.
   - **Interpretation:** The low complexity and system contract distinguish this from human entitlement work. The bot performs checks but should not be assumed to possess human exception authority.

2. **Front-office service generalists**
   - **Core:** A1, A2, A4, and A12 are frequent for both Amira and Ben and form a coherent routine-service responsibility.
   - **Peripheral:** Diagnosis, negotiation, configuration, implementation, reopen, and quality activity represent case ownership, escalation support, or experience-based extension of the role.
   - **Interpretation:** Amira’s additional medium/high work suggests a senior or escalation-capable lane within the same broad role. It is not necessary to create a completely separate formal role unless case routing, authorization, or performance evidence confirms that distinction.

3. **Restricted temporary front-office support**
   - **Core:** A1, A2, A4, and A12, but only for low-risk cases.
   - **Peripheral:** Minimal diagnosis and negotiation should be treated as exceptions until their circumstances are inspected.
   - **Interpretation:** High A2 counts do not establish entitlement expertise equivalent to permanent certified agents. The subsequent 17 reopens show that completion volume and closure authority can conceal documentation or validation defects.

4. **Technical diagnosis, configuration, and implementation**
   - **Core:** A3, A5, and A10 are consistently high for Chen and Dina.
   - **Peripheral:** Security review, quality, reopen handling, and KB updates are plausible extensions around difficult implementations.
   - **Interpretation:** Chen and Dina have almost identical activity profiles, but not identical risk exposure. Chen’s higher critical-case load and security-adjacent work suggest a senior technical specialization, escalation lane, or different assignment policy.

5. **Security risk specialist**
   - **Core:** A6 is core despite having only 96 events. It is highly concentrated in Erik and attached to the highest-complexity work.
   - **Peripheral:** Diagnosis, configuration, implementation, quality, reopen, and KB activity likely support security findings or remediation.
   - **Interpretation:** Certification and risk ownership are more important than total volume. Mateo’s temporary filtering of low-risk referrals reduced Erik’s observed A6 demand but did not transfer security-review authority to Mateo.

6. **Commercial exception specialist**
   - **Core:** A7 and A8, supported by Fatima’s formal pricing certification and high-complexity case mix.
   - **Peripheral:** Validation, A9-related events, closure, and implementation may reflect preparation, case ownership, or documenting a commercial decision.
   - **Interpretation:** An A9 event performed by a commercial specialist should not automatically be read as final supervisory approval. The event log needs decision-status or signer metadata to establish authority.

7. **Senior front-office/commercial bridge**
   - **Core:** Giorgio has stable front-office work and substantial commercial work. His role is therefore more than a one-off substitution, although part of the commercial volume is absence-driven.
   - **Peripheral:** A9, technical activity, quality, and reopen work indicate senior case ownership or escalation support.
   - **Interpretation:** His commercial shadow capacity is useful, but the substitution episode showed slower pricing-review duration. The data support backup capability, not full equivalence to Fatima or unrestricted commercial authority.

8. **Supervisory approval and escalation authority**
   - **Core:** A9 and final escalation decisions.
   - **Peripheral:** Negotiation support, reopened cases, quality, and implementation follow-up are expected around difficult approvals.
   - **Interpretation:** Hana’s authority is supported by her formal title, dominant A9 volume, high/critical workload, and later rechecks of Mateo’s decisions. Shared activity labels alone cannot establish equivalent authority for another resource.

9. **Quality assurance and knowledge stewardship**
   - **Core:** A11 and A14 are both frequent and shared almost symmetrically by Ivan and Julia.
   - **Peripheral:** A10, A12, A3, and A5 are likely implementation verification, defect follow-up, or closure support.
   - **Interpretation:** Their work acts as a control gate even though they do not have supervisory approval authority. Their near-identical profiles and complexity distributions provide unusually strong evidence for a genuine common role.

10. **Cross-functional incident and escalation coordination**
    - **Core:** Mateo’s core work is the orchestration pattern around A13, preliminary A9, specialist referral, and cross-domain handoffs. Coordination is partly latent because it is not represented by one dedicated process activity.
    - **Peripheral:** His A6, A7, A8, A10, and A11 events may represent triage or follow-up rather than substantive specialist execution.
    - **Interpretation:** Mateo’s 21 temporary A9 events should not be converted into permanent supervisory competence: 13 required Hana’s recheck. Similarly, successful filtering of low-risk security referrals supports triage skill but not final security-review authority.

Overall, authority should be inferred from a combination of formal title, certification, approval/recheck behavior, case complexity, and governance records. It cannot be inferred safely from activity frequency alone.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

- **R07_Giorgio — commercial shadow and customer-context bridge.**  
  Giorgio connects front-office case ownership with pricing review and negotiation. This gives the organization continuity when Fatima is absent and can reduce handoffs for complex customers. The total of 72 A7 and 88 A8 events suggests an ongoing shadow role, not only six days of emergency coverage. However, the substitution episode’s increase from 38 to 74 minutes indicates a proficiency, workload, or authorization difference. Management should recognize the shadow role, define its limits, and potentially certify it; it should not assume Giorgio is already interchangeable with Fatima.

- **R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker.**  
  Mateo bridges front office, technical, security, commercial, quality, and management. His high/critical exposure and broad activity profile are consistent with an incident coordinator rather than an ordinary generalist. This role provides valuable triage, reduces unnecessary specialist referrals, and supports reopened or multi-domain cases. The risks are becoming a bottleneck, obscuring accountability, and allowing preliminary actions to be mistaken for final decisions. The two critical security cases waiting more than two days show that aggressive filtering can improve referral quality while still creating delay risk.

- **R05_Erik — sparse but critical technical-risk bridge.**  
  Erik is a formal specialist rather than an informal substitute, but behaviorally he forms a narrow bridge between technical diagnosis and risk-controlled implementation. His role may appear weak in clustering because security events are rare relative to registration, validation, or quality events. In reality, the concentration of A6 and the 55 critical cases make the role strategically important. Benefits include specialist assurance and controlled risk acceptance; risks include single-person dependency, queue invisibility, and under-staffing because low frequency is mistaken for low importance.

- **R14_Nora — automation boundary role.**  
  Nora bridges customer intake and human service work. Treating the bot as a normal front-office worker would distort workload, availability, and entitlement-skill analysis. Management should distinguish automated precheck completion from human exception handling and monitor the rate at which bot-prechecked cases are corrected downstream.

- **R11_Kai and R12_Lina — elastic-capacity layer.**  
  The temporary agents bridge seasonal demand and permanent front-office capacity. This is beneficial when scope is restricted and controls are explicit. The reopen evidence indicates a risk of apparent short-term throughput being offset by downstream rework. Their role should therefore include clearly defined case eligibility, note-quality checks, and escalation triggers.

- **R09_Ivan and R10_Julia — quality and knowledge bridge.**  
  They connect completed implementation with closure, learning, and future standardization. Their A14 activity means the role is not merely inspection: it also converts case experience into organizational knowledge. This can improve resilience, but combining quality throughput and KB output into one target could incentivize superficial checks or low-value updates.

Shadow roles are not inherently improper. They can preserve continuity, develop successors, and reduce unnecessary handoffs. The risk arises when their scope, authority, training, or review requirements remain implicit.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The following is illustrative Python. PM4Py return structures and optional parameter names vary slightly by version, so the adapter functions should be checked against the installed version.

```python
import inspect
from collections.abc import Mapping

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"
COMPLEXITY = "case_complexity"
TEMP = "temporary_substitution"


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

raw = pd.read_parquet("order_to_resolution.parquet")
raw[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(raw[TIMESTAMP], utc=True, errors="coerce")

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP
).sort_values([CASE, TIMESTAMP])

# Normalize the substitution flag without treating missing as substitution.
df["temporary_substitution_bool"] = (
    df[TEMP]
    .fillna(False)
    .astype(str)
    .str.lower()
    .isin({"true", "1", "yes", "y"})
)

# Support either numeric complexity or Low/Medium/High/Critical labels.
numeric_complexity = pd.to_numeric(df[COMPLEXITY], errors="coerce")
complexity_labels = df[COMPLEXITY].astype(str).str.strip().str.lower()

complexity_map = {
    "low": 0.15,
    "medium": 0.45,
    "high": 0.75,
    "critical": 1.00,
}

df["complexity_score"] = numeric_complexity.fillna(
    complexity_labels.map(complexity_map)
)

df["high_or_critical"] = (
    complexity_labels.isin({"high", "critical"})
    | (df["complexity_score"] >= 0.67)
)


# PM4Py versions expose slightly different optional keyword parameters.
def pm_call(function, log, **kwargs):
    try:
        signature = inspect.signature(function)
        accepts_kwargs = any(
            p.kind == inspect.Parameter.VAR_KEYWORD
            for p in signature.parameters.values()
        )
        if accepts_kwargs:
            accepted = kwargs
        else:
            accepted = {
                key: value
                for key, value in kwargs.items()
                if key in signature.parameters
            }
        return function(log, **accepted)
    except (TypeError, ValueError):
        # If the installed function does not expose an inspectable signature.
        return function(log, **kwargs)


common_keys = {
    "activity_key": ACTIVITY,
    "resource_key": RESOURCE,
    "timestamp_key": TIMESTAMP,
    "case_id_key": CASE,
}


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Organizational role discovery
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

roles_all_raw = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_organizational_roles,
    df,
    **common_keys
)

# Run a second model without explicitly marked substitution events.
# This helps distinguish persistent role behavior from temporary coverage.
steady_df = df.loc[~df["temporary_substitution_bool"]].copy()

roles_steady_raw = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_organizational_roles,
    steady_df,
    **common_keys
)

print("All-event role output type:", type(roles_all_raw))
print("Steady-state role output type:", type(roles_steady_raw))
print("Example role:", list(roles_all_raw)[:1])


# PM4Py commonly returns each role as a pair containing an activity
# collection and a resource/originator collection. The exact object
# differs by release, so normalize it before joining to other data.
def names_from_collection(obj):
    if obj is None:
        return set()
    if isinstance(obj, Mapping):
        return set(obj.keys())
    if isinstance(obj, str):
        return {obj}
    return set(obj)


def normalize_roles(raw_roles, known_resources, model_name):
    member_rows = []
    activity_rows = []

    for role_number, role in enumerate(raw_roles):
        role_id = f"{model_name}_role_{role_number:02d}"

        if isinstance(role, Mapping):
            activity_obj = (
                role.get("activities")
                if "activities" in role
                else role.get("activity")
            )
            resource_obj = (
                role.get("resources")
                if "resources" in role
                else role.get("originators")
            )
        else:
            # Common PM4Py representation: (activities, resources).
            left, right = role[0], role[1]
            left_names = names_from_collection(left)
            right_names = names_from_collection(right)

            # Detect and correct reversed order if necessary.
            if len(left_names & known_resources) > len(right_names & known_resources):
                resource_obj, activity_obj = left, right
            else:
                activity_obj, resource_obj = left, right

        activities = names_from_collection(activity_obj)
        resources = names_from_collection(resource_obj)

        for resource in resources:
            weight = (
                resource_obj.get(resource, np.nan)
                if isinstance(resource_obj, Mapping)
                else np.nan
            )
            member_rows.append({
                "model": model_name,
                "role_id": role_id,
                RESOURCE: resource,
                "pm4py_membership_weight": weight,
            })

        for activity in activities:
            weight = (
                activity_obj.get(activity, np.nan)
                if isinstance(activity_obj, Mapping)
                else np.nan
            )
            activity_rows.append({
                "model": model_name,
                "role_id": role_id,
                ACTIVITY: activity,
                "pm4py_activity_weight": weight,
            })

    return pd.DataFrame(member_rows), pd.DataFrame(activity_rows)


known_resources = set(df[RESOURCE].dropna().unique())

members_all, role_activities_all = normalize_roles(
    roles_all_raw, known_resources, "all_events"
)
members_steady, role_activities_steady = normalize_roles(
    roles_steady_raw, known_resources, "steady_state"
)

role_membership = pd.concat(
    [members_all, members_steady],
    ignore_index=True
)


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Activity-based resource similarity
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

similarity_pm4py = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity,
    df,
    **common_keys
)

print("PM4Py similarity output type:", type(similarity_pm4py))
print(similarity_pm4py)

# Independently create an inspectable cosine matrix. This is useful for
# validating pairs such as Chen/Dina, Ivan/Julia, and Kai/Lina.
activity_profile = pd.crosstab(df[RESOURCE], df[ACTIVITY]).astype(float)

row_norm = np.linalg.norm(activity_profile.values, axis=1, keepdims=True)
normalized_profile = activity_profile.values / np.where(row_norm == 0, 1, row_norm)

cosine_similarity = pd.DataFrame(
    normalized_profile @ normalized_profile.T,
    index=activity_profile.index,
    columns=activity_profile.index
)

selected_pairs = [
    ("R03_Chen", "R04_Dina"),
    ("R09_Ivan", "R10_Julia"),
    ("R11_Kai", "R12_Lina"),
    ("R06_Fatima", "R07_Giorgio"),
    ("R08_Hana", "R13_Mateo"),
]

for first, second in selected_pairs:
    print(first, second, cosine_similarity.loc[first, second])


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Handover-of-work and working-together networks
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

handover_network = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network,
    df,
    beta=0,
    **common_keys
)

working_together_network = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_working_together_network,
    df,
    **common_keys
)

# Optional additional view of specialist substitution or repeated routing.
subcontracting_network = pm_call(
    pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network,
    df,
    n=2,
    **common_keys
)


def network_to_edges(network, network_name):
    """Normalize a PM4Py edge dictionary or a NetworkX-like object."""
    rows = []

    if hasattr(network, "edges"):
        for source, target, data in network.edges(data=True):
            weight = data.get("weight", 1.0)
            rows.append((source, target, weight))

    elif isinstance(network, Mapping):
        for key, value in network.items():
            if isinstance(key, tuple) and len(key) == 2:
                source, target = key
                weight = value.get("weight", 1.0) if isinstance(value, Mapping) else value
                rows.append((source, target, weight))
            elif isinstance(value, Mapping):
                # Handles nested dictionaries: source -> target -> weight.
                source = key
                for target, weight in value.items():
                    rows.append((source, target, weight))

    else:
        raise TypeError(
            f"Inspect the installed PM4Py representation for {network_name}: "
            f"{type(network)}"
        )

    return pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["source", "target", "weight"]).assign(
        network=network_name
    )


handover_edges = network_to_edges(handover_network, "handover")
together_edges = network_to_edges(working_together_network, "working_together")

print(handover_edges.sort_values("weight", ascending=False).head(25))
print(together_edges.sort_values("weight", ascending=False).head(25))

# Inspect Mateo's network position rather than classifying him only by
# his most frequent activity.
mateo_handoffs = handover_edges[
    (handover_edges["source"] == "R13_Mateo")
    | (handover_edges["target"] == "R13_Mateo")
].sort_values("weight", ascending=False)

mateo_collaboration = together_edges[
    (together_edges["source"] == "R13_Mateo")
    | (together_edges["target"] == "R13_Mateo")
].sort_values("weight", ascending=False)

print(mateo_handoffs)
print(mateo_collaboration)


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Join mined roles to complexity and substitution context
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

# Aggregate at case-resource level first. Aggregating directly from events
# would over-weight resources who perform several activities in one case.
case_resource = (
    df.groupby([CASE, RESOURCE], as_index=False)
      .agg(
          complexity_score=("complexity_score", "median"),
          high_or_critical=("high_or_critical", "max"),
          substitution_case=("temporary_substitution_bool", "max"),
          events_in_case=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
      )
)

resource_context = (
    case_resource.groupby(RESOURCE, as_index=False)
    .agg(
        unique_cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
        median_case_complexity=("complexity_score", "median"),
        p90_case_complexity=("complexity_score", lambda x: x.quantile(0.90)),
        high_critical_case_share=("high_or_critical", "mean"),
        substitution_case_share=("substitution_case", "mean"),
        mean_events_per_case=("events_in_case", "mean"),
    )
)

event_context = (
    df.groupby(RESOURCE, as_index=False)
      .agg(
          event_count=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
          substitution_event_share=("temporary_substitution_bool", "mean"),
      )
)

role_context = (
    role_membership
    .merge(resource_context, on=RESOURCE, how="left")
    .merge(event_context, on=RESOURCE, how="left")
)

# Governance data must come from an authoritative HR/skills/delegation source,
# not from the role-mining algorithm.
resource_master = pd.read_csv("resource_governance_master.csv")
# Expected fields might include:
# org:resource, formal_title, contract, home_team, certifications,
# approval_limit, security_authorized, pricing_authorized.

role_context = role_context.merge(
    resource_master,
    on=RESOURCE,
    how="left"
)

# Useful flags for human review, not automatic formal-role assignments.
role_context["substitution_sensitive"] = (
    role_context["substitution_case_share"].fillna(0) > 0
)

role_context["mined_role_is_not_formal_authority"] = True

print(
```

# 6. Operational interpretation

**Staffing**

- Forecast capacity by both activity demand and complexity tier. Chen and Dina may cover the same activity family, but critical technical coverage should not assume they are immediately interchangeable.
- Treat Erik, Fatima, and Hana as low-volume or specialized control capacities whose queues require explicit coverage plans. Their importance is not proportional to their event volume.
- Preserve separate staffing assumptions for permanent front office, restricted temporary support, and automation. Their similar activity names conceal different judgment, quality, and availability characteristics.
- Ensure late-shift coverage across technical and quality work, while checking whether security, commercial, and supervisory decisions have unacceptable day-shift dependencies.
- Monitor Mateo’s coordination load separately from task volume. A coordinator can become a bottleneck even when no individual activity count appears excessive.

**Training and progression**

- Consider a defined commercial-backup pathway for Giorgio: pricing certification, delegated thresholds, supervised reviews, and complexity-based limits.
- Clarify Mateo’s training and authority for security triage and preliminary approvals. Escalation brokering can be formalized without converting him into a final security or supervisory decision-maker.

**Risk control**

- Record approval status explicitly—for example, recommended, preliminary, delegated final, and final after recheck—rather than using a single A9 label.
- Introduce severity-sensitive timers for security referrals so that filtering low-risk cases cannot leave critical cases waiting unnoticed.
- During specialist absence, monitor service time, override frequency, rechecks, customer concessions, reopen rates, and residual risk, not merely the number of completed events.
- Audit bot-prechecked and temp-validated cases to detect whether intake throughput is shifting effort into downstream correction.
- Avoid concentrating all commercial continuity in Giorgio or all cross-functional routing in Mateo. Shadow capability should reduce key-person risk rather than create a new one.

**Resilience**

- Establish explicit primary, secondary, and emergency coverage for security, pricing, and supervisory approval, with documented authority thresholds.
- Run controlled substitution exercises before absences occur. Review decision quality and delay, not just whether a substitute can execute the activity in the system.
- Use handover and working-together networks to identify weak links and single points of dependency. Mateo’s centrality, Erik’s specialist isolation, and the bot-to-human boundary deserve particular attention.
- Retain the quality/KB bridge during peak periods; removing it may improve apparent throughput while increasing defects, reopens, and loss of organizational learning.

**Cautions when changing staffing**

1. **The log reflects demand, routing rules, and access permissions as well as competence.** A person may have little experience with an activity because cases were never assigned to them, not because they cannot perform it.
2. **Temporary substitution is not proof of permanent equivalence.** Giorgio’s slower pricing duration and Hana’s rechecks of Mateo’s approvals show why completion counts are insufficient.
3. **Similar activity profiles do not imply similar authority or risk capacity.** This is especially important for Chen versus Dina, Fatima versus Giorgio, and Hana versus Mateo.
4. **Rare roles can be operationally indispensable.** Reducing Erik’s coverage because A6 has low frequency would ignore criticality and single-point-of-failure risk.
5. **The ten-week window contains seasonal load and absences.** Staffing changes should be tested against a longer period, customer and product mix, service time, quality outcomes, and future demand before roles are consolidated or removed.
