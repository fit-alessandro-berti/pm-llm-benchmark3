# 2. Integrated explanation

The early fall in service time is compatible with warm-up, but it is also partly a change in the work arriving at and being assigned within the shift. The first 1–5 events include complex, high-priority carryover cases: mean complexity is 0.72 and high-priority share is 41%. Later bins contain easier, lower-priority cases. Seniors also select complex cases early, especially after a weekend or night backlog. A raw position curve therefore combines worker adaptation with a changing case mix and selective assignment. The drop from 18.8 to 13.6 minutes by positions 16–30, accompanied by fewer errors, supports a warm-up component, but does not measure it on its own.

After the middle of the shift, the pattern changes. Complexity and high-priority share continue to fall, while queue length rises from 31 to 62 and then 88. Service time falls further, but errors and seven-day rework climb. This is consistent with pressure-induced acceleration, abbreviated review, interruptions, or rushed handoffs. The WIP table reinforces a speed-quality tradeoff: under high WIP, service times are shorter even within complexity bands, but errors are more frequent, particularly for high-complexity work. Those are associations, not proof that WIP alone caused the errors, because staffing, demand, selection, and measurement may move with WIP.

Fatigue may contribute to late quality loss, but the aggregate evidence cannot separate it from pressure. Fatigue would be more convincing if later events had more errors at comparable WIP, complexity, activity and clock time, or if errors changed with cumulative work and recovery breaks. Here, late service time is faster, so a simple story in which tired workers merely slow down is not supported. Workers can be fatigued and still speed up under a growing queue, with quality deteriorating as they do so.

Circadian effects are also plausible but unresolved. Night staff have longer average service times, and night work has complex urgent exceptions and fewer events. Day shift ends with the largest queues and high error rates, while Early shift clears inherited backlog. Those operating differences prevent attributing shift-level contrasts to body-clock effects. Clock time, elapsed shift time, resource, queue, and case mix need to be separated in event-level analysis.

Batching and process design further distort event averages. Consecutive Document Review events may be processed as a batch with short recorded durations, and end-of-shift Quality Checks create bursts of corrections. Initial service time can look efficient even when review is delayed or correction work shifts to another worker or shift. Instant Bot_Risk events similarly contribute near-zero durations while potentially creating human correction work, especially under queue pressure. A credible account must follow the full case path and downstream rework, not just the speed of one activity.

Overall, early improvement likely combines warm-up and easier work after complex carryover; later faster service with worse quality likely reflects rising queue pressure, lower observed complexity, batching and possible fatigue. Their contributions vary by resource group, shift, activity and case. None of the provided aggregates identifies a single causal mechanism.

# 3. Proposed analyses

Start by validating event semantics. Confirm that `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp` represent activity start and completion, that timestamps use a consistent timezone, and that `error_flag` and `rework_flag` have stable definitions and attribution. Compute service duration from those timestamps where valid; do not treat a timestamp gap between events as service time. Define waiting time from the process model and available lifecycle/arrival data. Check missing timestamps, duplicate events, negative durations, open cases, events crossing shifts, and whether a case can be handled by multiple resources. Analyze event outcomes with case-level clustering because events in a case are not independent.

A useful event-level model separates service time from quality outcomes. For duration, use a log-duration or robust model; for error and rework, use logistic or suitable count/time-to-event models. Include resource effects, activity-family effects, shift and clock-time controls, flexible elapsed-shift position, complexity and priority, WIP/queue, and interactions such as WIP × complexity, WIP × experience, and position × experience. Account for clustering by resource-shift and case; report uncertainty. Compare adjusted predictions and within-resource contrasts, not only coefficients. Check overlap: if a resource never handles complex cases late at low WIP, the corresponding causal contrast cannot be learned from these data.

Specific comparisons:

- **Within-resource:** compare the same person across shifts and comparable activities/cases. This reduces stable skill differences but does not eliminate selective assignment, changing workload, or learning across calendar time.
- **Within-shift position:** model event position and minutes since shift start separately, with a nonlinear curve or position bins. Include breaks and cumulative active minutes if available. Position and elapsed time are closely related, so inspect collinearity and avoid claiming separate effects where the data lack variation.
- **WIP/queue stratification:** show outcome curves over WIP and queue, including low/medium/high strata and continuous sensitivity analyses. Use values measured before the event; assess interactions with complexity, resource group, and activity. Check whether queue pressure is a proxy for demand or staffing.
- **Complexity adjustment:** use the continuous score flexibly plus priority, activity, exception indicators, document completeness and prior case history if logged. Present both raw and standardized-to-common-case-mix results. Avoid adjusting for downstream variables that may be consequences of pressure when estimating a total pressure effect.
- **Shift and time-of-day controls:** include shift identity, clock hour/day, elapsed time, and resource or shift effects. Compare similar clock hours across schedules and similar elapsed times across shifts. Shift averages alone cannot establish circadian effects.
- **Batching detection:** use `pm4py.discover_batches` as a starting point, then validate detected batches against resource, activity, timestamps, shared batch identifiers, and process rules. Use event distribution and dotted-chart views to locate long runs and end-of-shift bursts.
- **Resource-experience interactions:** model `resource_experience_months` continuously, with interactions for position and WIP. Separate stable resource differences from within-resource change where possible; tenure may correlate with assignment to harder work.
- **Rework/error downstream analysis:** link an error or bot override to later corrections and seven-day rework, activity paths and ownership. Allow the full seven-day follow-up window; exclude or account for right-censored recent cases. Report error and rework separately, because a flagged error may not lead to rework and unflagged errors may be discovered later.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Risk or limitation | How to monitor impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers | May limit fatigue and reduce overtime-related quality loss if excessive hours are a real driver. | A blanket cap may worsen handovers, leave queues uncovered, or move pressure to remaining staff. Scheduled shift length and overtime exposure are not supplied, so it may target the wrong mechanism. | Track actual hours, overtime, break/recovery time, service and waiting time, WIP by hour, error and seven-day rework adjusted for mix, handover volume, and queue spillover to the next shift. Compare similar workloads before and after. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift | Could reduce overload during shift start-up and allow a deliberate allocation or specialist review. | First-hour cases include inherited complex backlog, and seniors often take it. Deferral can age urgent cases, increase queue length, or concentrate complexity later. It risks treating case position as the cause. | Track age and priority of deferred cases, first-hour WIP, complexity mix, time to decision, errors/rework by complexity, and burden transferred to later hours/shifts. Pilot with urgent-case safeguards and compare against current routing. |
| 3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70 | Makes overload visible and can trigger support, rebalancing, or a review pause before rushed errors accumulate. | An alert without capacity or response rules can add noise or encourage gaming of WIP counts. A single threshold may not fit activity, shift, or case complexity. | Monitor alert frequency, response time, staffing/reassignment actions, WIP duration above threshold, adjusted service time, errors/rework, waiting time and downstream backlog. Validate the threshold and track false alarms and unaddressed alerts. |
| 4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias | A constrained randomized or rotation-based assignment can improve comparability and reveal whether selection explains observed differences. | Unrestricted random assignment ignores expertise, urgency, workload and fairness; it can put high-risk cases with unprepared staff. It may also be infeasible or quickly overridden. | If piloted, stratify randomization by skill, complexity, priority and current load. Monitor allocation balance, exception/override rates, service and waiting time, errors/rework, and worker-load fairness. Use it as a learning design with safeguards, not blind routing. |
| 5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours | May support recovery and reduce fatigue-related quality loss, especially where quality falls at fixed pressure and case mix. | Breaks can temporarily reduce capacity during peak demand and may shift work to colleagues. A break will not resolve excessive queue pressure by itself. | Track actual break adherence, workload before/after, coverage and handoffs, adjusted late-period errors/rework, service and waiting time, WIP peaks, and fairness of break coverage. Evaluate a staggered, adequately staffed pilot. |
| 6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift | Could smooth end-of-shift bursts and reduce rushed checks if a safe, explicit queue is used. | Deferral delays error detection and correction, increases ownership ambiguity and handover risk, and can turn a visible quality step into hidden backlog. Quality checks must not be removed to improve throughput. | Keep checks mandatory and risk-based. Measure check completion and delay, aged unchecked cases, correction/rework rates, handover acceptance, downstream customer impact, and next-shift workload. Stop or revise if high-risk checks age or errors escape. |
| 7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks | Could provide timely review or coaching where queues and quality risk peak, and support complex cases or bot overrides. | Senior capacity is finite; pulling seniors from other work may create a new bottleneck or unfairly concentrate difficult cases. The day peak may require broader capacity, not only review. | Track reviewer utilization and queue, time to review, reviewer and originating-resource adjusted errors/rework, complex-case mix, total waiting time, and effects on other shifts. They can undermine learning and accurate reporting. | Do not use raw late-shift error rates for discipline. Monitor whether reporting falls after any performance policy. |

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair because assignment is selective: seniors take complex work early, juniors may receive different case types, and night staff handle urgent exceptions. Aggregated resource-group rates hide within-group variation and have different denominators. A high raw rate is not evidence of carelessness, and a low rate may reflect simpler cases, unobserved under-reporting, or fewer opportunities to detect mistakes.

Selection and survivor effects matter. Cases completed late in a shift are not a random sample of all cases: difficult cases may be held, reassigned, or deferred, while simple cases are batched. Restricting analysis to completed cases can omit unresolved, difficult work. Case complexity scores may be noisy or inconsistently applied, and event timestamps can represent logging rather than actual work. Error flags, rework links, and resource attribution also need validation. Seven-day rework is right-censored for recent cases, and a correction may be logged against the correcting worker rather than the source of the problem.

Queue pressure is partly an organizational condition. Staffing, scheduling, demand forecasts, routing rules, and backlog handovers can create the WIP workers face. Measuring pressure at event start is useful but incomplete: workers can face interruptions, concurrent work, uneven case ownership, and queues that change during service. Analysis should support staffing and process changes rather than locate blame at the end of the chain.

Automation quality and human oversight need separate measurement. An instant Bot_Risk activity is not necessarily a correct or cost-free decision. High-queue corrections may reflect weaker bot output, rushed human validation, different cases, or changed thresholds. Preserve bot version and decision data, independently assess correctness, and attribute downstream impact carefully.

Use error data for improvement with worker participation, transparent definitions, and protected reporting. Adjusted models can still be wrong and should not become an automated ranking or disciplinary score. Report uncertainty, denominators, case mix, and workload alongside quality outcomes; investigate system causes before individual performance conclusions.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Use an event-level dashboard with drill-down by shift, clock hour, activity family, resource group, experience band, complexity, priority, and WIP stratum. Show denominators, missingness, confidence intervals or control limits, and both raw and case-mix-standardized views. Avoid rankings based on small samples. Include at least these indicators:

1. **Service time:** median and upper quantiles from validated start/end timestamps, by activity and case mix; separately flag implausible or missing durations.
2. **Waiting time and case age:** arrival-to-start and between-activity waits, plus age of open and deferred cases, by priority and shift handover.
3. **WIP and queue pressure:** WIP and queue length at event start, time above calibrated thresholds, arrivals/completions, and workload per available resource.
4. **Errors:** error rate with numerator/denominator and adjustment for complexity, priority, activity, shift and pressure; show reporting completeness and later-discovered errors where available.
5. **Rework:** seven-day rework incidence, rework events per case, time to correction, and source activity/resource where attribution is defensible; flag cases without full follow-up.
6. **Complexity and priority mix:** continuous-score distribution and high-complexity/high-priority shares by shift position, resource group and assignment path, so performance changes are seen alongside work mix.
7. **Batching indicators:** batch count and size, consecutive same-activity events, within-batch service-time distribution, queue-batch membership, and end-of-shift QC/correction bursts.
8. **Handover and queue ownership changes:** cases reassigned, queue ownership transfers, handover acceptance, unowned/aged cases, and subsequent wait or rework after transfer.
9. **Worker-load fairness:** active workload and complexity-weighted assignments per available worker, high-WIP exposure, interruptions and break coverage, with group-level disparity measures and no small-sample ranking.
10. **Position and time pattern:** service, error, and rework by within-shift event position and elapsed minutes, displayed with clock time and WIP to distinguish warm-up from pressure and time-of-day patterns.
11. **Automation oversight:** Bot_Risk volume, independently validated error rate, human override/correction rate, review delay, and downstream outcomes by queue pressure and bot version.
12. **Quality-check completion:** time from eligible case to Quality Check, checks deferred across shifts, correction yield, and aged unchecked risk, ensuring throughput changes do not hide quality work.

Use alert thresholds as prompts for operational support (such as additional coverage, queue rebalancing, or protected review time), not as worker-blame triggers. Review the dashboard with frontline staff and recalibrate indicators when routing, staffing, definitions, or logging change.
