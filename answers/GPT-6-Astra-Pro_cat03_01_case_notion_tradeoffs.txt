## 1. Analytical assumptions

**Case notion.** For object type \(T\), one case is one object of type \(T\), containing exactly its directly related events, ordered by timestamp. This produces overlapping event sequences, not necessarily a partition of events: e16 belongs to three Order cases but only one Delivery case.

**Convergence** means multiple objects of the selected case type participate in the same event, causing that event to appear in multiple traces. **Divergence** means a selected object brings several other-object lifecycles into one trace, potentially making separate operations resemble repetition or a single sequential workflow. Thus, P3 creates convergence in the Order projection, while O3’s involvement in P3 and P4 creates divergence within its Order trace. Consolidation does not automatically imply convergence in every projection.

**Copied events are both evidence and duplication.** Each copy legitimately records an object’s participation. Copies become misleading when counted as separate operational occurrences or independent observations. Conversely, eliminating copies can erase genuine milestones.

**Assumptions for calculations.** Event IDs distinguish occurrences; timestamps are comparable on one clock. Differences measure calendar elapsed time, not service effort, business hours, or causal waiting. Missing activities in a projection are not evidence that those activities did not occur.

**Permitted transformations.** The baseline uses direct E2O only. A separately labelled sensitivity variant may propagate billing events through `Invoice → bills → Order → contains → Item`, preserving event IDs and recording derivation paths. This requires treating the supplied O2O links as static for that analysis. It represents billing exposure—not monetary allocation. Unrestricted graph closure is not an acceptable default.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

**1. Split shipment mistaken for rework — Order.**
O1 contains `Pack Package → Pack Package` through e11→e12, `Dispatch → Dispatch` through e15→e16, and `Confirm Delivery → Confirm Delivery` through e23→e24. These concern P1 versus P2, not repeated handling of the same package. The repetitions are informative about split fulfilment, but harmful when labelled rework.

**2. Cross-order P3 counted as two packing operations — Order and Package.**
e13 appears in both O2 and O3, although it records one packing event for P3. Package flattening counts it once, but P3’s `Pack Package → Dispatch → Confirm Delivery` variant looks identical to P1’s despite their different order composition. Order copies correctly describe customer exposure; neither projection alone describes both operational occurrence and composition.

**3. Cross-order P4 creates correlated delay observations — Order.**
e14, e17 and e27 appear in both O3 and O4. O3 additionally gets e13→e14, an eight-minute `Pack Package → Pack Package` edge joining P3 and P4. P4’s packing-to-confirmation span is 23h40m, shared by two orders—not two independent observations of slow package handling. This is useful exposure information but dangerous for workload counting or independent-case inference.

**4. Consolidated D2 changes frequency and time weighting — Package, Item and Delivery.**
e16/e24 occur in two Package cases, three Item cases and one Delivery case. Consequently, `Dispatch → Confirm Delivery` has frequency four in Package but three in Delivery. Its mean is **8h53m45s per package**, compared with **10h13m20s per delivery**. These are different estimands, not contradictory measurements: the package calculation gives D2’s 4h55m interval twice the weight.

**5. Consolidated invoice becomes apparently separate billing — Order and Invoice.**
e19 is copied into O2 and O3, giving four Order-level Issue Invoice occurrences from three distinct events. An activity-only Order view can conceal their common V2 obligation. Invoice flattening avoids that duplication but hides which orders are billed. Both views are useful; treating the copies as distinct invoices, or allocating invoice value without amounts, is unsupported.

**6. Instalments look like rework—or disappear as repetition — Invoice and Order.**
V2 has e19→e22→e25, creating `Record Payment → Record Payment`. M2’s `partially_settles` and M3’s `completes_settlement` qualifiers explain legitimate instalments. First payment takes 35 minutes after issuance; full settlement takes 20h55m. In O2/O3, e24 intervenes between payments, so that payment self-loop disappears. Neither its presence nor absence establishes payment failure.

**7. Singleton lifespan mistaken for instant processing — Payment.**
M2 contains only e22, just as M3 contains only e25. Each observed span is zero, even though the two records mark different stages of V2’s settlement. Zero is a property of observation boundaries, not payment-processing speed. The projection is informative for record existence and counts, not duration.

**8. Financial activity appears to control transport — Order.**
No direct `Dispatch → Confirm Delivery` edge exists in the Order DFG: invoice/payment events and other shipments intervene. Instead, e22→e24 creates `Record Payment → Confirm Delivery` in both O2 and O3. Its 3h20m gap is neither transport duration nor evidence that payment triggered delivery. Treating timestamp adjacency as causality is harmful.

**9. Uniform variants conceal structurally different fulfilment — Item and Package.**
All six Items have one four-activity variant; all four Packages have one three-activity variant. Yet I11 travels in P1/D1, while I31 shares P3 and D2 across orders. I11’s observed lifecycle is 4h45m, versus O1’s 26-hour creation-to-closure span. Simplicity and shortness are informative within these boundaries, not evidence of superior end-to-end performance.

**10. Closure-delay measurement selectively excludes O2 — Order.**
The `Confirm Delivery → Close Order` edge includes O1, O3 and O4 but excludes O2 because e25 intervenes between e24 and e29. Its mean is 7h05m; the mean from each order’s last confirmation to closure is 10h05m. Calling the former “post-delivery closure time” introduces adjacency-selection bias. Neither measure alone identifies administrative inefficiency.

A further model trap is **count equality without object-cardinality equality**: Add Item and Pack Package each occur `(2, 1, 2, 1)` times across the four Order traces. That does not imply one item per package: e13 and e14 each pack two items.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

Create all six labelled extracts, but use Payment chiefly for reconciliation rather than workflow discovery. Use Order for creation-to-closure and explicitly defined fulfilment milestones; Item and Package for their local milestone intervals; Delivery for dispatch-to-confirmation; Invoice for first versus complete settlement. Preserve source event IDs in every extract.

Use an **OC-DFG** to compare typed paths and event/object frequency measures. It does not automatically remove divergence within its Order layer: O1’s two package threads still inhabit one Order lifecycle.

Use an **ET–OT graph** to examine observation coverage: Record Payment connects to Order, Invoice and Payment, not Item. In the checked PM4Py implementation, ET–OT weights count E2O relation rows, not distinct events. ([GitHub][1])

Use an **OTG**, but distinguish inferred relations from declared business relations. PM4Py’s checked OTG implementation aggregates discovered interaction and lifecycle-based object graphs; it does not simply summarize the supplied `contains`, `packs` or `bills` qualifiers. Keep a separate qualified O2O summary. ([GitHub][2])

Use the **object interaction summary** to locate shared participation, while avoiding ownership inference. For example, e16 makes O1 and I21 co-participants, but `O2 contains I21` establishes ownership. The summary enumerates event/object/object combinations, so its rows are not independent business interactions. ([GitHub][3])

Explore **Order-centred execution clustering** using split cardinality, cross-order packaging, consolidated invoicing and instalments. These distinguish O1 from O3 and O2 from O4 despite partial behavioural similarities. Declare extraction boundaries and equivalence criteria; shared events make executions overlap.

Reconcile apparent contradictions by matching **unit, endpoint, event identity and relation policy**. The missing Order payment self-loop is explained by e24, not by rejecting the Invoice finding. Likewise, package-weighted and delivery-weighted means should coexist under different labels.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

**Decision criteria.** Prioritize customer-order accountability while retaining operational units for shipping and settlement. The criterion is meaningful endpoints and responsibility—not trace length.

**Primary and secondary roles.** Order is the primary navigation and reporting object. Delivery and Invoice are essential secondary lifecycles; Package and Item explain physical fulfilment structure. Payment remains a transaction-level audit object.

**Strongest supporting evidence.** Create Order and Close Order relate only to Orders, and every supplied event has a direct Order relation. Thus, order-level start/end measurement needs no invented propagation. Secondary views explain why apparently repeated activities occur between those endpoints.

**Strongest counterargument.** O3 shares P3 with O2, P4 with O4, V2 with O2, and D2 with O1/O2. An Order boundary therefore cuts across genuine operating units. A purely network-centred analysis is a defensible alternative.

**Residual risk.** Analysts may still interpret four Order cases as four independent executions or allocate shared costs repeatedly. The snapshot contains no effort, payment amounts or causal rules supporting such allocations.

**Conditions for change.** For network-capacity optimization, I would abandon a privileged lead. For a narrowly scoped receivables investigation, Invoice would become primary. A standalone Order view would be acceptable only for questions restricted to order-level milestones—not as a complete operational explanation.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

The DFG and skeleton outputs are descriptive, not normative rules. PM4Py’s DFG returns edge and start/end frequencies; skeleton constraints include within-case count equality. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][4])

The clustering call is justified by position B. Its checked implementation groups extracted, renamed, timestamp-free execution descriptions; business equivalence still requires examining boundaries and qualifiers. ([GitHub][3])

The outline below is syntax-checked; PM4Py was unavailable for runtime testing here.

```python
from copy import deepcopy
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

print(ocel)
pm4py_version = pm4py.__version__
required_api = (
    "ocel_flattening", "discover_dfg", "discover_log_skeleton",
    "discover_ocdfg", "discover_otg", "discover_etot",
    "ocel_objects_interactions_summary", "cluster_equivalent_ocel",
)
missing_api = [name for name in required_api if not hasattr(pm4py, name)]
if missing_api:
    raise RuntimeError(f"PM4Py {pm4py_version} lacks: {missing_api}")

base = deepcopy(ocel)  # Do not modify the supplied OCEL.
E, A, TS = base.event_id_column, base.event_activity, base.event_timestamp
O, T = base.object_id_column, base.object_type_column
CASE, ACT, TIME, SOURCE = (
    "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "source_event_id"
)
if base.events[E].duplicated().any() or base.objects[O].duplicated().any():
    raise ValueError("Event IDs and object IDs must each be unique in their tables.")

base.events[TS] = pd.to_datetime(base.events[TS], errors="raise")
base.relations[TS] = pd.to_datetime(base.relations[TS], errors="raise")
base.events[SOURCE] = base.events[E]
base.events = base.events.sort_values([TS, E], kind="stable")
base.relations = base.relations.sort_values([TS, E, O], kind="stable")

# E2O membership is a set: multiple qualifiers do not create extra occurrences.
# Preserve the original qualified relations; deduplicate only the audit membership.
direct_pairs = base.relations[[E, O]].drop_duplicates()
if not direct_pairs[E].isin(base.events[E]).all():
    raise ValueError("An E2O relation refers to an unknown event.")
if not direct_pairs[O].isin(base.objects[O]).all():
    raise ValueError("An E2O relation refers to an unknown object.")

direct_incidence = (
    direct_pairs.merge(base.objects[[O, T]], on=O, validate="many_to_one")
    .merge(base.events[[E, A, TS]], on=E, validate="many_to_one")
)
activity_event_frequency = base.events[A].value_counts()
object_type_frequency = base.objects[T].value_counts()
activity_type_incidence_frequency = (
    direct_incidence.groupby([A, T]).size().unstack(fill_value=0)
)
print(activity_event_frequency)
print(object_type_frequency)
print(activity_type_incidence_frequency)

# Expected (cases, flattened rows, distinct represented events), from this prompt.
expected_counts = {
    "Order": (4, 42, 31), "Item": (6, 24, 16), "Package": (4, 12, 10),
    "Delivery": (3, 6, 6), "Invoice": (3, 7, 7), "Payment": (4, 4, 4),
}
flat_logs, dfg_results, skeletons, variants = {}, {}, {}, {}
lifecycle_spans, edge_provenance, edge_frequency_audits = {}, {}, {}
event_weighted_activity_counts, audit_rows = {}, []

for object_type, expected in expected_counts.items():
    flat = pm4py.ocel_flattening(base, object_type)
    flat = flat.sort_values(
        [CASE, TIME, SOURCE], kind="stable"
    ).reset_index(drop=True)
    observed = (flat[CASE].nunique(), len(flat), flat[SOURCE].nunique())
    if observed != expected:
        raise ValueError(f"{object_type}: expected {expected}, found {observed}")

    multiplicity = flat.groupby(SOURCE)[CASE].transform("nunique")
    flat["event_share_weight"] = 1.0 / multiplicity
    flat_logs[object_type] = flat
    event_weighted_activity_counts[object_type] = (
        flat.groupby(ACT)["event_share_weight"].sum()
    )

    # These PM4Py discovery calls are UNWEIGHTED.
    # The custom weights above and below are separate audit metrics.
    dfg_results[object_type] = pm4py.discover_dfg(flat)
    skeletons[object_type] = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(
        flat, noise_threshold=0.0
    )
    variants[object_type] = (
        flat.groupby(CASE)[ACT].agg(tuple).value_counts()
    )

    spans = flat.groupby(CASE).agg(
        first_event=(TIME, "min"),
        last_event=(TIME, "max"),
        event_count=(SOURCE, "size"),
    )
    spans["observed_span_hours"] = (
        spans["last_event"] - spans["first_event"]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    spans["singleton_span_not_processing_time"] = spans["event_count"].eq(1)
    lifecycle_spans[object_type] = spans

    # Keep the actual event pairs behind each activity-level DFG edge.
    pairs = flat[[CASE, SOURCE, ACT, TIME]].copy()
    successor = pairs.groupby(CASE)[[SOURCE, ACT, TIME]].shift(-1)
    successor.columns = ["next_event_id", "next_activity", "next_timestamp"]
    pairs = pd.concat([pairs, successor], axis=1).dropna(
        subset=["next_event_id"]
    )
    pairs["gap_hours"] = (
        pairs["next_timestamp"] - pairs[TIME]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600

    # Edge weighting uses shared EVENT PAIRS, not endpoint event weights.
    pairs["event_pair_weight"] = 1.0 / pairs.groupby(
        [SOURCE, "next_event_id"]
    )[CASE].transform("nunique")
    edge_provenance[object_type] = pairs
    edge_frequency_audits[object_type] = (
        pairs.groupby([ACT, "next_activity"]).agg(
            unique_event_pairs=("event_pair_weight", "sum"),
            unique_objects=(CASE, "nunique"),
            object_traversals=(CASE, "size"),
            object_weighted_mean_gap_hours=("gap_hours", "mean"),
        )
    )
    audit_rows.append({
        "object_type": object_type,
        "cases": observed[0],
        "rows": observed[1],
        "distinct_events": observed[2],
        "extra_copies": observed[1] - observed[2],
        "event_coverage": observed[2] / len(base.events),
    })

flattening_audit = pd.DataFrame(audit_rows).set_index("object_type")

ocdfg_model = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(base)
ocdfg_frequency_counts = {}
for category in ("activities_ot", "edges"):
    for metric, by_type in ocdfg_model[category].items():
        ocdfg_frequency_counts[(category, metric)] = {
            typ: {
                label: len(instances)
                for label, instances in by_label.items()
            }
            for typ, by_label in by_type.items()
        }

otg_inferred = pm4py.discover_otg(base)
etot_e2o = pm4py.discover_etot(base)
object_interactions = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(base)

# Declared qualified O2O links remain separate from inferred OTG relations.
# This uses the standard PM4Py OCEL 2.0 O2O column names.
qualified_o2o = base.o2o.copy()
types_by_id = base.objects.set_index(O)[T]
qualified_o2o["source_type"] = qualified_o2o[O].map(types_by_id)
qualified_o2o["target_type"] = qualified_o2o[f"{O}_2"].map(types_by_id)
qualified_o2o_type_counts = qualified_o2o.groupby(
    ["source_type", "ocel:qualifier", "target_type"]
).size()

# Position B justifies exploratory Order-centred execution clustering.
# Use input without the added audit attribute; inspect extraction and overlap.
order_execution_clusters = pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel(
    deepcopy(ocel), object_type="Order"
)
cluster_audit = [
    {
        "cluster": cluster_number,
        "central_order": execution.parameters.get("@@central_object"),
        "event_ids": tuple(execution.events[E]),
        "included_orders": tuple(
            execution.objects.loc[execution.objects[T].eq("Order"), O]
        ),
    }
    for cluster_number, executions in enumerate(order_execution_clusters.values())
    for execution in executions
]

print("PM4Py version:", pm4py_version)
print(flattening_audit)
# Models, event pairs, qualifiers and counts remain available for cross-checks.
# Graph appearance is not treated as semantic validation.
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

**Shared-event weighting and removal.** Compare raw activity frequencies with weights \(1/k_T(e)\), where \(k_T(e)\) is the number of related objects of the selected type. For Order, e16’s three copies then sum to one. Deduplicate edge evidence separately by event pair. Removing shared events is a destructive ablation, not cleaning: O2/O3 would lose dispatch evidence. Never deduplicate by activity/timestamp alone—e29 and e30 are distinct closures.

**Direct versus propagated E2O.** Apply only the declared billing-to-item propagation. V2’s e19/e22/e25 would reach I21, I31 and I32. Item rows increase from 24 to 39, without creating events; represented event IDs increase from 16 to 23. I21’s endpoint moves from e24 to e25, demonstrating changed analytical meaning. Unrestricted undirected closure would connect all 24 objects and contaminate every lifecycle with unrelated branches.

**Change the lead object.** Repeat findings using Order, Package, Delivery and Invoice, holding event identity fixed. Instalments affect one of three invoices but two of four orders. Likewise, the D2-weighted transport means differ legitimately. Compare operational questions, not which projection has the simplest variants or longest traces.

**Compare all OC-DFG frequency bases.** At activities, distinguish events, unique objects and event–object incidences. At edges, distinguish unique event pairs, unique traversing objects and object-specific traversals; PM4Py exposes these as separate metrics. ([GitHub][5]) For Dispatch on Order, the counts are **3 events, 4 unique orders, 6 incidences**. For Package `Dispatch → Confirm Delivery`, they are **3 event pairs, 4 packages, 4 traversals**. Do not sum the six projections’ 95 rows as operational event volume.

**Validate endpoints and dependencies.** Compare first versus all-item delivery confirmation: O3’s completion moves 18 hours from e24 to e27; O1’s moves two hours from e23 to e24. Compare V2’s first versus complete settlement similarly. Preserve shared-object dependencies in any subsequent inference; these four interconnected orders cannot establish a general causal effect of consolidation.
