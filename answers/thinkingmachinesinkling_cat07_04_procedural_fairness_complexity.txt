# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The available evidence supports a conclusion of **indeterminate overall procedural fairness, with significant and specific concerns that warrant remediation.** It does **not** support a finding of intentional discrimination, but it also does **not** support a conclusion that the process is fully fair once case mix is accounted for.

**Calibrated findings:**

- **Not established:** That staff intentionally discriminate by applicant group. The adjusted approval disparities (B –5.5 pp, C –2.2 pp, D –3.1 pp) are smaller than raw differences, and some routing differences (V3 fraud review for D; V4 language support for B; V5 accommodation for C) are consistent with documented policy and case characteristics. The evidence is insufficient to prove that group membership causally lowers approval independent of all legitimate factors, particularly because `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` may themselves carry historical bias.

- **Strongly suggested:** That the process imposes **unequal procedural burdens** by group in ways not fully explained by legitimate complexity. Group B and Group C experience longer adjusted cycle times (+2.6 and +4.1 days), more frequent document requests and supervisor reviews, more handovers, and substantially longer waits at rights-preserving steps (3.8 days for language support; 4.7 days for accommodation review). These delays are linked to **resource concentration**—only 7 bilingual staff, 5 accommodation specialists with overlapping roles, and overloaded officers in two regions—rather than to applicant needs alone.

- **Concerning:** That rights-preserving steps function as **procedural friction points** rather than seamlessly integrated services. The absence of separate service-level targets for these steps, combined with high appeal and rework rates for B and D (+4.4 to +5.7 pp adjusted), suggests that applicants experience the process as slower, more burdensome, and less clear, even when outcomes are eventually correct.

- **Uncertain but requiring audit:** Whether `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` transmit historical disparities. If these variables partly encode past organizational inequities, then “adjusting for complexity” over-corrects and masks true unfairness. Sensitivity analyses excluding these variables should be conducted before accepting adjusted disparities as final estimates.

- **Resource fairness:** The allocation of staff—52 general workers for A versus highly concentrated pools for B and C—is structurally inequitable. This is not necessarily illegitimate if specialization improves quality, but the observed queuing and handover costs indicate that specialization is currently under-resourced.

**Conclusion:** The process appears **procedurally unfair in specific dimensions—timeliness, resource access, handover burden, and support-step integration—particularly for Group B and Group C, and possibly for Group D regarding risk-based routing transparency—while the overall fairness of decision-making remains indeterminate.** Remediation should focus on reducing bottlenecks at rights-preserving steps, auditing risk and documentation measures, redesigning handovers, and monitoring adjusted disparities, without removing required support or fraud-review protections.
