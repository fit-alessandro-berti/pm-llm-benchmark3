# Robust redesign of the diagnostic-lab sample process

**Framing.** The peak log is not "the normal log times 1.8". Three structural things change at once, and the evidence is in the logs:

- **Defect generation moves upstream.** Pre-analytical check sends 12.3% of cases to a non-standard path in peak ((780+420)/9730) versus 7.0% in normal ((760+500)/18120). The recollection branch alone doubles (8.0% vs 4.2%), and each recollection costs 60h median on `Request recollection -> Receive sample` — larger than the entire Routine target budget's slack.
- **Assay reliability collapses under load.** `Assay run -> Repeat assay` is 6.7% of assay completions in normal (1140/17030) and 14.8% in peak (1260/8500). Repeats then take 26h median to re-enter the assay (vs 10h) — the repeat loop is not just more frequent, it is far slower, because it re-queues behind a 98%-utilized machine pool.
- **The validation tail changes shape.** In normal, 74% of technically validated cases go straight to release (11700/15890); in peak only 56% (4050/7240). Medical validation touches 26% of released cases in normal (4170/15870) and 50% in peak (3990/8040), and specialist holds more than double in share (610/7240 = 8.4% vs 290/15890 = 1.8%). Medical validators go from the *least* loaded pool (58%) to 93% with a 22h median queue; specialist review goes 43% → 87% with a 36h queue.

Also note a flow-conservation signal: in peak, 9900 cases enter but only 8040 are released and 540 cancelled — roughly **1,300+ cases of net WIP accumulation** over 3 weeks (some is boundary truncation, but the queue metrics confirm real accumulation). The system is not merely slow in peak; it is **not clearing**. Any redesign that only shaves medians while WIP still grows will fail.

Consequently the design objective is **not** minimum average cycle time. It is: *keep Urgent p90 under 18h and keep the system clearing (departures ≥ arrivals) across a set of scenarios, accepting explicit, controlled degradation of Routine.*

---

## 1. Stress scenarios

Each scenario is defined by a **driver**, the **log evidence** that makes it plausible, and a **detection signature**.

### S1 — Mix shock: winter wave, Urgent + Complex-panel surge
Volume +80% on a weekly basis, but mix moves Routine 61%→42%, Urgent 18%→29%, Complex 13%→20%. This is exactly the observed peak log, and it is the *baseline* stress case, not the worst one.
Key amplifier: Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need medical validation and 2.4× more likely to enter specialist hold, so a +7pp Complex share generates a disproportionate load on the two thinnest pools (2-deep specialist, small medical-validator pool). Urgent cases bypass Batch wait, fragmenting machine schedules exactly when machine utilization is 98%.
Detection signature: rising `Technical validation -> Medical validation` *share* (not count) and falling direct-release share.

### S2 — Assay degradation: M2 partial failure or M1 outage
Two sub-variants, deliberately distinguished because they migrate bottlenecks differently:
- **S2a (degradation):** M2 failure rate rises; effective assay capacity drops ~20% and the repeat-assay branch climbs toward 20%. Plausible because repeats are already "clustered after night shifts and M2 usage" and peak repeats are 14.8%.
- **S2b (hard outage):** M1 down for 24–48h. Assay pool at 98% peak utilization has zero absorption; one machine down halves capacity for critical work.
Note the vicious loop: repeats consume assay capacity twice, so a capacity loss raises repeats, which further reduces effective capacity. This is the one scenario with genuine positive feedback.

### S3 — Validation-side absence: medical validator and/or specialist unavailable
The specialist is *already* documented as unavailable every second Friday, at 87% peak utilization and 36h median queue. Scenario: specialist absence coinciding with a peak week, plus one medical validator on sick leave. Effective medical-validation capacity −30%, specialist −50% for 2–3 days.
This scenario matters because it attacks the **terminal** stations: work already fully processed (all reagent, machine and technician cost sunk) sits undischarged. WIP and cost are maximal per case blocked.

### S4 — Courier compression / delay
Arrivals already spike at 10:00 and 17:00; peak courier intake is 88% with 7.5h queue. Scenario: snow or route failure collapses two routes into one 17:00 delivery, or delays it to 21:00. Same daily volume, but arriving as a single wave against an evening-thin staffing profile.
Consequence: `Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check` (already 2h→9h) blows out, and the pre-analytical station at 96% cannot absorb a burst, so the *defect discovery* time lengthens — which lengthens the 60h recollection loop, which pushes cases past the courier's next collection window. Courier delay converts into a multi-day penalty through the recollection loop, not a few hours.

### S5 (additional) — Compound worst realistic case: peak week + second-Friday specialist absence + M2 degradation
This is the scenario to design against, because these three are **positively correlated in time**, not independent: winter waves increase volume *and* staff sickness *and* night-shift running (which drives M2 usage and repeats) *and* weather-driven courier failure. Planning each scenario in isolation systematically underestimates the tail.

### S6 (additional) — Demand *drop* after a wave / false-alarm week
Volume returns to normal while peak-mode policies (large batches, strict urgent-first, outsourcing contracts, evening shifts) are still running. Under-recognised, but the cost is real: batching and outsourcing policies that are protective in peak actively harm Routine and Complex cycle time in normal periods. Any policy switched on must have an explicit switch-*off* trigger.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The stable insight across all scenarios: **the lab has one soft bottleneck (pre-analytical/accession) and two hard, non-substitutable ones (assay machines, medical/specialist validation). Relief applied upstream is converted into arrival-rate increase for a downstream station that cannot flex.**

### S1 — Mix shock
1. **First bind: pre-analytical check (74%→96%, queue 1.8h→9.5h).** Complex-panel and Urgent orders have richer requisition metadata and more tube-level checks; a mix shift raises work-per-case, so utilization rises faster than volume.
2. **Migrates immediately to accession correction (62%→91%, queue 3.0h→14.0h).** Only 2 specialists; the pool is a hard cap. `Manual accession correction -> Test preparation` goes 5h→16h, and correction throughput *falls* under load exactly when its demand rises (420 corrections but only 310 progressing, 110 escalating to recollection — a 26% escalation rate versus 14% in normal). This is the clearest evidence of a saturated station shedding work into the most expensive loop in the process (60h).
3. **Then preparation/batching (69%→89%).** Urgent bypass drops from 74% to 58% — i.e. in peak, *the priority mechanism itself is degrading*, and 42% of Urgent cases now sit in Batch wait, whose exit edge is 22h. That single fact explains most of Urgent p90 = 33h against an 18h penalty threshold.
4. **Then assay (71%→98%).** Fragmented schedules from Urgent bypass reduce effective machine capacity while nominal utilization reads 98%; repeats climb to 14.8% and cost 26h to re-enter.
5. **Terminal migration: technical validation (101% — beyond capacity, i.e. structurally accumulating) then medical validation (58%→93%, queue 2h→22h) and specialist review (36h queue).** Because Complex share is up, the *fraction* of cases needing the thinnest pools grows on top of the volume growth. This is why Complex p90 goes 118h→210h while Routine "only" doubles.

**Migration trap:** relieving registration (I8) or pre-analytical throughput without touching correction capacity simply delivers cases faster into a 91%-utilized, 2-person station. The hint about I8 is correct and generalises: *upstream acceleration in a system whose binding constraint is downstream converts queue time into WIP, not into throughput.*

### S2 — Assay degradation/outage
1. **Assay is instantly the constraint** (no headroom at 98%).
2. **Back-pressure travels upstream:** Batch wait inflates (already 760→1180 cases with a 22h exit), preparation benches back up, and pre-analytical starts holding completed-but-unschedulable work. Upstream stations look *less* utilized while the system gets worse — a classic false-signal risk for anyone reading utilization alone.
3. **Simultaneously, downstream starves then floods.** Technical and medical validators idle during the outage, then receive a compressed catch-up wave. Because technical validation is already at 101%, the recovery wave — not the outage — produces the worst validation queues. Recovery must be *rate-limited*, not maximum-speed.
4. **Feedback loop:** if Routine is dumped onto M2 (I3) during degradation, repeats rise, and each repeat re-consumes the constrained assay capacity, deepening the outage. I3 is a *normal/mild-peak* policy, and it inverts under S2.
5. **Cancellation channel:** the longer the assay backlog, the more samples exceed stability limits, generating new recollections — i.e. an assay problem manufactures pre-analytical work. Peak already shows cancellations rising 3.1%→5.5%.

### S3 — Validation-side absence
1. **Specialist review binds first** (36h queue at 87%, and holds run 610 in peak). `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` is 18h→48h.
2. **Medical validation binds second** — it receives specialist-released cases *plus* the 3420 direct referrals *plus* 120 straight from Repeat assay.
3. **Back-pressure does not stop upstream work**, because technical validation and assay keep producing. So WIP piles at the *most expensive* point: fully-processed, unreleased results. Cash, turnaround penalties and clinical risk all peak here.
4. **Perverse migration:** relieving medical validation with an evening shift (I5) without also relieving specialist review just moves the queue one hop and increases the *specialist* backlog, because more cases reach the hold decision per day. I5 must be paired with a specialist-substitution rule.
5. **Second-order:** clinicians chase unreleased Urgent results, consuming registration-clerk time — the same mechanism the log already flags for recollection loops ("clerks spend time explaining errors to physicians") — degrading intake.

### S4 — Courier compression
1. **Courier intake and registration bind** (88%, 7.5h; clerks 85%). Overtime raises clerk error rate after 9h shifts — and clerk errors are precisely what produce accession corrections and recollections. Absorbing an arrival wave with overtime **manufactures pre-analytical defects**.
2. **Pre-analytical binds next**, at 96%, unable to absorb a burst; `Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check` (2h→9h) is the visible edge.
3. **Delayed defect discovery is the real damage.** A defect found at hour 12 instead of hour 3 misses the next courier collection, so the 60h recollection loop becomes ~72h+. Recollection-to-cancel share is already 42% in peak (370/890) vs 35% normal — late discovery converts into abandoned orders, which is a revenue and clinical-safety loss invisible in cycle-time medians (cancelled cases leave the cycle-time denominator and make the numbers look *better*).
4. **Then a compressed wave hits assay** at a fixed clock hour, colliding with night-shift M2 usage — the documented repeat-cluster condition.

### S6 — Policy hangover
Large batches (I2), release batching (I9) and outsourcing (I10) all trade latency for efficiency. Left on in a normal period, they degrade Complex-panel and Routine service with no compensating benefit, and they hide the true return of normal-period capacity. The bottleneck "migrates" to policy itself.

## 3. Robust redesign package

Selection principle: prefer interventions that (a) **remove work** rather than reshuffle it, (b) **add flexibility** rather than fixed capacity, and (c) help in ≥3 scenarios or are neutral elsewhere. Fixed-capacity additions are reserved for scenario-triggered policies (§4).

### R1 — Early pre-analytical defect triage within 2 hours of receipt (I6), redesigned as a *split* station
Adopt I6, but implement it as a fast 2-hour screen separated from the full pre-analytical check: a short, standardized "is this sample and requisition usable?" gate, with the deeper check retained downstream.

*Why robust:* it is the only intervention that reduces total work rather than moving it. It attacks the highest-leverage number in the log — the 60h `Request recollection -> Receive sample` edge on 780 peak cases. Shortening discovery from ~13h to ≤2h saves roughly half a day per affected case, and, more importantly, catches the defect while the courier round is still collectable, which should reduce the 42% recollection→cancellation rate. It helps in S1 (fewer corrections entering the 2-person pool), S4 (fastest possible discovery on a delayed wave), and indirectly S2/S3 (fewer defective samples occupying constrained assay/validation capacity).

*Assumptions:* that a substantial share of the 780 recollections + 420 corrections are detectable by a cheap rule-based screen (label/volume/haemolysis/requisition completeness). If most defects are only detectable during full analysis, R1 adds a step and helps little.

*Where it fails / what it overloads:* the triage station is itself a new queue directly downstream of courier intake — under S4 it is the first thing to saturate. It must therefore be staffed from the registration/clerk pool with an explicit **shed rule**: if triage queue >2h, triage degrades to a *label-and-volume-only* screen rather than accumulating a backlog. Also, faster clearance of good samples means they arrive at pre-analytical and assay sooner — R1 must ship together with R2, or it is a pure I8-style trap.

### R2 — Elastic accession-correction capacity via cross-training (I4), capped
Cross-train two technical validators for accession correction (I4), **but** make the assignment demand-triggered and capped at one validator at a time.

*Why robust:* accession correction is the sharpest structural fragility in the table — 2 people, 62%→91%, queue 3h→14h, and it "unblocks many cases". A 2-person pool has no statistical resilience: one absence is −50%. Going from 2 to an effective 3 during surges is a large proportional gain at low cost.

*Assumptions:* 4-week cross-training lead time — so this must be started **before** the winter wave, not during it. It also assumes correction work is teachable to validators; if correction requires LIS privileges or accreditation the lab cannot delegate, this fails outright.

*Where it fails / what it overloads:* exactly as the hint says — technical validation is already at **101%** in peak. Pulling a validator out unaided makes the terminal queue worse. Therefore the cap: only one at a time, only while `accession queue > 6h AND technical-validation queue < 8h`, and reversed automatically otherwise. If both are saturated simultaneously (S5), the correct move is not reallocation but **demand shedding** (R5).

### R3 — Two-lane flow with a *reserved-capacity* rule instead of strict priority (replaces I1, tames I2/I3)
Do **not** adopt I1 as a strict urgent-first dispatch. Instead, reserve fixed capacity shares at the two hard constraints:

- Preparation and assay reserve an explicit share of each shift's slots for Urgent (sized to observed Urgent share +5pp, so ~34% in peak), a share for Complex-panel (~20%), and the remainder for Routine. Unused Urgent slots roll to Complex, then Routine, after a short hold window (e.g. 45 min).
- Routine batches are enlarged (I2, 12→24) **only within the Routine lane**, so batching efficiency never delays Urgent.
- Add a **Routine age-escalation rule**: any Routine case past 40h elapsed is promoted into the reserved pool. Without this, strict or near-strict prioritisation produces exactly the starvation the hints warn about — Routine peak p90 is already 120h against a 72h target.

*Why robust:* reserved-capacity policies dominate strict priority under uncertainty because they bound the damage to the deprioritized class. Strict I1 is optimal only if Urgent volume is known and small; in S1 Urgent is 29% and rising, at which point strict priority makes Routine unbounded and drives total WIP up (more WIP → more sample-stability failures → more recollections → more upstream load). It also stabilizes machine scheduling: the log says Urgent bypass "fragments machine schedules", and reserving *slots* preserves batch integrity in a way ad-hoc bypass does not — plausibly recovering some of the effective assay capacity lost at 98% utilization.

*Assumptions:* that slots can be reserved at shift granularity and that Urgent arrival is reasonably predictable within a shift. Reserved capacity wastes capacity when the reservation is wrong — hence the roll-forward rule.

*Where it fails / what it overloads:* accelerating Urgent and Complex through assay pushes them onto **medical validation and specialist review** — the S3 bottleneck. R3 without R4 relocates the problem from a 22h assay queue to a 22h/36h validation queue, with worse economics because the work is already paid for.

### R4 — Reshape the validation tail: autoverification + a substitution ladder
Three components, all aimed at reducing *demand for scarce validators* rather than adding validators:

1. **Rule-based autoverification for technical validation** on Routine results within reference limits and with passing QC. Technical validation at 101% is structurally infeasible; the only durable fix is removing cases from it. In normal periods 74% of technically validated cases release directly — those are the autoverification candidates.
2. **Tighten the specialist-hold criterion** and require a documented reason code. Holds jump 1.8%→8.4% of technically validated cases in peak. Some of that is genuine Complex-panel mix, but a 4.7× share increase suggests defensive escalation under time pressure. Reason codes make it measurable.
3. **Substitution ladder for specialist review:** a named deputy plus an on-call remote-review arrangement, activated by queue threshold, not by absence calendar. The "one specialist unavailable every second Friday" is a *known* recurring outage and should be planned capacity, not a surprise.

*Why robust:* helps S1, S3, S5, and the recovery phase of S2 (the catch-up wave lands on validation). Reducing validation demand is scenario-independent.

*Assumptions:* regulatory acceptance of autoverification with an audited rule set, and that a competent deputy exists. If accreditation forbids autoverification, fall back to a **structured triage of the technical-validation queue** (release-ready vs review-needed) to protect the easy 56–74%.

*Where it fails:* autoverification shifts risk from queueing to quality. Its failure mode is silent — a bad rule releases wrong results. It must ship with a mandatory random audit sample (e.g. 2% retro-reviewed) and immediate suspension on any QC drift.

### R5 — Explicit, pre-agreed service-degradation and demand-shedding protocol
Written before the wave, with clinical sign-off: which Routine test panels are deferred, which referring sites get extended turnaround, what Routine target becomes in a declared peak (e.g. 96h), and when outsourcing (I10) triggers. Include a **stop-intake / defer-scheduling** lever for elective Routine work.

*Why robust:* the peak log shows arrivals exceeding departures. No internal rearrangement fixes an over-capacity condition; only demand reduction or capacity addition does. Making degradation explicit and negotiated converts uncontrolled, invisible degradation (Routine p90 120h, cancellations 5.5%) into controlled, communicated degradation — which is both clinically safer and contractually defensible, since Urgent carries the penalties.

*Where it fails:* it is organizationally, not technically, hard, and it requires committing in advance to disappointing someone. It also depends on Routine demand being genuinely deferrable; if referrers simply re-order later, the wave is delayed, not reduced.

### R6 — Smooth arrivals at the source (I7 + I8, sequenced)
Adopt I7 (courier slot smoothing) — it is nearly free, reduces the 10:00/17:00 spikes that drive the 7.5h intake queue, and helps S1, S4 and S2 (steadier assay feed, less night-shift clustering that correlates with repeats). Adopt I8 (registration automation) **only after** R1/R2 are live, because I8's whole effect is to deliver cases faster into pre-analytical (96%) and correction (91%).

*Caveat:* I8's real value is not clerk time — it is **error elimination**. Auto-registered orders with complete digital metadata should generate fewer accession corrections. Measure I8 by correction rate on auto vs manual orders, not by clerk utilization. If auto-registration does *not* reduce corrections, I8 is a bottleneck-migration trap and should be deferred.

### Interventions deliberately rejected or restricted
- **I1 strict urgent-first:** rejected; replaced by R3 reserved capacity + age escalation.
- **I3 dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex, Routine to M2:** accepted only as a *normal and mild-peak* policy, suspended under S2. Sending all Routine volume to the higher-failure machine at high load raises repeats, and repeats consume the shared constraint twice. Instead, prefer a soft rule: reserve M1 slots for Urgent/Complex but allow Routine on M1 when M1 would otherwise idle, and cap M2 load when its repeat rate exceeds threshold.
- **I9 hold Routine releases for batch reports:** rejected during peaks. It adds waiting exactly where Routine p90 is already 120h and saves effort at *release*, which is not a bottleneck (`Medical validation -> Release result` is only 2h/5h). Acceptable as a normal-period efficiency only.
- **I10 outsource Complex overflow:** accepted as a scenario-triggered policy (§4), not a standing design, because the 12h transport delay is pure loss when specialist queue is short, and it adds traceability/accreditation risk on the most clinically complex cases.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Each policy has an ON trigger, an OFF trigger, and a named owner decision. Triggers are stated on **leading** measures (queues, shares, rates) — not on cycle time, which is known only after the fact.

| Policy | Scenario | ON trigger | OFF trigger | Downstream station it may overload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 — Peak mode declared** (activates R5 degraded Routine target 96h, Routine batch 24, release-batching I9 suspended) | S1, S5 | Weekly arrivals >1.4× 8-week baseline **for 3 consecutive days** AND Urgent share >24% | Arrivals <1.15× baseline for 5 consecutive days AND ≥2 pools below 80% utilization | Complex-panel: larger Routine batches free bench time but push more work to assay; watch Batch-wait entries |
| **P2 — Evening medical-validation shift (I5)** | S1, S3, S5 | Medical-validation queue >8h **or** unreleased technically-validated WIP >250 cases | Medical queue <4h for 48h | Release/reporting and specialist review — more cases reach the hold decision per day; pair with P3 |
| **P3 — Specialist substitution ladder + tightened hold criteria (R4.2/R4.3)** | S3, S5 | Specialist queue >12h **or** known absence (every second Friday) coinciding with peak mode | Specialist queue <6h and no scheduled absence in 72h | Medical validation absorbs the released holds — sequence P3 after P2 is running |
| **P4 — Assay contingency: suspend I3 dedication, single shared pool, defer Routine assays** | S2a, S2b | M1 or M2 unavailable >4h, **or** repeat-assay rate >12% over a rolling 24h | Both machines available 24h and repeat rate <8% | Preparation/Batch wait back up first; then a validation catch-up wave — enforce P5 |
| **P5 — Rate-limited recovery after outage** | S2 recovery, S4 recovery | Declared at the moment P4 clears | Backlog cleared to <1 day of arrivals | Technical validation (101%) — release backlog at ≤120% of normal validation throughput, oldest-Urgent-first, rather than flooding |
| **P6 — Courier surge protocol: flexed triage staffing, no clerk shift >9h, hold-and-screen at dock** | S4 | Single delivery >1.6× mean route volume, or route delayed >2h | Intake queue <2h for a full day | Pre-analytical check; the mitigation is the R1 degraded label-only screen plus deferring non-urgent registration data entry, **not** clerk overtime (which raises error rate → corrections) |
| **P7 — Outsource Complex-panel overflow (I10)** | S3, S5 | Specialist queue >24h AND Complex WIP awaiting specialist >60 cases AND P3 already active | Specialist queue <12h | Adds transport delay and split traceability; cap at a fixed daily quota and exclude cases already >48h old (12h transport would blow their target) |
| **P8 — Routine intake deferral (R5 shedding)** | S5 only | Departures < arrivals for 5 consecutive days AND ≥3 pools >90% | Departures ≥ arrivals for 3 days | Creates a post-wave rebound; schedule the deferred work into recovery capacity explicitly |
| **P9 — Normal-mode restoration** | S6 | P1 OFF | — | Explicitly reverts batch size to 12, ends evening shift, ends outsourcing; failure to revert is itself a designed-in risk |

Note the deliberate ordering constraint: **P3 before/with P2**, and **P5 always after P4**. Activating the downstream relief before the constraint that feeds it is the single most common way these packages fail.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

**Caused by the redesign itself:**

1. **Autoverification (R4) converts a visible queueing failure into an invisible quality failure.** Today a bad result is caught by a human at technical validation; afterwards it is caught by a rule, and rule gaps surface only in patient outcomes or retrospective audit. This is a genuinely worse *kind* of failure even if it is a rarer one. Mitigation (mandatory 2% audit, auto-suspend on drift) reduces but does not remove it.
2. **Reserved capacity (R3) wastes capacity when the mix forecast is wrong.** If Urgent arrives below reservation, slots idle at a 98%-utilized station for the length of the roll-forward window. In a fast-moving wave, mis-sized reservations could cost several percent of effective assay capacity — precisely what the lab cannot spare.
3. **Cross-training (R2) makes technical validation and accession correction *correlated* failure points.** Two previously independent pools now share people, so a shock that hits one now propagates to the other. The cap and the paired threshold are the control, but under S5 (both saturated) the policy has no good move and defaults to shedding.
4. **Policy complexity is a failure mode.** Nine triggered policies with ordering constraints will be operated by tired supervisors at 02:00 during a winter wave. If the triggers are not automated in the LIS dashboard, the realistic outcome is that policies are switched on late and off never (scenario S6). The redesign should be considered incomplete until the triggers are computed automatically.
5. **Explicit degradation (R5) can become permanent.** Once a 96h Routine target survives one winter, it tends to become the de facto standard.

**Not resolved by the redesign:**

6. **Hard assay capacity.** A true M1 outage during peak (S2b) cannot be absorbed by any policy; two machines at 98% have no redundancy, and repeats consume the constraint twice. This is the one place where a capacity argument (a third machine, a standby service contract with guaranteed 8h response, or a reciprocal-capacity agreement with the partner lab) is genuinely warranted, and the redesign does not fix it.
7. **The 2-person accession pool and the single specialist** remain structurally thin even after R2/P3. A deputy who is themselves absent leaves no path.
8. **The 60h recollection loop is bounded below by physical logistics** — even perfect 2h detection cannot beat courier round-trip time. Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure cases that need recollection will still exceed targets.
9. **Repeat-after-failure cases (9% of peak, 240h p90) receive the least direct attention** in this package. They are penalized twice by every prioritization rule, since they re-enter mid-process and carry no urgency flag. Consider giving re-entering cases an inherited priority equal to their elapsed age.
10. **Cancellations remain a blind spot.** Rising cancellations (3.1%→5.5%) *improve* every cycle-time statistic while representing the worst clinical outcome. Any dashboard that omits cancellation rate will show a redesign succeeding as it fails.
11. **Correlated shocks (S5).** The scenarios were analysed largely one at a time; the compound case has fewer degrees of freedom than the sum of its parts, and the honest answer is that under S5 the lab degrades — the design goal is that it degrades *predictably and in the chosen direction*, not that it does not degrade.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

**Design rule:** leading indicators are measurable *before* a case finishes; lagging metrics confirm outcomes but cannot steer. During peaks, review leading indicators twice daily (morning and after the 17:00 courier wave).

### Leading indicators — daily/twice-daily, drive policy triggers

| # | Indicator | Green | Amber | Red | Why it leads |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | **Departures ÷ arrivals (24h rolling)** | ≥1.00 | 0.90–0.99 | <0.90 for 3 days → P8 | The single most important number. Peak log shows ~9900 in vs 8580 out; a system not clearing cannot be fixed by sequencing |
| L2 | **Total WIP, split by station** | <1.2 days of arrivals | 1.2–2.0 | >2.0 | Rises before cycle time does |
| L3 | **Unreleased technically-validated cases** | <100 | 100–250 | >250 → P2 | Most expensive WIP; pure validation-side signal |
| L4 | **Medical-validation queue (h)** | <4 | 4–8 | >8 → P2 | Normal 2h, peak 22h — huge dynamic range |
| L5 | **Specialist-review queue (h)** | <6 | 6–12 | >12 → P3; >24 → P7 | Normal 5h, peak 36h; thinnest pool |
| L6 | **Accession-correction queue (h)** | <3 | 3–6 | >6 → R2 reallocation (if technical-validation queue <8h) | Normal 3h, peak 14h; 2-person cap |
| L7 | **Technical-validation queue (h)** | <5 | 5–8 | >8 → blocks R2 reallocation, triggers P5 rate limiting | The 101% station; gates other policies |
| L8 | **Repeat-assay rate (rolling 24h), split M1/M2** | <8% | 8–12% | >12% → P4, suspend I3 | Normal 6.7%, peak 14.8%; a rising repeat rate destroys assay capacity before queues show it |
| L9 | **Share of Urgent cases entering Batch wait** | <15% | 15–30% | >30% | Bypass decayed 74%→58% in peak — direct early warning that the priority mechanism is failing, visible long before Urgent p90 breaches 18h |
| L10 | **Pre-analytical defect rate (recollection + correction share)** | <8% | 8–12% | >12% | Normal 7.0%, peak 12.3%; upstream quality drives everything downstream |
| L11 | **Time from `Receive sample` to defect decision (p90)** | ≤2h | 2–6h | >6h | Directly measures whether R1 is working; gates the 60h loop |
| L12 | **Correction → recollection escalation rate** | <15% | 15–25% | >25% | Normal 14%, peak 26% — a saturated correction pool shedding work into the most expensive loop |
| L13 | **Arrival concentration: max hourly share of daily volume** | <18% | 18–25% | >25% → P6 | Measures the 10:00/17:00 spike directly |
| L14 | **Clerk hours worked >9h (count of staff/day)** | 0 | 1–2 | ≥3 | Overtime past 9h raises error rate → tomorrow's corrections and recollections |
| L15 | **Case-mix shares (Urgent, Complex) rolling 3-day** | Urgent <22% | 22–28% | >28% → P1 with L-volume | Mix shift, not volume, is the real stressor |
| L16 | **Machine availability + queue at assay (h)** | <5h | 5–12h | >12h or a machine down >4h → P4 | Hard constraint, no headroom |

### Lagging outcome metrics — weekly, for accountability and calibration, **not** for steering

- Urgent p90 cycle time vs 18h contractual threshold, and count/value of penalties incurred (peak p90 was 33h).
- Routine p90 vs the target in force (72h normal / 96h declared-peak); % Routine breaching.
- Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure median and p90 (111h/210h and 145h/240h in peak — the true tail of the process).
- % of cases with at least one queue >12h (22% → 54%): a good composite health measure, available only after completion.
- Overall rework rate (7.5% → 11.8%).
- **Cancellation rate and recollection→cancellation share** (3.1%→5.5%; 35%→42%) — must be reported *alongside* every cycle-time figure, because cancelled cases silently flatter cycle-time statistics.
- Autoverification audit-failure rate and any released-result corrections — the safety ledger for R4.
- Post-wave recovery time: days from P1-off to all pools <80% utilization, and clearance of deferred P8 backlog.

**Reporting discipline:** report every cycle-time metric as a distribution (median **and** p90) segmented by case type, never as a single overall average. The overall average is the one number that is guaranteed to be dominated by the 42–61% Routine share and to conceal the Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure tails where the real damage occurs.
