# 1. Objectives and constraints

## 1.1 What the policy is optimizing

The policy is deliberately multi-objective. No single weighting is "correct"; the weights encode a management stance that must be visible and revisable.

| Objective | Operational meaning | Proxy measure |
|---|---|---|
| O1 Short-term throughput | Cases completed per shift, low idle time | Service time, queue length |
| O2 SLA protection | Urgent/high cases finish before deadline | SLA slack ratio = time_remaining / expected_service_time |
| O3 Quality / low rework | Work done by someone competent enough | Rework rate, appeal rate, skill-mismatch rate |
| O4 Continuity | Cases stay with an owner who already has context | Reassignment count, handover cost band |
| O5 Workload fairness | No systematic dumping on the same few people | Gini/CV of assignments per resource, open-case spread |
| O6 Skill development | Trainees get real, safe exposure to their documented priority | Training assignment share |
| O7 Resilience | No single-point-of-failure specialists, no permanent bottleneck | Specialist concentration index, bus-factor per activity |
| O8 Explainability | Every assignment has a short human sentence | % assignments with a valid `assignment_reason` code |

These objectives genuinely conflict. O2 and O3 push work to the same two or three specialists; O5 and O7 push it away from them. O4 (continuity) fights O3 whenever the current owner is weak — and the historical evidence quantifies both sides: keeping a weak owner costs a median +3.3h service time and more rework, but changing owner on a high-complexity case costs a median +2.7h waiting, and >2 reassignments raise rework 1.8x and appeals 1.4x. There is no assignment that dominates on all axes; the policy makes the trade explicit rather than hiding it in a single score.

## 1.2 Hard constraints (safety, compliance, contractual)

These are filters, never weighted. A candidate violating any of them is removed from the eligible set; if the eligible set becomes empty, the case escalates to a human dispatcher rather than being force-assigned.

- **H1 Authorization.** skill = 0 → the resource may not perform the activity, in any form, supervised or not. Supervision upgrades *competence*, not *authorization*.
- **H2 Security Review.** Executed by skill 3 (R7_Gita, R8_Hugo), or by skill 1–2 only under explicit named supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo, recorded in the log.
- **H3 Final Approval.** Finalized only by skill 2–3. Skill 1 may prepare the file and record the recommendation; the sign-off event must carry a skill-2+ resource.
- **H4 High-priority floor.** No urgent/high-priority case to a skill-1 resource unless a named supervisor co-signs.
- **H5 Batch legality.** Quality Check may be batched only for priority normal/low **and** SLA remaining > 24h.
- **H6 Batch cap.** No resource receives more than 4 new assignments in one decision batch.

## 1.3 Soft constraints (preferences, tradeable with justification)

Each may be violated, but only with a logged reason code that becomes an audit event.

- **S1 Overtime protection.** Avoid new load on overtime_risk = high (R2_Bruno, R4_David, R9_Iris) unless SLA risk is urgent and no safer eligible alternative exists.
- **S2 Continuity.** For high-complexity (≥ 0.7) cases with high handover cost, prefer the current owner unless their skill ≤ 1 and no supervision is feasible.
- **S3 Reassignment brake.** A case already at reassignment_count ≥ 2 needs a strictly stronger justification to move again (the 1.8x rework evidence).
- **S4 Training quota.** ≥ 20% of normal/low-priority assignments support a documented training priority, provided SLA slack stays comfortable.
- **S5 Bottleneck relief.** Do not route to a specialist when a competent (skill 2) resource with slack can absorb the work at acceptable risk.
- **S6 Capacity realism.** Expected service time must fit hours_remaining, or the case is explicitly queued (not silently assumed done today).

The separation matters socio-technically: workers can challenge a soft trade-off ("why did I lose that case?") and get a substantive answer, while hard constraints are simply not negotiable and are never used as cover for workload decisions.

# 2. Assignment policy

## 2.1 Shape of the algorithm

Greedy, priority-ordered, per-case scoring with capacity feedback — not a global optimum. That is a deliberate choice: a global ILP would produce assignments no worker could reconstruct, and would churn wildly when one input changes. A greedy pass over a stable case ordering is explainable ("your case came up, and here is who was eligible"), stable under small perturbations, and cheap enough to re-run whenever the queue changes.

## 2.2 Pseudocode

```
INPUT: queue Q, resources R, skill matrix S, constraints, weights W
STATE: new_assign[r] = 0, hours_left[r], open[r]

# --- Step 0: order the queue by urgency, not by arrival ---
def urgency(c):
    p = {urgent: 3, high: 2, normal: 1, low: 0}[c.priority]
    slack = c.sla_remaining / max(expected_service_time(c), 0.5)
    return (p, -slack)                 # higher priority first, tightest slack first

Q_sorted = sort(Q, key=urgency, descending)

# --- Step 1: batching pre-pass (H5) ---
for group in group_by(Q_sorted, activity="Quality Check"):
    eligible_batch = [c for c in group
                      if c.priority in {normal, low} and c.sla_remaining > 24]
    if len(eligible_batch) >= 2:
        mark them as one batch unit, assigned together

# --- Step 2: per-case eligibility filter (hard constraints) ---
def eligible(c):
    E = []
    for r in R:
        if S[r][c.required_skill] == 0:            continue   # H1
        if new_assign[r] >= 4:                     continue   # H6
        if expected_service_time(c) > hours_left[r] and c.priority == urgent:
                                                   continue   # S6 as hard for urgent
        sup = None
        if c.required_skill == "Security" and S[r]["Security"] < 3:
            sup = pick_supervisor(["R7_Gita","R8_Hugo"])       # H2
            if sup is None: continue
        if c.required_skill == "Approval" and S[r]["Approval"] < 2:
            sup = pick_finalizer(skill>=2)                     # H3: r prepares only
            if sup is None: continue
        if c.priority in {urgent, high} and S[r][c.required_skill] < 2 and sup is None:
            sup = pick_supervisor(skill>=3, same_or_adjacent_team)   # H4
            if sup is None: continue
        E.append((r, sup))
    return E

# --- Step 3: score each eligible (resource, supervisor) pair ---
def score(c, r, sup):
    # (a) skill match, saturating: 3 is not 1.5x better than 2 for easy work
    need = 2 + (c.complexity > 0.7)            # hard cases really want a 3
    fit  = min(S[r][c.required_skill], 3)
    skill_match = 1.0 - 0.35 * max(0, need - fit) + 0.10 * max(0, fit - need)
    if sup: skill_match += 0.15                # supervision partially restores fit

    # (b) SLA urgency: how safely does THIS resource clear it in time
    eta   = expected_service_time(c) * (1 + 0.25*max(0, need - fit)) \
            + queue_delay(r)
    sla   = clip((c.sla_remaining - eta) / max(c.sla_remaining, 1), -1, 1)

    # (c) continuity / handover cost
    if r == c.current_owner:
        cont = 0.5 + 0.5*c.complexity + {high:0.6, medium:0.3, low:0.1}[c.hcost]
    else:
        cont = -( {high:0.6, medium:0.3, low:0.1, none:0.0}[c.hcost]
                  + 0.4*c.complexity
                  + 0.25*c.reassignment_count )        # S3 brake

    # (d) workload + overtime fairness
    load  = -(open[r]/max_open) - 0.5*(new_assign[r]/4) \
            + 0.4*(hours_left[r]/max_hours)
    if overtime_risk[r] == "high":
        load -= 0.8 if c.priority != "urgent" else 0.3   # S1

    # (e) training / development
    train = 0.0
    if training_priority[r] == c.required_skill and c.priority in {normal, low} \
       and c.sla_remaining > 3 * expected_service_time(c) and c.complexity < 0.7:
        train = 0.6 + (0.2 if sup else 0.0)
    if training_quota_unmet(): train *= 1.5              # S4

    # (f) resilience / bottleneck avoidance
    res = 0.0
    if S[r][c.required_skill] == 3 and scarce(c.required_skill):
        res -= 0.4 * (1 - urgency_pressure(c))   # protect scarce specialists
                                                 # on non-urgent work (S5)
    res -= 0.3 * concentration_share(r, c.required_skill)  # anti bus-factor-1

    return (W.skill*skill_match + W.sla*sla   + W.cont*cont
          + W.load*load        + W.train*train + W.res*res)

# --- Step 4: choose, with tie-breaking ---
best = argmax over E of score(c, r, sup)
# tie-break ladder (deterministic, publishable):
#   1. current owner wins ties (continuity is the default, not the exception)
#   2. then non-high-overtime-risk
#   3. then lower open-case count
#   4. then supports a documented training priority
#   5. then same team as current owner (cheaper context transfer)
#   6. then stable resource id (so the roster is auditable, not random)

# --- Step 5: commit and feed back ---
assign(c, best); new_assign[r] += 1; hours_left[r] -= eta; open[r] += 1
record assignment_reason = dominant scoring term + any soft-constraint override

# --- Step 6: exception handling ---
if E is empty:
    if c.priority == urgent: escalate to duty dispatcher, page on-call specialist,
                             and log an "eligibility starvation" event
    else: park case, re-evaluate next decision cycle, raise capacity alert
if best.score < accept_threshold:
    assign provisionally + flag for supervisor review (do not silently accept)
if soft constraint violated: require reason code
    {OVERTIME_OVERRIDE, CONTINUITY_BREAK, REASSIGN_3PLUS, SPECIALIST_PULL}
```

Weights used below (starting point, to be tuned on historical logs, not treated as truth): W.skill 1.0, W.sla 1.2, W.cont 0.9, W.load 0.8, W.train 0.5, W.res 0.4. The SLA weight is highest only because deadline misses are contractual; in a lower-stakes queue I would raise W.train and W.load instead.

## 2.3 Supervision as a first-class object

Supervision is not a note in a comment field. It is a second resource on the event, with its own small time cost (≈15–25% of the executor's service time charged to the supervisor's hours) and its own log record. Otherwise "supervised training" becomes a way to route hard work to trainees for free, which is exactly how training schemes lose worker trust.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

## 4.1 What the baselines would actually do here

- **First-available** would push C001 (urgent Security, complexity 0.88) toward whoever is least loaded and non-zero-skill — R3_Celine at skill 1 or R9_Iris at skill 1 — which violates H2 outright unless supervision is bolted on. It would spray C007 away from Celine despite reassignment_count 3, and would load R10_Jonas and R6_Farid heavily because they are simply the emptiest.
- **Specialist-only** would send C001, C008 **and** C015 into {Gita, Hugo}, both Approvals to Farid, both Diagnoses toward Celine/David, and all three Quality Checks to the skill-3 pool — recreating exactly the Security and Approval queues the historical evidence already documents.

## 4.3 Where the proposed policy is genuinely worse

Intellectual honesty matters more than advocacy here:

1. **Complexity cost.** It requires maintaining a skill matrix, training priorities, overtime flags and handover bands. Stale inputs produce confidently wrong assignments — worse than first-available, which needs almost no data.
2. **Slower on trivial work.** For a queue of routine, low-complexity cases with generous SLAs, first-available is close to optimal and vastly cheaper. The proposed policy earns its keep only when complexity and SLA pressure are heterogeneous — which this queue is.
3. **Supervision is a hidden tax.** The 33% training share consumes Celine's and Hugo's capacity on the same day they carry the hardest cases. If supervision time is not actually protected, the training component degrades into unsupervised trainee work — the failure mode the policy exists to prevent.
4. **Greedy, not optimal.** Ordering by urgency means late cases take what's left. C013 landing on Jonas is a leftovers artifact, not a considered match.
5. **It can be gamed.** Once workers know load reduces incoming assignments, "current open cases" becomes a managed number. Any deployed policy must anticipate this rather than assume good-faith inputs forever.

# 5. Explanation to workers

**How today's cases were assigned**

Every morning we look at the open queue and decide who picks up what. Here is how that decision is made, so nothing about it is a surprise.

**We start with what the work requires.** Some activities have rules that aren't ours to bend — Security Reviews are done by a security specialist, or by someone working with Gita or Hugo alongside them. Final Approvals are signed by someone qualified to sign; a colleague can prepare the file, but the signature carries responsibility and stays with the person authorized to give it. These aren't judgements about anyone; they're the boundaries the work comes with.

**We try to leave cases where they are.** If you already own a case, you know things about it that no handover note captures. When we move a case, it costs the customer time and it costs you the context you built. So the default is: you keep it. We move a case only when there's a specific reason, and you'll be told what that reason was. Today: C001 moved because it needs a security specialist and the deadline is three hours away. C003 and C014 moved off David because his open list is at fourteen and he's already flagged for overtime — that's about the load, and nothing else. C006 moved off Iris for the same reason.

**We watch how much you're carrying, not just how many cases.** Nobody gets more than four new cases in one round. If you're flagged for overtime risk, we route around you unless something urgent leaves no safe alternative. David and Iris get no new work today. That's intentional. It isn't a signal about your work — it's a signal that we gave you too much of it.

**We make room for learning.** Some cases are chosen because they're a good place to build a skill you're working toward — with someone experienced alongside you, and only when the deadline gives you room to think. Amal takes C009 with Celine available; Jonas prepares the C010 approval file with Bruno signing. If you're learning, you get real work, not busywork, and you get backup. One honest note: Jonas, your development priority is Security, and we couldn't give you a Security case today — the rules require a starting qualification you haven't been given yet. You'll shadow C015, but shadowing isn't the same thing, and getting you properly onto that track is a management job, not something the daily queue can solve.

**We try not to lean on the same few people.** When only two people can do something, every case in that area lands on them, and that isn't sustainable for them or safe for us. So when a case can be handled well by someone competent rather than by the one specialist, we sometimes make that choice — to protect the specialist's day and to build a second pair of hands.

**Where we know we're stretching.** Eva keeps C016 even though it's a hard case, because moving it would cost more than it saves — but she has Celine to consult, and she should use that. Farid has two urgent cases; C002 goes first because its clock is shorter. If any of this doesn't fit what you're seeing on the ground, say so — you can see something the queue snapshot can't, and the assignment can be changed. We track how often that happens, because frequent overrides mean the policy is wrong, not that you are.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Each indicator has a definition, a computation path from the log, and — critically — a stated failure mode, because an indicator that can only look good is not an audit.

| # | Indicator | Definition | Target / alarm | What it can hide |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Handover frequency** | Edges per 100 cases in the handover-of-work network; also mean handovers per case, split by complexity band | Stable or falling vs. pre-policy baseline | Falling handovers may mean hoarding, not efficiency — pair with A6 |
| A2 | **Reassignment count** | Distribution of `reassignment_count` at case close; share of cases with >2 | Share >2 should fall; alarm if it rises with rework | Counts reassignments, not their quality — a single bad move outweighs three good ones |
| A3 | **Skill mismatch rate** | Share of events where executor skill < required level for the activity, unsupervised | Near 0 for hard constraints; tracked (not zero) for skill-2-on-complex-work | Depends on the skill matrix being current; audit matrix freshness separately |
| A4 | **Workload distribution** | Gini / CV of assignments per resource per period; also concentration by *complexity-weighted* load | Gini falling; complexity-weighted spread also falling | Equal case counts can mask very unequal difficulty — always weight by complexity |
| A5 | **Overtime risk exposure** | Assignments to overtime_risk=high resources; share carrying an OVERTIME_OVERRIDE reason code | Overrides rare and always urgent-justified | Resources may be re-flagged to dodge the metric — audit flag changes |
| A6 | **SLA misses** | Share of cases closing after `sla_deadline`, by priority; plus near-misses (<10% slack) | Falling for urgent/high; watch normal/low for starvation | Urgent SLA gains routinely come from normal-case starvation — always report both |
| A7 | **Rework / appeal after assignment** | Rework Correction occurrences and appeals, attributed to the assignment decision that preceded them | Falling, especially after continuity breaks | Long lag — needs a 4–8 week window before conclusions |
| A8 | **Training assignment share** | Supervised/development assignments ÷ normal+low assignments; plus *match rate* to documented priority | ≥20%, with match rate reported separately | 20% can be met with off-priority filler (as C010/C015 partly are) — report both numbers |
| A9 | **Specialist bottleneck** | Share of an activity's events done by its top-1 and top-2 resources; queue/waiting time before specialist activities | Top-1 share falling; bus factor ≥2 per activity | A falling share may reflect risky delegation — cross-check with A3 and A7 |
| A10 | **Policy deviation** | Assignments whose realized (case, resource) differs from the policy's recommendation, by reason code and by whether dispatcher-overridden | Deviations logged, not zero; alarm on *unlogged* deviations | High compliance can mean workers rerouting informally outside the system |
| A11 | **Continuity index** | Share of activities per case performed by the case's initial owner; also handover cost incurred vs. predicted band | Rising, without A3 worsening | — |
| A12 | **Batch integrity** | Quality Checks executed in batches that violate H5 (priority or <24h slack) | Zero | — |
| A13 | **Supervision reality check** | Supervised events where the supervisor's own load exceeded a threshold that day, or where supervisor time ≈ 0 | Low; rising values mean supervision is nominal | The core failure mode of the whole training component |
| A14 | **Equity by person over time** | Per-resource trend in complexity-weighted load, training access, and specialist-work share | No resource systematically starved of development or over-fed hard work | Aggregate fairness can be flat while individuals drift persistently |

A13 and A14 are the ones I would watch hardest. Everything else measures whether the algorithm ran; those two measure whether the promises made to workers in Section 5 were actually kept.

**How learning feeds back into the policy.** `expected_svc` calibrates the service-time model per skill level; `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` populates the substitutability graph behind the resilience term; `discover_organizational_roles` is compared against the declared skill matrix, and any divergence (someone routinely doing work the matrix says they can't, or never doing work it says they can) is a matrix-maintenance ticket, not a licence to reroute. `discover_handover_of_work_network` before and after deployment shows whether the policy actually redistributed work or just relabelled it.

**A caution on learning from the log.** These logs record what the *old* policy did. If it consistently sent hard Security cases to Hugo, the data will "prove" Hugo is the only safe choice — a self-fulfilling loop that entrenches the bottleneck. Break it deliberately: reserve a small share of low-risk, generous-SLA cases for exploration (supervised), and evaluate counterfactuals on those rather than on the observational majority. Otherwise the audit machinery will faithfully certify the very concentration the policy was written to reduce.
