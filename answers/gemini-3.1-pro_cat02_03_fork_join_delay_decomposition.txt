# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Formulas using case index $c$ and branch index $i \in \{A, B, C\}$:

- **Upstream elapsed**: `upstream_elapsed[c] = fork_release[c] - arrival[c]`
- **Branch-specific queue**: `queue[c, i] = start[c, i] - fork_release[c]`
- **Branch active service**: `service[c, i] = end[c, i] - start[c, i]`
- **Join ready**: `join_ready[c] = max(end[c, A], end[c, B], end[c, C])`
- **Branch-specific synchronization wait**: `sync_wait[c, i] = join_ready[c] - end[c, i]`
- **Elapsed fork-join stage**: `elapsed_fork_join[c] = join_ready[c] - fork_release[c]`
- **Downstream queue**: `downstream_queue[c] = final_start[c] - join_ready[c]`
- **Final service**: `final_service[c] = final_end[c] - final_start[c]`
- **Total case flow time**: `total_flow_time[c] = final_end[c] - arrival[c]`

**Double counting of synchronization waits**: 
The `elapsed_fork_join[c]` measures the true clock duration from the moment branch work is permitted to the moment all branches are complete. Because branches execute in parallel, they overlap in real time. If we were to sum the total elapsed times of each branch (`queue[c, i] + service[c, i] + sync_wait[c, i]`), we would be counting the same parallel clock time three times. `sync_wait` is just the idle padding that aligns an earlier-completing branch to the `join_ready` envelope; summing these padded durations artificially multiplies the elapsed clock time by the number of parallel branches.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

| Branch | Mean Service | Mean Queue | Mean Sync Wait | Critical Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50.0m | 0.0m | 11.0m | 2 |
| B | 37.5m | 19.2m | 4.3m | 8 |

**Reasoned influence ranking**:
1. **Branch B (Most Influential)**: Despite having a smaller mean service time (37.5m) than Branch A (50.0m), Branch B is the critical branch in 67% (8 of 12) of cases. Its high influence is driven by substantial queue delays (averaging 19.2m and reaching up to 30m), which pushes its completion time past Branch A's predictable 50m elapsed time envelope. Its mean synchronization wait is the lowest (4.3m), confirming it usually finishes last.
2. **Branch A (Second)**: Branch A acts as a heavy baseline constraint. It has exactly 50m of service time and no queueing. It sets a hard minimum floor for `join_ready`. It is strictly critical in only 2 cases, but it acts as a very close runner-up in most others.
3. **Branch C (Least Influential)**: Branch C has the shortest service time and large synchronization waits (mean 30.4m). It is only critical in 2 edge cases (F04, F08) where its queue delay severely spiked (to 35m and 25m respectively).

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

**1. Reducing A service time by 20% (-10m):**
- **Direct benefits**: Branch A completes 10 minutes earlier. For the 2 cases where A is critical (F01, F11), their `join_ready` time advances by 5m and 10m, respectively.
- **Zero benefit cases**: 10 cases experience zero benefit because B or C remains the critical branch (e.g., A finishing earlier just increases its `sync_wait` without changing `join_ready`).
- **Secondary effects**: Even for F11 where `join_ready` improves by 10m, it may not start the final step earlier if the downstream final-station is still busy processing a previous case.

**2. Reducing B queue delay by 50%:**
- **Direct benefits**: Branch B completes earlier, advancing `join_ready` for most cases. The cumulative reduction in `join_ready` is 92.5 minutes across the dataset.
- **Zero benefit cases**: 4 cases (F01, F04, F08, F11) experience zero benefit to `join_ready` because A or C were critical or become critical immediately.
- **Secondary effects**: Because `join_ready` is pulled forward, cases arrive at the final station sooner. However, final-station congestion heavily masks these improvements; cases will often just shift their waiting time from "Branch B queue" to "Downstream queue".

**3. Adding final-station capacity (reducing downstream queueing to 0):**
- **Direct benefits**: Every case starts final service immediately at `join_ready`. This removes the downstream queueing delay entirely, resulting in 176 minutes of aggregate total flow time savings across all 12 cases.
- **Zero benefit cases**: 0 cases. Every single case currently experiences downstream queuing (between 8 and 20 mins).
- **Secondary effects**: It reveals the true process bottlenecks (Branch B queuing and Branch A service). The fork-join phase dictates the real flow time limits once the final station is unconstrained.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Redesign 1: Triage/Capacity augmentation for Branch B**
- **Discussion**: Since B dictates the critical path in 8 out of 12 cases due to queuing, augmenting its capacity or triaging work will directly pull `join_ready` forward. 
- **Impacts**: Throughput increases at the fork-join stage, and the flow-time tail (caused by 30m B-queues) shrinks. Parallel resource utilization improves as B's completion aligns closer to A. However, Branch A will immediately become the new critical bottleneck (due to its fixed 50m service), and the final station queue may absorb the time savings as a new masking bottleneck.
- **Action**: Pilot a queuing reduction in B first and measure if cases actually clear the final station faster or just pile up in the `downstream_queue`.

**Redesign 2: Increase Final Station Capacity (e.g., add resource F3)**
- **Discussion**: Cases wait between 8 to 20 minutes in `downstream_queue`. Expanding final station capacity prevents cases from stalling after passing the fork-join gate.
- **Impacts**: This strictly improves total flow-time tail and case throughput, guaranteeing that any upstream optimizations (like Redesign 1) translate directly to total case flow time savings rather than being masked. Resource utilization of F1/F2 will drop as work is distributed.
- **Action**: First, measure whether the current downstream wait is truly resource contention or an artificial release policy (e.g., batching logic). Pilot by temporarily adding an F3 resource or relaxing the release policy, measuring the drop in total flow time.

# 7. Limits of inference

There are several systemic attributes that cannot be confidently identified solely from these discrete timestamps:
- **Causality of Queueing**: We can observe that Branch B has high `queue` delays, but we do not know the cause. It could be a lack of physical machine capacity, batching policies, missing secondary inputs, or worker availability.
- **Branch Resource Capacity**: The dataset does not include explicit resource identifiers for branches A, B, and C. We cannot definitively determine how many parallel servers operate within each branch, preventing us from creating a true utilization profile for the upstream steps.
- **Work Preemption**: The timestamps only provide absolute start and end marks. If a branch's active work is preempted, paused, or experiences internal transport delays mid-process, it is conflated with the inflated `service` time.
- **Final-station Waiting Dynamics**: The downstream queue (`final_start - join_ready`) ranges from 8 to 20 minutes. Even when examining the resource utilization of F1 and F2, it is clear that cases often wait despite their assigned resource appearing to be free earlier. It is impossible to identify if this waiting is a strict release scheduling policy (e.g., fixed transport intervals, quarter-hour batches) or true queuing waiting for an unobserved constraint (e.g., a shared pre-final buffer).
