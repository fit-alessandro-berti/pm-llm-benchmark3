# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The frequent route through auto screening, fast approval and replacement is attractive, but frequency does not establish that it is the best route. The frequency DFG shows 3,600 intake-to-auto transitions, 2,550 auto-to-fast-approval transitions and 2,300 fast-approval-to-dispatch transitions. Selected mean edge times on the straightforward route total about 9.8 hours from intake through close, but this is only an illustrative sum of edge means, not a measured end-to-end mean for a cohort. It excludes later complaints and cannot be used to calculate the route's total cost. Fast approval is 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments and service credits; 14.1% of its cases reopen, versus 3.3% on the specified quote–approval–repair path. These are observed associations, not causal comparisons: case mix and selection into paths may differ.

The conflict is especially pronounced by segment. Premium low-risk accessories have an 8-hour fast-path median versus 46 hours manually, with 6% versus 3% complaints; crossing 24 hours carries high service-credit exposure. Premium high-value devices have a 20-hour fast median versus 96 hours reviewed/repaired, but 22% versus 5% complaints, with high compliance exposure. Standard high-value claims show 18% versus 6% complaints, and a wrong replacement has an expected cost of EUR 410, versus EUR 95 for an accessory. Standard low-risk customers can tolerate 72 hours if kept informed. A single speed or review target would therefore miss meaningful differences.

Other evidence points to specific failure mechanisms and queues:

- The performance DFG shows a 34-hour mean and 88-hour p90 from evidence request back to manual review; 26% of cases entering evidence requests have at least two requests. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence group has a 140-hour median and the highest abandonment. This is a customer-effort problem as well as a delay.
- Dispatch-to-complaint has a 96-hour mean and 180-hour p90, and the temporal profile links its long tail to incorrect part selection. Complaint-to-manual-review takes 40 hours on average, p90 100; complaint-to-board takes 54 hours, p90 130. Fast apparent closure can thus shift effort and customer waiting downstream.
- Replay fitness of 0.91 and only 68% fitting traces indicate material conformance problems, although they do not give rule-specific violation rates. Alignment diagnostics flag fast approval before evidence completeness, complaints after close, and high-value dispatch without risk review. The governing rules are more precise: claims above EUR 400 require manual eligibility or fraud/risk review before fast approval (R1); dispatch requires confirmed complete evidence except for Premium low-risk accessories (R2); reopened claims require manual eligibility review before final close or denial (R3). Risk review is not universally required for every high-value device, and an event named “evidence completeness” may need to be added or mapped reliably in the log before judging R2.
- Discovered-model precision is low: infrequent combinations of auto approval, risk review and repair include policy-disallowed behavior. The frequency DFG is an aggregate of directly-following events, not a complete case-routing or causal model; loops, post-close events and incomplete instrumentation make edge counts unsuitable as case denominators. The repair quality-check-to-rework edge and the 12.5% high-value versus 4% low-risk repair-rework statistics also show that reviewed repair is not automatically defect-free.
- Manual capacity is seven reviewers × 36 productive hours, or 252 hours/week; the stated queue-growth threshold is roughly 207 productive hours/week at 82% utilization, before considering variability. Review handling times and arrival rates are missing, so available headroom cannot be calculated. Updated screening could increase manual inflow 15–35%; fraud specialists already bottleneck on Mondays and after campaigns, and quote delay is mostly part-price waiting. The daily 45-minute board cannot absorb every complaint.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-routed fast replacement with a dispatch gate

**Flow and targets.** At intake, capture segment, claim value, defect and evidence status. A revised, versioned auto screen routes low-risk accessories to fast approval, with the R2 exception available only to Premium low-risk accessories; all other dispatches wait for an explicit evidence-complete confirmation. For claims above EUR 400, route to manual eligibility or fraud/risk review before any fast approval. High-value replacement candidates receive a lightweight pre-dispatch part-selection check; ambiguous or risky cases go to specialist review or the repair route. After dispatch or repair, record quality/compliance close. Any complaint after close reopens the case and triggers manual review before final close or denial, including cases sent to the escalation board. Keep the board for exceptional unresolved cases.

**Mechanism.** This retains the fast service advantage where the observed harm is relatively small, while placing a targeted control at the documented wrong-part failure point. A versioned product/part compatibility check and confirmation against the claim evidence could prevent wrong shipments; use the underused inspectors where feasible and instrument whether the gate actually catches errors. Reserve rapid review capacity for Premium high-value cases and use status updates and a promised decision time for Standards. Monitor the 6–18-hour added gate delay against the Premium 24-hour exposure; the gate must not be silently bypassed to meet service targets. Rules must enforce R1–R3, not merely flag exceptions after dispatch.

**Sacrifice or unresolved issue.** Gate labor and waiting raise first-pass cost and delay, particularly for high-value Premium customers; updated rules may overload reviewers or fraud specialists. Evidence loops and part-price waiting remain largely intact. The Premium accessory exception permits dispatch without completed evidence, but does not waive R1 or R3 where applicable. This design does not prove that all fast approvals are safe or that the gate pays for itself.

# 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first assisted resolution and quote coordination

**Flow and targets.** For unclear defects and missing evidence, replace repeated serial requests with one guided intake checklist tailored to defect and claim type, checking completeness and part identification together. Offer an accessible portal plus phone or agent-assisted submission and an offline alternative; do not require digital self-service. Record the completeness confirmation, give a named owner and status updates, and send a complete packet to eligibility review. In parallel, request part-price confirmation and reserve a technician quote slot where repair is plausible; no technical decision or dispatch is authorized before its required reviews and evidence are complete. Route cases above EUR 400 through manual eligibility or fraud/risk review before fast approval; permit the R2 dispatch exception only for Premium low-risk accessories. After approval, repair or replace as appropriate, check repaired work, and enforce manual review on any reopen before final close or denial.

**Mechanism.** The design attacks the 34-hour evidence-return edge, repeat requests and abandonment directly, while parallel price confirmation addresses the quote wait. The portal's stated potential is a 30–45% reduction in repeated evidence requests, not a guaranteed reduction in total case time or abandonment. A complete initial packet can improve both eligibility decisions and part selection; proactive updates suit Standard customers' tolerance for a 72-hour completion if visibility is good. Premium cases need an expedited assisted channel because their time expectations are tighter.

**Sacrifice or unresolved issue.** Portal and assisted-channel setup add cost and complexity. Gathering evidence earlier may slow simple claims and may increase abandonment for people with low digital literacy; assisted uptake must be measured. A better packet does not itself eliminate wrong replacements, repair rework or specialist shortages. Parallel quote preparation may be wasted on denied or abandoned claims.

# 4. Redesign C: Reviewed resolution with reserved Premium capacity and repair-quality feedback

**Flow and targets.** Direct high-value and ambiguous device claims to a coordinated reviewed-resolution lane: verify evidence, perform manual eligibility or fraud/risk review as appropriate, and start part-price confirmation promptly. A technician proposes repair or a verified replacement, with a clear customer approval step for a repair quote; repaired devices pass quality check and rework when needed before close. Reserve daily manual-review and technician capacity for Premium high-value claims, with explicit overflow and status commitments for Standard cases. Inspectors feed repair defects and wrong-part findings back into technician decision aids. Keep simple, eligible low-risk accessories on a separate fast lane, subject to R1–R3 and R2's narrow exception. Reopened claims in either lane go through manual eligibility before final close or denial; use the board only for exceptional disputes.

**Mechanism.** This changes the operating model toward staffed, coordinated resolution for cases where wrong replacement is costly and observed fast-path complaints are high. Reserved capacity addresses the Premium manual-review-to-quote tail, whose mean is 22 hours and p90 60, without claiming to remove the underlying price-confirmation wait. Quality feedback targets the 12.5% repair rework reported for high-value devices. It prioritizes a considered technical decision and repair quality over the fastest dispatch.

**Sacrifice or unresolved issue.** More review and repair work increases labor and may lengthen completion, with high Premium service-credit exposure; observed reviewed/repair medians of 96–120 hours are not acceptable as an assumed future result. Reservations can leave capacity idle or worsen Standard queues unless flexed. This approach does not fix evidence collection as directly as B, and repair can itself generate rework. It should not divert every low-risk claim to manual handling.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**A.** (1) Outdated or incorrect value, segment or product data can misroute claims, misuse the Premium exception or bypass R1. Validate fields, log rule versions and audit overrides. (2) A superficial part check may catch few errors while adding up to 18 hours, causing Premium credits; measure catch rate and downstream wrong-part outcomes. (3) The predicted 15–35% extra manual inflow could push utilization beyond 82%; plan overflow before enabling rules. The bottleneck can move from dispatch to inspectors, manual eligibility or the two fraud specialists, especially on Mondays.

**B.** (1) The portal can exclude customers or cause abandonment despite fewer repeat requests; compare assisted and digital journeys, including non-starters. (2) A checklist can collect the wrong evidence or hide incomplete submissions, so audit accuracy and later requests as well as the completeness flag. (3) Earlier price requests may produce speculative work or overwhelm suppliers. Faster evidence collection can move the queue to manual review, technician quotes or part-price confirmation.

**C.** (1) Reserved Premium slots may starve Standard customers or sit idle at the wrong times; monitor both segments and release unused capacity under a defined rule. (2) More repairs can overload technicians and inspectors or increase high-value rework; check repair outcomes and capacity before expanding. (3) Reviewers may rely on incomplete evidence or miss the mandatory reopen review despite the lane's intent. The bottleneck can move to part-price confirmation, customer approval, repair or quality inspection; the single board slot cannot serve as the overflow plan.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

1. **Establish measurement and safeguards first.** Link events by claim across initial close and subsequent reopen, reshipment, denial and service credits. Capture segment, value, defect, evidence-complete timestamp, review and rule version, part selected, actual staff effort and queue age. Validate event mapping and policy rules with compliance; do not use DFG edge counts as case counts. Measure baseline distributions and capacity by segment and weekday, including low-digital-literacy customers. Keep R1–R3 enforceable in every arm, including post-close cases; investigate existing violations and define an exception escalation path before rollout.
2. **First pilot: A on high-value replacement candidates, with a protected accessory fast lane.** Introduce the part gate and compliant routing in a limited, capacity-capped cohort, with reserved reviewer/inspector time and a comparable contemporaneous cohort using the current compliant workflow. Randomize gate allocation where compliance permits; otherwise match on segment, value, defect, channel and severity, and account for selection. Do not randomize permission to violate a policy rule. The gate is the first test because the long-tail wrong-part signal is concrete and the cost of an incorrect high-value replacement is high. Pilot Premium and Standard separately and preserve the Premium accessory exception only where it applies.
3. **Pilot B on unclear or incomplete claims**, initially with assisted and portal channels available together. Compare eligible claims with a concurrent control; randomize invitation or workflow where feasible, while retaining accessible assistance in both arms. Track abandonment from intake as well as after an evidence request, time to a genuinely complete packet, repeat requests and later corrections. Test price-request coordination in a small technician cohort, watching supplier and quote queues.
4. **Pilot C on a limited high-value device cohort** after measuring reviewer and technician headroom. Compare reserved-capacity coordinated resolution with the current compliant path, stratified by Premium/Standard and repair suitability. Release unused reserved slots on a schedule and protect Standard queue service. Expand only if staffing and part-price supply can sustain it. The pilots test distinct interventions; combining successful components later requires a further test because their combined capacity effects may differ.

For all pilots, monitor median and p90 end-to-end time from intake through final resolution, time to first meaningful decision, share of Premium cases beyond 24 hours, Standard status and completion performance, queue age and utilization, and segment-level abandonment. Track rule-specific R1–R3 violations and override reasons, verified evidence completeness before dispatch, wrong-part shipments, complaints/reopens, reshipments, repair rework, customer satisfaction or credits, and full per-case labor plus material and downstream costs. Include all assigned cases, not just completed cases; report sample sizes and uncertainty rather than treating small differences as proof.

Use a predeclared decision range rather than inventing a promised outcome: pause immediately for a confirmed systemic R1–R3 control failure or a material deterioration in customer harm. Pause or throttle when sustained reviewer utilization approaches/exceeds 82%, queue age rises materially, abandonment rises meaningfully in any vulnerable channel, or Premium service-credit exposure worsens without an agreed offset. Proceed only if rule compliance holds, the targeted defect or repeat-request rate improves beyond normal baseline variation, and segment-level p90, abandonment and total cost show no material unaccepted deterioration. For B, treat the stated 30–45% reduction in repeat requests as a testable opportunity, not a mandatory or guaranteed result; agree the minimum worthwhile improvement and acceptable trade-offs before analysis.

Follow each cohort for a fixed observation window long enough to capture late complaints and reshipments, with the same window for controls; extend it if outcomes are still arriving near its end. Compare outcomes by assignment, including abandoned and reopened cases, and inspect cumulative complaints after initial close. This distinguishes an actual reduction in end-to-end harm from faster closure that merely moves work, cost or customer waiting downstream. Review distributional results and uncertainty with the sponsor before expanding or combining designs.
