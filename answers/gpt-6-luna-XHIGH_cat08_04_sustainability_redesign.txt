# 1. System boundary and assumptions

I use an order-level, operational boundary from order receipt through production/picking, packaging, outbound transport, delivery confirmation, and any recorded rework or return-related activity. Within it, I count the observed machine and process energy (`energy_kwh`), production scrap (`material_scrap_kg`), packaging materials (`package_material_kg`), and outbound transport emissions (`transport_kgco2e`). I also treat waiting, late delivery, cancellation, partial shipment, and rework as operational outcomes: they affect service and can create additional shipments, wasted production, packaging, and customer returns. The boundary is therefore broader than choosing the route with the lowest recorded transport emissions.

The evidence does not quantify upstream raw-material production, supplier energy, packaging end-of-life, warehousing, employee travel, or the full lifecycle impact of returns and replacement parts. Nor does it provide a common carbon conversion for machine energy and scrap. I would report those measured dimensions separately and avoid adding them into an unsupported single carbon total. Return events and all downstream impacts may be incompletely captured. Because orders can contain several line items while the log is flattened by order, per-order averages may hide item weight, quantity, geometry, and allocation differences. Use item-level data where available; otherwise compare like-for-like order segments and treat the estimates as directional. The low precision of the discovered model, especially for intertwined emergency and partial-shipment paths, means the DFG is a diagnostic baseline rather than proof of causality.

# 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Use a segment-stratified dashboard (case type × customer type, with part weight/geometry and route where available) rather than a single average. Pair the following indicators with process path and policy conformance:

- **Energy:** `energy_kwh` per order and, where item quantity or weight is available, per shipped item/kg; split by machine group, emergency versus planned production, and batch size. Track energy alongside output so batching does not appear beneficial merely because more items were made, including items later unsold.
- **Transport:** `transport_kgco2e` per order and per delivered line item; route shares for `parcel_ground`, `parcel_air`, `dedicated_van`, and `consolidated_truck`; shipments per order; and air/van exceptions by promised-lead-time slack. Monitor empty dedicated-van returns if that operational attribute can be added.
- **Materials and packaging:** `material_scrap_kg` and `package_material_kg` per order/item, separated by machine group, `rework_flag`, packaging type, and route compatibility. Include damage returns and replacement shipments when observable.
- **Rework and quality:** rework-loop rate and count at inspection, CNC, and packaging; first-pass yield; damage-related returns/claims; and scrap or energy associated with rework. Stratify by `machine_group`, part family/weight, and inspection route.
- **Batching:** `batch_size`, `batch_id`, time in Batch wait and heat-treatment wait, energy per item, due-date slack at release, late delivery, and the share of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days. Monitor by CNC/heat-treatment group and segment.
- **Service:** median and p90 cycle time, late-delivery rate against `promised_lead_time_hours`, cancellations due to delay, partial shipments, time to complete all lines in an order, and customer contacts if available. Report each by the five case/customer segments, not just overall.
- **Resilience:** service during carrier disruption, availability of an alternative route for Service-contract customers, recovery time, emergency production and expedite rates, and share of dedicated vans with a recorded Critical-downtime justification. Track route and machine concentration as well as normal-period performance.
- **Policy conformance:** SP1 share of eligible orders shipped by ground/consolidated when the probability of meeting promise exceeds 85%, with the probability calibration and actual late outcome recorded; SP2 justification-event completeness for dedicated vans; and Q1 compliance for Made-to-order parts over 8 kg, including the inspection-certified low-damage-risk exception.

# 3. Intervention options

1. **Green shipping recommender (S1), with promise slack and reliability as inputs.** Target stock and made-to-order orders for which ground or consolidated service is feasible. Use promised time, route reliability, customer segment, and disruption status to recommend the lowest-impact qualifying route; enforce SP1 only when predicted on-time probability is above 85%. Record the recommendation, probability, selected route, and override reason. This should reduce unnecessary air use while protecting service; monitor late delivery and p90 cycle time by segment and carrier. Calibrate predictions because the observed model has low precision in disruption paths.

2. **Replace avoidable air on Standard-stock Service-contract orders (S4).** Target the 22% of this segment reported as violating SP1, and especially cases where the promise still allows priority picking plus ground. Prioritize picking and reserve air for insufficient slack, unreliable ground, or disruption. Air currently cuts median transport time by 24 hours but emits about 4–6 times as much per order as ground; the segment's air path is fast but has much higher transport emissions. Monitor route choice, predicted and actual on-time delivery, promised slack, late rate, and any additional handling or partial shipment. Do not impose ground solely to lower emissions when service probability falls below the threshold.

3. **Twice-daily consolidation for eligible non-urgent stock (S5).** Target stock orders whose promise has 12–24 hours of consolidation slack. Consolidated trucks reduce transport emissions by about 35–55% versus individual ground parcels. Offer priority picking before the cutoff and retain ground as fallback when the next departure jeopardizes the promise; keep an alternative carrier/route for Service-contract resilience. Measure truck fill/departure, transport emissions, wait, shipment count, late rate, and disruption performance. Avoid sending repeated small shipments simply to meet a batch cutoff.

4. **Dynamic batching with segment-specific maximum waits (S2).** Target planned CNC and heat-treatment work. Favor CNC_A and heat-treatment batches when demand and due-date slack support them: CNC_A energy per item falls from 2.8 kWh at batch size 1–3 to 1.3 kWh at size 12–20, while median batch wait rises from 4 to 38 hours; heat-treatment batches above 10 have the lowest energy per item but waiting cases have 1.7 times the late-delivery risk. Set maximum waits from promised slack and segment service targets, flush or split a batch when the cap is reached, and exclude urgent work. Monitor energy/item, wait distributions, lateness, utilization, cancellations, and the 14-day unsold share. This balances energy savings against delay and obsolescence.

5. **Route eligible Made-to-order work from CNC_B to CNC_A (S3).** Target due-date-slack cases with available CNC_A capacity, especially the observed CNC_B path with 32 kWh/order, 1.8 kg scrap, 32% lateness, and a rework loop. CNC_A's observed rework rate is 9% versus CNC_B's 18% for MTO. Dispatch based on queue and due-date slack, not machine preference alone, and avoid transferring work if CNC_A congestion creates a longer wait. Track matched part-family first-pass yield, energy, scrap, queue time, late delivery, and downstream heat/inspection loads.

6. **Inspection quality gate for high-rework combinations (S7).** Target machine/part combinations with repeat inspection-to-rework-to-CNC loops, especially CNC_B paths, and address inspection-cell queue conditions that lead to rushed checks. Add risk-based inspection capacity or checks before production proceeds to packaging; do not simply add a universal queue. This should prevent avoidable rework energy, scrap, repackaging, and returns, with a possible short-term inspection-time cost. Monitor first-pass yield, inspection wait, rework and return rates, scrap, energy, and delivery performance by combination.

7. **Packaging-route and geometry compatibility check (S6), preserving Q1.** Target rework-packaging loops and Q1 violations. Validate packaging against route mode, geometry, and weight before shipping. Continue reinforced packaging for MTO parts over 8 kg unless inspection certifies low damage risk: it doubles package material but reduces damage-related returns by 40% for heavy MTO parts. Use standard packaging where the verified damage risk permits it. Track packaging rework, material per shipped item, damage/return rate, replacement shipments, and Q1 conformance. Do not count reduced packaging mass as a gain if damage and replacement impacts rise.

8. **Shared emergency courier lanes and explicit van justification (S8, S9, S12).** Target Critical-downtime orders, whose dedicated-van path protects service (14-hour median, 3% late) but emits 52 kgCO2e/order on average and often returns empty. Split true downtime from precautionary urgent demand using reason codes and evidence of operational impact. Where response time and availability meet the case need, use a shared emergency lane; retain dedicated vans for justified true downtime or when alternatives cannot protect service. Require a justification event for every van under SP2 and record alternatives considered. Monitor response time, downtime impact, late rate, route emissions, vehicle utilization/empty return, category assignment, and override reasons. Maintain a fallback lane for disruptions rather than concentrating all critical demand on one carrier.

9. **Carbon-aware promise negotiation for Regular MTO (S10).** Target Regular MTO orders with customer agreement and schedule slack. Offer a later, credible promise that permits an efficient CNC_A/heat-treatment batch or consolidated ground transport, instead of defaulting to immediate production and air. Make the delivery date and trade-off explicit and optional; do not transfer avoidable delay onto customers or degrade Service-contract commitments. Monitor acceptance, cancellations, actual versus agreed promise, batch wait, energy/item, transport emissions, and unsold batch output.

10. **Availability rules to reduce avoidable partial shipments (S11).** Target stock orders with partial shipment followed by backorder wait or a second shipment (the selected path has 24% lateness and 7.2 kgCO2e/order). Improve line-level availability visibility and ask whether the customer prefers a complete shipment by an agreed date or a justified urgent split; prioritize pick/transfer when that avoids a second shipment without missing the promise. Do not hold a time-critical available line merely to improve shipment consolidation. Monitor completed-order time, split rate, number of shipments, emissions, late delivery, cancellations, and customer preference by segment.

# 5. Rebound and displacement risks

1. **Batching can exchange energy efficiency for delay and excess production.** Large CNC_A batches lower energy per item, but longer waits can miss promises; heat-treatment waits are associated with 1.7 times higher late risk. Forecast error also leaves 6% of batch-produced items unshipped within 14 days. A lower energy figure per manufactured item can therefore conceal obsolete inventory, scrap, or extra storage and expedite activity.
2. **Consolidation can displace emissions into lateness or recovery transport.** The 12–24-hour wait may cause an order to miss its promise, prompting air, a dedicated vehicle, a second shipment, cancellation, or customer downtime. Count the complete order path and subsequent recovery, not only the planned consolidated leg.
3. **Replacing air indiscriminately can harm service and resilience.** Air saves about 24 hours of transport time. A ground rule without the SP1 probability threshold can increase late delivery, especially for tight promises or carrier disruptions. Keep justified air exceptions and an alternative Service-contract route under R1.
4. **Packaging reduction can increase damage and lifecycle material use.** Reinforced packaging doubles packaging material but reduces damage returns by 40% for heavy MTO parts. Removing it in violation of Q1 may create returns, replacement parts, new packaging, and urgent transport that outweigh the initial material saving.
5. **Rework improvements can move the bottleneck to inspection.** Additional checks may reduce CNC rework and scrap, but if inspection capacity is unchanged they can increase queues and late delivery. Conversely, rushing inspection to clear a queue can miss defects and cause later packaging rework or returns. Track first-pass yield and inspection waits together.
6. **Routing away from CNC_B can overload CNC_A.** CNC_A has lower observed MTO rework, but concentrating work there can create queues, batch waits, or single-machine dependence. The total effect may then include late orders, emergency production, and expedite transport. Dispatch based on queue and slack, retaining qualified alternate capacity.
7. **A shared courier can shift risk to critical customers.** Fewer empty dedicated-van returns could lower transport impact, but scheduled shared capacity may not meet true downtime response needs or may be unavailable in disruption. Segment true downtime from precautionary urgency and preserve justified dedicated service and a fallback.
8. **Fewer partial shipments can mean either fewer trips or harmful holding.** Waiting to combine lines may reduce transport and packaging, but holding an available critical line can extend downtime; transferring stock may add energy and transport. Capture customer preference and total order completion time, not just split count.
9. **Customer promise changes can induce demand or cancel orders.** Carbon-aware later dates may enable efficient production, but customers may reject them, place replacement orders elsewhere, or request upgrades/expedites. Track acceptance, cancellation, subsequent urgent orders if observable, and unsold batch output.
10. **Order-level averages can hide displacement across line items.** Changing one line's route or batch may delay the complete multi-line order or shift emissions to a second parcel. Where possible, link line items to shipments and production batches before attributing benefits.

# 6. Balanced recommendation

Implement a controlled redesign in layers. First, establish reliable line-level links among order promises, batch waits, machine/inspection events, packaging, shipments, and returns, and require reason-coded events for overrides. Apply SP1 through a calibrated, slack-aware shipping recommender: use ground or consolidation only where on-time probability exceeds 85%, with priority picking and justified air exceptions. Start consolidated departures for non-urgent stock with sufficient slack, retaining a fallback for Service-contract customers and carrier disruption.

For production, pilot CNC_A routing for eligible MTO work and dynamic CNC/heat-treatment batching with segment-specific maximum waits tied to promise slack. Exclude Critical-downtime and other time-critical jobs from energy-driven batch waits. Pair this with risk-based inspection capacity on high-rework combinations, and packaging-route checks that enforce Q1 while avoiding incompatible repack loops. On the outbound side, reduce avoidable stock partial shipments through better availability information and customer choice, not by holding urgent lines automatically.

For Critical-downtime orders, require a true-downtime versus precautionary-urgent classification and an SP2 reason for every dedicated van. Test shared emergency lanes for cases whose response needs they can meet; retain the dedicated-van option when the service case warrants it and keep an alternate route during disruption. For Regular MTO customers, offer a transparent, optional later promise when it enables a lower-impact batch or ground service.

This package addresses the largest visible avoidable burdens without treating emissions as the only objective: air-heavy standard-stock service-contract shipments, empty-return dedicated vans, CNC_B rework/scrap, packaging loops, and partial shipments. It uses reliability and promised slack to protect service, quality gates and Q1 to protect product integrity, and alternative routes/machines plus explicit exception controls to protect resilience. Scale only interventions whose segment-level service, quality, and resilience guardrails hold while measured environmental indicators improve.

# 7. Validation and governance plan

- **Baseline and instrumentation:** Freeze a pre-change baseline by case/customer segment, part family/weight, route, machine, and disruption state. Capture event timestamps and keys for order line, batch, shipment, and return so emissions, wait, and outcomes can be attributed across the flattened order path. Add fields for route reliability/probability, promised slack, customer split preference, true downtime reason, exception approver/reason, carrier disruption, package compatibility, inspection certification, and van return utilization where operationally available.
- **Pilot and comparison:** Roll out by eligible cohorts or lanes in stages, retaining a comparable baseline cohort where feasible. Compare like-for-like segments and due-date slack; account for demand mix and disruption periods. Do not infer causality from overall before/after averages or the low-precision DFG alone. Review complete order paths, including all shipments, rework, cancellations, and returns.
- **Environmental outcomes:** Track energy per good shipped item/order, transport emissions per completed order and item, shipment count, package mass, scrap, rework energy/material, and unsold batch share at 14 days. Report observed dimensions separately unless valid lifecycle factors support aggregation. Check whether fewer air/van legs are offset by recovery trips or extra shipments.
- **Service guardrails:** For each segment, monitor median and p90 cycle time, late delivery against the promised lead time, time to complete every line, partial shipment, cancellation due to delay, and critical downtime response. Set thresholds before each pilot from existing service commitments; stop or adjust a change if the relevant segment breaches them. In particular, validate the >85% SP1 probability against actual on-time outcomes and recalibrate by route and segment.
- **Quality guardrails:** Monitor first-pass yield, inspection queue, CNC/rework loops, packaging rework, damage returns, replacement shipments, and Q1 exceptions by part weight/geometry and machine. Confirm that reinforced packaging is used for MTO parts over 8 kg unless inspection records low damage risk. A reduction in package mass counts as a gain only if damage and return outcomes remain acceptable.
- **Resilience guardrails:** Test the route recommender and consolidation plan against carrier disruptions and peak loads. For Service-contract customers, verify that an alternative route remains available under R1. Track recovery time, capacity, emergency production, expedited shipments, machine concentration, and whether shared emergency lanes meet true downtime needs. Maintain qualified alternative production capacity where routing shifts toward CNC_A.
- **Conformance and exception governance:** Produce an auditable event for each decision: SP1 eligibility probability and route choice/override; SP2 dedicated-van justification; and Q1 packaging choice or inspection-certified exception. Review conformance rates and override reasons weekly during pilots, with named operational owners for exceptions. Investigate repeat overrides and policy violations as process-design or capacity issues, not merely individual noncompliance. Reconcile DFG variants and event counts after rollout, since emergency and partial-shipment paths currently combine and reduce model precision.
- **Decision gates:** Expand an intervention only when it shows an improvement in its intended environmental measure and no unacceptable deterioration in segment service, quality, or resilience. Revise wait caps, eligibility thresholds, staffing, or routing rules when trade-offs appear; remove an intervention if benefits rely on shifted emissions, hidden returns, unsold production, or unrecorded exceptions.
