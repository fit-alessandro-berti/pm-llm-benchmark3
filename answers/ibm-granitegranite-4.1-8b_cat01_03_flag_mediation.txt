## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|-------------|-----------------------|----------------------|---------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------|------------------------------|
| E1          | P0 vs P1 (flag display only) | All cases in the system (N) | Total case completion time (cycle_time) | Total effect (natural direct + indirect) | Yes | Unobserved underlying risk (U_underlying_risk) may confound both flag display and completion time. |
| E2          | P0 vs P2 (mandatory manual review removed) | Flagged cases only (N_f) | Triage completion time | Direct effect (controlled direct) | Yes | Potential spill-over of manual review capacity to other cases (confounding by resource allocation). |
| E3          | P1 vs P3 (protected senior capacity) | Flagged cases only (N_f) | Manual queue wait time | Indirect effect mediated by escalation (interventional indirect) | Yes | Interaction between capacity protection and case complexity not captured in data. |
| E4          | P2 vs P4 (escalation criteria changed) | Flagged cases with manual review (N_f_m) | Escalation queue wait time | Direct effect (natural direct) | Partially | Arrival load may simultaneously affect escalation timing and flag status (reverse causation). |
| E5          | P0 vs P0 (no change) vs P3 (added capacity) | All cases (N) | Decision outcome (approval/rejection) | Indirect effect mediated by manual review quality | Yes | Quality of manual review may be confounded by reviewer fatigue (unobserved). |
| E6          | P0 vs P1 (flag display only) | All cases (N) | Triage start-todone interval | Direct effect (natural direct) | Yes | Prioritization lane may affect concurrent case throughput (interference). |
| E7          | P0 vs P2 (manual review removed) | All cases (N) | Overall process throughput (cases/day) | Total effect (mix of direct and indirect) | Yes | System demand fluctuations (arrival_load) may mask policy effects. |

*Framework*: We use natural direct/indirect effects where the flag is treated as a mediator of total time but also as a cause of downstream stages. Controlled direct effects are employed where a policy explicitly forces a stage (e.g., removing mandatory manual review). Interventional indirect effects capture how changes after seeing the flag propagate through other resources.

*Explanation (180 words)*: The graph shows flag generation from underlying risk and complexity, which routes cases to a priority lane that shortens triage. Flagged cases then routinely enter manual review, potentially increasing queue wait and service time, and may be escalated. Arrival load influences triage waiting indirectly via external congestion. Interference can arise when manualreview capacity is shared: adding more flagged cases compresses the queue, lengthening waits for other cases, creating exposureinduced confounding. Similarly, exposureinduced changes in reviewer behavior (fatigue, motivation) after seeing many flagged cases may bias the decision outcome, leading to feedback loops not captured in the static DAG.

*Interpretation (180 words)*: Flagged cases overall experience faster triage (1215min vs 20min for lowcomplexity flag0). This pattern suggests an acceleration at the triage stage and downstream delays through manualreview queue length and escalation sharing. The observed acceleration cannot be automatically causal because sharedqueue interference, arrivalload dynamics, and possible complexitydriven risk inflations could alternatively explain the faster early segment. A mediation analysis that isolates the triage effect from downstream queues is required to isolate causality.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| What is the pure effect of flag display? | Synthetic control using timevaryied flag exposure within the same risk_score deciles (withinriskscore design) | Control for risk_score, complexity, arrival_load | Parallel trends in flag0 and flag1 within each risk decile before flag introduction | If synthetic control shows no postintroduction acceleration in total cycle for flag1, the observed delay is downstream | Lack of acceleration would suggest upstream effect only |
| Is manualreview queue length a mediating bottleneck? | Interrupted timeseries on manualqueue wait time with a prepolicy lagged difference model, stratified by flag status | Adjust for arrival_load, complexity, triage speed | Queues are independent of triage speed after priority lane is in place | Place a temporary buffer (extra manual reviewer) for flagged cases and test if cycletime gap narrows | Narrowing gap would confirm mediation |
| Does escalation criteria bias decisions? | Randomized encouragement experiment: randomly assign flagged cases to either receive a “soft escalation” cue (without real senior allocation) or not, while keeping flag visible | Adjust for risk_score, complexity | Cues do not affect reviewer behavior beyond escalation intention | Compare rejection rates under cue vs no cue; if cue raises rejections disproportionately, escalation bias exists | Strong cue effect would indicate hidden escalation bias |
| Are arrivalload fluctuations confounding total cycle? | Lagged difference model controlling for arrival_load and its lagged values, using a differenceindifferences across highload vs normalload days | Include lagged arrival_load, dayofweek fixed effects | Arrival load does not carry forward effect on later stages | Test if flagged cases show a smaller load sensitivity than unflagged | If flagged cases remain insensitive to load, flag effect is independent of load |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycletime effect | quality/complexity risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|--------|-----------------------|--------------------------------|---------------------------|--------------------------|--------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **P0** (statusquo) | Fast triage via priority lane | Large manualreview queue congestion for flagged cases; escalation sharing | Total cycle  for flag1 (69h) | Moderate – decisions may be rushed | High – manual queue saturated | Strong (observed data) | Baseline for comparison |
| **P1** (hide flag) | Triage unchanged (priority lane persists) | Manual review still automatic for flagged cases (information hidden) | Total cycle similar to P0 (no reduction) | Low – reviewers unaware of flag | Moderate – unchanged queue dynamics | Weak (no flag manipulation) | Not recommended for cycle improvement |
| **P2** (no mandatory manual review) | Triage stays fast | Manualreview queue empties for flagged; possible overload of nonflagged | Cycle  for flag1 (fast triage persists) but  for flag0 (manual may be needed) | Risk of missing highrisk flagged cases | Low – queue resource released | Moderate (requires assumption of no quality loss) | Consider if quality risk acceptable |
| **P3** (protected senior capacity) | Triage unchanged | Manual review still automatic; senior pool protected  reduced escalation wait | Cycle  for escalated flagged cases (24h) but unchanged for nonescalated | May improve decision quality for highrisk | Low – senior pool isolated | Moderate (needs capacity data) | Recommended for bottleneck reduction |
| **P4** (retune escalation criteria) | Triage unchanged | Manual queue unchanged; escalation frequency  | Cycle  for nonescalated flagged (2h) | May underescalate some risky cases | Low – escalation rate lower | Weak (depends on criteria stability) | Pilot first |

*Pilot recommendation*: **PolicyP3** (protected senior capacity) is chosen for a limited pilot.  

**Pilot design (250 words)**  
- **Eligibility**: All flagged cases arriving during operating hours on weekdays (n12 cases/day).  
- **Randomization/assignment**: Within each arrival hour, randomly assign 50% of flagged cases to receive “protected senior capacity” (i.e., any manualreview that exceeds a seniorteam threshold is immediately routed to the senior pool) and the other 50% continue current routing.  
- **Queuecapacity guardrail**: Ensure senior pool staffing at 30% of total manualreview capacity, with an automatic cap that prevents overflow into nonsenior queue.  
- **Primary outcome**: Median total cycle time (from arrival to close) for flagged cases.  
- **Secondary outcome**: Escalation wait time and decisionoutcome agreement with an expert panel (binary match).  
- **Minimum process data needed**: Arrival timestamp, triage timestamps, manualreview timestamps, escalation timestamps, seniorservice timestamps, and decision outcome.  
- **Stopping conditions**: Prespecified interim analysis after 30 flagged cases (2days) if median cycle time reduction >1.5h or if seniorcapacity utilization exceeds 80%, then terminate or scale up.  

This pilot isolates the effect of seniorcapacity shielding on downstream delays while preserving triage acceleration, allowing us to assess both efficiency and quality impacts with clear stopping rules.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden underlying risk**: If the risk score systematically underestimates true risk for a subset of cases, flagged cases would actually be higher risk, leading to longer downstream processing *because* of intrinsic complexity rather than the flag itself.  
2. **Queue interference**: If the manualreview queue capacity is insufficient, priority lane acceleration merely pushes flagged cases ahead of others, causing longer manualreview waits due to crowding; increasing capacity could reverse the observed delay.  
3. **Decisionquality degradation**: If reviewers become fatigued after handling many flagged cases, the faster triage could paradoxically degrade decision quality, making rejections more frequent and extending the overall process (e.g., through followup actions).  
4. **Timestamp semantics error**: Misaligned clocks between triage system and escalation system could artificially inflate escalation waiting times for flagged cases, producing a spurious delay that disappears once timestamp synchronization is fixed.  
5. **Positivity violation**: If certain combinations of high load and high complexity never receive a flag (due to a threshold rule), the flag effect cannot be estimated for that subgroup, biasing the observed accelerations.  
6. **Reviewer behavior change**: If reviewers alter their thoroughness after seeing many flagged cases (e.g., rushing approvals), the flag could both speed triage and reduce decision quality, leading to downstream rework that lengthens the apparent total cycle time.

## 9. Final synthesis

**What is directly observed**: Flagged cases start triage faster (median 12min vs 20min for unflagged) but incur substantially longer manualreview and escalation phases, resulting in median total completion times about 6–9hours longer for flagged cases despite the early advantage. Timestamps confirm the acceleration at triage and the delayed downstream steps.

**What is a plausible mechanism**: The flag triggers a priority triage lane (direct acceleration) but simultaneously mandates automatic manual review and escalation for many cases (mediated delay). Shared manualreview and escalation queues amplify these delays (mediated through queue interference). Underlying risk scores may misclassify some cases, and complexity interacts with the flag, creating a mix of true risk and procedural handling. Arrival load further modulates the effective capacity of the shared queues.

**What remains unidentified**: The precise causal contribution of each policy component (P0–P4) cannot be fully disentangled without experimental variation. Whether the observed acceleration is purely due to the priority lane or also reflects earlier “prescreening” that shortens downstream effort is unclear. Moreover, the effect of reviewer fatigue or decisionquality shifts after prolonged flag exposure is not captured in the data.

**Which intervention produces the most useful next evidence**: A **protocolspecific pilot of PolicyP3 (protected senior capacity for flagged cases)** offers a sharp, ethically defensible test of whether easing the escalation bottleneck reduces total cycle time without compromising quality. By isolating the escalation pathway, the pilot directly addresses the largest mediated delay while preserving the beneficial triage acceleration, thereby clarifying the balance between speed and thoroughness. This focused experiment also generates observable outcomes (escalation wait, decision agreement) that can be used to refine subsequent policy choices or to test alternative designs (e.g., removing mandatory manual review). 

Stated succinctly, the data reveal a paradox of faster early handling offset by downstream congestion. The most informative next step is a targeted pilot that protects the scarce senior resource for flagged cases, which can reveal whether downstream delays are primarily resourcecapacity driven or reflect deeper process interactions, guiding a balanced redesign of the flagdriven workflow.
