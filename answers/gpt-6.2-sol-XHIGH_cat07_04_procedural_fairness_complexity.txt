# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity.** Approval was 78% for A, 63% for B, 69% for C and 58% for D. These raw gaps describe who received benefits. Differences in eligibility and case mix can contribute to them, and approval rates alone cannot establish discrimination.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity.** The preliminary model estimates lower approval probabilities relative to A by 5.5 percentage points for B, 2.2 for C and 3.1 for D. The estimated gaps depend on the recorded covariates and the model. Their precision, overlap and sensitivity are unknown.
- **Procedural burden.** Measure document requests, repeat requests for the same item, applicant contacts, transfers and reopened steps per case. B's adjusted excess in missing-document requests (+9.5 percentage points) warrants attention. A single request can carry different burdens depending on its language, clarity and submission options.
- **Timeliness.** Measure time to first decision notification, activation for eligible applicants and appeal resolution separately. Compare median and tail delays and deadline breaches. C has the largest preliminary adjusted cycle-time gap (+4.1 days) and the longest raw 90th percentile (38.8 days versus 21.4 for A). Verify the endpoint and estimand of the adjusted result before comparing it with raw quantiles.
- **Consistency of routing.** Applicants meeting the same policy trigger should receive the required review or support, subject to documented authorized exceptions. Check both missing required steps and additional scrutiny imposed on comparable cases. Fair routing can involve different steps for different needs.
- **Quality and accessibility of support.** Assess whether help was delivered early enough to use, in the required language or format, and with effective follow-through. The activity record leaves open whether an applicant could understand instructions, supply evidence or participate.
- **Appeal and rework burden.** B, C and D have raw rework rates of 22%, 24% and 20% versus 11% for A. Separate agency rework, reopened cases and applicant appeals. Measure filing effort, access to appeal, time to resolution and correction of agency errors. A high appeal rate can reflect contestable decisions or better access to review. A low rate can reflect barriers to filing.
- **Resource-allocation fairness.** Assess access to qualified workers, queue age, workload and handovers among cases eligible for the same services. Worker counts describe assignment concentration. Available qualified hours, demand and coverage determine whether each queue can deliver timely service.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

1. **Define cohorts and endpoints.** Build one record per application with an intake snapshot and a common observation window. Follow cases from submission to first notification, with separate clocks for activation, appeal and closure. Retain pending cases and use time-to-event methods that account for right censoring. Report applications with an appeal and appeals per eligible decision with comparable follow-up from notification. Check missingness, logging coverage and event order by group, region and channel.
2. **Standardize to overlapping baseline case mix.** Match or stratify on region, intake month or policy period, channel, household-size band, prior-case count, and risk band and documentation status as recorded before the routing being assessed. Use coarsened strata or a balancing model if exact cells are sparse. Report covariate balance, the target population, overlap and the shares excluded from the comparison. Use documented requests or independently assessed needs where they define eligibility for a specific service. Record when each covariate was measured so that ratings revised after assistance, document collection or escalation do not enter as baseline complexity.
3. **Compare overall experience, routing and performance within routes.** First standardize overall outcomes and burdens on baseline variables, allowing subsequent routing and resource assignment to contribute to the differences. Compare route incidence, loops and transfers, and test whether the same policy trigger led to the same required step. Within comparable routes or stages, examine wait and rework among cases with comparable needs and complexity. Analyze application-to-notification prefixes separately from sequences containing activation or appeal. Full variants can be selected by outcomes, and conditioning on a route or assigned worker can introduce selection bias. These conditional comparisons locate disparities. Estimating how much a route or worker caused them would require additional assumptions or a stronger design.
4. **Compare resource allocation and decompose time.** Within the same eligibility, region, intake period and assignment opportunity, compare the likelihood of assignment to specialist teams or backlogged workers, transfers per case, and queue age and delay. Use case priority, eligible staff on duty and workload at assignment where available. Separate agency queueing, active handling, applicant response or other holds, and unclassified time. For example, distinguish time awaiting evidence from time waiting for an available qualified worker after the case is ready. Report upper tails and repeat visits. With a single recorded timestamp per event, start with elapsed gaps and seek queue-ready, start and completion timestamps for the decomposition.
5. **Test sensitivity and subgroup variation.** Repeat estimates with and without measured risk and completeness and with independently reviewed measures if available. Examine policy thresholds, overrides and documentation classifications by group. Show both comparisons within the same channel and region and estimates that retain the effects of uneven access to channels or regional capacity. Examine group-by-region and group-by-channel patterns, especially in the two backlog regions, and intersections with support needs where there is overlap. Report counts and uncertainty intervals, accounting for shared queues, workers or sites as feasible. Limited overlap or unstable results should narrow the conclusions.
6. **Interpret against policy and file evidence.** A routing difference can be justified when a valid, relevant requirement is applied consistently. A residual difference is a disparity under the stated adjustment. Review comparable files for decision reasons, requests, notices and exceptions to test for differential treatment. Applicant feedback and workload records can corroborate mechanisms. A staffing or routing pilot with a credible comparison can help establish whether a queue or transfer caused delays. The preliminary estimates provide neither uncertainty intervals nor a design that identifies discriminatory treatment.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Policy-triggered complexity.** Higher risk can require additional fraud checks, and genuinely missing evidence can require more collection and assessment. Effective language and accommodation support also requires qualified work and preserves participation.
2. **Limited staffing and dual roles.** Demand for bilingual staff or accommodation specialists can exceed coverage. Specialists who also conduct supervisor reviews face competing demands, and regional backlogs can amplify delays even under consistent routing.
3. **Transfers and fragmented ownership.** Each reassignment can put a case at the back of another queue, lose context or generate repeated evidence requests. Some transfers supply necessary expertise. Case review can establish what work each transfer enabled.
4. **Barriers to evidence submission.** Untranslated instructions, inaccessible forms or slow in-person document intake can make evidence harder to supply or slower to record. Recorded incompleteness can reflect those obstacles.
5. **Historical scoring bias or discretionary escalation.** Prior investigations and interactions with the system can increase measured risk. Staff can also vary in the evidence they accept or the cases they escalate, giving applicants with the same recorded characteristics different scrutiny.
6. **Notice and appeal access.** Unclear or inaccessible notices can generate avoidable rework, prompt challenges or deter meritorious appeals. Different rates of adverse decisions also change exposure to the opportunity to appeal.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- Risk bands mask differences within bands and may reflect historically unequal investigation or enforcement. Prior-case counts can reflect earlier access to or scrutiny by the system. Historical fraud referrals alone are a weak validation target because investigations and labels are selectively observed.
- Documentation completeness is coarse and may reflect staff judgment, a translation barrier, a processing lag or a revision after assistance. The log lacks the items missing, their necessity, receipt dates and reasons why evidence was rejected.
- Language preference and recorded accommodation requests only partially capture actual needs. Unrecorded requests, unmet needs, channel accessibility, support offered, accommodation type and whether it worked are missing. Validate the meaning and coverage of group and request fields before assigning cases to need-based cohorts.
- The single event timestamp and daily workload observations give limited visibility into intraday coverage, queue entry, work starts, concurrency, staff availability and assignment priority. Resource IDs may represent individuals, teams or automated steps. Missing, tied or off-system events can distort apparent paths and transfers.
- Outcome and appeal flags leave decision explanations, appeal eligibility, deadlines, abandonment, review quality, reversals and reasons for reversal largely unobserved. File review and applicant feedback are necessary to evaluate explanation quality, dignity of treatment and the usability of appeal rights. Open cases and unequal follow-up can affect both recorded approval and appeal rates.
