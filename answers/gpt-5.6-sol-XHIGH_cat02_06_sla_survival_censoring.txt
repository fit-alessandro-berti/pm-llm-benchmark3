# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let time zero be the recorded `Submit` activity, and let \(h=72\) elapsed hours. A resolution at exactly 72 hours is assumed timely; a resolution after 72 hours is a breach.

Define:

- \(T_R\): time to internal `Resolve`;
- \(T_W,T_J,T_X\): times to `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`;
- \(T=\min(T_R,T_W,T_J,T_X)\): time to the first observed internal terminal disposition;
- \(C\): administrative observation time for an `open` case.

Terminal durations are event times, not censoring times. An open case is right-censored at its age at cutoff. A transfer is an observed process transition; it is only treated as censoring under a specific external-outcome estimand.

## Estimand 1: probability of no internal resolution by 72 hours

\[
Q_{\text{int}}(72)=P(T_R>72)=1-F_R(72),
\]

where \(F_R(72)=P(T\leq72,J=R)\) is the cumulative incidence of internal resolution, with withdrawal, rejection, and—under the primary interpretation—transfer as competing outcomes.

This is the natural **SLA governance estimand** when operational success specifically requires an internal `Resolve`. It includes both:

- cases still active at 72 hours; and
- cases that reached a terminal but unsuccessful disposition before 72 hours.

Its decomposition is:

\[
1-F_R(72)=S_{\text{disp}}(72)+F_W(72)+F_J(72)+F_X(72).
\]

Thus, this metric should not be interpreted as backlog alone.

Under the primary internal interpretation, the observed sample gives a simple fixed-horizon identification interval before modeling censoring:

- 9 cases are known timely resolutions;
- 18 are known not internally resolved by 72: 4 late resolutions, 4 open beyond 72, 7 withdrawals/rejections, and 3 transfers;
- 5 open cases were observed for fewer than 72 hours.

Therefore, the raw sample bound is \(18/32=56.3\%\) to \(23/32=71.9\%\). This is a descriptive bound, not a population confidence interval.

## Estimand 2: probability of no terminal disposition by 72 hours

\[
S_{\text{disp}}(72)=P(T>72).
\]

Here `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` all remove a case from the internal active queue. This is the most relevant estimand for **backlog, staffing, queue ageing, and workload planning**. An early withdrawal is unfavorable under the success estimand but favorable in the narrow sense that it no longer consumes internal queue capacity.

Nine cases are definitely without a terminal disposition at 72:

- late terminal resolutions S03, S13, S20, and S28;
- transfer at 96 hours, S32;
- open beyond 72 hours, S09, S11, S25, and S26.

Five open cases were censored before 72, giving a raw sample bound of \(9/32=28.1\%\) to \(14/32=43.8\%\).

## Estimand 3: cumulative incidence of late resolution

For \(t>72\),

\[
F_R^{\text{late}}(t)=P(72<T\leq t,J=R)=F_R(t)-F_R(72).
\]

This estimates how much eventual internal resolution occurs only after the SLA. It is useful for **recovery planning, exception management, and quantifying the tail of completed resolution work**. It is not the same as conditioning on eventual resolution, which selects cases using future information.

Because follow-up ends at different ages, “eventual late resolution” is not identified without a finite reporting horizon and censoring assumptions. The full curve should be reported with the number at risk rather than reducing it to the four observed late resolutions.

## Contributions of illustrative cases

| Case | No internal resolution by 72 | No terminal disposition by 72 | Late-resolution CIF |
|---|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76 | Known yes; this is an already-observed SLA breach | Yes, because its terminal event is after 72 | Resolution event at 76 |
| S09, open at 92 | Known yes; it was observed unresolved through 72 | Known yes | At risk after 72 and right-censored at 92 without a resolution |
| S06, withdrawn at 27 | Yes under the internal-success definition; withdrawal is a competing event at 27 | No; it left the active queue at 27 | Competing event at 27, so it cannot subsequently have an internal late resolution |

For a global outcome that recognizes external resolution, S08’s status at 72 is unknown rather than necessarily failed.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use one row per case for nonparametric competing-risk analysis. Event code 0 denotes censoring, R denotes resolution, W withdrawal, J rejection, and X transfer.

| Observed status | Resolution event \(I_R\) | Any internal terminal event \(I_T\) | Primary competing-risk code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | 1 | 1 | R | None; event at `duration_or_age_h` | Split into timely \(T\leq72\) and late \(T>72\) resolution |
| `withdrawn` | 0 | 1 | W | None under real-world analysis | Combine with rejection as unsuccessful closure; treat as censoring only for a hypothetical “resolution if withdrawal were eliminated” estimand |
| `rejected` | 0 | 1 | J | None under real-world analysis | Combine with withdrawal; censoring requires a strong hypothetical and independent-censoring assumption |
| `transferred` | 0 under primary interpretation | 1 for the internal queue | X | None under primary internal analysis | Competing handoff, right-censoring, adverse composite failure, or assumed external resolution |
| `open` | 0 | 0 | 0 | Right-censor at age at cutoff | IPCW, censoring-stratified analysis, or pattern-mixture bounds if administrative censoring is informative |

For a terminal event observed after 72, the case is known to have remained without that terminal event through 72. For example, S32’s transfer at 96 establishes that it had no internal terminal disposition at 72.

## Transfer assumptions

At least four defensible transfer treatments answer different questions:

1. **Observed competing terminal outcome — primary internal analysis.**  
   `Transfer` is a distinct absorbing cause. It prevents internal resolution and removes the case from the internal queue. This directly matches internal SLA and workload governance.

2. **Right-censor at transfer.**  
   This treats external outcome follow-up as missing. It targets a resolution distribution in a population represented by non-transferred cases, or a hypothetical world in which censoring at transfer is independent after adjustment. This assumption is doubtful here because all transfers—S08, S21, and S32—are P1 and escalated.

3. **Adverse composite outcome.**  
   Transfer is grouped with withdrawal and rejection as an unsuccessful terminal disposition. At 72 this gives the same internal success/failure classification as treating transfer as a competing cause, but it supports a different operational event curve: time to unsuccessful closure.

4. **Optimistic external-resolution bound.**  
   Assume external resolution occurs immediately at transfer. S08 and S21 then become timely under a global-resolution definition, while S32 remains beyond 72 because transfer occurs at 96. This is a bounding assumption, not an observed fact.

Open cases are administratively right-censored only in the statistical sense that the extract was frozen. Non-informative censoring is still an assumption. Censoring age depends on submission calendar, which is not supplied, and may differ by cohort. Long gaps such as S26’s 72 hours, S11’s 55 hours, and S25’s 48 hours also indicate that risk depends on the state in which observation ends. Those gaps do not by themselves prove informative administrative censoring, but they should enter a conditional censoring or dynamic-risk model once historical snapshots are available.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Among the 13 observed resolutions, 9 are timely and 4 are late: S03, S13, S20, and S28. A resolved-only calculation would therefore report \(4/13=30.8\%\) late. That quantity is merely:

\[
P(T_R>72\mid\text{resolution observed by cutoff}),
\]

not the probability that a submitted case breaches.

## Survivor and completion bias

Resolution must occur before the cutoff for a case to enter the completed-resolved denominator. Slow cases are preferentially excluded:

- S09 is open at 92 hours;
- S11 at 81;
- S25 at 79;
- S26 at 104.

All four are already known to have missed the 72-hour internal resolution target. Dropping them makes the tail look shorter.

The five younger open cases—S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31—also cannot simply be discarded or labeled. They were observed for only 47, 35, 67, 51, and 23 hours respectively, so their 72-hour outcomes remain unknown.

## Competing outcomes are not ordinary censoring

S06 withdrew at 27 hours and S05 was rejected at 9. They can no longer reach internal `Resolve` if those statuses are truly terminal. Treating them as ordinary non-informative censoring leaves hypothetical versions of those cases in a resolution risk set and can overstate actual resolution incidence.

Transfer is even more clearly process-dependent. S08, S21, and S32 were all P1, escalated, and routed externally. Censoring them as if they were exchangeable with non-transferred cases would remove a concentrated high-risk path.

## Open below 72 and open above 72 are not equivalent

- An open case aged **above 72**, such as S09 or S26, is right-censored for eventual resolution but has a known fixed-horizon breach.
- An open case aged **below 72**, such as S24 at 67 or S31 at 23, is censored before the horizon and has an unknown breach indicator.

Coding every open case as a breach is pessimistic; coding every open case as non-breaching is optimistic; dropping all of them is selection-biased.

## Cohort imbalance and incomplete policy compliance

Cohort A has 15 cases and B has 17. Priority composition differs: A has 3 P1 cases while B has 5. This matters because all three transfers are P1.

Cohort is also not equivalent to receipt of automated screening:

- AutoCheck appears in 2 of 15 A prefixes;
- AutoCheck appears in 13 of 17 B prefixes.

Thus there is overlap and incomplete compliance. A crude A-versus-B comparison mixes policy period, priority, calendar time, assignment decisions, and actual path taken. The superficially lower resolved-only late proportion in B—2/7 versus 2/6 in A—cannot be interpreted as a policy effect.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## Kaplan–Meier

Kaplan–Meier is suitable for \(S_{\text{disp}}(t)\) if all internal terminal dispositions, including transfer, are coded as events and open cases are censored. It then estimates the probability of remaining in the active internal process.

Kaplan–Meier is not the preferred estimator of actual resolution probability if withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are censored. That construction estimates a hypothetical or “net” resolution distribution under independent competing-event censoring, not the observed probability of resolution.

## Aalen–Johansen and cumulative incidence

Aalen–Johansen is the recommended primary estimator. Use causes:

- R: resolution;
- W: withdrawal;
- J: rejection;
- X: transfer.

It directly estimates \(F_R(t),F_W(t),F_J(t),F_X(t)\), and the event-free probability \(S_{\text{disp}}(t)\). The primary SLA output is:

\[
Q_{\text{int}}(72)=1-F_R(72),
\]

while the queue-tail output is \(S_{\text{disp}}(72)\).

Because there are only 32 cases and sparse causes, withdrawal and rejection may also be combined in a secondary display. Confidence intervals should reflect sampling uncertainty, for example through case-level bootstrap or an Aalen–Johansen variance estimator. These intervals should be shown separately from the fixed-horizon identification bounds caused by pre-72 censoring.

## Cause-specific hazards

For cause \(k\),

\[
\lambda_k(t\mid H(t))=
\lim_{\Delta t\to0}
\frac{P(t\le T<t+\Delta t,J=k\mid T\ge t,H(t))}{\Delta t}.
\]

Cause-specific models are useful for identifying stage-specific mechanisms: whether cases currently in review are more likely to resolve, transfer, or withdraw next. A counting-process Cox model could eventually include time-varying process state.

A resolution cause-specific hazard alone does not determine the probability of resolution; all competing hazards are needed to reconstruct cumulative incidence.

## Subdistribution hazards

A Fine–Gray model directly relates covariates to a cumulative incidence such as resolution by 72. It can be useful for risk stratification, but is not preferred here because:

- there are too few events for stable regression;
- subdistribution hazard ratios are not intuitive transition rates;
- process covariates are time-varying and awkward to represent safely;
- policy coefficients would remain associational and confounded.

## Restricted mean time

Two restricted-mean quantities are useful:

- \(\int_0^{72} S_{\text{disp}}(t)\,dt\): expected active internal queue-hours over the first 72 hours;
- \(\int_0^{72} [1-F_R(t)]\,dt\): restricted time without successful resolution, including terminal unsuccessful cases.

The first is preferable for staffing because withdrawal, rejection, and transfer stop consuming internal queue time. The second is closer to a service-success burden but should not be labeled queue time.

## Landmarking

At operational landmarks such as 24 or 48 hours, estimate:

\[
P(T_R>72\mid T>t,H(t)),
\]

where \(H(t)\) contains only history known by the landmark: current stage, events completed so far, review count to date, inactivity duration, and time remaining.

The supplied data cannot support a valid retrospective landmark model because exact event times are absent and each completed prefix contains future events relative to earlier landmarks. Landmarking is nevertheless the preferred dynamic prediction design once timestamped histories are available.

## Multi-state modeling

A multi-state model can estimate transition-specific hazards among screening, review, rework, escalation, transfer, and terminal outcomes. It is the most process-aware long-term approach, especially if external outcomes create a latent post-transfer state.

With the current extract, only activity order and total duration are known. Transition times and state sojourns cannot be reconstructed without fabricating timestamps, so the present data support sequence features and case-level survival analysis but not transition-hazard estimation.

## Recommended hierarchy

1. **Primary now:** nonparametric Aalen–Johansen analysis with withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as competing outcomes. Report \(F_R(72)\), \(1-F_R(72)\), all competing CIFs, and \(S_{\text{disp}}(72)\).
2. **Secondary now:** Kaplan–Meier for time to any internal terminal disposition, together with restricted mean active time through 72 and transfer/censoring sensitivity bounds.
3. **Dynamic extension:** landmark or multi-state cause-specific models after exact event timestamps and historical snapshots become available.

Cohort and priority results should be descriptive strata only. Regression or causal policy claims are not supportable with this sample size and assignment process.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

## Feature classification

| Feature | Role | Safe use and risks |
|---|---|---|
| Priority | Baseline | Available at Submit; usable for stratification and baseline prediction |
| Cohort | Baseline proxy | Known at start, but confounded with calendar period, assignment, and rollout compliance |
| Attained case age | Time scale / dynamic | Essential at a landmark; terminal duration must not be used as a baseline predictor |
| Current stage | Time-varying | Use the latest activity known at prediction time; terminal states are outcomes, not predictors |
| AutoCheck observed | Time-varying/post-assignment | Actual occurrence is after Submit; it is not interchangeable with baseline policy assignment |
| Escalation to date | Time-varying, post-treatment | Set to 0 before `Escalate` and 1 afterward; final `escalated` is leakage at baseline |
| Rework count to date | Time-varying, post-treatment | Potential mediator and severity signal; final count leaks future activity |
| Number of Review visits | Time-varying | Captures review loops even when `rework_count` differs |
| Path pattern or variant | Time-varying | Examples include AutoCheck-before-Screen and Review-after-Escalate; truncate at prediction time |
| Inactivity gap | Time-varying snapshot | Useful for live alerts, but historical values must be reconstructed at each training landmark |
| Full final prefix | Post-outcome/leakage | Contains `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer` and must not be a baseline feature |

The supplied prefixes reveal that repeated review and recorded rework are related but not identical. S03 has two `Review` visits without recorded rework, while S27 records rework followed directly by `Resolve`. Both token counts and explicit rework indicators may therefore be useful, subject to timestamp validation.

`last_event_gap_h` is especially leakage-prone in this extract. It is zero for every terminal case because the endpoint is the latest event, while it is positive for open cases. A model trained on that final snapshot would largely learn whether the outcome has already occurred. It is valid for predicting from today’s open-case snapshot only if comparable historical open snapshots are generated for training.

## Immortal-time and confounding risks

A case labeled `escalated=yes` must have remained in the process long enough to reach `Escalate`. Assigning that final label at Submit creates immortal-time bias: pre-escalation time is incorrectly attributed to the escalated group. Escalation is also likely triggered by complexity or anticipated delay, causing confounding by indication.

The correct alternatives are:

- a counting-process covariate \(E(t)\) that changes from 0 to 1 at the escalation time;
- a landmark model using escalation status known at the landmark;
- a multi-state model with an explicit escalation transition.

The same logic applies to AutoCheck occurrence, rework, review loops, and full path variants.

## Proposed state-transition structure

A process model consistent with the observed control flow is:

- `Submit`  `AutoCheck` or `Screen`
- `AutoCheck`  `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, or `Resolve`
- `Screen`  `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Reject`, or `Withdraw`
- `Review`  `Rework`, `Escalate`, `Resolve`, `Reject`, or `Withdraw`
- `Rework`  `Review` or `Resolve`
- `Escalate`  `Review`, `Resolve`, or `Transfer`
- `Resolve`, `Reject`, and `Withdraw` are absorbing
- `Transfer` is absorbing for the internal process, but can lead to an unobserved external state and eventually to external resolution or another outcome

For modeling stability, `Screen` and `AutoCheck` could initially be grouped into a broader screening state, while preserving an indicator for the order in which they occurred.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

## 1. Internal competing-risk analysis

Primary assumptions:

- transfer is a competing internal terminal outcome;
- withdrawal and rejection are separate competing outcomes;
- open cases are administratively censored;
- Aalen–Johansen estimates actual internal resolution incidence.

This directly answers internal SLA governance. Its fixed-sample no-resolution bound is 56.3%–71.9%, depending on the five pre-72 open cases.

For the no-terminal workload estimand, the corresponding bound is 28.1%–43.8%.

## 2. External transfer scenarios

Compare:

- transfer as a competing terminal outcome;
- transfer censored at handoff;
- transfer grouped into adverse closure;
- transfer assumed to resolve externally at handoff.

S08 and S21 transfer before 72 and therefore drive the difference between internal and optimistic global resolution. S32 transfers at 96 and is a known 72-hour tail case under all interpretations.

If early external outcomes are treated as unknown, a broader global-resolution sample bound is \(16/32=50.0\%\) to \(23/32=71.9\%\). Transfer treatment can particularly reverse conclusions about P1 and escalation because all transfers are both P1 and escalated.

## 3. Withdrawal and rejection scenarios

Compare:

- actual competing outcomes;
- a composite “unsuccessful terminal disposition” outcome;
- censoring at withdrawal/rejection for a hypothetical elimination estimand;
- exclusion only if an external SLA contract explicitly removes such cases from the denominator.

Censoring withdrawal and rejection will generally increase estimated hypothetical resolution relative to actual cumulative incidence, but its interpretation depends on strong exchangeability assumptions. Early withdrawal/rejection also creates an apparent improvement in queue clearance while worsening operational success, so conclusions can reverse depending on whether the decision concerns backlog or successful resolution.

## 4. Informative-censoring and pattern-mixture analysis

The five cases censored before 72 can be assigned in turn to:

- all timely;
- all breaches;
- stage- and inactivity-dependent outcomes.

The all-timely/all-breach extremes produce the primary 56.3%–71.9% sample bound. By cohort:

- A has 9 known no-resolution outcomes and 2 unknown, giving 60.0%–73.3%;
- B has 9 known no-resolution outcomes and 3 unknown, giving 52.9%–70.6%.

These intervals overlap and permit either cohort ordering. For example, B looks better if its unknown cases are timely and A’s are breaches, but B can look worse under the opposite assignment.

With richer data, use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights conditional on submission calendar, cohort, priority, stage, workload, and process history. Current data lack the start and cutoff dates needed to estimate that model credibly.

## 5. Cohort-policy analysis

Compare:

- crude cohort A versus B;
- priority-standardized cohort estimates;
- assignment-period or intention-to-policy analysis;
- actual AutoCheck receipt analyzed as a time-varying process feature;
- rollout-compliance strata.

Because assignment was not randomized, actual AutoCheck is post-start, and compliance was incomplete, none of these comparisons identifies a causal policy effect. A conclusion that cohort B improved the SLA could reverse after accounting for unresolved cases, transfer handling, priority mix, calendar time, or workload.

# 7. Operational interpretation

## Alerts

For an open case younger than 72 hours, produce conditional quantities such as:

- probability of no resolution by 72;
- probability of transfer, withdrawal, or rejection before resolution;
- probability of still being active at 72;
- expected remaining active time.

Alerts should use only history available at the alert time. An open case already older than 72 does not need a “breach probability”: the breach has occurred. Its model should instead predict recovery time and next disposition.

Thresholds should reflect intervention capacity and costs. A top-\(k\) queue may be more operationally stable than a fixed probability threshold when staffing is limited.

## Workload actions

Aggregate individual conditional probabilities to estimate:

- expected number of current cases still active at 72;
- expected active queue-hours by stage;
- likely demand for senior review or external transfer coordination.

Possible actions include checking stalled cases, assigning specialists to repeated-review paths, and following up externally after transfer. These actions should be tested experimentally or quasi-experimentally; a risk association does not show that escalation or reassignment will reduce risk.

## SLA governance

A dashboard should separately show:

1. cumulative incidence of timely resolution;
2. withdrawal, rejection, and transfer incidence;
3. probability of no terminal disposition at 72;
4. observed late resolutions;
5. number at risk and censored before the horizon;
6. transfer-treatment sensitivity results.

This prevents early unsuccessful closure from being mistaken for healthy throughput and prevents completed-case turnaround from being presented as overall SLA performance.

## Calibration and fairness

At each operational landmark, assess:

- censoring-adjusted calibration plots;
- time-dependent Brier scores;
- observed versus predicted risk by priority, cohort, stage, and calendar period;
- temporal validation across policy periods;
- calibration drift as rollout compliance changes.

Fairness checks should compare alert rates, false-negative rates, intervention allocation, and calibration across priority groups and any relevant protected attributes once collected. Transfer-related label missingness is already differential by priority in this extract: all transfers are P1. That must be addressed before interpreting a P1 risk gradient.

Cohort, AutoCheck, escalation, rework, and path features should be presented as predictive associations. They are not causal effects or estimates of intervention benefit.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

The following executable Python uses pandas and PM4Py. It implements Aalen–Johansen calculations directly, so it does not assume `lifelines` or another survival library. PM4Py is used only for control-flow parsing; no event timestamps are created.

```python

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

raw = """case,cohort,priority,duration_or_age_h,observed_status,prefix,escalated,rework_count,last_event_gap_h

"""

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(raw))
df["time_h"] = df["duration_or_age_h"].astype(float)
H = 72.0

# Primary competing-risk encoding: open=0/censor, R/W/J/X are event causes.
status_to_code = {
    "resolved": "R",
    "withdrawn": "W",
    "rejected": "J",
    "transferred": "X",
    "open": "0"
}
df["cr_code_primary"] = df["observed_status"].map(status_to_code)
df["event_resolve"] = (df["observed_status"] == "resolved").astype(int)
df["event_any_terminal"] = (df["observed_status"] != "open").astype(int)
df["observed_late_resolution"] = (
    (df["observed_status"] == "resolved") & (df["time_h"] > H)
).astype(int)
df["event_unsuccessful_terminal"] = df["observed_status"].isin(
    ["withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"]
).astype(int)

# Fixed-horizon labels. NaN means that the 72-hour outcome is not identified.
def no_resolution_label(row, transfer_scenario):
    status, t = row["observed_status"], row["time_h"]
    if status == "resolved":
        return float(t > H)
    if status in ("withdrawn", "rejected"):
        return 1.0
    if status == "open":
        return 1.0 if t >= H else np.nan
    if status == "transferred":
        if transfer_scenario == "competing_internal":
            return 1.0
        if transfer_scenario == "censor_at_transfer":
            return 1.0 if t >= H else np.nan
        if transfer_scenario == "resolve_at_transfer":
            return 0.0 if t <= H else 1.0
    raise ValueError("Unexpected status or scenario")

for scenario in [
    "competing_internal",
    "censor_at_transfer",
    "resolve_at_transfer"
]:
    df["no_resolve72_" + scenario] = df.apply(
        no_resolution_label, axis=1, transfer_scenario=scenario
    )

# Second estimand: no observed internal terminal disposition by 72.
def no_terminal_label(row):
    t, status = row["time_h"], row["observed_status"]
    if status == "open":
        return 1.0 if t >= H else np.nan
    return float(t > H)

df["no_terminal72"] = df.apply(no_terminal_label, axis=1)

# Event coding for three transfer assumptions.
def event_codes(frame, transfer_scenario):
    codes = frame["observed_status"].map(status_to_code).copy()
    if transfer_scenario == "censor_at_transfer":
        codes.loc[frame["observed_status"] == "transferred"] = "0"
    elif transfer_scenario == "resolve_at_transfer":
        codes.loc[frame["observed_status"] == "transferred"] = "R"
    elif transfer_scenario != "competing_internal":
        raise ValueError("Unknown transfer scenario")
    return codes

# Direct Aalen-Johansen calculation at a horizon.
# Event-first handling is used for any tied event/censor times.
def aj_at_horizon(frame, horizon, transfer_scenario="competing_internal"):
    codes = event_codes(frame, transfer_scenario)
    causes = ["R", "W", "J", "X"]
    F = {cause: 0.0 for cause in causes}
    S = 1.0

    event_times = sorted(
        frame.loc[
            (codes != "0") & (frame["time_h"] <= horizon), "time_h"
        ].unique()
    )

    for t in event_times:
        risk = int((frame["time_h"] >= t).sum())
        at_t = (frame["time_h"] == t)
        d_by_cause = {
            cause: int((at_t & (codes == cause)).sum()) for cause in causes
        }
        d_all = sum(d_by_cause.values())
        if risk == 0 or d_all == 0:
            continue

        S_before = S
        for cause, d_k in d_by_cause.items():
            F[cause] += S_before * d_k / risk
        S = S_before * (1.0 - d_all / risk)

    return {
        "F_R": F["R"],
        "F_W": F["W"],
        "F_J": F["J"],
        "F_X": F["X"],
        "S_no_event": S,
        "Q_no_resolution": 1.0 - F["R"]
    }
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The most important missing data are:

1. **Exact timestamps for every activity.**  
   Submit, screening, review, rework, escalation, transfer, and terminal timestamps are required for stage sojourns, time-varying covariates, valid landmarks, and multi-state transition hazards. Total duration cannot be apportioned across prefix activities.

2. **External outcomes after transfer.**  
   Link transfer records to external resolution time, external rejection/withdrawal, and loss to follow-up. Without this, global resolution estimands are only bounded or assumption-dependent.

3. **Submission and cutoff calendar dates.**  
   These are needed to verify follow-up time, characterize administrative censoring, distinguish cohort from calendar effects, and assess whether later submissions systematically have less opportunity for observation.

4. **Inclusion and sampling rules.**  
   The extract does not specify the enrollment window, whether all submitted cases were included, or whether reopened and duplicate cases are represented.

5. **Time-varying workload and capacity.**  
   Queue length, staffing, reviewer availability, shifts, holidays, and external capacity may affect both policy assignment and delay. The SLA is elapsed time, but operational calendars can still explain hazards.

6. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance.**  
   Needed fields include policy eligibility, assignment rules, actual automation availability, opt-outs, rollout site or team, and reasons AutoCheck was or was not used. These are essential for any causal policy analysis.

7. **Reasons and timing for escalation and transfer.**  
   Escalation may be triggered by predicted lateness or complexity. Without indications and timestamps, its association with delay is strongly confounded.

8. **Baseline case complexity and fairness attributes.**  
   Case type, channel, documentation quality, geography, customer characteristics, and legally permitted protected attributes are needed for risk adjustment and fairness auditing.

9. **Terminal-state definitions and quality.**  
   It should be confirmed whether withdrawal and rejection permanently preclude later resolution, whether transferred cases can return, and whether `Resolve` quality or reopenings matter.

10. **Larger temporal samples.**  
    Thirty-two cases and sparse competing events are insufficient for stable multivariable hazards, Fine–Gray regression, or reliable subgroup comparisons. A larger out-of-time sample is needed for calibration and validation.
