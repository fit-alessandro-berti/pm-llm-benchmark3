**# 1. Mechanism map**

Restructuring actions map to the following causal mechanisms and observable process-mining effects.

**Action: Merge N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech into a centralized Technical Shared Service (TSS) and route all high-complexity cases plus difficult Quality Check / legal-risk triage into shared queues.**

- Nominal local specialist handover loops (current caseworker ↔ embedded tech ≈ 36 + 31 per 100 cases) decline sharply because capacity is no longer co-located.  
- A central queue forms that absorbs the former local volume plus newly routed high-complexity and difficult QC work.  
- Specialist utilization rises (management target +15 %) by pooling, yet the same pool also absorbs legal/risk triage and difficult QC, raising capacity risk (current Legal/Risk already at 91–93 %).  
- Local informal advice and early triage disappear; local caseworkers lose rapid technical input that currently prevents unnecessary referrals or rework.  
- Local expertise erodes over time (local_expertise_flag declines; regional convention knowledge weakens).  

**Observable PM effects:** Handover-of-work network density of local caseworker–tech edges drops; new high-centrality TSS nodes appear. Waiting times (pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time / get_service_time, performance DFG) show higher median and especially 90th-percentile waits after TSS handovers (management estimate 4.0 h vs current 3.2 h). Resource activity frequencies and discover_organizational_roles / activity-based resource similarity reveal broader activity sets for TSS resources. Rework_flag and get_rework_cases_per_activity may rise on high-complexity and regionally nuanced cases. Filters on case_complexity, required_specialty, local_expertise_flag, team/location expose differentials.

**Action: Centralize Specialist Review, Legal Review, Risk Review and difficult Quality Check; broaden detection rules.**

- Frequency of referrals to Legal/Risk rises (9 → ≈12 per 100). Near-capacity specialists face longer queues; additional volume from TSS triage compounds the bottleneck. Standardization improves consistency but loses local context (already weak in cross-team notes).  

**Observable PM effects:** Increased edges in handover and subcontracting networks to shared Legal/Risk resources; elevated sojourn times on specialty activities in performance DFGs; possible initial rise in rework after specialty handovers (current baseline already 14 %). Variants and temporal features lengthen for cases with required_specialty ≠ none.

**Action: Introduce shared approval queue for medium/high-value cases and remove one local supervisor.**

- Decision criteria standardize, reducing local variation. Ownership and accountability diffuse (“no one’s case”). Frequency of caseworker → approval rises (42 → 49); waits increase (2.4 h → 3.0 h estimate).  

**Observable PM effects:** discover_organizational_roles shows blurred supervisor roles; working-together network density among remaining supervisors rises. Handover network gains a high in-degree shared-approval node. Reassignment_count and handover_reason codes rise as proxies for unclear ownership; correlation of outcomes/SLA misses with specific resources weakens.

**Action: Keep Intake and simple Eligibility local; allow teams to pull completed specialist advice.**

- Front-end local control is retained and pure cross-team handovers fall (6 → 3 estimate). Pull semantics can create idle waiting. Local skill development slows because hard cases are routed away.  

**Observable PM effects:** DFG and variants retain high-frequency local Intake → Eligibility paths; new “pull-from-shared” activities appear. Service/waiting times between specialist completion and local resumption become measurable. Over months, local resources perform fewer technical activities (resource-similarity matrices, activity-based roles decline).

**Cross-cutting mechanisms**

- **Resilience to absence:** Local redundancy disappears; TSS becomes a single point of failure. Absences or spikes produce sudden rises in waiting times, reassignment_count and residual cross-team handovers.  
- **Fairness:** East (current E_Tech util 82 %, slower cycles, lower satisfaction) gains capacity relief. North/South lose local-knowledge advantage. Customer segments needing language or regional accommodation face more handovers and context loss. Segmented performance DFGs, cycle-time distributions, SLA-miss and rework rates by team × complexity × customer_segment reveal disparities.  
- **Learning:** Routing hard cases away reduces on-the-job learning → progressive drop in local_expertise_flag and rising formal specialist dependence.  
- **Net handovers:** Local specialist loops decline, yet total handovers per case may stay similar or rise because of shared-queue round-trips (caseworker → TSS → caseworker → shared approval) and broader specialty routing. Overall handover frequency, network degree/betweenness of central nodes, and before/after discover_handover_of_work_network quantify the net effect.

**# 2. Scenario forecasts**

**Optimistic scenario**  
Assumes adequate shared-service capacity, effective knowledge-transfer protocols, prioritization by complexity/priority, and low resistance.

- Handover pattern: Local specialist loops essentially eliminated; TSS handovers ≈41/100, Legal/Risk ≈12, cross-team ≤3. Network becomes star-like (local → central → local) with lower local pairwise density.  
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: TSS median wait stabilizes near or below 4 h; shared approval 2.5–3 h; Legal/Risk modestly higher but controlled. Variance managed; high-priority cases dequeued preferentially.  
- Cycle-time distribution: Median improves 10–15 % overall (larger gain for East). 90th percentile stable or improved; tails compress.  
- Rework and quality: Overall rework falls slightly (≈7–8 %); complex cases maintain quality via specialist depth. local_expertise_flag decline is slow.  
- Role clarity and accountability: Clear RACI and audit trails keep ownership visible; organizational-role discovery shows cleaner separation.  
- Fairness: East benefits most; North/South neutral-to-positive; regional-accommodation segments retain local intake contact; variance across teams and segments declines.  
- Early warning signs: Rising TSS centrality without wait spikes; stable rework by complexity; balanced utilization 75–85 %; converging segmented CT/SLA metrics.

**Central scenario**  
Utilization gains materialize, standardization helps, yet moderate expertise loss and queue friction appear; Legal/Risk capacity remains tight.

- Handover pattern: Local tech loops largely gone; TSS 38–45/100, shared approval 45–50, Legal/Risk 11–14, residual cross-team 4–5. Occasional clarification back-flows.  
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: TSS median 4.5–6 h, shared approval 3–4 h, Legal/Risk 12–18 h. Variance rises; high-complexity cases queue longer.  
- Cycle-time distribution: Overall median stable or +≈5 %. East improves (median –1 to –1.5 d); North/South flat or +0.5 d. 90th percentile modestly worse, especially for complex cases.  
- Rework and quality: Overall rework 9–11 %; complex/regional cases show higher rework. Outcomes mixed by segment.  
- Role clarity and accountability: Moderate diffusion; reassignment_count rises; roles blur (caseworkers less technical, specialists more triage).  
- Fairness: East gains; North/South mild degradation on complex/regional cases and local satisfaction; high-complexity and special-need segments relatively disadvantaged.  
- Early warning signs: Rising median/90th waits on TSS and approval; increasing reassignment_count and “clarification” handover reasons; local_expertise_flag drop correlated with rework on complex cases; East vs North/South CT divergence; shared-service util >90 %.

**Adverse scenario**  
Capacity under-estimated, knowledge loss cascades, cultural resistance and single-point bottlenecks dominate.

- Handover pattern: Formal local loops gone but compensatory reassignments and clarification handovers rise; TSS volume exceeds estimates (≈50+); Legal/Risk overload produces escalations. Congested star network with high back-flow.  
- Queue/waiting-time pattern: Severe bottlenecks—TSS median 8–15 h+, shared approval 5–8 h, Legal/Risk >20 h. High-complexity and specialty cases suffer longest waits.  
- Cycle-time distribution: Median may appear stable (simple local cases improve) while 90th percentile and tails inflate sharply. East relatively better than its baseline but absolute tails poor; North/South worse on complex cases. Heavy-tailed or bimodal distribution.  
- Rework and quality: Rework rises to 12–16 %+ (especially post-specialist and complex/regional). local_expertise_flag drops sharply; outcomes and satisfaction decline for affected segments.  
- Role clarity and accountability: Low; “not my case” behaviors, high reassignments, fragmented organizational roles, weak accountability for SLA misses.  
- Fairness: East still relatively better but absolute performance limited by bottlenecks; North/South lose local expertise advantage; special-need customer segments and high-complexity cases systematically disadvantaged.  
- Early warning signs (days–weeks): Sharp rise in shared-queue waits and queue lengths; spike in reassignment_count and clarification reasons; rapid local_expertise_flag drop + rework_flag rise on complex cases; util saturation >95 %; segmented performance divergence; increase in long-wait variants; staff reports of ownership confusion or skill atrophy.

**# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time**

Even if mean cycle time remains stable or improves, the following can deteriorate:

1. **Tail latency / 90th-percentile delays** – Central queues create heavy-tailed waits; high-complexity or specialty cases pile up while simple local cases move faster, pulling the average down.  
2. **High-complexity case degradation** – All high-complexity cases are routed to TSS; loss of local informal triage produces longer effective queues and higher rework precisely on the hardest cases (filter by case_complexity).  
3. **Local knowledge / expertise loss** – Decline in local_expertise_flag and local tech loops raises rework on regionally nuanced cases. North/South (previously strong) lose advantage while East may improve, masking net loss in averages.  
4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments** – East benefits from unloading overloaded E_Tech; North/South may suffer longer waits or lower quality for local-convention customers. Segments needing language/regional accommodation experience more handovers and worse outcomes; averages hide subgroup harm.  
5. **Learning and skill atrophy** – Local staff stop developing technical skill because hard cases are routed away. Observable later as reduced activity variety and changing resource-similarity matrices; long-term resilience declines.  
6. **Accountability and role-clarity diffusion** – Shared queues blur ownership (reassignment_count ↑, unclear organizational roles). Errors become harder to attribute; quality issues surface only in outcomes, not in average speed.  
7. **Exception handling and residual cross-team failure** – Although nominal cross-team handovers fall, remaining or new exceptions still suffer high waits/rework because case notes remain inconsistent; averages ignore rare but severe failures.  
8. **Resilience to absence or demand spikes** – Single central TSS becomes a single point of failure; local buffers disappear. Average performance under normal load looks acceptable until an absence or volume spike produces cascading delays invisible in pre-spike means.

These risks are detectable only by examining distributions, segments (team, complexity, specialty, customer_segment), network structure, rework flags and role metrics—not by mean cycle time alone.

**# 5. Pilot design**

**Pilot scope**  
Phased team-based pilot: convert East (highest current specialist utilization and slowest cycles) to the full shared-service model first, while North and South remain on the current embedded model as contemporaneous controls. Optionally, within East, retain a temporary “shadow” local-tech advice channel for a random 20–30 % of high-complexity cases to quantify the value of residual local expertise. Duration: 8–12 weeks of live operation after a short transition.

**Comparison group**  
North and South continue unchanged. This isolates the differential effect on the team most likely to benefit (East) versus the teams most likely to lose local expertise (North/South).

**Pre/post observation windows**  
- Pre: minimum 6–8 weeks of clean event-log data under the current model (all three teams).  
- Transition: 1–2 weeks (exclude from primary analysis or flag).  
- Post: 8–12 weeks under the pilot configuration.  
Match calendar periods where possible to control seasonality.

**Segmentation (mandatory)**  
All analyses stratified by:  
- team / location (East vs North vs South),  
- case_complexity (low / medium / high),  
- required_specialty (none / legal / risk / security / other),  
- customer_segment,  
- priority.  

**Guardrail metrics (drawn from leading indicators)**  
- 90th-percentile waiting time after TSS, shared-approval and Legal/Risk handovers,  
- specialist utilization (target band 75–90 %; alert >93 %),  
- reassignment_count and clarification-related handover_reason rates,  
- local_expertise_flag rate and early rework_flag rate on high-complexity cases,  
- residual cross-team handover frequency and waits,  
- segmented cycle-time and SLA-miss gaps (East vs North/South; complexity and customer-segment strata).

**Staff feedback collection**  
Weekly short pulse surveys + bi-weekly structured interviews with caseworkers, remaining supervisors and shared-service staff covering: perceived role clarity, ownership of cases, informal advice access, learning opportunities, fairness perceptions, and exception-handling friction. Link qualitative themes to quantitative flags (reassignment, expertise, rework).

**Rollback or adjustment triggers**  
- Any shared-service 90th-percentile wait exceeds 2× management estimate for two consecutive weeks,  
- segmented rework rate on high-complexity or regional cases rises >3 percentage points relative to pre-pilot baseline,  
- shared-service utilization sustained >95 %,  
- fairness gap (e.g., 90th CT or SLA-miss difference between East and North/South, or between special-need vs other segments) widens beyond a pre-agreed threshold,  
- rapid drop in local_expertise_flag accompanied by rising clarification handovers,  
- staff feedback indicates widespread ownership confusion or skill-atrophy concerns.  

If any trigger fires, pause further rollout, restore local tech capacity for the affected scope, and re-evaluate routing rules or staffing before expanding.

**# 6. Recommendation**

Pilot first (phased, starting with East, North/South as controls), then modify on the basis of pilot evidence, then decide on broader rollout. Do not proceed to full organizational restructuring on the strength of the current management estimates alone.

The expected benefits—nominal reduction in local specialist loops, higher specialist utilization, and greater standardization—are plausible and could be material for East. At the same time the countervailing risks (central bottlenecks, local-expertise erosion, role-clarity diffusion, fairness differentials, learning atrophy and loss of resilience) are equally plausible and are only partially visible in average cycle time. Because East starts from a weaker base it is the natural first pilot site; North and South provide the necessary contrast to measure expertise-loss effects.

Management must not interpret early stability or modest improvement in average cycle time as success. Average CT can improve while 90th-percentile waits, high-complexity rework, segmented fairness gaps, and long-term learning capacity all deteriorate. Only the leading indicators (handover-network shifts, waiting-time distributions, utilization, reassignment counts, local_expertise_flag, early rework by complexity) and the segmented lagging indicators, together with staff feedback, can support a go/no-go decision. If the pilot guardrails are breached, the design already contains explicit rollback or adjustment triggers. A carefully instrumented pilot is therefore both the lowest-risk path to realizing genuine benefits and the only reliable way to discover whether the restructuring is net positive.
