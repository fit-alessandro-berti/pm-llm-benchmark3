## 1. Decision rule

I would deploy a **marginal-value-and-evidence rule**, not a breach-probability threshold.

A case is eligible for a Senior Diagnostic Swarm only if all of the following hold:

1. **Stage eligibility:** the case is still before `Diagnosis Complete`. Once `Diagnosis Complete` has occurred, the swarm is treated as the wrong intervention, even if breach risk is high.

2. **No duplicate action without new evidence:** repeated model refreshes are treated as the same alert episode unless there is a material change: new diagnostic artifact, customer log, stage transition, new owner/handover, missing-data repair, large queue change, or a meaningful probability movement. As a deployable default, I would require either:

   * a new process/customer event, or
   * an adjusted probability increase of roughly 0.08 or more, or
   * a move into a tighter time-to-SLA band, such as below 4 hours or below 2 hours.

3. **Positive marginal value after externalities:** for each eligible case, compute a conservative marginal score:

   `score ≈ expected absolute risk reduction × value of avoiding breach − €450 − false-alarm burden − expected spillover cost − reserve option value`

   The effect range is not treated as a known causal effect. I would look at the lower bound, midpoint, and segment reliability before spending a slot.

4. **Calibration robustification:** I would not use `p_now` directly as truth.

   * **SME:** increase the breach-risk interpretation by about 0.04–0.09. This raises concern for I-05, I-07, and I-10.
   * **Strategic Enterprise:** decrease the breach-risk interpretation by about 0.05–0.12. This downweights I-02, I-04, I-08, and I-03, though high value can still justify action.
   * **Public Sector:** use the probability approximately as reported, but keep uncertainty because the sample size is only moderate.
   * **Non-Native-Language Support:** do not mechanically penalize the case for uncertainty. Treat both calibration and effect estimates as wider intervals, require human review of the alert reason, and monitor this segment separately.

5. **Capacity rule:** do not automatically fill all four slots. Spend a slot only when the marginal direct value is large enough to justify the queue delay imposed on untreated diagnosis-stage cases. Keep one slot reserved until 15:00 unless an eligible case has a very high robust lower-bound benefit or a non-monetary/regulatory obligation that dominates the reserve preference.

6. **Operational ordering:** among candidates that pass the above gates, prioritize:

   * cases in `Diagnosis Started` before cases still in `Triage`, because the intervention window is closing;
   * shorter time to SLA;
   * stronger lower-bound direct benefit;
   * complete and reliable data;
   * fairness constraints, so low-revenue or uncertain-language-support cases are not systematically ignored.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

Repeated alerts should be managed as **alert episodes**, not independent alarms.

A deployable temporal policy:

* **Episode creation:** a case enters an alert episode when adjusted risk and estimated marginal value first become concerning.
* **Suppression window:** subsequent model refreshes within the same episode are suppressed unless there is material new evidence.
* **Cool-down after swarm:** after a swarm, no second swarm is allowed for the same case for the rest of the episode unless a materially new event occurs. Examples include a new customer diagnostic log, a failed validation, a new technical symptom, a major handover, or discovery that the previous diagnosis was based on wrong information.
* **Re-entry logic:** a suppressed case can re-enter the candidate pool if it changes stage, enters `Diagnosis Started`, crosses a tighter SLA band, has repaired missing data, receives new customer information, or changes expected-value tier.
* **Escalation without swarm:** cases can still receive cheaper actions: customer information request, ordinary priority bump, specialist asynchronous review, or queue-owner notification.

I-05 and I-07 should be treated differently.

**I-05** has a third alert but no new event since the first alert. The probability moved only from 0.55 to 0.57. Even after SME upward calibration, this is mainly alert persistence, not new evidence. It should remain on the watchlist, but the repeated alert alone should not consume a swarm slot.

**I-07** also has a third alert, but the new customer diagnostic log is material evidence. The slightly lower `p_now` does not make the alert irrelevant. It should re-enter human review because the log may reveal an actionable blocker. However, the full 45-minute swarm is still hard to justify now because the direct economic value is small and the spillover cost may exceed the benefit. A cheaper diagnostic-log review is the proportionate response unless capacity becomes abundant or the log reveals a severe new issue.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

A value-based rule can improve portfolio economics while still creating unfairness. The safeguards need to be built into the policy, not added later.

First, **lower-value cases can be starved** if value of avoiding breach dominates the score. I would add a fairness floor: a fixed share of daily swarm or senior-review capacity should be available to lower-value but high-risk cases, especially when they have strong estimated effect or customer-impact obligations. The audit should track intervention rates by value band, not only total avoided loss.

Second, **Non-Native-Language Support uncertainty must not become a reason for systematic denial**. I-09 should not be downgraded merely because its validation sample is sparse. The safeguard is to widen uncertainty intervals, require human review of the alert reason, and collect enough segment-specific outcomes to recalibrate. Also track whether language-support cases receive swarms later in the process than other cases, because late intervention would make the policy look ineffective for that group.

Third, **differential calibration across segments can create unequal treatment**. SME cases are underpredicted and Strategic Enterprise cases are overpredicted. Without correction, the policy would under-serve SME and over-serve Strategic Enterprise. The probability adjustment should be visible in the audit log, and decisions should be evaluated using adjusted-risk bands rather than raw `p_now`.

Fourth, **selective intervention changes future labels**. If swarmed cases avoid breaches, the training data may later show them as non-breaches without indicating that intervention caused the outcome. This can teach the model that similar future cases are safe, reducing alerts exactly where intervention was useful. Every prediction record should store whether the case was eligible, selected, swarmed, not swarmed, and why.

Fifth, **spillover harms may fall unevenly on untreated groups**. If specialists are repeatedly pulled away from the same team, language-support queue, SME queue, or Public Sector queue, untreated cases in those groups may breach more often. The policy should monitor spillover exposure by segment, team, queue, language-support status, and value band. If one group repeatedly bears the delay cost, the rule should either cap swarms from that queue or add compensating capacity.

Concrete safeguards:

* maintain segment-level calibration tables and decision thresholds;
* log every suppressed repeat alert and every selected swarm;
* reserve some senior-review capacity for low-value/high-risk cases;
* audit untreated diagnosis-stage cases after every swarm block;
* use random or rotating tie-breaks among similar cases;
* require written override reasons when a lower-value case with high adjusted risk is skipped;
* separately monitor Non-Native-Language Support outcomes and false-alarm burdens.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

The policy should be monitored at five levels.

**Operational monitoring:** track swarm slots used, reserved slots used, time from alert to swarm, stage at intervention, whether the case was still before `Diagnosis Complete`, specialist interruption time, ordinary diagnosis backlog, queue delay added per slot, and whether swarms were clustered in the same queue. The expected 8–20 minutes of aggregate delay per slot should be compared with observed delay after each swarm block.

**Predictive monitoring:** monitor calibration, Brier score, log loss, recall, precision, and alert persistence by segment, stage, time-to-SLA band, language-support status, and data-quality status. SME underprediction and Strategic Enterprise overprediction should be checked in rolling windows. A live missingness dashboard is needed for features like I-06’s queue field.

**Causal-effect monitoring:** estimate the effect of swarms separately from prediction accuracy. Use eligible-but-untreated cases as controls, with matching or doubly robust adjustment for stage, time to SLA, value, segment, complexity, and queue congestion. Where ethically acceptable, use randomized encouragement among borderline candidates: for example, when two cases have similar priority and only one slot is available, randomize the tie-break and log it. Estimate heterogeneous effects by segment and stage.

**Capacity and spillover monitoring:** after every swarm, measure untreated diagnosis-stage cases’ waiting time, breach-rate movement, and queue aging. The policy should estimate a real-time marginal spillover cost, not rely permanently on the 0.01–0.03 assumption. If observed spillover exceeds the direct expected benefit of the next candidate, stop spending slots even if slots remain.

**Fairness monitoring:** report selection rates, swarm timing, repeated-alert suppression, false-alarm burden, breach outcomes, and spillover exposure by segment, value band, language-support status, and team. The audit should distinguish “not selected because ineligible,” “not selected because stale repeat,” “not selected because low marginal value,” and “not selected because reserve protected.”

Recalibration triggers:

* change segment probability adjustments if rolling calibration error exceeds about 0.05 for a sustained window;
* raise the marginal-value requirement when observed spillover is high;
* lower it or release more slots when observed spillover is low and swarm effects are confirmed;
* revise the reserve rule if regulatory arrivals are much more or less frequent than expected;
* retrain or recalibrate the model with intervention indicators, so avoided breaches after swarms are not misread as naturally low-risk cases;
* run policy-induced drift checks comparing the distribution of alerted, selected, treated, and untreated cases over time.

The key governance rule is that the model recommends candidates, but the intervention policy decides actions using stage eligibility, causal-effect uncertainty, queue externalities, repeated-alert evidence, and fairness constraints.
