## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I define P1 narrowly: the binary badge is hidden from humans, while the risk score remains available and backend priority/routing rules remain as under P0. Turning off flag-triggered routing would be a different intervention, closer to P2. P3 means genuinely incremental protected capacity, not reassignment from unflagged cases. P4 has two distinct versions: P4a changes escalation criteria at fixed capacity; P4b retains criteria and adds senior capacity.

Effects below are intervention minus reference; positive cycle-time effects mean slower completion. I use policy total effects, separable component effects, controlled component effects, and interventional indirect analogues. I do **not** use natural direct or indirect effects: their cross-world assumptions are especially implausible with structurally absent stages, post-flag findings, and shared queues. A bare `do(flag=0)` is not organizationally meaningful unless its consequences for display, priority, routing, and score visibility are specified.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TE-P2-cycle | P2 versus P0 | All arrivals in eligible shift-days, including unflagged cases exposed to spillovers | Mean total cycle time | System-level total policy effect | Yes, after specifying discretionary-review criteria | Hidden risk, queue interference, and behavioral substitution by triagers |
| TE-P3-cycle | P3 versus P0 | All cases using the shared review system during the intervention horizon | Mean total cycle time | System-level total policy effect | Yes if capacity is truly incremental | Staffing may be reallocated rather than added; queue state is incompletely observed |
| TRIAGE-L | Assign priority-lane eligibility versus standard-lane eligibility, holding downstream routing policy fixed | Flag-eligible or near-threshold cases | Arrival-to-triage completion | Controlled component effect of priority assignment, not a direct effect of “flag” | Yes under randomized lane assignment and fixed saturation | One case’s lane assignment changes other cases’ waits |
| SEP-DISPLAY | P1 versus P0, with backend lane and routing assignments fixed by design | Flagged cases and reviewers handling them | Cycle time and decision outcome | Separable display/salience effect | Conditionally; only if UI display can be separated from backend use | Staff may reconstruct the flag from the visible score; reviewer behavior is not stable |
| IIE-MR-OCC | Replace the joint manual-review occurrence distribution under P0 by that induced by P2, while retaining reference capacity and downstream rules | Cohorts of eligible flagged cases | Mean cycle time | Interventional indirect analogue through manual-review occurrence | Partly | Removing reviews changes queue load, so occurrence cannot realistically vary without changing waits for others |
| IIE-MR-WAIT | Hold P0 routing occurrence fixed but replace the joint manual-queue-wait distribution by that under P3 | Reviewed cases plus the full routed cohort | Mean cycle time | Interventional indirect analogue through manual-review waiting | Partly | Waiting is a system-level mediator; referral times, queue discipline, and capacity are missing |
| IIE-ESC-OCC | P4a escalation rule versus P0 rule, with manual routing and senior capacity fixed | Manually reviewed cases eligible for escalation | Mean cycle time | Interventional indirect analogue through escalation occurrence | Yes as a policy component, but not identified here | Manual findings are exposure-induced confounders of escalation and outcome |
| IIE-ESC-WAIT | P4b versus P0, retaining escalation criteria and replacing senior-queue conditions through added capacity | Escalated cases and all cases sharing senior capacity | Escalation waiting and total cycle time | Separable capacity effect and waiting-time interventional analogue | Yes if added capacity is specified | Sparse escalations, interference, and unrecorded senior-team availability |
| TE-P2-QUALITY | P2 versus P0 | Eligible flagged cases, with independently adjudicated follow-up | Serious error, compliance failure, calibrated decision accuracy, and final approve/reject decision | Total policy effect on quality and final outcome | Yes once the quality endpoint and horizon are defined | `outcome` records the decision, not whether it was correct; rare harms require larger samples |

Interference occurs because each routed case adds demand to shared review and senior queues, affecting other cases’ waiting times. Thus individual potential outcomes are insufficient unless an exposure mapping or system-level policy is specified. Exposure-induced confounding can arise when the flag changes triage timing or resource assignment, which changes findings and the queue state encountered at triage completion; those variables then affect both later mediators and outcomes. Manual findings are post-flag causes of escalation and decision quality, so adjusting for them can block part of the policy effect while failing to recover a natural direct effect. Reviewer assignment may also respond to the flag, creating treatment-confounder feedback for service time, escalation, and quality.

## 3. Stage decomposition

Values are arithmetic means in hours; bracketed numbers are the number of cases contributing to that stage. Waiting and service times are conditional on the stage occurring. An absent stage remains missing rather than being coded as zero. `residual-to-close` runs from the final observed stage completion—triage, manual review, or escalation—to close. Consequently, mixed-route row components do not necessarily sum to the row’s mean total cycle.

Flagged cases complete triage descriptively much faster: mean arrival-to-triage completion is 0.84 hours, versus 3.43 hours for unflagged cases. Yet their total cycle is 16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All sampled flagged cases enter manual review, compared with 4/10 unflagged cases, and their conditional manual wait is 6.50 versus 1.88 hours. Manual service itself is similar across flags, suggesting that routing and waiting—not faster work execution—are candidate mechanisms. Escalated flagged cases average 21.42 hours, versus 9.15 for flagged non-escalated cases. High load also corresponds to longer flagged manual waits. These are not causal contrasts: flag status is strongly associated with score and complexity, manual and escalation conditioning selects cases using post-triage information, resources differ by case type, and every routed case can alter the shared queues faced by others.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| What is the local effect of crossing the flag threshold? | Reconstruct the exact flag rule and use regression discontinuity or local randomization within narrow score bands, separately by any complexity-specific threshold. Estimate discontinuities in triage time, review occurrence, waits, escalation, and cycle time. | Complexity, threshold version, calendar time, arrival shift, and pre-arrival queue state; do not adjust for downstream mediators when estimating the total bundle effect | Score cannot be precisely manipulated; cases just above and below the threshold are exchangeable; routing actually changes discontinuously | Density and covariate-balance tests around the threshold; check whether review and triage routing show the expected first-stage discontinuities | No routing discontinuity, score bunching, or a cycle-time discontinuity already present in pre-flag variables would weaken a causal flag-policy explanation |
| Are downstream delays caused by queue state rather than service effort? | Reconstruct concurrent active and waiting work at each referral using all event logs, reviewer rosters, capacity, enqueue times, priorities, and shifts. Model cause-specific hazards for service start and completion. | Pre-referral queue length/age, active workload, capacity, time of day, day, resource skills, complexity, and score | Enqueue timestamps and queue discipline are correct; no large unlogged workload | Wait should increase with queue length and fall after exogenous capacity increases, while active service duration changes less | Little association between reconstructed queue state and waiting, or equally large changes in service time, would weaken the bottleneck account |
| What is the total effect of P2? | Randomize shift-days or use a stepped-wedge rollout of discretionary rather than mandatory review, retaining priority triage. Measure all arrivals to capture spillovers. | Randomization strata: shift, weekday, baseline load, and site/team | No anticipation; policy adherence is measurable; enough independent clusters | Verify reduced automatic-review routing, unchanged triage priority, and no pre-intervention trend break | No reduction in review occurrence or queue load, or a rise in serious audited errors, would reverse support for P2 |
| How much is mediated by review occurrence versus review waiting? | Use a factorial or sequential trial of P2 routing and P3 incremental capacity. Fit a multistate g-formula or joint queue simulation for cohort-level interventional effects, with absent stages represented structurally. | Baseline score, complexity, intake information, shift, pre-arrival queue state; time-varying findings only under an explicitly modeled interventional estimand | Consistency of each component, adequate support, correct joint mediator model, and specified interference mapping | P2 should mainly change occurrence; P3 should mainly change start hazards among reviewed cases. Cross-effects reveal induced demand or behavioral adaptation | If P3 does not shorten review waits despite verified added capacity, or P2 changes service intensity rather than occurrence, the proposed decomposition is wrong |
| Is the apparent effect contaminated by pre-policy scheduling or data generation? | Add a negative-control outcome measured before flag display, such as registration-to-score-generation time. Use future non-overlapping shift flag saturation as a negative-control exposure. | Calendar time, intake channel, site, complexity, and arrival shift | Negative controls share confounding structure but cannot be caused by the current badge exposure | Test for a threshold discontinuity in the pre-display outcome and for effects of future demand on already-closed cases | A strong negative-control association suggests time, site, or recording artifacts rather than flag handling |
| How strong must unobserved underlying risk be to explain the cycle gap? | Perform quantitative bias analysis over plausible associations of latent risk with flag, review, escalation, service intensity, and audit outcome. Calibrate using external risk indicators or later adjudication. | Recorded score, complexity, detailed intake features, pre-arrival load, and calendar variables | Sensitivity parameters span credible hidden-risk effects; external adjudication is not itself affected by routing | Compare conclusions across omitted-risk strengths and use residual variation in later adverse outcomes as calibration | If modest hidden-risk confounding removes the review/wait association, the policy mechanism should not be treated as established |
| How large are direct and spillover effects through shared queues? | Use a two-stage randomized saturation design: randomize shift-days to different fractions of P2 or P3 eligibility, then randomize cases within shifts where safe. Estimate effects by own assignment and saturation. | Baseline shift load, capacity, score, complexity, and pre-period queue state | Partial interference within defined shift/queue clusters; saturation is implemented as assigned | Unflagged or control-case waits should vary with the fraction of flagged cases routed or given protected capacity | Large adverse spillovers, especially if aggregate cycle time increases despite treated-case gains, would favor a system-capacity intervention instead |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Preserves the observed fast flagged triage | Continues high review incidence and long manual/senior waits | Baseline; flagged mean is 16.51 hours in this sample | Current safeguards retained, but delays may themselves create risk | Flagged demand may delay all users of shared queues | Descriptive baseline only | Retain as control condition, not as default long-term conclusion |
| P1: hide badge; retain backend actions and visible score | Little intended change because priority remains | Little mechanical change; human escalation behavior may change | Probably small or uncertain | Staff may underreact or simply infer the badge from score | Minimal mechanical effect | Very weak | Useful as a salience experiment, not the primary cycle-time remedy |
| P2: retain priority; remove automatic mandatory review | Should preserve fast triage | Likely lowers review arrivals and may benefit both flag groups | Likely shorter for safely screened eligible cases; magnitude unknown | Highest concern is missed risk, inconsistent discretion, or noncompliance | Potentially beneficial through lower shared demand | Low, but the occurrence pathway is strongly suggested descriptively | Conduct a restricted randomized noninferiority pilot |
| P3: current routing plus incremental protected review capacity | No expected change | Should reduce flagged review waits and may free shared capacity; reallocation would instead harm unflagged cases | Likely shorter while retaining review, especially under high load | Low if review content is unchanged | Positive only if capacity is genuinely additional | Low-to-moderate mechanistic evidence; no causal estimate | Good operational fallback when review is legally mandatory |
| P4: retain review; change escalation criteria or capacity | No expected change | P4a lowers senior demand; P4b shortens the senior queue without changing eligibility | Benefits mainly escalated cases; potentially material given seven-hour waits | P4a may miss severe issues; P4b has lower quality risk but higher cost | Can benefit all senior-queue users; restrictive criteria may shift work back to reviewers | Low and based on only eight escalations | Separate criteria and capacity experiments; do not implement them as one bundled policy |

For a limited pilot, select **P2**.

- **Eligibility:** flagged, medium-complexity cases with scores from the validated threshold to threshold + 0.10, after triage, excluding statutory-review cases and cases with prespecified red flags independent of the badge.
- **Assignment:** randomize shift-days 1:1 to P0 mandatory review or P2 triager-discretionary review; preserve the priority lane and planned staffing. Cluster assignment limits contamination and estimates the queue-level policy effect.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** retain urgent-review slots; pause if the oldest eligible review exceeds its SLA or concurrent unflagged review waits rise more than 20% relative to control shifts.
- **Primary outcomes:** total cycle time and independently audited serious-decision-error rate, with quality tested for noninferiority.
- **Secondary outcomes:** review occurrence, manual and escalation waits, service times, escalation, rework, complaints, final decisions, and spillovers to all arrivals.
- **Minimum process data:** referral/enqueue timestamps, queue priority, capacity rosters, resource assignment, rule version, triage findings, score/version, discretionary rationale, rework, and blinded audit results.
- **Stopping conditions:** any sentinel missed-risk event, quality noninferiority boundary crossed, material compliance breach, sustained adverse spillovers, or evidence that triagers bypass the assignment rule.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** If modest unrecorded underlying risk explains both flag assignment and the need for intensive review or escalation, the dominant mechanism would be case mix rather than avoidable mandatory routing, favoring P0 or P3 over P2.

2. **Queue interference:** If reducing review for pilot-eligible cases induces extra discretionary reviews, rework, or later urgent escalations that worsen aggregate queues, P2 could increase system-wide cycle time despite shortening treated cases.

3. **Decision quality:** If independent audits show that mandatory review prevents a clinically, legally, or financially important number of serious errors, its quality benefit could outweigh the observed completion delay.

4. **Timestamp semantics:** If stage starts are assignment or logging times rather than active-service starts, or local-clock intervals cross unhandled shutdown or daylight-saving periods, the apparent queue/service decomposition could be incorrect.

5. **Positivity and overlap:** If no genuinely comparable flagged and unflagged cases exist within score, complexity, intake-risk, and queue-state strata, local findings cannot support the proposed routing change for the broader flagged population.

6. **Reviewer behavior:** If reviewers infer hidden flags from scores, compensate for P2 by escalating more cases, or reduce effort when the badge is absent, the separable policy effects and the recommended pilot mechanism would change.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** In these 20 cases, flagged cases reach triage completion much sooner than unflagged cases—0.84 versus 3.43 hours—but close much later—16.51 versus 8.17 hours. All flagged cases receive manual review, their conditional manual wait is 6.50 hours, and escalated flagged cases average 21.42 hours total. Manual service duration is nearly identical across flag groups, while high complexity and escalation strongly predict long cycles.

**Plausible mechanism.** The most coherent process explanation is that the policy bundle exchanges roughly 2.6 hours of early triage acceleration for greater review occurrence, long review queues, and, for some cases, a scarce senior queue. This mechanism includes spillovers: flag-triggered demand can delay other cases. Resource specialization and high-load timing may amplify it.

**Unidentified.** The flag is not underlying risk. The sample cannot separate policy handling from hidden severity, complexity, reviewer assignment, time of day, or human findings. Conditioning on review or escalation is selective, and natural mediation effects are not credible under shared queues. Approve/reject is also not a measure of correctness.

**Next evidence.** A restricted, cluster-randomized P2 pilot offers the best next information. Retaining priority triage while randomizing mandatory versus discretionary review directly tests the largest candidate routing mechanism. Independent blinded quality audits, exact enqueue timestamps, capacity records, and measurement of all-arrival spillovers make the pilot informative about both speed and safety rather than assuming that shorter cycle time is automatically preferable.
