# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions
Time origin is the `Submit` activity for every case. The fixed horizon is 72 hours. Observed terminal events are `Resolve` (success), `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`. Right-censoring applies to `open` cases at the observation cutoff. Two estimands are defined: (E1) the probability a case has not reached `Resolve` by 72 hours (1 minus the cumulative incidence function of resolution); (E2) the probability a case still lacks any terminal disposition at 72 hours (probability of remaining in the non-terminal state).  

S03 (resolved at 76 h) contributes an event for E1 after 72 h and is observed past the horizon. S09 (open at 92 h) is right-censored at 72 h under both estimands. S06 (withdrawn at 27 h) contributes a competing event before 72 h under both estimands. S08 (transferred at 68 h) is a competing event before 72 h under E1 and E2 when transfer is treated as terminal; it is censored at 68 h when transfer is treated as non-informative right-censoring.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| withdrawn | 0 | 0 | 2 | none | treat as competing or as censoring |

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased
Restricting to resolved cases drops S09, S10, S11, S15, S24–S26, S29, S31 (open) and S08, S21, S32 (transferred). This induces survivor bias because long-running cases that breach 72 h (S03, S13) or remain open past 72 h are under-represented. Informative censoring arises for S11 (last_event_gap_h = 55 h) and S26 (last_event_gap_h = 72 h), where long inactivity predicts continued non-resolution. Competing risks are ignored when withdrawals (S06, S12, S22, S30) and transfers remove cases from the risk set. Cohort imbalance appears because Cohort B shows earlier AutoCheck usage; dropping open cases distorts the post-policy comparison. Active open cases below 72 h (S15, S24, S29, S31) remain at risk, while those already above 72 h (S09, S11, S25, S26) have already contributed to tail risk; completed-case analysis erases this distinction.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan
Kaplan–Meier ignores competing events. Aalen–Johansen estimates cumulative incidence functions in the presence of competing risks. Cause-specific hazards model the instantaneous rate of each event type conditional on remaining event-free. Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray) directly target cumulative incidence. Restricted mean time up to 72 h summarizes expected time to resolution within the SLA window. Landmarking resets the clock at 72 h for cases still at risk. A multi-state model represents transitions among Submit, Review/Escalate, terminal states, and an absorbing “open at cutoff” state.  

Primary approach: Aalen–Johansen stratified by cohort and priority, because the data contain competing terminal outcomes and right-censored open cases. Secondary approach: cause-specific Cox models with time-varying escalation status, because they allow direct comparison of resolution intensity versus withdrawal/transfer intensities while accommodating the small sample.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards
From prefixes, usable features are: current_stage (last activity before horizon or censoring), has_rework (binary), escalation_ever (time-varying after Escalate appears), inactivity_gap (last_event_gap_h), and path_pattern (first two activities). has_rework and escalation_ever are time-varying after their first occurrence; treating them as baseline covariates creates immortal-time bias. current_stage and inactivity_gap are legitimate time-varying covariates when updated at each activity. Prefix-derived path_pattern is a baseline covariate only when formed from activities observed before any risk time.  

Proposed states: Start  {Screen, AutoCheck}  {Review, Escalate, Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}  absorbing terminal or censored.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis
Three analyses: (1) transfer = competing event, (2) transfer = right-censored, (3) transfer = SLA breach. Withdrawal/rejection treated either as competing events or as censoring. Cohort-policy effect examined by fitting separate Aalen–Johansen curves versus a pooled model with cohort interaction. Qualitative conclusions that could reverse: whether Cohort B shows lower 72-hour breach probability (reverses if transfers are counted as breaches); whether escalation increases resolution intensity (reverses if escalation is treated as baseline rather than time-varying).

# 7. Operational interpretation
Survival outputs supply risk-set sizes and cumulative incidence at 72 h stratified by priority and cohort; these can trigger workload alerts when the proportion of cases still unresolved at 48 h exceeds a calibrated threshold. Workload actions include reassigning P1 cases with long inactivity gaps. SLA governance uses the cumulative incidence curves to set realistic targets rather than claiming causal effects of escalation. Calibration is checked by comparing predicted versus observed incidence within priority strata; fairness is checked by ensuring priority-specific curves do not systematically mis-calibrate for either cohort.

# 9. Limitations and additional data
Exact activity timestamps, external resolution outcomes after transfer, calendar start and cutoff dates, time-varying workload or staffing levels, the assignment mechanism that placed cases into Cohort A versus B, and measured policy-rollout compliance are required for stronger inference. Without them, informative censoring from long gaps and selection effects between cohorts cannot be fully quantified.
