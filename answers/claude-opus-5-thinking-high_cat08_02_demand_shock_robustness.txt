# Robust redesign of the diagnostic-lab sample process

## 1. Stress scenarios

### 1.0 Evidence baseline used to build the scenarios

The two logs cover different window lengths (8 weeks vs 3 weeks), so raw DFG counts are not comparable. Normalising to **cases per week** changes the diagnosis fundamentally:

| Segment | Normal /wk | Peak /wk | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| All cases | 2,300 | 3,300 | 1.43 |
| Routine | 1,403 | 1,386 | **0.99** |
| Urgent | 414 | 957 | **2.31** |
| Complex-panel | 299 | 660 | **2.21** |
| Repeat-after-failure | 184 | 297 | 1.61 |

**Routine volume does not grow at all.** The peak is not a volume shock, it is a *composition* shock: the added work is exactly the work that consumes medical validators, specialist review, and multi-assay machine time. Any redesign built around "more throughput for the routine pipeline" is aimed at a load that is not increasing.

Normalised loop and branch rates (per week, and as branch probabilities):

| Behaviour | Normal /wk | Peak /wk | Ratio | Rate normal → peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-analytical defects (recollection + correction) | 158 | 400 | 2.5 | 7.0% → 12.3% of checks |
| Recollection requests | 104 | 297 | 2.9 | — |
| Recollection ending in Cancel order | 36 | 123 | **3.4** | 35% → 42% of recollections |
| Manual accession correction | 63 | 140 | 2.2 | — |
| Batch wait entries | 95 | 393 | **4.1** | 4.4% → 12.7% of prep output |
| Repeat assay | 143 | 420 | 2.9 | 6.7% → **14.8%** of assay output |
| Medical validation inflow | 530 | 1,367 | 2.6 | 24.5% → **42.3%** of tech-val output |
| Hold for specialist review | 36 | 203 | **5.6** | 1.8% → 7.5% of tech-val output |

Four further pieces of evidence shape the scenarios:

1. **The peak system is not in steady state.** Completions (releases + cancels) / starts = 89.3% normal vs 86.7% peak — about 440 cases per week accumulating, ~1,320 over the 3 weeks. The largest single accumulation sits at the assay step: assay inflow 3,417/wk vs outflow 2,833/wk (584/wk gap), consistent with 98% utilisation and an 18h median queue. Because unfinished cases cannot appear in a completed-case cycle-time statistic, **the published peak medians and p90s are optimistically biased**. The true peak tail is worse than 120h/210h/240h.
2. **Urgent performance is a distribution problem, not an average problem.** Peak Urgent median is 17h against an 18h penalty threshold, so roughly half of ~957 Urgent cases per week incur contractual penalties (vs roughly 10% at normal, where median 11h / p90 19h). Penalty exposure grows about 12x. The control variable must be `P(Urgent > 18h)`, not median Urgent time.
3. **The front end, not the assay, spends the Urgent budget.** Peak median edges Register→Receive 8h + Receive→Pre-analytical 9h + Pre-analytical→Test preparation 13h = 30h before a tube reaches preparation. Since Urgent cases actually complete in ~17h, they are already running an informal expedited path; the pooled performance DFG is dominated by Routine. Two implications: the performance DFG must be re-mined **per case type**, and the addressable Urgent time is front-end waiting (courier intake 7.5h queue, pre-analytical 9.5h) plus medical validation (22h queue) — not assay run time.
4. **Queues convert into rework.** The recollection loop costs 42h normal / 60h peak on the return edge alone, and 42% of peak recollections end in cancellation. Long queues also push tubes past analyte stability limits, which generates *new* recollections. Loops feed back upstream, so congestion is self-amplifying rather than self-limiting.

Two conformance concerns worth flagging: `Repeat assay → Medical validation` (80 normal, 120 peak) bypasses technical validation entirely, which is a control gap that appears to be used as an informal shortcut; and the log is flattened by order, so a Complex-panel order consuming several assays counts as one case. **Assay capacity planning from order counts understates tube-level load, and understates it most exactly when the Complex share rises from 13% to 20%.** The log should be re-mined at tube/assay level before any machine investment decision.

### 1.1 Scenario definitions

| ID | Scenario | Quantified assumptions |
|---|---|---|
| **S1** | **Winter mix shift (sustained)** | The observed peak mix, but sustained 5–6 weeks instead of 3. Urgent 957/wk, Complex 660/wk, Routine flat. WIP starts each week ~440 cases higher than the last. |
| **S2** | **Assay degradation / outage** | M2 offline 48h mid-peak, then returns; alternatively M2 runs with double failure rate for a week. Two-server assay pool becomes one server at S1 arrival rates. |
| **S3** | **Validation scarcity** | One medical validator absent a full week (MV effective capacity −30%) coinciding with the fortnightly Friday specialist absence, at S1 mix. MV offered load moves from 93% to >115%. |
| **S4** | **Courier compression / delay** | The 10:00 and 17:00 route spikes merge into one 16:00 arrival, or a route arrives 6h late. Same daily volume, roughly double arrival burstiness, arrivals landing after day-shift validation coverage. |
| **S5** | **Quality shock (correlated loops)** | A bad reagent lot plus new phlebotomy staff: pre-analytical defect rate 12% → 20% *and* repeat-assay rate 15% → 22% simultaneously. Both feedback loops fire at once at S1 mix. |
| **S6** | **Digital/LIS outage (redesign-induced)** | 12h loss of the automated registration, slot scheduler, and autoverification introduced by this redesign, at S1 mix, with staffing already sized for the automated process. |

S1 is the base layer; S2–S6 are stacked on it. The realistic worst case is S1 + S2 + S3 on the same Friday, which is a plausible correlation rather than an independent coincidence: winter waves increase machine duty cycles and staff sickness at the same time.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The stations rank at peak as: technical validation 101%, assay 98%, pre-analytical 96%, medical validators 93%, accession correction 91%, preparation 89%, courier intake 88%, specialist review 87% (but with a fortnightly absence that pushes it over 100% on those days), registration 85%. Anything at or above ~95% is in the region where waiting time is governed by `ρ/(1−ρ)` and small load changes produce large queue changes. Six stations sit in or near that region, which is why single-point fixes migrate rather than resolve.

### S1 — Winter mix shift

Migration sequence: **courier intake → pre-analytical check → accession correction → batch wait → assay → technical validation → medical validation → specialist review**, with two feedback loops re-entering the front end.

- Arrival bursts hit courier intake (60→88%), which loads pre-analytical (74→96%, queue 1.8→9.5h). Pre-analytical is where defects are *created into the process*: its own failure rate doubles under load (7.0% → 12.3%), because rushed checks catch less and flag more conservatively.
- Accession correction is the sharpest hidden cliff: volume per week only doubles (63→140) but the queue goes 3h→14h because there are **only 2 specialists** — a two-server station has no ability to absorb a doubling. Each waiting correction blocks a case that has already consumed intake and check capacity.
- Preparation pushes 12.7% of output into Batch wait (vs 4.4%), and the batch path costs 6+9=15h normal but 14+22=36h peak. Batching is not degrading because batch rules changed; it is degrading because the downstream assay queue is 18h, so batches wait for machines that are already full.
- Assay is the throughput constraint at 98%, and it is being taxed twice: Repeat assay rises to 14.8% of output, and each repeat consumes a full second slot. 420 repeats/wk is roughly 12% of assay inflow. This is capacity destroyed by the congestion that the capacity shortage caused.
- Technical validation at 101% is formally unstable: its queue has no steady state, it can only be held by overtime and inherited slack. (The peak DFG shows tech-val outflow 8,080 exceeding inflow 7,240, i.e. it is draining a pre-existing backlog while the assay queue builds behind it — a whipsaw, not health.)
- Medical validation is the migration destination nobody staffed for, because in the normal period it looked comfortable at 58%. Mix does the damage: MV takes 24.5% of tech-val output normally but 42.3% at peak, since Complex-panel cases are 3.1x more likely to need it and Complex volume doubled. MV queue 2h→22h, and MV serves **both** Urgent and Complex — the one station where the two protected classes collide and cannot both be prioritised.
- Specialist review has the largest relative explosion of all (1.8%→7.5% of tech-val output, 5.6x per week; queue 5h→36h; Hold→MV edge 18h→48h) on a pool with a known fortnightly gap.

Relief-migration couplings that must be respected:

- Relieve registration (I8) → pre-analytical (96%) receives work faster and earlier; the front-end bottleneck moves one step right and defect creation rises.
- Relieve assay by dedicating M1 (I3) → Routine goes to the failure-prone M2 at high load, repeats rise, and repeats consume assay capacity twice, so the intervention can *increase* effective assay load.
- Relieve accession correction with technical validators (I4) → takes capacity from the only station already above 100%.
- Relieve medical validation (I5) → exposes specialist review as the true Complex-panel tail constraint, and does nothing unless technical validation is running on the same clock to feed it.
- Relieve specialist review (I10) → adds 12h transport and shifts load to medical validation and traceability administration.
- Prioritise Urgent absolutely (I1) → Routine and Complex WIP builds in preparation and assay queues, total WIP rises, and long-held tubes cross stability limits and become recollections, which lands back on intake and pre-analytical.

### S2 — Assay degradation / outage

Migration: **assay (immediately) → preparation benches (blocked upstream) → sample stability → recollection loop → front end → then, on recovery, technical validation and medical validation.**

With M2 out, one machine faces S1 arrivals, so offered load exceeds 100% within hours and the 18h queue grows without bound. Preparation cannot release work, so prepared tubes accumulate on benches (89% utilised, no storage slack). The critical non-obvious step is the third one: prepared samples have analyte stability limits, so a long assay queue does not merely delay cases, it **destroys** them — forcing recollection at 60h round-trip and 42% cancellation, which loads intake and pre-analytical exactly when they are at 96%. A capacity problem at one station becomes a demand problem at another.

If strict Urgent-first (I1) is running, Routine and Complex are starved for the whole outage, so the loss concentrates in the classes with the largest tails (Complex p90 210h, Repeat 240h) and Routine breaches the 72h target in bulk.

Recovery is the second bottleneck event: when M2 returns, a large backlog is released into technical validation, which is already at 101%, and then into medical validation. **A backlog must be metered out, not flushed**, or the downstream whipsaw creates a second failure a day later.

### S3 — Validation scarcity

Migration: **medical validation → the tech-val/MV buffer → release → apparent throughput collapse, with no station utilisation looking alarming.**

MV goes above 115% offered load, so its queue grows monotonically. Upstream technical validation keeps producing 2,413 cases/wk, so WIP piles in the buffer *between* stations. This is the scenario most likely to be missed by a utilisation dashboard: the accumulation is in a queue, not on a resource, and it shows up first as the "% of cases with at least one queue over 12h" indicator (22% → 54%) and as the completion-ratio gap, long before cycle-time statistics move — and completed-case statistics are precisely the ones biased by censoring.

Because MV serves both Urgent (penalty-bearing, 18h) and Complex-panel (multi-assay, 3.1x MV likelihood), the priority conflict cannot be resolved inside the station. If the fortnightly specialist absence coincides, the Hold→MV path (48h at peak) stalls first, then dumps its accumulated batch onto the already-overloaded MV when the specialist returns — a self-inflicted arrival burst.

The only intervention that does not simply move this queue is **reducing MV demand** rather than adding MV supply: 42.3% of tech-val output going to MV is partly mix-driven and partly the result of conservative hold rules.

### S4 — Courier compression / delay

Migration: **courier intake → pre-analytical → defect-detection timing → recollection loop length → batch formation → assay, displaced onto the night shift → repeat assay.**

Same volume, worse burstiness: waiting scales with arrival variability even at unchanged utilisation, so intake (88%) and pre-analytical (96%) queues grow without any extra cases. Two second-order effects matter more than the direct one:

- **Detection timing vs transport timing.** If a defect is found after the last courier run, the recollection request cannot travel until the next day, which is why the return edge is 42h normal / 60h peak — far longer than any processing time in the process. The recollection loop's length is set by courier scheduling, not by lab work.
- **Load displaced onto the night shift.** A compressed 16:00 arrival reaches assay 6–8h later, i.e. overnight. The variant notes state repeats cluster **after night shifts and M2 usage**. So arrival compression converts directly into repeat-assay volume, which consumes double assay capacity, which lengthens the assay queue for the next morning's Urgent cases. Courier scheduling and the repeat rate are causally linked.

### S5 — Quality shock (correlated loops)

Migration: **pre-analytical and assay simultaneously → accession correction → intake (clerk time lost explaining errors) → global WIP explosion.**

This is the most dangerous scenario because the two loops are additive on the *same* constraint set and there is no intervention that relieves both from a single station. Defects at 20% put ~650 cases/wk into recollection or correction on a 2-specialist station; repeats at 22% add ~600 extra assay runs/wk to a 98% resource. Clerk capacity is consumed by physician explanation calls (explicitly noted), so intake degrades even though its nominal volume is unchanged. Recollection cancellations rise further, which makes the cycle-time dashboard *improve* while patient outcomes worsen — cancelled cases leave the denominator.

### S6 — Digital/LIS outage (redesign-induced)

Migration: **registration → pre-analytical → everything, with the flex pool unavailable because it was reallocated on the assumption of automation.**

Once I8 automates registration and clerk hours are redeployed to accession correction, an LIS outage returns manual registration volume to a pool that no longer exists. The note "error rate rises after 9h shifts" then applies: an outage forces overtime, overtime raises the error rate, and errors land on pre-analytical at 96% and accession correction with 2 specialists. Loss of autoverification simultaneously returns suppressed MV demand. This scenario is created by the redesign and is discussed further in Section 6.

## 3. Robust redesign package

Design principles, derived from the migration analysis: (a) attack load that is *self-inflicted* (repeats, recollections, avoidable MV/hold referrals) before attacking capacity, because at ρ≈0.98 removing 6 points of load cuts waiting roughly 4x — better than any staffing increment being discussed; (b) reduce variability, which is free capacity; (c) protect deadlines with *reserved and time-boxed* capacity rather than absolute priority classes, since absolute priority provably starves other classes; (d) keep resources pooled — splitting a 2-server pool at 98% utilisation destroys pooling gains and typically worsens overall waiting; (e) make flexibility available *before* the disruption, since cross-training takes 4 weeks and the peak lasts 3.

### R1. Arrival smoothing with courier-synchronised recollection (I7, extended)

Move from two spike arrivals to 4–6 smaller runs, and add a **guaranteed same-day return leg** so a defect found before a cutoff triggers a recollection that travels on the next run. Add a dedicated STAT lane for Urgent tubes (Register→Receive is 8h at peak, i.e. 44% of an 18h budget, spent before the lab does anything).

*Why robust:* helps S1, S2, S4, S5 simultaneously and depends on no capacity addition. It reduces the variability term that drives queues at intake (88%) and pre-analytical (96%), and it shortens the single longest edge in the process (42/60h) by attacking its true cause, which is transport scheduling.
*Assumptions:* courier cost allows more, smaller runs; physicians can respond to recollection requests within a run cycle.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* smoothing arrivals raises the *average* daily occupancy of pre-analytical (work arrives earlier and more evenly), so pre-analytical becomes the visible constraint sooner — R1 must ship with R4. Thinner runs also mean a single missed run has proportionally more effect, so a minimum tube-count per run and a fallback taxi trigger are needed.

### R2. Time-boxed batching: fixed cadence with a hard wait cap (replaces I2)

Do not increase batch size from 12 to 24. Replace *size-triggered* batching with **cadence-triggered** batching: run every 60–90 minutes or when full, whichever comes first, with a hard cap of 4h in Batch wait and automatic release of an under-full batch when the cap is reached.

*Why robust:* it captures most of I2's setup efficiency without I2's failure mode. Batch wait affects 12.7% of peak prep output and costs 36h at peak; capping it bounds a 36h exposure to ~4h + run time and removes the need for the fragile "priority exceptions" that I2 depends on. It also removes the reason Urgent cases bypass Batch wait (74% normal, 58% peak), which is what fragments machine schedules.
*Assumptions:* setup time is a modest fraction of run time, so under-full runs are affordable; instruments support flexible batch sizes.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* more, smaller runs means more setups, which consumes **assay** capacity at 98% — the cadence must be tuned against measured setup time, and during S2 (one machine) the cadence should lengthen and the cap relax for Routine only. Larger batches genuinely help when assay is *not* the constraint, so cadence is a policy parameter, not a constant.

### R3. Reserved-slot dispatch with deadline-driven sequencing (replaces I1 and I3)

Keep M1 and M2 **pooled**. Instead of dedicating a machine or granting absolute Urgent priority:

- Reserve a rolling fraction of assay and preparation slots (start at ~30–35%, computed from the next 24–48h booked Urgent volume: Urgent is ~28% of peak assay inflow) for Urgent and deadline-at-risk cases.
- **Release unused reserved slots to Routine at a T−30-minute cutoff**, so protection never becomes idleness.
- Sequence within the non-reserved stream by **earliest due date with slack**, not by class label: Urgent 18h, Routine 72h, Complex against an explicit internal target (which today does not exist — Complex p90 is 118h normal and 210h peak with nothing to breach).
- Give **Repeat-after-failure cases deadline inheritance**: they carry the original order's deadline plus a small allowance, with one guaranteed slot per assay cycle. Today's 145h median / 240h p90 shows repeats are being re-queued as new arrivals at the back of the line; they are only 9% of volume, so fixing their tail is cheap, but it is capacity Urgent also wants, hence the cap of one slot per cycle.

*Why robust:* it gives Urgent protection under every scenario while keeping the pooling gains that hard dedication (I3) throws away, and it avoids the Routine/Complex starvation and WIP growth that strict priority (I1) causes. Deadline sequencing is the honest answer to "not all case types can be prioritised simultaneously": it makes the trade-off explicit and continuous rather than binary.
*Assumptions:* a reasonably accurate 24–48h view of booked Urgent volume; a scheduler able to hold and release slots; discipline against manual overrides.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* if reserved fractions are set too high, Routine WIP builds in preparation and Routine p90 breaches 72h — the reserved fraction must be reviewed daily against the mix indicator. Under S6 the scheduler is unavailable and the policy degrades to a manual whiteboard rule (see Section 6).

### R4. Front-end defect prevention: early triage + automated registration + clerk redeployment (I6 + I8, coupled)

Ship I6 and I8 **together**, and redeploy the clerk hours I8 frees into accession correction and pre-analytical rather than removing them.

- I6's early triage (2h decision) shortens time-to-detection, but its larger value is enabling R1's same-day recollection return leg. Detection speed alone saves only ~5h of the 67h peak loop; detection *before the next courier run* saves tens of hours.
- I8 relieves registration (85%) and, critically, frees the clerk time currently lost to explaining errors to physicians — a stated cause of intake capacity loss.
- Redeployment fixes the real constraint that I4 aims at: accession correction is a 2-person station at 91% with a 14h queue. Accession errors are metadata errors, so clerks are the correct skill-adjacent pool — **not** technical validators, who are already above 100%.
- Add deterministic auto-correction rules for the most frequent metadata defect classes so that a share of the 140 corrections/wk never reaches a specialist.

*Why robust:* covers S1, S4, S5 and reduces the load that all other scenarios amplify. Front-end defect prevention is the only lever that reduces work at *every* downstream station simultaneously, because a case that never enters the recollection loop never re-consumes intake, check, or correction capacity.
*Assumptions:* a meaningful share of orders carry complete digital metadata; triage rules can be made specific enough to avoid rejecting analysable samples.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* I8 explicitly exposes pre-analytical (96%), which is why the freed capacity must land there. The serious failure mode is **over-triage**: rejecting borderline samples converts delay into cancellation, and cancellations leave the cycle-time denominator, so the dashboard improves while patients are harmed. Peak recollection-to-cancellation is already 42%. Triage must therefore be governed by a balancing metric (Section 7, indicator L12) and audited on rejection reason codes.

### R5. Reduce medical-validation and specialist-review *demand* before adding supply (new, enabling I5 and I10)

- Introduce rule-based **autoverification** for defined analytes: results within validated ranges, passing delta checks against the patient's history, with no instrument or QC flags, release without human medical validation. Target the gap between 24.5% (normal MV share) and 42.3% (peak MV share) of tech-val output, part of which is conservative rule-driven rather than genuinely clinical.
- Re-derive the **Hold for specialist review** criteria. A 1.8% → 7.5% jump in hold rate is far larger than the mix shift alone justifies (Complex is only 2.4x more likely to hold, and Complex grew 2.2x); this suggests staff are holding defensively under load, on the station with the worst queue (36h) and a known fortnightly absence.
- Only then add I5's evening medical-validation shift — and **synchronise it with evening technical-validation coverage**. MV can only work on what tech val releases (3,420/wk at peak from a station at 101%); an evening MV shift with no evening feeder will idle while its queue sits upstream.

*Why robust:* addresses S1 and S3 directly, and S2's recovery whiplash. Demand reduction cannot be starved by an upstream feeder, unlike a supply increase.
*Assumptions:* accreditation permits autoverification for the chosen analytes (standard practice, but requires validation and documentation); patient history is available for delta checks.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* this trades safety margin for speed and is the intervention most in need of governance — restrict scope to defined analytes, keep a mandatory human review sample, and audit released-without-MV cases for missed findings. It also increases load on Release result (cheap at 2/5h, so acceptable) and, by clearing MV faster, makes **specialist review** the visible Complex-panel constraint, which is what R6 is for.

### R6. Complex-panel diversion valve, routed early (I10, modified)

Outsource Complex-panel overflow, but decide at **order registration or pre-analytical check** based on panel type and predicted specialist need — not after technical validation.

The arithmetic favours diversion at peak: 12h transport plus the partner's shorter specialist queue beats an internal 36h specialist queue plus a 48h Hold→MV edge. But that only holds if the 12h is paid *instead of* the internal wait, not *after* it. Late routing pays both. Diversion also relieves **two** binding constraints at once, since Complex-panel orders consume multiple assays on a 98%-utilised machine pool.

*Assumptions:* partner capacity is genuinely available during a regional winter wave (this is the weakest assumption in the package — waves are regional, so the partner is probably also in peak); result formats and reference ranges are harmonised; transport aligns with R1's courier runs.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* traceability and accreditation complexity, split chain of custody, and a selection effect on metrics — diverting the hard cases makes internal statistics look better without the patient cohort improving. Volume must be capped so in-house Complex competence and the partner relationship both stay viable, and internal plus outsourced turnaround must be reported as one number.

### R7. Gated flex pool, trained ahead of the season (replaces I4)

Cross-train in a gated, bidirectional way, and start **4+ weeks before the forecast wave**, because the training lead time (4 weeks) exceeds the disruption (3 weeks) — flexibility bought reactively arrives after the peak ends.

- Train preparation-bench staff and clerks into accession correction (the 2-person cliff).
- Train technical validators into correction as I4 proposes, but attach a **lending gate**: a validator may be lent only while the technical-validation queue age is under 4h and its offered-load ratio is under 0.9. Since tech val sits at 101% during peak, this gate will usually be *closed* during peak — which is the correct outcome and the reason I4 as written is harmful.
- Train technical validation depth in the *other* direction (the 101% station is the one that needs reinforcement), using the stated 4-week cross-trainability.

*Assumptions:* competence and accreditation permit task-switching; the gate is enforced by the schedule rather than negotiated per shift.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* every lend is a bet that the lender's queue stays short; if the gate is bypassed under pressure, this becomes exactly the I4 failure mode of breaking the most loaded station.

### R8. Deadline-aware WIP caps and metered backlog release

Cap WIP between stages (a CONWIP-style cap on release into preparation and assay) and meter recovery after any outage.

Samples cannot be refused, but the *timing of release into the lab process* can be controlled. Capping WIP keeps queues short enough that expediting still works — in a system with an 18h assay queue and 22h MV queue, "expedite" has almost no meaning, because the case is buried behind hours of committed work. Capped WIP is also what prevents S2's stability-driven conversion of delay into recollection: tubes held unprepared in controlled storage are safer than tubes prepared and queued.

*Assumptions:* adequate pre-preparation storage; deadline data available at release time.
*Where it fails / what it overloads:* a cap moves the queue upstream, where it is cheaper but not free — it will appear as intake/storage pressure, and it must never delay Urgent or short-stability samples, which need cap exemptions.

### Rejected candidates

- **I1 (strict Urgent-first)** — rejected as a standing rule. It starves Routine and Complex, raises total WIP, and, via stability limits, converts starvation into recollections. Superseded by R3. Retained only as a scenario-triggered emergency policy (P2).
- **I2 (batch 12→24)** — rejected as stated. Routine volume is flat per week, so the throughput gain is aimed at a load that is not growing, while the Urgent penalty is real. Superseded by R2.
- **I3 (hard M1/M2 dedication)** — rejected. It splits a pool at 98% utilisation and pushes all Routine volume onto the machine with the higher failure rate, and repeats consume assay capacity twice, so it can increase effective assay load. Replaced by pooled reserved slots (R3) plus risk-based routing and reliability work (P3).
- **I4 (validators → correction)** — rejected as stated; technical validation is above 100%. Reshaped into R7's gated pool.
- **I9 (hold Routine releases for a batch report)** — rejected. Release result costs 2h normal / 5h peak and is not a constraint, so this saves work nowhere it matters while adding waiting to the class already breaching 72h in ~40% of peak cases. Permissible only off-peak.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Each policy names its trigger, its action, its exit condition, and the station it will overload.

**P1 — Mix-shift surge policy (S1).**
*On:* forecast Urgent + Complex share of the next 24h > 45% (normal 31%, peak 49%) for two consecutive days, **or** completion ratio (releases + cancels)/starts < 0.92 for 3 days.
*Action:* raise reserved Urgent slot fraction to 35–40%; open the evening synchronised tech-val + MV shift (R5/I5); lengthen Routine batch cadence and relax the Routine batch-wait cap from 4h to 8h; open the Complex diversion valve (R6) at 15–20% of Complex volume.
*Off:* mix share < 40% and completion ratio > 0.97 for 5 days.
*Overloads:* Routine WIP in preparation, and Release result plus outsourcing administration. Watch Routine p90 against 72h.

**P2 — Assay-scarcity policy (S2).**
*On:* one assay machine unavailable > 4h, **or** single-machine offered load > 95%.
*Action:* temporary near-absolute Urgent-plus-deadline-risk priority at assay (this is where I1 is legitimate — it is a short, bounded emergency, not a standing rule); freeze Routine releases into preparation under R8's cap and hold Routine samples unprepared in controlled storage to protect stability; extend batch cadence to maximise machine efficiency for the surviving machine; push Complex diversion to its cap.
*Off:* machine restored **and** metered recovery complete.
*Recovery sub-policy (mandatory):* release the backlog into technical validation at no more than 110% of its measured sustainable rate, in earliest-due-date order, until the tech-val queue age is under 6h. Do not flush.
*Overloads:* technical validation and MV on recovery; Routine and Complex cycle times during the outage. Cap the policy at 24–36h and escalate to diversion beyond that, because Routine starvation becomes recollection-generating.

**P3 — M2 reliability and risk-based routing (S2, S5).**
*On:* M2 first-pass yield < 95% on any shift, or a repeat cluster after two consecutive night shifts.
*Action:* run a control batch before the first production batch of each shift and at night-shift handover; move calibration/maintenance into the handover window; route high-repeat-risk and Complex assays away from M2 while the flag is on (risk-based routing, not blanket dedication); require repeat-assay root-cause coding.
*Off:* first-pass yield ≥ 97% for 5 shifts.
*Overloads:* M1, and setup time on the pool. Justification: 420 repeats/wk is ~12% of peak assay inflow; halving it returns ~6 points of load at ρ≈0.98, which cuts waiting by roughly 4x — a larger effect than any staffing option under discussion, and it is the only lever that returns capacity for free.

**P4 — Validation-scarcity policy (S3).**
*On:* MV offered-load ratio > 1.0 for two consecutive shifts, **or** oldest Urgent item in the MV queue > 4h, **or** a rostered MV/specialist absence within 48h with a backlog above one day of work.
*Action:* widen autoverification scope to the pre-approved secondary analyte list; convert specialist review to a scheduled twice-daily consult round with a written pre-read from the technical validator (removes the 48h Hold→MV edge caused by ad-hoc availability); pre-book locum or remote MV sessions; hard-route Complex overflow to the partner (R6); explicitly deprioritise Complex-panel MV relative to Urgent MV and communicate the internal Complex target being suspended — this is the trade-off that cannot be avoided.
*Off:* MV offered-load ratio < 0.9 and oldest Urgent MV item < 2h for 3 days.
*Overloads:* Complex-panel tails, and the autoverification audit burden. Never suspend the every-second-Friday specialist gap without a named backup — this is a roster fix, not a queueing fix.

**P5 — Courier-disruption policy (S4).**
*On:* share of daily tubes arriving in the two busiest hours > 45%, **or** a route more than 2h late.
*Action:* pre-notify pre-analytical to pull staff forward into the arrival window (shift the shift, do not add headcount); trigger STAT taxi for Urgent tubes on the delayed route; extend the pre-analytical triage cutoff so defects found late still make the last return leg; when arrivals land after 15:00, defer non-urgent Routine assay to the morning rather than running it on the night shift with M2 — this deliberately trades Routine cycle time for a lower repeat rate.
*Off:* arrival dispersion normal for 3 days.
*Overloads:* Routine cycle time (deliberately), and evening pre-analytical staffing.

**P6 — Quality-shock policy (S5).**
*On:* pre-analytical defect rate > 15% for two shifts, **or** repeat rate > 18%, **or** both above their peak baselines simultaneously.
*Action:* quarantine the suspect reagent lot and stop the affected assay rather than repeating into it (repeating on a bad lot burns the binding constraint twice for nothing); open a same-day phlebotomy feedback loop to the top defect-generating collection sites; move all available flex pool to correction; temporarily raise the internal Urgent target so protection is not diluted across a suddenly larger at-risk population; freeze I8's automated straight-through registration for affected order types so a human checks metadata.
*Off:* both rates back within one point of peak baseline for 3 days.
*Overloads:* correction and intake; cancellation rate is the metric most at risk of quietly rising.

**P7 — Degraded-digital policy (S6).**
*On:* LIS, scheduler, or autoverification unavailable > 30 minutes.
*Action:* fall back to a printed reserved-slot rule (fixed fraction, no dynamic recalculation) and a manual deadline whiteboard; recall redeployed clerks to registration; suspend autoverification and route everything through MV with a pre-agreed reduced scope of what must be validated *today* versus what may wait; cap intake into preparation under R8 to avoid an unmeasurable WIP build; hard limit shifts to 9h despite the backlog, because the error rate rises past that point and errors feed the recollection loop.
*Off:* systems restored plus a reconciliation pass on cases handled manually.
*Overloads:* registration, correction, and MV simultaneously. This policy must be rehearsed quarterly, not written.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

**Caused by the redesign itself:**

1. **Automation and scheduler dependence (S6).** R4's automated registration, R3's slot scheduler, and R5's autoverification create a single digital point of failure, and R4 explicitly removes the manual clerk capacity that used to absorb an outage. The system becomes faster and more brittle at the same time. Mitigation is P7 plus quarterly rehearsal plus a deliberately retained manual competency, but the exposure cannot be eliminated — it is the price of the throughput.
2. **Autoverification erodes the safety margin.** R5 reduces MV demand by removing human review from cases that currently receive it. The failure mode is silent: a missed abnormal result does not appear in any cycle-time or queue metric. This requires a permanent random-sample human audit and a hard rule that scope expansion is a validated change, never a peak-time expedient.
3. **Over-triage converts delay into cancellation.** R4's 2h rejection gate and P6's aggressive posture both improve every cycle-time statistic by removing cases from the denominator. Peak cancellation-after-recollection is already 42%; the redesign could push it higher while the dashboard turns green. This is the most dangerous metric-gaming path in the package, which is why the cancellation rate must be a first-class monitored indicator rather than a report footnote.
4. **Outsourcing hollows out internal capability and metrics.** R6 systematically diverts the hardest Complex-panel work, so internal statistics improve through case selection, internal Complex competence atrophies, and the lab becomes dependent on a partner during exactly the season the partner is also stressed.
5. **Reserved capacity is a standing tax.** R3 protects Urgent by withholding slots; if the mix forecast is wrong in the low direction, that protection is paid for in Routine p90 against a 72h target that is already breached ~40% of the time at peak.
6. **Gate discipline is the weakest link in R7.** Lending rules that depend on a queue threshold get renegotiated under pressure by exactly the supervisors who are under pressure. A bypassed gate reproduces the I4 failure mode at the 101% station.

**Not fixed by the redesign:**

7. **The 42–60h recollection round trip is only partly a lab problem.** It depends on physician and collection-site responsiveness. R1 shortens the transport component; nothing here fixes a physician who does not act for a day.
8. **Technical validation at 101% remains the structural constraint.** Every intervention here reduces its load or defers work around it, but none of them creates technical-validation capacity, and R7's pre-season training is the only supply-side answer — with a lead time longer than the disruption.
9. **Specialist review's fortnightly single-point absence is a roster defect.** Queueing policy cannot fix a station that has zero coverage every second Friday; only a named backup can.
10. **Correlated shocks defeat scenario-by-scenario policies.** P1–P7 were designed one at a time, but S1+S2+S3 on the same Friday is plausible, not exotic, and several policies then conflict (P2 wants Urgent-first at assay, P4 wants Complex diverted, R8 wants releases capped). A single documented precedence order and a named incident commander are required, or the policies will be resolved ad hoc by whoever is loudest.
11. **The measurement base is still weak.** The log is flattened by order, so tube-level assay load — the load that matters most when the Complex share goes 13% → 20% — is unmeasured. Performance edges are pooled across case types, so no per-variant baseline exists for Urgent. Peak completed-case statistics are censored by ~1,320 unfinished cases, so all peak targets are set against optimistic numbers. Until the log is re-mined at tube level, per variant, with a conformance check on the `Repeat assay → Medical validation` bypass, every threshold in Section 7 is a first estimate to be recalibrated.
12. **Sustained peaks are untested territory.** All figures come from a 3-week peak. S1 assumes 5–6 weeks, which means starting each week with more inherited WIP; there is no evidence in either log about how the process behaves after a month above 95% utilisation, and overtime-based holding (which is what keeps technical validation alive at 101%) degrades as staff fatigue and the stated post-9h error rate compounds.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Leading indicators are reviewed each morning and at each shift handover during peak. They are load, queue-age, and quality measures that move **before** cycle time. Lagging outcomes are reviewed weekly and are used for accountability, never for daily steering — they are censored and they arrive too late to act on.

### Leading indicators (daily during peak)

| # | Indicator | Green | Amber | Red | Why it leads |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Urgent + Complex share of next-24h registered orders | < 40% | 40–48% | > 48% | The peak was 49%; mix predicts MV, specialist, and assay load a day ahead. Triggers P1 |
| L2 | Arrival dispersion: share of daily tubes in the 2 busiest hours | < 35% | 35–45% | > 45% | Variability, not volume, drives the intake and pre-analytical queues. Triggers P5 |
| L3 | Pre-analytical defect rate per shift | < 8% | 8–12% | > 12% | Normal 7.0%, peak 12.3%; this is the source term for both loops. Triggers P6 |
| L4 | Accession-correction queue age (oldest waiting case) | < 3h | 3–6h | > 6h | A 2-person station; normal median 3h, peak 14h. Earliest visible sign of a front-end cliff |
| L5 | Recollection request-to-resample turnaround | < 24h | 24–48h | > 48h | Peak is 60h; the single longest edge in the process and the best test of R1 |
| L6 | Batch-wait cap breaches (% of Routine batches over 4h) | < 2% | 2–8% | > 8% | Direct test of R2; a rising figure means assay is backing up into preparation |
| L7 | Assay first-pass yield, by machine and by shift (M2 post-night especially) | ≥ 97% | 94–97% | < 94% | Peak repeat rate 14.8%; repeats consume the binding constraint twice. Triggers P3 |
| L8 | Reserved-slot spillover: % of Urgent tubes missing their reserved slot | < 5% | 5–15% | > 15% | Tests R3 before penalties are incurred |
| L9 | Technical-validation offered-load ratio (arrivals ÷ served, rolling 3 shifts) | < 0.90 | 0.90–0.98 | ≥ 1.00 | Peak is 1.01, i.e. no steady state. Also the R7 lending gate |
| L10 | MV queue oldest-item age, split Urgent / Complex | < 2h / < 8h | 2–4h / 8–16h | > 4h / > 16h | Peak median queue 22h. Oldest-item age exposes the tech-val→MV buffer that station utilisation hides. Triggers P4 |
| L11 | Specialist hold arrivals per day vs roster coverage for the next 48h | < 15/day, covered | 15–25/day | > 25/day or an uncovered absence with > 1 day backlog | Hold volume rose 5.6x per week; the fortnightly gap is foreseeable |
| L12 | **Cancellation-after-recollection rate** (balancing metric) | < 35% | 35–45% | > 45% | Peak 42%. Guards against R4/P6 improving cycle time by discarding cases |
| L13 | WIP by stage vs CONWIP cap, plus deadline-risk counts (Urgent > 8h elapsed not yet at MV; Routine > 48h not yet at assay) | within cap | 90–100% of cap | over cap | The 440-cases/week accumulation is invisible in any completed-case metric |
| L14 | Samples in queue approaching analyte stability limit | 0 | 1–10 | > 10 | The mechanism that turns delay into rework in S2 and S4 |
| L15 | Completion ratio: (releases + cancels) ÷ starts, rolling 7 days | > 0.97 | 0.92–0.97 | < 0.92 | Normal 0.89 over 8 weeks, peak 0.87 over 3 weeks — the cleanest single test of whether the process is keeping up |
| L16 | Overtime hours and shifts exceeding 9h | 0 | any | sustained | The stated error-rate threshold; overtime today is defects tomorrow |

Escalation rule: any single red, or three ambers on consecutive days, activates the matching policy from Section 4 and is reviewed at the daily stand-up with an explicit named owner and an off-trigger date.

### Lagging outcome metrics (weekly, for accountability and recalibration)

- **`P(Urgent > 18h)`** and the resulting contractual penalty count and cost — the primary commercial outcome, and the reason median Urgent time is the wrong target (peak median 17h sits 1h under the threshold, implying roughly half of ~957 Urgent cases per week breach).
- Routine p90 vs the 72h target (peak 120h); Complex-panel p90 (peak 210h) against a newly defined internal target; Repeat-after-failure p90 (peak 240h) against the inherited-deadline rule from R3.
- Overall rework rate (normal 7.5%, peak 11.8%), split into recollection, correction, and repeat-assay components with root-cause codes.
- Percentage of cases with at least one queue over 12h (normal 22%, peak 54%) — the best existing single summary of queueing health.
- Diverted Complex-panel volume and end-to-end turnaround including the 12h transport, reported jointly with in-house Complex turnaround so R6 cannot flatter the internal figure.
- Autoverification audit: cases released without medical validation that the audit judges should have been held.
- Cancellations as a share of starts, and the reason mix, to confirm that cycle-time gains are real rather than denominator effects.

All lagging metrics must be reported **including cases still in progress** (as censored observations or with an explicit in-flight count) and normalised per week. The single largest analytical error available to this team is comparing an 8-week log with a 3-week log at face value, which is what makes the peak look like a volume problem instead of the mix problem it is.
