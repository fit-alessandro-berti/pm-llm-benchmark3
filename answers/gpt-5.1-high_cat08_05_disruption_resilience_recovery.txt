### 1. Disruption timeline

**T0 – Baseline (Day -30 to Day -1)**  
Operations are stable and largely conformant to the normative model (fitness 0.96; 91% fitting traces). Throughput is high (42,000 cases over 21 days) with differentiated but predictable service levels: median cycle time 8h for Hospital-critical, 23h for Pharmacy-standard, 36h for Wholesale-bulk; p90s mostly within 3 days even for bulk. Control quality is strong: traceability exceptions 0.8%, cold‑chain temperature documentation missing 0.4%, controlled‑substance dual‑control missing 0.2%, and rework at 3.5% with a 2.1% cancellation rate. The baseline DFG shows a disciplined path through `Receive order -> Validate authorization -> Inventory reservation -> Pick -> Pack -> Quality/release check -> Dispatch -> Proof of delivery -> Invoice close`, with structured loops for compliance hold, backorder wait, and repack/recheck.

**T1 – Incident onset (Day 0 morning)**  
The warehouse management system becomes unavailable after a cyber incident, effectively disabling standard inventory reservation, scanning, and the usual order-to-dispatch orchestration. This marks a sharp transition from high‑fit, automation‑driven flows to ad‑hoc manual work; however, the detailed metrics for this very short window are incorporated into the T2 “manual workaround” period. Risk posture flips from “controlled and predictable” to “uncertain,” with a high likelihood of both service degradation and control gaps unless mitigations are put in place.

**T2 – Manual workaround phase (Day 0 afternoon to Day 2)**  
Teams switch to manual variants centered on `Emergency triage`, `Manual authorization check`, `Manual inventory snapshot check`, `Manual pick list`, `Temporary release control`, `Dispatch without scan`, `Phone proof of delivery`, and `Spreadsheet invoice hold`. Hospital-critical orders are routed through a `Hospital-critical fast lane` and protected by deferring noncritical orders to a `Backlog queue`. Operationally, throughput is constrained (5,600 cases) and cycle times degrade sharply, especially for Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk (medians 54h and 96h, p90s up to 118h and 180h); nevertheless Hospital-critical median remains 10h with p90 at 21h and fill rate 91%, showing strong prioritization of critical care. Risk posture, however, is clearly unsafe from a compliance and traceability perspective: traceability exceptions spike to 9.5%, missing cold‑chain documentation to 4.8%, controlled‑substance dual‑control missing to 2.9%, rework to 12.5%, and cancellations to 7.8%. Conformance fitness drops to 0.71 with only 44% fitting traces; move‑on‑log deviations include `Skip system authorization check`, `Dispatch without scan`, `Phone proof of delivery`, and `Spreadsheet invoice hold`, and move‑on‑model deviations include missing quality checks, scans, proof of delivery, and temperature records. The risk posture is “mission‑protective but control‑fragile”: Hospital-critical service is preserved, but at the cost of substantial hidden and overt nonconformance.

**T3 – Partial recovery (Day 3 to Day 6)**  
The order intake system returns, enabling more standard upstream processing, but inventory synchronization and scanning remain unreliable. The DFG begins to resemble the baseline again for earlier steps (`Receive order -> Validate authorization -> Inventory reservation -> Pick -> Pack -> Quality/release check`), while some manual remnants and reconciliation behaviors persist. Operational metrics improve relative to T2 but remain degraded vs baseline: 11,400 cases processed; Hospital-critical median 9h (p90 18h), Pharmacy-standard median 38h (p90 90h), Wholesale-bulk median 84h (p90 160h). Control metrics are still worse than baseline but better than T2: traceability exceptions at 5.4%, missing cold‑chain documentation 2.3%, controlled‑substance dual‑control missing 1.4%, rework 9.0%, cancellations 5.2%. Conformance fitness rises to 0.82 with 59% fitting traces. Risk posture is “improving but still exposed”: more orders flow through the normal model, but partial automation creates a hybrid environment where both manual and system records can be inconsistent, and where some T2 shortcuts may linger.

**T4 – Stabilization and tail remediation (Day 7 to Day 21)**  
Normal systems mostly return; the DFG largely reverts to baseline paths, but with new work for clearing the backlog and repairing evidence: `Backlog prioritization`, `Reconcile manual reservation`, `Proof-of-delivery remediation`, and `Audit remediation`. Throughput recovers (35,000 cases over 15 days) and Hospital-critical median cycle time returns to 8h; Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk medians (28h and 58h) and p90s (74h and 120h) are still somewhat slower than baseline, reflecting residual backlog and reconciliation workloads. Control metrics remain worse than baseline but significantly better than T2/T3: traceability exceptions 2.2%, cold‑chain documentation missing 0.9%, controlled‑substance dual‑control missing 0.5%, rework 5.8%, cancellations 3.0%. Conformance fitness is 0.90 with 76% fitting traces—close to but not fully at baseline. The key risk posture here is “operationally recovered but compliance‑burdened”: routine KPIs (cycle time, fill rates) look nearly normal, yet an `Audit remediation` queue persists and the temporal profile shows a long tail from `Invoice close -> Audit remediation`, indicating substantial after‑the‑fact reconstruction work and potential residual regulatory exposure.

### 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this case, **resilience** means the ability to maintain or quickly restore critical outcomes (patient safety, continuity of supply to hospitals) **while retaining minimum acceptable controls and traceability**, even if those controls are executed differently (e.g., manually rather than by system). **Concealed noncompliance** occurs when throughput or service levels are preserved by quietly removing or bypassing critical controls, producing metrics that look acceptable while hiding regulatory, safety, or recall risks and creating large after‑the‑fact remediation tails.

Patterns such as **B (Manual authorization check)**, **E (Manual pick list, when supported by reconstruction and reconciliation)**, **F (Temporary release control)**, **I (Defer noncritical order)**, **J (Backlog prioritization)**, and **K (Reconcile manual reservation)** are primarily **resilience patterns**. They attempt to preserve the intent of baseline controls (authorization, QA, risk‑based prioritization, data integrity) using alternative means and make the associated work visible in the DFG. These adaptations explain why Hospital-critical service remained relatively strong despite the cyber incident, and why conformance fitness and exception rates improved from T2 to T4.

By contrast, **C (Skip system authorization check)** and **G (Dispatch without scan)** are mostly **risky shortcuts**. They remove safeguards instead of substituting them, generate the largest traceability gaps and authorization uncertainties, drive up exception and rework rates, and leave some risks fundamentally unrecoverable—no amount of audit remediation can recreate missing real‑time authorization or scan events with full evidential strength. Certain uses of **H (Phone proof of delivery)** and late‑phase reliance on **D (Manual inventory snapshot check)** can also cross into concealed noncompliance, especially for controlled or cold‑chain products where phone confirmation and stale inventory data are unlikely to satisfy regulators.

Some patterns are **ambiguous and depend on risk appetite and governance**. **A (Hospital-critical fast lane)** is clearly mission‑aligned resilience if it retains manual authorization and QA for high‑risk products; it becomes concealed noncompliance when it routinely bypasses authorization because “the hospital is trusted.” **D (Manual inventory snapshot check)** is reasonable and resilient in the first hours after T1 but becomes risky as inventory diverges from the snapshot. **H (Phone proof of delivery)** is a pragmatic resilience measure for low‑risk ambient bulk shipments but an inadequate control for controlled substances. **L (Audit remediation)** is a valuable backstop, yet if management accepts large, persistent audit tails as normal, it can mask chronic control failures and incentivize shortcuts during incidents. The core distinction is whether an adaptation preserves the control objective in an alternate form (resilience) or sacrifices it while appearing operationally successful (concealed noncompliance).

### 4. Recovery process model

Below is a structured recovery model for future disruptions, expressed in phases with key decision points and flows.

**4.1 Trigger conditions and activation**

- **Trigger 1 – System outage:**  
  - WMS or scanning subsystem unavailable or unreliable for > X minutes (e.g., 30 minutes) **or** detected cyber compromise of core order/warehouse systems.  
  - Automated health checks detect drop in conformance fitness below a threshold (e.g., <0.9) or significant spike in missing scan/temperature events.
- **Trigger 2 – Control degradation without full outage:**  
  - Evidence that specific control events (authorization checks, temperature logging, dual‑control) are intermittently failing or delayed beyond acceptable windows, even if the system is nominally online.
- **Activation step:**  
  - Incident commander declares **Emergency Operating Mode (EOM)**, logs start time, affected systems, and expected scope.  
  - Pre‑defined emergency playbook is activated, including triage rules, manual control kits, and communication to all teams and key customers.

**4.2 Triage rules (customer and product risk)**

Define a 2×2 risk matrix combining **customer criticality** and **product risk**:

- **Customer tiers:**  
  - Tier C1 – Hospital-critical  
  - Tier C2 – Pharmacy-standard  
  - Tier C3 – Wholesale-bulk
- **Product tiers:**  
  - Tier P1 – Controlled-substance / high‑security  
  - Tier P2 – Cold-chain / temperature‑sensitive  
  - Tier P3 – Recall‑sensitive (on active watch lists)  
  - Tier P4 – Ambient, standard

From this, derive **priority bands**:

- **Band 1 (Highest):** C1+P1/P2/P3 (hospital + controlled/cold/recall)  
- **Band 2:** C1+P4 and C2+P1/P2/P3  
- **Band 3:** C2+P4 and C3+P1/P2/P3  
- **Band 4 (Lowest):** C3+P4

Rules in EOM:

- Band 1 orders **must** be processed first and continuously unless a specific control for that product class is impossible to execute even manually.  
- Bands 2–4 may be **deferred** in a controlled way, with explicit logging into a `Backlog queue` including reason and timestamp.  
- Any deviation from these rules (e.g., urgent public-health bulk shipment) requires a documented exception by the incident commander.

**4.3 Minimum controls by product/customer risk**

Design minimum control sets for each priority band, ensuring that even in EOM:

- **Common to all bands (non‑negotiable):**  
  - Some form of **authorization verification** (system or manual) before release; “skip authorization” is not allowed.  
  - A **unique shipment identifier** (label/barcode or manual identifier) linking order, contents, and recipient.  
  - At least one recorded **dispatch event** with responsible person and time.

- **Band 1 (C1+P1/P2/P3):**  
  - Manual authorization check by trained compliance/pharmacy staff; dual review for new or complex cases.  
  - Manual inventory verification beyond snapshots for critical SKUs (physical check, lot and expiry).  
  - Mandatory temperature checks with manual logs for cold‑chain; no shipment without documented reading.  
  - Full dual‑control documentation for controlled‑substance handling and release.  
  - No use of “Dispatch without scan”; if scanners are down, use serialized paper chain‑of‑custody logs with signatures at each transfer point.

- **Band 2:**  
  - Manual authorization check for all controlled/cold/recall‑sensitive products; system check if available.  
  - Accept use of fresh inventory snapshots within strict time/volume limits, plus random spot physical counts.  
  - Temperature documentation required for cold‑chain; phone POD allowed only as secondary evidence, backed by pre‑printed POD forms.

- **Bands 3–4:**  
  - Allow more extensive use of `Defer noncritical order` and backlog creation.  
  - For low‑risk ambient products, manual authorization may rely on pre‑approved customer lists plus periodic sample review by compliance.  
  - Dispatch may proceed with minimal manual POD (signature on delivery note) if scanners are down, but not with zero evidence.

These rules explicitly **disallow** high‑risk shortcuts like generic `Skip system authorization check` and unlogged `Dispatch without scan` for all but the lowest‑risk segments—and even there require basic manual evidence.

**4.4 Manual evidence capture**

Prepare a **manual control kit** for EOM, including:

- Pre‑printed **manual order and pick forms** with fields for: order ID, customer, segment, product, lot/batch, expiry, quantity, temperature (where applicable), picker ID, packer ID, checker ID, timestamps, and authorization reference (system ticket or manual approval ID).  
- Serialized **emergency labels** (barcodes or alphanumeric codes) to attach to each shipment, so that later scans or entries can link back to manual records.  
- **Manual POD forms** for couriers and customers, with structured sections for high‑risk products (e.g., identity verification for controlled substances, temperature at receipt for cold‑chain).  
- A simple **electronic or paper log** for phone confirmations, with mandatory fields and scripts to reduce ambiguity.

Process fragment in EOM:

- `Receive order -> Emergency triage -> (Band 1/2/3/4) -> Manual authorization check (if required) -> Manual inventory verification/snapshot check -> Manual pick list -> Manual pack -> Temporary release control -> Dispatch with manual identifier -> Manual/phone proof of delivery -> Spreadsheet/temporary invoice hold`

Every order entering the manual path is tagged for later `Reconcile manual reservation` and `Audit remediation` if any control event is missing in the final system record.

**4.5 Backlog prioritization**

Upon partial or full system recovery:

- Instantiate a **Backlog dashboard** showing counts and **age distribution** of orders in each band and queue (deferred orders, partial picks, pending remediation).  
- Apply formal **Backlog prioritization** rules:  
  - Clear Band 1 clinical risks first; then aged orders in Bands 2–3; finally low‑criticality Band 4.  
  - Apply maximum wait‑time thresholds per tier (e.g., 24h for Band 1, 72h for Band 2, etc.).  
- Record in the system which orders were `Deferred noncritical order -> Backlog queue` during EOM, and surface this in customer communication and SLA reporting.  
- Avoid fairness issues by publishing the policy internally and periodically reviewing throughput and cancellation by customer group.

**4.6 Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow**

After operational recovery, run a distinct **Recovery & Remediation flow**:

- **Reconciliation phase:**  
  - `Inventory reservation -> Reconcile manual reservation -> Pick/Cancel`  
  - Cross‑check manual pick and dispatch logs against system records; adjust inventory, correct reservations, and flag discrepancies for investigation.  
  - Require dual review for high‑value or controlled‑substance discrepancies.

- **Audit-remediation phase:**  
  - `Invoice close -> Audit remediation -> END`  
  - For any case lacking mandatory events (authorization, quality check, temperature, POD, scan), attempt to reconstruct from manual logs, courier records, and phone confirmations.  
  - Classify each case as: fully remediated; partially remediated (documented limitation); or non‑remediable (requires incident report and possible regulatory notification).  
  - Track **age and volume** of the audit‑remediation queue as a managed program with a planned end date, not as open‑ended background work.

This model ensures that **emergency adaptations are explicit, controlled, and traceable**, and that the organization deliberately returns to baseline rather than normalizing shortcuts.

### 5. Resilience indicators

**5.1 Leading indicators (real‑time or near real‑time)**

- **Throughput:**  
  - Orders processed per hour/day by segment (Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, Wholesale-bulk) compared to baseline; alert when Hospital-critical throughput drops below a defined percentage of baseline or when low‑criticality throughput remains high while hospitals suffer.  
  - Percentage of orders flowing through emergency manual paths vs standard paths.

- **Service protection:**  
  - **Hospital-critical fill rate** vs Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk; monitor the **gap** to ensure hospitals are protected without completely abandoning others.  
  - Real‑time view of median and p90 cycle times by segment during EOM.

- **Traceability and control completeness:**  
  - Percentage of dispatched orders with complete **minimum data sets** (authorization, pick, pack, QA/release, dispatch, POD, temperature where applicable) recorded either in system or manual logs within X hours of dispatch.  
  - Rate of **move‑on‑model deviations** (missing scans, missing temperature, missing quality checks) per 100 orders, by product risk tier.  
  - Share of shipments using high‑risk adaptations (e.g., dispatch without scan, phone POD) as a proportion of total, with thresholds that trigger escalation.

- **Backlog age and volume:**  
  - Number of orders in backlog queues by band, plus p90 and maximum **backlog age**; alerts when thresholds are exceeded (e.g., more than Y orders in Band 1 backlog or any Band 1 order older than 24h).  
  - Ratio of deferred to newly received orders over time, indicating whether the backlog is shrinking or growing.

- **Rework and exception inflow:**  
  - Rate of new **rework/correction cases** opened per day and per 1,000 orders; significant spikes signal that manual controls are failing.  
  - Inflow to `Audit remediation` and `Proof-of-delivery remediation` queues during and immediately after the incident.

- **Fairness across customer groups:**  
  - Relative changes in cycle times, fill rates, cancellations, and backlog age across Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk segments.  
  - Indicator of whether backlog prioritization is systematically disadvantaging a customer group beyond agreed triage policy.

**5.2 Lagging indicators (post‑incident and periodic review)**

- **Traceability exceptions and control failures:**  
  - Overall traceability exception rate, missing cold‑chain documentation, and missing controlled‑substance dual‑control compared against baseline (0.8%, 0.4%, 0.2%) for T2–T4.  
  - Number and severity of cases that remained partially or non‑remediable after `Audit remediation`.

- **Recovery and remediation tail:**  
  - Time from operational stabilization to closure of the `Audit remediation` queue; p90 age of items in this queue; percentage of orders requiring remediation in each incident.  
  - Conformance fitness progression (0.71 → 0.82 → 0.90…) after each incident as a measure of how quickly the process returns to normative behavior.

- **Service outcomes and fairness:**  
  - Incident‑period cancellation rates and customer complaints by segment, especially for deferred Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk customers.  
  - Post‑incident satisfaction or complaint trends among hospitals and pharmacies.

- **Learning and preparedness:**  
  - Implementation status of corrective and preventive actions from post‑incident reviews.  
  - Frequency and results of disruption drills testing the emergency playbook and manual controls.

Together, these indicators help distinguish **healthy resilience** (controlled deviations with fast recovery and bounded audit tails) from **unhealthy, concealed noncompliance** (high throughput with chronic traceability gaps and long remediation tails).

### 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

Below are prioritized recommendations with indicative effort, expected benefit, and residual risk.

- **1. Formalize an emergency operating playbook (EOM) with explicit triage and control rules.**  
  - **Effort:** Medium – requires cross‑functional design, documentation, and training.  
  - **Benefit:** High – reduces improvisation under stress, clarifies when to invoke fast lanes, deferrals, and manual checks, and lowers the chance of high‑risk shortcuts like C and G.  
  - **Residual risk:** Some scenarios will still require judgment calls, and novel cyber failure modes may not match the playbook perfectly; periodic review is needed.

- **2. Build and maintain manual control kits and standardized evidence forms.**  
  - **Effort:** Medium – design and pre‑position forms, labels, and procedures; integrate with existing quality systems.  
  - **Benefit:** High – turns manual adaptations (B, D, E, H, F) into structured, auditable processes; reduces rework and improves the success of reconciliation and audit remediation.  
  - **Residual risk:** Manual work is inherently error‑prone; staff turnover and fatigue may still cause gaps, so periodic drills and audits of manual records are necessary.

- **3. Technically harden critical controls with offline/edge capabilities.**  
  - **Effort:** High – may require investment in offline‑capable scanners, local data caches for inventory and authorization, and resilient network segments.  
  - **Benefit:** Very high – reduces the need for high‑risk workarounds like `Dispatch without scan` and stale inventory snapshots; keeps key events (scan, temperature, POD) captureable even during central system outages.  
  - **Residual risk:** Extreme or prolonged cyber incidents may still compromise local capabilities; residual dependence on manual processes should be assumed and planned for.

- **4. Prohibit or strictly constrain “skip authorization” and “dispatch without scan” behaviors.**  
  - **Effort:** Low to Medium – policy updates, system configuration changes (e.g., blocking these paths), and training.  
  - **Benefit:** High – directly addresses the largest sources of concealed noncompliance and traceability gaps; simplifies audit remediation.  
  - **Residual risk:** In rare life‑or‑death situations, leadership may still override controls; such overrides must be logged, reviewed, and minimized.

- **5. Institutionalize backlog prioritization and fairness monitoring.**  
  - **Effort:** Medium – implement prioritization logic, dashboards, and governance.  
  - **Benefit:** Medium to High – ensures that deferred Pharmacy-standard and Wholesale-bulk customers are treated systematically rather than arbitrarily, while keeping hospitals protected; helps avoid long‑lived backlogs and uneven service recovery.  
  - **Residual risk:** Some customers will still experience degraded service in severe incidents; fairness perceptions must be managed through communication and transparent metrics.

- **6. Resource and time‑box reconciliation and audit-remediation programs.**  
  - **Effort:** Medium – dedicated teams and planning for post‑incident clean‑up, with clear SLAs.  
  - **Benefit:** High – prevents chronic audit tails, reduces rework rates in subsequent periods, and demonstrates to regulators that the organization treats nonconformance seriously.  
  - **Residual risk:** Some gaps (e.g., missing real‑time temperature readings) may remain irrecoverable even with best efforts; these require risk acceptance and possible regulatory engagement.

- **7. Conduct regular disruption exercises and scenario‑based training.**  
  - **Effort:** Medium – periodic drills, simulations, and tabletop exercises.  
  - **Benefit:** Medium – improves staff familiarity with EOM procedures, exposes weaknesses in manual controls and indicators, and strengthens cross‑functional coordination.  
  - **Residual risk:** Exercises cannot perfectly mirror real cyber events; complacency can develop if drills are too predictable or infrequent.

---

### 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should **accept** that during severe cyber incidents some degradation in service for noncritical segments and some increase in rework and remediation workload are necessary trade‑offs to protect Hospital-critical supply and maintain core patient‑safety controls. It should **not accept** adaptations that remove essential authorization, traceability, or temperature/security controls—particularly C (skip authorization) and G (dispatch without scan) in their current form—nor the normalization of large, long‑lived audit‑remediation tails as a routine cost of doing business. Instead, leadership should commit to codifying resilient emergency modes that preserve minimum controls, investing in technical and procedural safeguards, and monitoring resilience indicators so that future deviations are deliberate, bounded, and recoverable. Evidence that would change this recommendation includes robust proof that new offline/edge capabilities can preserve controls even under cyber stress, regulatory feedback on acceptable emergency practices, and data from drills or real incidents showing materially lower traceability exceptions and shorter remediation tails under the redesigned model.
