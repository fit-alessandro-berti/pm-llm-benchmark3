## 1. Conformance semantics

I distinguish findings by the following classes:

- **Forbidden behavior**: A relation explicitly marked `forbidden` is closed-world. If observed and not explained by a data artifact, it is noncompliant.
- **Missing mandatory behavior**: A relation marked `mandatory` is expected only when its stated scope applies. Missing edges are candidates for noncompliance, but scope, abstraction, and data extraction must be checked first.
- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior**: A relation not covered by the norm is open-world. It is not a violation merely because it is absent from the normative edge set.
- **Typical-pattern absence**: A `typical` relation may be absent or weaker than expected because legitimate exceptions exist. Absence is an investigation signal, not automatic noncompliance.
- **Likely data-quality error**: A suspicious relation or missing relation caused by joins, identifier reuse, extraction failure, or labeling gaps. This is repaired, not escalated as behavior.
- **Abstraction mismatch**: A violation at one model-family level may be an artifact of directly-follows or object-extraction abstraction, especially when the norm is eventual or when intermediate events are unmodeled.
- **Insufficient evidence**: Counts alone cannot establish timestamp order, lifecycle state, scope, or business intent.

Open-world versus closed-world affects this classification: forbidden rules are closed-world, so observed forbidden edges are treated as violations unless disproven by data repair. Mandatory rules are closed-world only inside their stated scope; if scope cannot be established, there is insufficient evidence. Typical and unspecified rules are open-world; absence or presence is interpreted, not automatically enforced.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

OC-DFG can establish direct-follows event paths per object type and reveal gross missing paths such as `Create Order -> Validate Order` or `Pack Package -> Dispatch Package`. It cannot establish eventual occurrence, exact timestamp ordering, cancellation state at dispatch, or whether a mandatory event eventually happened after an intermediate event.

Counts and scope matter. For N1, 98 of 100 is a count of direct successions, not a count of Orders that eventually validated. The two missing edges may be due to an unrecorded review event between creation and validation. This is the central danger of interpreting an “eventually expected” rule as directly-follows: a conforming process can look noncompliant merely because an intermediate event breaks the DFG link.

For N3, the forbidden `Cancel Order -> Dispatch Package` relation has high normative significance, but OC-DFG alone cannot confirm cancellation was effective before dispatch. It shows a direct path, not a timestamp comparison. For N4, 198 of 200 is likely significant, but ET counts show both Pack and Dispatch events exist for 200 Packages, so the two missing OC-DFG edges may be abstraction or extraction artifacts rather than missing operational steps.

### OTG

OTG can establish object interactions, object cobirth, and object codeath. It cannot establish event ordering, lifecycle state, or mandatory event-to-object cardinality. OTG edge counts are not the same as event-level object relations.

The T1 `Order–Item` edge weight is 1450, but ET shows only 1448 `Add Item` events with an Order relation. This may indicate two event-level missing Order links that an aggregated OTG graph can obscure. Similarly, T2 `Package–Delivery` has weight 200 while ET shows only 198 `Dispatch Package` events with a Delivery relation; the two graph families measure different things. This is not automatically a contradiction, but it is a reconciliation requirement.

T6 `Order–Payment` cobirth is explicitly forbidden. However, cobirth depends on birth semantics. If an event creates both objects in the source system, it is a violation; if the object birth extraction is too coarse, it is a data-quality issue. The codeath edge is unregulated and should not be escalated.

### ET–OT

ET–OT is the strongest model for verifying per-event mandatory object relations. It can establish that every `Add Item` event must relate to an Order and an Item, every `Dispatch Package` event must relate to a Package and a Delivery, and every `Record Payment` event must relate to a Payment and an Invoice, subject to the stated unapplied-cash exception.

It cannot establish whether a relation is semantically valid, whether an event actually occurred at a certain lifecycle state, or whether a forbidden event-object link is a data artifact. E8 is mandatory except for unapplied cash, but no event is typed as unapplied cash. Therefore the one missing Invoice relation is a formal mandatory gap, but it may be a labeling gap rather than a process violation.

A relation can be conforming at one abstraction and suspicious at another. For example, OTG T1 appears satisfied at weight 1450, but ET–OT exposes two missing mandatory Order relations. Conversely, OC-DFG N1 may look violated, but the event-level behavior may be conforming when intermediate review events are considered.

## 6. Norm-completeness assessment

1. **N1 eventual validation versus directly-follows.**  
   Risk: false nonconformance for Orders that validate after an intermediate event.  
   Clarification: state that N1 is an eventual-occurrence expectation; use a maximum allowed delay or a reachability rule, not only direct succession.  
   Status: mandatory for eventual validation; directly-follows check should be treated as operational approximation.

2. **N4 “intended for external shipment” has no explicit attribute.**  
   Risk: packages not intended for external shipment may be incorrectly classified as missing mandatory dispatch.  
   Clarification: require a package attribute such as `shipment_intent=external` and use it to define N4 scope.  
   Status: mandatory attribute for packages in the N4 scope.

3. **E6 Delivery relation versus carrier reference attribute.**  
   Risk: two dispatch events appear to violate E6 even though a carrier reference exists.  
   Clarification: if a Dispatch Package event has a carrier reference, the referenced Delivery object must be materialized as an ET–OT relation.  
   Status: mandatory extraction/data-object rule.

4. **E8 unapplied cash exception is not typed in the snapshot.**  
   Risk: one missing Invoice relation may be misclassified as mandatory noncompliance.  
   Clarification: require a `payment_type` attribute with at least `applied`, `unapplied`, and `write_off`; E8 applies only to `applied`.  
   Status: mandatory for applied payments; unapplied must be explicitly labeled.

5. **N5 typical payment relation lacks timing and exception definitions.**  
   Risk: open invoices, write-offs, and credit terms may be escalated as process failures.  
   Clarification: define acceptable payment suspense windows and formally recognize write-offs, credit notes, and unapplied cash as legitimate exceptions.  
   Status: typical, with explicit exception categories.

6. **N3 cancellation timing is represented as directly-follows.**  
   Risk: a DFG edge may appear after cancellation due to event ordering artifacts, not actual dispatch after cancellation.  
   Clarification: require timestamp comparison: dispatch must occur after cancellation and the Order state must be cancelled at dispatch time.

   Risk: the one `Order–Payment` cobirth edge may be a batch-event artifact rather than true object co-creation.

   Risk: the seven `Package–Delivery` codeath edges may be interpreted as suspicious or noncompliant.
