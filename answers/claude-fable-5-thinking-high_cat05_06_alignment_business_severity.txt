## 1. Materiality Framework

Severity is assessed on a 1–5 scale against the *control objective* each activity protects, not against edit distance. The dimensions and how they are applied:

- **Control objective.** What failure does the deviation imply? Skipping Fraud Screen defeats the anti-fraud objective; a duplicated Notify Claimant defeats nothing. Deviations that defeat a preventive financial control (approval-before-payment, fraud screening) start at severity 4–5; deviations affecting only evidence or logging start at 2–3; pure noise is 1.
- **Amount / exposure.** Exposure scales severity within a control class: the same missing fraud screen is worse at EUR 480,000 (CL-602) than at EUR 4,500 (CL-610) — although a fraud *indicator* (bank change) can outweigh a small amount because it signals a scalable attack pattern.
- **Reversibility.** Money that has left the bank (CL-602, CL-604, CL-610) is largely unrecoverable; a mislabeled event (CL-611) is fully repairable. Irreversible outcomes add at least one severity level relative to a reversible equivalent.
- **Timing.** Order matters even when the activity set is complete: Pay Claim *before* Manual Approve (CL-604) converts an approval into a rubber stamp (policy P3). A notification at +3 hours (CL-608) is within the 24-hour window, so timing there is compliant.
- **Customer / regulatory harm.** Notification duties (P4), fraud controls expected by regulators, and fair treatment of claimants weigh here. Paying a legitimate claimant late or notifying late harms customers; paying a fraudster harms the portfolio and invites regulatory findings.
- **Recurrence.** A one-off error is contained; the month-level evidence (74 retry duplicates, 9 adjuster gaps, 4 zero-cost auto-approval violations) shows systemic issues. Recurrence raises the priority of remediation even when per-case severity is modest.
- **Evidence reliability.** A deviation supported by two independent sources (bank acceptance timestamp + approval log in CL-604) is high-confidence; a "deviation" that may be a logging artifact (CL-606 external adjuster report) is a candidate evidence gap, not a proven operational failure. Mutable evidence (CL-608 free-text link) *lowers* confidence in conformance without proving non-conformance.
- **Model scope.** If the model was never meant to cover the case (denied leads CL-607, withdrawals CL-612), the alignment measures a scope dispute, not a process failure.

**Confidence vs. severity.** Severity answers: *if the deviation is real, how bad is it?* Confidence answers: *how sure are we that the business event happened (or failed to happen) as the alignment suggests?* They are orthogonal and must be reported separately. CL-604 is high-severity *and* high-confidence (bank-confirmed timestamps). CL-602 is maximum severity even though confidence that fraud screening was truly absent is only medium (a verbal check is claimed) — because at EUR 480,000 with a fresh bank change, even *unevidenced* screening is itself a control failure. CL-606 is low severity precisely because confidence that the assessment actually occurred (externally) is high. A missing event is never treated as proof the activity did not occur; it is treated as absent evidence whose reliability must be tested.

## 3. Repeated Deviation Patterns

**Pattern A — Retry duplicates of Notify Claimant (CL-603 + 74 month-level cases).**
Frequency evidence: 74 cases with `L(Notify Claimant)` immediately following a synchronous notification, identical message IDs, all traced to one retrying connector. Business risk: near zero direct risk, but material *analytical* risk — 74 units of fake alignment cost pollute dashboards and can mask real notification failures. Likely cause: non-idempotent messaging connector writing an event per delivery attempt (logging, not operational). Confidence: High (message-ID and transport-log corroboration). Recommended owner: Integration/middleware engineering, with process-mining team adding message-ID deduplication upstream.

**Pattern B — External-adjuster evidence gaps (CL-606 + 9 high-value cases).**
Frequency evidence: nine high-value cases with `M(Assess Loss)` and an external-adjuster document; two lack a stable document-to-case identifier. Business risk: moderate — assessments likely occurred, but on high-value claims the firm cannot *prove* it from the system of record, and the two unlinkable cases could conceal a genuinely skipped assessment. Likely cause: boundary-spanning activity performed outside the core system with no event interface (logging/architecture, not operational). Confidence: High for the seven cases with stable identifiers, Low-Medium for the two without. Owner: Claims operations (process) + data engineering (ingestion feed); internal audit to sample the two weak-ID cases.

**Pattern C — Label mapping drift (CL-611; screen the full log for `Assess Damage` and similar synonyms).**
Frequency evidence: one confirmed case; the post-hoc mapping-table update implies other cases in the same window may be affected. Business risk: low direct risk, but every mismapped label creates a false "missing mandatory control" finding on high-value claims — expensive false positives. Likely cause: source-system vocabulary change not synchronized with the mapping table (data governance). Confidence: High. Owner: Data governance / master-data management, with change-control review of the late mapping update.

**Pattern D — Out-of-scope denials and withdrawals (CL-607, CL-612; "most alignments with raw cost ≥ 4").**
Frequency evidence: the supplied statement that denied and withdrawn cases account for most raw-cost ≥ 4 alignments. Business risk: low operational risk, high *reporting* risk — the worst-looking conformance numbers come from correctly handled cases, eroding trust in the analysis and burying real deviations. Likely cause: model M0 scoped to payable claims while conformance runs on the full intake population; scope ownership disputed (P6). Confidence: High. Owner: Process owner and model owner jointly, via a documented scope decision (extend the model with Deny/Withdraw branches, or filter the population, but not silently).

**Pattern E — Data-aware auto-approval failures (CL-609, CL-610; 4 cases month-wide).**
Frequency evidence: four fully synchronous Auto Approve paths violating P1 or P2. Business risk: high — these are zero-cost, invisible-to-alignment bypasses of the firm's core financial control, and CL-610 carries a live payment-diversion indicator. Likely cause: the auto-approval decision engine does not enforce (or receives stale) amount, risk-rating, and bank-change conditions; the control exists on paper (P1/P2) but not in the system (operational + control-design). Confidence: High that violations occurred (data fields are logged); Medium on root cause pending rule-configuration review. Owner: Head of claims / control owner for payment release, with fraud team on CL-610-type cases.

**Pattern F — Approval-chain integrity failures on large claims (CL-602, CL-604).**
Frequency evidence: two of the three claims above EUR 50,000 in this sample show either a missing Fraud Screen with irreversible payment (CL-602) or payment preceding approval (CL-604) — a striking hit rate that mandates a full-population query. Business risk: severe — irreversible high-value disbursements without effective preventive controls; regulatory and fraud exposure. Likely cause: payment release is technically possible without completed approval prerequisites (system control gap), compounded by informal "verbal" practices. Confidence: High (bank timestamps, absent fraud-screen records). Owner: CFO/CRO sponsorship; internal audit and fraud investigation immediately; IT for the payment-gate fix.

**Pattern G — Telemetry contamination (CL-601).**
Frequency evidence: one case here, but telemetry events are typically pervasive once present. Business risk: none directly (P5); inflates costs and analyst workload. Cause: extraction pipeline does not separate UI/transport events from business activities. Confidence: High. Owner: Process-mining/data pipeline team.

## 4. Causal Hypotheses and Evidence Tests

A missing event is treated throughout as *absent evidence*, never as proof of an absent activity.

**CL-602 (missing Fraud Screen, EUR 480,000).**
H1: No fraud screening of any kind occurred; the approver waved it through. H2: An informal ("verbal") screen occurred but was never documented. H3: A screen was performed in a fraud-team tool that does not write to the core log. Discriminating evidence: query the fraud-case-management system and analyst worklogs for this claim ID and date; phone/Teams records supporting the "verbal check" claim; the approver's screening history on other cases; whether the beneficiary bank change was itself verified. H3 would downgrade this to an evidence gap (like CL-606); H1/H2 confirm a severity-5 control failure — and under P2, even H2 fails the control because the screen must be evidenced.

**CL-604 (pay at 10:03, approve at 10:20).**
H1: The payment system allows release before approval (systemic gate failure). H2: Approval was given earlier out-of-band and only *recorded* at 10:20 (clock/recording lag). H3: Someone with payment-system access deliberately bypassed the workflow. Discriminating evidence: payment-system audit trail showing who/what triggered the 10:03 release and under which state; approval-workflow state history (was the task even open at 10:03?); population query for other pay-before-approve gaps; system clock synchronization check. H2 still violates P3's evidencing requirement but changes remediation from access review to recording discipline.

**CL-606 / Pattern B (missing Assess Loss with adjuster report).**
H1: Assessment fully performed externally; only ingestion is missing. H2: The adjuster report exists but was not actually used in the approval decision (checkbox compliance). H3: For the two weak-ID cases, the report belongs to a *different* claim. Discriminating evidence: content match between report details (loss amounts, dates, parties) and the claim; whether Fraud Screen/Manual Approve records reference the report; document-management metadata (upload time, uploader) for the two unlinkable cases.

**CL-608 (missing Notify Claimant, mutable CRM link).**
H1: Notification genuinely sent via secure portal at +3h (P4-compliant). H2: The CRM link was edited after the fact to simulate compliance. H3: A notification was sent but to the wrong recipient/channel. Discriminating evidence: portal-side immutable delivery logs and read receipts; CRM field audit history showing when the link text was created/modified; claimant confirmation. H1 reduces this to an evidence-hardening task; H2 escalates it to an integrity incident.

**CL-609 / CL-610 / Pattern E (zero-cost auto-approval violations).**
H1: The decision engine's rules omit or mis-specify the P1/P2 conditions (design defect). H2: The rules are correct but consumed stale data — e.g., CL-610's bank change one hour pre-payment arrived after the approval decision (latency defect). H3: A user manually forced the auto path (override/abuse). Discriminating evidence: the rule configuration and its change history; the decision engine's input snapshot at decision time (what amount/risk/bank data it saw); override logs and user IDs; distribution of the four violating cases across time and users (clustered = override/abuse; uniform = design/latency). H2 vs. H1 determines whether the fix is a data-feed SLA or a rule rewrite; H3 triggers HR/security action.

**CL-611 (Assess Damage vs. Assess Loss).**
H1: Pure synonym — one source system renamed the activity and mapping lagged. H2: "Assess Damage" is a genuinely *different, lighter* procedure that does not satisfy the Assess Loss control. H3: The post-case mapping update was a retrofit to make the case look compliant. Discriminating evidence: compare the work product of "Assess Damage" events (fields captured, roles, duration) with standard Assess Loss cases; mapping-table change log (who changed it, when, ticket reference); whether other cases in the window show the same label.

**CL-603 / Pattern A (duplicate notifications).**
H1: Connector retry duplicates the *event record* only (one customer message). H2: The customer actually received two messages (nuisance, minor). H3: Duplicates mask cases where the *first* send failed and only the retry succeeded, i.e., some notifications are later than logged. Discriminating evidence: transport-level delivery receipts per attempt; sampling of the 74 cases for delivery timestamps vs. the 24-hour P4 window.

**CL-607 (denied lead forced through payable model).**
H1: Pure scope artifact — the model was never meant for denials. H2: Some "denied" cases are mis-denials that should have been assessed (real operational risk hiding inside the scope dispute). Discriminating evidence: denial-reason codes and coverage-rule audit for a sample of denied leads; complaint/appeal rates on denied claims. H2 would justify extending the model rather than filtering the cases.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

Raw alignment cost is an *edit distance under an arbitrary cost function*; business risk is a function of control objectives, money, data conditions, evidence, and irreversibility. The supplied cases falsify any monotonic mapping between the two, in both directions:

1. **Cost-function design (uniform weights).** Every visible move costs 1, so skipping a fraud screen on EUR 480,000 (CL-602, cost 1) is "as bad as" a connector logging a duplicate notification (CL-603, cost 1) and *less bad* than three UI telemetry events (CL-601, cost 3). The metric is not wrong — it is answering a different question ("how far is this trace from the model?") than the business one ("how much should I worry?").

2. **Zero cost ≠ zero risk: data conditions are invisible.** CL-609 and CL-610 are *fully synchronous, cost 0*, yet both violate P1 (and CL-610 also P2). The process tree's exclusive choice permits Auto Approve unconditionally; the policy conditions it on amount, risk rating, and bank-change recency — attributes a control-flow alignment never inspects. CL-610 is the sharpest case: a beneficiary-bank change one hour before an automatic payment is a classic diversion fingerprint, and the alignment certifies it as perfect. Only data-aware conformance (or the policy overlay applied manually, as here) can see it.

3. **High cost ≠ high risk: model scope.** CL-607 (cost 4) and CL-612 (cost 4) carry the highest costs in the set while representing *correct* business outcomes — a properly denied lead and a properly honored withdrawal. The cost measures the model's missing branches, not operational failure. The month-level evidence confirms this systematically: most cost ≥ 4 alignments are denials/withdrawals. Unfiltered, the "worst" conformance cases are the safest ones.

4. **Ordering and irreversibility are flattened.** CL-604's cost of 3 arises from a log move, a model move, and a missing Fraud Screen — arithmetically similar to CL-601's three telemetry moves. But CL-604 encodes *payment before approval* of EUR 90,000, an irreversible P3 breach the moment the bank accepted at 10:03; CL-601 encodes autosaves. Cost counts edits; it cannot express that the *sequence* Pay→Approve destroys the preventive nature of the approval, or that money, once gone, converts a "cheap repair" into an unrecoverable loss.

5. **Missing-but-external evidence and activity granularity/labels.** CL-606 (cost 1) and CL-611 (cost 2) look like skipped or mutated mandatory assessments; in reality the work was done — by a licensed external adjuster outside the core system, and under a synonymous label ("Assess Damage") respectively. A model move signals *no matching event in this log*, which is an evidentiary statement, not an ontological one. Similarly, CL-608's `M(Notify Claimant)` most plausibly reflects a notification living in the CRM; the true issue there — a mutable evidence link on a EUR 250,000 claim — costs nothing in the alignment at all.

6. **Duplicated telemetry and hidden moves.** The 74 retry duplicates (Pattern A, CL-603) add 74 cost units of pure logging artifact, inflating aggregate "non-conformance" while carrying no risk. Conversely CL-605's silent `T(tau,c=0)` is correctly free: hidden transitions are model plumbing from the tree-to-net conversion, and reporting them to the business as anything at all would be a category error.

The disciplined conclusion: use alignment cost as a *locator* (where does the log disagree with the model?) and a business-severity assessment as the *evaluator* (does the disagreement matter, and how confident are we?). The two must be computed, stored, and reported in separate columns — as the remediation portfolio and the code below both enforce.
