# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in a public-benefits process requires examining multiple dimensions simultaneously. Outcome parity is the most visible metric, but it is the least informative about *why* groups fare differently. Below I identify and discuss the key dimensions the evidence supports.

---

## Outcome parity (raw)

The raw approval rates range from 78% (Group A) to 58% (Group D), a 20-percentage-point spread. Raw cycle times, appeal rates, and rework rates also differ substantially. By themselves, these numbers do not tell us whether the process is fair or unfair—they tell us that groups experience the process differently.

## Adjusted outcome disparity

After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, a residual gap remains for every group relative to Group A:

| Group | Adjusted approval gap | Adjusted cycle-time gap |
|---|---|---|
| B | 5.5 pp | +2.6 days |
| C | 2.2 pp | +4.1 days |
| D | 3.1 pp | +0.9 days |

These adjusted gaps are smaller than the raw gaps but not zero. The fact that they persist after conditioning on observed complexity means we cannot fully attribute the raw disparities to documented case mix. However, we also cannot conclude discrimination, because:

- The adjustment covariates themselves may be endogenous (e.g., documentation_completeness may be *caused by* language barriers, not independent of group membership).
- Unobserved applicant needs (e.g., cognitive disability, housing instability) may differ across groups.
- The adjusted gaps are modest in absolute terms, and their practical significance depends on context.

**Key concern:** Groups B, C, and D all show lower adjusted approval probabilities *and* longer adjusted cycle times. The pattern is not symmetric—some groups face slower *and* less favorable outcomes, which suggests procedural burden may compound with outcome disadvantage rather than compensating for it.

## Procedural burden

Procedural burden refers to the number of steps, handovers, delays, and informational demands placed on an applicant before a decision is reached. Several indicators suggest disproportionate burden:

- **More steps:** Groups B and C are far more likely to traverse variant V4 (Language Support Contact  Completeness  Request Docs) or V5 (Accommodation Review  Supervisor Review), both of which contain more activities before a decision.
- **More requests for documents:** Group B has a +9.5 pp adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents; Group C has +4.2 pp. Each request adds a full cycle (agency requests  applicant responds  agency re-checks) that is burdensome for applicants.
- **More handovers:** Group B cases involve handovers between general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker. Group C cases involve handovers between general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor. Each handover creates a context switch, potential loss of case knowledge, and a new waiting queue.
- **More supervisor reviews:** Group B has +8.0 pp and Group C +5.5 pp adjusted probability of Supervisor Review. While supervisor oversight can be protective, it also adds time and signals that these cases are treated as more complex or risky even after controlling for documented complexity.

The critical fairness question is whether the *additional* burden is proportionate to legitimate needs. Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving—they exist to ensure meaningful access. But when the *process infrastructure* for delivering those rights is under-resourced (only 7 bilingual caseworkers; a specialized accommodation team with a 4.7-day median wait), the exercise of a right becomes a procedural penalty.

Group B's +2.6-day adjusted delay is partly explained by the 3.8-day wait from Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment.

## Consistency of routing

Routing consistency asks whether similar cases follow similar paths regardless of group membership. The data show that routing is *not* consistent after conditioning on observable features:

- Variants V4 and V5 are almost exclusive to Groups B and C, respectively. These variants are triggered by legitimate policy requirements (language support, accommodation), but the *consequences* of entering those variants—longer waits, more handovers, different resource pools—are not neutral.
- Group D is disproportionately routed through variant V3 (Risk Screening  Fraud Review  Supervisor Review): 28% vs. 9% for Group A. This is consistent with Group D's higher measured risk scores, but risk_score_band itself may encode historical disparities.
- The distribution of cases across the resource pool is highly unequal: 52 caseworkers for Group A, 7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B, 5 accommodation specialists for Group C. When a group's cases are concentrated in a small resource pool, the group's experience is highly sensitive to that pool's workload, skill, and morale.

Routing that is *legitimately* different in its triggering conditions can still be *procedurally unfair* if the downstream consequences are not managed.

## Quality and accessibility of support

The available data do not directly measure the quality of explanations, the dignity of treatment, or the accessibility of communication. However, several proxies raise concerns:

- **Language support bottleneck:** Only 7 bilingual caseworkers handle 57% of Group B cases. When language support is scarce, caseworkers may rush, provide less thorough explanations, or default to less favorable information-gathering strategies (e.g., requesting documents that could have been resolved by conversation).
- **Document requests as a proxy for communication failure:** The +9.5 pp adjusted increase in Request Missing Documents for Group B, even after controlling for documentation_completeness, suggests that the completeness check may be applied more stringently or that the information provided at intake is not adequately accessible.
- **Accommodation review bottleneck:** Group C's cases wait 4.7 days at the Accommodation Review  Eligibility step. During this wait, no progress is made on eligibility. The accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review, creating a dual-role conflict that may delay both functions.
- **No measure of explanation quality:** case_outcome captures approval/denial but not whether the applicant understood the decision, received it in their preferred language, or was treated with respect.

## Appeal and rework burden

| B | 13% | +4.4 pp | 22% |

Groups B and D show substantially elevated adjusted appeal probabilities. Appeals are costly for both applicants and the agency. The elevated appeal rate for Group B (+4.4 pp) and Group D (+5.7 pp) after adjustment may indicate:

1. **Legitimate dissatisfaction:** These groups may have stronger grounds for appeal because their initial decisions are less favorable.
2. **Procedural confusion:** Complex paths with more handovers and steps may leave applicants less informed about the basis for a decision, prompting appeals that could have been avoided with better communication.
3. **Lower trust:** Applicants who experience longer waits and more document requests may be more skeptical of the outcome, regardless of its correctness.

Group C's rework rate (24%) is the highest, though its appeal rate is more moderate. This suggests that Group C cases may require corrections or reopened processing more often, possibly because accommodation needs are not fully captured in the initial processing.

## Resource-allocation fairness

Resource fairness is not about equal numbers of caseworkers per group—it is about whether each group has *sufficient* capacity to receive timely and high-quality service. The data show:

- **Group A:** 52 caseworkers for 3,600 cases  69 cases per caseworker.
- **Group B:** 7 bilingual caseworkers (plus 3 overloaded regional officers) for 1,250 cases. If we attribute the full load to the 7 bilingual caseworkers, that is ~179 cases per caseworker—more than 2.5× the Group A ratio. The 3 overloaded regional officers add to the burden but may not have bilingual capacity.
- **Group C:** 5 accommodation specialists for 820 cases  164 cases per specialist, plus these specialists also handle supervisor review for other cases.
- **Group D:** No specific resource bottleneck is identified in the data, but the higher fraud-review routing means these cases pass through a queue that may have its own capacity constraints.

The capacity ratios suggest that Groups B and C face structural resource scarcity relative to their caseload. This is not an indictment of individual caseworkers—it is a resource-allocation decision that disproportionately affects groups whose needs require specialized support.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A rigorous fairness assessment must distinguish among four concepts:

| Concept | Definition | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Disparity** | A difference in outcomes or process experience between groups | Descriptive statistics |
| **Discrimination** | A disparity caused by intentional or structural bias in the process | Causal identification of bias after removing legitimate explanations |
| **Legitimate routing** | Differences in process paths that are required by policy and proportionate to documented needs | Evidence that routing is driven by policy-relevant factors and that downstream consequences are managed |
| **Uncertainty** | Ambiguity about whether a remaining gap reflects bias, unobserved needs, or model specification error | Sensitivity analysis, bounding exercises |

The strategy below is designed to separate these as cleanly as the data allow.

## Matching or stratification variables

The preliminary model adjusts for: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. This is a reasonable baseline, but several refinements are needed:

1. **Stratify by support-step participation.** Create a binary indicator for whether the case entered a support step (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review). Within each stratum, compare outcomes and cycle times across groups. This separates "Group B waits longer because it enters the language-support path" from "Group B waits longer *even within* the language-support path."

2. **Stratify by variant.** Within V1 (the fast, no-frills path), compare approval rates and cycle times across groups. If Group B cases that happen to reach V1 have the same outcomes as Group A V1 cases, the disparity is driven by path assignment, not by differential treatment within a path. If they differ even within V1, there is a within-path concern.

3. **Treat risk_score_band as a possibly endogenous covariate.** Run the model with and without risk_score_band. If the adjusted gap changes substantially, risk_score_band is an important mediator—but if risk_score_band itself reflects historical bias, controlling for it may mask discrimination. Report both specifications.

4. **Treat documentation_completeness as a possibly endogenous covariate.** Similarly, if language barriers cause lower documentation_completeness, then controlling for it *understates* the disadvantage for Group B. Run a model that instruments documentation_completeness with language_preference or channel.

5. **Include resource-pool indicators.** Add a variable for the assigned caseworker or resource pool. If the group effect diminishes after controlling for resource assignment, the disparity is mediated by resource concentration, which is itself a fairness concern.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

**Exercise 1: Omit risk_score_band.** Re-run the adjusted model without risk_score_band. If the adjusted approval gap for Group D widens substantially, risk_score_band is absorbing some of the disparity. If it changes little, risk_score_band is not a major mediator.

**Exercise 2: Omit documentation_completeness.** Re-run without documentation_completeness. If the adjusted gap for Group B widens, documentation_completeness is acting as a mediator—and since it may be endogenous to language barriers, the model *with* documentation_completeness understates the disadvantage.

**Exercise 3: Bounding exercise.** Use the Oster (2019) coefficient stability approach or the Altonji-Elder-Taber method to estimate how much unobserved confounding would be needed to explain away the adjusted gap. If the required confounding is implausibly large, the gap is more likely to reflect genuine process disparity.

**Exercise 4: Propensity-score trimming.** If there are regions of the covariate space where no Group A cases exist (or very few), trim those observations and re-estimate. This addresses the concern that extrapolation into unsupported covariate regions drives the adjusted gap.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

- **Region:** The data note that two regions have backlog issues affecting Supervisor Review  Decision Draft. Within those regions, compare Group B and non-Group B cycle times. If the gap is concentrated in those regions, the issue is regional capacity, not group-level bias.
- **Channel:** Group B has 38% in-person applications. Within the in-person channel, compare outcomes across groups. Within the online channel, do the same. If the gap is larger in-person, the in-person process (routing, staffing, information collection) may be the problem.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## Variant-level analysis

**Variant V1 (fast path, 5.8-day median):** This is the ideal path—straightforward eligibility determination with no support steps, no document requests, no fraud review, and no appeal. Group A reaches V1 in 42% of cases; Groups B, C, and D reach it in only 18–22% of cases. This means that the *majority* of Group A cases experience the simplest, fastest process, while the majority of Groups B–D cases experience more complex, longer paths. This is the single most important structural disparity in the data.

Is it justified? Partly. Group B cases often need language support, Group C cases need accommodation review, and Group D cases have higher risk scores triggering fraud review. These are legitimate routing triggers. But:

- The *consequences* of not reaching V1 are severe: 14.9–21.6 days median cycle time vs. 5.8 days.
- The gap between V1 share and the others is large: Group A has more than double the V1 share of any other group.
- If some Group B or C cases *could* reach V1 with better intake processes (e.g., better initial documentation gathering, proactive language support at submission), the V1 share gap would narrow.

**Variant V2 (document request path, 14.9-day median):** Group B is most likely to reach V2 (34%). The adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp for Group B. This is concerning because:
- The completeness check and document request cycle is inherently burdensome.
- The waiting time from Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents is 1.6 days for Group B vs. 0.4 days for Group A, suggesting that bilingual staff availability is delaying even the *initiation* of the document request.
- If documentation completeness is lower partly because of language barriers, then the document request path is not purely a legitimate response to case complexity—it is a *consequence* of inadequate language accessibility at intake.

**Variant V3 (fraud review path, 18.4-day median):** Group D is disproportionately on V3 (28%). This is consistent with Group D's higher measured risk scores. However:
- The risk_score_band may encode historical patterns (e.g., prior-case count is high for Group D at 49%, and prior cases may reflect prior system interactions, not applicant behavior).
- The fraud review path includes Supervisor Review, adding oversight but also delay.
- The adjusted appeal gap for Group D is +5.7 pp—the largest of any group. This suggests that Group D applicants may view the fraud review process as unfair or the decisions as inadequately explained.

**Variant V4 (language support path, 19.2-day median):** This variant is almost exclusively a Group B path (24% of Group B cases, 3% of other groups). The 3.8-day wait from Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment is a bottleneck. With only 7 bilingual caseworkers, this is a structural capacity constraint. The variant's 19.2-day median is more than triple the V1 median, and the applicant has no control over the delay.

**Variant V5 (accommodation path, 21.6-day median):** This variant is almost exclusively a Group C path (31%). The 4.7-day wait from Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment is the longest single-step bottleneck in the data. The accommodation specialists also handle supervisor review, creating a dual-role bottleneck. The 21.6-day median is the longest of any non-appeal variant.

**Variant V6 (appeal path, 30.7-day median):** This is the end-of-process variant for cases that are denied and then appealed. Group D has the highest share (13%), followed by Group B (8%). The 30.7-day median is punishing for applicants who need benefits quickly. The high appeal rates for Groups B and D may reflect lower initial approval rates, inadequate explanation of decisions, or genuine errors that are caught on appeal.

## Handover and resource disparities

The handover patterns are particularly concerning:

- **Group B:** general  bilingual  general. Each handover creates a context switch. The bilingual caseworker may not have full case history, and when the case returns to a general caseworker, there may be information loss. This pattern is a *direct consequence* of having only 7 bilingual caseworkers instead of distributing bilingual capacity more broadly.
- **Group C:** general  accommodation specialist  supervisor. This handover chain is even longer in terms of oversight layers. The accommodation specialist's dual role (also doing supervisor review) means that the same person may be reviewing their own work, or that accommodation review and supervisor review compete for the same person's time.

The concentration of Group B cases in 7 caseworkers and Group C cases in 5 specialists means that these groups' experiences are highly sensitive to those individuals' workload, judgment, and turnover. A single sick day or leave of absence for one of the 7 bilingual caseworkers would directly impact Group B's timeliness.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

## Mechanism 1: Capacity-constrained support pathways (concerning)

The most prominent mechanism is that rights-preserving support steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review) are implemented through understaffed specialized teams. This creates a *structural penalty* for applicants who exercise their rights. The 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists are bottlenecks that directly generate the longest single-step waiting times in the process. This is not discrimination in intent—it is a resource-allocation failure that disproportionately affects specific groups. It is concerning because the process *promises* support but *delivers* delay.

## Mechanism 2: Endogenous documentation barriers (concerning)

Group B has 46% incomplete documentation vs. 21% for Group A. The adjusted gap in Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp even after controlling for documentation_completeness. A plausible mechanism is:

1. Group B applicants, who prefer minority language, may find intake forms or instructions less accessible.
2. This leads to lower documentation_completeness at submission.
3. The completeness check triggers a document request cycle.
4. The document request is issued in the majority language or requires bilingual staff to explain, adding delay.
5. The applicant may need additional help to gather documents, especially if the required documents are from institutions that operate in the majority language.

This creates a *cycle* where language barriers  incomplete documentation  document requests  more delay  lower approval likelihood. Controlling for documentation_completeness in the adjusted model partially addresses this, but the *root cause* is the accessibility of the intake process, not the applicant's failure to provide documents.

## Mechanism 3: Risk-score-band as a historical bias amplifier (concerning)

Group D has 51% high risk_score_band vs. 18% for Group A. Risk scores often incorporate prior interactions with the system—prior cases, prior denials, prior appeals. If Group D applicants have historically been treated more harshly (higher denial rates, more fraud reviews), their risk scores will be higher, which triggers more fraud reviews, which leads to more denials, which feeds back into higher risk scores. This is a classic feedback loop in algorithmic decision-making.

The +5.7 pp adjusted appeal gap for Group D suggests that Group D applicants may perceive the process as unfair—possibly because the fraud review feels punitive rather than neutral. If the fraud review process is experienced as accusatory or stigmatizing, it may deter some applicants from applying or appealing, which would create *survivorship bias* in the observed appeal rate (only the most determined applicants appeal, meaning the true level of dissatisfaction is higher than the appeal rate suggests).

## Mechanism 4: Dual-role conflict for accommodation specialists (concerning)

The accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review. This means:
- When an accommodation specialist finishes an Accommodation Review, they may also be the one to do the Supervisor Review on a different case, creating a scheduling conflict.
- If accommodation review and supervisor review draw from the same pool, increasing one delays the other.
- Group C cases may be "double-counted" in the workload of these specialists, leading to lower effective capacity for accommodation review.

This is an organizational design problem, not individual bias, but it has fairness implications because it systematically disadvantages Group C.

## Mechanism 5: Channel and information-quality differences (potentially legitimate)

Group B has 38% in-person channel share vs. 9% for Group A. In-person applications may be more likely to require identity verification at a counter, which takes longer (1.4-day wait vs. 0.8-day). In-person intake may also capture information differently—oral vs. written—and the caseworker's note-taking or form-filling may introduce errors or omissions that later trigger document requests.

This is partly legitimate (in-person and online channels have different workflows) and partly concerning (if the in-person channel is less efficient because it serves a more linguistically diverse population without adequate bilingual support).

## Mechanism 6: Caseworker discretion and implicit bias (concerning but unverifiable)

With 52 caseworkers serving Group A and only 7 serving Group B, there is much less diversity of judgment for Group B. If a bilingual caseworker has a stricter interpretation of documentation requirements or eligibility criteria, that interpretation affects a large share of Group B cases. For Group A, any individual caseworker's idiosyncrasy is diluted across a large pool. This is not evidence of bias, but it is a structural vulnerability: small-group resource pools amplify individual-level variation, which may include implicit bias.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

## Measurement bias in risk_score_band

Risk_score_band is a key covariate in the adjusted model, but it is not exogenous. If it incorporates prior-case count (49% high for Group D), and prior cases reflect prior system interactions that may have been biased, then risk_score_band *measures the cumulative effect of past unfairness*. Controlling for it will absorb some of the disparity that should be attributed to the process. We cannot determine from the available data whether risk_score_band is a legitimate complexity indicator or a bias amplifier.

## Measurement bias in documentation_completeness

Documentation_completeness may be measured as a binary or ordinal indicator by the caseworker during the Completeness Check step. If the threshold for "complete" is applied more stringently to Group B cases (e.g., a caseworker unfamiliar with foreign-language documents marks them as incomplete), then documentation_completeness is not an objective measure—it is a process output that may itself reflect bias.

## Missing applicant needs

The data do not capture:
- **Applicant literacy level.** Low-literacy applicants may struggle with forms regardless of language.
- **Applicant disability type.** Group C captures "disability accommodation requested" but not the type or severity of disability. A physical accommodation (e.g., wheelchair access) is very different from a cognitive accommodation (e.g., simplified communication), and they may affect processing differently.
- **Housing stability.** Unhoused applicants may have difficulty receiving notifications or gathering documents.
- **Employment status and time constraints.** Applicants who cannot take time off work for in-person appointments face different barriers.
- **Digital literacy.** Group A's high online share may correlate with digital literacy, which facilitates faster processing. Applicants who must use in-person channels may face structural barriers unrelated to case complexity.

## Unobserved resource workload

Resource assignment is observed at the case level, but *workload* is observed only at daily granularity. We cannot determine:
- Whether a specific bilingual caseworker was overloaded on a specific day, causing a specific delay.
- Whether the accommodation specialists' dual role actually caused scheduling conflicts on specific dates.
- Whether supervisor review delays in the two flagged regions are due to caseload, case complexity, or individual supervisor behavior.

Without case-level workload data, the resource-assignment analysis remains descriptive, not causal.

## Appeal quality and disposition

The data include appeal_flag but not:
- Whether the appeal was successful (overturn rate).
- The basis for the appeal (procedural error, new evidence, disagreement with eligibility criteria).
- Whether the applicant received assistance with the appeal.
- Whether the appeal decision was timely.

Without this information, we cannot determine whether the elevated appeal rates for Groups B and D reflect (a) more initial errors, (b) more procedural unfairness, or (c) lower trust in the system. These have very different policy implications.

## Historical bias in risk scores

As discussed above, risk_score_band likely incorporates historical data. If the process has historically been unfair to certain groups, the risk score will encode that unfairness, and the adjusted model will *understate* the current disparity. This is a fundamental limitation of observational fairness analysis: the "controls" may be contaminated by the very bias we are trying to measure.

## Case_outcome limitations

case_outcome captures approval/denial but not:
- **Benefit amount.** Two approved cases may receive very different benefit levels.
- **Conditionality.** Some approvals may come with burdensome conditions (e.g., frequent re-certification).
- **Explanation quality.** A denied case with a clear, respectful explanation is procedurally different from a denied case with a form letter.
- **Dignity of treatment.** The applicant's subjective experience of fairness is not captured.

## Regional confounding

Region is included as a covariate, but the interaction between region and group is not fully explored. If certain regions have both a high share of Group B applicants *and* worse process infrastructure, the region covariate may absorb group-level variation that should be attributed to infrastructure under-investment.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

## Option 1: Add capacity for language support and accommodation review

**Action:** Increase the number of bilingual caseworkers (currently 7) and accommodation specialists (currently 5). Target a ratio closer to the Group A caseworker-per-case ratio.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces waiting time at Language Support Contact  Eligibility (currently 3.8 days) and Accommodation Review  Eligibility (currently 4.7 days).
- Reduces handovers (fewer cases need to be routed to a small specialist pool).
- Improves quality of support (caseworkers are less rushed).

**Side effects:**
- **Cost.** Hiring bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists requires budget and may face labor-market constraints.
- **Training lag.** New staff need training, which temporarily reduces effective capacity.
- **Risk of re-specialization.** If new bilingual caseworkers are *only* assigned to Group B cases, the concentration problem persists. A better approach is to build bilingual capacity *across* the general caseworker pool.

## Option 2: Service-level targets for support steps

**Action:** Establish explicit timeliness targets for Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment and Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment (e.g., maximum 2-day wait).

**Benefits:**
- Creates accountability for the steps that currently have no targets.
- Makes support steps visible in performance dashboards.
- Aligns operational management with the policy intent of rights-preserving steps.

**Side effects:**
- **Gaming.** If targets are not accompanied by capacity increases, staff may rush support steps, reducing quality.
- **Measurement burden.** Tracking step-level SLAs requires more granular data collection.
- **Tension with fraud review.** If timeliness targets are applied uniformly, they may conflict with the thoroughness of fraud review for Group D cases.

## Option 3: Routing redesign to reduce handovers

**Action:** For Group B cases, train general caseworkers in basic language-support procedures (e.g., using translated templates, phone interpretation services) so that only complex language cases require the bilingual specialist pool. For Group C cases, separate the accommodation specialist role from the supervisor review role.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces the general  bilingual  general handover chain for Group B.
- Eliminates the dual-role conflict for Group C accommodation specialists.
- Increases the effective capacity of both specialist pools.

**Side effects:**
- **Quality risk.** General caseworkers may provide lower-quality language support than bilingual specialists.
- **Cost of training.** Broader language-support training requires investment.
- **Loss of specialization.** If general caseworkers handle more language-support cases, the bilingual specialists may lose proficiency or case volume, potentially reducing institutional commitment to bilingual capacity.

## Option 4: Decision-explanation improvements

**Action:** Require that all denial decisions include a plain-language explanation (in the applicant's preferred language) of (a) the specific reason for denial, (b) what evidence was considered, and (c) how to appeal. For fraud review cases (Group D), include an explanation of what triggered the review and what was found.

**Benefits:**
- May reduce appeal rates for Groups B and D by addressing the informational basis for appeals.
- Improves dignity of treatment.
- Creates a record that can be audited for consistency.

**Side effects:**
- **Increased workload.** Writing individualized explanations takes time, potentially increasing cycle time.
- **Legal risk.** If explanations are not carefully drafted, they may create grounds for additional legal challenges.
- **Template rigidity.** If templates are used, they may not capture the nuance of individual cases, reducing the perceived quality of the explanation.

## Option 5: Monitor adjusted disparities continuously

**Action:** Implement a recurring (e.g., monthly) adjusted disparity report that tracks approval probability, cycle time, step-level waiting time, handover count, and appeal rate by group, controlling for the covariates in the adjusted model. Flag when any adjusted gap exceeds a threshold (e.g., 3 percentage points for approval, 2 days for cycle time).

**Benefits:**
- Creates early-warning capability for emerging disparities.
- Institutionalizes fairness monitoring rather than relying on ad hoc analyses.
- Enables before/after evaluation of policy changes.

**Side effects:**
- **Threshold-setting controversy.** Any threshold is somewhat arbitrary and may be contested.
- **Covariate lag.** If the model is not updated frequently, it may not reflect changing case mix.
- **False positives.** Random variation may trigger alerts, creating unnecessary investigation burden.
- **False negatives.** If the model's covariates absorb bias (as discussed in §5), adjusted gaps may be too small, providing false assurance.

## Option 6: Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness

**Action:**
- **Risk_score_band:** Examine the inputs to the risk score. If prior-case count is an input, assess whether it is a legitimate predictor of current risk or a proxy for historical system interaction. Run a "counterfactual fairness" test: what would Group D's risk scores be if they had Group A's prior-case distribution?
- **Documentation_completeness:** Examine whether the completeness threshold is applied consistently across groups. Conduct a blinded review: give the same set of documents (with group-identifying information removed) to multiple reviewers and check for inter-rater reliability by group.

**Benefits:**
- Addresses the root-cause concern that "controls" may be biased.
- May reveal actionable findings (e.g., remove prior-case count from risk score; retrain completeness reviewers).

**Side effects:**
- **Political sensitivity.** Auditing risk scores may face resistance from the teams that developed them.
- **Complexity.** Counterfactual fairness analysis requires careful causal reasoning and may not yield clear conclusions.
- **If risk_score_band is reformed,** fraud review rates for Group D may decrease, potentially increasing fraud risk. This requires a cost-benefit analysis.

## Option 7: Proactive intake support

**Action:** For applicants in the minority language or requesting accommodation, provide a proactive intake assistance step *before* the Completeness Check. This could include a bilingual intake specialist reviewing the application for completeness, or a pre-submission checklist in the applicant's preferred language.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces the documentation completeness gap at the root.
- May increase V1 (fast path) share for Groups B and C.
- Reduces downstream document requests, saving time for both applicants and caseworkers.

**Side effects:**
- **Cost and logistics.** Requires bilingual staff or translated materials at the intake stage, before the case is formally assigned.
- **Equity concern.** If only some groups get proactive support, others may perceive unfairness. However, the support is designed to equalize *access*, not outcomes.
- **Timing.** Proactive support at intake may slightly delay submission, but this is likely offset by faster downstream processing.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

## What the evidence supports

The process exhibits **substantial procedural disparities** that are **not fully explained by documented case complexity**. After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, Groups B, C, and D all experience lower approval probabilities, longer cycle times, and higher probabilities of additional review steps relative to Group A. The adjusted gaps are modest in absolute terms (2–5 percentage points for approval; 1–4 days for cycle time) but are consistently disadvantageous for all three non-reference groups.

The most important structural finding is that **rights-preserving support steps—language support and accommodation review—function as procedural penalties** rather than as neutral accommodations. The waiting times at these steps (3.8 days and 4.7 days, respectively) are the longest bottleneck waits in the process, and the resource pools serving these steps are severely undersized relative to demand. This is not discrimination in the sense of intentional differential treatment, but it is a **structural fairness failure**: the process promises support and delivers delay.

The elevated appeal rates for Groups B (+4.4 pp adjusted) and D (+5.7 pp adjusted) suggest that these groups experience the process as less fair or less comprehensible, leading to more contested outcomes. Without appeal disposition data, we cannot determine whether these appeals are well-founded, but their existence is a signal of procedural dissatisfaction.

## What the evidence does not support

The evidence **does not support a conclusion of intentional discrimination**. The observed disparities are consistent with multiple mechanisms, including resource under-investment, endogenous covariates, and possibly historical bias encoded in risk scores. The adjusted gaps, while real, could be driven by unobserved confounders that the model does not capture.

The evidence also **does not support a conclusion that the process is fair**. The residual adjusted gaps, the structural bottlenecks in support pathways, the concentration of cases in small resource pools, and the elevated appeal rates all point to procedural problems that disadvantage specific groups. These problems persist after controlling for the available measures of complexity.

## Calibrated conclusion

**The process appears procedurally unfair in specific, identifiable ways, but the overall picture is indeterminate with respect to the full extent of the disparity.** The clearest fairness failures are:

1. **Capacity-constrained support pathways** that penalize applicants for exercising their rights (language support, accommodation review). This is the most actionable finding.
2. **Resource concentration** that creates single-point-of-failure risk and amplifies individual-level variation for Groups B and C.
3. **Elevated procedural burden** (more steps, more handovers, more document requests) for Groups B and C, some of which is driven by legitimate needs and some of which is driven by inadequate intake accessibility.
4. **Endogeneity of key covariates** (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness) that makes it impossible to determine with confidence how much of the adjusted gap reflects genuine complexity vs. historical bias vs. current process unfairness.

The process is **not clearly fair, not clearly discriminatory, but clearly in need of structural reform** in the areas identified above. The most defensible interventions are those that address resource capacity and process design rather than those that alter decision criteria, because the evidence most clearly points to capacity and design as the drivers of procedural disparity.
