**Design basis.** Peak demand should not be modeled as a proportional increase in the average historical mix. Approximate weekly volumes show where the additional work actually occurs:

| Case type | Normal share | Peak share | Normal cases/week | Peak cases/week |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Routine | 61% | 42% | 1,403 | 1,386 |
| Urgent | 18% | 29% | 414 | 957 |
| Complex-panel | 13% | 20% | 299 | 660 |
| Repeat-after-failure | 8% | 9% | 184 | 297 |
| **Total** |  |  | **2,300** | **3,300** |

Thus, total volume rises about 43%, but Urgent and Complex-panel volumes more than double while Routine volume is essentially unchanged. Complex-panel orders also require multiple assays, which the order-flattened log cannot convert into machine hours.

The redesign objective is therefore not to minimize average cycle time. It is to:

- Protect the **18-hour Urgent** requirement, whose p90 is already 19 hours normally and 33 hours at peak.
- Protect the **72-hour Routine** target; Routine p90 deteriorates from 68 to 120 hours.
- Prevent silent starvation of Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure cases. Their peak p90s of 210 and 240 hours should not become accepted planning norms; clinical aging limits are needed where no formal target is supplied.
- Preserve sample quality and validation quality even when service must degrade.

The following are stress tests, not claims that each disruption occurred historically.

| ID | Demand/case-type stress | Disruption |
|---|---|---|
| **S1: Winter mix shift** | Approximately the observed peak: Urgent 29%, Complex-panel 20%, Repeat-after-failure 9%, with elevated recollection and correction work | No equipment outage; otherwise normal staffing |
| **S2: Assay degradation** | Peak mix plus an existing Routine backlog and elevated Repeat-after-failure work | M2 unavailable or severely degraded for 12–24 hours, followed by questionable night-shift/QC performance |
| **S3: Validation absence** | Complex-panel demand at or above its peak share, without requiring extreme total volume | Specialist absent on a scheduled every-second-Friday, combined with loss of an evening Medical-validation shift |
| **S4: Courier compression/delay** | A delayed route contains Urgent orders; another route carries substantial Routine and recollection volume | The 10:00 and 17:00 arrival waves effectively collapse into one compressed arrival window |
| **S5: Compound Friday incident** | Winter mix, high Complex-panel volume, and a growing repeat backlog | M2 degradation, specialist absence, and late courier arrivals occur together |

For S1–S4, the redesign should provide controlled operating modes. For S5, “acceptable” may require explicit, clinically approved degraded service; it would not be credible to promise every target when effective capacity is physically insufficient.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1: Winter mix shift

The likely sequence is:

**Intake/pre-analytical check  accession correction and preparation  assay  Technical validation  Medical validation/specialist review.**

- The fraction of pre-analytical exits going directly to Test preparation falls from **93.0% to 87.7%**. Equivalently, recollection-plus-correction paths rise from about **7.0% to 12.3%**. Request recollection  Receive sample also lengthens from **42 to 60 hours**. I6 and I8 can remove intake delays, but unless they reduce defects rather than merely process them faster, they release more good cases into an already constrained downstream system.
- Preparation and assay then become critical: peak utilization is 89% and 98%, with median queues of 11 and 18 hours. The two Batch-wait edges sum to **15 hours normally versus 36 hours at peak**, while Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%.
- Strict I1 would protect some Urgent orders but starve Routine and Complex-panel work. Larger Routine batches under I2 can create a discharge bow wave. Fixed I3 can appear to relieve M1 while increasing M2 repeats and total assay work.
- Clearing assay does not clear the process. Technical validation is at **101% peak utilization** with a 16-hour queue. Medical validation has a 22-hour queue and specialist review a 36-hour queue. Hold-for-specialist-review arcs increase from roughly **36 per week normally to 203 per week at peak**. Complex-panel cases are additionally 3.1 times more likely to need Medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist hold.
- Consequently, I8 alone moves the bottleneck downstream; I4 can worsen Technical validation; and I5 can clear direct Medical-validation work while leaving Complex-panel cases stuck in specialist review.

### S2: Assay degradation or outage

The sequence becomes:

**M2 effective-capacity loss/repeats  preparation and prepared-sample WIP  M1/remaining assay capacity  Technical validation  Medical/specialist validation during recovery.**

Repeat arcs represent about **6.7% of recorded Assay-run exits normally and 14.8% at peak**. Repeats consume assay capacity again, and Repeat assay  Assay run lengthens from 10 to 26 hours. The M2/night-shift clustering is a diagnostic signal, not proof of causation; machine, assay, lot, and shift must be separated before rerouting everything.

Fixed I3 fails in this scenario: Routine work assigned to M2 stalls, and rerouting it all to M1 destroys the protected Urgent/Complex-panel lane. Conversely, upstream automation or triage provides little benefit and can increase prepared-sample WIP. When the machine returns, clearing the assay backlog too aggressively produces a recovery bow wave at Technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization, followed by Medical validation and specialist review.

### S3: Medical-validator or specialist absence

Here, upstream stations can look relatively healthy while released outcomes deteriorate:

**Technical-validation output  Medical-validation queue or specialist hold  return Medical validation  Release.**

Technical validation  Medical validation increases from **8 to 24 hours**, Medical validation  Release from **2 to 5 hours**, and the two specialist-hold edges sum to **24 hours normally versus 60 hours at peak**.

More urgent-first assay dispatch, faster registration, or larger preparation batches merely creates more unreleased WIP. I5 helps cases waiting directly for Medical validation but cannot clear specialist holds. I10 can reduce the local specialist queue, but it moves work into a 12-hour transport leg, partner accession/review, and return-result reconciliation. If local Medical validation remains required, I10 does not solve a Medical-validator absence.

Average utilization is particularly misleading here: specialist review can have a 36-hour queue despite 87% peak utilization because absences and arrival patterns are not visible in the period average.

### S4: Courier compression or delay

The likely migration is:

**Courier intake  Registration/pre-analytical check  accession correction  preparation/assay  late-shift Technical and Medical validation.**

Register order  Receive sample increases from 5 to 8 hours at peak, while Receive sample  Pre-analytical check increases from 2 to 9 hours. A compressed wave can therefore first overload intake and then create a second wave downstream.

Long clerk overtime is not a robust response: errors rise after nine-hour shifts, creating more corrections and recollections. Recollection also consumes intake capacity because clerks explain errors to physicians. I8 and I6 can flush the compressed wave rapidly into preparation and assay; I2 can then hold that wave in Batch wait. If processing catches up late in the evening, output may arrive when Technical/Medical validation coverage is thin and when night-shift repeat risk is higher.

I7 can smooth **future** arrivals but cannot recover time already lost by a delayed Urgent sample. An Urgent order requiring recollection may be unable to meet 18 hours even with immediate downstream priority and requires early clinical communication.

### S5: Compound incident

There is no stable single bottleneck. Relieving intake exposes assay; reserving M1 exposes Routine aging; restoring assay exposes Technical validation; clearing Medical validation leaves specialist review; outsourcing exposes transport and traceability.

The operating objective must therefore change from local throughput to **controlled, visible degradation**: preserve QC and sample integrity, protect feasible Urgent paths, maintain minimum Complex-panel and Routine progress, and prevent recovery actions from creating successive downstream bow waves.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The target flow is: **capacity-based courier slotting and digital pre-registration  receipt with early defect screening  finite-capacity preparation and dynamic assay routing  Technical-validation pacing  coordinated Medical/specialist validation  continuous release**, with case-age control across all steps.

### R1. Level and clean intake: combine I7, I8, and I6

- Use I7 to slot routine courier arrivals against demonstrated receipt **and downstream** capacity, with exceptions for clinically urgent or stability-limited samples.
- Use I8 only for complete, validated digital metadata. Incomplete orders enter a visible exception queue rather than becoming downstream accession corrections. Pre-registration while samples are in transit can remove avoidable receipt work.
- Implement I6 as a resequenced receipt screen, not a duplicate full check. Separate metadata problems from physical sample defects, initiate standard physician communication and recollection arrangements immediately, and feed defect patterns back to collection sites and routes.
- Do not pace sample registration or defect detection. If downstream constraints are red, pace only the release of suitable, stable Routine work into preparation rather than building an unlimited prepared-sample buffer.

**Why robust:** It addresses both S1 defect amplification and S4 arrival variability and reduces avoidable load in other scenarios.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** Digital metadata must actually be complete; screening must have high clinical specificity; courier slots must be changeable without violating sample stability. False rejection can increase recollection, cancellation, and physician workload. Smoothing arrivals into nights can simply move the problem to validation. Faster clean intake can overload assay and Technical validation unless R2 is active.

### R2. Replace strict priority and fixed batching with finite-capacity, age-aware dispatch

Modify I1 and I2 rather than deploying them as standing rules:

- Reserve short, non-preemptive Urgent capacity windows based on forecast Urgent demand and remaining time to 18 hours. Do not interrupt an assay already running.
- Use small Urgent-compatible preparation batches where necessary, but do not maximize bypass percentage at the expense of excessive setup fragmentation.
- Permit Routine batches of 24 only when they can be filled without holding samples, no Urgent/Complex-panel case is put at predictable risk, sample stability permits the wait, and downstream validation can absorb the output. Otherwise revert to 12 or smaller batches.
- Give Complex-panel work a reserved path through its multiple assays and required validation, not merely a high priority at one machine.
- Add an aging escape and minimum-throughput guard for Routine and Complex-panel work. Repeat-after-failure work should follow clinical urgency and QC requirements, not receive either automatic top priority or automatic lowest priority.
- Base dispatch on **latest safe start**, using tail estimates of remaining assay and validation time, rather than a single permanent class ranking.

**Why robust:** It protects Urgent work without assuming Routine and Complex-panel work can be deferred indefinitely.

**Failure modes:** Excessive reservations idle capacity; insufficient reservations recreate Urgent misses; a late aging escape can discharge a large starved backlog into validation. Reserves therefore need daily adjustment and gradual release of aged work.

### R3. Use dynamic machine routing and attack repeat load

Modify I3 into a reversible routing preference:

- Route only compatible assays, using each machine’s **effective good-output capacity**—runtime, availability, and repeat behavior—rather than M2’s nominal speed.
- M1 may temporarily protect Urgent/Complex-panel capacity, but fixed dedication ends when M2 is unhealthy or M1 itself becomes the single point of failure.
- Stratify repeats by machine, assay, lot, and night shift. Confirm QC before rerunning, quarantine suspect work where required, and target maintenance or competency review at the actual failure mode. Include expected reruns in the assay schedule.
- During recovery, release nonurgent backlog in controlled lots that Technical and Medical validation can absorb.

**Why robust:** It reduces assay load itself and avoids converting an M2 problem into an M1 congestion problem.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** Assay transferability and reliable machine/shift QC data are required. Protecting M1 concentrates critical demand on M1, and unnecessary QC holds or routing restrictions can reduce capacity further.

### R4. Preserve the validation pacer and make staffing flexibility conditional

- Do **not** implement I4 as a standing peak transfer. Moving Technical validators into accession correction when Technical validation is at 101% merely exchanges one backlog for another.
- Complete the four-week cross-training as an option, but deploy staff to accession only under the dual-queue trigger in Section 4. Corrections should be released downstream in manageable lots because they unblock preparation and assay work.
- Assess whether a small, competency-checked backup block for Technical validation can be created before winter using suitable staff and the stated cross-training lead time. This is a targeted constraint option, not capacity addition everywhere. Its output must be coordinated with Medical/specialist capacity.
- Pre-authorize I5, but activate the evening Medical-validation shift only when there is actual or forecast Medical-validation work for it to clear.
- Do not adopt I9 as a standing policy. Release work is not identified as the primary constraint, while Routine p90 is already 120 hours at peak. Holding Routine releases would hide WIP and create later release bursts.

**Failure modes:** Accession and Technical validation may both be saturated, leaving no safe transfer. Extra Technical-validation capacity can overload Medical validation, while an evening Medical shift can be starved by Technical validation or unable to clear specialist holds. Fatigue and competency must be monitored.

### R5. Manage end-to-end age and WIP, not station output

Maintain one operational view showing, by case: class, age, remaining target time, assays still required, current queue, machine/QC status, validation requirements, and any outsourced location. Use it to limit Routine prepared WIP and to detect starvation.

Keep I10 commercially and operationally ready but dormant until triggered. The partner agreement must specify assay scope, QC, technical/medical validation responsibilities, result interfaces, and chain of custody.

**Assumptions and failure modes:** Forecasts initially lack tube- and assay-level workload data. WIP limits must never override sample-stability or clinical requirements. A dashboard without enforced decision rights becomes another reporting exercise.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

The thresholds below are starting operating bands, to be recalibrated against hourly staffed capacity. A failed QC condition or predicted contractual breach overrides a green median queue.

| Policy | Switch-on trigger | Actions and downstream guard | Switch-off/de-escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1 winter-mix surge** | Rolling three-day Urgent+Complex share 45%, or their absolute run-rate >1.5× normal, **plus** assay/validation capacity forecast saturation or Assay/Medical queue >8h | Activate age-aware reserves; disable unrestricted 24-sample batching; keep I4 off if Technical queue >8h; use I5 only if its own trigger is met | After 48h with mix/load below trigger, Assay, Technical, and Medical queues <6h, and at-risk WIP no longer growing; reduce reserves gradually |
| **S2 machine degradation** | M2 unavailable/degraded, failed QC, or machine/shift repeat rate >1.5× its own baseline on an adequate sample; Assay queue >8h confirms congestion | Freeze fixed I3 and large Routine batches; quarantine suspect work; route compatible assays by effective capacity; protect feasible critical work; pace recovery output to Technical/Medical validation | Only after QC acceptance and repeat performance near baseline for three shifts, with Assay queue <6h for 24h; restore Routine routing gradually |
| **S3 validation absence** | Known roster absence; Medical queue >8h with at-risk WIP; or specialist queue >12h and rising/predicted local review delay >24h | Activate I5 for direct Medical-validation backlog. Use I10 only when transport plus partner turnaround plus return processing is expected to beat local remaining delay and meet clinical constraints | I5 off after Medical queue <4h for two shifts and at-risk WIP is cleared. I10 off after specialist queue <8h for 48h and all partner WIP is reconciled |
| **S4 courier compression/delay** | Forecast two-hour arrivals >120% of demonstrated receipt/pre-analytical capacity, route delay >2h with Urgent orders aboard, or Receive-to-check queue >4h | Smooth future slots with I7; pre-register in transit; use receipt defect triage; avoid prolonged clerk overtime; use small downstream batches and align late output with validation coverage | After two arrival windows within capacity, pre-analytical queue <4h, and the delayed downstream wave has cleared—not merely when courier intake looks normal |
| **Guarded I4 accession flex** | Accession queue >8h and blocked WIP rising, **while** Technical queue <6h, Technical WIP is not at risk, and validation coverage remains adequate | Move at most one trained validator for a defined block; monitor correction quality; release unblocked cases in controlled lots | Return immediately if Technical queue >8h or critical Technical WIP emerges; otherwise stop when accession queue <4h |
| **S5 compound degraded mode** | Any two scenario-level red triggers | Freeze I2, fixed I3, I4 diversion, and I9. Preserve QC/sample integrity first, then feasible Urgent paths, with explicit Complex/Routine aging guards. Use overflow only if transport and partner capacity are reliable | Remove one restriction at a time after its own trigger clears; do not simultaneously release all accumulated backlogs |

If all targets cannot be met, Routine is the deliberate flex class only until its 72-hour risk requires escalation. Complex-panel work cannot be deferred silently, and clinically necessary repeats or validation steps cannot be skipped.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Technical validation remains a structural constraint.** At 101% peak utilization, scheduling and intake improvements cannot guarantee a stable process. If suitable backup competency cannot be created, simultaneous accession and Technical-validation overload has no safe internal flex solution.
- **The evidence is insufficient for precise capacity sizing.** The log is flattened by lab order and hides tube counts, multiple assay durations, machine compatibility, and sample-stability constraints. Period frequencies also include WIP/censoring: peak Batch wait has 1,180 entries but only 1,030 exits. Median queues do not describe the oldest cases.
- **Recollection remains largely outside laboratory control.** Two-hour triage does not eliminate the 60-hour peak recollection-return edge. False rejects, physician response delays, or courier problems can turn an intake improvement into more cancellations and explanation work.
- **The redesign itself can create oscillation and starvation.** Excess Urgent reservation can starve Routine/Complex-panel work; the aging escape can then release a large bow wave into validation. Similarly, WIP gates can age prepared samples, while automation can feed assay faster than validation can absorb. Small release lots, hysteresis, and oldest-WIP monitoring are therefore essential.
- **New concentration risks remain.** Protecting critical work on M1 makes M1 availability more important. Evening validation creates fatigue risk, and I10 creates dependence on partner performance, transport, interfaces, and explicit local validation responsibilities.
- **Compound disruptions can still be infeasible.** In S5, some Urgent penalties or Routine target breaches may be unavoidable. The residual control is transparent prioritization and early communication—not hidden WIP, excessive overtime, or omitted QC.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Review the dashboard daily during peaks, but refresh arrival, queue, QC, and at-risk-WIP tiles every 2–4 hours. Show current median queue **and oldest WIP**; a green median must not override a red at-risk case.

### Lagging outcome metrics

Use these to evaluate the redesign, not as the sole triggers for intervention:

- Urgent percentage released after 18 hours, penalties, and median/p90 cycle time; reference p90 is 19 hours normally and 33 at peak.
- Routine percentage exceeding 72 hours and p90; Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure p90 and oldest-case age.
- Recollection cancellations, overall rework, and percentage of cases experiencing any queue over 12 hours. References are 7.5%/11.8% rework and 22%/54% long-queue incidence in normal/peak periods.
- Partner door-to-door turnaround and post-recovery backlog clearance, reported separately so outsourcing or machine recovery cannot make local performance appear artificially healthy.
