# 2. Integrated explanation

The most plausible account combines changing assignments, some warm-up, and an increasing speed–quality tradeoff under pressure. Early shifts inherit difficult work: complexity averages 0.72 in the first five positions, and seniors deliberately take complex carryover. These events should take longer even for fully alert, proficient workers. Their 18.8-minute service time therefore cannot be treated as a pure lack of practice. The decrease in both service time and errors through positions 16–30 is consistent with warm-up, but easier assignments and stabilization of backlog could produce part of the same pattern.

By positions 31–50, service averages 12.9 minutes and errors remain near the earlier minimum, at 2.7%. Beyond that point, service continues falling while errors and rework increase sharply. At positions 76+, service is 10.8 minutes, mean queue length is 88, errors are 7.9%, and seven-day rework is 13.7%. Meanwhile, complexity and the high-priority share continue to decline. Easier cases plausibly explain part of the speed gain, but they do not make the late deterioration in quality reassuring.

The WIP table strengthens the pressure explanation: high WIP is associated with roughly 25–26% shorter service within each broad complexity band, accompanied by much higher error rates. Experience modifies the association: juniors have 4.4% later low-WIP errors versus 12.4% later high-WIP errors, while seniors show 2.2% versus 4.9%. These are not causal treatment effects, but they suggest that the system's demands exceed available checking or decision capacity, particularly for less experienced workers. Pressure may encourage abbreviated review, easier-case selection, or rapid document batches. Rework then adds future demand, potentially reinforcing the pressure that contributed to it.

Fatigue could amplify these responses by reducing attention or the ability to resist rushing. However, the supplied resource-group figures show little later quality deterioration under low WIP for several groups. They do not provide the matched, late low-pressure observations needed to isolate fatigue. Clock time also matters as a possible mechanism, but the Day shift's late demand peak, Night's different staff and urgent cases, and the changing backlog prevent a direct circadian interpretation of shift averages.

Finally, recorded event duration and error timing need careful interpretation. Bulk completion of Document Review can create very short durations without proportionate reductions in total labor. Quality Check deferred to the last 45 minutes can make earlier defects appear as late-shift errors. Bot decisions may shift effort into human verification and corrections. Thus faster recorded handling can reflect genuine proficiency, easier work, efficient batching, or compressed checking; rising errors occur particularly when those changes coincide with high workload, vulnerable assignments, or delayed detection. The relevant operational outcome is timely, correct resolution including downstream work, rather than event speed alone.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Establish trustworthy event and outcome definitions.** Parse timestamps with the center's local time zone, retain overnight shifts across midnight, and validate starts, completions, duplicates, missing values, zero durations, and overlapping events. Define event service as recorded completion minus start, while distinguishing elapsed duration from actual active labor. Verify whether sequence position is resource-specific or shared across the shift; construct a resource–shift sequence if necessary and distinguish start order from completion order. Define elapsed shift time from the event start for explanatory models, so it precedes the outcome. Verify that WIP and queue measurements refer to the relevant work pool immediately before handling begins, with a consistent convention for the current case.

Separate human events, bot events, and end-to-end cases. Identify carryover using case arrival and earlier events, rather than just first-hour position. Audit complexity scores to ensure they were available before the decision and were not revised using eventual errors. Check whether the table's “cases/events” counts represent cases or events. Event error rates, case error rates, repeated activities, and seven-day rework require different denominators.

Record the activity and resource that originated a defect separately from the activity, time, and resource that detected or corrected it. Do not attribute every Rework Correction or QC detection to the correcting worker. Where this linkage is unavailable, state the uncertainty and analyze detection timing separately.

**Within-resource, within-shift comparisons.** Compare the same resource performing the same activity on cases with comparable complexity, priority, and workload. Use resource effects to absorb stable worker differences and shift-instance effects where appropriate to compare changes within a particular worker's shift. Plot adjusted curves over elapsed time and sequence position, with sample counts and uncertainty. Sparse late bins should not be given the same evidential weight as well-supported earlier bins.

Sequence position is partly an outcome: faster workers and batched processing reach higher positions, and only certain workers or shifts contribute to position 76+. Analyze fixed elapsed-time windows as well as event order; show which workers contribute to each bin. Use cumulative prior events within an activity family to assess practice, and cumulative prior work or time since a break to assess fatigue when such measurements are available. Examine subsequent shifts to distinguish retained learning from a daily warm-up effect. Sequence, cumulative work, and elapsed time are strongly correlated, so fit alternative specifications and avoid claiming that their independent effects are identified when overlap is insufficient.

**Model speed and quality separately, then interpret them together.** A suitable starting specification for positive-duration human events is:

\[
\log(S_i)=\alpha_{r(i)}+\delta_{a(i)}+\gamma_{\text{date/shift type}(i)}
 +f(T_i)+g(C_i)+h(W_i)+q(Q_i)+c(H_i)+\beta^\top Z_i+\epsilon_i,
\]

where \(S\) is recorded service time, \(T\) elapsed shift time, \(C\) pre-event complexity, \(W\) WIP, \(Q\) queue length, \(H\) local clock time, and \(Z\) contains priority, carryover, and relevant routing information. Use flexible functions rather than assuming linear changes. Fit event-error and appropriately defined downstream-rework models with analogous predictors and a logistic or time-to-event framework. Compare elapsed-time specifications with sequence/practice specifications rather than relying on one overfilled model. For zero-duration human events, audit recording practices or use a separate duration model; automated events need their own analysis.

Include prespecified interactions for elapsed time × workload, complexity × workload, and experience × workload. An elapsed-time deterioration under comparable low workload would strengthen the fatigue account; a high-workload deterioration across different elapsed times would strengthen the pressure account. Their interaction would support a combined mechanism. Account for dependence among events from the same case and resource/shift through an appropriate hierarchical model or clustered uncertainty estimates.

Stable experience differences are absorbed by resource fixed effects, so a cross-worker experience main effect cannot also be interpreted independently in that specification. Experience–workload interactions can be estimated if workers experience sufficiently varied workload. Use a hierarchical alternative or longitudinal experience changes for other experience questions, stating the additional assumptions.

**Stratify workload and adjust case mix.** First reproduce WIP bands, then examine continuous WIP and queue curves, including sensitivity around 70 rather than assuming a causal discontinuity. Report low, medium, and high workload results within activity, fine complexity ranges, experience group, and elapsed-time bands. Model WIP and queue separately as well as jointly where there is sufficient independent variation; do not interpret unstable coefficients from highly collinear measures.

Standardize early and late predictions to the same case mix and workload distribution, and restrict matched comparisons to common support. Also show actual operational distributions: standardization explains differences, while actual distributions reveal where harm occurs. Include weekday/weekend carryover, routing, age of case, and staffing or capacity where observed. WIP per available qualified worker may be more informative than a center-wide count.

**Separate clock time from elapsed time where possible.** For example, at 09:00 an Early worker is three hours into the shift while a Day worker is just starting. Overlapping schedules provide useful comparisons, subject to different workers, assignments, and workload. Clock time equals shift start plus elapsed time, so these are not fully independent within a fixed schedule. Night_L and Night_M do not by themselves provide a within-worker daytime comparison. Use schedule rotations if available; otherwise report circadian effects as only partly identifiable. A clock-time spline in a regression cannot create missing comparisons.

**Analyze batching and process paths.** Discover candidate batches and inspect their timing, resources, activities, and available batch identifiers. The listed schema has no explicit queue-batch ID, so membership in the same queue batch requires additional logging or manual validation. Compare Document Review runs with non-batch work of similar difficulty, distinguishing efficient shared setup from abbreviated review or bulk timestamping. Avoid counting overlapping recorded intervals as distinct active labor without validation.

Use performance directly-follows graphs to examine where delays and rework accumulate, especially Risk Classification → Quality Check/Rework Correction and delayed QC near shift end. A directly-follows edge describes recorded case order; it does not establish causation, and its duration is not automatically queue waiting. Inspect start/end intervals and readiness rules before interpreting a transition as waiting. Use dotted charts to identify carryover, simultaneous completions, reviewer bursts, and handovers. Exploratory clustering can reveal case routes, but clusters must not be labeled “fatigue” or “poor worker” without independent evidence.

**Analyze downstream quality and detection.** Link original decisions to confirmed errors, subsequent corrections, and time to correct closure. Define seven-day rework precisely—for example, a correction within seven days after the index resolution—and count cases once for case-level rates. Use only index resolutions with a complete seven-day observation window, or explicitly handle censoring. Repeated activity counts are a useful process signal but do not distinguish necessary repetition from an error and do not implement a seven-day outcome.

Compare full case cycle time and total human effort including rework, not only the first service event. Examine whether high-pressure bot routes require more corrections and whether those corrections explain later queues. Since the chance of detecting an error may change with QC coverage, use consistent audits or randomized audit samples across workload and shift strata. Report QC coverage and detection delay alongside error rates.

For interventions, use a feasible randomized or staggered pilot at the worker-shift or shared-queue level. # 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level comparisons can reverse after accounting for assignments. Seniors receive difficult carryover, night staff handle urgent exceptions, and junior document batches have different timestamp behavior. Workers also differ in how often their work is checked and who records corrections. Resource effects and complexity adjustment reduce some distortions but do not guarantee fair attribution: missing case difficulty and limited comparison overlap remain important.

Measurement errors can create apparent performance differences. Bulk timestamps, concurrent cases, bot zero durations, missing starts, and uncertainty about whether an error flag describes creation or detection all affect interpretation. A meticulous checker may record more defects precisely because they prevent harm. Verify comparable QC coverage before treating lower observed error rates as better quality, and allow time for seven-day outcomes to mature.

Queue pressure is partly a management outcome: staffing, targets, routing, batch policies, and scheduled QC determine workers' available capacity. Do not interpret the response to an overloaded system as an individual character flaw. Similarly, track automated decision quality and review requirements so human workers are not held responsible merely for correcting Bot_Risk. A correction is evidence of recovery as well as potential upstream failure.

Use identifiable worker information only where needed for supportive operational action. Default shared dashboards to groups with adequate sample sizes, display uncertainty, protect access, and avoid performance league tables. Let workers explain unusual assignments and contest erroneous attribution. Randomization must respect competence and urgency constraints, and break or staffing pilots should assess who inherits displaced work.

Raw late-shift error rates do not justify punishment. Any individual review requires validated origin attribution, comparable exposure, adequate observations, and operational context. Improvements should reduce defects and pressure without creating incentives to hide mistakes, avoid complex work, or delay quality checks beyond the reporting window.
