# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Let time zero be the `Submit` event. Let (H=72) hours. For terminal rows, `duration_or_age_h` is the elapsed event time; for `open` rows it is the administrative observation age at the common cutoff. Let (T_R) denote time to an internally observed `Resolve`, and let (D) denote the first observed internal terminal disposition, with causes Resolve ((R)), Withdraw ((W)), Reject ((J)), and Transfer ((X)). An open case is right-censored at its observed age. A transfer is not automatically ordinary censoring: it is an observed handoff, but its external outcome is missing.

I would report these related estimands separately:

1. **Internal SLA non-resolution (breach) at 72 hours:**
   [
   B_{72}=P(T_R>72).
   ]
   This is the operational probability that no internal `Resolve` has occurred by the SLA horizon. It includes a case that is still active at 72, a case that eventually resolves after 72, and a case with an observed non-resolution disposition before 72. It is the right estimand for an internal SLA dashboard, subject to the stated transfer convention. Its complement is the cumulative incidence of internal resolution by 72, not the probability of eventual resolution.

2. **No internal terminal disposition at 72 hours:**
   [
   A_{72}=P(T_D>72).
   ]
   This is a workload/backlog estimand: the case has not yet resolved, withdrawn, been rejected, or transferred at the horizon. A transfer before 72 therefore removes a case from the internal active queue even though it does not establish an external resolution.

3. **Late-resolution cumulative incidence:** for a later analysis time (t>72),
   [
   L_{72,t}=P(72<T_Rleq t).
   ]
   This measures the marginal mass of resolutions that miss the SLA and subsequently resolve. It is useful for recovery workload and customer-experience analysis. It should be reported together with competing withdrawal, rejection, and transfer risks. If the operational question is instead “among cases still active and observed at 72, what is the chance of eventual resolution?”, use a 72-hour landmark conditional estimand, not the unconditional (L_{72,t}).

For competing-risk analysis, the resolution cumulative incidence is (F_R(h)=P(T_Rleq h,	ext{cause}=R)), and (B_{72}=1-F_R(72)) under the internal-resolution definition. Withdrawals, rejections, and transfers are competing causes, not independent censoring. Open cases below 72 are right-censored before the horizon, so their value at 72 is unknown. A terminal resolution after 72 and an open case already aged beyond 72 are observed SLA breaches, not censored observations for the 72-hour question.

The requested examples contribute as follows:

| Case pattern | (B_{72}): no internal Resolve by 72 | (A_{72}): no internal terminal disposition by 72 | (L_{72,t}) / post-72 analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolve at 76 hours, e.g. S03 | 1: an observed SLA breach | 1 at 72, because its disposition occurs later | 1 late-resolution event at 76 |
| Open at age 92, e.g. S09 | 1: no Resolve by 72 is known | 1: it is still active at 72 | Right-censored at age 92; it is at risk at 72 but has no observed post-92 outcome |
| Withdraw at 27, e.g. S06 | 1 for the success-by-Resolve estimand | 0: a terminal disposition occurred before 72 | A competing non-resolution event at 27; it is not a late resolution |
| Transfer at 68, e.g. S08 | 1 for the internal-SLA interpretation; unknown/censored if the target is eventual external resolution | 0: Transfer is an observed internal handoff before 72 | An (X) competing event, or censoring at 68 under an external-resolution censoring scenario; no observed late Resolve |

The data contain 13 observed resolutions, of which 9 are at or below 72 and 4 are late (S03, S13, S20, and S28). That descriptive count is not by itself an estimate of (F_R(72)), because it ignores the 10 other terminal outcomes, the 9 open cases, and unequal follow-up.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The following is the primary internal-system encoding. `C` means right censoring, not a failure. A terminal event after 72 remains an event at its actual time; at the 72-hour horizon it is simply still beyond the horizon and therefore represents no timely Resolve.

| Observed status | Event indicator and time | Competing-risk code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | (Delta_R=1) at `duration_or_age_h`; `breach_by_72` is 0 if time (leq72), otherwise 1 | (R) | Never censored | Analyze late Resolve separately; do not recode a late Resolve as a censoring event |
| `withdrawn` | (Delta_{	ext{nonresolve}}=1) at its observed time; it is a breach for success-by-Resolve | (W) | Never censored | Keep (W) as a competing cause; alternatively use a composite non-Resolve failure for a purely operational failure rate |
| `rejected` | (Delta_{	ext{nonresolve}}=1) at its observed time; it is a breach for success-by-Resolve | (J) | Never censored | Keep (J) separate; alternatively use the composite failure outcome |
| `transferred` | No internal (R) is observed; its event time is the transfer time under an internal disposition model | (X) if transfer is a competing handoff cause | Not censored in the primary internal disposition model | Treat as a non-resolution failure, censor at transfer for an eventual-resolution target, or use bounds/imputation for unknown external outcomes |
| `open` with age (<72) | No event observed by its age | `C` at age | Right-censored before the SLA horizon; `breach_by_72` is unknown | IPCW, selection-model sensitivity, or best/worst-case imputation; do not silently call it a breach |
| `open` with age (geq72) | No event by the horizon; censor later at its cutoff age for survival beyond the horizon | `C` at age | It is known to be an SLA breach and active at 72, while still right-censored for any later event | Use as a post-72 risk-set member; do not discard it as an incomplete completed case |

Transfers need at least three explicit conventions:

- **Transfer as internal failure:** code (X) as a non-resolution failure. This answers whether the internal service produced a Resolve and is conservative for an internal SLA report.
- **Transfer as competing handoff:** retain (X) as its own competing cause. This does not claim that the external outcome is bad; it estimates the probability of internal Resolve versus withdrawal, rejection, transfer, and remaining under observation. It is the preferred primary convention when reporting internal process outcomes.
- **Transfer as right censoring:** censor at the handoff time when the target is eventual resolution in any channel and external follow-up is assumed conditionally independent of the unobserved outcome. This assumption is doubtful here because transfer is a process decision associated with escalation.

An additional bounding analysis can assign every transferred case an external failure or an external success by the relevant deadline. Those are not estimates of reality; they are bounds or scenario analyses. For the active-internal-disposition estimand (A_{72}), Transfer remains a disposition at its observed time under all three transfer conventions.

For the binary horizon variable, cases with `resolved` after 72, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and open age at least 72 are known non-Resolve-by-72 cases. A transfer before 72 is known not to have an internal Resolve, but its value is unknown if “resolution” means an external eventual resolution rather than an internal event. This distinction should be visible in the data dictionary and in the reported denominator.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Analyzing only the 13 completed `resolved` cases estimates a selected quantity, not the SLA risk of all submitted cases. Among those 13, 9 resolved within 72 and 4 did not, so the completed-case late fraction is (4/13). That calculation excludes all 4 withdrawals, 3 rejections, 3 transfers, and 9 open cases. It can make a process with many unresolved or externally handed-off cases look artificially successful.

- **Survivor/selection bias:** S09, S10, S11, S15, S24, S25, S26, S29, and S31 are absent from a completed-resolution denominator. S11 and S26 are already open beyond 72, and therefore directly demonstrate tail risk that a resolved-only analysis cannot see.
- **Informative censoring:** The open records are not necessarily random administrative censoring. S11 has a 55-hour last-event gap and S26 a 72-hour gap; long inactivity can be related to a stalled case or to a missing event feed. Transfer is even more concerning: S08, S21, and S32 are routed out after escalation, and that routing is plausibly associated with difficulty, priority, or external capacity. Censoring such cases as though it were independent can bias resolution estimates.
- **Competing risks:** S05 and S23 are rejected early, and S06, S12, S22, and S30 are withdrawn. These cases cannot subsequently appear as internally resolved in the same process. Censoring them and then applying ordinary Kaplan-Meier to resolution treats them as if they remained at risk for the same resolution process, usually inflating the apparent resolution probability. Their causes are operational outcomes in their own right.
- **Cohort and policy imbalance:** Cohort B has 17 cases versus 15 in A and has 5 P1 cases versus 3 in A. B also has 12 prefixes with `AutoCheck` immediately after `Submit`, whereas A has none with that exact immediate order. Cohort B follows a policy change, but assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete; a cohort contrast mixes policy, case mix, assignment, calendar, and implementation effects.
- **Open below versus above the SLA:** S10 and S15 in A, and S24, S29, and S31 in B, are open but younger than 72. Their eventual 72-hour status is not observed and they should be censored at their ages for a horizon estimate. In contrast, S09 and S11 in A, and S25 and S26 in B, are open at ages beyond 72 and are known active breaches at the horizon. Treating all nine open rows alike either invents breaches for the younger cases or hides breaches for the older ones.

An already observed late Resolve, such as S13 at 110 hours, is not censoring: it is a known breach at 72 and a later resolution event. Similarly, a non-Resolve terminal event is not missing merely because it is not a success.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Kaplan-Meier.** Kaplan-Meier is appropriate for time to *any observed terminal disposition* if open cases are plausibly non-informatively censored. For time to Resolve, treating withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as censoring estimates a hypothetical cause-specific survival quantity under a strong assumption; it is not the marginal probability of resolution in the presence of competing outcomes. I would not use a resolution-only Kaplan-Meier curve as the primary SLA result.

**Aalen-Johansen and cumulative incidence.** Use Aalen-Johansen to estimate (F_R(72)), (F_W(72)), (F_J(72)), and (F_X(72)), with open cases censored at their observed ages. The CIFs sum with the event-free probability and directly answer how much of the population has reached each operational outcome by the horizon. Report (1-F_R(72)) as internal non-resolution, with the transfer convention stated. A post-72 late-resolution curve can be obtained from a 72-hour landmark or from differences in CIFs at later times, provided the risk-set and competing-event definitions are explicit.

**Cause-specific hazards.** Estimate separate hazards for Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer. These are useful for identifying where cases are exiting or resolving at each elapsed time and for modeling stage-specific operational interventions. A cause-specific hazard ratio is not a marginal probability ratio and is not a causal policy effect.

**Subdistribution hazards.** A Fine-Gray model can relate covariates directly to the cumulative incidence of resolution while retaining competing-event cases in a modified risk set. It is a secondary descriptive model here, not the primary result: (n=32), several causes are sparse, and the modified risk-set interpretation is less natural for queue operations.

**Restricted mean time.** Report restricted mean time to any terminal disposition, or cause-specific restricted mean time with an explicitly stated competing-risk estimand, up to a follow-up limit supported by the data. RMST can be easier to communicate than unstable tail hazards. It cannot repair informative transfer censoring or extrapolate beyond the observed support.

**Landmarking.** At the 72-hour landmark, define the eligible population as cases observed and without an earlier terminal disposition, then model post-72 resolution or continued active status using information available at 72: age, current stage, escalation status, rework history, and recent inactivity. Open cases younger than 72 are not eligible for that landmark because their 72-hour state has not been observed; they remain in the primary pre-horizon risk table. Landmarking prevents future process events from being used to predict an earlier outcome.

**Multi-state model.** With exact event timestamps, model transitions among process states and terminal outcomes, for example Submit/Screen/AutoCheck, Review, Rework, Escalated, Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer, and an external-unknown state. This separates time spent in a stage from the probability of reaching a stage. The present extract gives activity order and total duration/age but not timestamps for intermediate events, so stage dwell hazards cannot be estimated credibly from it. The prefixes can support a descriptive state graph and variant features now, with transition-time modeling deferred until timestamps are available.

My primary analysis would therefore be an Aalen-Johansen competing-risk report for the internal process: resolution CIF by 72, cause-specific CIFs for withdrawal, rejection, and transfer, and the event-free active probability. Open cases are right-censored at their ages, while transfers are retained as (X). The primary operational breach is (1-F_R(72)), supplemented by (A_{72}) for backlog. The secondary analysis would be a 72-hour landmark plus cause-specific hazards and RMST, repeated under transfer-failure and transfer-censoring sensitivities. With 32 observations, I would use descriptive estimates, bootstrap or exact/appropriate small-sample intervals, and avoid a heavily parameterized regression model.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Useful features are functions of the observed history at the time a prediction is made:

- **Baseline or pre-process:** priority, cohort, intake date once available, and any routing assignment known at Submit. `AutoCheck-first` is not baseline merely because it appears in the row; it becomes usable only after that routing decision, or it requires a delayed-entry/time-varying design.
- **Current stage:** the last activity in the observed prefix (`Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Rework`, or `Escalate`) is time-varying. For an open case it is available at cutoff, but not necessarily at every earlier time. Current stage should be paired with elapsed age and a risk set that conditions on having reached that stage.
- **Repeated review and rework:** review count, whether `Review>Review` occurs, `rework_count`, and the number of returns from Rework to Review are process-history variables. They are post-Submit and therefore time-varying or landmark covariates, not safe baseline predictors. In this extract, the rework paths include S07, S13, and S27.
- **Escalation:** an `Escalate` indicator and time since escalation are time-varying. The prefix shows whether escalation occurred, but the time of escalation is not supplied, so escalation timing and a post-escalation hazard cannot be calculated from these rows without inventing timestamps.
- **Path pattern and variant:** the full prefix or a compressed path class can capture AutoCheck-first versus Screen-first, escalation before Review, and Review/Rework loops. It is a useful descriptive stratifier or a time-aware history feature, but using the completed full prefix as a baseline predictor leaks future events and terminal outcomes.
- **Inactivity:** `last_event_gap_h` can be a time-varying freshness/stall feature at the observation or scoring time. It must not be used as a baseline feature, and terminal rows have gap zero by construction. For open cases, a large gap may be either genuine inactivity or an extraction failure, so it also belongs in data-quality monitoring.

A proposed transition graph, expressed without assigning unobserved timestamps, is:

`Submit → Screen ↔ AutoCheck → Review → Resolve`

`Review → Rework → Review; Review → Escalate; Escalate → Review; Escalate → Resolve`

`Screen/AutoCheck/Review → Withdraw or Reject; Escalate → Transfer → External outcome unknown`

The observed prefixes show both `Screen→AutoCheck` and `AutoCheck→Screen`, Review loops, Rework loops, escalation, and terminal exits. In a real multi-state model, each arrow would have a transition intensity and the terminal states would be absorbing internally. `Transfer` should have a separate external follow-up state if the business estimand includes external resolution.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

I would pre-specify at least the following analyses and report the changes in (F_R(72)), (B_{72}), (A_{72}), late-resolution mass, and cause-specific CIFs.

1. **Primary internal competing-risk analysis:** Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer are separate events; open cases are right-censored. This answers internal process performance and treats transfer as an operational exit without declaring its external outcome bad.
2. **Transfer-as-failure analysis:** Combine Transfer with non-resolution for the binary SLA measure, or use a composite (F/W/J/X) failure versus Resolve. This is appropriate if the SLA promises an internally resolved case and external handoff is a miss. It will generally show more breach and less success than treating transfer as censoring.
3. **Transfer-as-censor analysis:** Censor at transfer for an eventual-resolution target. Use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights based only on pre-transfer history where feasible, and state that conditional independent censoring is an assumption. Because transfer follows escalation and likely depends on latent difficulty, the result should be labeled a sensitivity estimate, not the primary truth.
4. **Competing versus composite non-resolution:** Keep Withdraw and Reject as separate causes for governance, then repeat with both combined into a single non-Resolve event. This shows whether a conclusion is driven by preventable workflow exits versus eligibility/business decisions. They should not be censored in either version.
5. **Open-case censoring sensitivity:** For the five open cases younger than 72, provide bounds or a simple scenario table in which all resolve by 72, none resolve by 72, or their outcomes are imputed from a pre-horizon model. The four open cases already beyond 72 remain known breaches. For later outcomes, apply IPCW or a selection model using age, stage, priority, cohort, escalation, rework, and gap, while acknowledging that these variables may not capture missingness mechanisms.
6. **Policy/cohort sensitivity:** Report unadjusted A versus B descriptions, priority-stratified estimates, and estimates standardized to a common priority distribution. Include a cohort-by-priority interaction only if the data support it, and separately flag `AutoCheck-first` as an implementation/path feature. Do not describe the cohort contrast as a causal effect without assignment and rollout information.

Qualitative conclusions can reverse under these analyses. B has two transfers versus one in A and more P1 cases, so classifying transfers as failures or censoring can materially change any apparent cohort difference. The apparently favorable B timing among observed resolves can disappear after accounting for the different policy path, incomplete compliance, and censoring. Treating the five young open cases as all breaches can make the tail look worse than the observed-data analysis; treating them as all timely resolutions can make it look better. Likewise, censoring withdrawals or rejections as if they were ordinary loss to follow-up can reverse comparisons by inflating apparent resolution.

# 7. Operational interpretation

At any scoring time (t<72), an alert should use a conditional quantity such as the probability of missing 72 given survival and the observed history through (t): (P(T_R>72mid T_R>t,mathcal H_t)). This can be calibrated separately for current stage and priority, with alert thresholds selected from the cost of intervention, customer impact, and queue capacity rather than from an arbitrary probability cutoff. A case past 72 should be labeled an observed breach, not merely assigned a high predicted risk.

Workload actions can be tied to the state graph: route cases near the deadline in Review or Escalate to a monitored queue, investigate repeated Review/Rework loops, and separate transferred work from internally actionable backlog. Report the number and age of active cases beyond 72, not just the fraction of completed cases that met SLA. A transfer queue needs an external-outcome reconciliation process if the organization wants an end-to-end success measure.

Governance reporting should show, by cohort and priority, at least timely Resolve CIF, late Resolve CIF, Withdraw/Reject/Transfer CIFs, active-without-disposition probability, number censored before 72, and uncertainty intervals. It should state the transfer convention on every dashboard. A model risk score is an association useful for triage; it is not evidence that AutoCheck, escalation, or any intervention causes a change in SLA performance.

Calibration checks should compare predicted and observed horizon risks within priority and cohort, using bootstrap intervals because the sample is small. Also check calibration over age bands, Brier score or time-dependent scoring where feasible, and the distribution of alerts. Fairness and service-quality checks should compare false-alert rates, missed-breach rates, calibration, time-to-alert, transfer rates, and observed resolution/withdrawal/rejection outcomes by priority and cohort. Small cells should be shown with uncertainty rather than suppressed into a misleading aggregate. Priority is an operational category, not necessarily a protected attribute, but disparate service or error patterns still require review.

        if row["observed_status"] == "open":
            return 1 if row["time"] >= H else pd.NA
        return int(row["time"] > H)
    out["no_disposition_by_72"] = out.apply(no_disposition, axis=1).astype("Int64")

    # A late-resolution indicator for a 72-hour landmark; the full landmark
    # risk table is retained separately below.
    out["late_resolution"] = ((out["observed_status"] == "resolved") &
                               (out["time"] > H)).astype(int)
    out["at_risk_at_72"] = (out["time"] >= H).astype(int)
    return out

def cif_at(encoded, target_cause, horizon):
    """Aalen-Johansen CIF at horizon for one cause, with C as censoring."""
    y = encoded[["time", "cause"]].sort_values("time")
    cif, event_free = 0.0, 1.0
    times = sorted(t for t in y["time"].unique() if t <= horizon)
    for t in times:
        n_at_risk = int((y["time"] >= t).sum())
        if n_at_risk == 0:
            continue
        at_t = y["time"] == t
        d_all = int((at_t & (y["cause"] != "C")).sum())
        d_target = int((at_t & (y["cause"] == target_cause)).sum())
        cif += event_free * d_target / n_at_risk
        event_free *= 1.0 - d_all / n_at_risk
    return cif

def strata_summary(encoded):
    x = encoded.copy()
    x["known_breach"] = x["breach_by_72"].notna().astype(int)
    x["breach_int"] = x["breach_by_72"].fillna(0).astype(int)
    x["timely_resolve"] = ((x["observed_status"] == "resolved") &
                            (x["time"] <= H)).astype(int)
    ans = (x.groupby(["cohort", "priority"], dropna=False)
             .agg(n=("case", "size"), known_at_72=("known_breach", "sum"),
                  known_breaches=("breach_int", "sum"),
                  timely_resolves=("timely_resolve", "sum"))
             .reset_index())
    ans["known_breach_rate"] = ans["known_breaches"] / ans["known_at_72"]
    return ans

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The highest-priority additions are:

1. **Exact event timestamps** for Submit and every activity, including Screen, AutoCheck, Review, Rework, Escalate, Transfer, and Resolve. The current prefixes establish order but cannot support stage dwell times, escalation timing, time-varying risk sets, or credible transition hazards.
2. **External outcomes after Transfer**, including whether and when the external organization resolved, withdrew, rejected, or abandoned the case. A linked external case identifier and a common end-of-follow-up rule are needed to define end-to-end SLA success.
3. **Cutoff and start dates**, timezone, calendar/business-hour definition, and the case-entry window. This is necessary to distinguish administrative censoring from delayed ingestion, account for calendar effects, and verify that elapsed-hour durations are comparable.
4. **A complete event-feed and missingness audit**, including records of late-arriving events, reopenings, cancellations, and cases absent from the extract. `last_event_gap_h` alone cannot distinguish inactivity from missing observation.
5. **Time-varying workload and capacity**, such as queue length, staffing, external capacity, assignment delay, holidays, and system incidents. These may explain both long gaps and policy-period differences.
6. **Assignment mechanism and policy rollout compliance**, including eligibility rules, who received the automated screening, timing of the change, overrides, and reasons for noncompliance. Without this, cohort and AutoCheck-first contrasts are associational.
7. **Case-level covariates available at Submit**, such as request type, customer or channel, complexity, geography, and eligibility. These are needed for risk adjustment, priority standardization, and fairness analysis, subject to privacy and governance controls.
8. **Outcome definitions and service clocks**, including whether withdrawal/rejection is customer- or agent-initiated, whether transfer pauses the SLA clock, and whether an external Resolve counts. Different answers define different estimands rather than merely different estimators.

Finally, (n=32) is too small for reliable high-dimensional hazard models or fine subgroup claims. The current answer supports a transparent descriptive competing-risk analysis and pre-specified sensitivity bounds. Stronger inference requires more cases, repeated cohorts, complete follow-up, and validation on a later frozen period.
