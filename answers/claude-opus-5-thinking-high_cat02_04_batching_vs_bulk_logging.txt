# 1. Cluster diagnoses

Preliminary note on vocabulary, because the whole task depends on it:

- **Execution semantics** = when physical work happened.
- **Recording semantics** = when a human or system wrote a row.
  A timestamp equality is evidence about *recording* first, and about *execution* only after the recording channel has been excluded as its cause.
- I therefore add a ninth hypothesis to the supplied list, used below for G4/G8: **derived-interval artifact**, i.e. the source emits one event per item (a scan/confirmation) and the "start" is synthesised as the previous item's event. This produces perfect adjacency and zero idle by construction, and is temporally indistinguishable from true back-to-back service.

Confidence is my subjective posterior that the `top_explanation` is the dominant generating mechanism, on the evidence supplied only.

Observed mapping to clusters: G1 matches rows 1 and 7 equally on equality alone and is separated only by precision metadata and capacity; G2 matches row 2; G3 matches row 3 (shared `recorded_at` is decisive); G4 and G8 match rows 4 and 9 identically, with G8 additionally matching row 8; G5 matches rows 5/6; G6 matches row 3 in extremis (start equality *and* end equality *and* shared `recorded_at`); G7 matches row 7 with a logical impossibility as confirmation.

PM4Py caveat: `discover_batches` assigns exactly these category names (simultaneous, batching at start, batching at end, sequential, concurrent) from timestamps alone. It will happily label G6 "simultaneous" and G7 "simultaneous", which is precisely the inference the quality bar forbids. The labels are *temporal-pattern detections*; the physical claim requires the extra columns above (precision, `recorded_at` cardinality, capacity, identity).

# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

Conventions: waiting = start − `queue_arrival`; service = complete − start; utilisation = Σ service / elapsed window per resource; flow time = complete − `queue_arrival`.

**G1 — genuine batch.** Waiting (6, 5, 4, 2 min) is trustworthy: arrivals and the common release are both real. Service is trustworthy *per batch* (3 min) but not per item: naive Σ service / elapsed = 12 min / 3 min = **400 % utilisation**, an impossible value produced by treating one 4-capacity server as four servers. Throughput (4 items per 3 min = 80/h) is correct. Bottleneck detection is distorted upward unless capacity 4 is modelled; conformance is unaffected except that any model asserting sequential handling of L01–L04 will show spurious parallelism.

**G2 — start batching.** Every item except the first has its start pulled backwards to 10:00. Therefore **waiting is biased downwards** (recorded 18/14/11/6 min; under serial reconstruction 18/22/25/27 min, i.e. the real signal — a growing queue — is inverted into a shrinking one) and **service is biased upwards** (recorded 8/14/21/27, mean 17.5 min; reconstructed ≈ 8/6/7/6, mean ≈ 6.8 min). Utilisation is inflated because three phantom parallel executions exist in 10:00–10:08. The station will look like a fast-queue/slow-service bottleneck when it is actually a slow-queue/fast-service one — the worst possible sign error for improvement work.

**G3 — end batching.** Starts are plausibly real, so waiting (22/18/19/19 min) is roughly unbiased. Service is inflated in a strongly *position-dependent* way (28/20/13/6 min vs ≈ 8/7/7/6 reconstructed): the first case of every folder looks pathological. Utilisation is inflated (up to 4 concurrent reviews at 11:24–11:30 for a single reviewer, > 100 %). Flow time for E01 is overstated by up to 22 min. Variance of service time is hugely inflated, which corrupts any queueing model fitted to it (an SCV computed here is meaningless). Conformance checking on lifecycle order is unaffected, but any "duration deviation" rule will fire on the first item of each folder.

**G4 — serial service.** Waiting (7/10/13/16/19 min) grows linearly — the classic single-server-with-burst-arrival signature, and it is credible. Service (4 min flat) and utilisation (20 min busy in a 20 min window = 100 %) are credible *if* starts are observed. If the derived-interval hypothesis holds, the 4 min includes handling and idle, so service is an **upper bound**, true utilisation is < 100 %, and the station's apparent saturation (and hence its selection as bottleneck) is overstated. Note that this cluster is the one where the temporal pattern is most informative and the least correction is required.

**G5 — shared account.** Waiting (≈ 15 min each) is credible. Service per case is credible per *task*. Utilisation is not: Σ service = 25+15+20+10 = 70 min over a 30 min window = **233 %**, which is impossible for one worker and merely tells us the resource label is a pool. Dividing by the documented pool size 3 gives ≈ 78 %, but the observed max concurrency of 4 contradicts that pool size, so even the denominator is unknown. Bottleneck detection will falsely flag "analyst" as the most overloaded resource in the whole log, purely because 3+ people share one identifier. Social-network and handover mining on this resource is meaningless. Conformance is unaffected; resource-based conformance rules (segregation of duties, four-eyes) are actively unenforceable here.

**G6 — bulk entry: inflated waiting *and* near-zero service simultaneously.** This is the important pathology, and it is not a contradiction but a direct consequence of one substitution. When the recorded start is the *data-entry* moment rather than the work moment, that single timestamp appears on both sides of two different differences:

- waiting = start − arrival is stretched all the way from the arrival to the end of shift: 175, 148, 122, 110, 76, 58, 35 min, mean ≈ 103 min — an upper bound, inflated by however long each ticket had actually been finished before 17:00;
- service = complete − start uses the *same* timestamp twice, so it collapses to exactly 0 for all 7 cases; utilisation = 0/shift, i.e. T-F appears completely idle.

The two errors reinforce each other rather than cancelling: all elapsed time is reassigned from "service" to "waiting". The step then looks like an infinite-capacity pure delay — a queue that no amount of staffing would fix — whereas the true constraint may well be the operator's capacity. Throughput analysis is corrupted too: 7 completions land in the single minute 17:00–17:01, producing a spurious throughput spike and a spurious end-of-day burst in any drift/seasonality analysis. Conformance timing rules ("close within X of arrival") will fail systematically for reasons that have nothing to do with the process. Zero-duration activities also break most log-based queueing estimators (division by zero, degenerate service distributions), so they must be encoded as missing, never as 0.

**G7 — coarsening.** Waiting is arithmetically impossible for 4 of 5 cases (−1, −4, −7, −11 min) because arrivals postdate the rounded-down start; naive tooling either produces negatives or clips them to zero, silently destroying the queue signal. Service is inflated to 15 min each, so Σ service = 75 min in a 15 min window = **500 % utilisation**. Under proper interval censoring the group's total work is ≤ 15 min, and M05 (arrival 14:11) has service ≤ 4 min. Bottleneck detection will rank this legacy station first purely because its clock is coarse — a systematic bias that always favours the worst-instrumented step, which is exactly backwards from what an improvement programme wants.

**G8 — downtime queue.** Waiting (38/43/48/54/58 min) is real in magnitude but wrongly *attributed*: 30 of those minutes are resource unavailability, not congestion. Service (7 min) and utilisation (35/35 = 100 % over the working window) suggest a saturated server, so capacity analysis would recommend adding a triage station; the correct intervention is shortening or rescheduling the safety check. Bottleneck detection therefore identifies the right *place* for the wrong *reason*. The undocumented clock offset between the safety system and the repair queue means the 3 min between 15:35 and 15:38 cannot be read as changeover time; it may be zero or negative.

def parse_batches(result):
    """-> {cluster: {batch_type: n_batches}} , robust to tuple-shape changes."""
    out = {}
    for entry in result:
        act_res = tuple(entry[0])
        info = next((e for e in entry[1:] if isinstance(e, dict)), {})
        cats = {}
        for btype, payload in info.items():
            cats[str(btype)] = len(payload) if isinstance(payload, (list, tuple)) else 1
        out[key2cluster.get(act_res, str(act_res))] = cats
    return out

def observed_grid(series):
    """Coarsest grid (s) consistent with the observed values; gcd-based.
    Only informative when several distinct values are present."""
    vals = ((series - series.dt.normalize()).dt.total_seconds()
            .dropna().astype(int).tolist())
    vals = [v for v in vals if v > 0]
    return reduce(gcd, vals) if vals else 0


def max_concurrency(starts, ends):
    """Touching intervals (end == next start) do NOT count as concurrent."""
    pts = [(t, +1) for t in starts] + [(t, -1) for t in ends]
    pts.sort(key=lambda p: (p[0], p[1]))   # -1 before +1 at equal instants
    cur = best = 0
    for _, d in pts:
        cur += d
        best = max(best, cur)
    return best


def diagnose(g):
    g = g.sort_values("sequence_no")
    n = len(g)
    st, en = g["start_log"], g["complete_log"]
    pairs = list(combinations(range(n), 2))
    overlaps = sum(
        1 for i, j in pairs
        if (min(en.iloc[i], en.iloc[j]) - max(st.iloc[i], st.iloc[j])).total_seconds() > 0
    )
    by_start = g.sort_values("start_log")
    adj = sum(1 for k in range(n - 1)
              if by_start["start_log"].iloc[k + 1] == by_start["complete_log"].iloc[k])
    lag = (g["recorded_at_ts"] - g["complete_log"]).dt.total_seconds()
    svc = (en - st).dt.total_seconds() / 60
    wait = (st - g["queue_arrival_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 60
    window = (en.max() - st.min()).total_seconds() / 60
    exec_grid = observed_grid(pd.concat([st, en]))
    rec_grid = observed_grid(g["recorded_at_ts"])
    return pd.Series({
        "n": n,
        "resource": g["resource"].iloc[0],
        "declared_prec_s": int(g["precision_sec"].iloc[0]),
        "exec_grid_s": exec_grid,
        "recorded_grid_s": rec_grid,
        "grid_mismatch": exec_grid > max(rec_grid, int(g["precision_sec"].iloc[0])),
        "max_equal_starts": int(st.value_counts().max()),
        "max_equal_ends": int(en.value_counts().max()),
        "adjacency_rate": round(adj / (n - 1), 2) if n > 1 else float("nan"),

                                   - g["queue_arrival_ts"].min()).total_seconds() / 60, 1),
        "idle_gap_before_first_start_min": round((st.min()
                                   - g["queue_arrival_ts"].max()).total_seconds() / 60, 1),

        "suspect_rows": int(g["lifecycle_suspect"].sum()),

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

**Resource IDs trusted vs. treated as shared accounts.**

If every identifier denotes one worker, then: G5 becomes a single analyst genuinely running four investigations at once, with 233 % utilisation — arithmetically impossible, so this branch is self-refuting and forces the shared-account reading. G2 and G3 become one inspector and one reviewer, so the apparent 4-way concurrency must be a logging artifact, which is exactly the reading in §1. G1's "RBT-7" is a machine, so multiple concurrency is fine.

If identifiers are treated as *possibly* shared (note that `T-B`, `T-C`, `T-D`, `T-F`, `T-G`, `T-H` look like terminal or workstation identifiers rather than personal names, and only `RBT-7` and `analyst_pool` are self-describing), the picture changes materially: G2 and G3 could be two or three people working through a shared terminal, in which case the overlapping intervals are physically real, no correction is needed, and the "start batching"/"end batching" diagnosis collapses. G5's contradiction disappears entirely. The decisive test is cheap: does the login map 1:1 to a badge? Until that is answered, per-resource utilisation is unidentifiable for every cluster except G1 and G4, and I would not publish a utilisation figure for T-B, T-C, T-F, T-G or T-H.

Consequences by measure: waiting-time estimates are insensitive to this question (they depend on arrival and start), service-time estimates are insensitive, but utilisation, capacity, handover/social-network mining, and any four-eyes or segregation-of-duty conformance rule are all directly falsified by shared identifiers. The bias runs one way — shared accounts always *overstate* single-resource load and *understate* headcount.

**Timestamps exact vs. interval-censored by their stated precision.**

Under the exact reading, every equality is a real coincidence and G7 contains an outright contradiction (arrival after start) that must be blamed on some other field. Under the censored reading, a value `t` with precision `p` denotes the interval `[t, t+p)` (or a rounded neighbourhood, since the G7 rule is undocumented and rounds "down/up" asymmetrically). Then:

- G1 (1 s): censoring is negligible against a 180 s activity, so the equality remains genuine evidence of simultaneity. This is the only cluster where precision *earns* the simultaneity claim.
- G2, G3, G5, G6 (1 min): ±30 s of slack against 6–28 min activities. Censoring changes nothing qualitatively; in particular G6's zero durations cannot be rescued by censoring (the true service could be anything from 0 to hours, which is exactly why it must be recorded as missing).
- G4, G8 (declared 1 s, but execution fields observed on a 60 s grid): the mismatch is itself the finding. Either the lifecycle fields are rounded before storage or they are derived; the perfect adjacency is then partly an artifact and service times are upper bounds with roughly ±60 s of slack, which is 25 % of G4's 4 min service.
- G7 (15 min): everything becomes an interval. Service ∈ (0, 15] min per item, total group work ≤ 15 min, waiting ≥ 0 by construction once the start is allowed to be as late as 14:14:59, and M05's service ≤ 4 min from its own arrival at 14:11. All point estimates for this station must be replaced by bounds; any average that includes G7 as points is meaningless.

**Cases where one temporal pattern supports several physical explanations.**

1. **Identical start *and* identical end: G1 vs. G7 (and G6).** The pattern "all four rows share both endpoints" is produced by true parallel processing on a 4-item fixture (G1), by a 15-minute rounding grid (G7), and by end-of-shift data entry (G6). PM4Py calls all three "simultaneous". Discriminators, in order of strength: declared vs. observed timestamp grid; whether `recorded_at` is finer and staggered than the execution fields; station capacity; and whether an arrival postdates a start.
2. **Identical completion with staggered starts: G3 vs. G6.** Both are "batching at end". In G3 the starts carry real information and only the completions are corrupted, so service can be reconstructed. In G6 nothing survives. The discriminator is whether the starts are distinct at all, plus the cardinality of `recorded_at`. A third reading is available for both: a genuine deadline or gate at 11:30 / 17:00, which would make the equality real; this cannot be refuted from timestamps and needs the operational answer "is there a scheduled release/close event?".
3. **Perfect adjacency with zero overlap: G4 vs. G8 vs. derived intervals.** The identical signature supports (a) genuine back-to-back single-server service, (b) a queue drained after downtime (G8, where an external unavailability event supplies the cause), and (c) intervals synthesised from consecutive scan confirmations, in which case "service time" is really inter-scan time and includes handling and idle. The three imply different utilisation and different improvement actions (nothing, fix the safety interlock, fix the instrumentation).
4. **Common start with staggered ends: G2.** Supports a group-start button with serial work, genuinely parallel exposure of four panels, and round-robin multitasking. The completion gaps (8/6/7/6 min) are equally consistent with all three; only the UI evidence tips it, and UI evidence constrains *recording*, not *execution*.
5. **Overlapping, out-of-order intervals: G5.** Supports several analysts on one login, one analyst multitasking, and simply wrong timestamps — reinforced here by the conflict between `sequence_no` order and `recorded_at` order, which proves at least one ordering field is untrustworthy regardless of which physical story is true.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

Prioritised; each item states the specific failure it removes.

**P0 — changes without which no performance number is defensible.**

1. **Separate execution timestamps from recording timestamps, and never let one substitute for the other.** Every event needs `start_time`, `end_time` *and* `recorded_at`, with a hard validation rule that `recorded_at ≥ end_time` and an explicit `entry_mode` field (`live`, `retrospective`, `bulk`). Fixes G6 (which currently destroys both waiting and service simultaneously) and makes G3 detectable automatically.
2. **Forbid zero-length and imputed durations from masquerading as measurements.** If the true start is unknown, store `NULL` plus a reason code, never a copy of the completion timestamp. Downstream, missing service must propagate as missing, not as 0.
3. **Persist per-source timestamp precision, rounding rule and clock-sync status as metadata attached to every event**, not as documentation. `precision` here is described as "documented, not necessarily true"; the redesign should record *observed* resolution (the gcd diagnostic in §4 can run nightly) and flag disagreement. Fixes G7's undocumented rounding and G4/G8's declared-1 s-but-minute-grid mismatch.
4. **Synchronise all source clocks to a single reference (NTP/PTP) and log the residual offset per source per day.** Without this, the G8 story (safety check ends 15:35, first triage starts 15:38) is unprovable — the two systems are explicitly on different clocks.

**P1 — changes that make the batching question answerable rather than debatable.**

5. **Badge-level identity even behind shared logins.** Keep `login_id` for access control and add `actor_id` captured at the workstation. Removes G5's ambiguity, and lets utilisation be computed per person and capacity per station.
6. **First-class group/batch identifiers.** When a UI has a "begin group" button (G2) or a folder submission (G3), the system already knows the group; emit `batch_id`, `batch_size` and `batch_role` (`start_marker`, `member`, `submission`). This converts the hardest inference problem in this dataset into a lookup, and it distinguishes an *operational* batch (G1: physically processed together) from a *logging* batch (G3, G6: recorded together) by construction.
7. **Declared workstation capacity and a machine-readable resource type** (`human`, `machine`, `pool`, `terminal`) with `parallel_capacity`. Turns "233 % utilisation" from an unexplained anomaly into an automatic validation failure, and legitimises G1's 400 % item-level figure as a 100 % batch-level one.

**P2 — refinements that sharpen the remaining estimates.**

8. **Pause/resume and blocked/unavailable events.** G8's 30 min gap should appear as an explicit `resource_unavailable` interval (safety check), so waiting can be decomposed into congestion and unavailability rather than being attributed wholesale to capacity shortage.
9. **Item-level rather than cycle-level emission from automated cells.** RBT-7 should emit four rows with genuinely per-item timing (or, honestly, one batch row with `batch_size = 4`), so the analyst is not left guessing whether four rows mean four measurements.
10. **Queue events at the same fidelity as work events**: enqueue, dequeue, requeue, priority, and abandonment. `queue_arrival` alone cannot distinguish "waiting for a resource" from "waiting for a gate to open".
11. **Retain source insertion order *and* database insertion time, and validate their consistency** — the G5 conflict between `sequence_no` and `recorded_at` should have raised an alert on the day it happened.
12. **Sample-based ground truth.** A few observed shifts, or a short time-and-motion study per station, calibrate the correction factors (e.g. G3's true service ≈ 7 min vs. recorded 16.75 min) and let the rest of the log be corrected statistically.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High confidence, usable with stated caveats.**

- **G6** — the strongest diagnosis in the set (start = complete = 17:00, one shared insert, a source named `manual_bulk_form`). Confidently a *logging* batch, and confidently *not* evidence about execution.
- **G7** — confidently timestamp coarsening; the arrival-after-start impossibility settles it. What happened inside the quarter hour remains unknown.
- **G3** — confidently a bulk submission; the single shared `recorded_at` across four distinct starts is close to decisive.
- **G1** — confidently a real batch *at the fixture level*, given a documented capacity of 4, 1 s source precision and sub-3 s machine recording lag. The residual doubt is whether four rows represent four measurements or one replicated cycle event; both readings agree that the four items were processed together in 09:00–09:03, which is what performance analysis needs.
- **G4** — the pattern is clear (serial single-server service of a burst) even though the mechanism behind the starts is not; both readings support the same qualitative conclusion, differing only in whether service is exact or an upper bound.

**Exclude, or carry as interval-censored, in any queue or service-time analysis.**

- **G6**: exclude from service-time and utilisation analysis entirely; retain only for flow-time upper bounds and for throughput of the *closing* activity if the analyst is willing to call 17:00 an administrative event. Waiting times here are upper bounds, not estimates.
- **G7**: carry as intervals (service ∈ (0, 15] min; start ∈ [14:00, 14:15) bounded below by each arrival). Never mix its point values into an average with 1 s sources — doing so systematically ranks the worst-instrumented station as the bottleneck.
- **G2**: exclude the recorded starts; use left-censored starts (start ∈ [10:00, previous completion]) or the serial reconstruction. Uncorrected, this cluster inverts the sign of the queue trend.
- **G3**: right-censor completions (complete ∈ (start, 11:30]); the reconstruction complete_i = start_{i+1} is defensible as a point estimate but should be labelled an estimate.
- **G5**: exclude from all per-resource utilisation, capacity, handover and segregation-of-duty analysis. Case-level waiting and service remain usable.

**Require operational validation before any performance conclusion is published.**

- **G1**: does the PLC emit one record per item or one cell-cycle record replicated across four carriers? This decides whether item-level timing exists at all.
- **G4 and G8**: are `start` values observed, or synthesised from the previous item's scan? This decides whether 100 % utilisation is real or definitional, and it is the same question for both clusters because they share the `scanner` source and both show minute-grid values from a source declared at 1 s.
- **G5**: badge-level identity, and the true size of the analyst pool — the observed concurrency of 4 contradicts the documented maximum of 3, so at least one of the two is wrong.
- **G8**: the clock offset between the safety system and the repair queue, before the 15:35→15:38 sequence is used to attribute 30 minutes of waiting to the safety check.
- **G3 and G6**: whether a genuine scheduled gate exists at 11:30 and 17:00. If it does, part of what I have called an artifact is a real constraint.
- **G7**: the undocumented rounding rule of the legacy MES, without which the censoring interval itself is a guess.

Overall: of the eight clusters, exactly one (**G1**) supports a claim of genuine simultaneous physical work, and one further (**G5**) supports genuine concurrency but by an unknown number of people rather than by one resource. Three (**G2, G3, G6**) are logging batches of increasing severity, one (**G7**) is a measurement-resolution artifact, and two (**G4, G8**) are serial work whose "batch" appearance is an arrival-pattern and parameter effect rather than an execution property. Identical timestamps were, in six of eight clusters, evidence about how the data was written rather than about how the work was done.
