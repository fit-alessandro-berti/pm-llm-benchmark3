## 7. Final Recommendation

**Recommend the Bottleneck & Synchronization portfolio: A4, A6, A7, A12.**

### Why the highest-frequency activity (A1) should *not* be automated first

Frequency is a measure of how many cases touch an activity, not how much waiting or risk that activity creates. The Receive  Register pair accounts for ~12,000 cases and 11 minutes of service time per case, which is small compared with the waits that dominate the end-to-end cycle:

- Resolve entity mismatch: 31 h wait × 1,750 cases = ~54,000 case-hours
- Wait for external valuation: 58 h wait × 2,350 cases = ~136,000 case-hours
- Normalize financial statements: 20 h wait × 5,200 cases = ~104,000 case-hours
- Customer signing: 44 h wait × 7,300 cases = ~321,000 case-hours (acknowledged, deferred)
- Receive + Register combined: ~3 min service × 12,000  600 hours of *labor*

By Little's Law, throughput is bounded by the slowest synchronization, not by the busiest station. Automating A1 saves labor at a station that is not a bottleneck, and (because the resource network flags back office as already underutilized after 15:00) does not even free meaningful capacity. Worse, it accelerates clean inflow into Credit analysis and Normalize financial statements — exactly the activities where the credit analyst pool is central and overloaded. Without A6 in parallel, A1 makes the process worse for analysts. It also does not strengthen controls, so it fails the compliance mandate. And it does not break any rework loop.

### Why A4, A6, A7, A12 is the defensible trade-off

1. **Process-mining alignment.** A4 is on the critical path 82% of the time when it occurs and is the dominant cause of KYC clearance delay — a rare-but-devastating pattern. A7 is the single largest wait in the DFG. A6 gates Credit analysis and Committee packaging for ~5,200 cases. A12 directly attacks the four-eyes, provisional-signing, and contract-remediation control gaps.
2. **Resource demand shifts favorably.** Compliance specialists gain capacity at the routine end (A4), credit analysts are protected from overload (A6), and external valuers are routed rather than added (A7). Back-office and legal-ops seasonality are not worsened.
3. **Compliance mandate satisfied.** Two of four modules (A4, A12) explicitly strengthen controls; A6 and A7 do not weaken them.
4. **Customer friction is minimized.** No module pushes complexity onto customers in year one, which addresses RM resistance.
5. **Core-banking constraint respected.** Only A6 needs deep integration in the first six months, leaving the second slot for year-2 deployment of A9.
6. **Auditability is built in.** A12's workbench establishes the trail every other module will require, and A4/A6/A7 can all be deployed *with* rather than *after* the audit posture is ready.

### Acknowledged trade-offs
- Lower "quick-win" optics for senior management than A1 + A5 + A9 + A10 would produce.
- A10 (e-signature) is deferred despite a large 44 h wait because it has customer-facing and identity/consent risk and depends on KYC clearance — which A4 provides. It is the natural year-2 follow-on.
- A9 (contract generation) is deferred because deploying it before A12 and A4 are stable replicates the contract-remediation loop risk.
- A5 (missing-document nudging) is deferred because of RM resistance and customer-friction risk; its value is real (it attacks the 84.8% missing-doc loop) but its political and customer cost is higher in year one.

In short: automate where the process-mining evidence says the time and risk live, not where the case count is loudest. The highest-frequency activity should be automated eventually — but only after the synchronization waits, control gaps, and analyst capacity that it would otherwise expose are addressed.
