# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness asks whether *similarly situated applicants are treated similarly in process* — not merely whether outcomes are equal. Raw outcome gaps are a screening signal, not a verdict. The dimensions below separate what the data supports from what it merely suggests.

### Outcome parity (screening signal only)
Raw approval gaps of 15 pp (B), 9 pp (C), and 20 pp (D) versus Group A are large, but the case-mix table shows the groups are not comparable as-is: B has 46% incomplete documentation and 57% language-support need; D has 51% high risk scores and 49% high prior-case counts. Raw gaps therefore **overstate** differential treatment. Conversely, equal approval rates would not by itself prove fairness if the burden of reaching that rate differed by group. Outcome parity is necessary to monitor, insufficient to conclude.

### Adjusted outcome disparity
After controlling for risk band, documentation, channel, household size, prior cases, region, and month, residual gaps remain: 5.5 pp (B), 2.2 pp (C), 3.1 pp (D) approval; +2.6 / +4.1 / +0.9 days cycle time. Observables absorb roughly two-thirds to four-fifths of the raw gaps, but the residuals are not negligible — and two of the controls (risk score, documentation completeness) are themselves suspect (Sections 2 and 5), so the adjusted numbers are not a clean acquittal either. C's small residual with n = 820 carries meaningful sampling uncertainty.

### Procedural burden
This is the strongest dimension of concern. Even holding outcomes aside, B and C experience more steps, more handovers (general  bilingual  general; general  specialist  supervisor), higher rework (22–24% vs. 11%), and higher adjusted rates of Request Missing Documents (+9.5 pp for B) and Supervisor Review (+8.0 pp for B, +5.5 pp for C) than observably similar cases. Burden is a fairness object in itself: effort cost, evidentiary demands, and dignity are distributed unequally.

### Timeliness
Adjusted cycle-time penalties are +2.6 days (B) and +4.1 days (C), and the tails are worse: 90th-percentile gaps of 12.8 days (B) and 17.4 days (C) versus A, more than double the median gaps. That tail-dominant pattern is the signature of **queueing at small resource pools**, not uniform slowness. Critically, the delay concentrates at rights-preserving steps: Language Support Contact  Eligibility (3.8 days median, 7 bilingual caseworkers) and Accommodation Review  Eligibility (4.7 days, 5 specialists). No SLA covers these steps, so the delay is structurally unmanaged.

### Consistency of routing
Like cases should follow like paths. D's fraud-review routing (V3: 28% vs. 9%) is largely consistent with its 51% high-risk share — conditionally legitimate. But B's supervisor-review residual (+8 pp) is *not* explained by its dominant paths (V4 contains no Supervisor Review; V3 covers only 11% of B), and B's doc-request residual (+9.5 pp) persists after controlling for completeness. C's supervisor review may be mechanically coupled to the accommodation path (V5 includes it) — which is itself a design question: whether that coupling reflects policy or staffing (two accommodation specialists also supervise) determines whether it is consistency or artifact.

### Quality/accessibility of support
Support exists formally but is thin materially: ~710 language-support-needing B cases served by 7 bilingual caseworkers; the support step is serialized *before* progression rather than run in parallel; B's 38% in-person share plausibly reflects an online channel not accessible in its languages, and in-person submissions wait longer for identity verification (1.4 vs. 0.8 days). Notification timing is similar across groups, but content quality, language of notices, and comprehension are unmeasured.

### Appeal/rework burden
Appeals concentrate where approvals are lower (D: 16% raw, +5.7 pp adjusted; B: 13% raw, +4.4 pp adjusted), and the appeal path (V6, 30.7 days median) roughly quadruples total cycle time for affected cases. Some of the appeal gap is mechanical (more denials  more appeals); how much reflects thin decision explanations or distrust is unknown. Appeal *outcomes* are not recorded, so we cannot tell whether appeals are correcting errors or burning time.

### Resource-allocation fairness
A's cases spread across 52 caseworkers; B's concentrate on 10 staff (7 bilingual + 3 overloaded officers); C's on 5 specialists, 2 of whom also supervise — coupling the accommodation queue to the supervisor-queue backlog (B waits 1.9 days, C 1.6 days vs. A's 1.0 at Supervisor Review  Decision Draft, concentrated in two regions). Capacity allocation is an *agency choice*: the groups whose needs require specialized resources receive systematically less capacity and redundancy per case. This is disparity attributable to organizational design, not to applicant attributes.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

**Inference framework first.** Four categories must be kept distinct:

| Category | Definition | Evidence standard |
|---|---|---|
| Disparity | Adjusted difference among similarly situated applicants | Achievable with these data (below) |
| Legitimate routing | Policy-mandated steps (fraud review for high risk; accommodation review; language contact) | Requires a *validated* trigger and *proportionate* burden |
| Discrimination | Treatment differing by group membership per se | Needs designs these data cannot support alone (caseworker fixed effects, audit, appeal outcomes); cannot be concluded here |
| Uncertainty | Sampling error, thin overlap, model dependence | Report CIs, overlap diagnostics, sensitivity ranges |

### 2.1 Matching / stratification variables
- **Stratify/match on:** `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count` (banded), `region`, `channel`, and `month` (to remove seasonality and capacity shocks). Use coarsened exact matching or propensity weighting; check standardized mean differences and overlap, and report where comparisons rely on extrapolation (B in-person minority-language cases may have thin A counterparts — say so rather than silently extrapolate).
- **Two adjustment sets, always:** (a) *full* (all controls above) and (b) *restricted* (excluding `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`). These two covariates are potentially biased or endogenous (Section 5); bracketing the estimates between the two sets is more honest than a single number.
- **Treat language preference and accommodation requests as exposures, not only controls:** compare B-cases-with-support-need vs. B-cases-without (dose–response), and C-with vs. C-without, since the question is precisely whether exercising a right carries a penalty.

### 2.2 Path/variant comparison
- Compare variant distributions *within* matched strata: are B cases in the same risk/documentation/channel/region cell still more likely to follow V2/V4 than A cases?
- Compare outcomes and timing *within* variants — V1 is the common fast path: if B cases in V1 are still slower or less approved, the problem is not only path composition.
- Decompose the total cycle-time gap into a **composition effect** (which paths) and a **within-path effect** (how fast each path runs), using variant shares and per-variant medians.
- Look beyond the top six variants for rework loops (repeated Request Missing Documents, re-verification).

### 2.3 Resource-assignment comparison
- Handover counts per case by group; probability of assignment to the bilingual or specialist pool conditional on need; caseload per resource per day.
- Test **role coupling**: do accommodation specialists' supervisor duties predict accommodation-queue delays (their supervisor shifts starve the accommodation queue)?
- Include **caseworker fixed effects** in outcome/routing models where feasible — this separates "tougher decision-makers" from "treated differently by the same decision-maker."

### 2.4 Waiting-time decomposition
- Insert per-case service vs. waiting time; decompose each group's adjusted cycle-time gap into per-edge contributions (waiting vs. working).
- Compare edge-wait distributions (median *and* p90) by group × region for the critical edges: Language Support  Eligibility, Accommodation Review  Eligibility, Completeness  Request Docs, Supervisor Review  Decision Draft.
- Classify each edge: if the gap is in **waiting**, it is a capacity/allocation issue (fixable by the agency); if in **service time**, it reflects effort per case (possibly legitimate complexity).

### 2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates
- **Risk score:** re-estimate all adjusted models without it; validate it against downstream ground truth — fraud-review *substantiation rates by group within risk band*. If flagged D cases substantiate at lower rates than flagged A cases, the score is over-flagging D and the "legitimate" fraud routing is partly laundered disparity.
- **Documentation completeness:** treat as a potential mediator (language barrier  incomplete submission  doc request  delay). Run mediation-style bounds: the B disparity attributable to completeness may itself be agency-caused (inaccessible forms/notices), in which case "controlling for it" absorbs the agency's own contribution and understates the disparity.
- Report E-values / Rosenbaum-style sensitivity to quantify how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to explain the residuals.

### 2.6 Subgroup checks by region and channel
- **Region × group:** is B's language-support wait concentrated in the two backlog regions (capacity mechanism) or uniform everywhere (scrutiny mechanism)?
- **Channel × group:** compare online-only A vs. online-only B to remove channel confounding; examine whether B's in-person share is itself an accessibility fallback.
- **Month × group:** do gaps move with capacity changes (natural experiment)?
- **Within-group dose–response:** among B cases, does needing language support predict delay after controls?

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Ranked by importance, with a verdict on justification for each:

1. **Accommodation-queue delay (Group C) — step legitimate, delay concerning.** V5 covers 31% of C at 21.6 days median (3.7× the fast path); the Accommodation Review  Eligibility wait alone is 4.7 days; C carries the largest adjusted cycle-time penalty (+4.1 days) and the highest rework (24%). The accommodation review is a right; the queue is a staffing choice, worsened by two specialists doubling as supervisors (coupled queues). A disability accommodation should not function as a delay penalty.

2. **Language-support bottleneck (Group B) — step legitimate, delay and serialization concerning.** V4 covers 24% of B at 19.2 days; the support-contact edge waits 3.8 days on a 7-person pool; B also waits 1.6 vs. 0.4 days for document requests partly because bilingual staff must be scheduled. The support step is *serialized before* completeness and eligibility rather than run in parallel — a design decision that converts a right into added elapsed time.

3. **Documentation-request differential (Group B) — partially justified, residual concerning.** V2 (34% of B vs. 24% of A) costs ~9 extra days versus V1. Part is explained by 46% incomplete documentation; the **+9.5 pp adjusted residual is the single largest unexplained routing difference in the data** — same risk, same completeness, same channel, same region, yet more document demands. Possible stricter verification, translation friction, or defensive practice. Note also that completeness is partly *caused* by language barriers, so even the "explained" share is not cleanly the applicant's fault.

4. **Supervisor-review differential (B +8.0 pp, C +5.5 pp adjusted) — concerning unless policy-mandated.** B's residual cannot be attributed to its dominant paths (V4 has no supervisor step; V3 covers 11% of B). C's may be mechanical via V5 or via specialist dual roles — determine from policy and log-skeleton constraints whether the coupling is rule or artifact. Either way it adds scrutiny and 0.6–0.9 days of waiting to groups already slower.

5. **Fraud-path routing (Group D) — legitimate in form, unverified in substance.** V3 (28% of D) is consistent with 51% high risk scores, so conditional on the score this routing is defensible. But the score is unvalidated and correlates strongly with prior-case count (49%) — i.e., with prior *contact with the system*, which may encode historical disparities. If the score over-flags D, the fraud path, the supervisor review, and part of the denial and appeal burden are disparity laundered through an instrument. D's modest adjusted cycle time (+0.9 days) is entirely conditional on the score's neutrality.

6. **Fast-path access (B, C, D) — mixed.** Only 18–22% reach the straight-through V1 versus 42% of A. Part is genuine complexity; the within-stratum check (Section 2.2) is needed to know how much is routing. Channel design contributes: B's in-person identity verification waits 1.4 vs. 0.8 days.

7. **Appeal burden (D, B) — mixed, unresolved.** D: 13% of cases include an appeal, +5.7 pp adjusted; B: +4.4 pp. Some of this is mechanical (more denials). Without appeal outcomes we cannot tell whether appeals are correcting erroneous denials (a decision-quality problem) or yielding little (a burden problem). Either reading is adverse; they imply different fixes.

8. **Handover chains and rework (B, C) — concerning.** Longer multi-hop chains in the two backlog regions and rework at 22–24% vs. 11% indicate process friction that compounds queueing risk at every hop.

**What is *not* a disparity signal here:** the existence of Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, or Fraud Review as steps, and higher raw denial rates for higher-risk or less-documented caseloads. **What is:** where those steps sit in the flow, how long their queues are, who staffs them, and the routing residuals that survive adjustment.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Specialized-capacity bottleneck (legitimate need, concerning execution).** Small pools (7 bilingual staff, 5 specialists) with no SLA and no redundancy create queueing whose cost falls on exactly the applicants exercising rights. The tail-heavy time gaps (p90 gaps more than double median gaps) are the classic signature.
2. **Sequential process design (concerning).** Support steps are placed *in series* before progression; with no timeliness target they are schedulable-last. The same support delivered in parallel with completeness checking would remove much of B's +2.6-day adjusted gap without touching the substance of the right.
3. **Access-barrier endogeneity of documentation (concerning).** Language and format barriers produce incomplete submissions; the process responds with document requests and delay; adjusting for completeness then attributes to applicants a condition the agency partly caused. The +9.5 pp doc-request residual suggests additional friction beyond measured completeness (e.g., translation re-submission requirements invisible in the activity log).
4. **Risk-score bias via historical contact (concerning).** If the score was trained on past flags/investigations, prior-case count becomes a proxy for poverty, disability, or language status, and D's heavier fraud routing, denials, and appeals partly reflect feedback loops rather than fraud propensity.
5. **Defensive adjudication / scrutiny differential (concerning).** Caseworkers may over-request documents and escalate to supervisors where ambiguity is higher or error liability feared — consistent with B's unexplained +8 pp supervisor and +9.5 pp doc-request residuals.
6. **Role coupling (concerning).** Two accommodation specialists also supervise; their supervisor backlog directly starves the accommodation queue, and the V5 supervisor step may be a staffing artifact rather than a case need.
7. **Denial-driven appeal dynamics plus thin explanations (mixed).** More denials mechanically raise appeals; amplified if notifications are in the majority language or lack reasons — B's and D's elevated adjusted appeal probabilities fit both stories.
8. **Regional backlog × group concentration (concerning).** B's in-person concentration in two backlog regions turns a geographic capacity problem into an apparent group effect — checkable via region × group decomposition.
9. **Channel accessibility upstream (concerning).** An online system not usable in B's languages pushes applicants to a slower in-person channel before the process even starts.

---

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in `risk_score_band`.** Likely trained on historical decisions and contacts; may encode past discrimination. Using it as a control *and* as a routing justification risks double-counting it as legitimate.
- **Endogeneity of `documentation_completeness`.** Measured at intake, shaped by form design, language access, and channel — not a pure applicant attribute. Controlling for it can absorb agency-caused disparity (mediator/collider problems).
- **Missing applicant needs.** No literacy, digital access, transportation, caregiving, health severity, or income-volatility attributes; `disability_accommodation_requested` captures requests, not need (stigma suppresses requests); `language_preference`  actual comprehension of agency documents.
- **Unobserved resource workload.** Daily granularity hides intraday queues, multitasking, absences, and effort per case; true utilization of the 7 bilingual workers and 5 specialists cannot be computed, so we cannot separate "pool too small" from "pool badly scheduled."
- **Appeal quality invisible.** `appeal_flag` has no outcome, reason, or reversal indicator. Appeal rates alone cannot distinguish justified grievance from friction; appeal success rates by group would be among the most informative missing variables.
- **Outcome coarseness.** `case_outcome` is binary: no partial awards, no accuracy against ground truth, no measure of explanation quality or dignity of treatment. Approval parity among *wrong* decisions is not fairness.
- **Selection into the log.** Only applicants appear. If eligible members of B or C were deterred by anticipated burden and never applied, all observed rates understate the true disparity.
- **Confounding structure.** Group  region  resource assignment are entangled; rework/reopen definitions are unclear; the top-six variants summarize away loop behavior; medians conceal the tails where the worst experiences live.

---

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

1. **Add capacity for language support and accommodation review** (hire/retain bilingual caseworkers, expand the specialist pool, cross-train generalists; forecast demand from V4/V5 volumes).
 *Side effects:* cost and long ramp-up; diluted specialist expertise if cross-training is shallow; hiring must be geographically targeted or the two backlog regions stay problematic; overshoot risk without demand forecasting.
2. **Service-level targets for support steps** (e.g., Language Support Contact initiated within 24 h; Accommodation Review completed within 48–72 h), with escalation triggers — putting rights steps on the same clock as everything else.
 *Side effects:* gaming (contacts logged but not substantive); SLA pressure cutting thoroughness; cliff effects at thresholds; must be paired with quality sampling or the target becomes the task.
3. **Routing redesign to reduce handovers** — end-to-end case ownership by bilingual caseworkers; run language support *in parallel* with completeness checks; direct-to-specialist intake queuing.
 *Side effects:* single-owner cases stall when the owner is absent (needs coverage rules); specialists doing end-to-end work lose focus; monitor re-openings to ensure continuity does not become corner-cutting. **Explicitly: keep the support steps; remove the delay penalty attached to them.**
4. **Decision-explanation improvements** — reason-coded, plain-language, translated notices; pre-decision contact where documents are insufficient; explicit appeal-rights statements.
 *Side effects:* longer drafting per case; boilerplate risk unless templates are tested with actual applicants; possible short-term rise in appeal volume as applicants understand rights better (which is a success, not a failure, but must be resourced).
5. **Monitor adjusted disparities** — monthly dashboard of adjusted cycle time, supervisor-review rate, doc-request rate, and appeal rate by group, with control charts and caseworker-level outlier review.
 *Side effects:* Goodhart's law — pressure to hit parity metrics can discourage recording language need or accommodation requests, or shift burden into unmeasured channels; requires governance and protected-attribute safeguards; small regional cells produce noisy signals.
6. **Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`** — validate the score against substantiated fraud findings by group within band; test flag rates conditional on outcome; rebuild with fairness constraints and drift monitoring; audit whether doc requests are proportionate.
 *Side effects:* if the current score is genuinely predictive, constraining it may raise false negatives in fraud detection — an explicit, accountable trade-off to be made, not hidden; rebuilding takes time and revalidation; audits add workload to the same stretched staff.
7. *(Supplementary)* **Proactive intake assistance** in preferred languages (help completing submissions at the front door). *Side effects:* intake cost rises; may lengthen first contact even as it removes downstream loops.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**The process shows substantiated procedural unfairness in specific, identifiable respects, while claims of discrimination remain unproven — and untestable — with these data.**

What the evidence supports: (1) Group B and Group C applicants bear a *timeliness and burden penalty attached to rights-preserving steps* — 3.8-day and 4.7-day median waits concentrated in seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists, with no SLA governing those steps; this is an organizational capacity and design outcome, not applicant complexity, and it is the clearest fairness failure in the data. (2) Routing is not fully consistent among similarly situated applicants: B's adjusted +9.5 pp document-request and +8.0 pp supervisor-review rates, and the elevated adjusted appeal probabilities for B and D, persist after controls and are not explained by path composition. (3) Resource allocation concentrates the groups with specialized needs onto the smallest, most coupled pools.

What the evidence does not support: equating raw approval gaps with discrimination — most of the raw gap is absorbed by observable case mix; treating the adjusted residuals as proof of differential treatment by group — unmeasured needs and possibly biased controls (risk score, documentation completeness) cut both ways; and accepting D's heavier fraud routing as legitimate without validating the risk score, whose correlation with prior-case count is a genuine warning sign.

**Calibrated verdict: procedurally unfair as currently operated** — principally in the timeliness, burden, and resource dimensions for Groups B and C, and conditionally for Group D pending risk-score audit — **with the outcome-level disparity beyond case mix real but modest (roughly 2–6 percentage points), and discriminatory intent or effect neither established nor excluded.** Priority actions: capacity and SLAs for support steps, parallelized routing, risk-score and documentation audits, and adjusted-disparity monitoring with appeal outcomes collected.
