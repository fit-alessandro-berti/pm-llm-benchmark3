## 1. Decision rule

The rule is a sequence of gates, followed by a scored ranking under a capacity constraint. A case must pass every gate to be considered; passing the gates does not earn a slot, it only earns a place in the ranking.

**Gate A — Causal eligibility (stage, not risk).**
The swarm is a diagnosis-phase instrument. A case is eligible only if it is at `Triage` or `Diagnosis Started` and `Diagnosis Complete` has not been recorded. Cases at or past `Diagnosis Complete` are structurally ineligible regardless of `p_now`. This is the gate that disqualifies I-03: a breach probability of 0.82 is a statement about the outcome, not about our ability to change it. Spending €450 plus two specialists plus queue delay on a case whose stated risk reduction is 0.00–0.03 is close to a pure transfer of harm onto untreated cases.

**Gate B — Actionable time window.**
The swarm consumes 45 minutes and its benefit accrues through faster/redirected diagnosis. A case needs enough remaining runway for the swarm plus the resolution work it unblocks. Operationally: require time-to-deadline ≥ 2.0 h for a case at `Diagnosis Started`, and ≥ 2.5 h at `Triage` (triage cases still have more downstream work). Cases far from the deadline (≥ 10 h) are not blocked but are *deferred*: they can be re-evaluated at the next refresh with better information, and slots today are scarcer than slots tomorrow morning.

**Gate C — Evidence change / alert persistence.**
A repeat alert on a case that has already alerted is admitted only if there has been a *materially new event* since the previous alert: a new process event in the log, a new customer artifact, a change of assignee/priority, or a change in `p` exceeding the model's own refresh noise (|Δp| ≥ 0.10 attributable to new events rather than clock drift). Re-scoring of an unchanged prefix is not new evidence. This gate suppresses I-05 and admits I-07.

**Gate D — Data trust.**
If a driving feature is missing and imputed, the probability is admitted but *shrunk* toward the last trusted prediction, and the case cannot occupy the top slot on the strength of the imputed jump alone. This applies to I-06.

**Calibration robustification.**
We do not act on raw `p_now`. We form a *decision probability* `p*` by applying the segment bias correction conservatively and by widening, not narrowing, the interval:

- SME: `p* = p_now + 0.065` (midpoint of the +0.04…+0.09 underprediction), interval `[p_now+0.04, p_now+0.09]`.
- Strategic Enterprise: `p* = p_now − 0.085`, interval `[p_now−0.12, p_now−0.05]`.
- Public Sector: `p* = p_now`, but with an inflated interval (±0.06) reflecting moderate sample size.
- Non-Native-Language Support: no point correction is defensible; carry `p_now` with a wide interval (±0.12) *and* shrink the assumed intervention effect by ~40%, because both calibration and effect estimates are thin there.

Adjusted values: I-01 0.61 (PS), I-02 0.575, I-03 0.735 (ineligible), I-04 0.395, I-05 0.635, I-06 ~0.40–0.52 (imputation-shrunk), I-07 0.785, I-08 0.345, I-09 0.69 ±0.12, I-10 0.525.

**Effect handling.**
The supplied ranges are not known causal effects. We plan on a *conservative point* at the lower third of each range, `Δ̂ = lo + 0.3·(hi − lo)`, further multiplied by an attributability factor (0.6 for NNLS, 0.5–0.7 where the alert's stated reason rests on imputed data). Optimistic figures are used only in scenario analysis, never in the allocation decision.

**Priority score.**
```
Score = Δ̂ · V  −  450  −  B_fa  −  S_marginal
```
where `V` is the value of avoiding breach, `B_fa` the false-alarm burden, and `S_marginal` the marginal queue externality of the slot. With six untreated diagnosis-stage cases each gaining 0.01–0.03 breach probability per used slot and a portfolio-average untreated case value near €15k, one slot costs roughly 6 × 0.02 × €15,000 ≈ **€1,800** in base conditions (€900 optimistic, €2,700 adverse). Because congestion is convex, we charge an escalating externality: €1,500 for the first slot, €1,800 for the second, €2,200 for the third, €3,000 for the fourth. A case is funded only if its score stays positive at *its own* marginal slot price, and ties are broken toward (a) shorter runway, (b) cleaner data, (c) segments that have recently been under-served.

**Non-monetary override.** Regulatory/public-sector obligations and contractual escalation commitments can promote a case one tier even if its monetary score is mid-pack; conversely no case is funded on monetary score alone if it would breach the "one reserve slot" preference before 15:00 without sign-off.

Reading across scenarios: the portfolio has a positive expected value under base assumptions and a real, bounded downside under adverse ones. The asymmetry is acceptable at three slots and becomes uncomfortable at four, since the fourth slot adds ~€3,000 of externality against a best remaining candidate worth ~€2,550. That is the quantitative reason the reserve is not merely a management preference today.

A caveat on the arithmetic: the spillover estimate is the least trustworthy number here. It converts a probability nudge on six unnamed cases into euros using an assumed average case value. If the untreated diagnosis-stage cases happen to be high-value Strategic Enterprise incidents, the adverse-case loss doubles. Measuring queue composition at allocation time — not assuming an average — is a monitoring priority (§6).

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

**Cool-down.** After any alert on a case is surfaced to a decision-maker, that case enters a 2-hour cool-down for *unchanged-evidence* re-alerts. Model refreshes every 30 minutes; without a cool-down, a single elevated case produces four alerts per two hours and mechanically crowds the queue.

**Suppression (minimum evidence change).** During cool-down, an alert is re-surfaced only if at least one of:
1. a new process event is recorded (stage transition, reassignment, priority change);
2. a new artifact arrives (customer log, vendor response, diagnostic output);
3. `Δp ≥ 0.10` that survives a check that it is driven by new features rather than by the shrinking time-to-deadline;
4. the case crosses a stage-eligibility boundary in either direction.

Time-decay alone — `p` rising because the deadline approaches with no new information — is *not* an evidence change. It is the model restating the same fact more loudly.

**Escalation.** A case that alerts three or more times *with* qualifying evidence changes, and is not funded, escalates to human review rather than to automatic intervention. The review asks a different question — "is this a case the swarm can help, or a case that needs a different instrument (customer deadline renegotiation, vendor escalation, resourcing)?" Repetition is evidence that our instrument may be mismatched, not that we should apply it harder.

**Re-entry.** A suppressed case re-enters the ranking immediately on any qualifying evidence change, with no penalty for having been suppressed. Suppression is about information content, not about punishing noisy cases.

**Repeat swarms.** Forbidden unless a materially new event has occurred *since the swarm*, and additionally the first swarm must have completed and been documented with a stated reason for failure. A second swarm on the same case competes at a 1.5× effective slot price, because the prior of "the swarm helps here" should be lower after one has already failed.

**I-05 versus I-07 — the same alert count, opposite decisions.**

Both are SME, both are on their third alert, both are stage-eligible. That is where the similarity ends.

*I-05* has produced three alerts because the model refreshes twice an hour and no new event has occurred since the first. Its `p` moved 0.55 → 0.57, within refresh noise. There is exactly one alert's worth of information here, repeated three times. Its conservative score (€1,515) is not trivial, and after SME correction `p*` = 0.635 is genuinely elevated — but funding it would mean our allocation is driven by clock ticks. Worse, if the model is systematically noisy on SME cases, a policy that responds to repetition will spend disproportionate capacity on the noisiest segment. **Suppressed, logged, re-entering on any qualifying event.** If I-05 breaches, that is a scored, honest miss — recorded as a suppression outcome so the suppression rule itself can be evaluated.

*I-07* has a new customer diagnostic log. Its `p` actually *fell* slightly (0.75 → 0.72), which under a naive `p_now` ranking looks like de-escalation, but the log is precisely the kind of information the swarm exists to exploit. The evidence gate passes cleanly. I-07 nonetheless does not get a slot — for a completely different reason: with 1.5 h to deadline, a 45-minute swarm consumes a third of the remaining runway and cannot plausibly change the outcome, and at €5,000 of value it cannot carry a ~€1,800 externality. So I-07 receives a **cheaper, faster instrument**: a 10-minute senior read of the new log, plus a proactive deadline-extension conversation with the customer.

The contrast is the point. I-05 is refused because its alert carries no new information; I-07's alert carries real information but arrives too late for this particular instrument. Collapsing both into "third alert, deprioritise" would get the right answer on I-05 for the wrong reason and would mishandle the next I-07 that arrives with 6 hours of runway.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

**Value-based prioritization starving low-value cases.** A pure `Δ · V` ranking is a machine for allocating senior capacity to large accounts. In this portfolio, SME cases I-05, I-07 and I-10 all lose, and I-07 loses despite having the second-highest adjusted probability (0.785) and a strong effect range. Over months this becomes structural: SME customers receive worse service, which shows up as higher SME breach rates, which the model learns as "SME cases breach", which raises their scores in a way that never quite compensates.

*Safeguards:* (a) a **capacity floor** — at least 25% of swarm slots per rolling week are allocated by adjusted probability and effect size within the SME/low-value tier, ignoring `V`; (b) **value capping** in the score, `V_effective = min(V, €40,000)`, so a single €100k case cannot dominate the ranking on exposure alone; (c) a **cheap-instrument tier** so low-value cases are not simply dropped — senior log review, deadline renegotiation, priority reclassification are logged as interventions and tracked for coverage; (d) a monthly report of slot share and breach rate by segment and by contract value band, reviewed by service management, not by the modelling team alone.

**Non-Native-Language Support uncertainty (I-09).** The validation sample is small; both calibration and effect are uncertain. There are two failure modes with opposite signs. Excluding NNLS cases as "too uncertain to act on" guarantees the sample never grows and freezes the exclusion permanently. Over-treating them on the basis of a possibly-miscalibrated 0.69 wastes capacity. We chose to fund I-09 with a 40% effect discount, accepting a lower expected return in exchange for information — but this only works if the information is actually captured.

*Safeguards:* (a) every NNLS intervention is **documented with the mechanism** the swarm actually addressed — if the recurring blocker is translation latency or terminology mismatch rather than diagnostic difficulty, the swarm is the wrong instrument and we need a language-access instrument instead; (b) a **standing minimum quota** of NNLS cases in the intervened set (roughly proportional to their share of the diagnosis queue) so the calibration sample grows; (c) NNLS predictions are reported with explicit wide intervals in the operator UI, never as a bare number; (d) a scheduled re-validation once ≥ 50 NNLS outcomes have accumulated, with the discount factor revisited then.

**Differential calibration across segments.** We are applying a +0.065 lift to SME and a −0.085 haircut to SE. These corrections are themselves estimates, from backtests, on a workflow that was *recently redesigned*. The SE overprediction may be shrinking as the redesign beds in, in which case we are now systematically under-serving Strategic Enterprise; or the redesign may have changed the process enough that the correction is understated. Applying a fixed offset indefinitely converts a temporary measurement into a permanent policy.

*Safeguards:* (a) segment corrections carry an **expiry date** (60 days) and must be re-estimated to be renewed; (b) corrections are applied as **intervals**, not points, and a case whose decision flips depending on where in the interval you sit is flagged for human judgement rather than resolved by the midpoint; (c) publish per-segment reliability diagrams monthly; (d) forbid corrections estimated on fewer than a pre-agreed minimum of outcomes — for thin segments, widen the interval instead of shifting the point.

**Selective intervention changing future training labels.** This is the most insidious risk. We intervene on the cases the model flags as high-risk; if the intervention works, those cases do not breach; the model learns that cases with those features do not breach; scores fall; we stop intervening; breaches return. The model's own success erases its evidence. Simultaneously, cases we never treat generate clean, untreated labels, so the model becomes progressively better calibrated on the population we ignore and worse on the one we act upon.

*Safeguards:* (a) **record the treatment as a feature** — every training row carries whether a swarm occurred, when, and at what stage; never train on a mixed treated/untreated pool as if it were observational; (b) train the risk model on **untreated cases only**, or with explicit treatment adjustment, so `p` retains its meaning as "risk absent intervention"; (c) maintain a **randomised holdout**: in the mid-priority band — cases whose scores are close enough that the ranking is arbitrary anyway — randomise a modest fraction (say 10–15% of slots) to no-intervention. This costs little because those cases were near-ties, and it is the only clean source of causal effect estimates; (d) log every *suppressed* and *deferred* alert with its outcome, so the counterfactual arm is not silently discarded.

**Spillovers systematically falling on untreated groups.** Each slot adds 8–20 minutes of aggregate delay and 0.01–0.03 breach probability to six untreated diagnosis-stage cases. If the treated set is persistently high-value and the untreated set persistently low-value, we are not merely failing to help SME cases — we are actively making them worse in order to protect enterprise accounts, and the mechanism is invisible in any per-case audit. Three slots today impose roughly 0.36 expected additional breaches on untreated cases. That is not a rounding error; it is comparable to the benefit delivered to one of the three treated cases.

*Safeguards:* (a) **measure, do not assume** — record the segment composition of the diagnosis queue at each allocation and attribute the estimated spillover to specific segments in a monthly ledger; (b) charge the externality at the *actual* queue composition rather than a portfolio average; (c) set a **daily slot ceiling** tied to observed congestion — when queue depth exceeds a threshold, the marginal slot price rises and fewer slots are used, which is the correct response to congestion; (d) if the spillover ledger shows one segment absorbing a disproportionate share for two consecutive months, that segment's cases receive a compensating priority bonus until the imbalance clears; (e) prefer instruments that do not draw on the shared diagnosis queue (customer deadline renegotiation, external escalation) whenever their expected benefit is within ~20% of the swarm's.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

**Operational monitoring (daily).** Slots offered, used, and reserved; alerts generated, suppressed by cool-down, suppressed by stage-ineligibility, and deferred; time from alert to decision; swarm start-time adherence and actual duration against the 45-minute assumption. Watch for two specific pathologies: slots systematically going unused (threshold too high, or the reserve policy is over-binding), and the reserve slot being consumed by non-regulatory work (policy erosion).

**Predictive monitoring (weekly, segment-stratified).** Reliability diagrams, Brier score and calibration slope/intercept per segment (SME, Strategic Enterprise, Public Sector, NNLS), computed on **untreated cases only** so treatment does not contaminate the calibration signal. Report sample sizes alongside every metric; a favourable calibration curve on 15 cases is not evidence. Track the SME and SE offsets explicitly: if the SE overprediction has decayed below 0.03, retire the haircut. Also monitor prediction *stability* — the distribution of |Δp| per refresh — since the I-05 pattern is a symptom of a jumpy model as much as of a permissive alerting rule.

**Causal-effect monitoring (monthly, or on accumulating N).** This is the piece that cannot be skipped, because everything in §3 rests on effect ranges we did not estimate. Sources, in descending order of credibility:
1. the **randomised mid-band holdout** (§5) — the only clean estimate, and worth its cost;
2. a **natural experiment** in capacity — days when slots are exhausted create quasi-random non-treatment among near-tied cases; compare treated to just-missed cases;
3. **regression discontinuity** at the score cutoff, comparing cases just above and just below the funding line;
4. matched observational comparison on prefix features, treated as weakest evidence and reported with caveats.

Estimate effects separately by stage (`Triage` vs `Diagnosis Started`) and by segment, because the whole policy assumes the effect is concentrated pre-`Diagnosis Complete` — that assumption should be *tested*, not merely asserted. Report effects as intervals; if the credible interval for a segment includes zero after 40+ treated cases, stop funding that segment's swarms pending review.

**Capacity and spillover monitoring.** Queue depth and waiting-time distribution for diagnosis-stage cases, before/after swarm days. Directly estimate the delay externality: compare cycle times of untreated diagnosis-stage cases on high-slot-usage days versus low-usage days, controlling for arrival volume. If the measured aggregate delay exceeds the assumed 8–20 minutes per slot, the slot price in §1 rises and fewer slots are funded. Maintain the segment-attributed spillover ledger from §5.

**Fairness monitoring (monthly).** Slot share, alert share, suppression share, and breach rate by segment, contract-value band, and NNLS status. Track the SME capacity floor and the NNLS quota against their targets. Track outcomes of *suppressed* alerts specifically — if suppressed cases breach at a materially higher rate than funded ones, the evidence gate is too strict or the ranking is mis-specified.

**Policy-induced drift detection.** Watch the joint distribution of features among treated cases over time. Two warning signs: (i) predicted risk in the treated population falling while realised breach rates in the untreated population rise — the model is unlearning the patterns we successfully intervened on; (ii) the alerting population narrowing toward a single segment or feature signature, indicating the policy has begun selecting its own training data. Both trigger a review of the untreated-only training discipline.

**Change-control for the rule.** Threshold and allocation changes are made on a fixed monthly cadence, not reactively after a single salient breach — post-hoc reactions to individual cases are how thresholds ratchet downward until capacity is exhausted. A change is warranted when: a segment's calibration offset changes by more than 0.03; a measured effect interval excludes the range we have been planning on; measured spillover exceeds assumptions by more than 50%; a fairness floor is missed twice consecutively; or slot utilisation sits below 60% or at 100% for two consecutive weeks. Every change is recorded with its justification, its expected effect, and the metric that would show it was wrong — and the previous rule is retained so its counterfactual performance can be tracked for at least one cycle.

**Standing review of the instrument itself.** Every quarter, ask whether the swarm is the right response to the alerts we are generating. If escalated repeat-alert cases (§4) consistently turn out to need customer renegotiation, vendor escalation, or resourcing rather than diagnostic seniority, the correct fix is a broader intervention menu, not a better-tuned threshold on a single instrument.
