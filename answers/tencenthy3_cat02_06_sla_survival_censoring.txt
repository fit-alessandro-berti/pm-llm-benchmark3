# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Observation:** The SLA is a 72-hour elapsed-time threshold from `Submit` to internal `Resolve`. `duration_or_age_h` is exactly that elapsed time for terminal rows and age at cutoff for `open` rows.

**Time origin:** `t = 0` at `Submit` (every prefix begins with `Submit`).  
**Horizon:** ` = 72` hours.  
**Event time of interest:** `T_R` = time to internal `Resolve`.  
**Competing terminal internal time:** `T_D` = time to any internal disposition (`Resolve`, `Reject`, `Withdraw`, `Transfer`).  
**Censoring time:** `C` = `duration_or_age_h` for `open` rows (observation stopped at cutoff).

**Assumption 1:** For operational success we require *internal* `Resolve`. `Transfer` stops internal observation, so its external outcome is unknown; therefore `T_R` is unobserved after transfer and must be handled by explicit scenarios, not hidden.

At least two useful estimands are:

- **Estimand 1 — Crude SLA-breach probability:** `P(internal Resolve not achieved by )`. A case breaches if it is resolved after 72h, or if it reaches a non-resolution terminal (`Reject`, `Withdraw`, or internal `Transfer` treated as non-resolution) before 72h, or if it is still open and already past 72h. This matches **SLA governance / customer-facing breach reporting** because it answers “what fraction of cases miss the success SLA?”
- **Estimand 2 — Probability of no terminal internal disposition at :** `P(T_D > )`. This counts only cases that are still active at 72h. It matches **backlog and workload planning** (“how many cases will still be in process at the SLA horizon?”).
- **Estimand 3 — Cause-specific cumulative incidence of resolution by :** `P(T_R  )` with `Reject`/`Withdraw`/`Transfer` as competing risks. This matches **process-improvement evaluation** of whether screening/escalation converts cases to success among those eligible.
- **Estimand 4 — Subdistribution probability of late resolution:** directly models `P(T_R > )` in the presence of competing events and supports **risk-based alerting**.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Observation:** Of 32 cases, 13 are resolved, 3 rejected, 4 withdrawn, 3 transferred, and 9 open. Only the 13 resolved cases have a success timestamp.

- **Survivor bias:** A completed-case analysis of resolved rows alone (e.g., S01–S04, S07, S13, S16–S20, S27, S28) excludes 9 open cases. Four open cases (S09, S11, S25, S26) already have age `> 72` and are observed breaches for Estimand 1. Excluding them makes mean resolution time look shorter and tail risk invisible.
- **Informative censoring:** The three transferred cases (S08 at 68h, S21 at 26h, S32 at 96h) all passed through `Review`/`Escalate` first. If transfers indicate more complex cases, treating them as ordinary censored would systematically remove high-risk cases from the risk set and overestimate resolution probability.
- **Competing risks:** Cases such as S05, S14, S23 (`rejected`) and S06, S12, S22, S30 (`withdrawn`) can never reach internal `Resolve`. If they are coded as censored rather than competing, any Kaplan–Meier estimate of “time to resolution” is biased upward.
- **Cohort imbalance:** Cohort B contains an `AutoCheck` step in many prefixes, but not all (S20, S23, S26, S30 lack it), confirming incomplete rollout. B also contains 3 of the 4 observed breaches beyond 72 and 3 transfers. A naive comparison of resolved-case durations between A and B ignores this mix and may misattribute faster B resolutions (e.g., S18 at 12h) to policy rather than priority or selection.
- **Active open below versus above 72:** S15 (35h), S24 (67h), S31 (23h) are open but below the horizon; their outcome at 72 is unknown and they must be censored. In contrast, S09 (92h), S11 (81h), S25 (79h), S26 (104h) are already past 72 and known non-resolved. A naive “censor everyone at `duration_or_age_h`” approach keeps S26 in the risk set at 72 even though it cannot meet the SLA, understating breach probability.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Comparison of candidates:**

- **Kaplan–Meier (KM):** Simple survival estimator. Only valid for Estimand 2 if censoring is non-informative. For Estimand 1/3 it overestimates resolution when competing events are censored. Not primary.
- **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence (CIF):** Directly estimates cause-specific probabilities of resolution and competing terminals. Handles `Reject`/`Withdraw`/`Transfer` correctly under explicit codes. **Primary recommendation** for Estimand 1/3 because the data are small (N=32), heavily censored/competing, and the operational question is a probability by a fixed horizon.
- **Cause-specific Cox hazards:** Useful for testing associations of priority/cohort/path with each transition, but with N=32 and few events, semi-parametric estimates are unstable; use only for secondary hypothesis generation.
- **Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray):** Models the probability of late resolution directly, attractive for alerts. However, it is sensitive to informative censoring from transfers and open cases, so we treat it as a secondary sensitivity only.
- **Restricted mean survival time (RMST) up to 72h:** Intuitive SLA metric (expected delay), but requires valid censoring before and after 72; with open cases beyond 72, RMST needs imputation or monotone censoring assumptions.
- **Landmarking at 24h or 48h:** Conditions on survivors and on observed state (e.g., escalated vs not). This avoids immortal-time bias from post-baseline `Escalate`. Recommended secondary for process-aware risk scoring.
- **Multi-state model:** Represents `Submit  Screen/AutoCheck  Review  Escalate/Rework  Resolve/Transfer/Reject/Withdraw`. It uses the supplied prefixes directly and respects event ordering. Recommended as the **process-mining primary companion** to CIF: CIF answers “how many breach?”, multi-state answers “where do they breach?”.

**Recommended plan:**  
1. **Primary:** Aalen–Johansen CIF for internal resolution with `Reject`/`Withdraw` as competing and three transfer scenarios.  
2. **Secondary:** Multi-state / landmarked cause-specific model using time-varying stage and escalation, stratified by cohort and priority.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Features derived from prefixes and supplied columns:**

- `priority`, `cohort`: **Baseline** (known at `Submit`).  
  *Assumption 3:* We treat cohort as a proxy for policy exposure, not a randomized treatment.
- First activity after `Submit` (`Screen` vs `AutoCheck`): **Time-varying / early post-baseline**, not safe as a pure baseline because it is observed only after process start.
- `current_stage` = last activity in prefix: **Time-varying**.
- `escalated` / `rework_count`: **Post-treatment, time-varying**. Using the final column `escalated = yes` as a baseline covariate is **leakage / immortal-time bias**: only cases that survived to `Escalate` can have it (e.g., S06 withdrawn at 27h never escalated). A correct design uses escalation as a time-dependent covariate or a landmark at first escalation.
- `last_event_gap_h`: **Time-varying inactivity signal**. Large gaps (S26: 72h, S11: 55h) suggest stall and likely informative censoring.
- Path pattern / variant: presence of `AutoCheck`, presence of `Escalate`, count of `Review`. These are only fully observed at terminal/open cutoff; for real-time scoring we use the **prefix observed so far**, not the full final prefix.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

We compare at least three analyses:

1. **Base / non-informative censor:** `Transfer` censored at transfer time; `Reject`/`Withdraw` competing; open `> 72` counted as breach, open `< 72` censored.
2. **Competing-transfer:** `Transfer` coded as internal non-resolution (competing event). This raises the estimated breach probability.
3. **Worst-case informative:** `Transfer` counted as SLA breach and assumed to represent cases with elevated late/never-resolution hazard; additionally, open `> 72` are treated as certain breaches (lower bound on success). A corresponding best-case (all transfers externally resolved by 72) would be an upper bound on success but requires data we do not have.

Additional sensitivity:

- **Naive censoring of `Reject`/`Withdraw`:** shows how much the resolution probability is overestimated if competing risks are ignored.
- **Cohort/priority stratification:** because assignment was not randomized. If we stratify by `priority`, the apparent Cohort B speed-up may disappear if B simply received easier P2/P3 mixes or if B’s transfers (S21, S32) and open `>72` cases (S25, S26) are counted as failures.

**Conclusions that could reverse:** Under the censor scenario, Cohort B may appear to improve SLA adherence because its early resolved cases (S16, S18) are prominent and transfers are ignored. Under competing/worst-case transfer treatment, B’s breach probability may rise relative to A because B has 3 transfers and 2 open cases already past 72. Thus the policy recommendation (“B’s automated screening reduced tail risk”) is not robust without the transfer sensitivity.

---

# 7. Operational interpretation

- **Alerts:** Use Estimand 1 CIF at 72h conditional on current state. Example: a case in `Review` at age 48h with `last_event_gap_h > 20` and not yet `Escalate` has an estimated `P(non-resolution by 72) > threshold` and should trigger a supervisor alert. This is a *risk score*, not a causal claim.
- **Workload actions:** Stratify the open backlog by `current_stage` and `priority`. P1 open cases above 48h (e.g., S11, S25) should receive capacity first, but only after confirming that escalation status is treated as time-dependent.
- **SLA governance:** Report CIF by cohort and priority monthly. Track whether Cohort B’s `AutoCheck` variants reduce the incidence of `Reject`/`Withdraw` before claiming success.
- **Calibration:** Compare predicted `P(breach by 72)` with observed breach fractions within strata (cohort × priority). If predicted probabilities systematically miss observed rates, recalibrate thresholds.
- **Fairness:** Check that P3 cases are not disproportionately censored, transferred, or stalled (`last_event_gap_h`) relative to P1. The data show P3 cases among open `>72` (S26) and rejected (S05, S14, S23), so fairness monitoring is needed.
- **Causality guardrail:** Because assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance incomplete, differences by cohort are observational. We label any “effect of escalation” statement as a hypothesis, not a proven causal effect.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Missing data prioritized for stronger inference:**

1. **Exact event timestamps:** We only have total duration/age and `last_event_gap_h`. Per-activity timestamps are needed to fit continuous-time multi-state models and to place `Escalate`/`Rework` precisely on the time axis.
2. **External outcomes after `Transfer`:** Without linked external resolution times, transfer must remain scenario-dependent; this is the largest source of uncertainty for Estimand 1.
3. **Cutoff / start dates:** Calendar time of `Submit` and the freeze date are needed to assess time-varying workload and seasonal effects, and to verify which B cases fell under the new policy.
4. **Policy rollout compliance and assignment mechanism:** Cohort B is not randomized; we need the rule that assigned `AutoCheck` (e.g., S20, S23, S26, S30 lacked it) to distinguish policy effect from selection bias.
5. **Time-varying workload:** Queue length, staff availability, and escalation capacity would explain `last_event_gap_h` and inform whether censoring is informative.
6. **Rejection/withdrawal reasons:** To decide whether `Reject`/`Withdraw` are truly competing risks or should be excluded from SLA denominator (e.g., invalid submissions).
7. **Priority assignment criteria:** To check fairness and avoid confounding between priority and cohort.

**Summary of bounds:** Given observed data, the true internal SLA-breach probability at 72h lies between a lower bound that treats transfers as censored and open `<72` as censored, and an upper bound that treats transfers and all non-resolved terminals as breaches. The qualitative claim that Cohort B improved tail risk is not established until those bounds are reported.
