### 1. Stress scenarios

- **Scenario A – Winter wave with Urgent/Complex shift and higher defect rate**  
  - Volume rises to near-peak or slightly above (similar to 9,900 cases over 3 weeks scaled up), but the **mix shifts further toward Urgent and Complex-panel** beyond the observed peak (e.g. Routine 35%, Urgent 35%, Complex-panel 22%, Repeat-after-failure 8%).  
  - Clinicians are under pressure, so **sampling and labeling quality worsen**, increasing pre-analytical defects, recollection loops, and repeat assays.  
  - Machines are available but run closer to saturation because more cases need non‑batched handling, multiple assays, and Medical validation.

- **Scenario B – Assay-machine degradation/outage (M2 unstable or offline)**  
  - Demand is similar to the observed peak mix, but **M2 has frequent failures or a multi-day outage**, reducing effective assay capacity and increasing repeats, especially for Routine cases routed to it.  
  - **Repeat-after-failure cases spike**, particularly after night shifts and M2 usage (as already observed), consuming assay capacity twice and adding variability.  
  - Pre-analytical and preparation continue to feed work, so queues at **Test preparation, Batch wait, and Assay run** extend well beyond the current 14–22h medians at peak.

- **Scenario C – Medical validator and specialist-review shortage**  
  - Volumes are around the current peak or slightly lower, but **one Medical validator is absent for a week**, and the specialist is unavailable for several consecutive days (beyond the “every second Friday” pattern).  
  - The **case mix is skewed toward Complex-panel and Urgent**, both of which often require Medical validation and are more likely to enter Hold for specialist review (Complex-panel are 3.1× more likely to need Medical validation and 2.4× more likely to require specialist review).  
  - Upstream stages continue to operate reasonably, so **work piles up at Technical validation, Medical validation, and Specialist review**, pushing Complex-panel and some Urgent cases far beyond their current p90 cycle times (Complex-panel 210h, Repeat-after-failure 240h in peak).

- **Scenario D – Courier disruption and arrival compression**  
  - Weather or transport issues **delay and compress courier arrivals** so that most samples arrive in two or three big spikes (e.g. 10:00 and 17:00), exacerbating the already noted spikes at those times.  
  - **“Register order → Receive sample” and “Receive sample → Pre-analytical check” times grow**, with many samples waiting in Courier intake and Registration.  
  - Recollection loops become longer and more resource‑intensive because recollected samples suffer the same compressed arrival pattern (median 42/60h “Request recollection → Receive sample” already shows long delays), further straining Registration and Pre-analytical check.

These four scenarios vary both by **case mix** (especially Urgent/Complex) and by **disruption type** (assay capacity, validators, courier timing) rather than only total volume.


### 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

#### Scenario A – Winter wave with Urgent/Complex shift

- **Starting bottlenecks: Pre-analytical check and Accession correction**  
  - At peak, Pre-analytical check is already at **96% utilization with a 9.5h median queue** (vs 74% and 1.8h normal). More Urgent/Complex cases and poorer samples increase:  
    - “Pre-analytical check → Request recollection” (already 760/780 cases in normal/peak).  
    - “Manual accession correction → Request recollection” (70/110 cases).  
  - Accession correction specialists are only two people, at **91% utilization and a 14h median queue** at peak. More Complex-panel and defect-prone orders will send more cases through “Pre-analytical check → Manual accession correction”, making accession correction the first major queuing point.

- **If Pre-analytical and accession are relieved → Preparation benches and Batch wait become bottlenecks**  
  - With better early checks, more clean samples reach “Pre-analytical check → Test preparation” (8530 peak).  
  - Preparation benches already show **89% utilization and 11h median queue** at peak. Higher Urgent/Complex share means:  
    - More cases that **cannot wait for large Routine batches**, stressing individual setups.  
    - If we follow I2 (larger Routine batches), Urgent cases risk waiting behind big Routine batches unless urgent exceptions are tightly controlled, amplifying queues for Urgent and Complex.  
  - “Test preparation → Batch wait” and “Batch wait → Assay run” medians are 6/14h and 9/22h normal/peak; under this scenario, both are likely to extend, with **Batch wait** specific to Routine cases becoming a visible holding buffer.

- **If preparation is improved or sped up → Assay machines M1/M2 saturate fully**  
  - At peak, assay machines run at **98% utilization with an 18h median queue** (vs 4h normal).  
  - Higher Urgent/Complex proportions increase **bypass of Batch wait** and fragment machine schedules (already urgent cases bypass Batch wait in 74% of normal and only 58% of peak cases). With more Urgent work:  
    - Strict Urgent-first dispatch (I1) and dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex (I3) improve Urgent turnaround but **starve Routine and some Complex-panel** if not constrained, inflating total WIP and queue time for non-urgent work.  
    - Repeat-after-failure cases (already clustered after night shifts and M2 usage) add further volatility, since they consume assay capacity twice.

- **If assay stage is optimized → Technical and Medical validation plus Specialist review bottleneck**  
  - Technical validators are already at **101% utilization and 16h median queue** at peak. Routing more timely assay output to them without adding flexibility pushes Technical validation into chronic overload.  
  - Medical validators are at **93% utilization and 22h median queue**, and Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to require Medical validation and 2.4× more likely to go to Hold for specialist review.  
  - Specialist review has **36h median queue** at peak; one specialist is unavailable every second Friday, which in this scenario combines with higher Complex-panel volume.  
  - Therefore, solving upstream bottlenecks **moves the constraint to Medical validation and Specialist review**, where Urgent and Complex cases directly compete.

#### Scenario B – Assay-machine degradation/outage

- **Initial bottleneck: Assay run on M1/M2 and Repeat-assay loop**  
  - With M2 degraded or offline, effective assay capacity falls well below the peak configuration that already had **98% utilization and 18h median queue**.  
  - When many Routine cases are routed to M2 (I3), any instability there drives **more “Assay run → Repeat assay → Assay run” loops**, doubling consumption of scarce capacity and spiking Repeat-after-failure cases (already clustered after M2 usage and night shifts).  
  - Urgent-first dispatch (I1) pulls remaining capacity toward Urgent and Complex-panel, so **Routine and Repeat-after-failure queues explode**, and total WIP in the assay buffer increases sharply.

- **If assay capacity is temporarily relieved → Preparation benches and Pre-analytical become visible bottlenecks**  
  - If lab responds by throttling inputs to the assay stage (e.g. delaying Pre-analytical or preparation) or outsourcing some Complex-panel (I10), the **queue moves upstream**:  
    - Preparation benches at 89% peak utilization and 11h queue become saturated by partially processed samples awaiting stable machine slots.  
    - Pre-analytical check may start **holding samples longer** before sending them to preparation, raising its queue beyond 9.5h and further lengthening recollection loops.

- **If upstream throttling is removed once machines stabilize → Validation stages become next bottleneck**  
  - When machines recover, a surge of delayed Routine and Repeat-after-failure cases hits Technical validation.  
  - Technical validators (already at 101% utilization) become a severe bottleneck, especially if some of them were cross‑trained and diverted to accession correction (I4).  
  - Medical validation experiences a delayed wave of Complex-panel and Urgent results, with Specialist review again at risk of long queues.

#### Scenario C – Medical validator and specialist-review shortage

- **Primary bottleneck: Medical validation and Specialist review**  
  - Even in current peak, Medical validators have a **22h median queue**, and Specialist review has **36h median queue**. Removing one validator and lengthening specialist absence pushes those queues markedly higher.  
  - Complex-panel cases, which are much more likely to need both Medical validation and Specialist review, dominate these queues and **drive p90 cycle times** far beyond the current 210h for Complex-panel and 240h for Repeat-after-failure.

- **Upstream relief moves pressure forward**  
  - If we improve assay performance (e.g. via better scheduling, I1/I3) or pre‑analytical quality (I6), more cases arrive earlier and more evenly to Technical validation and Medical validation.  
  - Technical validation is already fully utilized; diverting two validators to accession correction (I4) would **further tighten the constraint at Technical validation**, meaning any upstream improvements just feed a growing queue there, which then spills into Medical validation when staff are available.

- **If we add an evening Medical-validation shift (I5) or outsource Complex-panel (I10)**  
  - I5 temporarily relieves Medical validation, pushing more cases to Specialist review sooner; Specialist review, with already long queues and periodic absences, becomes the new bottleneck.  
  - I10 sends some Complex-panel to a partner lab, introducing a **fixed 12h transport delay** but relieving Specialist review locally. This shifts the bottleneck:  
    - For outsourced cases, the constraint becomes **transport + external validation capacity**.  
    - For remaining local cases, either Technical validation or Pre-analytical might become the limiting step if validators are still scarce.

#### Scenario D – Courier disruption and arrival compression

- **First bottlenecks: Courier intake and Registration**  
  - At peak, Courier intake already has **88% utilization and 7.5h median queue**, and Registration has **85% utilization and 2.8h median queue**. Compressed arrivals make short‑term utilization spike well beyond 100%, causing multi‑hour queues.  
  - “Register order → Receive sample” (5/8h normal/peak) stretches further, delaying every downstream step, especially Urgent cases arriving in late waves.

- **If courier and registration are smoothed (I7, I8) → Pre-analytical and accession correction become bottlenecks**  
  - Shifting courier pickup slots (I7) and automating registration for digitally complete orders (I8) reduce Registration and Courier queues and **push more cases quickly into Pre-analytical check**.  
  - Pre-analytical check (96% utilization, 9.5h queue at peak) quickly becomes the next active constraint; more cases also reach Manual accession correction, where utilization is 91% with a 14h queue.  
  - Recollection loops can momentarily improve (faster identification of errors), but if recollected samples also arrive in compressed waves, **intake and Pre-analytical see oscillating overload**.

- **If early pre-analytical triage (I6) is added → Assay and validation bottlenecks**  
  - I6 rejects poor samples earlier (within 2h) and reduces later recollection loops, but **increases the throughput of “clean” samples** to Test preparation and Assay run.  
  - Assay machines and Technical validation again emerge as next bottlenecks. Any strict Urgent-first rule (I1) at preparation or assay improves Urgent performance but shifts longer waits to Routine and some Complex-panel cases, especially when arrival compression coincides with peak volumes.


### 3. Robust redesign package

The redesign should favor **structural robustness** (smoother flow, reduced rework, flexible capacity at true constraints) over optimizing average historical cycle time. The following package aims to perform acceptably across all four scenarios.

- **R1 – Flow smoothing and early defect triage (I6 + I7 + selective I8)**  
  - **Design:**  
    - Use I7 to **re-time courier pickups** to flatten the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes, aligning routes so that no more than a fixed percentage of daily volume arrives in any 2‑hour window.  
    - Implement I6 as a **rapid pre-analytical defect triage** within 2 hours of Receive sample for high‑risk specimen types (poor labeling, complex panels, night‑shift draws).  
    - Apply I8 **only to orders with fully validated digital metadata**, automatically flagging obvious inconsistencies for triage rather than sending them directly to the main Pre-analytical queue.
  - **Why robust:**  
    - Reduces **recollection loops and long “Request recollection → Receive sample” delays** in all scenarios, freeing intake and Pre-analytical capacity.  
    - Smoother arrivals lower peak queues for Courier intake, Registration, and Pre-analytical, making the system less sensitive to demand spikes and courier disruptions.  
    - Earlier rejection of bad samples avoids wasted work at preparation, assay, and validation, which is particularly valuable during machine degradation (Scenario B) and validator shortages (Scenario C).  
  - **Downstream overload risk:**  
    - More “good” samples reach **Test preparation and Assay run faster**, so if R1 is implemented without changes downstream, **assay machines and Technical validation** will see higher instantaneous load and can become the new bottlenecks.

- **R2 – Guardrailed priority and routing at preparation/assay (I1 + I2 + I3 with explicit limits)**  
  - **Design:**  
    - Apply an **Urgent-first dispatch rule (I1)** at Test preparation and Assay run, but cap Urgent/Complex share of machine time (e.g. 60–70%) and reserve protected slots for Routine/Repeat cases to avoid starvation.  
    - Increase Routine batch sizes from 12 to 24 (I2) **only when Routine queues exceed a threshold** and Urgent queues are under control, to avoid delaying Urgent cases behind overly large Routine batches.  
    - Dedicate **M1 primarily to Urgent and Complex-panel cases (I3)** but allow dynamic rollback (e.g. when M1 downtime or queue age crosses a limit) and keep some Routine capacity on M1 during off‑peak hours.
  - **Why robust:**  
    - Directly protects Urgent SLAs (18h target) under Scenario A (demand mix shift) and Scenario D (arrival spikes) while still processing Routine work steadily.  
    - Allows **dynamic adaptation** when machines degrade (Scenario B): if M2 is unstable, routing can be temporarily rebalanced rather than blindly sending all Routine volume to M2.  
  - **Downstream overload risk:**  
    - Better and earlier assay completion sends larger waves into **Technical validation and Medical validation**, which are already at 101% and 93% utilization at peak.  
    - If R2 is aggressive without added validation flexibility, **Medical validation and Specialist review** become severe bottlenecks, especially in Scenario A and C.

- **R3 – Flexible validation and accession capacity (I4 + I5 as a shared pool, not a permanent reassignment)**  
  - **Design:**  
    - Cross‑train two Technical validators (I4) to cover both **Technical validation and Accession correction**, treating them as a **flexible pool** that shifts based on real‑time queues rather than permanently moving FTEs from validation.  
    - Add a **temporary evening Medical-validation shift (I5)** during predicted peak weeks, with actual activation controlled by queue‑based triggers (see Section 4). This shift focuses on **Urgent and Complex-panel** cases.  
  - **Why robust:**  
    - In Scenario A (winter wave), R3 helps keep **Accession correction queues (14h at peak)** and Medical validation queues (22h at peak) within acceptable limits, preventing long tails for Complex-panel and Urgent cases.  
    - In Scenario B (machine degradation), when Repeat-after-failure waves hit, flexible Technical validators can manage both increased validation load and accession fixes that would otherwise block many cases upstream.  
    - In Scenario C (validator shortage), evening shifts and cross‑trained staff offer **surge capacity** to prevent complete gridlock at Medical validation and Specialist review.  
  - **Downstream overload risk:**  
    - When cross‑trained staff focus on accession, **Technical validation** capacity drops and may become the bottleneck; conversely, when they focus on validation, **Accession correction** may again starve and cause many cases to stall earlier in the process.  
    - Evening Medical-validation shifts push more complex cases quickly into **Specialist review**, which can then become overloaded unless combined with selective outsourcing (R4).

- **R4 – Controlled Complex-panel outsourcing (I10) as a safety valve**  
  - **Design:**  
    - Use I10 to **outsource only Complex-panel overflow** when local **Medical validation or Specialist review queues exceed predefined thresholds** (e.g. p90 Complex-panel cycle time projected above 180h, or Specialist review median queue > 36h).  
    - Limit outsourced volume to a fraction of total Complex-panel work and prioritize clear, well‑documented cases to minimize external traceability and coordination errors.
  - **Why robust:**  
    - Provides a **backstop during Scenario A and C**, where local specialist capacity is the hardest constraint.  
    - Helps absorb demand surges or staff absences without requiring permanent over‑staffing locally.  
  - **Downstream overload risk:**  
    - Introduces a fixed **12h transport delay** and new failure modes (lost or delayed shipments, misaligned validation criteria).  
    - May create additional follow‑up work when external results require local clarification, potentially increasing **rework at Medical validation**.

Overall, this package accepts that **the bottleneck will move** but aims to confine it to stations where delays are clinically less critical (e.g. Routine preparation or local Specialist review), while protecting Urgent SLAs and avoiding explosive WIP growth.


### 4. Scenario-specific policies

#### Scenario A – Winter wave with Urgent/Complex shift

- **Policies:**  
  - Activate **strong Urgent/Complex prioritization** within R2:  
    - Urgent-first dispatch at preparation and assay with up to ~70% of machine time reserved for Urgent and Complex-panel.  
    - Allow urgent cases to **bypass Batch wait** more frequently, but only while Urgent queues exceed a threshold (e.g. median Urgent queue at preparation > 3h).  
  - Use R3’s flexible pool to **favor Medical validation and Specialist review** in the evenings, ensuring Complex-panel and Urgent cases are cleared daily.  
  - Use R1’s triage (I6) to **tighten rejection criteria** for poor samples from overloaded clinics to reduce downstream rework.
- **Activation / deactivation triggers:**  
  - **Activate** when, over the last 24h:  
    - Urgent share of new orders > 30–35%, or  
    - Median Urgent cycle time > 15h, or  
    - Percentage of Urgent cases with any queue > 12h exceeds a set limit (e.g. 20%).  
  - **Deactivate or relax** when, for at least 48h:  
    - Urgent share falls below 25% and  
    - Urgent p90 cycle time is consistently < 18h.

#### Scenario B – Assay-machine degradation/outage

- **Policies:**  
  - Switch R2 into **“machine-protection mode”**:  
    - Temporarily **suspend the rule that routes most Routine to M2**; balance load between machines and protect the healthier machine from overload.  
    - Reduce maximum Routine batch size or extend batch intervals, so machines run with **smaller, more predictable mixed batches**, limiting repeats on the unstable machine.  
  - Throttle input to assay when necessary by **slowing Test preparation** for low‑priority Routine cases, keeping upstream WIP in less critical queues.  
  - Preferentially route **Repeat-after-failure cases** to the more reliable machine during dedicated time blocks to avoid cascading failures.
- **Activation / deactivation triggers:**  
  - **Activate** when, over the last 8h:  
    - M2 downtime exceeds a threshold (e.g. > 10–15%), or  
    - Repeat-after-failure rate (Assay run → Repeat assay) rises above a control limit.  
  - **Deactivate** when machine uptime and repeat rates return to baseline for at least 24h.

#### Scenario C – Medical validator and specialist-review shortage

- **Policies:**  
  - Prioritize **Urgent and Complex-panel cases** at Medical validation and Specialist review; Routine cases can **wait for batch validation** once or twice per day.  
  - Activate the **evening Medical-validation shift (I5)** and allocate R3’s cross‑trained staff primarily to Technical validation (to prevent additional backlog) while limiting time spent on accession correction unless its queue becomes extreme.  
  - Enable **Complex-panel outsourcing (R4)** when local Specialist review queues exceed defined thresholds, focusing on cases whose delay would be most harmful.
- **Activation / deactivation triggers:**  
  - **Activate** when:  
    - At least one Medical validator or the specialist is unavailable for > 1 full day, and  
    - Median queue at Medical validation > 16–18h or Specialist review > 30h.  
  - **Deactivate** when full staff return and queues at Medical validation and Specialist review remain below those thresholds for several days.

#### Scenario D – Courier disruption and arrival compression

- **Policies:**  
  - Use I7 to **negotiate temporary courier schedule changes** (additional mid‑day pickups, earlier morning runs, or extended evening pickups) to spread arrivals.  
  - Intensify **early defect triage (I6)** on the most overloaded arrival windows so poor samples are rejected quickly rather than queued.  
  - Apply I8 to **pre‑validate digital orders** before physical samples arrive, so that Registration time per order is reduced when spikes hit.  
  - Temporarily relax some batching for Routine samples to prevent blockages when a large compressed wave arrives late in the day.
- **Activation / deactivation triggers:**  
  - **Activate** when, on two or more days in a week:  
    - More than a threshold fraction of daily volume (e.g. > 50%) arrives within a 4h window, or  
    - Median “Register order → Receive sample” exceeds 8–10h.  
  - **Deactivate** when arrivals revert to a more even pattern and “Register order → Receive sample” median is back near 5–6h.

### 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Extreme combined shocks remain dangerous.**  
  - A coincident winter wave (Scenario A), assay-machine outage (Scenario B), and validator absence (Scenario C) would still overwhelm the system even with the redesign, because all critical constraints (assay, Medical validation, Specialist review) would tighten simultaneously.

- **Dependence on cross-trained staff and evening shifts.**  
  - R3 increases reliance on a small group of versatile staff. If they are absent or burnt out, both Accession correction and Technical validation weaken together.  
  - Evening Medical-validation shifts may **raise error rates** or create staff fatigue, potentially increasing rework or misclassifications during off‑hours.

- **New complexity from outsourcing (R4).**  
  - Outsourcing Complex-panel overflow introduces vulnerabilities in **transport reliability, data exchange, and traceability**. Delayed courier routes or interface problems can cause long, opaque delays not visible in local queues.  
  - Differences in interpretation between labs could generate more **post‑hoc clarifications and rework**, particularly for borderline cases.

- **Automation and triage misclassification risks.**  
  - Registration automation (I8) and early triage (I6) may **amplify wrong metadata or mis‑triage samples** if validation rules are flawed, causing either unsafe fast‑tracking or unjustified rejections.  
  - This is a new vulnerability introduced by the redesign: **systematic error** can now impact many cases quickly instead of being caught one‑by‑one.

- **Limited resilience to sudden changes in test menu or regulation.**  
  - If new assays require additional specialist review or have stricter turnaround requirements, the redesigned flows might still channel too much work through a small number of experts, recreating a Specialist review bottleneck.


### 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Focus on a **small set of leading indicators** that signal emerging bottlenecks, plus a few **lagging outcomes** to validate that policies are working.

- **Leading indicators (daily, with intra‑day checks during peaks)**  
  - **Queue age and WIP by station and case type**:  
    - Pre-analytical check, Accession correction, Test preparation, Assay run, Technical validation, Medical validation, Specialist review.  
    - Warning thresholds (approximate):  
      - Pre-analytical median queue > 6h (amber), > 9h (red).  
      - Accession correction median queue > 8h (amber), > 12–14h (red).  
      - Assay-run median queue > 10h (amber), > 18h (red).  
      - Technical validation median queue > 10h (amber), > 16h (red).  
      - Medical validation median queue > 16h (amber), > 22h (red).  
      - Specialist review median queue > 24h (amber), > 36h (red).  
  - **Case-mix and arrival pattern metrics:**  
    - Share of Urgent and Complex-panel cases in the last 4–8h.  
    - Fraction of daily volume arriving in the busiest 2h and 4h windows; large spikes trigger courier and batching policies (Scenario D).  
  - **Rework and repeat precursors:**  
    - Rate of “Pre-analytical check → Request recollection” and “Assay run → Repeat assay” per 100 orders, split by machine and shift; sharp increases suggest sampling or machine problems before overall outcome metrics move.  
  - **Machine and validator health:**  
    - Assay-machine uptime and per‑machine repeat rates, especially on M2.  
    - Availability of Medical validators and specialist; planned vs unplanned absences.  
  - **Policy activation flags:**  
    - Whether R2 priority rules, R3 cross‑trained pool allocation, and R4 outsourcing are currently active, so observed queues can be interpreted in context.

- **Lagging outcome metrics (reviewed daily/weekly)**  
  - **Cycle times and SLA performance by case type:**  
    - Median and p90 cycle times for Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure.  
    - Fraction of Urgent cases exceeding 18h and Routine cases exceeding 72h.  
  - **Overall rework and repeat rates:**  
    - Percentage of cases with at least one recollection or repeat assay; compare to 7.5% normal and 11.8% peak baselines.  
  - **Staff load and quality indicators:**  
    - Overtime hours and error rates for Registration clerks, validators, and specialists to detect hidden burnout caused by the redesign.

These indicators, with clearly defined thresholds and scenario‑specific triggers, allow the lab to **anticipate bottleneck migrations** under varying demand and disruption scenarios and to switch policies on or off before queues and p90 cycle times become clinically unacceptable.
