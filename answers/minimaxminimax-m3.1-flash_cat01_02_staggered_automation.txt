## 1. What is observed

Automation availability is a branch-month exposure. Alpha and Beta have it from April (five observed months each), Gamma from July (two), and Delta and Epsilon never; availability does not mean every case was automated. The data do not report the automated-escalated share, so `automated_fast_path_pct` is an incomplete measure of automation exposure. That fast-path share starts at 25–27% in April adopters and 25% in Gamma, then reaches 48–50% and 32% by August, versus 4% in nonadopters.

Rollout was endogenous. Alpha and Beta were selected for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma followed a readiness pilot, and local practices could change during implementation.

Contemporaneous operations also changed. April routing added about 10% more arrivals to Alpha/Beta and July added 5% to Gamma. Alpha lost two FTEs in May and Gamma one in June; Beta gained one in April and Delta one in June. A centralized fraud desk started in March and received somewhat more borderline automated cases, but branch-level waits are unavailable. Escalation rose in every adopter.

Outcomes are monthly branch summaries. Median and p90 cannot identify which cases or stages changed, and rework/escalation percentages lack event sequences and exact counts. CSAT is a survey mean with 20–45% response rates but no branch-month response counts or composition, so sampling error and selection are unobserved. These are descriptive observations, not causal effects.

**Interference:** The centralized fraud desk means one branch’s adoption can delay another branch’s cases. Consequently, the estimands include spillovers unless branch-specific queue exposure is measured. A no-interference assumption is implausible.

**Treatment versions:** No software version, configuration, routing-rule release, or local-practice identifier is supplied. The automated-escalated share is also missing, so fast-path share is only a marker of exposure. Pooling assumes that the 2026 implementation was sufficiently homogeneous, an untestable treatment-version assumption.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary: cohort-specific staggered-adoption DiD/event study**, implemented as group-time ATTs à la Callaway–Sant’Anna or an interaction-weighted Sun–Abraham event study—not pooled two-way fixed effects | Each branch’s first availability month defines event time; Alpha/Beta form the April cohort and Gamma the July cohort | Delta and Epsilon are never treated. Gamma is not-yet-treated for the April cohort but not for its own post-period; Gamma’s effect therefore relies on Delta/Epsilon | Baseline-only matching or weighting on pre-period outcome levels/trends, arrivals, FTE per arrival, and complexity. Post-rollout covariates are not adjusted in the total ITT because they may mediate the rollout package | Total effect of branch availability, including direct effects, routing consequences, and spillovers; dynamic event-time effects | Cohort-specific counterfactual parallel trends; no anticipation; stable measurement; no spillover contamination; sufficient overlap with comparison branches | Event-time leads and lags by cohort; pre-period placebo dates; leave-one-branch-out estimates; alternative control sets; influence of Alpha versus Beta versus Gamma; Goodman–Bacon-type contribution diagnostics | Only five branches, weak never-treated controls, shared fraud-queue interference, and coincident staffing, routing, and complexity shocks |
| **Alternative 1: synthetic control / synthetic difference-in-differences for each adopter** | Fit each treated branch’s pre-period trajectory; treatment begins at its observed adoption date | Weighted donors: Delta/Epsilon throughout; Gamma only while not yet treated; possible leave-one-donor-out fits | Pre-period predictors only: outcome levels/trends, arrivals, FTE, complexity, and escalation/rework | Adopter-specific total ITT path and cumulative effect | Treated branch is a convex combination of untreated donors in the pre-period; no important post-treatment spillover | Pre-period fit and leave-one-donor-out robustness; placebo adoption dates for donors; in-space and in-time permutation tests; post-period gap decomposition | Two clean never-treated donors are insufficient for a well-fitting convex combination; poor pre-fit and donor contamination |
| **Alternative 2: controlled interrupted time series with cohort-specific segmented trends** | Segmented level and slope at April for Alpha/Beta and July for Gamma; report separate cohorts | Delta/Epsilon as common controls; Gamma before July for the April cohorts | Time-varying arrivals, FTE per arrival, and complexity only in a secondary specification because they may be mediators | Short-run level change and post-adoption slope, especially for learning dynamics | Stable pre-adoption slope; no concurrent slope change from routing, staffing, case mix, or the fraud desk | Residual plots and slope tests; placebo intervention months; alternative windows; interrupted-series estimates versus DiD | Only three pre-period months for Alpha/Beta, weak seasonal coverage, and several important shocks occur shortly after adoption |

The primary design is preferable because it matches the actual decision and timing: availability is assigned at branch level, cohorts start in April and July, and use continues to evolve. A single post dummy would mix a two-month launch shock with later learning, while conventional two-way fixed effects can compare early adopters with already-treated units and assign negative weights under heterogeneous effects. Cohort-specific group-time estimates let Gamma serve as not-yet-treated for April adopters and never adopters remain controls, with April and July results reported separately. The design also makes the estimand explicit: the ITT-like contrast is the effect of offering the branch automation package under the observed staffing and routing context, not the pure effect of choosing an automated path. Actual use is analyzed separately as an endogenous dose; availability is not treated as a valid instrument. Pretrend, placebo, leave-one-branch-out, and influence checks reveal dependence on Alpha, Beta, Gamma, or the two never-treated units. Inference remains deliberately weak: five clusters cannot support routine asymptotic cluster-robust standard errors, so cohort-specific estimates, finite-sample sensitivity, and full magnitude uncertainty are essential. No estimator can repair weak controls, shared fraud-queue interference, or unmeasured local-practice changes.

**Treatment-intensity analysis.** Among the 12 adopter-months, the following slopes remove each adopter’s post-adoption mean but do not adjust for calendar shocks, case mix, or staffing. They are descriptive dose associations, not causal effects.

| Outcome | Within-adopter slope per +10 percentage points of automated fast-path share | Immediate interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| `median_cycle_h` | **1.12 h** | Greater use is associated with lower routine-case duration |
| `p90_cycle_h` | **+1.50 h** | Greater use is associated with a worse tail, consistent with queue displacement rather than net capacity creation |
| `rework_pct` | **+0.99 pp** | Higher-use months have more rework in this raw sample |
| `escalation_pct` | **+0.24 pp** | Greater use is associated with modestly more specialist routing |
| `csat_1to5` | **+0.012 points** | Small positive raw association with no reliable sampling information |

The intensity results are highly fragile: Gamma contributes only two observations and has a large leverage; there is no intensity variation among controls; and early low-use months are also early post-routing or post-shock months. The opposite median and p90 signs are consistent with redistribution of work, but do not establish it.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** There are only three pre-adoption months for Alpha/Beta. Beta’s median rises about 0.5 hours per month, while Alpha is nonmonotonic; Delta and Epsilon also dip in February and rebound in March. For Gamma’s April–June window, median time rises from 39.7 to 45.9 hours, but Delta and Epsilon also deteriorate, and all three rebound in July–August. The March fraud-desk launch is a common break. Alpha/Beta were selected on poor historical performance: their pre-period median averaged 47.4 hours versus 37.4 hours for Delta/Epsilon. A natural rebound toward control performance could therefore create a favorable DiD without automation. Gamma’s readiness pilot may also have changed behavior before the recorded adoption date. Pre-trend evidence is therefore weak rather than reassuring.

**Window sensitivity.**

The raw median contrast is 3.84 to 4.87 hours, or 8.8% to 10.3% of adopter pre-period means; the p90 contrast is 6.18 to 7.13 hours, or 6.5% to 8.3%. Replacing Delta/Epsilon with not-yet-treated Gamma for the Alpha/Beta launch comparison changes the median contrast only from 4.23 to 4.13 hours and p90 from 6.18 to 6.02 hours. These are window-sensitivity ranges, not confidence intervals or causal bounds. If the entire relative gain is mean reversion or a common-shock rebound, the causal effect could be zero; if queue congestion dominates, p90 could be worse.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta were chosen for high volume and weak historical performance | Toward an apparent benefit through regression to the mean | Compare long pre-periods, matched historical ranks, and synthetic pre-fit | A favorable DiD may merely be expected normalization |
| Nonparallel trends | Only three pre-period points for Alpha/Beta, with February dips and March rebounds | Either direction | Cohort event-study leads, placebo dates, and branch-specific pre-slope estimates | Rollout timing cannot be separated from trend differences |
| Time-varying confounding | Product complexity, routing, staffing, and fraud-desk changes overlap adoption | Often toward understating benefit when adverse shocks hit adopters; sign otherwise varies | Reconstruct a timeline; use case-mix-standardized secondary analysis | The availability contrast becomes a rollout-package contrast rather than a technical effect |
| Treatment intensity | Fast-path share is self-selected and rises with learning and demand | Toward benefit if easy cases are automated first; opposite if difficult cases receive more attention | Randomize eligible fast-path cases prospectively; record eligibility and assignment | Actual-use dose-response estimates are confounded |
| Fraud-desk spillover | A shared queue receives cases from all branches and has no branch-level wait data | Usually attenuates contrasts by contaminating controls; can also amplify treated harm | Queue-level origin, arrival, service, WIP, and wait data | No-interference and causal attribution both fail |
| Staffing changes | Alpha and Gamma lost productive capacity near adoption; Beta gained capacity | Alpha/Gamma losses bias toward understating benefit; Beta’s gain can overstate it | Shift-level staffing, capacity, overtime, and FTE-per-arrival analysis | Capacity creation cannot be separated from automation |
| Case-mix change | High-complexity share rose sharply in several branches | Usually makes automation look worse if unadjusted, but routing may select complex adopters | Registration-risk standardization and route-specific outcomes | Apparent median or rework effects may be mix changes |
| Routing campaign | More incoming work arrived in adopting branches at adoption | Could worsen cycle and workload or improve selection; direction depends on mix and capacity | Counterfactual arrivals, route reason, and capacity forecast | Volume growth is misattributed to technology |
| Anticipation/readiness pilot | Gamma prepared before July, and managers could alter practices during rollout | Can bias pre-periods and dynamic coefficients in either direction | Document pilot dates, training, and management actions; use leads | Event-time “learning” may precede recorded treatment |
| Survey nonresponse | Response rates vary 20–45%; counts and respondent mix are unknown | Unknown | Response counts, disposition, weights, and respondent risk/service mix | CSAT changes cannot be interpreted as customer-wide effects |
| Measurement aggregation | Branch medians and p90s conceal case distributions, path transitions, and subgroup effects | Either direction; can make median improvement coexist with tail harm | Event-level durations, quantiles, path shares, and subgroup results | One “cycle-time effect” will be misleading |
| Small number of branches | Only five clusters and three adopters; conventional cluster errors are unreliable | Winner’s curse tends to exaggerate favorable estimates | Exact cohort results, leave-one-out, wild-cluster sensitivity, finite-sample ranges | Small estimates will appear falsely precise |
| Dynamic treatment effects | Launch, learning, and saturation differ materially across cohorts | Conventional TWFE can assign negative weights and mix dynamic effects | Group-time ATT/event-study estimates by cohort | A single average coefficient may have the wrong sign |
| Common shocks and seasonality | Fraud desk, March escalation jump, and product changes affect several branches/months | Either direction | Month-specific common shocks, placebo months, and longer follow-up | Controls do not represent a clean counterfactual |
| Treatment-version heterogeneity | No software/configuration, automated-escalated share, or local-practice identifiers | Unknown | Version/configuration log, policy changelog, and practice coding | Effects may not transport to Delta/Epsilon |
| FTE interpretation | Productive FTE is an average capacity proxy, not headcount or shift coverage | Unknown | Shift roster, hours, queue capacity, and absence data | Staffing confounding may be understated |

## 7. Effect assessment

The intervals below are decision-scenario ranges, not statistical confidence intervals. They are deliberately broad because the unobserved counterfactual cannot be bounded from these data.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

`expand with an embedded evaluation`

Make Delta the next treated branch, and retain Epsilon as not-yet-treated for 8–12 weeks, unless an operational safety review makes delay unacceptable. Delta has lower volume and complexity than Epsilon, so it is the lower-disruption first test. Once Delta is stable, deploy to Epsilon under the same protocol. At Delta, randomize, if technically and ethically feasible, otherwise-eligible fast-path cases to automated-first versus standard manual-first, stratified by registration risk and arrival week. High-risk cases requiring specialist review should remain outside the lottery. If case-level randomization is impossible, use a prespecified stepped timing design rather than relying on managers to choose intensity. The existing adopters are not clean controls.

Collect at least 12 pre- and 12 post-adoption weeks, preferably 26 post weeks, using daily or weekly measurements while retaining monthly summaries for comparability. The event model should contain case identifiers and timestamps for registration, triage, specialist review, every rework loop, resolution, and closure, plus route, risk, disposition, and treatment version. Capture arrival and departure queues, work in progress, waiting and touch time, backlog, capacity, reassignment, fraud-desk queue length and wait, and specialist capacity. Monitor outcomes by branch and by fraud-desk origin so cross-branch congestion and displaced work are visible.

Pre-specify guardrails: pause if p90 or p95 rises more than 5% above the contemporaneous countertrend, rework or escalation rises over one percentage point, CSAT falls 0.10 points or more, abandonment or backlog breaches its cap, or fraud-desk wait rises 10%. Epsilon receives the system only if the randomized or credible adjusted estimate shows at least a 5% median improvement, tail non-inferiority within 2%, no material rework, escalation, or satisfaction harm, and demonstrated net staff-time capacity. Report cohort-specific and actual-use effects with small-cluster uncertainty; do not convert a favorable point estimate into a universal percentage. This staged policy expands earlier than pausing while preserving a genuine counterfactual and a case-level estimate of actual use.

        "2026-04", "2026-06", "2026-07", "2026-08",
    ),
    (
        "Gamma long pre",
        ["Gamma"],
        "2026-01", "2026-06", "2026-07", "2026-08",
    ),
]

did_rows = []
for label, adopters, pre0, pre1, post0, post1 in comparisons:
    for outcome in OUTCOMES:
        adopter_change = np.mean([
            branch_change(b, pre0, pre1, post0, post1, outcome)
            for b in adopters
        ])
        control_change = np.mean([
            branch_change(b, pre0, pre1, post0, post1, outcome)
            for b in ["Delta", "Epsilon"]
        ])
        did_rows.append({
            "comparison": label,
            "outcome": outcome,
            "adopter_change": adopter_change,
            "control_change": control_change,
            "raw_did": adopter_change - control_change,
        })

did = pd.DataFrame(did_rows)
print("\nRAW DID ARITHMETIC; NOT CAUSAL")
print(did.round(4).to_string(index=False))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Transparent treatment-intensity slope:
# pooled post-adopter observations after removing each branch's post mean.
# This is not adjusted for calendar time, staffing, routing, or case mix.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
intensity = df[df["automation"] == 1].copy()
intensity_rows = []
for outcome in OUTCOMES:
    numerator = 0.0
    denominator = 0.0
    for _, group in intensity.groupby("branch"):
        x = group["automated_fast_path_pct"].astype(float).to_numpy()
        y = group[outcome].astype(float).to_numpy()
        xc = x - x.mean()
        yc = y - y.mean()
        numerator += float(np.sum(xc * yc))
        denominator += float(np.sum(xc * xc))
    slope_per_pp = numerator / denominator
    intensity_rows.append({
        "outcome": outcome,
        "slope_per_1pp": slope_per_pp,
        "slope_per_10pp": 10 * slope_per_pp,
    })

print("\nRAW BRANCH-DEMEANED INTENSITY SLOPES; NOT CAUSAL")
print(pd.DataFrame(intensity_rows).round(5).to_string(index=False))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# Descriptive panel models
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
df["log_arrivals"] = np.log(df["arrivals"].astype(float))
df["fte_per_100"] = 100.0 * df["fte"] / df["arrivals"]
model_df = df.dropna(
    subset=[
        "log_arrivals",
        "fte_per_100",
        "high_complexity_pct",
        "automated_fast_path_pct",
    ]
).copy()

try:
    import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
except ImportError as exc:
    raise RuntimeError(
        "statsmodels is required for the panel regressions; install statsmodels"
    ) from exc

def fit_clustered(outcome, treatment_term):
    formula = (
        f"{outcome} ~ C(branch) + C(month_key) + {treatment_term} "
        "+ log_arrivals + fte_per_100 + high_complexity_pct"
    )
    return smf.ols(formula, data=model_df).fit(
        cov_type="cluster",
        cov_kwds={
            "groups": model_df["branch"].to_numpy(),
            "use_correction": True,
        },
    )

# CAUSAL ASSUMPTIONS, EXPLICIT:
# 1. A causal interpretation of the binary-availability model would require
#    cohort-specific counterfactual parallel trends, no anticipation, stable
#    outcome measurement, and no important cross-branch spillovers.
# 2. These assumptions are not established by the supplied data.
# 3. The binary model is printed only as a descriptive availability panel;
#    it is not the preferred causal estimator because timing and effects are
#    heterogeneous.
# 4. The intensity coefficient is associational because actual use is selected
#    by demand, learning, case mix, and managers.
# 5. Availability is not assumed to be a valid instrument for actual use:
#    direct routing, shared queues, and bundled work practices could violate
#    exclusion and monotonicity.
# 6. Post-rollout FTE, routing, arrivals, and complexity may be mediators or
#    confounders; adjustment is therefore not automatically causal.
# 7. Five branch clusters make conventional cluster-robust uncertainty weak.
# 8. Branch-month medians and p90s are aggregate summaries, not raw event
#    durations; they cannot identify which cases or process transitions changed.

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE BINARY-AVAILABILITY PANEL; NOT A PROOF OF CAUSALITY")
for outcome in OUTCOMES:
    result = fit_clustered(outcome, "automation")
    ci = result.conf_int().loc["automation"]
    print({
        "outcome": outcome,
        "automation_coef": result.params["automation"],
        "cluster_se": result.bse["automation"],
        "ci_low": ci.iloc[0],
        "ci_high": ci.iloc[1],
        "n": int(result.nobs),
    })

print("\nDESCRIPTIVE TREATMENT-INTENSITY PANEL; NOT A PROOF OF CAUSALITY")
for outcome in OUTCOMES:
    result = fit_clustered(outcome, "automated_fast_path_pct")
    ci = result.conf_int().loc["automated_fast_path_pct"]
    print({
        "outcome": outcome,
        "auto_pct_coef": result.params["automated_fast_path_pct"],
        "cluster_se": result.bse["automated_fast_path_pct"],
        "ci_low": ci.iloc[0],
        "ci_high": ci.iloc[1],
        "n": int(result.nobs),
    })

warnings.warn(
    "Only five branch clusters are available. Cluster-robust standard errors "
    "and asymptotic p-values are unreliable; use cohort-specific estimates, "
    "leave-one-branch-out checks, and finite-sample sensitivity.",
    RuntimeWarning,
)
warnings.warn(
    "median_cycle_h and p90_cycle_h are aggregated branch-month summaries. "
    "They do not identify case-level distributions, process transitions, "
    "subgroup effects, or changes in queue waits.",
    RuntimeWarning,
)
warnings.warn(
    "These regressions are descriptive. They do not establish causality, "
    "and neither model resolves fraud-desk interference, selection into "
    "adoption, treatment-intensity endogeneity, or survey nonresponse.",
    RuntimeWarning,
)

## 10. Final claim ladder

- `Observed:` Automation availability began in April for Alpha/Beta and July for Gamma, while actual fast-path use rose to 48–50% and 32% by August; median and tail responses differed materially across branches and launch versus mature periods.
- `Suggestive:` Raw adopter-minus-control contrasts show about 4 hours lower median cycle time, 6–7 hours lower p90, and roughly 2 percentage points less rework, but escalation was about 0.9 points higher and these contrasts are not protected from selection, shared shocks, or mean reversion.
- `Conditionally causal:` Under credible cohort-specific staggered-DiD assumptions, the broader availability-and-rollout package may have improved median cycle time over the observed launch horizon, while its effect on p90, rework, escalation, and satisfaction remains materially less certain.
- `Not supported:` “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a general or pure technical effect, a reliable CSAT improvement, or unconditional same-day expansion to all remaining branches is not supported by these data.
