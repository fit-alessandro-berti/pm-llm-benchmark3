## 1. What is observed

**Availability.** Alpha and Beta got the system in April 2026 and Gamma in July. Delta and Epsilon never had it in the eight observed months, giving two adoption cohorts and two never-treated branches.

**Use (treatment intensity).** Availability is not use. Before availability, 4–6% of cases everywhere already completed via the "automated fast path", a baseline artifact. The share jumped to 25–27% in the adoption month, then ramped to 48–50% in Alpha and Beta by August and to 32% in Gamma.

**Selection.** No branch was randomized. Alpha and Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma after a readiness pilot.

**Contemporaneous changes.**
- March: a shared fraud desk started; escalation rose about 2 points everywhere.
- Routing: about 10% more arrivals to Alpha and Beta from April, about 5% more to Gamma from July.
- Staffing: Alpha −2 FTE (May), Beta +1 (April), Gamma −1 (June), Delta +1 (June).
- May: the high-complexity share rose 5–8 points everywhere.
- Arrivals peaked in June and fell in July–August in all branches.
- Managers could change local practices.

**Measurement limitations.** Every outcome is a branch-month summary (median, p90, percentage or survey mean). There are no case-level distributions, path-specific cycle times, specialist or fraud-desk waiting times, or survey response counts. CSAT response rates varied between 20% and 45%. Rework, escalation and complexity move in flat steps, which suggests coarse recording.

**Interference.**
- **Fraud desk.** The desk is shared by all branches, and automated triage sends more borderline cases to it. This can slow down the control branches' escalated cases, which violates the no-interference assumption and inflates any difference-in-differences benefit.
- **Routing.** The routing campaign moved volume *between* branches, so adopters' extra arrivals may be the controls' missing arrivals.

**Treatment versions.** "automation=1" bundles four things that differ by branch and month:
- the system itself;
- a ramping share of cases actually automated;
- extra routed volume (+10% for Alpha and Beta, +5% for Gamma);
- local practice changes and a readiness pilot (Gamma).

The Alpha and Beta version (April, with a routing surge) is therefore not the same treatment as a hypothetical Delta or Epsilon version.

## 3. Identification strategy

**Primary strategy – cohort-by-time difference-in-differences (staggered adoption)**

**Alternative 3 – continuous-treatment (dose-response) difference-in-differences**

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two-way fixed effects on `automated_fast_path_pct` above the 4% baseline (per 10 percentage points), with branch and month effects, plus branch-specific DiD per unit of dose | A continuous dose that varies by month | Lower-dose branch-months, including never-treated months at the 4% baseline | Complexity; cases per FTE only as a check for bad-control bias | E3 (descriptive only) | "Strong" parallel trends across dose levels; uptake does not respond to outcome shocks; no effect of availability except through use | Linearity check (e = 1 versus e = 4); stability of the slope across branches; sensitivity to adding cases per FTE | The dose is completion-defined (post-treatment) and moves together with time since adoption, so dose, learning and calendar cannot be separated. |

**Why the primary strategy is preferable for these data.**

1. **It avoids forbidden comparisons.** A static two-way fixed-effects regression with two adoption dates implicitly uses the already-treated Alpha and Beta as controls for Gamma in July–August, while their own effects are still evolving. Heterogeneous and dynamic effects then contaminate the single coefficient. Here the static coefficient (−10.9 log points on the median) happens to be close to the cohort-by-time aggregate (−10.3), but nothing guaranteed that.
2. **It is transparent.** Every number is a hand-checkable 2×2 contrast on a stated window. With five branches, auditability matters more than efficiency.
3. **It replicates across cohorts.** The two cohorts adopted in different calendar months (April, a flat month; July, a declining month). Seeing similar event-time 0–1 effects in both is the only protection against a calendar shock masquerading as an effect.
4. **Short base periods reduce drift.** They minimize the gap over which parallel trends must hold, and pre-period placebo cells come for free.
5. **The alternatives are worse here.** Synthetic control cannot reproduce Alpha's and Beta's high levels without extrapolating. Interrupted time series lacks enough pre-period data. The intensity model's "dose" is an outcome.

The honest limitation is that the primary design is essentially two 2×2 comparisons against two control branches. Its inference must be design-based and presented as fragile.

**Interpretation.**
- A linear dose-response is **not** supported. Most of the association comes from the initial jump from about 5% to 25–33% use. From e = 1 to e = 4, use rose another 16 points with no additional relative gain in the median.

- Because the dose is completion-defined, the slope is a descriptive mediator association, not a per-case effect.

### 5.4 Pre-trends and regression to the mean

**Arithmetic facts.**
- **April-cohort placebo cells** (base = March): median +1.0 at e = −3 and +2.9 at e = −2; p90 +0.9 and +2.6. With not-yet-treated controls: +3.4 and +3.6.
- **Gamma pre-period cells:** −3.7, +1.6, +3.7, −1.6, +1.8. The pre-period noise band is therefore about ±4 log points.
- **Differential linear pre-trend** (future adopters versus never-treated): +0.14 log points per month (standard error 0.9).
- **A placebo "adoption" in February** gives −1.7 (median) and −1.6 (p90).
- **Rework and escalation are exactly constant within pre-periods.** Parallel pre-trends are therefore uninformative for these outcomes, not reassuring.

**Interpretation.**
- Post-period effects of −6 to −13 log points on the median are about 2–3 times the pre-period noise band. That is not decisive, given only 3 pre-months for the April cohort and 2 control branches.
- **Regression to the mean, Alpha and Beta.** They were selected on poor *historical* cycle times, but their Jan–Mar levels are stable (Alpha 46.9–50.4 h, Beta 45.4–46.4 h) and show no reversion within the pre-period.
- However, the Alpha-minus-Delta gap shrinking from 14.4 h to 6.4 h is exactly what both an effect and long-run convergence would produce. The data cannot tell them apart without earlier history.
- **Regression to the mean, Gamma.** Gamma adopted right after its two worst months (May–Jun, +16% versus Jan–Mar). But the controls spiked similarly (+14% to +20%), and Gamma's idiosyncratic excess is only about 2–4 log points. Regression to the mean could plausibly explain up to a third of Gamma's −10.6.
- **Scale.** In hours, Alpha's late median DiD is −6.9 h (−14% of its pre-period level) versus −16.5 log points. The direction is robust to scale; the magnitude is not.

### 5.5 Sensitivity and bounded conclusions

1. **Median cycle time.**
   - The estimate depends on which control is used: Alpha −13 to −20, Beta −4 to −11, Gamma −9 to −12.
   - Plausible biases toward overstatement are regression to the mean for Gamma (≤ 4), Beta's added FTE, and fraud-desk spillover onto the controls.
   - Spillover mostly delays escalated cases, which are about 9–11% of cases and sit in the tail, so its effect on the controls' median is probably small (≤ 2–3 points).
   - Plausible biases toward understatement are the routing surge and Alpha's FTE loss.
   - Adjusting for load (a bad control) halves the implied effect.
   - **Bounded conclusion:** the ATT on adopters' median is plausibly −3% to −15% (central about −9%). It is not transportable as a single number, and part of it may be pure path substitution.

2. **p90 cycle time.**
   - The DiD ranges from −4 to −14 log points, but the adopters' own p90 **rose** 7–10% (Alpha, Beta).
   - The tail is exactly where fraud-desk spillover lands. If spillover inflated the controls' p90 by 5–10 of their observed +15% to +24% increase, the p90 ATT ranges from about −10% to +3%.
   - The tail ratio widened in 2 of 3 adopters.
   - **Bounded conclusion:** p90 effect in the range −12% to +5%; the sign is not established.

3. **CSAT.**
   - The respondent-mean DiD is +0.06 to +0.12.
   - Suppose response rates shifted between 45% and 20% across periods and non-respondents differ from respondents by δ points. The population mean can then move by up to (0.80 − 0.55)·δ relative to the respondent mean.
   - With δ = 0.3 the ranges are: Alpha [+0.05, +0.20], Beta [−0.02, +0.13], Gamma [+0.03, +0.18]. With δ = 0.5, every interval includes zero.
   - **Bounded conclusion:** −0.05 to +0.15 for the effect on customers; not identified.

4. **Rework.** Estimates range from −1.2 to −2.6 percentage points depending on window and control. The bounds are −0.5 to −2.5 once possible definitional and composition artifacts are allowed for.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**Decision: `expand with an embedded evaluation`.** Median and uptake results justify expanding; tail, fraud-desk and customer effects remain unresolved.

**Next branch.** Epsilon goes first: its complexity (38%) and load resemble Alpha's, making it the best transportability test. Delta stays a concurrent comparison for 16 weeks, then adopts (a two-step stepped wedge). A coin flip may set the order; the leverage comes from within-branch randomization.

**Assignment mechanism.**
- In Epsilon, randomize eligible cases at registration by case-ID hash: 50% automated in weeks 1–6, 80% in weeks 7–10, then full availability.
- Alpha, Beta and Gamma keep a 10% randomized manual-triage holdout for three months. It cheaply measures the case-level path effect and substitution versus capacity creation.
- Freeze routing reallocations during the evaluation.

**Measurement windows.** At least 8 weeks of pre-period case-level logs in Epsilon and Delta. At least 16 weeks of follow-up, since uptake plateaued after about 4 months; the primary endpoint is weeks 9–16.

**Data to collect.**
- Event logs with timestamps for triage assignment and override, specialist enqueue/start/end, fraud-desk handoff and return, rework loops, resolve, close, and 30-day reopening.
- Path-specific cycle-time quantiles (median, p90, p95), split into waiting and processing time.
- Daily WIP and queue lengths per queue; productive hours by activity; survey invitations and responses per case.

**Spillover monitoring.** Monitor the fraud desk as its own unit: arrivals by source branch and trial arm, waiting-time percentiles, and Delta's escalated-case cycle times before and after Epsilon goes live. Throttle borderline referrals if the desk's p90 wait rises more than 15%.

**Guardrails for customer harm.** Revert to the previous assignment probability if Epsilon's p90 exceeds its manual arm by more than 10% for two consecutive weeks, if reopenings or rework rise by 2 or more points, or if CSAT falls by 0.15 or more on at least 100 responses. High-complexity cases always keep a manual override.

**Decision rule for scale-up (pre-registered).** Scale to Delta and beyond only if, at week 16:
1. the randomized contrast shows a median reduction for eligible cases, with a 90% interval excluding zero;
2. branch p90 is non-inferior (upper bound at most +5%);
3. fraud-desk waiting time is non-inferior (at most +10%);
4. manual-path cases are not slower (the capacity-creation check);
5. response-weighted CSAT is non-inferior within −0.1.

If medians improve but tails or the fraud desk worsen, redesign the escalation thresholds before expanding further.

def att_gt(col, g_t, t, control="never"):

        ctrl = df[df["cohort_t"].isna()]["branch"].unique()

        ctrl = df[(df["cohort_t"].isna()) | (df["cohort_t"] > max(t, g_t - 1))]["branch"].unique()
        ctrl = [c for c in ctrl if c not in treated]
    base = g_t - 1

    def val(bs, tt):
        v = df[(df["branch"].isin(bs)) & (df["t"] == tt)][col]
        return np.log(v).mean() * 100 if col in LOG_OUTCOMES else v.mean()

    if len(ctrl) == 0:
        return np.nan, []
    return (val(treated, t) - val(treated, base)) - (val(ctrl, t) - val(ctrl, base)), list(ctrl)

# With 5 clusters and many fixed effects the cluster covariance is often rank-deficient;
# a zero or NaN cluster SE signals a degenerate fit, not precision.
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore", message="invalid value encountered in sqrt")


def fit(formula, data):
    m = smf.ols(formula, data=data)
    return m.fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": pd.factorize(data["branch"])[0]})


def wild_cluster_p(formula, data, term, null=0.0):
    """Restricted wild cluster bootstrap with Rademacher weights, enumerating all
    2^G sign vectors (G=5 -> 32 draws; the p-value can't be smaller than ~1/32)."""
    full = fit(formula, data)
    t_obs = (full.params[term] - null) / full.bse[term]
    restricted_formula = formula.replace(f" + {term}", "").replace(f"{term} + ", "")
    r = smf.ols(restricted_formula, data=data).fit()
    fitted, resid = r.fittedvalues, r.resid
    y_name = formula.split("~")[0].strip()
    codes, uniques = pd.factorize(data["branch"])
    tstats = []
    for signs in itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=len(uniques)):
        d = data.copy()
        d[y_name] = fitted + resid * np.array(signs)[codes]
        f = fit(formula, d)
        tstats.append((f.params[term] - null) / f.bse[term])
    tstats = np.array(tstats)
    tstats = tstats[np.isfinite(tstats)]
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(tstats) >= abs(t_obs) - 1e-12))

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: In the month automation became available, all three adopting branches (in two separate cohorts, at different calendar dates) showed a jump of about 21 points in cases completing via the automated fast path, a drop of about 2 points in recorded rework, and a rise of about 1 point in escalation, relative to non-adopting branches. Their median cycle times then worsened less than the non-adopters' did, while Alpha's and Beta's absolute p90 rose by 7–10%.
- Suggestive: Adopting branches' medians ran roughly 5–15% below what the non-adopter trends imply. This is mainly consistent with fast-path substitution: about half of cases moved onto a short path and manual workload per FTE fell by about a third. It is not clear evidence that the freed capacity made other cases faster, and it cannot be separated from routing, staffing and local practice changes.
- Conditionally causal: If parallel log trends with Delta and Epsilon hold, there was no fraud-desk spillover, and there was no anticipation, then availability reduced adopters' median cycle time by about 10% (plausibly 3–15%) within one to five months, with a smaller and possibly zero effect on p90 and a genuine increase in specialist or fraud-desk referrals.
- Not supported: "Automation reduces cycle time by X%" as a single organization-wide statement, or as a prediction for Delta and Epsilon. This applies especially to tail cycle time, customer satisfaction, and any claim that automation created capacity rather than re-sorting cases between paths.
