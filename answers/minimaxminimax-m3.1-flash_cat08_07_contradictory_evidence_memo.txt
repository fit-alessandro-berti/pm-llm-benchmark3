## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive leadership  
**Subject:** Decision on Redesign R  
**Recommendation:** Approve **E**, implemented as **C plus D**: a constrained hybrid deployment with a controlled learning program. Do not scale R nationally and do not fully roll it back.

R has a real but narrow observed benefit. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%. The strongest benefit is for simple, low-risk houses, 34% of the pilot: median duration improved by 6 days, p90 by 8 days, first-time-right by 3 percentage points, and complaints by 1 point.

The downside is material. Overall p90 rose from 31 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45 to 62 days. First-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, manual activation from 7% to 15%, second visits from 11% to 18%, and SLA breaches from 13% to 16%. NPS declined from +21 to +17. Visible cost fell from €184 to €161, but the incomplete estimate including complaints and revisits rose from €198 to €207.

These are associations, not a clean causal estimate. The pilot had more apartments and older buildings, risk attributes were incomplete before week 3, and no randomized comparison is reported. The most plausible synthesis is that R accelerates easy cases while shifting complexity into exceptions. Survey skipping may misclassify apartments; missing permit checks, billing before successful activation, failed visits, and overloaded coordinators and second-visit teams plausibly lengthen tails and create rework.

The process-mining artifacts reinforce that interpretation. Pilot model fitness rose to 0.94, but precision fell to 0.51 and generalization to 0.56: the model fits permissive pilot paths, not necessarily a sound target process. Observed alignments include billing before activation, repeated installation without survey, and complaint escalation after billing. Permit-check absence is an expected-model deviation, not confirmed proof of a violation. The handover network became simpler, but the working-together network became more centralized; coordinator absence could stall cases.

No component is a universal winner. Coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling appear valuable for simple cases; survey skipping and post-billing activation appear risky for complex cases. National scale would amplify the tail and rework risks. Full rollback would discard the simple-house gains and may not address baseline bottlenecks.

Immediately, retain coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling only for simple low-risk houses. Suspend survey skipping outside a controlled trial and suspend billing before successful activation. Retain baseline survey, permit, and activation controls for apartments, old buildings, business, and rural cases pending evidence. Add shared-team fallback and context, a coordinator-load guardrail, named ownership for manual activation, and conformance monitoring.

Run a stratified, randomized regional experiment with complete risk data, start timestamps, reason codes, 60-day downstream cost, and separate speed-versus-working-service feedback. Broader rollout requires mix-adjusted, segment-level non-inferiority on quality, tail, cost, and SLA; no unapproved activation or permit deviations; and demonstrated capacity and absence resilience. If gates fail, revert affected components rather than abandoning all of R.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

1. **High fitness but low precision.** Fitness asks how much of the observed behavior the model explains; precision asks how much of the model’s permitted behavior is meaningful and consistent with the real process. A permissive model can achieve high fitness by allowing skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits, and billing before activation. Thus, 0.94 fitness with 0.51 precision is not evidence that R is a good target process. Generalization of 0.56 also weakens the case for national transfer. The alignments are diagnostically useful but not interchangeable: move-on-log findings describe observed paths, while the permit finding is a mismatch with the model’s expectation.

2. **Improved median but worse tail.** The median is insensitive to the upper tail. Simple houses and merged validation/scheduling can improve the typical case while apartments, old buildings, failed visits, second visits, missing permits, manual activation, and post-billing complaints lengthen the tail. The segment p90 changes support this explanation. However, the pilot’s harder building mix and sparse start timestamps mean the aggregate tail comparison is not causal by itself.

3. **Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** The handover-of-work network became simpler, which can improve accountability and reduce transfer cost. The working-together network became more centralized, so actual work depends more heavily on a small number of coordinators and activation specialists. At 93% coordinator utilization and 92% second-visit utilization, absence or overload can create queues and stalled cases. Fewer handovers therefore do not necessarily mean fewer coordination dependencies.

4. **Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.** The €161 figure excludes complaint handling after week 4. Manual activation, failed visits, second visits, billing disputes, and complaints may move costs to later stages or other teams. The €207 estimate is therefore a warning signal, not a settled full-cost result. A 60-day downstream-cost view is needed before making an economic scale decision.

5. **Simple-house improvement but apartment/old-building deterioration.** The redesign changes the risk profile of each path. Simple houses benefit from faster validation and scheduling. Apartments and old buildings require surveys, permits, and reliable activation; the skip rule may be wrong for them, and missing permit checks or billing before activation can create rework and complaints. Business and rural cases also show worse tails and quality. The pilot’s greater apartment and old-building share further complicates aggregate comparisons. The segment table is consistent with heterogeneous effects, but it is observational.

The DFG changes reinforce this: some lower-frequency edges are intentional consequences of merging or skipping, while rising manual-activation, failed-visit, second-visit, and billing-to-complaint edges are consistent with work being displaced downstream. Edge shifts alone do not establish causation.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Approve E now, with C as immediate containment and D as the learning plan.**

- **Immediate action:** Continue a reversible deployment of coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple, low-risk houses only. Restore survey, permit, and activation controls for apartments, old buildings, business, and rural cases pending evidence. Require successful activation before billing outside a documented, customer-protected exception. Require reason codes, named ownership, and customer notification for manual activation. Add shared-team fallback, context sharing, a coordinator-load guardrail, and conformance monitoring. The reported 52% field-survey utilization suggests there may be capacity to restore surveys for risky cases, subject to validation.

- **Pause:** National scaling; survey skipping outside the controlled experiment; billing before successful activation; and unapproved manual activation. Do not treat high model fitness as evidence that the process is safe or generalizable.

- **Scale:** Scale only the coordinator-ownership and merged-scheduling components for simple low-risk houses, in stages and with shared fallback. Do not scale survey skipping or post-billing activation to other segments until they independently pass the same gates.

- **Measure next:** Run M1 and M6 with complete segment/risk data; implement M2, M3, and M7 immediately; collect M4 for 60 days; use M8 to distinguish speed from working-service value; and test M5 before relying on a single-point-of-failure ownership model.

- **Broader-rollout threshold:** Use a pre-registered, mix-adjusted comparison against baseline with segment-level non-inferiority. Illustrative gates are first-time-right no more than 1 percentage point below 82%, complaints no more than 0.5 points above 6.1%, SLA no more than 1 point above 13%, p90/p95 within pre-agreed margins of 31/45 days, manual activation and second visits no worse than 7%/11%, and 60-day full cost no higher than €198 beyond a pre-agreed margin. No segment should materially deteriorate. There should be zero unapproved billing-before-activation or permit deviations, demonstrated coordinator and second-visit capacity, and a successful absence-resilience test. The process model should be revalidated on held-out data, with precision and generalization at least at baseline or demonstrably effective conformance controls.

If these gates pass, expand one segment or region at a time. If they fail, revert the affected component while retaining the simple-house deployment. If evidence remains inconclusive, maintain the safe subset and baseline controls rather than using the median improvement to justify national scale.
