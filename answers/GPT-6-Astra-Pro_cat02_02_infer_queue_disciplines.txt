# 2. Reconstructed dispatch decisions

## Interpretation and comparison assumptions

**A1 — Readiness.** For the baseline reconstruction, a scanned job is ready unless a stated constraint excludes it. This assumption is necessary because arrival is not necessarily operational queue entry. An unrecorded parts hold or release condition remains an alternative explanation, not an observed fact.

**A2 — Scan uncertainty.** If the recorded arrival is \(a\), physical arrival may have occurred in \([a-2,a]\). Thus some jobs might already be present before their scan. This uncertainty does **not** justify moving arrival later than the scan or explaining substantial post-scan delays. Boundary candidates are marked `?`.

**A3 — Resource feasibility.** A job occupies one resource, required skills are conjunctive, and a job already running elsewhere is not a waiting candidate. Thus D05 requires D-F, while D07 requires D-X. Calendar-fit filtering is stated explicitly where used; whether a job can be interrupted for a meeting is not assumed known.

**A4 — Rule definitions.** FIFO ranks feasible jobs by original arrival, including suspended jobs unless a separate resumption rule applies. Priority chooses the lowest priority number, with FIFO as an explicit tie-break assumption. SEPT uses supplied dispatch-visible estimates—not realized durations. For suspended B01, the remaining-time proxy is its original estimate minus accumulated active service; its actual updated expectation is unavailable.

At an actual interruption, the comparison includes the just-suspended job as a continuation/resumption candidate. A non-preemptive rule would not itself cause that interruption. Strict preemptive priority is therefore also assessed separately at urgent arrivals.

Within each queue’s table, job prefixes are omitted: `04` means A04 in QA, B04 in QB, and so forth. **“Any” means that skill-based routing alone permits any listed candidate; it does not determine their order.** Appointment entries identify reservations or due jobs, not a complete sequencing rule. Future appointment jobs might be held outside their release window.

# 3. Supporting and contradicting cases

## QA: strong ordinary FIFO, weaker appointment semantics

**Supporting jobs:** A02, A05, and A07 provide useful evidence. A02 precedes both higher-priority and shorter A04. A05 precedes higher-priority A08 and shorter A07. A07 then precedes priority-1 A08.

**Apparent exception with a known explanation:** A03 overtakes older A02 exactly at its appointment. This supports appointment insertion rather than arbitrary FIFO abandonment.

**Unresolved or interpretation-dependent exceptions:** A06’s five-minute lateness contradicts exact appointment timing, but may satisfy an unknown start window. The two-minute post-A03 gap requires an unrecorded turnaround, dispatch latency, or availability explanation. Calibration explains only 08:52–08:58.

A04’s selection at 08:58 should not be counted as distinctive priority evidence: FIFO, priority, and SEPT all agree on A04.

## QB: selective preemption, not a priority guarantee

**Supporting jobs:** B02 and B01 directly establish interruption and resumption. The urgent-arrival reason supports an urgency override; the later B01 → B04 → B06 → B07 sequence supports a FIFO-like backbone.

**Challenging jobs:** B03 challenges strict priority when B01 resumes at 09:31. B05 challenges it again at 09:48. B06 is bypassed by lower-priority B01 at 10:14 and B04 at 10:28. B07 challenges a strong appointment-precedence interpretation.

The second stop has a recorded operational reason, so treating it as unexplained random noncompliance would be inappropriate. Nevertheless, the reason is insufficient to reconstruct the eligibility changes it caused.

B01 receives \(14+17+14=45\) active minutes over an 86-minute start-to-completion span. Its two pauses total \(15+26=41\) minutes. **Those forty-one minutes are interruption-related elapsed delay, not a measured switching or restart cost.**

## QC: local shortest-job evidence, substantial contradictory choices

**Supporting jobs:** C02, C03, and C06 each support selection by the supplied shortest estimate.

**Challenging jobs:** C04 challenges online SEPT because C06 is already waiting. C05 challenges both FIFO and SEPT, although priority or deadline escalation could explain it. C01 challenges SEPT, static priority, and earliest-deadline selection when C07 remains waiting.

A committed-batch explanation is plausible for C04, but not observed. An aging explanation is plausible for C01, which has waited 100 minutes when selected, but there is no recorded age threshold. Inferring a threshold specifically chosen to reproduce that one decision would overfit.

The resulting deadline misses are observable: C05 completes **44 minutes late**, and C07 completes **34 minutes late**. They challenge any claim that the observed hybrid reliably protects deadlines.

## QD: skills explain exclusions; they do not fully explain waiting

**Supporting jobs:** D05 and D07 support conjunctive skill restrictions. D04 preceding D08, and D07 preceding D10, support FIFO-like ordering within feasible resource queues rather than priority or SEPT.

**Constraint-explained delay:** D05’s 73-minute arrival-to-start wait comprises fourteen minutes until D02 completes, fifteen minutes serving D03, a twenty-four-minute pre-meeting gap, and the twenty-minute meeting. The gap is explainable by uninterrupted calendar fit once D03 has been selected.

**Unresolved exception:** D10 could fit on D-F during that gap. Fixed assignment to D-X, preparation requirements, or reservation of D-F for blocked D05 could explain its continued waiting, but would be undocumented additions.

In particular, **head-of-line blocking** is a viable alternative to fixed assignment: D-F may reserve its next position for older D05 and refuse to backfill with D10. That reproduces idleness without implying D10 lacks the necessary skills.

Two schedule counterfactuals quantify why these ambiguities matter.

**QC at 11:40.** Assuming both jobs are ready, no extra setup effects, and the supplied estimates remain applicable, selecting C07 before C01 gives:

* C07: 11:40–11:58, before its 12:00 deadline.
* C01: 11:58–12:34, before its 13:00 deadline.

The observed order instead makes C07 thirty-four minutes late. The alternative preserves C1’s final completion time of 12:34 and both jobs’ deadline compliance, although C01 completes eighteen minutes later. That is a meaningful trade-off between old-job waiting and deadline protection—not a cost-free improvement for every job.

**QD at 11:36.** Under the same no-hidden-constraint assumption, D10 could run on D-F from 11:36–11:48. Its observed completion is 12:28, so this would finish it **forty minutes earlier** without moving the supplied service intervals of any other job or conflicting with the meeting. This is a strong diagnostic counterfactual, but not an operational recommendation until ownership, setup, and reservation constraints are verified.

**Scan delay has a bounded role.** It can add A05 to QA’s possible 08:20 candidate set, C05 to QC’s possible 10:10 set, or D03 to D-F’s possible 11:01 set. It cannot make C06’s seven-minute-old scan disappear at 10:32, or explain B05 being bypassed after its 09:46 scan. A separate operational readiness hold could do so, but that is a different hypothesis requiring different evidence.

**Preemption costs are unidentified.** QB shows no separate switching interval between consecutive service segments at minute resolution. That does not prove zero restart effort, context loss, or preparation cost. A cost-aware hybrid might protect ongoing or suspended work, but the data do not support estimating that cost or its override threshold. Likewise, splitting D05 around the meeting would require evidence that the task is safely interruptible.

**Noisy estimates and strategic understatement are possibilities, not observed explanations.** Across all 32 supplied jobs, `est_min` equals the sum of active service-segment lengths. These records therefore show no realized estimation error.

More importantly, inaccurate predictions do not themselves explain a departure from an estimate-based selection rule: SEPT follows the estimates visible to dispatch, even when those estimates are wrong. To reconcile QC with SEPT, one would need evidence of **revised dispatch-time estimates, additional setup costs, or different eligibility**, not merely the general possibility of noisy estimates.

**SLA-triggered policy switching remains plausible but unproven.** C05 was already endangered by 10:18 using its supplied estimate. Its later selection could reflect a threshold triggered by an actual breach rather than anticipated lateness. However, choosing C01 over C07 at 11:40 would then require a further aging, commitment, or job-specific exception. One universal deadline-protection rule does not explain both decisions.

The collection unit should be the **dispatch decision**, linked to job and resource lifecycle events—not merely the completed-job trace. Start, suspend, resume, completion, release, reservation, and no-dispatch decisions are needed to distinguish scheduling behavior from recorded completion order.

Candidate policies should then be replayed against those decision states. A useful model must explain both selections and exclusions, while avoiding an unlimited collection of post hoc exceptions.

# 7. Final synthesis

**QA is the most identifiable dispatch-order pattern:** ordinary FIFO with appointment insertion explains several choices that directly contradict priority and SEPT. Exact appointment semantics remain unresolved.

**QD has the clearest identifiable constraint—conjunctive skill eligibility—but not a fully identifiable dispatcher.** Resource-level FIFO, appointment reservations, fixed assignments, and no-backfill blocking remain partly observationally equivalent.

**QC is least identifiable.** It exhibits genuine shortest-estimate choices, but online SEPT fails at important opportunities. Batching, urgency, and aging can explain portions of the sequence, not one uniquely supported overall policy.

**QB demonstrates selective preemption, not a strict priority guarantee.** The most dangerous inferred claim would be that “QB always protects urgent jobs through preemptive priority.” B05’s bypass and B01’s resumptions show why relying on that guarantee would be unsafe.

The central distinction is between **a job being present, being eligible for a particular resource, and being selected under the active policy**. These records reveal the first reasonably well, the second incompletely, and the third only through a small number of discriminating decisions.
