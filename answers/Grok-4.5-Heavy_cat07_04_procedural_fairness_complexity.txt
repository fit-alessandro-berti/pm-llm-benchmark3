# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness must be assessed across multiple dimensions rather than raw outcome parity alone. The evidence supports examination of the following:

- **Outcome parity**: Raw approval rates differ markedly (Group A 78 %, B 63 %, C 69 %, D 58 %). Median and 90th-percentile cycle times, appeal rates, and rework rates are also higher for B, C, and D. These unadjusted differences are real but cannot be interpreted as unfairness without reference to case mix.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity**: After preliminary controls for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, residual gaps remain—most notably for Group B (−5.5 percentage points approval probability, +2.6 days cycle time, +4.4 pp appeal probability) and Group C (+4.1 days cycle time). Group D shows smaller residual approval and cycle-time gaps but elevated appeal probability. These residuals are informative yet still subject to residual confounding.

- **Procedural burden**: Groups B and C experience systematically longer and more complex paths (elevated shares of Request Missing Documents, Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and Supervisor Review). Rights-preserving support steps impose additional activities, handovers, and waiting even when the underlying needs are legitimate.

- **Timeliness**: Cycle-time distributions and step-level waits are longer for non-A groups. Language-support and accommodation queues show multi-day medians (3.8 d and 4.7 d). Service-level targets do not isolate these steps, so the burden falls on the applicants who need them.

- **Consistency of routing**: Routing to Fraud Review (higher for D), Language Support (higher for B), and Accommodation Review (higher for C) tracks measured attributes and stated policy, suggesting rule-based consistency at the policy level. However, adjusted elevations in Supervisor Review probability (+8 pp for B, +5.5 pp for C) and regional variation in handovers raise questions about consistency of front-line escalation and regional practice.

- **Quality/accessibility of support**: Language and accommodation supports exist and are rights-preserving, yet capacity is tightly constrained (7 bilingual caseworkers, 5 accommodation specialists). This produces queues, forced handovers, and potential differences in interaction quality or continuity that are not captured by binary outcome measures.

- **Appeal/rework burden**: Higher appeal and reopen/rework rates for B, C, and D (partially residual after adjustment) impose additional procedural load. The appeal path itself has a median cycle time of 30.7 days. Whether appeals reflect outcome dissatisfaction, explanation quality, or process friction cannot be fully distinguished.

- **Resource-allocation fairness**: Group A cases are distributed across 52 caseworkers. Group B cases concentrate on a small bilingual pool and overloaded regional officers; Group C cases concentrate on a small specialist pool (some of whom also perform supervisor review). The resulting load imbalance and extra handovers create differential intensity of procedural friction.

Taken together, the dimensions indicate that differences in procedure are only partly explained by measured complexity; capacity constraints around rights-preserving steps and residual adjusted disparities remain relevant to fairness.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A robust strategy must separate legitimate complexity-driven differences from residual disparities, potential discrimination, and irreducible uncertainty.

- **Matching or stratification variables**: Stratify or exact/ propensity-match on risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, disability_accommodation_requested, language_preference, and month. Within strata of comparable measured complexity, compare approval probabilities, cycle times, path frequencies, wait times, and resource sequences.

- **Path/variant comparison**: Extract variant distributions overall and within matched strata. Contrast frequencies and performance of the dominant simple path (V1) against language-support (V4), accommodation (V5), fraud (V3), and appeal (V6) paths. Residual differences in path composition or length after matching indicate procedural divergence beyond measured needs.

- **Resource-assignment comparison**: Compare the sets of resources handling each group, concentration indices, and handover counts. Examine whether bilingual or accommodation specialists are used as primary case owners or only as late hand-off points, and whether workload intensity differs after matching on complexity.

- **Waiting-time decomposition**: Decompose total cycle time into service time versus waiting time at each activity and transition, with particular attention to support steps and supervisor queues. Compare conditional waits for cases that do versus do not require language or accommodation support, again within complexity strata.

- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates**: Treat risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as potentially endogenous. Re-estimate residuals after (a) excluding them, (b) instrumenting or bounding the degree of historical bias, and (c) using alternative proxies. Assess how much of the residual gap for Group B and C is sensitive to these assumptions.

- **Subgroup checks by region and channel**: Re-run the stratified comparisons separately by region (especially the two regions with noted supervisor backlog) and by channel (online versus in-person). Test for interactions that would indicate that disparities are concentrated in particular operational contexts rather than systemic.

Additional declarative checks (log skeleton or DECLARE constraints) can test whether required support steps occur when indicated and whether extra optional steps appear disproportionately for certain groups. Uncertainty bands and multiple-specification reporting are essential; the strategy aims to bound rather than eliminate residual confounding.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most important procedural disparities visible in the supplied data are:

**Path/variant structure.** Group A is concentrated on the short, low-complexity path V1 (42 %, median 5.8 days). Group B shows elevated shares of V2 (Request Documents) and especially V4 (Language Support Contact, 24 %). Group C shows a large share of V5 (Accommodation Review + Supervisor Review, 31 %). Group D shows elevated V3 (Risk Screening → Fraud Review → Supervisor Review, 28 %) and higher appeal paths. These patterns track measured attributes (language preference, accommodation requests, risk_score_band) and are therefore largely consistent with legitimate routing policy. They nevertheless produce systematically longer and more complex procedures for B, C, and D.

**Waiting-time differences.** Support-step waits are substantial: Language Support → Eligibility Assessment median 3.8 days (Group B); Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment median 4.7 days (Group C). Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents is also longer for B (1.6 d versus 0.4 d for A). Supervisor Review → Decision Draft waits are elevated for B and C. Early-stage waits are modestly longer for non-A groups, consistent with higher in-person shares. These waits are only partially explained by individual case complexity; scarce specialized capacity is a major contributor.

**Resource assignment and handovers.** Group A cases are dispersed across 52 caseworkers. Group B cases are disproportionately assigned to 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, producing more general → bilingual → general handovers. Group C cases concentrate on 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom also perform supervisor review), producing general → specialist → supervisor sequences. Group D routing into the fraud-review queue aligns with higher measured risk. Concentration and extra handovers increase coordination cost, waiting, and potential information loss.

**Justified versus concerning.** Higher Fraud Review for high-risk cases, Language Support for minority-language applicants, and Accommodation Review for disability requests are policy-legitimate and consistent with measured needs. Residual concerns remain: (a) capacity scarcity converts rights-preserving steps into multi-day queues and extra handovers; (b) adjusted elevations in Supervisor Review probability for B and C are not fully explained by measured complexity; (c) absence of separate service-level targets for support steps allows those steps to absorb delay without accountability; (d) regional variation in backlog and handovers introduces inconsistency. The net effect is that applicants who legitimately require language or accommodation support experience greater procedural burden even after controlling for the documented complexity indicators that are available.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least four mechanisms, spanning legitimate and concerning explanations, can generate the observed patterns:

1. **Legitimate complexity-driven routing.** Higher shares of incomplete documentation (especially Group B), elevated risk_score_band (Group D), minority-language preference, and disability-accommodation requests correctly trigger additional activities under existing policy. This accounts for a substantial fraction of the variant-frequency and cycle-time differences.

2. **Resource scarcity and specialization bottlenecks.** The small pools of bilingual caseworkers (7) and accommodation specialists (5) create queues and force multi-step handovers. Applicants whose cases require these rights-preserving supports therefore experience longer waits and more transitions solely because of capacity limits, not because of greater individual complexity. Dual-role specialists who also perform supervisor review further concentrate load for Group C.

3. **Channel–documentation feedback loops.** Higher in-person shares and language barriers can increase measured incompleteness, which in turn triggers Request Missing Documents steps that then wait for bilingual staff. The feedback amplifies procedural burden for Group B beyond the initial need for language support.

4. **Potential historical or measurement bias in risk scores and priors.** Group D’s elevated Fraud Review is consistent with measured risk_score_band, yet if the score itself reflects historical disparities or proxies correlated with group membership, the routing inherits that bias. Higher prior-case counts may similarly reflect earlier process friction rather than solely applicant behavior.

Additional contributing mechanisms include differential supervisor escalation (possibly caution, possibly regional backlog or lower front-line trust) and downstream appeal/rework arising from residual outcome differences or weaker decision explanations. The combination of legitimate policy application and capacity-driven friction produces residual adjusted disparities that cannot be dismissed as pure case-mix differences.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The supplied attributes leave substantial uncertainty:

- **Measurement bias and historical bias in risk scores.** risk_score_band may embed past disparities; controlling for it can “adjust away” part of the very discrimination under investigation. documentation_completeness is endogenous to language barriers, digital access, and accommodation needs, so it partially reflects process design rather than exogenous applicant characteristics.

- **Missing applicant needs.** No direct measures exist for digital literacy, transportation barriers, cognitive or literacy load of forms, cultural factors, or the quality and dignity of interactions. Binary case_outcome omits benefit amount, explanation adequacy, and applicant comprehension.

- **Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity; real-time queue lengths, individual caseloads, expertise matching, and fatigue effects are invisible. Handover counts indicate friction but do not quantify information loss or rework caused by transitions.

- **Appeal quality and process experience.** Appeal_flag and rework rates lack information on appeal success, stated reasons, or whether rework stems from staff error versus applicant issues. The experience of support steps (whether they felt respectful and effective) is unobserved.

- **Other gaps.** Intersectionality, unmeasured complexity, and the possibility that support steps improve outcome quality (not just add time) cannot be assessed. The preliminary adjusted model therefore leaves residual confounding of unknown magnitude.

These limitations imply that residual disparities after controls cannot be cleanly partitioned into discrimination, unmeasured legitimate factors, or measurement error.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review.** Expand the bilingual and specialist pools.  
  Side effects: fiscal cost, training lag, possible under-utilization if demand is variable, risk of diluted expertise if hiring is rushed. Improves equity of access but does not automatically close residual approval gaps.

- **Service-level targets for support steps.** Introduce explicit median and 90th-percentile wait targets for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review, distinct from overall cycle-time targets.  
  Side effects: pressure to rush substantive review, displacement of other work, metric gaming, overtime or backlog elsewhere if capacity is unchanged.

- **Routing redesign to reduce handovers.** Assign bilingual or accommodation-capable staff as primary owners from the outset, or enable asynchronous specialist consultation without full case transfer.  
  Side effects: training investment, possible loss of specialized depth, new bottlenecks if specialists become primary owners for too many cases, risk of inconsistent application by less-experienced generalists.

- **Decision-explanation improvements.** Standardize plain-language, translated, and accessible notifications that explain reasons, evidence, and appeal rights; train staff on respectful communication.  
  Side effects: initial drafting and training cost; possible short-term rise in appeals if explanations surface additional grounds; longer decision-draft time until templates mature.

- **Monitoring adjusted disparities.** Maintain ongoing stratified and matched dashboards of cycle times, path shares, waits, approvals, and appeals by group, with uncertainty bands and periodic deeper audits.  
  Side effects: administrative burden, risk of gaming or over-reaction to noisy residuals, privacy considerations with granular demographic data.

- **Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.** Conduct disparate-impact and calibration audits of the risk model; review how completeness is assessed and whether language or disability supports are offered before incompleteness is recorded; consider re-engineering scores to reduce historically biased proxies.  
  Side effects: possible reduction in predictive power (higher undetected fraud risk or lower detection rates); temporary disruption of case-mix patterns; compliance and legal review requirements.

Additional complementary measures include cross-training, improved self-service accessibility, and proactive outreach for incomplete documentation in relevant languages. Support steps themselves should not be removed merely to shorten cycle times.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process exhibits clear raw disparities in outcomes and cycle times, a substantial portion of which is associated with measured differences in risk, documentation completeness, language preference, accommodation needs, and channel. After preliminary adjustment, residual differences persist—particularly longer cycle times and higher procedural intensity for Groups B and C, driven in large part by capacity-constrained rights-preserving support steps and associated handovers.  

These residual procedural burdens are concerning from a fairness perspective because they systematically impose extra waiting and coordination costs on applicants who exercise legitimate support rights. At the same time, the evidence does not support a strong claim of intentional discrimination: routing rules appear largely consistent with stated policy, and unobserved complexity, measurement bias in risk scores and documentation completeness, and incomplete resource data leave substantial uncertainty.  

The most defensible characterization is therefore that the process is **procedurally imperfect with residual fairness concerns centered on capacity and design of support pathways**, rather than demonstrably fair or demonstrably discriminatory. Further stratified process-mining analysis, capacity expansion, SLA design for support steps, and bias auditing of risk and completeness measures are required before stronger conclusions can be drawn.
