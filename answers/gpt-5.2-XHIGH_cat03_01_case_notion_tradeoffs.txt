## 1. Analytical assumptions

- **Case notion**: a rule that projects the OCEL into a traditional event log by choosing a “lead” object type and creating one trace per object instance of that type. An event belongs to a case **only if it is directly related (E2O) to that object**, unless I explicitly apply a transformation.
- **Convergence**: multiple distinct business entities/lifecycles are **merged into one case** (e.g., flattening on `Invoice` makes `V2` a single case that inherently covers both `O2` and `O3`; flattening on `Delivery` makes `D2` a single case that inherently covers multiple packages/orders).
- **Divergence**: a business lifecycle that stakeholders often think of as “one thing” is **split across multiple cases**, or a single coordinated operation is **copied** into several cases (e.g., flattening on `Package` splits `O1` into `P1` and `P2`; flattening on `Order` copies shared dispatch `e16` into cases `O1`, `O2`, `O3`).
- **Copied shared events**: I treat them as **both**:
  - **Evidence** of coordination/interaction (e.g., `e13` packs `P3` for `O2` and `O3` together; that is operationally meaningful).
  - **Duplication** for frequency-based metrics and “how many times did this activity happen” questions (because the same physical/logical action appears multiple times after flattening).
- **Permitted analytical transformations (always labeled and compared to the direct view)**:
  1. **Weighting instead of copying for metrics**: for a flattened log on lead type \(T\), weight an event by \(1/k\) where \(k\) is the number of related objects of type \(T\) (e.g., `e16` has \(k=3\) orders: `O1`,`O2`,`O3`).
  2. **Controlled relation propagation through O2O** (for specific questions, not as default): attach an event to a lead object if there exists a **typed, qualified path** through O2O relations that supports causality/ownership (e.g., propagate `Issue Invoice` from `V2` to `O2` and `O3` is already direct in `e19`, but propagating invoice/payment to `Package`/`Delivery` would be a deliberate enrichment via `Order → Item → Package → Delivery` links). I do **not** take the transitive closure blindly because it can explode duplication and blur ownership.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1) **Cross-order package `P3` creates frequency inflation in `Order` and `Item` flattenings**
- **Affected flattening**: `Order`, `Item`
- **Cite**: `P3` packs `I21` and `I31`; packing event `e13` relates to `O2`,`O3`,`I21`,`I31`,`P3`
- **Resulting distortion**: In an `Order`-flattened DFG, `Add Item→Pack Package` is counted once in `O2` and once in `O3`, even though `e13` is one packing action. In an `Item`-flattened DFG, `e13` appears in both item cases `I21` and `I31`, inflating “number of packs” and making the packing workload look higher than the 4 unique pack events (`e11–e14`).
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** for workload/frequency KPI and variant prevalence; **informative** if your goal is to detect multi-order batching (because the duplication pattern itself signals that `P3` is shared).

2) **Cross-order package `P4` makes order `O3` look like it has “repeated packing/shipping” variants**
- **Affected flattening**: `Order`
- **Cite**: `e14` packs `P4` with `O3`,`O4`,`I32`,`I41`; `O3` also participates in `e13` for `P3`
- **Resulting distortion**: `O3`’s trace contains two `Pack Package` events (`e13` and `e14`) and two `Dispatch` events (`e16` and `e17`), which can be misread as **rework** or “double shipping” caused by internal order issues. In reality, it is a legitimate split across two packages/deliveries, and each pack is additionally cross-order (with `O2` and `O4`).
- **Interpretation**: **Context-dependent**. For customer experience, “multiple shipments” is real; for operational conformance, labeling it “nonconforming repetition” is misleading unless the model explicitly allows split shipments and cross-order packaging.

3) **Consolidated delivery `D2` produces spurious independence and synchronized timestamps across three orders**
- **Affected flattening**: `Order`
- **Cite**: dispatch `e16` and confirm `e24` relate to `D2` and to orders `O1`,`O2`,`O3` (and packages `P2`,`P3`)
- **Resulting distortion**: In `Order` traces for `O1`,`O2`,`O3`, the same dispatch time (11:05) and confirm time (16:00) appear. Performance analysis “pack→dispatch” and “dispatch→confirm” per order will treat these as independent outcomes, potentially overstating statistical confidence and hiding that one logistics decision drove all three. DFG edges also inflate: `Dispatch→Confirm Delivery` is counted three times for the same delivery execution.
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** if used for per-order carrier SLA attribution or bottleneck staffing; **informative** if used to surface consolidation practices (the shared event itself is the signal).

4) **`Package` flattening can hide that packages are not “owned” by a single order**
- **Affected flattening**: `Package`
- **Cite**: `P3` includes orders `O2` and `O3` via `e13`; `P4` includes `O3` and `O4` via `e14`
- **Resulting distortion**: A package-centric DFG (Pack→Dispatch→Confirm) looks perfectly well-formed, inviting an analyst to implicitly assume “each package corresponds to one order shipment.” That assumption fails for `P3` and `P4`, which would break per-order cost allocation (“shipping cost per order”) and would also break naive conformance rules like “one order maps to one package.”
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** for attribution/accounting; **informative** for logistics if you explicitly keep/inspect related-order cardinalities per package (e.g., count how often a package touches >1 order).

5) **Consolidated invoice `V2` makes per-order payment behavior look like two payments per order**
- **Affected flattening**: `Order`
- **Cite**: invoice issue `e19` relates to `O2`,`O3`,`V2`; payments `e22` (`M2`) and `e25` (`M3`) relate to `O2`,`O3`,`V2`
- **Resulting distortion**: In both `O2` and `O3` cases, the sequence includes two `Record Payment` events. A DFG will show an edge `Record Payment→Record Payment` twice (once in each order), suggesting instalment payments are an order-level phenomenon. But the instalments belong to the **shared invoice** `V2`, so attributing the full two-payment pattern to each order double-counts the “number of payments needed per order.”
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** for “payment friction per order” KPIs; **informative** for detecting invoice consolidation (orders sharing identical invoice/payment events is a signature of merged billing).

6) **Partial payments create an apparent “loop” that is ambiguous between instalments and rework**
- **Affected flattening**: `Invoice` (and secondarily `Order`)
- **Cite**: `V2` has `Record Payment` at `e22` (12:40) and `e25` (next day 09:00); O2O says `M2` partially settles and `M3` completes settlement
- **Resulting distortion**: The invoice DFG contains `Issue Invoice→Record Payment→Record Payment`. Without settlement semantics (partial vs complete), an analyst might interpret the second payment as correction/rework. Conversely, the gap between `e22` and `e25` is a real credit/collection delay that matters for cash-flow but is invisible in item/package views.
- **Interpretation**: **Informative** for finance if you encode settlement type (from O2O qualifiers) as an event attribute; **harmful** if you treat the loop as process instability rather than intentional instalments.

7) **Near-singleton lifecycles: `Payment` flattening is structurally incapable of control-flow discovery**
- **Affected flattening**: `Payment`
- **Cite**: each payment `M1`…`M4` appears only in one event (`e21`,`e22`,`e25`,`e26`)
- **Resulting distortion**: Any discovered DFG/log skeleton is degenerate (no directly-follows edges), and performance metrics like “time to settle” are undefined because there is no predecessor event within the case. Analysts may wrongly conclude “no process exists” or may attempt to join to other objects post hoc, essentially abandoning the case notion.
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** if mistakenly used as a main event log; **informative** only as an audit list (e.g., verify all invoices have at least one payment object, such as `V2` having `M2` and `M3`).

8) **Lifecycle truncation in `Item` and `Package` flattenings biases end-to-end lead-time conclusions**
- **Affected flattening**: `Item`, `Package`
- **Cite**: item traces begin at `Add Item` (`e05–e10`) and never include `Close Order` (`e28–e31`); package traces begin at `Pack Package` (`e11–e14`) and never include invoicing/payment (`e18–e22`,`e25–e26`)
- **Resulting distortion**: If you compute “cycle time” on item/package cases, you measure only a sub-process (item handling or shipping). That can make the process appear compliant and fast, masking delays or policy choices in billing/collection (e.g., `V2` completes settlement only at `e25`, after `e24` and before `e27` for `O3`).
- **Interpretation**: **Context-dependent**. Valid for operational sub-KPIs (“pack→confirm per package”), harmful if reported as “order lead time.”

9) **Order split shipments create false “repetition” edges that can be misread as rework**
- **Affected flattening**: `Order`
- **Cite**: `O1` has two dispatches (`e15` via `D1`, `e16` via `D2`) and two delivery confirms (`e23` via `D1`, `e24` via `D2`)
- **Resulting distortion**: A DFG for orders will contain `Dispatch→Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery→Confirm Delivery` edges for `O1`. Without recognizing the two distinct deliveries (`D1` vs `D2`) and packages (`P1` vs `P2`), this looks like repeated attempts, escalation, or exception handling.
- **Interpretation**: **Informative** about split fulfillment if you label events by delivery/package cardinality; **harmful** if interpreted as failure/rework.

10) **Propagated-relation enrichment can create an “everything relates to everything” artifact**
- **Affected flattening**: any flattening where you propagate broadly (commonly `Delivery` or `Package`)
- **Cite**: if you propagate invoice/payment from orders (`V2`,`M2`,`M3`) through items and packages to deliveries (`D2`,`D3`), then `D2` could inherit finance events of `O2` and `O3` (and also `O1` via `P2`)
- **Resulting distortion**: Delivery traces could become `Dispatch→Issue Invoice→Record Payment→Confirm Delivery`, implying the carrier process includes billing and payment steps. This introduces spurious DFG edges and performance biases (e.g., attributing credit-collection delay `e22→e25` to delivery `D2`).
- **Interpretation**: **Harmful** unless the question explicitly studies cross-domain synchronization and you keep propagated vs direct views separated with clear semantics.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

- **Flattened logs worth creating (purpose-limited)**:
  - **`Order`-flattened**: use for customer/order KPIs (create-to-close per `O1`…`O4`, number of shipments per order, pay-before/after-deliver patterns). Use **event weighting** for shared multi-order events like `e16` and `e24` to avoid inflating “how many dispatches occurred.”
  - **`Package`-flattened**: use for warehouse/logistics throughput (pack→dispatch→confirm per `P1`…`P4`) and to detect cross-order packaging by inspecting related-order counts (not by assuming 1:1).
  - **`Delivery`-flattened**: use strictly for carrier SLA (dispatch→confirm durations for `D1`,`D2`,`D3`), explicitly noting that `D2` consolidates multiple orders/packages.
  - **`Invoice`-flattened**: use for finance (issue→settle timing, instalment behavior on `V2` via `e22` and `e25`).
  - **`Item`-flattened**: optional for micro-operations (item handling path); avoid using it for end-to-end conclusions.
  - **Avoid `Payment`-flattened as a process log**; treat payments as supporting objects/attributes.

- **Questions each flattened log can answer safely (examples grounded in identifiers)**:
  - **Order**: “How many deliveries does `O1` require?” (two: `D1` via `e15/e23`, `D2` via `e16/e24`); “Is `O4` paid after invoice?” (`e20→e26`).
  - **Package**: “Is there a standard shipping sequence?” (Pack→Dispatch→Confirm for `P1`: `e11→e15→e23`; similarly for others); “Which packages are cross-order?” (`P3` in `e13`, `P4` in `e14`).
  - **Delivery**: “Dispatch-to-confirm duration for `D2`?” (`e16` 11:05 to `e24` 16:00).
  - **Invoice**: “How often are invoices paid in instalments?” (here: `V2` has two payments `e22` and `e25`).

- **Questions that require object-centric views (and which view)**:
  - **“How much consolidation is happening, and where?”**: use **object interaction summary** and **OTG** to quantify multi-order packages (`P3`,`P4`), multi-package deliveries (`D2`), and multi-order invoices (`V2`).
  - **“Which activities create cross-object coupling?”**: use **ET–OT graph** (e.g., `Dispatch` links to `Order`,`Item`,`Package`,`Delivery` in `e16`; `Issue Invoice` links to `Order`,`Invoice` in `e19`).
  - **“What is the true workload of packing/dispatching?”**: use **OC-DFG** frequency definitions based on unique events vs object-counted interactions (e.g., 4 unique `Pack Package` events `e11–e14` vs 6 item-pack interactions).
  - **“Are there distinct execution modes (single-order vs consolidated)?”**: use **equivalent-execution clustering** only on a representation that encodes consolidation explicitly (e.g., treat `e16` as “Dispatch(consolidated)” via attributes), otherwise clustering can be dominated by copied shared events.

- **Reconciling contradictory findings (flattened vs object-centric)**:
  - Compute and report a **duplication factor** per flattening (flattened-event-count / unique-event-count) to explain why “Pack Package frequency” differs across `Item` vs `Package`.
  - When a flattened DFG suggests a loop (e.g., `Record Payment→Record Payment`), validate whether it is **one object’s true lifecycle** (`V2`) or a **copied interaction** (`O2` and `O3` inheriting the same invoice payments).
  - Prefer object-centric interaction metrics to decide attribution, then use flattened logs for readable narratives and stakeholder-facing KPIs with explicit caveats.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

- **Decision criteria**
  - **End-to-end coverage** of the business storyline (creation → fulfillment → billing → payment → closure).
  - **Minimal lifecycle truncation** for the primary narrative.
  - **Controlled handling of consolidation** (cross-order packages `P3`,`P4`; consolidated delivery `D2`; consolidated invoice `V2`) without pretending these are independent per-case events.
  - **Stakeholder interpretability**: most stakeholders naturally reason in terms of orders, but operations and finance have their own “native” objects.

- **Strongest supporting evidence**
  - Only **`Order`** objects appear across *all* phases in the provided E2O relations: create (`e01–e04`), fulfillment (`e11–e17`,`e23–e24`,`e27`), finance (`e18–e22`,`e25–e26`), and close (`e28–e31`). This makes order a defensible primary narrative for “end-to-end.”
  - The process explicitly includes multi-order constructs (`P3`,`P4`,`D2`,`V2`) that are **not well represented** if orders are the *only* lens; hence the need for secondary lifecycles on `Package/Delivery` (logistics) and `Invoice` (finance).

- **Strongest counterargument**
  - Order flattening **copies** key shared events (`e13`,`e14`,`e16`,`e19`,`e22`,`e24`,`e25`,`e27`), inflating frequencies and creating misleading “repetition” edges (e.g., `Dispatch→Dispatch` in `O1` via `e15→e16`). This can produce incorrect conclusions about workload, variants, and conformance if treated as a plain single-case log.

- **Residual risk**
  - Even with a primary/secondary setup, **attribution remains ambiguous**: e.g., how to allocate `V2`’s two payments (`M2`,`M3`) between `O2` and `O3`, or how to allocate `D2`’s performance to `O1` vs `O2` vs `O3`. Any per-order “cost” or “delay” metric that touches consolidated objects can be biased.

- **Conditions that would change the decision**
  - If the main goal is **logistics performance**, switch the lead to **`Package` or `Delivery`** (because `D2` consolidation is the core phenomenon).
  - If the main goal is **cash-flow/collections**, switch the lead to **`Invoice`** (because instalments on `V2` are central).
  - If consolidation becomes pervasive (many more `e16`-like multi-order events), I would move toward **C (reject a single lead)** and rely primarily on OC-DFG + interaction summaries.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

print(ocel)

# --- Inspect basic frequencies (direct OCEL view) ---
events_df = ocel.events.copy()
objects_df = ocel.objects.copy()

print("Activity frequency (unique events):")

    events_df["omap_size"] = events_df["ocel:omap"].apply(lambda x: len(x) if isinstance(x, (list, set, tuple)) else None)

    print(events_df["omap_size"].describe())

log_item = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Item")

# --- Quantify flattening-induced duplication (do not infer “workload” from raw flattened counts) ---
unique_event_count = events_df["ocel:eid"].nunique() if "ocel:eid" in events_df.columns else len(events_df)
flat_event_counts = {
    "Order": sum(len(t) for t in log_order),
    "Item": sum(len(t) for t in log_item),
    "Package": sum(len(t) for t in log_package),
    "Delivery": sum(len(t) for t in log_delivery),
    "Invoice": sum(len(t) for t in log_invoice),
    "Payment": sum(len(t) for t in log_payment),
}
duplication_factors = {k: v / unique_event_count for k, v in flat_event_counts.items()}
print("\nFlattened event counts:", flat_event_counts)
print("Duplication factors vs unique events:", duplication_factors)

# --- Discover DFGs and log skeletons on selected flattened logs ---
dfg_order, sa_order, ea_order = pm4py.discover_dfg(log_order)
skeleton_order = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log_order)

dfg_package, sa_package, ea_package = pm4py.discover_dfg(log_package)
skeleton_package = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log_package)

dfg_invoice, sa_invoice, ea_invoice = pm4py.discover_dfg(log_invoice)
skeleton_invoice = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log_invoice)

print("\nOrder DFG size:", len(dfg_order), "start:", sa_order, "end:", ea_order)
print("Package DFG size:", len(dfg_package), "start:", sa_package, "end:", ea_package)
print("Invoice DFG size:", len(dfg_invoice), "start:", sa_invoice, "end:", ea_invoice)

# --- Object-centric discovery to explain interactions behind shared/cross-object events ---
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
interactions_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

print("\nInteractions summary (object coupling):")
print(interactions_summary)

# Note: Use OC-DFG/OTG/ET-OT outputs to decide whether observed flattened variants
# are true behavioral variants or artifacts of copied shared events (e.g., e16/e24, V2 payments).
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1) **Down-weight or remove copied shared events (required)**
- In the `Order`-flattened log, identify multi-order events such as `e13` (`O2,O3`), `e16` (`O1,O2,O3`), `e19` (`O2,O3`), `e24` (`O1,O2,O3`), and `e27` (`O3,O4`).
- Recompute DFG and performance metrics under:
  - **Uniform weighting** \(1/k\) per event per related order,
  - **Exclusion** of multi-order events (to isolate “purely order-local” behavior),
  - Compare to the raw copied view to quantify inflation in counts and edges like `Dispatch→Confirm Delivery`.

2) **Direct E2O versus propagated relations (required)**
- Build an explicitly labeled propagated variant (e.g., propagate `Invoice/Payment` events to `Package`/`Delivery` via `Order→Item→Package→Delivery`) and compare:
  - How many additional events each delivery would inherit (especially `D2`),
  - Whether new edges like `Dispatch→Record Payment` appear and whether they are semantically acceptable.
- Keep the direct variant as the baseline; treat propagation as hypothesis-testing, not truth.

3) **Change the lead object (required)**
- Re-run the same “top KPIs” under `Order`, `Package`, and `Invoice` leads:
  - Order: create→close times (`e01→e28`, `e02→e29`, etc.).
  - Package: pack→confirm times (`e11→e23`, `e13→e24`, `e14→e27`).
  - Invoice: issue→final payment (`e18→e21`, `e19→e25`, `e20→e26`).
- Validate that conclusions are stable only within the domain each lead actually preserves.

4) **Compare frequency metrics across event-, object-, and interaction-based denominators (required)**
- For “packing frequency,” compare:
  - **Unique events**: 4 pack events (`e11–e14`),
  - **Item interactions**: 6 item-pack involvements (`I11`,`I12`,`I21`,`I31`,`I32`,`I41`),
  - **Order interactions**: pack events touch orders (`O1` twice via `e11,e12`; `O3` twice via `e13,e14`).
