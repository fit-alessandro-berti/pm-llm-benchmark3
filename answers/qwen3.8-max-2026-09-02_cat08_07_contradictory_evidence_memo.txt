# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### High fitness but low precision

Fitness measures how well the discovered model can replay the observed event log. The pilot has high fitness (0.94) because many cases follow the new simplified coordinator path. Precision measures how much the model restricts behavior to observed, controlled patterns. The pilot’s precision is low (0.51) because the log contains many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, failed visit, second visit, billing start, and complaint escalation.

This combination is a warning sign. The process is being followed often enough to be represented, but it is not tightly controlled. The low-precision model is not a reliable “standard process” for conformance checking; it is a signal that the redesign has created many possible exception paths.

### Improved median but worse tail duration

The median improved because simple, low-risk cases move faster through merged validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership. These cases have fewer handoffs and fewer dependencies.

The tail worsened because complex and exception-heavy cases are now more exposed to risk: missing permit checks, wrong survey skips, failed visits, second visits, manual activation, billing disputes, and coordinator overload. Median metrics hide these cases. The p90 and p95 metrics reveal that the redesign may be accelerating the easy majority while allowing the difficult minority to stall or cycle through rework.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Fewer handovers can improve accountability and reduce waiting. The evidence shows 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. However, the working network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. Coordinator utilization reached 93% in some regions, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent because context was not shared.

Thus, the redesign reduced visible handovers but increased hidden dependency. A process with fewer handovers is not automatically more resilient. If one person owns the case end-to-end without backup, absence or overload can create larger delays than a shared-team model.

### Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

Visible cost before complaint handling improved from EUR 184 to EUR 161. This likely reflects fewer handovers, fewer field surveys for some cases, and faster simple-case processing.

But the estimated cost including complaint/revisit handling worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207, and this estimate is incomplete because downstream complaint handling after week 4 is excluded. The pilot also shows higher second visits, manual activations, complaints, and billing-to-complaint escalations. Therefore, the apparent cost saving may be partly a shift of cost from upstream installation activities into downstream service, complaint, and rework activities.

### Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings

The redesign is not segment-neutral. Simple low-risk houses benefit strongly: faster median, better p90, higher first-time-right, and fewer complaints. These cases can tolerate merged steps and limited surveys.

Apartments, medium-risk addresses, older buildings, permit-sensitive cases, and business customers are more dependent on permits, surveys, correct activation, and coordination with building infrastructure. For these segments, skipping surveys or starting billing before activation creates failed visits, rework, billing disputes, and complaints. The pilot regions also had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, so the aggregate metrics are negatively affected by both segment mix and segment-specific harm.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally**

- **Advantages:** Captures median cycle-time improvement, reduces handovers, simplifies ownership, and may reduce visible cost for simple cases.
- **Risks:** Ignores worsened p90/p95 duration, lower first-time-right, higher complaints, higher second visits, higher manual activation, billing-before-activation conformance issues, missing permit checks, and high coordinator utilization. Pilot regions are not fully representative, and downstream cost is incomplete.
- **When reasonable:** Only after the risky components are fixed and evidence shows segment-level non-inferiority on quality, tail duration, compliance, and full downstream cost. Not reasonable now.

**B. Roll back to the baseline process**

- **Advantages:** Stops the risky survey-skip and post-billing activation behavior, restores known controls, and gives time to investigate.
- **Risks:** Throws away clear gains for simple low-risk houses, including faster median duration, better first-time-right, and fewer complaints in that segment. It also does not address the possibility that coordinator ownership and merged scheduling have real value.
- **When reasonable:** If evidence shows severe compliance harm, customer harm, or downstream cost that cannot be controlled. It may also be appropriate for specific high-risk segments, but not as a blanket response.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**

- **Advantages:** Preserves the accountability and handoff-reduction benefits while removing two of the riskiest redesign elements.
- **Risks:** Coordinator ownership alone may still create bottlenecks and resilience problems if utilization remains high and context is not shared. It may also not improve complex segments enough to justify the organizational change.
- **When reasonable:** If coordinator ownership is shown to be the main source of benefit and can be supported with backup coverage, utilization caps, and shared case context. This is a plausible fallback if hybrid deployment becomes too complex.

**D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**

- **Advantages:** Produces causal evidence, improves segment and risk data, tests survey skipping and activation controls, and avoids premature national scaling.
- **Risks:** Delivers benefits more slowly, requires discipline and instrumentation, and may be operationally costly. Results may still be affected by regional differences.
- **When reasonable:** When uncertainty is high and the decision depends on whether specific redesign components are harmful or beneficial. This should be embedded in the recommended hybrid approach.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and run measurement before wider scale**

- **Advantages:** Keeps the clear simple-house gains, contains the riskiest elements in controlled settings, addresses compliance concerns, and creates evidence for future decisions. It respects the segment-level heterogeneity in the data.
- **Risks:** Creates a two-speed process, increases governance complexity, and requires clear case classification. It also depends on coordinator capacity, backup coverage, and reliable conformance monitoring.
- **When reasonable:** Now. It is the most defensible option because it preserves value where evidence is strong and limits exposure where evidence is weak or negative.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E — segmented hybrid deployment with controls, targeted experiments, and measurement before broader scale.**

### Immediate action

1. **Classify cases at address validation** into at least:
   - simple low-risk houses;
   - apartments / medium-risk addresses;
   - older or permit-sensitive buildings;
   - business customers;
   - rural long-distance installs.

2. **Deploy the redesigned process only for simple low-risk houses**, using:
   - coordinator ownership;
   - merged address validation and appointment scheduling;
   - backup coordinator assignment;
   - coordinator utilization monitoring.

3. **Introduce hard controls for all non-simple segments**:
   - mandatory permit check where permit-sensitive;
   - no survey skip unless explicitly approved under experiment;
   - no billing start before successful activation test except in a tightly monitored experiment;
   - activation specialist sign-off for manual activation.

4. **Implement conformance monitoring immediately** for:
   - billing start before activation test;
   - missing permit check in older apartment buildings;
   - repeated installation visit without prior survey where survey is required;
   - complaint escalation after billing start.

5. **Set operational guardrails**:
   - coordinator utilization target below 85% where possible;
   - shared case notes or backup ownership when coordinator is absent;
   - escalation path for cases stuck longer than a defined threshold after failed visit.

### What to pause

- Pause national scaling of Redesign R.
- Pause survey skipping for apartments, medium-risk addresses, older buildings, permit-sensitive cases, business customers, and rural installs.
- Pause billing start before successful activation test outside a controlled and monitored experiment.
- Pause expansion into new regions until segment-level evidence and downstream cost evidence are available.
- Pause use of the low-precision pilot process model as a normative standard until controls and reason codes are stabilized.

### What to scale

- Scale coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for **simple low-risk houses only**, with backup coverage and utilization caps.
- Scale conformance monitoring and reason-code instrumentation across all pilot and future deployment regions.
- Scale the simple-house scheduling improvement where it is clearly beneficial, while preserving baseline controls for complex segments.

### What to measure next

Priority order:

1. **M7:** Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and missing permit checks.
2. **M1:** Randomized survey-skip experiment by building-risk stratum.
3. **M6:** Validation of the building-risk predictive rule.
4. **M4:** Full 60-day downstream cost after billing start.
5. **M3:** Structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.
6. **M2:** Start timestamps for installation visit, activation test, and manual activation.
7. **M5:** Coordinator absence resilience test comparing single ownership with shared-team backup.
8. **M8:** Customer experience split between speed to appointment and working service after installation.

### Thresholds for broader rollout

Broader rollout beyond simple low-risk houses should require evidence that, for the target segment:

- median cycle time is no worse than baseline and preferably improved;
- p90 cycle time is not materially worse than baseline;
- first-time-right installation is at or above baseline;
- complaint rate is at or below baseline;
- second-visit rate is at or below baseline;
- manual-activation rate is at or below baseline;
- full 60-day cost including complaint handling is not materially worse than baseline;
- permit-check conformance is effectively 100% for permit-sensitive cases;
- billing-before-activation violations are below a tight exception threshold, with approved reason codes;
- coordinator utilization remains at a sustainable level, with absence coverage tested;
- the predictive survey-skip rule, if used, shows validated precision/recall and does not increase rework or complaints in randomized testing.

If these thresholds are met, management can expand the redesign segment by segment. If they are not met, the affected components should be rolled back or redesigned before further scale.
