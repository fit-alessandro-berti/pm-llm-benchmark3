## 2. Rule Interpretation

**RR1 — Drafter/approver separation**

- Let \(D(c)\) be the set of normalized humans who perform `Draft_Change_Request` for case \(c\), and \(A(c)\) the set who perform `Approve_Change` for \(c\).
- **Rule:** For every case \(c\), \(D(c) \cap A(c) = \emptyset\). If any human appears in both sets (after alias normalization), RR1 is violated.
- The rule applies to all risk levels and scopes; no exceptions for low-risk or emergency changes are stated.

**RR2 — Implementer/verifier separation**

- Let \(I(c)\) be the set of normalized humans who perform `Implement_Change` for case \(c\), and \(V(c)\) the set who perform `Verify_Implementation` for \(c\).
- **Rule:** For every case \(c\), \(I(c) \cap V(c) = \emptyset\) when evaluated on human identities.
- Service accounts are first treated as distinct from humans; a second pass substitutes high-confidence service-account attributions with the underlying human to detect hidden violations.

**RR3 — High-risk approval authority**

- For each **HIGH-risk** case \(c\) that is **not** using the emergency path (emergency flag false), every `Approve_Change` event must be performed by:
  - the standing QM (Asha), or
  - a delegate with a **QM delegated role** whose delegation interval contains the event timestamp, whose scope includes the case’s `scope`, whose principal is absent/unavailable at that timestamp, and whose delegation does not violate a “no subdelegation” constraint.
- Evaluation is done at **event time**, not from current master data. Eva, for example, is not treated as QM at any time point in this log.

**RR4 — Emergency substitution**

- For **HIGH-risk, emergency=true** cases:
  - `Approve_Change` may be performed by the standing IC (Diego) or a valid IC delegate (e.g., Farid under D-IC-03) whose delegation interval and scope cover the event and respect any “no subdelegation” rule.
  - A QM or valid QM delegate must perform `Retrospective_QM_Review` **within two business days**.
- **Business-day counting for RR4:**
  - Business days are Monday–Friday according to the site calendar; 2026-08-15 and 2026-08-16 (weekend) are not business days; there are no holidays in the period.
  - For each emergency case, I anchor the deadline to the **calendar day of `Implement_Change`**:
    - Day 0 = date of `Implement_Change`.
    - The QM (or delegate) must complete `Retrospective_QM_Review` by the end of the **second subsequent business day**.
    - Example: implementation on Tuesday 2026-08-11 → deadline is Thursday 2026-08-13 23:59 local (two subsequent business days: Wed and Thu).
- **Actions by a principal while on leave:**
  - A principal (e.g., Asha as QM) retains their underlying authority even while formally on leave; actions taken personally under their own identity (e.g., CHG-504 approval from VPN) are treated as **technically authorized** under RR3/RR4.
  - Such actions are nonetheless flagged as **control-objective weaknesses** (leave policy, workload, and independence), not as delegation violations.
- **Emergency exception credibility:**
  - The emergency path is treated as justified when `emergency=true` and the scope (`cooling_system`) is plausibly safety-related; the underlying “safety rationale” is not in the log and must be assessed manually.

**RR5 — Identity evidence**

- Alias normalization is mandatory before SoD and authority checks; for example, `u_bk` and `b.koch` are consolidated to “Ben Koch.”
- Service accounts such as `svc_qms` are **not** considered human identities; they cannot by themselves satisfy SoD or authority requirements.
- Where session evidence (workstation, badge, token) allows attribution of a service-account action to a specific human, that human is used in a second-pass analysis with a confidence tag (e.g., “weak” or “strong”):
  - Weak attribution (CHG-503) is treated as an indicator, not proof.
  - Strong attribution (CHG-509) is treated as the most likely actor but still does not override hard SoD rules that rely on personal accounts.
- Actions taken under a human’s own personal account during their recorded absence (e.g., Ben’s account during sick leave) are treated as **identity anomalies** requiring investigation; they are neither automatically accepted nor rejected.

**RR6 — Suspicion versus proof**

- Repeated resource pairs, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations, and concentrated handovers are treated as **risk indicators** only.
- They support classifications such as `SUSPICIOUS_PATTERN` or “elevated monitoring required” but do **not** alone justify classifying a case as `UNAUTHORIZED`.
- Stronger evidence (conflicting absence and activity logs, invalid delegations, explicit rule breaches) is required for `UNAUTHORIZED`.
- A later database entry can support an earlier delegation **only when** there is corroborating evidence with an earlier timestamp (e.g., a signed PDF predating the interval). A database record created after use and lacking independent evidence would be insufficient.
  - In this dataset, D-QM-01 has a signed PDF timestamped 2026-08-09; despite the QMS record being created on 2026-08-12, I treat D-QM-01 as valid from 2026-08-10 for its stated scope.
  - D-QM-02 is recorded before its effective interval and is treated as valid for its narrow scope.
  - D-IC-04 is treated as invalid because it conflicts with the explicit “no subdelegation” constraint of D-IC-03 and lacks controlled registration.

## 4. Collusion and Alternative-Explanation Analysis

**Clara–Ben repeated pair on high-risk production-equipment changes (18 of 23 cases)**

- **Benign explanation:** Clara and Ben may be the primary Process Engineer and QM delegate assigned to a specific production cell, naturally leading to frequent Clara-drafts/Ben-approves pairs without any intent to collude.
- **Adverse explanation:** Stable pairing without rotation could enable tacit or explicit collusion, whereby Clara drafts and Ben approves insufficiently challenged changes or masks nonconformant implementations.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Rotational assignment records, workload distribution across other engineers, historical rejection rates for Clara’s drafts compared to peers, and content-based audits of a sample of Clara–Ben changes (e.g., whether they are systematically riskier or sloppier) would help distinguish assignment-driven pairing from collusive behavior.

**Reciprocal pattern on low-risk training changes (Ben drafts, Clara approves)**

- **Benign explanation:** For training documents, Clara might be the subject-matter expert or line manager who logically approves Ben’s drafts; the reciprocity reflects job responsibilities rather than favoritism.
- **Adverse explanation:** Reciprocal approvals could be a “you approve mine, I approve yours” arrangement to bypass scrutiny, particularly if training changes alter how procedures are documented or interpreted.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Comparison of training-document approval patterns across other pairs, examination of whether Clara ever rejects Ben’s drafts, and qualitative review of the content to see if reciprocal cases are unusually permissive or error-prone.

**Service-account verification from Diego’s workstation on CHG-503**

- **Benign explanation:** `svc_qms` could represent an automated verification tool or shared QA session routinely run from WS-DM-04 due to local configuration, with minimal human discretion involved and any engineer in the area allowed to trigger it. Diego’s badge entry may simply mean he opened the room, not that he personally executed the verification.
- **Adverse explanation:** Diego might habitually perform his own verifications using `svc_qms` from his workstation to hide RR2 violations, effectively implementing and verifying high-risk changes without independent review.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Application logs showing who initiated the `svc_qms` workflow, whether other users or workstations also run `svc_qms`, and, if available, user-specific authentication within the QMS application when `svc_qms` steps are executed.

**Service-account verification from Eva’s verifier station on CHG-509**

- **Benign explanation:** `svc_qms` may be a standard automation account that applies a scripted verification pipeline, while Eva oversees results at the verifier station; physical presence and tokens indicate supervision rather than direct manual verification.
- **Adverse explanation:** Eva could be using `svc_qms` to avoid logging her personal account for verification tasks, obscuring that she both approves (as QM delegate) and verifies, undermining independence.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Configuration of WS-IV-02 (is `svc_qms` the only account allowed?), application-level audit trails tying `svc_qms` actions to Eva’s FIDO token events, and process descriptions clarifying whether independent verification must be performed by someone other than the approver.

**Ben’s account activity during sick leave on CHG-506**

- **Benign explanation:** Ben may have briefly worked despite being recorded as on sick leave (e.g., remote return to the office), or HR may have back-dated the sick-leave record for payroll/administration purposes, making his approval operationally legitimate though poorly documented.
- **Adverse explanation:** Someone else in the office could have used Ben’s account (shared password, unattended session, or deliberate impersonation) to approve a high-risk change without proper authority, potentially to bypass scrutiny or accelerate work.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Detailed workstation and network session logs (login origin, interactive use), badge access to the approving workstation’s location around 13:00, and any corroborating evidence of Ben’s whereabouts (e.g., medical check-in timestamps) would help determine whether Ben or another person used the credentials.

- **Adverse explanation:** Organizational pressure or understaffing might lead Asha to work informally while on leave, effectively undermining the rationale for delegations and increasing dependence on a single person, which could in turn facilitate unnoticed deviations.
- **Discriminating evidence:** HR and QA policies on remote work during leave, historical frequency of Asha’s approvals during leave periods, and any documented justification for CHG-504 as an “urgent” case.

**Attempted IC subdelegation (D-IC-04) and Clara’s emergency approval on CHG-510**

- **Benign explanation:** Farid and Clara may have misunderstood the “no subdelegation” clause in D-IC-03 and believed that passing IC authority via email was acceptable under time pressure during CI-44, intending to comply rather than circumvent controls.
- **Adverse explanation:** The email subdelegation could be a deliberate attempt to bypass Diego’s oversight, granting Clara IC-like authority over an emergency change despite her lack of formal role, which constitutes a serious control failure and potential misconduct.

**Repeated Clara–Ben pairing and network centrality**

- **Adverse explanation:** Over-centralization could indicate informal gatekeeping by this pair, potentially enabling selective enforcement of rules or coordinated manipulation of change controls.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Social-network metrics adjusted for role and workload, comparisons with other cells/sites, and rotation or vacation periods to see whether patterns normalize when either Clara or Ben is unavailable.

def business_deadline(impl_ts: pd.Timestamp, days: int = 2) -> pd.Timestamp:
    # Count subsequent business days (Mon–Fri, no holidays)

    added = 0
    while added < days:

        if ts.weekday() < 5:

    # End of that business day
    return ts + timedelta(hours=23, minutes=59, seconds=59)

## 6. Control Improvements

**Delegation creation and effective-time recording**

- Store delegations in a controlled system of record before their effective start, with explicit fields for scope (assets, risk levels, and process types), principal-absence conditions, and “no subdelegation” flags enforced by the workflow engine.
- Require electronic approval workflows for delegations (not just email), and ensure that every approval step can be linked back to the specific delegation record used at event time.

**Principal availability and use of delegations**

- Enforce mutual exclusivity between principal and delegate for a given approval step: when the principal is actively logged in and performing approvals, workflows should either route exclusively to them or require explicit justification for using a delegate.
- Align HR leave/sick records with access-control systems so that accounts belonging to people on leave either cannot be used for approval or require step-up authentication and explicit override logging.

**Alias and identity management**

- Prohibit multiple active user IDs for the same human in approval workflows; where aliases are necessary, they should be technical-only and always mapped to a single human identity before analytics and SoD checks.
- Periodically run alias-normalization checks (like the logic in the code above) and reconcile inconsistencies with HR and directory data.

**Individual authentication and service-account usage**

- Minimize use of shared service accounts like `svc_qms` for approval or verification activities; where automation is required, use per-user tokens or per-session impersonation that preserves the underlying human identity in the audit trail.
- Introduce mandatory re-authentication (smartcard/FIDO) before high-risk approval and verification steps, especially on shared workstations, and log these events at the application level.

**Emergency-review discipline**

- Implement automatic reminders and escalations to ensure `Retrospective_QM_Review` is completed within the defined business-day window, with dashboards highlighting overdue emergency reviews.
- Require explicit documentation of the safety rationale and emergency justification for each emergency case, linked to the change record and reviewed during the retrospective QM step.

**Pair rotation and workload balancing**

- Introduce rotation policies for drafter/approver pairs on high-risk changes so that no single pair (e.g., Clara–Ben) dominates approvals over extended periods without oversight.
- Use social-network analysis outputs (handover-of-work and working-together networks) to identify over-centralized individuals or pairs and adjust staffing or supervision accordingly.

**Network monitoring and evidential retention**

- Regularly compute and review handover and working-together networks to detect unusual clustering, reciprocal patterns, or structural changes that may indicate emerging risk.
- Improve evidential retention by keeping detailed workstation, badge, token, and application-level logs, and where proportionate, preserving CCTV in high-risk areas long enough to support post-incident investigations.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

Across these ten cases, the apparent nonconformance splits roughly into three buckets: a minority of **clear authority failures** (notably CHG-507 and CHG-510, plus the late/out-of-scope emergency review for CHG-505 and SoD breaches in CHG-502 and CHG-508), a significant share of **identity/data-quality issues** (CHG-503 and CHG-506, and the masking effect of aliases and service accounts), and a background of **organizational-pattern risk** (stable Clara–Ben pairing, reciprocal approvals, principal activity during leave) that weakens control objectives even where technical rules are met. Uncertainty remains high for episodes involving service accounts and approvals during recorded absences, where logs admit multiple plausible explanations. The three highest-priority investigations should be: (1) CHG-510’s invalid IC subdelegation and QM review scope, including the handling of the CI-44 incident; (2) the CHG-506 approval using Ben’s credentials during sick leave, to establish whether credentials were misused or records are inaccurate; and (3) the design and use of `svc_qms` in CHG-503 and CHG-509, ensuring that high-risk verifications are truly independent and individually attributable. Strengthening delegation governance, identity management, and social-network monitoring should materially reduce the gap between technical resource conformance and the underlying safety and compliance objectives.
