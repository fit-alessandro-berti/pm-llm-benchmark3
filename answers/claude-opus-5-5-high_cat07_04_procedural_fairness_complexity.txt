# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness asks two questions. First, do similarly situated applicants get similar procedures? Second, do applicants who use rights-preserving steps (language support, disability accommodation) avoid paying an unnecessary penalty in time, effort, or outcome for using them? Equal approval rates are neither necessary nor sufficient for either. The dimensions below are assessed separately because they can move in different directions.

| Dimension | Key evidence | Preliminary reading |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome parity (raw) | Approval: A 78%, B 63%, C 69%, D 58% | Large gaps (15, 9, 20 pp). Descriptive only. |
| Adjusted outcome disparity | Residual: B −5.5 pp, C −2.2 pp, D −3.1 pp | A gap remains for B. C and D are smaller and statistically uncertain. |
| Procedural burden | Adjusted Request Docs +9.5 pp (B); Supervisor Review +8.0 pp (B), +5.5 pp (C); more handovers | Concerning for B and C |
| Timeliness | Adjusted cycle time +2.6 d (B), +4.1 d (C), +0.9 d (D); P90 up to +17 d | Concerning for B and C, mostly explained for D |
| Consistency of routing | Fraud routing roughly proportional to risk; support steps visible in only about half of the cases that need them | Mixed; possible unmet needs |
| Quality/accessibility of support | 7 bilingual staff, 5 specialists; 3.8 d and 4.7 d waits; no SLA | Support exists on paper but is slow |
| Appeal/rework burden | Rework B 22%, C 24% vs A 11%; adjusted appeal +5.7 pp (D), +4.4 pp (B) | Concerning signal; appeal outcomes unknown |
| Resource-allocation fairness | A spread over 52 caseworkers; B/C concentrated on 10 and 5 staff | Structural concern |

**1.1 Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates differ by 15 pp (B), 9 pp (C) and 20 pp (D) relative to A. Case mix differs sharply: D has 51% high-risk vs 18% for A, and B has 46% incomplete documentation vs 21%. So these gaps cannot be read as discrimination. They are still worth reporting. A benefits system is accountable for total disparities, and part of the "case mix" may itself be produced by the process (see 1.2 and Section 5).

**1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity.** After adjustment, about 63% of B's raw approval gap, 76% of C's, and 84% of D's is statistically associated with the covariates. The residuals are −5.5, −2.2 and −3.1 pp. As a rough, illustrative check with simple binomial standard errors (about 1.6–1.7 pp given the sample sizes), B's residual is several standard errors from zero. C's is not clearly different from zero. D's is borderline. Clustering by caseworker and region would widen all of these intervals, and the preliminary model reports none. Two specification problems cut in opposite directions:
- **Over-control.** The model conditions on `documentation_completeness` and `risk_score_band`. These may be partly *consequences* of group-related barriers or historical bias. That would absorb real disparity and *understate* the gap, especially for B (documentation) and D (risk).
- **Omitted legitimate need.** It does *not* condition on language need, accommodation, or financial eligibility determinants (which are absent from the log). That may *overstate* the gap attributable to treatment.

The residual is therefore an ambiguous quantity, not a discrimination estimate.

**1.3 Procedural burden.** Burden means the number of steps, contacts, document requests, handovers, and repeat submissions an applicant must go through. At equal measured documentation completeness, B cases are 9.5 pp more likely to receive a Request Missing Documents step and 8 pp more likely to go to Supervisor Review. C cases are 5.5 pp more likely to go to Supervisor Review. Rework is twice A's level for B (22%) and C (24%). Burden has two sides: burden on the applicant (travel, time, re-submission, stress) and burden on the agency. Only the agency side is directly observable in the log.

**1.4 Timeliness.** Median cycle time is 5.6 days longer for B, 7.8 for C and 4.0 for D. At P90 the gaps are 12.8, 17.4 and 8.1 days. For subsistence benefits, tail delays are material harm, not a convenience issue. After adjustment, B keeps +2.6 d and C +4.1 d. These are roughly half of their median gaps, although the adjusted estimand may be mean-based, so the comparison is approximate. D keeps only +0.9 d, about a quarter. Rights-preserving steps have no timeliness threshold, so their delays sit outside what management monitors.

**1.5 Consistency of routing.** Consistency means that similarly situated cases take similar paths, that mandated steps are applied to *everyone* who qualifies, and that discretionary steps are applied by the same criteria.
- **Fraud routing looks proportional to risk.** The ratio of the fraud variant V3 to high-risk share is A 0.50, B 0.38, C 0.33, D 0.55.
- **Mandated support steps may be under-applied.** Language support is visible in V4 for only 24% of B cases, although 57% need it. Accommodation Review is visible in V5 for 31% of C cases, although 62% requested it. Top variants cover about 99% of B cases and 98% of C cases, so this is not just a long-tail artefact of the summary. The gap is roughly similar across groups (about 40–50% visible coverage everywhere). It is either a logging gap or a systemic under-provision whose impact falls mainly on B and C.
- **Discretionary steps (Supervisor Review, document requests) are applied more often to B and C** at equal measured complexity.

**1.6 Quality and accessibility of support.** A rights-preserving step is only rights-preserving if it is available, timely, competent and does not trigger further burden. Language support depends on 7 bilingual caseworkers (3.8 d wait before eligibility). Accommodation depends on 5 specialists (4.7 d wait), 2 of whom also do supervisor review. Neither step has a service-level target. `case_outcome` captures nothing about explanation quality, interpretation quality, or dignity of treatment. This dimension is therefore largely *unmeasured*, and absence of evidence here is not evidence of fairness.

**1.7 Appeal and rework burden.** Appeals per denial, as a rough ratio of appeal rate to denial rate, are A about 0.27, B 0.35, C 0.32, D 0.38. D's adjusted appeal difference (+5.7 pp) is larger than its adjusted approval difference (−3.1 pp). That could indicate contested or poorly explained decisions (for example, fraud-related denials with opaque reasons), greater system familiarity, or both. Appeals are both a burden and a correction mechanism. Without overturn rates we cannot tell whether first-instance decisions for D or B are more often wrong. A low appeal rate is not reassuring either: burdened applicants may be deterred from appealing.

**1.8 Resource-allocation fairness.** A's cases are spread across 52 caseworkers. B's are concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and C's on 5 specialists. Specialisation is legitimate. When specialist capacity is small, however, group membership effectively determines which queue an applicant waits in. That is a structural, group-correlated allocation of capacity even if no individual acts with bias. It also creates decision-maker concentration: if one of the few B-serving officers has a stricter decision tendency, B's approval rate shifts as a whole.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

## 2.1 Categories to be distinguished

| Category | Operational definition | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Disparity | Any difference in a process or outcome metric between groups | Descriptive comparison (raw) |
| Legitimate routing | Difference explained by a policy-mandated trigger (high risk → Fraud Review, accommodation → Accommodation Review, language → Language Support), *and* the time and burden of that step do not exceed its intrinsic requirement | Trigger compliance, step service time, waits within an agreed norm |
| Unjustified procedural burden | Legitimate route, but excess waiting, handovers, repeats or discretionary add-ons beyond intrinsic need; *or* different treatment of similarly situated cases | Residual differences within strata or within identical paths; queue attribution |
| Discrimination (direct or indirect) | Differential treatment attributable to group membership or its proxies, or a practice with disproportionate impact that is not necessary or proportionate | Cannot be established from logs alone; needs case-file review, audit testing, natural experiments, or policy analysis |
| Uncertainty | Differences whose classification depends on untestable assumptions (for example, the validity of risk scores) | Sensitivity bounds; report as a range |

Covariates should be classified explicitly before any modelling:
- **Allowable** (legitimately eligibility- or need-relevant): household size, month, genuinely missing documents at submission, and the need flags *as triggers for a step*.
- **Contested** (may encode bias or be process-generated): `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness` (especially if recorded after contact), `prior_case_count`, `channel`, which may be constrained by digital access or language.
- **Structural**: `region`. Controlling for region removes regional under-resourcing, which is itself a fairness question when groups are concentrated in under-resourced regions.

The need flags justify *extra steps*. They do not justify *extra queueing* or *lower approval*.

## 2.2 Estimands

- **E1, total disparity:** the raw group difference.
- **E2, disparity net of allowable covariates:** household size, month, need flags, documents missing at submission.
- **E3, disparity net of allowable plus contested covariates:** roughly the preliminary model. Report E2 and E3 side by side. The gap between them measures how much the conclusion depends on trusting the contested covariates.
- **E4, support-step penalty:** the effect of going through a support step on cycle time, burden and approval, compared with otherwise similar cases without it, estimated within and across groups.
- **E5, path-conditional differential treatment:** the group difference in waits, handovers and outcomes *among cases with the same variant and strata*.
- **E6, "clean cell" test:** among low-risk, complete-at-submission, online, no-language-need, no-accommodation, low-prior-count cases, do groups get the happy path V1 at the same rate and speed? Differences here are the strongest observational evidence of differential treatment, because the legitimate explanations are removed by design.

## 2.3 Matching or stratification variables

- **Exact matching or strata:** region, channel, quarter or month, language need, accommodation requested, risk band (in specifications that include it), documentation completeness *at submission* (the first recorded value, never a post-contact value).
- **Coarsened matching:** household size band, prior case count band.
- **Positivity problem.** Only 2% of A needs language support and only 4% requested accommodation. So "B with language need vs A with language need" has very thin support. Supplement the cross-group comparisons with:
  - within-group contrasts (B with vs B without language support) combined with the same contrast in other groups, a difference-in-differences on the support-step penalty;
  - a normative benchmark: the process-defined acceptable duration of the step, rather than A's experience.
- **Estimators:** doubly robust (propensity weighting plus outcome regression) with overlap trimming. Standard errors clustered by caseworker and region. Multiple-comparison control across the many metrics. Partial pooling (hierarchical models) for small region-by-channel-by-group cells.

## 2.4 Path and variant comparison

- Classify each activity as **mandated-conditional** (Fraud Review, Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review), **standard** (Verify, Completeness Check, Eligibility, Decision, Notify), or **discretionary** (Supervisor Review, Request Missing Documents, re-work loops).
- **Coverage of mandated steps:** the share of triggered cases that actually receive the step, by group and region. Also the share of *untriggered* cases that receive it (over-routing).
- **Discretionary-step incidence** by group within strata (E5). For example: Request Docs among cases complete at submission, and Supervisor Review among cases with no mandated trigger.
- **Variant-distribution distance** within strata (chi-square or Jensen–Shannon), plus happy-path share within the clean cell (E6).
- **Ordering and repetition:** loops of Request Docs, repeated Completeness Checks, handover ping-pong.
- **Outcome by variant and group:** does the same path produce different approval rates for different groups?

## 2.5 Resource-assignment comparison

- **Assignment probability to specialist and overloaded resources** conditional on need. Are B cases *without* language need also sent to the 3 overloaded regional officers?
- **Workload at assignment:** open cases per resource per day, the best available given daily granularity.
- **Within-resource vs between-resource decomposition** of approval and wait gaps, using caseworker fixed effects:
  - a gap *within* the same caseworker suggests differential treatment;
  - a gap *between* caseworkers suggests assignment or capacity effects.
- **Decision-maker tendency:** approval and supervisor-escalation rates per decision maker on matched cases, and exposure of each group to stricter decision makers.
- **Handovers:** count per case and ping-pong patterns (general → bilingual → general), and their association with rework.
- **Role conflicts:** cases where the same resource performs Accommodation Review and Supervisor Review.

## 2.6 Waiting-time decomposition

Decompose each group's excess cycle time into:
1. **Path-composition effect:** extra steps. Split this into mandated support steps (legitimate step, but check duration against a norm) and discretionary steps (needs justification).
2. **Same-step rate effect:** longer waits on the *same* transition, for example Supervisor Review → Decision 1.9 d vs 1.0 d.
3. **Applicant-side waiting:** time awaiting documents from the applicant, vs agency-side waiting in queues. This needs a "documents received" signal that the log currently lacks.
4. **Handover-induced waiting:** the queue re-entry each time a case changes hands.

Use a counterfactual reweighting, which is Oaxaca–Blinder-like. First, give group B A's per-transition waits on B's own paths; this isolates the rate effect. Second, give B A's paths with B's waits; this isolates the composition effect. Only completion timestamps appear to be logged, so inter-event times are sojourn times (waiting plus service). True service/waiting separation needs start events or reliable task-start proxies. Daily granularity limits precision for short steps.

## 2.7 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

- **Risk score:**
  - Estimate D's gaps with and without `risk_score_band`, and with only its non-historical components if these are available.
  - Treat risk as a *mediator*, splitting total disparity into a part via risk and a residual.
  - Run a **calibration or outcome test:** within each risk band, compare the Fraud Review confirmation rate by group. A lower confirmation rate for D at the same band suggests over-flagging. Mind the infra-marginality caveat.
  - Bound the analysis: "If k% of D's excess high-risk share is bias, D's adjusted disparity becomes X."
- **Documentation completeness:**
  - Compare completeness at submission with completeness at decision.
  - Test whether "incomplete" flags cluster after Language Support Contact, or on forms available only in the majority language.
  - Report estimates with completeness treated as a confounder, as a mediator, and omitted.
- **Unmeasured confounding:** E-values or Rosenbaum-style Γ bounds. These answer how strong an unobserved confounder, such as financial eligibility, would have to be to explain B's −5.5 pp.
- **Negative controls:** Decision Draft → Notification should differ by group only through translation time. A large residual difference there would indicate a model or data problem.

## 2.8 Subgroup checks by region and channel

- Group × region × channel tables for every key metric, with intervals.
- Separate reporting for the **two backlog regions** (Supervisor Review → Decision). Test whether B's excess supervisor wait disappears outside them, which would point to a regional capacity effect rather than group treatment.
- **Online B without language need vs online A:** if the gaps vanish, the mechanism is the language pathway, not group membership as such.
- **In-person vs online** within each group for Submit → Verify, since the in-person share is 38% for B vs 9% for A.
- **Simpson's paradox checks:** the pooled direction of a gap may reverse within regions.
- **Time trends:** month-by-group interactions, to detect whether gaps coincide with staffing changes in the bilingual or specialist teams.
- **Intersections:** B applicants who also request accommodation, and D applicants with language needs, may face compounded queues.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The variant and waiting-time summaries are aggregates. Path summaries are truncated (only V1 shows Benefit Activation, and none shows Case Closure). Top variants cover about 82% of A cases but 98–99% of B and C cases. Findings below are ranked by importance and labelled by how far case complexity can justify them.

## 3.1 Rights-preserving steps carry large capacity-driven queues (most important; concerning)

- **Language Support Contact → Eligibility waits 3.8 d**, attributed to "only 7 bilingual caseworkers."
- **Accommodation Review → Eligibility waits 4.7 d**, attributed to a "specialized team queue."

The *step* is legitimately required. The *queue* is not intrinsic complexity: it is a capacity choice. As an order-of-magnitude reconciliation (medians do not add, so this is not a decomposition):
- For B, the language queue contributes about 0.24 × 3.8 ≈ 0.9 d (share observed in V4) up to 0.57 × 3.8 ≈ 2.2 d (all language-need cases).
- For C, the accommodation queue contributes about 0.31 × 4.7 ≈ 1.5 d up to 0.62 × 4.7 ≈ 2.9 d.

These are the same magnitude as the adjusted gaps (+2.6 d and +4.1 d). The model did not control for support needs, so its "adjusted" cycle-time residuals are plausibly driven largely by support-step queueing. Applicants are effectively paying a time penalty for exercising a right. No SLA covers these steps, so the penalty is invisible to management. It is the clearest procedural-fairness concern in the evidence, and it does not require any assumption of individual bias.

## 3.2 Possible under-provision of mandated support (potentially serious; indeterminate)

- 57% of B needs language support, but V4 (the only top variant containing Language Support Contact) covers 24% of B.
- 62% of C requested accommodation, but V5 covers 31% of C.
- Similar roughly 40–50% visible coverage appears in the other groups.

There are two possible explanations:
- **Logging gap:** for example, bilingual caseworkers giving support informally within other activities, or summaries omitting steps.
- **Unmet need:** requests not honoured.

The second would be a direct rights violation, and it would fall disproportionately on B and C because they have the most need. This must be verified from case-level data before anything else. It also means that fixing the queue (3.1) alone might *increase* measured cycle time as coverage improves.

## 3.3 Excess document requests for B (partly justified; residual concerning)

- The adjusted Request Docs probability is +9.5 pp *after* controlling for documentation completeness.
- Request-docs variants (V2 + V4) cover 58% of B vs 46% incomplete. For A, the figures are 25% vs 21%.
- Completeness → Request Docs waits 1.6 d for B vs 0.4 d for A ("waiting for bilingual staff").
- In V4, Language Support Contact is *always* followed by Request Docs.

**Possible justifications:** documents in another language needing translation or certification, or genuinely more missing items not captured by a coarse completeness band.

**Concerning readings:**
- Completeness is being judged against majority-language forms.
- Requests are issued after language contact reveals issues the form design caused.
- Caseworkers apply stricter evidentiary standards to B.

Repeated requests during long waits also risk procedural denials (deadlines expiring), which could contribute to B's −5.5 pp approval residual.

## 3.4 Supervisor Review escalation for B and C (weakly justified; concerning)

- Adjusted Supervisor Review is +8.0 pp for B and +5.5 pp for C. Supervisor Review is not among the stated legitimate triggers.
- V5 routes accommodation cases through Supervisor Review as a pattern, adding a second queue: Supervisor → Decision waits 1.6 d for C vs 1.0 d for A.
- Two of the five accommodation specialists also do supervisor review. That creates capacity coupling (their review work competes with accommodation work) and potential self-review or segregation-of-duties problems.

For B, the +8 pp is *not* visible in the top variants (V3 + V5 give B 14% vs A 11%). So B's supervisor reviews occur in rarer variants, loops or rework, or are omitted from the summaries. That needs reconciling with case-level data.

The regional backlog (1.9 d for B) may be structural, since B is assigned to 3 overloaded regional officers. Unless a documented policy requires supervisor sign-off for accommodations or interpreted cases, and that policy is shown to be necessary, this is an unexplained additional layer of procedure for B and C.

## 3.5 Handover fragmentation (not explained by complexity; concerning)

- In two regions, B cases move general → bilingual → general, and C cases move general → specialist → supervisor.
- Each handover re-enters a queue and risks information loss.
- Rework is 22% (B) and 24% (C) vs 11% (A).

Complexity explains the *need for specialist input*. It does not explain handing the case *back* to a generalist, which is a routing-design choice. The pattern is plausibly linked to both the rework excess and the extra document requests.

## 3.6 Concentrated, overloaded resources (structural; concerning)

A is spread across 52 caseworkers. B depends heavily on 10 staff (7 bilingual plus 3 overloaded regional officers), and C on 5. Under queueing dynamics, waits grow non-linearly as utilisation approaches capacity, so small specialist teams yield long and volatile waits. This matches the wider P90 gaps for B (+12.8 d) and C (+17.4 d). Concentration also means a few individuals' decision tendencies disproportionately shape B and C outcomes. That is untested.

## 3.7 Front-end verification delay (largely explained by channel)

Submit → Verify waits 1.4 d for B vs 0.8 d for A. This is attributed to in-person applications (38% of B vs 9% of A). This is plausibly legitimate as a channel effect. Two caveats apply:
- Channel is constrained choice for many B applicants (language, digital access).
- Under-resourced in-person intake is itself an allocation decision.

The difference should vanish in the within-channel comparison. If it does not, it becomes concerning.

## 3.8 Fraud routing for D (mostly justified conditional on the risk score; upstream uncertainty)

- V3 share relative to high-risk share is D 0.55, A 0.50, B 0.38, C 0.33. This is roughly proportional, consistent with rule-based routing.
- Risk → Fraud waits 2.8 d for D vs 2.0–2.5 d, a modest excess plausibly from D's larger volume in that queue.
- Adjusted cycle time is only +0.9 d.

The *process* treats D consistently with its measured risk. Whether that is fair depends on whether the risk score is valid and unbiased. D also has high prior-case counts, which may reflect prior system contact rather than fraud propensity. This is the main upstream fairness question for D, and the log cannot answer it.

## 3.9 Appeals (concerning signal; indeterminate)

- V6 (appeal path, 30.7 d median) covers 13% of D and 8% of B vs 4% of A.
- Adjusted appeal differences are +5.7 pp (D) and +4.4 pp (B).

High appeals with modest approval residuals suggest contested or poorly explained decisions. Fraud-based denials and notices not in the applicant's language are plausible candidates. Without appeal outcomes this cannot be resolved. High overturn rates would mean first-instance errors concentrated on D and B. Low overturn rates would point more to explanation or trust issues.

## 3.10 Notification delay (small; low priority)

Decision Draft → Notification waits 0.8 d for B vs 0.5 d for A, plausibly translation. This is small, but the *quality* of notices is unobserved.

## Summary

| Disparity | Justified portion | Concerning portion | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support-step queues (B, C) | Step itself | Queue length, absence of SLA | High that it exists; causal share moderate |
| Support-step coverage gap | Possibly logging | Possible unmet rights | Low until verified |
| Extra document requests (B) | Translation or certification needs | Residual +9.5 pp, waits, possible procedural denials | Moderate |
| Supervisor escalation (B, C) | Only if a necessary policy exists | Otherwise an extra unexplained layer; role coupling | Moderate |
| Handover ping-pong (B, C) | Specialist input | Return handovers, rework | Moderate |
| Resource concentration | Specialisation | Under-capacity, decision-maker concentration | High (structural) |
| Verification delay (B) | Channel | Constrained channel choice | Moderate |
| Fraud routing (D) | Rule-consistent given risk | Validity of risk score | Low (upstream unknown) |
| Appeals (D, B) | Familiarity with the system | Decision or explanation quality | Low |

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Each mechanism predicts a distinguishing pattern that can be tested.

**Legitimate (or largely legitimate) mechanisms**

1. **Genuine complexity and mandated routing.** Higher risk triggers Fraud Review. Accommodation and language needs trigger their review steps. More incomplete files trigger document requests.
   - *Predicts:* gaps shrink toward zero within need and risk strata, and step durations are similar across groups for the same step.
   - *Evidence:* fits D well, and fits part of B and C.
2. **Applicant-side response time.** Time waiting for documents is partly under the applicant's control.
   - *Predicts:* wait concentrated between Request Docs and document receipt, not in agency queues.
   - *Caveat:* language barriers and in-person dependence lengthen applicant response for reasons not attributable to the applicant.
3. **Channel-specific processing.** In-person applications need scanning, manual identity checks and appointment scheduling.
   - *Predicts:* the Submit → Verify gap disappears within channel.

**Concerning mechanisms**

4. **Under-capacity of rights-preserving steps (structural queueing).** With 7 bilingual staff and 5 specialists, utilisation is likely high, so waits grow non-linearly and variance explodes.
   - *Predicts:* waits correlate with daily open workload of the specialist team, rise with arrival surges, and are longest at P90.
   - *Evidence:* fits the 3.8 d and 4.7 d waits and the large P90 gaps.
5. **Handover fragmentation and information loss.** Returning cases from specialist to generalist re-enters queues and loses context.
   - *Predicts:* rework and document requests rise with handover count, even within the same need stratum.
6. **Discretionary escalation: defensive practice or implicit bias.** Caseworkers may escalate interpreted or accommodated cases "to be safe," or apply stricter evidentiary scrutiny to some groups.
   - *Predicts:* excess Supervisor Review and Request Docs *within the same caseworker* and within the clean cell. Escalated cases then show low rates of supervisor-changed decisions (escalation adds no value).
7. **Measurement bias feeding routing.**
   - Risk scores trained on historically targeted investigations, with prior-case counts reflecting past system contact, can create a feedback loop: more scrutiny, more recorded issues, higher future risk.
   - Documentation "completeness" judged on majority-language forms can make language barriers look like missing documents.
   - *Predicts:* lower fraud-confirmation rates for D within a band, and completeness flags changing after language contact.
8. **Role coupling of specialists and supervisors.** Two accommodation specialists also do supervisor review.
   - Accommodation cases may default into supervisor review (convenience routing).
   - Supervisor duties may drain accommodation capacity, and self-review may occur.
   - *Predicts:* accommodation waits rise when those two individuals carry supervisor load, and V5 concentrates on them.
9. **Regional backlog × group concentration.** B relies on 3 overloaded regional officers, and supervisor backlogs exist in two regions.
   - *Predicts:* B's supervisor wait excess is confined to those regions. The "group" effect is actually a place-of-residence resource effect, which region-adjusted models hide.
10. **Procedural denials from deadline expiry.** Document-request deadlines run while applicants wait for bilingual staff or accommodation.
    - *Predicts:* B's approval residual concentrates in denials coded "no response" or "insufficient documentation" rather than "ineligible," and in cases with long support waits.
11. **Explanation deficits driving appeals.** Notices not in the preferred language or format, or opaque fraud-related reasons.
    - *Predicts:* appeals concentrated on fraud-path denials (D) and language-need cases (B), with high overturn or settlement at appeal.
12. **Logging artefacts.** Informal interpretation not logged as Language Support Contact, bulk logging of supervisor decisions, daily timestamp granularity.
    - *Predicts:* the coverage gap disappears when validated against case files, and waits cluster at day boundaries.

Several mechanisms probably operate at once. For example, mechanisms 4, 5 and 10 would compound for a B applicant with incomplete documents in an overloaded region.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Measurement bias in covariates.**
  - `documentation_completeness` can be a product of language barriers, form design and caseworker judgement. It is not a neutral applicant property, and it may be recorded after support contact (post-treatment).
  - `channel` is partly constrained by digital access and language.
  - `prior_case_count` reflects past interaction with the system, including past errors or wrongful denials.
  - Adjusting for these can launder process-generated disparity into "case mix."
- **Historical bias in risk scores.** If `risk_score_band` was trained on labels from past fraud investigations that targeted certain groups more intensely, the score encodes enforcement intensity, not just fraud propensity. Controlling for it treats that history as legitimate. The log cannot tell calibration from bias unless fraud-review outcomes by band and group are available. The coarse band also hides within-band differences.
- **Missing applicant needs.**
  - `language_preference` is not language proficiency or literacy.
  - Accommodation needs are under-requested due to stigma, lack of awareness, or undiagnosed conditions.
  - Digital access, housing instability, caregiving duties, and cognitive or literacy needs are absent.
  - Group definitions overlap with needs (C is partly *defined* by accommodation requests), which complicates the separation of "group" from "need."
  - Intersectional needs (language plus disability) are not separable from the aggregates.
- **Missing eligibility determinants.** Income, assets and residency status are not in the attribute list. An approval-probability model without them cannot tell an eligibility difference from a treatment difference. This is a major reason the −5.5 pp residual is not a discrimination estimate.
- **Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only daily. There are no queue lengths at assignment, absences, part-time schedules, skill levels, experience, or assignment rules. Capacity-driven delay therefore cannot be cleanly separated from resource-level behaviour, and fine-grained waits (under 1 day) are imprecise.
- **Appeal quality and outcomes.** `appeal_flag` shows only that an appeal occurred. It does not show grounds, representation, outcome (overturn or uphold), or duration from the applicant's view. Deterred appeals are invisible. High appeal rates may reflect better access to appeal rather than worse decisions.
- **Outcome narrowness.** `case_outcome` omits explanation quality, respect and dignity, number of applicant contacts, travel and time costs, benefit amount correctness, and time to first payment. Much of procedural fairness is about exactly these things.
- **Event-log incompleteness.**
  - Only completion timestamps are recorded (service vs waiting inseparable), and there is no "documents received" event.
  - There are no reason codes for Supervisor Review or Request Docs.
  - Informal support may go unlogged.
  - Withdrawn or abandoned applications may be under-recorded. Abandonment is a hidden burden outcome: the most burdened applicants may drop out and vanish from approval statistics.
- **Censoring and selection.** Open cases at extraction are right-censored and more likely to be slow cases, which biases durations downward, especially for B and C. Appeals are observed only for decided cases.
- **Aggregate summaries.** The top-variant table is truncated and incomplete for A and D, and adjusted estimates come without intervals or model diagnostics. All numerical reasoning above is provisional.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

None of these options removes or dilutes rights-preserving steps. The aim is to make them timely, reliable and low-burden.

**6.1 Add capacity for language support and accommodation review**
- *Options:*
  - hire or contract bilingual caseworkers;
  - add telephone or video interpreting pools to decouple interpretation from caseworker assignment;
  - cross-train general caseworkers in common accommodations;
  - build surge capacity rules tied to queue length;
  - separate the 2 dual-role specialists' supervisor duties from accommodation work.
- *Side effects:*
  - cost and recruitment lead time;
  - interpreter or cross-trained staff may deliver lower-quality support than specialists;
  - reallocating staff from general queues may lengthen A's waits and create new, visible disparities;
  - better coverage may reveal latent unmet demand (Section 3.2), temporarily increasing measured support volume and waits;
  - capacity alone does not fix handover design.

**6.2 Service-level targets for support steps**
- *Options:*
  - time-to-first-contact and time-to-completion targets for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review, with P90 targets, not just medians;
  - an end-to-end cycle-time target that *includes* support steps, so they are not treated as excusable delays;
  - fair clock-stop rules for applicant-side waiting;
  - reporting stratified by group and region.
- *Side effects:*
  - Goodhart effects: superficial contacts logged to stop the clock, premature closure of accommodation reviews, and reclassification of needs to avoid SLA coverage;
  - staff may subtly discourage support requests;
  - clock-stop rules can be abused to hide delay;
  - attention may shift away from steps without targets;
  - pair SLAs with quality audits and coverage metrics.

**6.3 Routing redesign to reduce handovers**
- *Options:*
  - end-to-end case ownership by the bilingual caseworker or specialist, instead of general → specialist → general;
  - interpretation "brought to" the general caseworker;
  - run support steps *in parallel* with Identity Verification and Completeness Check rather than serially;
  - capture needs at submission so support is scheduled immediately;
  - make Supervisor Review criteria-based and documented, not default for accommodation or interpreted cases;
  - ensure no one supervises their own accommodation review.
- *Side effects:*
  - end-to-end ownership can create a segregated "second-tier" service with less oversight and specialist burnout;
  - parallelisation can cause rework if eligibility work proceeds before an accommodation changes what evidence is needed;
  - reducing supervisor review may reduce error-catching, so first test whether escalation changes decisions;
  - separating roles reduces flexibility in small teams;
  - redesign transitions temporarily disrupt performance.

**6.4 Accessible document-request process**
- *Options:*
  - multilingual and accessible checklists at submission;
  - one consolidated request instead of serial requests;
  - deadlines that pause while support steps are pending;
  - no denial for non-response without a confirmed accessible contact attempt;
  - accept documents in other languages with agency-side translation where feasible.
- *Side effects:*
  - longer-open cases (tension with timeliness metrics);
  - translation cost;
  - perceived fraud-control risk;
  - more discretion in accepting documents, which needs its own consistency checks.

**6.5 Decision-explanation improvements**
- *Options:*
  - plain-language, translated and accessible-format notices with specific reason codes;
  - what evidence would change the decision;
  - how to appeal;
  - for fraud-related denials, as much reasoning as legally permissible;
  - optional explanation calls for language-need and accommodation cases.
- *Side effects:*
  - drafting and translation time lengthens Decision → Notification;
  - templates can become generic and lose case-specific reasoning;
  - clearer explanations may *increase* appeals in the short term (arguably a desirable correction, but a workload shock);
  - disclosure of fraud reasoning may raise legal or security concerns;
  - translation errors create new risks.

**6.6 Monitoring adjusted disparities**
- *Options:* a recurring dashboard showing:
  - raw and adjusted gaps side by side with intervals, under several covariate specifications (E1–E3);
  - process-level fairness indicators: mandated-step coverage, discretionary-step rates in the clean cell, support-step waits, handovers per case, rework, procedural denial share, appeal overturn rate;
  - alert thresholds that trigger case-file review rather than automatic conclusions.
- *Side effects:*
  - the adjustment specification can mask disparity (over-control on biased covariates) or manufacture false alarms (small cells, multiple testing);
  - metric fixation;
  - collecting and using group attributes raises data-protection and legitimacy issues;
  - staff may avoid "risky" cases or reshape records to look fair;
  - requires analytical capacity and governance.

**6.7 Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness**
- *Options:*
  - **Risk score:**
    - calibration by group (fraud-confirmation rates within band);
    - error-rate balance;
    - feature review, especially prior-case-count and region proxies;
    - label provenance (which past investigations generated training labels);
    - periodic independent review.
  - **Completeness:**
    - inter-rater reliability across caseworkers;
    - comparison of completeness at submission vs after support contact;
    - review of form language and accessibility;
    - reason-coded completeness judgements.
- *Side effects:*
  - cost and time;
  - recalibration may reduce fraud-detection sensitivity or shift scrutiny to other groups;
  - publishing score details may enable gaming;
  - fairness criteria for risk scores conflict mathematically (calibration vs equal error rates), so trade-offs must be decided explicitly;
  - findings may create legal or political exposure;
  - interim uncertainty about which routing rules to use.

**6.8 Supporting measures**
- *Options:*
  - record appeal outcomes and reasons;
  - add a "documents received" event;
  - log start timestamps and reason codes for escalations;
  - brief applicant-experience surveys (including language and accessibility);
  - rebalance regional backlogs through remote processing.
- *Side effects:*
  - extra logging burden;
  - survey non-response bias;
  - remote processing may reduce local knowledge and in-person contact that some applicants rely on.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**Overall:** discrimination against any group is indeterminate from this evidence. On procedural burden and timeliness for Groups B and C, the process shows **material, plausibly unjustified procedural inequality**. That conclusion is likely to hold up unless the case-level checks contradict it.

**Supported with reasonable confidence:**
- Raw approval gaps are largely, but not entirely, associated with case mix. They should not be read as discrimination.
- B and C carry substantially higher procedural burden and longer delays after adjustment:
  - more document requests and supervisor escalations;
  - twice the rework;
  - P90 delays 13–17 days longer than A.
- The magnitude of B's and C's adjusted delays is consistent with **capacity-driven queues in rights-preserving steps**, **handover fragmentation**, and **extra supervisor layers**. These are features of process design and staffing, not of applicant complexity.
- The absence of SLAs for support steps leaves this burden unmanaged. Applicants are effectively penalised in time for exercising rights the policy is meant to protect. This is a procedural-fairness problem even though routing to the support steps is itself legitimate, and it does not require any individual bias to exist.
- Fraud routing for D appears consistent with the measured risk band, and D's adjusted delay is small.

**Plausible but not established:**
- B's −5.5 pp approval residual *may* reflect procedural denials, stricter scrutiny, or decision-maker concentration. It may equally reflect unmeasured eligibility factors (income and assets are absent) or over- or under-control via documentation completeness.
- The support-step coverage gap (about half of language-need and accommodation-request cases show no support step) *may* indicate unmet rights, or may be a logging artefact. It is the highest-priority fact to verify.
- D's elevated appeals *may* signal weak explanations or contested fraud-related decisions. Without appeal outcomes this is open.

**Cannot be inferred:**
- Whether any disparity constitutes discrimination in a legal or intentional sense.
- Whether the risk score is fair to D. Controlling for it assumes the answer.
- Whether applicants experienced respectful, understandable treatment. The outcome data does not measure it.
- C's and D's approval residuals: small and statistically uncertain.

**Recommendation:**
1. Treat the process as **not yet demonstrably fair**.
2. Prioritise verifying support-step coverage and the queue decomposition.
3. Add support-step SLAs and capacity without removing or diluting the steps.
4. Audit the risk score and completeness measure.
5. Re-estimate disparities under multiple covariate specifications, with intervals, before reaching firmer conclusions.
