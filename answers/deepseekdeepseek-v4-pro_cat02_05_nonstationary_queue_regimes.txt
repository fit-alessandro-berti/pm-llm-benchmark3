# 1. Data audit and estimands
This section defines the variables, their measurement conventions, and the main inferential hazards.

**Quantities and units**

- `arrivals`: Number of new cases entering the service center during opening hours (07:00–19:00, 12h) on that calendar day.
- `completed`: Number of cases whose service was finished inside opening hours. Cases may have arrived on a previous day and can carry over overnight; no service occurs while closed.
- `median_service_min`, `p90_service_min`: Active handling times (service time, excluding any waiting) for cases completed during the day. Both are quantiles of the daily servicetime distribution—**not** the mean.
- `mean_wip`: Timeaverage number of cases in the system (queue + service) from 15minute snapshots taken between 07:00 and 19:00. It is the samplepath average over the opening hours, written \( \bar{L}_{\text{open}} \).
- `staff_fte`: Mean fulltimeequivalent agents concurrently available during opening hours. Each FTE handles one case at a time.
- `complex_pct`: Percentage of daily arrivals that follow a multistep complex variant.
- `outage_min`: Total minutes of system outage inside opening hours; zero otherwise.
- `peak_08_10_pct`: Percentage of the day’s arrivals occurring in the interval [08:00, 09:59].

**Observation window convention**

- All counts and WIP refer to the 12hour opening window. Overnight (19:00–07:00) the system is closed but existing cases stay; backlog at 07:00 is **not** recorded. Therefore `mean_wip` is a truncated timeaverage, and the relationship with Little’s Law is delicate.

**Key limitations and threats to naive inference** (at least five)

1. **No mean service time, only median and P90.** Any load calculation that substitutes the median for the mean will systematically underestimate offered load, because service times are positively skewed (P90  median).  
2. **WIP only during opening hours; carryover is invisible.** The overnight WIP trajectory is unknown. Without the 07:00 backlog, we cannot compute flow times exactly, and using `mean_wip` as a 24hour average is unsafe.  
3. **Completions  arrivals on the same day.** A nonnegligible fraction of cases spans multiple days, so daily “throughput” is not cleanly linked to sameday arrivals.  
4. **Four hourly vectors may be inconsistent with daily aggregates.** For example, the vector for 20260421 gives only 11 arrivals in 0809 and 0910, while `peak_08_10_pct` = 47% of 115 arrivals implies 54 arrivals. Such discrepancies limit intraday modelling.  
5. **Serial dependence and evolving conditions.** The daily observations are a time series with trend, abrupt changes (outage, mix shift, staffing intervention), and possible memory from the backlog. Pooling without accounting for nonstationarity yields misleading averages (Simpson’sparadox effects).  
6. **Confounding between capacity, complexity, and backlog.** The increase in `complex_pct` around 20260420 may be a true casemix change, a coding change, or both, and it coincides with rising WIP. The later drop in WIP is confounded with extra staffing.  
7. **Snapshots at 15min intervals may miss finetimescale dynamics** (e.g., short bursts inside an hour) and cannot capture actual waitingtime distributions.

All of these hazards imply that a single pooled queueing model (e.g., an M/M/c fitted to aggregate arrival and service rates) would be unreliable without careful segmentation.

# 3. Alternative segmentation
A materially different segmentation could **ignore the outage and staffing intervention** and partition the timeline purely by the complexity jump detected at 20260420:

- **Lowcomplexity period** (20260406 to 20260419) – including outage and recovery days.  
- **Highcomplexity period** (20260420 to 20260503) – including the later staffing increase.

**Operational story**  
This segmentation assumes that the only persistent change is the mix of work; the outage is treated as a random shock within an otherwise stable lowcomplexity regime, and the staffing change is considered part of the system’s response to a permanent demand shift, not a distinct phase.

**Strengths**  

- Simple twophase structure, easy to communicate.  
- Reduces the number of free parameters; avoids overfitting regimes with few observations (e.g., the outage single day).

**Weaknesses compared with primary segmentation**  

- The lowcomplexity period pools the preoutage steady state with the outage and the recovery, severely inflating its variance and biasing its servicetime and WIP estimates upward.

- It loses the ability to evaluate the effectiveness of the outage recovery actions and the precise timing of the staffing intervention.

Thus, while simpler, the alternative segmentation blurs the distinct pressure points and risks underestimating the need for capacity in highcomplexity periods **before** intervention.

# 6. Little’sLaw applicability matrix
Little’s Law \( \bar{L} = \lambda \bar{W} \) requires a welldefined system where  
- the average WIP \( \bar{L} \) is a timeaverage over the whole period of interest,  
- \( \lambda \) is the average throughput (arrival or completion rate, depending on how \( \bar{W} \) is measured), and  
- the system is “empty” at the boundaries or the period is long enough that boundary effects are negligible **and** the underlying rates are stationary.

Here, WIP is measured only during opening hours, and the overnight state is unobserved. I therefore evaluate applicability for windows that are at least *approximately* stationary and where the carryover risk can be bounded. Throughput proxy is chosen as **completions per hour** because, in a stationary window, completions equal arrivals and represent output. Flow time is computed as \( \bar{W} = \bar{L}_{\text{open}} / (\text{completions per hour})\) **provided** the system is in a periodic steady state with the same WIP at each 07:00. This is a strong assumption.

# 7. Operational implications
1. **Differentiated weekday/weekend staffing**  
   The poolbased average FTE of 5.0 is irrelevant. Weekdays require at least 6 FTE under normal mix, and 7 FTE under the recent complex mix. Weekends can be covered with 2.5–3.0 FTE. Maintaining this split avoids both understaffing during the week and wasteful idle capacity on weekends.

2. **Backlog recovery after outages**  
   The observed temporary staffing boost (7.0 FTE on 0416) successfully worked off the outage backlog within a few days. This practice should be formalised: keep a rapidresponse surge plan that adds ~1 FTE for 2–3 days after any outage > 1 hour. The data show that without extra capacity, backlog persists and compounds with new arrivals.

3. **Peak smoothing**  
   The extreme burstiness in the postmix period (47% of arrivals in a 2hour window) produces severe shortterm overloads, even if the daily  is just above 1. Consider:
   - Encouraging appointment scheduling or spreading arrivals (e.g., via incentives).
   - Aligning shift schedules so that more staff are present during the 0810 (or identified peak) window. Even with the same total FTE, a better shift profile can reduce waiting times.

4. **Datacollection improvements**  
   - Log individual case timestamps (arrival, service start, service end) to compute actual flow times and waiting times.  
   - Record the exact start and end of the staffing intervention, not just the day.  
   - Track case complexity at the case level (not just daily percentage) to separate simple/complex servicetime distributions.  
   - Take WIP snapshots at 07:00 (or at closing) to monitor overnight backlog and enable proper Little’s Law calculations.

5. **Policy that looks good under pooled averages but is risky under segmentation**  
   *Staffing to the pooled utilisation target of 0.85–0.90.*  
   Because the pooled  is ~0.94, an operations manager might decide that 6 FTE on weekdays is comfortable (“we are at 94% utilisation, maybe a little stretch”). However, under the highcomplexity preintervention regime, the same 6 FTE would run at   1.44, inevitably creating an evergrowing queue and unacceptable delays. The segmented view forces the recognition that **during the highcomplexity weeks, 6 FTE is insufficient**; the system needed 7 FTE (or a radical reduction of service time through process improvement) to keep  below 1. The intervention data confirm that a move to 7 FTE brought  close to 1.0 and allowed the backlog to shrink.

6. **Coupling of backlog and future load**  
   Because the process has strong serial dependence in WIP, a spell of high complexity can fill the queue, and even if the mix later normalises, the legacy backlog prolongs high WIP. Capacity decisions should account for the current backlog size, not just today’s arrivals.

def regime_summary(subdf):
    avg = subdf.agg({"arrivals":"mean","completed":"mean","median_service_min":"mean",
                     "p90_service_min":"mean","mean_wip":"mean","staff_fte":"mean",
                     "complex_pct":"mean","outage_min":"mean"})
    # Estimate mean service time (lognormal assumption)
    sig = (np.log(subdf["p90_service_min"]) - np.log(subdf["median_service_min"])) / norm.ppf(0.90)
    mean_svc = subdf["median_service_min"] * np.exp(sig**2 / 2)
    avg["est_mean_svc_min"] = mean_svc.mean()
    # Utilisation proxy: (arrivals/12 * mean_svc) / (staff_fte * 60)
    rho = (avg["arrivals"]/12 * avg["est_mean_svc_min"]) / (avg["staff_fte"] * 60)
    avg["util_proxy"] = rho
    return avg

# 5. Little's Law implied flow times for specifically chosen windows
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
def little_flow(subdf, regime_name):
    L = subdf["mean_wip"].mean()          # average over opening hours
    mu_c = subdf["completed"].sum()       # total completions
    hours = len(subdf) * 12               # total opening hours
    throughput_per_hour = mu_c / hours
    W_hours = L / throughput_per_hour if throughput_per_hour > 0 else np.nan
    W_min = W_hours * 60
    return L, mu_c, hours, throughput_per_hour, W_min
