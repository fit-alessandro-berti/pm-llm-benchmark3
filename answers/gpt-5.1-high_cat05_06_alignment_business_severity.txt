## 1. Materiality Framework

### 1.1 Dimensions of business severity

For these claim-payment cases, I assess **business severity (1–5)** mainly against the following dimensions:

- **Control objective affected**
  - **Fraud and payment authorization** (P1–P3): correct approval path, segregation of duties, and fraud screening before cash leaves the bank.
  - **Customer communication and fairness** (P4): timely and evidenced notification.
  - **Model scope and interpretability** (P5–P6): whether the process model faithfully represents the business and whether diagnostics are interpretable.
- **Amount / exposure**
  - Nominal claim amount as a proxy for maximum loss.
  - Risk level (LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH) and presence of bank changes as multipliers of fraud/operational risk.
- **Reversibility / irreversibility**
  - Ease of recovering an incorrect payment versus irrecoverable cash outflows.
  - Whether deviations happen **before** or **after** money leaves the bank (P3).
- **Timing and ordering**
  - Whether approvals and fraud checks **precede** payments (P2–P3).
  - Whether notifications occur within the required SLA (P4).
- **Customer / regulatory harm**
  - Customer detriment, complaints, and fairness.
  - Regulatory expectations on audit trail, fraud controls, and governance.
- **Recurrence / systematic pattern**
  - One-off anomaly versus recurring patterns (e.g., repeated missing events or rule violations).
  - Whether a deviation indicates a **design flaw** (systemic) or **execution error** (sporadic).
- **Evidence reliability**
  - Strength and stability of evidence: signed documents, immutable system logs, versus mutable free-text links or model assumptions.
  - Distinguishing **missing event** from **missing activity**: an absent log entry is not proof of absence of work.
- **Model scope and fidelity**
  - Whether the process model purposely excludes certain paths (e.g., denied/withdrawn claims).
  - Whether deviations reflect **logging/model gaps** or **real operational non-compliance**.

### 1.2 Severity scale (1–5)

- **Severity 1 – Negligible / technical noise**
  - Only telemetry or logging artefacts, no control objective impacted.
  - No material financial, customer, or regulatory risk even if repeated.
- **Severity 2 – Low**
  - Minor model or logging issues, or low-value cases with good evidence that controls worked.
  - Worth fixing for clarity and analytics quality, but not urgent from a risk perspective.
- **Severity 3 – Moderate**
  - Real control or evidence weaknesses with limited financial exposure, or stronger weaknesses in low-value cases.
  - Requires remediation and monitoring, but not an immediate crisis.
- **Severity 4 – High**
  - Significant control failures or strong indications thereof in higher-value / higher-risk cases, or systemic design gaps.
  - Could attract regulatory attention or create substantial loss if repeated; remediation should be prioritized.
- **Severity 5 – Critical**
  - Clear or strongly indicated **breach of key fraud/payment controls** (P1–P3) in high-value or high-risk claims.
  - Irreversible exposure (large payments made before adequate approval/fraud checks) or pervasive design failure.

### 1.3 Confidence

**Confidence** is distinct from severity:

- It captures **how sure we are** about the business interpretation given:
  - completeness and quality of logs,
  - consistency of external evidence (documents, CRM, connectors),
  - clarity of model scope and policies (P1–P6).
- I use a qualitative scale:
  - **High** – multiple consistent sources support the interpretation; deviations are unambiguous.
  - **Medium** – some ambiguity or missing links; at least one plausible alternative hypothesis.
  - **Low** – strong dependence on assumptions about logging or scope.

A case can therefore be **high severity but low/medium confidence** (e.g., a missing fraud-screen event on a large claim) or **low severity but high confidence** (e.g., harmless telemetry noise consistently documented).

## 3. Repeated Deviation Patterns

### Pattern 1 – Telemetry and retry duplicates

- **Constituent cases**: CL-601 (`Open Workbench`, `Autosave`, `Close Workbench`), CL-603 (duplicate `Notify Claimant`), plus 74 cases in the month with `L(Notify Claimant)` immediately after a synchronous notification with the same message ID.
- **Frequency evidence**: Dozens of occurrences tied to a single retrying connector and UI telemetry.
- **Business risk**: **Very low**. These are benign technical artefacts that do not change which business activities occur or when; they mainly distort conformance metrics and analytics.
- **Likely cause**: Logging layers are capturing UI and transport-level events directly into the process-mining log without clear separation between technical and business semantics.
- **Recommended owner**: **IT / Integration & logging team**, with input from the **process-mining team**.

### Pattern 2 – Missing or mistimed fraud/approval versus payments (high-value cases)

- **Constituent cases**: CL-602 (missing Fraud Screen on €480k HIGH-risk, bank-change case), CL-604 (payment at 10:03, approval recorded at 10:20, missing Fraud Screen).
- **Frequency evidence**: At least two concrete examples; likely more in the full dataset given P2’s design and alignment notation (e.g., `M(Fraud Screen)` combined with `L(Pay Claim)` before approvals).
- **Business risk**: **Very high**. These represent potential violations of **P2** and **P3**, with large, often irreversible cash outflows before mandatory controls are evidenced.
- **Likely operational/logging/model causes**:
  - Payment execution is not technically blocked by missing Fraud Screen or Manual Approve.
  - Late or offline approvals back-filled into the system (backdating).
  - Fraud checks performed in other tools/channels without integration into the core log.
- **Recommended owner**: **Claims operations and risk / fraud management**, supported by **core-systems engineering** to enforce hard gating.

### Pattern 3 – External adjuster assessment without core Assess Loss events

- **Constituent cases**: CL-606, plus nine high-value cases with `M(Assess Loss)` and external-adjuster documents; two lacking a stable case–document identifier.
- **Frequency evidence**: Nine high-value cases in a month is non-trivial; this is a systematic integration gap, not a one-off.
- **Business risk**: **Moderate**. Controls P2–P3 are met in terms of fraud screening and approval timing, but auditability and traceability of the assessment step are weakened, especially where document–case linking is unstable.
- **Likely cause**: External adjuster system not fully integrated; assessments are stored and signed externally but not converted into structured Assess Loss events in the core process log.
- **Recommended owner**: **Architecture / integration team** with the **claims governance / quality** function.

### Pattern 4 – Label mapping / semantic mismatches

- **Constituent cases**: CL-611 (`Assess Damage` vs `Assess Loss`).
- **Frequency evidence**: At least one explicit case; likely more during and shortly after the naming change, as indicated by the updated data-mapping table.
- **Business risk**: **Low**. Operational behavior and controls are fine; risk lies in misinterpretation of logs, misclassification in analytics, and confusion during audits.
- **Likely cause**: Change in procedure and UI terminology not promptly or consistently reflected in the event-log mapping and process model.
- **Recommended owner**: **Process owner / model owner** and **data engineering / ETL**, jointly responsible for maintaining label dictionaries.

### Pattern 5 – Out-of-scope denial and withdrawal paths

- **Constituent cases**: CL-607 (denied claim), CL-612 (withdrawn claim), plus “denied and withdrawn cases account for most alignments with raw cost ≥ 4”.
- **Frequency evidence**: These cases dominate the high raw_cost tail, implying substantial volumes of denied/withdrawn leads not represented in the model.
- **Business risk**: **Low direct financial risk** (no payments) but **moderate modelling/governance risk**:
  - Intake and closure behaviors are not explicitly modelled.
  - Conformance diagnostics overstate severity and obscure real risk patterns.
- **Likely cause**: The process tree was intentionally designed “for payable claims” (P6), but actual intake includes denial and withdrawal branches not captured in the model.
- **Recommended owner**: **Process owner and model owner**, potentially overseen by **operational risk** to ensure model scope is clearly documented.

### Pattern 6 – Data-aware Auto Approve rule violations

- **Constituent cases**: CL-609 (MEDIUM-risk €18k Auto Approve), CL-610 (LOW-risk €4.5k with recent bank change via Auto Approve), plus two additional synchronous Auto Approve cases violating P1 or P2 mentioned in pattern evidence (total of four).
- **Frequency evidence**: At least four known violations in one month; likely more if rules were not enforced historically.
- **Business risk**: **High**, especially where recent bank changes or higher-risk attributes are present (CL-610).
  - These are **systematic design failures**: the workflow allows Auto Approve outside the eligibility criteria defined in P1 and P2.
- **Likely operational/logging/model causes**:
  - Auto Approve path not constrained by amount, risk level, or bank-change flags.
  - Eligibility implemented only as a soft rule (e.g., UI hint) instead of hard gating in the engine.
- **Recommended owner**: **Fraud/risk team** and **workflow / rules-engine engineering**, with oversight from **internal control**.

### Pattern 7 – Notification evidence gaps and mislocation

- **Constituent cases**: CL-608 (Notify in CRM only, with mutable link); CL-603 (duplicated notifications via connector retries).
- **Frequency evidence**: 74 retry-induced duplicates show that the connector and CRM path is heavily used; the absence of some notifications in the core log (CL-608) indicates inconsistent logging.
- **Business risk**: **Moderate** for high-value cases:
  - Risk of failing to prove notifications for regulators or in disputes.
  - Possible inconsistent treatment of customers if some channels are not fully integrated or monitored.
- **Likely cause**: Fragmented notification architecture where core, CRM, and secure-portal systems are not synchronized under a single, stable case-based identifier.
- **Recommended owner**: **Customer communications / CRM team** and **core-systems engineering**, with **compliance** to specify evidencing requirements under P4.


## 4. Causal Hypotheses and Evidence Tests

For at least six selected cases/patterns, I outline competing hypotheses and the evidence needed to discriminate between them, explicitly not treating missing events as proof of missing activity.

### 4.1 CL-602 – Missing Fraud Screen on a €480k HIGH-risk, bank-change claim

- **Hypothesis A (control actually missing – severe)**: Fraud Screen was never performed; the manual approver relied solely on informal checks.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Query dedicated fraud-screen engine logs and audit tables for the claimant, account, and time window; confirm absence or presence of a screening transaction.
    - Interview the manual approver and inspect any checklists or local spreadsheets to see if an undocumented check occurred.
    - Look for similar claims with the same approver where Fraud Screen is properly logged to assess behavior.
- **Hypothesis B (performed elsewhere but not integrated – evidence gap)**: Fraud screening occurred in another system (e.g., AML platform) but the result is not surfaced as a Fraud Screen event in the core log.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Cross-system correlation between AML/fraud systems and the claim ID or claimant; verify that a screening record exists with an outcome for this case.
    - Trace any middleware or ETL that should translate a fraud check into `Fraud Screen` events; check for failures or configuration gaps.
- **Hypothesis C (incorrect alignment / naming – partial)**: A combined activity (e.g., “Approve with Fraud Review”) is being logged, but not mapped as `Fraud Screen`.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Examine raw event names near Manual Approve; see if any composite activity semantically covers both approval and screening.
    - Review UI and procedure documentation to confirm whether users see separate or combined steps.

### 4.2 CL-604 – Payment before approval and missing Fraud Screen

- **Hypothesis A (actual backdated approval – severe control failure)**: Payment was executed at 10:03, and the manual approval recorded at 10:20 was backdated in substance but not in the system; Fraud Screen never occurred.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Compare payment ledger timestamps (including batch-processing details) with the approval event timestamp to ensure they reflect real execution times.
    - Review user audit trails for the approver (session logs, IP, workstation) to determine when they actually interacted with the claim.
    - Investigate whether similar patterns exist for the same approver or business unit.
- **Hypothesis B (logging/timestamp mis-order – less severe but still problematic)**: The approval was performed before payment, but asynchronous logging, clock skew, or replication delays made the payment appear earlier.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Check server clocks and cross-system timestamp consistency; audit for known clock-drift issues.
    - Examine other events in the same timeframe to see if similar ordering anomalies occur across cases.
    - Inspect raw logs for ingestion delays (e.g., queued messages flushed later).
- **Hypothesis C (external approval not captured – evidencing issue)**: Some form of offline approval (e.g., signed email) occurred prior to payment, but the system record was entered later.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Search for signed emails or documents referencing the claim prior to 10:03.
    - Review local procedures and training to understand whether staff habitually record approvals late.

### 4.3 Pattern 6 – Data-aware Auto Approve violations (CL-609, CL-610 and peers)

- **Hypothesis A (eligibility logic not enforced – design flaw)**: Auto Approve is available regardless of amount, risk, or bank-change flags; users are routinely using it for ineligible claims.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Analyze Auto Approve usage across all claims, segmenting by amount, risk level, and bank-change presence; quantify how often P1 and P2 are violated.
    - Inspect configuration of business-rule engines / workflow models to confirm whether eligibility rules exist and are active.
- **Hypothesis B (data quality issue – false positives for violation)**: Risk flags or bank-change timestamps are sometimes wrong (e.g., bank change flagged but not truly recent, or risk misclassified).
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Reconcile customer master-data change logs and timestamps with the bank-change flag used in P1/P2.
    - Review how risk level is computed and updated; check for late updates or overrides after approval.
- **Hypothesis C (special-case overrides not reflected in policy text)**: Certain categories (e.g., long-standing customers) have unwritten or local exceptions allowing Auto Approve beyond P1 criteria.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Interview process owners and underwriters about unwritten practices.
    - Compare exception cases with formal policy documents to identify discrepancies.

### 4.4 Pattern 3 – External adjuster assessments without core Assess Loss (CL-606 and peers)

- **Hypothesis A (pure integration gap – moderate)**: All external adjuster reports correspond to completed and appropriately timed assessments; only the conversion into `Assess Loss` events is missing.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Sample a subset of cases with `M(Assess Loss)` and verify that each has a signed external report with timestamps preceding Fraud Screen and Manual Approve.
    - Verify that no claims in this set lack any adjuster documentation.
- **Hypothesis B (incomplete documentation – higher risk)**: Some claims do not actually have a signed report, or the report is unsigned / draft, weakening the assessment control.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Check for missing or unsigned documents among the nine high-value cases.
    - Review adjuster SLAs and monitoring for late or incomplete reports.
- **Hypothesis C (case–document linkage weaknesses)**: Documents exist but are not consistently or immutably tied to the claim, leading to future traceability or tampering risk.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Examine how case IDs or other keys are stored in the document repository; test whether they can be edited after the fact.
    - Attempt to re-link documents programmatically using current rules to determine determinism and robustness.

### 4.5 Pattern 7 – Notification evidence gaps (CL-608, CL-603)

- **Hypothesis A (notifications always sent, but fragmented evidence – governance risk)**: Notifications are correctly sent (e.g., via secure portal), but only CRM records them; core system lacks consistent events and stable links.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Reconstruct notification status for a sample of high-value claims from CRM and portal logs; confirm that customers were notified within 24h.
    - Check for cases where CRM shows no notification while payment was made.
- **Hypothesis B (occasional missed notifications – operational failure)**: Some notifications genuinely fail or are never initiated, and CRM linking issues mask this.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Compare CRM and core logs to identify claims with payment but no corresponding notification evidence in any system.
    - Analyze customer complaints or inquiries following claim payments as potential signals of non-notification.
- **Hypothesis C (over-logging retries – analytical noise)**: The retrying connector creates duplicate notification entries that confuse conformance analysis but not the customer experience.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Confirm that all duplicate `Notify Claimant` events share a message ID and that customers do not receive multiple messages.
    - Examine connector settings to understand retry policies and logging behavior.

### 4.6 Pattern 5 – Denied and withdrawn cases (CL-607, CL-612)

- **Hypothesis A (model intentionally excludes these – out-of-scope)**: The model M0 is strictly for payable claims; denied and withdrawn leads should be analysed with a separate intake/closure model.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Review model design documentation and governance approvals that define scope as “payable claims only.”
    - Check whether there exists another process artefact for denial/withdrawal handling.
- **Hypothesis B (scope drift – incomplete modelling)**: Business stakeholders expect the model to cover full claim intake including denials and withdrawals; the omission is an unintended gap.
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Consult process owner statements versus model owner statements; resolve the dispute documented in P6.
    - Compare standard operating procedures with model content; see if denial/withdrawal is described but not modelled.
- **Hypothesis C (mixed practice – inconsistent use of denial/withdrawal paths)**: Some business units use out-of-model paths for denials/withdrawals, while others use workarounds within the payable-claims model (e.g., small negative payments).
  - **Evidence tests**:
    - Analyze event logs across branches/regions to see if denial/withdrawal behavior differs structurally.
    - Check whether certain units show low frequencies of `Deny Claim` or `Withdraw Claim` events but similar business volumes.

## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

Alignment raw_cost here is a **technical distance measure**: it counts visible log/model mismatches with uniform weight 1, ignoring **amounts, risk levels, data conditions, evidence quality, and irreversibility**.
Several concrete cases illustrate why this cannot be equated with business risk:

1. **CL-601 vs. CL-604 (telemetry vs. payment-before-approval)**  
   - CL-601 has **raw_cost=3** purely from UI telemetry (`Open Workbench`, `Autosave`, `Close Workbench`) that P5 explicitly treats as non-business activities.  
   - CL-604 also has **raw_cost=3**, but its deviations indicate **payment executed before mandatory controls** and a missing Fraud Screen for a €90k claim (clear P2/P3 breach).  
   - Treating these costs as equally severe would be absurd: one is benign noise, the other is a critical control failure with large, irreversible financial exposure.

2. **CL-602 (raw_cost=1) vs. CL-603 (raw_cost=1)**  
   - CL-602’s single missing `Fraud Screen` event on a €480k HIGH-risk, recent-bank-change claim represents a potentially catastrophic fraud-control gap.  
   - CL-603’s extra `Notify Claimant` is simply a retry with the same message ID—no additional customer impact, no financial risk.  
   - Under the current cost function, these look identical (cost=1), demonstrating that the metric is **blind to exposure, policy criticality, and control type**.

3. **CL-609 and CL-610 (raw_cost=0 but policy violations)**  
   - Both cases follow a fully synchronous Auto Approve path, so raw_cost=0 indicates perfect fitness to the **control-flow model**.  
   - However, CL-609 violates P1 (amount €18k, MEDIUM risk), and CL-610 violates both P1 and P2 (recent bank change one hour before payment). These are **data-aware policy failures**: the model’s XOR branch does not encode P1/P2 conditions.  
   - Pure alignment therefore **misses high-risk Auto Approve misuse** entirely; zero cost is not the same as zero risk.

4. **CL-605 (silent moves) and CL-611 (label mismatch)**  
   - CL-605 includes a hidden `T(tau)` transition before Auto Approve; this silent move has cost 0 and is correctly treated as **fitting**, but it reminds us that some model behavior is invisible to the cost metric.  
   - CL-611 incurs **raw_cost=2** solely due to a label change (`Assess Damage` vs `Assess Loss`), even though the underlying activity and controls are intact.  
   - Here, cost captures **granularity and naming artefacts**, not actual control deficiencies.

5. **CL-607 and CL-612 (high cost, low exposure)**  
   - Both are **non-payable** outcomes (denied or withdrawn claims). CL-607 and CL-612 each have **raw_cost=4**, driven by process-model mismatches, not by missing controls on payments.  
   - They carry virtually **no financial exposure**, and their main risk area is around modelling scope and communication.  
   - High cost thus flags **model incompleteness** rather than direct operational or fraud risk.

6. **Missing-but-external evidence vs. missing activity (CL-606, CL-608)**  
   - CL-606 and CL-608 each have **raw_cost=1** from missing internal events (`Assess Loss`, `Notify Claimant`) despite strong external evidence (signed adjuster report, CRM notification).  
   - The cost function cannot distinguish between “activity never happened” and “activity happened but was logged elsewhere or linked weakly”.  
   - Business severity depends on the **reliability and immutability of external evidence**, not the mere presence of a model move `M(...)`.

These examples show that:

- **Cost-function design** (uniform weights) ignores control criticality: missing Fraud Screen and duplicate notifications both cost 1.
- **Scope and hidden moves**: `T(tau)` and out-of-scope branches (denials/withdrawals) distort cost without implying risk.
- **Data conditions** (amount, risk level, bank change) are not part of the model transitions, so alignment cannot see when Auto Approve violates P1/P2.
- **Activity granularity and label changes** (e.g., `Assess Damage` vs `Assess Loss`) inflate cost for semantic synonyms.
- **Duplicated telemetry** and **missing-but-external evidence** create high or low costs independent of customer, regulatory, or financial impact.

Therefore, alignment cost is useful as **diagnostic evidence**, but **business risk assessment must overlay policy, data, exposure, and evidence reliability**—as done in the severity framework and per-case judgments above.

import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from datetime import datetime, timedelta


def build_process_model():
    # 1. Parse the supplied process-tree string
    process_tree_str = "-> ( 'Register Claim', 'Validate Coverage', 'Assess Loss', X ( -> ( 'Fraud Screen', 'Manual Approve' ), 'Auto Approve' ), 'Pay Claim', 'Notify Claimant' )"
    tree = pm4py.parse_process_tree(process_tree_str)

    # 2. Convert it to an accepting Petri net
    net, initial_marking, final_marking = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(tree)
    return net, initial_marking, final_marking


def build_event_log():
    # 3. Construct a small event log from case traces using a pandas dataframe
    # (Times are illustrative; attributes capture amount, risk, bank-change flags.)
    base_time = datetime(2024, 1, 1, 9, 0, 0)
    events = []

    def add_trace(case, activities, amount, risk, bank_change=False):
        t = base_time
        for act in activities:
            events.append(
                {
                    "case:concept:name": case,
                    "concept:name": act,
                    "time:timestamp": t,
                    "amount": amount,
                    "risk": risk,
                    "bank_change_7d": bank_change,
                }
            )
            t += timedelta(minutes=5)

    # Sample traces approximating some of the described cases
    add_trace(
        "CL-601",
        ["Register Claim", "Validate Coverage", "Assess Loss", "Auto Approve", "Pay Claim", "Notify Claimant"],

        "LOW",
        bank_change=False,
    )
    add_trace(
        "CL-602",
        ["Register Claim", "Validate Coverage", "Assess Loss", "Manual Approve", "Pay Claim", "Notify Claimant"],
        480000,
        "HIGH",
        bank_change=True,
    )
    add_trace(
        "CL-604",
        ["Register Claim", "Validate Coverage", "Assess Loss", "Pay Claim", "Manual Approve", "Notify Claimant"],
        90000,
        "MEDIUM",
        bank_change=False,

    df_events = pd.DataFrame(events)
    # Convert to an event log that pm4py can consume
    from pm4py.objects.log.util import dataframe_utils

    df_events = dataframe_utils.convert_timestamp_columns_in_df(df_events)

    alignments = pm4py.conformance_diagnostics_alignments(event_log, net, im, fm)

    # 5. Note (in comments) that optimal alignments / formatting can differ
    # The alignments returned here may not match the supplied notation exactly,
    # but they serve as a reproducible diagnostic example.
    return alignments


def build_move_summary_df():

        "withdrawn_out_of_scope": "model_scope_mismatch",

    # Example: sort by (policy_violations, total_amount) to highlight where

    risk_table = agg.sort_values(
