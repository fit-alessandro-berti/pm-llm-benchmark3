# 2. Integrated explanation

The early decline in service time and errors coincides with a change in work. Positions 1–5 average 18.8 minutes, 3.4% errors, and complexity of 0.72. By positions 16–30 those figures are 13.6 minutes, 2.5%, and 0.54. The first hour includes complex, high-priority carryover, with seniors taking some of the hardest cases. As that backlog clears, simpler assignments and a stabilizing queue can improve both measures. Repetition and warm-up are plausible contributors if the improvement persists within comparable work.

From positions 31–50 to 76+, service falls from 12.9 to 10.8 minutes while errors rise from 2.7% to 7.9% and seven-day rework rises from 5.0% to 13.7%. Mean queue length rises from 31 to 88 while case complexity and the high-priority share continue to fall. Easier work can account for some of the speed-up. Shorter service and higher errors within both reported complexity bands suggest that the shift toward simpler work is insufficient on its own. Under pressure, workers may shorten scrutiny, select quick jobs, or batch steps. The shorter recorded service can also reflect timestamp entry. Any time saved on an initial step has to be weighed against later checks and repair, including work passed to another shift.

The late quality decline is conditional. Juniors have 4.4% later errors at low WIP and 12.4% at high WIP, versus 2.2% and 4.9% for seniors. Early shift finishes with a smaller queue and reports 4.2% errors in its last two hours, while Day finishes with a larger queue and reports 7.5%. These are comparisons across different case mixes and staffing arrangements. They make pressure and experience important to test and leave room for fatigue to interact with pressure. Night's urgent exceptions and different staffing make its shift averages insufficient to identify circadian effects. End-of-shift checks may reveal earlier defects, and human fixes to Bot_Risk outputs can inflate the apparent late workload and misattribute errors if grouped by the person or hour of detection.

# 3. Proposed analyses

1. **Build the event and shift timelines.** Use start time for exposure and completion minus start for elapsed event service. Audit missing, negative, rounded, zero, and overlapping intervals. Distinguish bot events, valid instant human events, and questionable records. Verify the unit of the supplied sequence and reconstruct or cross-check it within each resource and dated shift instance using starts, with an explicit rule for ties. Reconcile high event counts and concurrent intervals before interpreting counts or summed durations as workload. Derive elapsed minutes and local clock hour with the correct overnight shift date. Record when the case was ready, assigned, started, completed, checked, and transferred when those timestamps exist. Keep WIP and queue snapshots as observed before work starts and document what population each counts. Use pre-assignment or lagged snapshots as a sensitivity check if the start snapshot may already reflect dispatch.

2. **Compare like work within workers and shifts.** Show sample sizes and coverage by worker, dated shift, activity, position, elapsed-time band, and load. Compare the same worker doing the same activity at similar pre-start complexity, priority, carryover age, prior process state, and pressure. Where the data support it, compare early and late starts within the same dated shift. Use flexible curves for elapsed time and position, fit them separately first, then separate them where there is enough independent variation. At similar elapsed times, compare prior same-activity repetitions and cumulative active minutes as distinct exposures. Position depends on earlier pace and cases. Show how the estimates change when the worker and activity mix is held fixed, and flag unsupported early-to-late comparisons. Check whether incomplete or deferred work is missing from the fastest-looking cohorts.

3. **Estimate pressure, time, and experience patterns.** Fit a model suitable for skewed positive service times, such as a Gamma model with a log link, with median and 90th-percentile checks. Model errors with a binomial model. Use worker and activity effects and account for repeated cases and shared shift or day shocks in uncertainty estimates. With few workers, show sensitivity to leaving out individual workers and to resampling appropriate day or shift blocks. Adjust for continuous pre-start complexity, priority, carryover and available staffing and arrival measures. Report standardized minutes and error probabilities with uncertainty, separately for low, medium, and high WIP, and check continuous load curves around 70. Estimate WIP-by-complexity, WIP-by-experience, and WIP-by-elapsed-time interactions where data permit. Test queue length separately before estimating joint effects. Estimate experience differences in response to load or position within workers, and treat baseline differences between experience groups as descriptive.

   Use local clock hour on a 24-hour cycle alongside shift schedule and day-of-week controls. At 13:00, Early staff are seven hours in and Day staff are four hours in. At two hours into shift, their respective clocks read 08:00 and 11:00. Seek comparable work and, where possible, use workers who rotate for both kinds of comparison. Within a fixed schedule, elapsed time and clock time move together. Where overlap is weak, report their joint pattern. Show how time and position curves change as mix, pressure, and batching enter the analysis. Pressure and batching can themselves be consequences of events earlier in the shift, so the adjusted curves describe conditional associations. Use controlled staffing or break trials for stronger causal tests.

4. **Trace batches, errors, and downstream work.** Use batch detection by activity and resource within dated shifts. Inspect start and completion clusters, consecutive runs, and queue batch membership if recorded. Vary candidate batch gaps and sizes and check whether the effects survive among comparable unbatched events. Use dotted charts and activity/resource timelines to check late reviews and performance DFGs to locate delays on paths into Quality Check, Rework Correction, and handovers. Keep whole case histories for selected cohorts and validate delays on overlapping paths against start and completion times.

   Define the index action and link errors and rework to that action where the case trail supports it. Keep source, detection, and correction timestamps and resources separate. Use only cohorts with seven days of follow-up from the index completion for a simple seven-day rework rate, or account for right censoring in a time-to-rework analysis. Count eligible index actions with at least one linked rework and count repeat corrections and human repair minutes separately. Where source attribution is ambiguous, report case-level results and sensitivity to linkage rules. Report audited-event error rates alongside review coverage and detection lag, or use a consistently sampled audit to compare defect rates across conditions. Compare first-pass case resolutions and total human handling across shifts to account for the costs of apparent speed.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Assignment, deferral, handovers, and skill routing affect both who appears in each position bin and which defects become visible. Difficult carryover can make an experienced worker look slow. A junior assigned a late batch can inherit a very different risk profile. Someone who checks work thoroughly can log more errors by finding more of them. Raw rates by worker or shift are therefore poor evidence for individual performance. Even after adjustment, unrecorded case difficulty and differences in audit coverage can remain.

Timestamps can capture delayed data entry, interruptions, and concurrent work. An error flag can record the time of discovery, and a rework flag or repeated activity can capture a legitimate repeat. Validate samples with operational staff, make denominators and coverage visible, and treat unlinked errors as unresolved for individual attribution. Account for Bot_Risk output volume, review coverage, and human correction time in automation evaluations. Assess the quality of the original automated action separately from the work of the person who detects or repairs it.

Staffing levels, queue design, scheduling, and performance targets are decisions management can change. Investigate pressure and review burdens at the process level, including spillovers to the next shift. Restrict identifiable views to authorized operational review, suppress unstable small-cell rates, and give workers a way to correct records or explain context. Use corroborated case review and a consistent audit protocol before individual conclusions enter personnel decisions.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Allow drill-down by activity, shift type, local clock hour, elapsed-time and sequence bands, experience, complexity, priority, and pressure. Include counts, data coverage, and comparable baselines beside trends. Annotate changes in staffing, break schedules, dispatch rules, and alert rollout so that changes in speed can be read alongside waiting, quality, and work transferred between shifts.
