## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I use **policy effects**, **controlled effects**, and **interventional indirect analogues**, not natural direct/indirect effects. Natural effects would require cross-world quantities such as manual-review behavior under one policy combined with outcomes under another, plus strong assumptions violated by exposure-induced routing, reviewer assignment, congestion, and shared-queue interference.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P1 suppress flag display vs P0 current system | Eligible arriving cases and the operational system over a fixed horizon | Mean arrival-to-close time | Total policy effect | Yes, if score visibility and all fallback rules are specified | Human behavior under hidden flag; system-wide interference; no observed P1 data |
| E2 | P2 retain priority triage but remove mandatory review vs P0 | Flagged cases under current eligibility rules | Arrival-to-triage completion | Controlled policy effect with priority held on | Yes | Concurrent workload and behavior changes; P2 may alter later feedback into triage queues |
| E3 | Stochastically replace review occurrence under P0 by its P2 distribution, retaining P0 downstream handling conditional on history | Flagged cases with covariate overlap | Cycle time | Interventional indirect analogue through manual-review occurrence | Partly | Exposure-induced judgment and queue state confound review–outcome relations |
| E4 | P3 protected review capacity vs P0, holding review eligibility/routing fixed | Flagged reviewed cases plus all cases sharing queues | Manual queue wait | Controlled queue-capacity effect; indirect pathway analogue | Yes, if capacity, staffing, and protection rules are specified | Queue interference, time-varying demand, displacement of unflagged work |
| E5 | Draw manual waiting times from the P3 distribution while retaining P0 review occurrence and service rules | Reviewed cases with feasible queue histories | Total cycle time | Interventional indirect analogue through manual waiting | Conceptually, but not from these data alone | Waiting is jointly generated by other cases, staffing, priority, and review composition |
| E6 | P4 revised escalation criteria/capacity vs P0 | Reviewed cases eligible for escalation | Escalation occurrence, queue wait, and arrival-to-close | Separable policy-component effect / interventional escalation analogue | Yes only after criteria and capacity are operationalized | Risk-dependent escalation, scarce-team interference, quality consequences |
| E7 | P2 vs P0 | Flagged cases and system over a fixed horizon | Total cycle time | Total policy effect | Yes | Removing review changes routing, escalation, outcomes, and queues simultaneously |
| E8 | P3 vs P0 | All arriving cases, reported separately by flag | Total cycle time | Total policy effect including spillovers | Yes | Capacity may be genuinely additional or merely reassigned; interference |
| E9 | P1, P2, P3, or P4 vs P0 | All completed eligible cases | Correct decision, adverse-event rate, rework, or calibrated decision utility | Total policy effect on quality | Only if a post-decision quality endpoint is defined | Recorded approve/reject is a decision, not ground-truth quality; delayed labels |
| E10 | Priority triage enabled vs disabled while downstream routing is held to a prespecified common rule | Cases near the flag threshold | Triage completion time | Controlled direct/separable component effect through priority | Yes in a factorial or threshold-based design | Priority changes others’ waiting; threshold manipulation and imperfect overlap |
| E11 | P1 vs P0 among cases near the score cutoff | Cases in a narrow risk-score bandwidth | Decision outcome | Total effect of displaying the flag | Locally well-defined | Score may directly remain visible, threshold sorting, limited local generalizability |

Interference arises because priority, review, and escalation consume shared capacity: one case’s policy can change another case’s waiting time. Review occurrence can induce mediator–outcome confounding by changing reviewer assignment, accumulated evidence, escalation eligibility, and queue state. Manual findings are exposure-induced variables affecting both escalation and completion, so adjusting for them may block part of the effect while opening selection paths. Treatment-confounder feedback also occurs when the flag accelerates triage, which changes calendar-time queue entry and hence downstream congestion. System-level estimands therefore require a fixed arrival horizon and staffing regime; individual stable-treatment assumptions are inappropriate.

The raw contrast strongly supports the descriptive pattern: flagged cases start triage about 2.3 hours sooner and complete triage slightly faster, yet average total cycle time is roughly 9 hours longer. Every flagged case is reviewed in this sample, versus four of nine unflagged cases. Among reviewed cases, flagged cases wait substantially longer after triage before manual review; escalated flagged cases then incur another seven-hour average wait. High complexity and escalation also align with long cycles, while high-load flagged arrivals are slower than normal-load flagged arrivals. These are not causal effects. Flagged cases have higher scores and complexity, review and escalation are selected by evolving case information, resources differ by pathway, and queue entry times depend on earlier policy effects. Shared queues additionally violate case-level independence. Thus the decomposition locates where elapsed time accrues but cannot uniquely attribute it to the flag, underlying risk, routing rules, staffing, or congestion.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does flag display cause priority and routing near the score threshold? | Regression discontinuity around the operational cutoff, estimating local jumps in triage wait, review, escalation, cycle time, and quality | Smooth functions of score; complexity, arrival time/load for precision | Deterministic threshold, no score manipulation, potential outcomes smooth at cutoff | Density/heaping test; covariate continuity; placebo cutoffs; verify a jump in flag and priority | No first-stage jump in priority/review, or discontinuities at placebo cutoffs |
| Within similar recorded risk, is flag status associated with delay? | Matched or flexible outcome modeling within narrow score bands and complexity strata; useful descriptively if flag exceptions create overlap | Score, complexity, arrival hour/day, load, resource availability, pre-policy history | Conditional exchangeability and positivity | Standardized balance; overlap plots; alternative bandwidths | Estimates disappear with narrow overlap or are dominated by extrapolation |
| Is downstream waiting generated by actual queue state? | Reconstruct queue lengths/workload at each stage entry from event timestamps and staffing logs; model wait as a function of state and policy | Queue length, work in process, service capacity, shift, arrival cohort, complexity, score | Event logs capture arrivals, starts, departures, and capacity; queue discipline is modeled | Predicted wait should rise with workload; residuals should not cluster by shift/resource | Flag coefficient remains large after rich queue state, suggesting specialization or hidden work rather than congestion |
| What is the effect of changing display or routing? | Clustered stepped-wedge policy change or randomized encouragement to suppress display/remove automatic review, with system-level analysis | Randomization strata: score band, complexity, shift, load; intention-to-treat | Compliance measured, no concurrent policy change, clusters limit contamination | Check first-stage changes in display/routing and pre-trends; randomization balance | Weak first stage or simultaneous staffing changes make the policy contrast uninformative |
| How much of P2’s effect operates through review occurrence versus waiting? | Longitudinal g-computation or interventional effect model that separately randomizes review occurrence and queue-wait distributions | Baseline score, complexity, arrival conditions; time-varying triage findings, queue state, resource assignment | Sequential exchangeability, consistency, positivity, correctly measured time-varying confounders; interference modeled or bounded | Compare parametric, weighting, and simulation estimates; inspect mediator overlap and weight instability | Severe non-overlap or sensitivity to modest mediator–outcome confounding |
| Is escalation the main avoidable bottleneck? | Randomize an escalation-screening rule or incremental senior-capacity blocks among eligible reviewed cases | Review findings, score, complexity, queue state, shift | Eligibility specified before assignment; capacity is incremental; quality follow-up complete | First-stage change in escalation wait/occurrence; no deterioration in adverse outcomes | Cycle time barely changes, or quality worsens beyond the safety margin |
| Could hidden underlying risk explain the flag–duration association? | Quantitative bias analysis or negative-control calibration using later adverse events/rework as proxies | Recorded score, complexity, load, time, observed resources | Sensitivity parameters span plausible effects of latent risk | E-values/bias contours; compare with repeated risk assessments or audit subsample | A modest latent-risk association fully explains the adjusted difference |
| Is there shared-queue interference? | Randomize policy by shift/day or saturation level; estimate direct and spillover effects on treated and untreated cases | Cluster workload, staffing, case mix, arrival timing | Partial interference within clusters; little cross-cluster queue sharing | Unflagged waits should change with treated saturation if spillovers exist | No saturation response despite valid first stage weakens the congestion mechanism |
| Are apparent effects artifacts of clock or data recording? | Audit event timestamps against system/server logs and calculate durations around midnight, shifts, daylight transitions | Site/time zone, shift, resource, event source | Audit source is more accurate and event semantics are stable | Negative durations, implausible concurrency, systematic round-hour lags, source disagreement | If “start” means assignment rather than work start, queue/service decomposition must be revised |
| Negative-control check | Test whether future flag saturation predicts already-completed triage service, or whether an irrelevant future-shift exposure predicts current outcomes | Arrival cohort, shift, score, complexity | Future exposure cannot causally affect already completed stages | Significant future-exposure association indicates temporal confounding or logging artifacts | A robust association would undermine queue-model exchangeability and timestamp interpretation |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 current bundle | Fast flagged triage | Heavy review routing and scarce senior queue remain | Baseline; flagged mean observed at 17.98 h | Current safeguards preserved | Likely delays both flagged and other queue users | Descriptive evidence only | Use as control |
| P1 suppress flag display | Priority may disappear unless implemented independently; human score use may partially recreate it | Review/escalation may fall or become inconsistent | Ambiguous | High risk of undocumented discretionary substitution and missed compliance rules | May relieve queues, but behavior is unpredictable | Weak | Do not deploy first |
| P2 retain priority, remove mandatory review | Flagged triage benefit preserved | Review demand should fall if exceptions are selective; escalation demand may also fall | Likely shorter for screened-out cases; system effect potentially favorable | Material risk if automatic review captures true latent risk | Likely benefits shared queues | Moderate mechanistic plausibility, no causal estimate | Preferred limited pilot with safety screening |
| P3 add protected flagged review capacity | Triage unchanged | Flagged review waits should fall; effect on others depends on whether capacity is incremental | Likely shorter, especially where review wait dominates | Low if review content is unchanged | Beneficial if new capacity; harmful if carved out of existing staff | Strong descriptive bottleneck evidence, weak causal evidence | Good alternative if compliance forbids P2 |
| P4 revise escalation criteria or capacity | Triage unchanged | Senior waits or demand should fall; manual queue largely unchanged | Large benefit mainly for escalated cases | Potentially substantial if escalation prevents bad decisions | Added capacity helps; threshold tightening frees capacity | Moderate bottleneck evidence, limited sample | Pilot after review-screening evidence or with incremental capacity |

**Limited pilot: P2**

- **Eligibility:** Flagged, medium-complexity cases in a prespecified score band where current rules permit discretion; exclude legally mandated review, known high-severity indicators, and high complexity.
- **Assignment:** Individual randomization within shift and score band: P0 mandatory review versus P2 priority triage plus a standardized post-triage screen that permits review only on explicit criteria. Analyze intention-to-treat. If queue spillovers are strong, randomize shift-level saturation instead.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** Do not reduce existing staff during the pilot. Pause enrollment if manual work-in-process or senior-queue age exceeds a prespecified operational limit; report effects on unflagged cases.
- **Primary outcome:** Arrival-to-close time.
- **Secondary outcomes:** Triage completion, review occurrence, stage waits/services, escalation, rework, audit disagreement, delayed adverse outcomes, approval/rejection, and spillover waiting.
- **Minimum data:** All event start/end timestamps with time zone, routing reasons, queue state at entry, staffing/capacity by interval, resources, score version, complexity, review findings, escalation reasons, overrides, and delayed quality labels.
- **Stopping conditions:** Excess adverse outcomes or audit failures beyond a noninferiority margin; compliance breach; severe unflagged spillover; failed randomization/implementation; or missing quality follow-up above the prespecified limit.

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(CSV), na_values=["-"])
time_cols = [
    "arrival", "triage_start", "triage_done", "manual_start", "manual_done",
    "escalation_start", "escalation_done", "close"
]
for c in time_cols:
    # Local clock timestamps are retained as naive times because no timezone was supplied.
    df[c] = pd.to_datetime(df[c], errors="coerce")

HOUR = pd.Timedelta(hours=1)
def hours(delta):
    return delta / HOUR

# Explicit case-level intervals. Missing stages remain NaN, never zero.
df["arrival_to_triage_start"] = hours(df["triage_start"] - df["arrival"])
df["triage_service"] = hours(df["triage_done"] - df["triage_start"])
df["triage_done_to_manual_start"] = hours(df["manual_start"] - df["triage_done"])
df["manual_service"] = hours(df["manual_done"] - df["manual_start"])
df["manual_done_to_escalation_start"] = hours(
    df["escalation_start"] - df["manual_done"]
)
df["escalation_service"] = hours(
    df["escalation_done"] - df["escalation_start"]
)

last_done = df["triage_done"].copy()
last_done = last_done.where(df["manual_done"].isna(), df["manual_done"])
last_done = last_done.where(df["escalation_done"].isna(), df["escalation_done"])
df["residual_to_close"] = hours(df["close"] - last_done)
df["total_cycle"] = hours(df["close"] - df["arrival"])
df["manual_review"] = df["manual_start"].notna()
df["escalated"] = df["escalation_start"].notna()

# Wide-to-event conversion. Arrival and Close are instantaneous;
# processing stages have distinct start and completion timestamps.
events = []
for _, r in df.iterrows():
    attrs = {
        "case:concept:name": r["case"],
        "flag": int(r["flag"]),
        "risk_score": r["risk_score"],
        "complexity": r["complexity"],
        "arrival_load": r["arrival_load"],
        "outcome": r["outcome"],
    }

    def add_event(name, start, end, resource=None):
        if pd.isna(start) or pd.isna(end):
            return
        ev = dict(attrs)
        ev.update({
            "concept:name": name,
            "start_timestamp": start,
            "time:timestamp": end,
            "org:resource": resource if pd.notna(resource) else None,
        })
        events.append(ev)

    add_event("Register", r["arrival"], r["arrival"], None)
    add_event(
        "Triage", r["triage_start"], r["triage_done"], r["triage_resource"]
    )
    add_event(

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** Audit data show that unrecorded underlying risk almost fully determines both flagged routing and long downstream work, while safe P2 exclusions cannot capture it.
2. **Queue interference:** Protected or reduced review demand unexpectedly increases system-wide waiting—for example, by starving flexible shared resources or creating unstable priority cycles.
3. **Decision quality:** Skipping mandatory review causes adverse decisions, rework, audit disagreement, or compliance failures beyond the prespecified noninferiority margin.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** “Start” timestamps represent assignment, reservation, or documentation rather than actual work start, invalidating the inferred queue/service decomposition.
5. **Positivity/overlap:** Flagged and comparable unflagged or discretionary-review cases have essentially no overlap in score, complexity, or eligibility, so local evidence cannot support P2’s target population.
6. **Reviewer behavior:** Reviewers compensate for removed mandatory routing by escalating more cases, prolonging triage, informally queueing work, or overriding the screen, eliminating cycle-time gains.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed:** Flagged cases begin triage much sooner than unflagged cases—about 0.25 versus 2.52 hours after arrival—and have slightly shorter triage service. Nevertheless, their mean cycle time is about 17.98 versus 9.13 hours. All flagged cases receive manual review, and flagged reviewed cases wait about 6.36 hours after triage. Escalated flagged cases average 23.83 hours total and incur roughly seven additional hours before senior service.

**Plausible mechanism:** The policy bundle accelerates entry through triage but routes many cases into slower, shared downstream queues. High complexity, reviewer specialization, escalation, shift timing, and scarce senior capacity plausibly amplify this effect. Priority may also move cases into review at congested times and impose spillovers on other cases.

**Unidentified:** These data do not separate the flag’s organizational effect from underlying risk, complexity, human judgment, resource assignment, or queue interference. Suppressing the flag is not equivalent to preserving priority while changing review rules. Natural mediation effects are especially unsupported because review and escalation create exposure-induced confounding, and one case’s routing affects others. Approve/reject is not a quality label.

**Most useful next evidence:** A guarded P2 pilot among medium-complexity flagged cases would preserve the clearly observed triage benefit while randomizing removal of mandatory review subject to explicit safety criteria. It directly tests review occurrence, downstream demand, total cycle time, quality, and spillovers. If mandatory review cannot legally be varied, incremental P3 capacity is the safer alternative, but it answers a narrower capacity question.
