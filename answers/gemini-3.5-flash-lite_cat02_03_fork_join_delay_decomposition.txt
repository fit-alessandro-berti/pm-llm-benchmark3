# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

To rigorously decompose total case flow time without double-counting or omitting intervals, let $c$ index cases ($c \in \{F01, \dots, F12\}$) and $i$ index parallel branches ($i \in \{A, B, C\})$. 

Let timestamps be mapped to elapsed minutes from process entry (`arrival`). The total case flow time $T_{\text{total}}(c)$ is partitioned into mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive sequential phases:

$$T_{\text{total}}(c) = T_{\text{upstream}}(c) + T_{\text{fork\_join}}(c) + T_{\text{downstream\_queue}}(c) + T_{\text{final}}(c)$$

Where:
1. **Upstream Elapsed Time**: 
   $$T_{\text{upstream}}(c) = \text{fork\_release}_c - \text{arrival}_c$$
2. **Fork-Join Stage Time**: The elapsed time from fork release until all parallel branches have completed and synchronized:
   $$T_{\text{fork\_join}}(c) = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$$
   Where $\text{join\_ready}_c = \max_{i \in \{A, B, C\}} (\text{end}_{i, c})$.
3. **Downstream Queueing**: The waiting time for the final resource after the join condition is satisfied:
   $$T_{\text{downstream\_queue}}(c) = \text{final\_start}_c - \text{join\_ready}_c$$
4. **Final Service**: 
   $$T_{\text{final}}(c) = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{final\_start}_c$$

### Branch-Level Internal Decomposition
Within the fork-join stage, each branch $i$ undergoes an internal lifecycle:
$$\text{fork\_release}_c \le \text{start}_{i, c} \le \text{end}_{i, c} \le \text{join\_ready}_c$$

For each branch $i$, we define:
- **Queue Delay**: $Q_{i, c} = \text{start}_{i, c} - \text{fork\_release}_c$
- **Active Service**: $S_{i, c} = \text{end}_{i, c} - \text{start}_{i, c}$
- **Branch Total Elapsed**: $E_{i, c} = Q_{i, c} + S_{i, c} = \text{end}_{i, c} - \text{fork\_release}_c$
- **Synchronization Waiting**: $W^{\text{sync}}_{i, c} = \text{join\_ready}_c - \text{end}_{i, c}$

### Why Summing All Branch Synchronization Waits Double Counts Elapsed Time
A common analytical error is summing branch-level metrics independently across parallel threads. The fork-join stage operates as a **maximum-completion constraint**: $\text{join\_ready}_c = \max_i (\text{end}_{i, c})$. 

If one were to sum the synchronization waiting times $\sum_{i} W^{\text{sync}}_{i, c}$, the idle time spent by non-critical branches waiting for the lagging critical branch would be counted multiple times. Specifically, the time between the *first* branch completion and the *last* branch completion would be accumulated for every non-critical branch simultaneously. Elapsed clock time advances only once; therefore, the fork-join stage duration equals $E_{i, c} + W^{\text{sync}}_{i, c}$ for *any* single branch, rather than the sum over all branches.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

### Counterfactual 1: Reducing A Service Time by 20%
- **Mechanism**: A's service time drops from 50 minutes to 40 minutes ($50 \times 0.8 = 40$).
- **Direct Benefits**: In cases where Branch A was critical (F01, F02, F08, F10, F11), $\text{join\_ready}$ shifts 10 minutes earlier.
- **Zero-Benefit Cases**: In cases where Branch B or C was already driving $\text{join\_ready}$ and finished *after* the new A completion time (e.g., F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F12 where B finishes at $\ge 11:10$), accelerating A yields **zero reduction in total flow time** because Branch B remains the bottleneck.
- **Secondary Effects**: Shifts the bottleneck entirely to Branch B. Synchronization waiting for Branch A increases when Branch B is critical.

### Counterfactual 2: Reducing B Queue Delay by 50%
- **Mechanism**: B's queue delay is halved (e.g., a 30-minute queue drops to 15 minutes).
- **Direct Benefits**: Directly compresses Branch B's start-to-end elapsed time in congested cases (F06, F07, F12), bringing B's completion time earlier and reducing $\text{join\_ready}$ by up to 15 minutes.
- **Zero-Benefit Cases**: In cases where Branch B's original queue was already short or where Branch A was already critical (e.g., F01, F02, F08, F10, F11), reducing B's queue yields zero benefit because Branch A's 50-minute service time continues to govern the critical path.
- **Secondary Effects**: Uncovers hidden queueing constraints at the upstream fork or machine resource allocation.

### Counterfactual 3: Eliminating Downstream Queueing Before F
- **Mechanism**: Downstream final resources (F1 and F2) are scaled or scheduled such that $\text{final\_start} = \text{join\_ready}$ instantaneously (eliminating $T_{\text{downstream\_queue}}$).
- **Direct Benefits**: Across all 12 cases, total flow time is reduced by exactly the downstream queue duration, which averages **16.25 minutes** per case (ranging from 8 to 25 minutes).
- **Zero-Benefit Cases**: None. Every single case experiences positive downstream queueing (ranging from 8 min in F01 to 25 min in F04 and F10).
- **Secondary Effects**: Substantially increases utilization pressure on resources F1 and F2. If F1 and F2 are already fully occupied, eliminating the queue before F simply shifts the queue upstream or causes resource contention at the final station.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

### Recommendation 1: Implement Dedicated Resource Pooling for Branch B (Machine Stress Test)
- **Operational Concept**: Branch B exhibits variable queue delays (up to 30–35 minutes) and frequent critical-path dominance. Invest in additional machine capacity or implement strict pre-dispatch scheduling at `fork_release`.
- **Throughput & Tail Impact**: Eliminating Branch B queueing shifts the system's critical path permanently to Branch A (50 min fixed service). This compresses the upper tail of flow times, eliminating high-latency outliers (like F07 at 130 min).
- **Resource Utilization & Bottlenecks**: Increases utilization on Branch B machines during peak arrival windows. Once B queueing is eliminated, **Branch A becomes the absolute system bottleneck**, capping minimum fork-join time at 50 minutes.
- **Synchronization Effects**: Increases synchronization waiting for branches B and C when Branch A is running.
- **Pilot/Measurement**: Pilot queue-reduction scheduling for Branch B on odd-numbered cases while measuring changes in $\text{join\_ready}$ variance.

### Recommendation 2: Dynamic Load-Balancing and Capacity Expansion at the Final Station (F1 / F2)
- **Operational Concept**: Every single case experiences downstream queueing (mean 16.25 minutes) despite parallel branches taking 40–50 minutes. This proves that the final station is under-resourced relative to upstream generation. Add a third resource (F3) or cross-train staff.
- **Throughput & Tail Impact**: Directly reduces total case flow time by ~16 minutes across the board without altering technical branch work.
- **Resource Utilization & Bottlenecks**: Lowers idle time between `join_ready` and `final_start`. However, if downstream capacity exceeds upstream fork-join capacity, the bottleneck shifts entirely upstream to Branches A and B.
- **Pilot/Measurement**: Measure resource idle times for F1 and F2 to determine whether queueing is caused by processing duration (20 min service) or synchronization gaps in case arrival batches.

# 7. Limits of inference

From the supplied timestamps alone, several critical operational dimensions cannot be definitively identified:

1. **Causality vs. Correlation**: Timestamps record *when* events occurred, but not *why*. For instance, why Branch B queues for 30 minutes in some cases and 5 minutes in others cannot be determined from completion logs alone (it could be operator breaks, batching rules, or upstream equipment failures).
2. **Branch Resource Capacity**: We observe start and end times, but we do not know how many physical machines or personnel are assigned to Branch A, B, or C. A 30-minute queue on Branch B could reflect a single shared machine processing cases sequentially or strict batching constraints.
3. **Work Preemption**: The logs record complete-event data. We cannot determine whether work on a branch was continuous or if tasks were paused, preempted, or interrupted mid-stream.
4. **Final-Station Waiting Nature**: It is analytically ambiguous whether downstream queueing ($\text{final\_start} - \text{join\_ready}$) represents true physical resource contention (both F1 and F2 busy) or an administrative release policy (e.g., cases held intentionally until a specific batch window or quality sign-off).
5. **Upstream Preparation Dynamics**: The interval between `arrival` and `fork_release` is treated as a black-box upstream delay; internal subprocesses within preparation are entirely unobserved.
