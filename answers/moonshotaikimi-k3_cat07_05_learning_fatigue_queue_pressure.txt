# 2. Integrated explanation

The defensible explanation is a sequence of changing operating regimes, not a simple “workers learn and then become fatigued” story.

**At shift start, slow service is heavily selected.** The first five events occur around minute 22, with mean complexity 0.72, queue length 46, and 41% high-priority work. Early shifts inherit night or weekend backlog, and seniors deliberately take complex carryover cases. Some genuine warm-up may also occur, especially for juniors, but the early 18.8-minute service time should not be interpreted as cold-start inefficiency alone.

**The middle of the shift is consistent with stabilization.** By positions 16–50, queue length has fallen, complexity and priority are lower, and service time declines to about 13 minutes. Errors reach their lowest levels, around 2.5%–2.7%. This is the region most consistent with warm-up or learning without a quality penalty. However, because case mix is simultaneously becoming easier, the raw service-time decline overstates any pure learning effect.

**Late in the shift, the process changes character.** Case mix is easier, yet errors rise sharply. Queue length increases from 31 in positions 31–50 to 62 and then 88. Service time continues to fall, but the WIP-stratified results show that high WIP produces faster work within both low- and high-complexity bands. That pattern supports queue-induced work intensification, shortened checking, batching, or deferral of difficult subtasks—not merely improved skill.

This explains why service time can fall while errors rise only under some conditions:

- Under low or moderate WIP, practice and familiarity can reduce service time without increasing errors.
- Under high WIP, workers may preserve throughput by spending less time on verification, handling items in batches, deferring Quality Check, or escalating less thoroughly.
- Complexity moderates the tradeoff: high-WIP/high-complexity work has a 10.9% error rate.
- Experience also moderates it: juniors have a 12.4% error rate under high WIP, compared with 4.9% for seniors.
- Lower average complexity late in the shift makes the late error increase more concerning, because easier mix would otherwise predict lower errors.

Fatigue remains plausible as a contributing or moderating mechanism, particularly in the final two hours. It may manifest as faster but less careful work rather than slower work. Nevertheless, the data do not isolate fatigue from WIP, case mix, breaks, or time of day.

Circadian effects are also not established. Night work is slower, but night cases are more complex and urgent, staffing is different, and night errors are not the highest. Conversely, Day shift has the worst late error rate while its queue grows sharply. That pattern points more strongly to demand and queue pressure than to clock time alone.

Finally, some late “errors” may be detection artifacts. If Quality Check is postponed until the last 45 minutes, errors made earlier are timestamped late. Batched Document Review can also make individual service times appear artificially short. Therefore, the late period combines real pressure-induced risk with possible measurement and workflow-timing effects.

Most importantly, **falling service time is not the same as improved end-to-end performance**. Under high WIP, touch time may fall while waiting time, rework, customer delay, and downstream correction costs rise.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The analysis should estimate conditional, within-worker patterns rather than compare raw position-bin means. It should also distinguish the event where an error was made from the event where it was detected.

**Data preparation and PM4Py enrichment**

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

log = pd.read_csv(
    "claims_event_log.csv",
    parse_dates=["time:timestamp", "start_timestamp", "shift_start_time"]
)

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    log,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

log["event_uid"] = np.arange(len(log))
log["service_min"] = (
    log["time:timestamp"] - log["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60

log["shift_type"] = log["shift_start_time"].dt.hour.map({
    6: "Early", 9: "Day", 14: "Late", 22: "Night"
})
log["shift_date"] = log["shift_start_time"].dt.date.astype(str)
log["hour_of_day"] = log["time:timestamp"].dt.hour

log["position_bin"] = pd.cut(
    log["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, np.inf],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"]
)

log["wip_band"] = pd.cut(
    log["wip_at_start"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 30, 70, np.inf],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"],
    right=False
)

# Use the operationally validated threshold rather than assuming the median.
COMPLEXITY_CUT = 0.65
log["complexity_band"] = np.where(
    log["case_complexity_score"] >= COMPLEXITY_CUT, "high", "low"
)

# Waiting before this activity began.
log = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
log["waiting_before_start_min"] = (
    log["start_timestamp"]
    - log.groupby("case:concept:name")["time:timestamp"].shift()
).dt.total_seconds() / 60

# Within-resource workload and interruption proxies.
log = log.sort_values([
    "org:resource", "shift_id",
    "sequence_position_within_shift", "time:timestamp"
])
rg = log.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id"], sort=False)

log["previous_resource_completion"] = rg["time:timestamp"].shift()
log["idle_gap_min"] = (
    log["start_timestamp"] - log["previous_resource_completion"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60
log["active_service_min_before"] = (
    rg["service_min"].cumsum() - log["service_min"]
)

# Approximate work spells; a gap threshold should be validated operationally.
log["new_work_spell"] = (log["idle_gap_min"] > 15).fillna(True)
log["work_spell_id"] = (
    log.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id"], sort=False)["new_work_spell"]
       .cumsum()
)

# Task-specific career exposure, rather than relying only on months employed.
log = log.sort_values(["org:resource", "time:timestamp"])
log["career_family_events"] = (
    log.groupby(["org:resource", "activity_family"], sort=False).cumcount()
)

# PM4Py case-level service, waiting, arrival, and completion context.
pm_log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log.copy())
pm_log = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(pm_log)
temporal_log = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log.copy())

# Inspect demand, end-of-shift bursts, batches, and handoffs.
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(
    log, "events_by_hour.svg", distr_type="hours"
)
pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(
    log,
    "dotted_by_resource_activity.svg",
    attributes=["org:resource", "concept:name"]
)
```

**Within-resource comparisons and within-shift position effects**

Use resource fixed effects or resource-by-activity fixed effects so that Senior_A is compared primarily with Senior_A, not with workers receiving different cases. A strict within-shift specification can use `shift_id` fixed effects.

```python
d = log.rename(columns={
    "case:concept:name": "case_id",
    "concept:name": "activity",
    "org:resource": "resource"
})

service_fit = smf.ols(
    """
    np.log1p(service_min) ~
        C(position_bin) * C(wip_band)
        + case_complexity_score * C(activity_family)
        + C(priority)
        + queue_length_at_start
        + resource_experience_months
        + C(wip_band):resource_experience_months
        + C(position_bin):case_complexity_score
        + C(shift_type) + C(hour_of_day) + C(shift_date)
        + C(resource)
    """,
    data=d
).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": d["resource"]}
)

error_fit = smf.logit(
    """
    error_flag ~
        C(position_bin) * C(wip_band)
        + case_complexity_score * C(activity_family)
        + C(priority)
        + queue_length_at_start
        + resource_experience_months
        + C(wip_band):resource_experience_months
        + C(shift_type) + C(hour_of_day) + C(shift_date)
        + C(resource)
    """,
    data=d
).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": d["case_id"]}
)
```

Recommended variants are:

- Replace `C(position_bin)` with a spline in `minutes_since_shift_start`.
- Use `C(shift_id)` instead of shift type and date for a strict within-shift estimate.
- Add `C(resource):C(activity_family)` when sample size permits.
- Use median or quantile regression because service times are usually skewed.
- Use penalized or mixed-effects logistic regression because errors are relatively rare.
- Cluster uncertainty by both case and resource, or use a cluster bootstrap.

Do not include raw position and minutes since shift start without checking collinearity. They measure nearly the same exposure in these data.

To separate warm-up, longer-term learning, and fatigue, estimate models with different exposure variables:

- first five events indicator;
- cumulative same-activity events this shift;
- cumulative same-activity events over the worker’s history;
- active service minutes before the event;
- events or minutes since the last substantial gap;
- clock hour.

Learning is supported when cumulative task-family exposure predicts faster service with stable or improving quality. Fatigue is supported when active time or time since a break predicts deterioration after clock hour, WIP, and mix are held fixed.

**WIP/queue stratification**

Analyze WIP as a continuous and nonlinear variable, not only as three bands. Also distinguish queue length, WIP, arrival rate, completion rate, and staffed capacity.

```python
service_by_band = {}
rework_by_band = {}

for band in ["low", "medium", "high"]:
    sublog = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, "wip_band", [band], level="event", retain=True
    )

    service_by_band[band] = pm4py.get_service_time(
        sublog,
        aggregation_measure="median"
    )

    # This detects repeated activities in cases; it complements, but does not
    # replace, a future "Rework Correction within 7 days" outcome.
    rework_by_band[band] = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(sublog)
```

Estimate:

- WIP splines and a possible threshold around 70;
- `WIP × complexity`;
- `WIP × activity family`;
- `WIP × resource experience`;
- `WIP × shift type`;
- position effects separately at low, medium, and high WIP.

WIP at event start is preferable to WIP at event completion because it is predetermined for that event. Nevertheless, it is still management- and demand-generated, so it should not automatically be interpreted as an exogenous causal treatment.

**Complexity adjustment and selection analysis**

Use several levels of adjustment:

1. Stratify by activity family, complexity band, and priority.
2. Fit activity-specific models with continuous complexity.
3. Standardize every worker or position bin to a common case-mix distribution.
4. Match or weight cases on complexity, priority, arrival hour, queue state, and activity.
5. Model the assignment process itself:
   - probability a senior receives the case;
   - probability it is handled in the first hour;
   - probability it goes to Bot_Risk;
   - probability it is deferred or reassigned.

Report both raw and adjusted results. Raw results describe operational burden; adjusted results help explain mechanisms. Neither should be presented alone.

**Shift and time-of-day controls**

Use the overlapping shifts to separate clock hour from elapsed shift time where possible. For example, at 10:00, Early workers have been working about four hours while Day workers have been working about one hour.

Use `pm4py.filter_time_range` to construct common clock windows, last-two-hour windows, and pre/post-intervention windows:

```python
# Illustrative single window; loop over dates and shifts in practice.
window = pm4py.filter_time_range(
    log,
    "2025-01-10 15:00:00",
    "2025-01-10 17:00:00",
    mode="events"
)
```

Fit models with:

- clock-hour fixed effects or splines;
- shift type;
- shift date;
- minutes since shift start;
- worker fixed effects;
- worker-by-shift or rotating-schedule contrasts.

If workers are permanently assigned to one shift, clock-time effects and worker selection cannot be fully separated.

**Batching detection**

Use PM4Py’s batch discovery and a transparent custom rule as a sensitivity analysis:

```python
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(
    log,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    resource_key="org:resource",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
    merge_distance=10 * 60,
    min_batch_size=3
)

# Flatten the version-specific result into:
# event_uid, batch_id, batch_type, batch_size, batch_start, batch_end.
# Then join it back to log.
```

A custom rule should flag consecutive events with:

- the same resource;
- the same activity or activity family;
- short gaps between completion and next start;
- similar queue or case attributes;
- unusually short individual service times.

Then compare:

- batched versus matched nonbatched events;
- batch size and event position within the batch;
- total batch duration per case rather than each compressed event duration;
- downstream Quality Check errors and seven-day rework;
- end-of-shift concentration of Quality Check.

Because batching may be a mediator of queue pressure, report models with and without batch indicators. Including batching estimates the non-batching pathway; excluding it estimates the total association of WIP.

**Resource-experience interactions**

Do not rank workers from raw late-shift errors. Instead, estimate:

- `high_WIP × resource_experience_months`;
- `high_WIP × role`;
- `complexity × experience`;
- `position × experience`;
- worker-specific learning slopes with shrinkage toward the role average.

The observed experience gradient is consistent with seniors having greater resilience under pressure, but it could also reflect differences in support, authority to escalate, or unmeasured case assignment.

**Rework/error downstream analysis**

An error flag at Quality Check may identify where an error was discovered, not where it originated. Construct a case timeline and link:

1. originating activity;
2. Quality Check or detection event;
3. Rework Correction;
4. subsequent completion and customer outcome.

Useful PM4Py diagnostics include:

- distinguish repeated activity detected by `get_rework_cases_per_activity` from the explicit `rework_flag`.

The interpretive decision rules should be:

- Position effect remains at fixed low WIP and fixed mix, with stable errors: **learning/warm-up**.
- Active time or time since break predicts deterioration at fixed clock hour and WIP: **fatigue**.
- Clock hour remains after elapsed work and shift position are controlled: **circadian/time-of-day association**.
- Errors and speed change nonlinearly with lagged WIP: **queue pressure**.
- Position effects disappear after matching or weighting: **case-mix/selection**.
- Short service and errors concentrate in detected batches or delayed QC: **batching/detection artifact**.
- Corrections follow automated decisions differentially under WIP: **automation interaction**.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed intervention | Expected benefit | Main risk | Event-log monitoring |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers** | May reduce cumulative workload and fatigue-related end-of-shift errors; creates clearer handoff boundaries. | Blunt because position effects are confounded. More handoffs can increase waiting, information loss, and end-of-shift carryover. Without added capacity, WIP may rise. | Risk-adjusted errors and rework by active minutes, WIP, and complexity; end-of-shift queue; handoff count; waiting after handoff; overtime; service and waiting time. Pilot before broad adoption. |
| **2. Move complex cases away from the first hour** | Could reduce the combination of warm-up and difficult carryover work; may smooth senior workload. | May delay urgent cases, move the risk peak later, increase carryover, and remove appropriate early senior attention. A blanket rule is likely harmful. | Complexity and priority mix by hour; time to triage and decision; SLA breaches; early and late adjusted errors; carryover; senior versus junior assignment. Prefer balanced complexity or senior pairing over blanket deferral. |
| **3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70** | Strongly supported by the data. Can trigger load balancing, temporary staffing, escalation support, or suspension of nonurgent work before errors rise. | A fixed WIP count ignores complexity, staffing, and activity mix. It can create alert fatigue or gaming if workers are blamed for crossing it. | Frequency and duration above threshold; risk-weighted WIP; time from alert to action; post-alert service, waiting, errors, and rework; high-complexity exposure; distribution of high-WIP work across workers. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases** | Reduces selection bias, improves fairness, and creates credible evidence about worker and mechanism effects. | Unrestricted randomization can assign complex or urgent cases to workers without required skills, harm customers, and destroy useful specialization. | Use constrained randomization within priority, complexity, skill, and authorization blocks. Monitor covariate balance, assignment propensities, SLA performance, adjusted errors, rework, and complexity-weighted workload equity. |
| **5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours** | May reduce fatigue and improve attention, especially under high WIP. | Without coverage, breaks can increase WIP and waiting. Poorly timed breaks can interrupt batches or urgent cases. | Stagger breaks and monitor adherence; post-break service and error changes within worker; WIP and waiting during breaks; interruption-related handoffs; high-WIP error rates. Use a stepped-wedge pilot. |
| **6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift** | Could smooth the end-of-shift correction burst and reduce time pressure. | Delays error discovery, lengthens rework latency, loses case context, and shifts backlog to the next shift. It can hide rather than reduce defects. Quality checks should not be removed for throughput. | Age of unchecked work; QC waiting time; time from error to detection; downstream customer impact; seven-day rework; next-shift backlog and handoff quality. Use only bounded, risk-based deferral with explicit ownership and deadlines. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Directly addresses the Day-shift WIP increase and provides expertise for complex or risky cases. | May overload seniors, pull them from other work, or create a review bottleneck. Benefits may be small if the bottleneck is elsewhere. | Day-shift WIP and queue length; high-complexity waiting; adjusted errors and rework; senior utilization; changes in downstream bottlenecks; complexity-weighted workload fairness. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | No reliable performance benefit as designed. It may appear to create accountability, but the raw metric mainly reflects exposure to WIP, case mix, and detection timing. | Underreporting, avoidance of complex or late-shift work, gaming, reduced psychological safety, unfair punishment for management-created queues, and worse error learning. | Do not implement raw penalties. If accountability metrics are used, monitor risk-adjusted outcomes with confidence intervals, reporting rates, assignment avoidance, workload exposure, coaching completion, and system-level corrective actions. |

For all interventions, use a stepped-wedge or interrupted time-series design where feasible. Compare risk-adjusted outcomes, not raw before/after averages, because demand and case mix will also change.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias is central.** Seniors receive complex early cases, juniors may receive different support, night staff receive urgent exceptions, and high-throughput workers contribute more late-position events. Comparing raw worker averages confounds performance with assignment.

- **Sequence position is not a neutral exposure.** Faster workers reach high positions sooner, while long tasks prevent other workers from reaching those positions. Event-level averages can therefore be length-biased.

- **Queue pressure is often created by management.** Staffing, arrival routing, shift boundaries, automation rules, and backlog policies determine WIP. It is unfair to attribute high-WIP errors solely to individual workers.

- **Error measurement is imperfect.** Error flags may depend on audit intensity, Quality Check timing, documentation practices, and willingness to report. An end-of-shift error may have originated hours earlier. Seven-day rework is censored for recently closed cases.

- **Service timestamps may not equal focused work.** Workers may multitask, pause, batch cases, or backfill timestamps. Bot events have near-zero duration. Short service time can therefore be accounting compression rather than genuine efficiency.

- **Automation complicates accountability.** More human corrections of Bot_Risk under high WIP could indicate automation weakness, better human scrutiny, different case selection, or rushed review. Workers should not be blamed for model drift or poor automation thresholds.

- **Punitive use of error rates can increase risk.** Penalties encourage underreporting, avoidance of difficult cases, premature closure, and concealment of near misses. Use errors for system learning, staffing decisions, coaching, and hazard detection.

- **Worker-level estimates need uncertainty and context.** Small samples, especially at night, can produce unstable rates. Use confidence intervals, shrinkage, minimum denominators, and case-mix adjustment. Avoid public leaderboards.

- **Privacy and due process matter.** Workers should know what is collected and how it is used. Individual-level drill-downs should be role-restricted, and consequential decisions should allow contextual review and appeal.

- **Do not sacrifice quality controls for apparent throughput.** The objective should be timely, appropriately staffed quality assurance, not fewer checks or lower reported errors.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should show raw and risk-adjusted measures side by side, with confidence intervals and minimum-denominator suppression. A 15-minute operational view and a daily/weekly management view can share the same event-log pipeline.

| Indicator | Definition and granularity | Operational use |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Service time** | Median, mean, and P90 `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`; also model-adjusted service-time residuals. Slice by activity, resource group, shift, clock hour, position, WIP, complexity, and priority. | Detects slowdowns and suspicious speed-ups. Do not interpret faster touch time as improvement without waiting and quality indicators. |
| **2. Waiting and cycle time** | Waiting from previous case-event completion to current start; case service and waiting from `insert_case_service_waiting_time`; key path delays from `filter_paths_performance`. | Shows whether queue pressure is increasing customer delay even when service time falls. |
| **3. Flow pressure** | WIP, queue length, arrivals, completions, arrival-to-capacity ratio, and risk-weighted WIP by 15-minute interval and shift. Use `insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` plus rolling staffing data. | Triggers load balancing before the process enters the high-risk WIP region. |
| **4. Error rate** | Raw and case-mix-adjusted error rate with confidence intervals. Separate error creation, detection, and reporting where possible. | Identifies emerging quality risk without raw worker ranking. Suppress unstable small samples. |
| **5. Seven-day rework** | Percentage of eligible cases with Rework Correction within seven days; median time to rework; repeated-activity rates from `get_rework_cases_per_activity`. Only mature cases should enter the denominator. | Measures downstream quality and avoids mistaking delayed detection for improved quality. |
| **6. Complexity and priority mix** | Mean and distribution of complexity, high-complexity share, high-priority share, and activity-family mix by hour, shift, worker group, and queue state. | Makes selection visible and prevents misleading comparisons across periods or workers. |
| **7. Batching indicators** | Share of events in batches, average and maximum batch size, batch duration per case, sequential-batch frequency, and Quality Check concentration in the final 45 minutes using `discover_batches`. | Distinguishes real efficiency from timestamp compression, delayed checking, and end-of-shift correction bursts. |
| **8. Handover and ownership changes** | Consecutive-case resource changes, cross-shift handoffs, reassignment count, waiting after handoff, and—if available—queue-owner changes. | Detects information loss and carryover created by shift caps, deferrals, or load balancing. |
| **9. Worker-load fairness** | Complexity- and priority-weighted cases per staffed hour, high-WIP exposure, urgent-case share, utilization, and distribution metrics such as Gini or 90/10 ratios within role and shift. | Identifies systematic overloading rather than labeling exposed workers as poor performers. |
| **10. Automation quality** | Bot share of Risk Classification, human correction rate, override rate, downstream rework, model confidence/version, and bot outcomes by WIP and complexity. | Separates automation drift or selection from human performance. |
| **11. Time and recovery context** | Performance by clock hour, minutes since shift start, active minutes, time since break, and shift type. Include staffing and scheduled breaks where available. | Helps distinguish warm-up, fatigue, circadian patterns, and demand-driven pressure. |
| **12. Alert and intervention response** | Time from WIP or risk-alert crossing to management action; action type; post-alert WIP, waiting, service, errors, and rework. | Measures whether alerts produce operational relief rather than merely documenting overload. |

Recommended dashboard rules:

- Use a sustained, risk-weighted WIP alert rather than a single raw WIP reading.
- Display expected versus observed performance from the risk-adjustment model.
- Mark seven-day rework metrics as “immature” until the observation window closes.
 -Provide drill-down from organization  shift  activity  case variant; restrict identifiable worker-level views.
- Never generate an automatic individual penalty alert from raw late-shift errors.
- Use PM4Py formatted logs, temporal distributions, dotted charts, batch discovery, service/waiting metrics, and performance DFGs as the reproducible event-log layer beneath the dashboard.
