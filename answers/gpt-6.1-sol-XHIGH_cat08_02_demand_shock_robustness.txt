## 1. Stress scenarios

“Acceptable” should mean protecting the 18-hour Urgent commitment and 72-hour Routine target, maintaining validated-result quality and traceability, and preventing sustained growth in unfinished work. Set separate, clinically agreed deadlines for Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure orders; none are supplied here. A repeat or recollection must retain the original order’s priority and clock. Report performance by case type and scenario, including tails and breaches.

The observed peak is a mix shock, not simply a volume shock. Weekly registrations increase from 2,300 to 3,300, about 43%. Urgent orders increase from 414 to 957 per week and Complex-panel orders from 299 to 660; Routine orders remain approximately unchanged at 1,403 versus 1,386. Uniformly increasing every station’s staffing would miss this change in resource demand.

Use the following scenarios. The disruption magnitudes are illustrative test settings, not estimates established by the logs.

- **S1 — Sustained winter mix shift:** three weeks at the observed peak volume and mix: 42% Routine, 29% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel, 9% Repeat-after-failure. Equipment and staffing remain nominal. Multiple assays per panel and mandatory medical validation drive disproportionate downstream work.
- **S2 — Assay reliability or availability shock:** peak mix with either M2 losing 25% of available capacity for 48 hours while failures rise, or M1 unavailable for 12 hours. Test these separately: degradation creates repeat work, whereas an outage removes capacity and changes routing.
- **S3 — Validation absence:** peak mix with medical-validator availability reduced by 30% for one day, or the specialist absent on the known alternating Friday. Preserve the distinction between general medical validation and specialist-dependent panels.
- **S4 — Courier compression and delay:** unchanged daily volume, first at normal mix and then at peak mix, with 70% of arrivals compressed into a two-hour window after a six-hour route delay. Urgent orders arrive with less deadline slack; older or compromised samples may generate additional recollections.
- **S5 — Compound disruption:** peak mix, delayed evening courier arrivals, a 12-hour M1 outage, and Friday specialist absence. This tests whether fallback arrangements still work when several resources fail together. It may require partner capacity and explicit service escalation; local scheduling alone cannot guarantee all deadlines.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Interpret DFG counts as recorded transitions, not unique cases or assay workload. Normalize comparisons for eight normal weeks versus three peak weeks. The flattened order log hides tube counts, parallel assays, and panel completion dependencies. The DFGs are also not fully flow-conserving, and observed END counts are below registrations; reconcile extraction rules and incomplete cases before estimating routing probabilities or capacity.

Performance-edge medians include elapsed time between events and may combine transport, waiting, and processing. Do not add them to predict a case’s turnaround or infer service rates from them.

- **S1: intake defects → preparation/assay → technical validation → medical/specialist validation.** Pre-analytical utilization rises to 96%, with a 9.5-hour median queue. Accession correction reaches 91% and a 14-hour queue. Removing these delays feeds preparation, already at an 11-hour queue, and machines at 98% utilization and an 18-hour queue. More assay completions then reach technical validation, whose 101% reported utilization indicates insufficient capacity under the reporting convention. Its peak median queue is 16 hours. Urgent and panel growth subsequently concentrate medical work: Technical validation → Medical validation rises from 8 to 24 hours. Specialist holds increase from about 36 to 203 recorded transitions per week; Hold for specialist review → Medical validation rises from 18 to 48 hours. Extra assay throughput therefore need not produce extra timely releases. Strict Urgent-first dispatch also postpones Routine and non-Urgent panels, increasing their age and unfinished work.

- **S2: machine capacity/reliability → repeats → post-recovery validation.** Machines have little observed peak headroom. Repeats compete with first attempts for the same capacity: Repeat assay → Assay run rises from 10 to 26 hours. Recorded Assay run → Repeat assay transitions rise from 6.2 to 12.7 per 100 registrations, an event-frequency indicator distinct from the supplied overall rework percentage. Routing all Routine work to faster M2 could amplify its failure-associated loop, especially during night shifts. Reliable M1 then becomes the scarce resource for first attempts, clinically necessary repeats, and panels. During an outage validators may temporarily receive less work; a recovery burst can subsequently overwhelm technical and medical validation. Accelerating machine recovery without pacing completions merely moves the queue downstream.

- **S3: medical/specialist gate → held validated results → upstream accumulation.** Medical validators have a 22-hour peak median queue despite 93% utilization; specialist review has a 36-hour queue despite 87%. Resource calendars, bursts, and eligibility restrictions matter alongside average utilization. Complex-panel cases are 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist hold. An evening medical shift cannot resolve a missing specialist’s decisions. Increasing preparation or assay production for these blocked orders can accumulate unreleasable panels and consume capacity needed by releasable work. Eligible Routine orders can still follow the existing Technical validation → Release result route. Outsourcing specialist-dependent panels moves risk to transport, partner accession, partner validation, and local receipt/release.

- **S4: courier/intake burst → pre-analytical/correction → batching/assay → validation burst.** Courier intake already has a 7.5-hour peak median queue. Register order → Receive sample increases from 5 to 8 hours, consuming Urgent slack before internal processing. Compressed arrivals overload screening and correction, then create preparation and machine bursts. Poor samples incur a particularly expensive loop: Request recollection → Receive sample takes 60 hours at peak versus 42 normally. Explaining errors also consumes clerk time. Registration automation removes one delay but exposes screening and assay constraints. Smoothing arrivals helps only if it does not extend pickup-to-receipt time enough to worsen deadlines or sample condition. Moving a courier wave into an unstaffed evening may simply transfer the problem to medical validation.

- **S5: several bottlenecks coexist and change during recovery.** Intake may be congested while machines are unavailable and specialist-dependent results are held. Clearing accession first cannot overcome the machine outage; restoring machines cannot overcome missing validation. Unrestricted backlog clearance creates another surge when resources return. The binding constraint must be reassessed by assay family and validation eligibility, rather than selecting one permanent “system bottleneck.”

These mechanisms match the observed deterioration: Urgent p90 rises from 19 to 33 hours, Routine p90 from 68 to 120 hours, and cases with a queue over 12 hours from 22% to 54%. Even normal Urgent p90 exceeds the contractual threshold, so the normal configuration is not an adequate resilience target.

## 3. Robust redesign package

**A. Introduce end-to-end, workload-aware dispatch instead of unconditional Urgent-first scheduling.** At each decision point, estimate deadline slack as the release deadline minus current time minus a conservative estimate of remaining processing and downstream waiting. Use compatible assay-family slots, bounded urgent interruptions, and ageing protection for Routine and non-Urgent panels. Preserve the original urgency of repeat orders.

Allocate preparation and machine time in workload units, including expected repeats and every required panel assay. Set minimum service allocations for non-Urgent work from scenario testing, and borrow unused allocations when queues are empty. If protecting an imminent Urgent deadline consumes a protected allocation, record the exception and restore service promptly. Priorities cannot guarantee every class simultaneously: under infeasible load, escalate or divert suitable work instead of silently starving another class.

Use downstream workload limits to control starts of stable, deferrable work, while always receiving samples, performing timely defect checks, and respecting sample-stability constraints. A limit should represent estimated work to clear, not a fixed number of flattened orders. Reservation and pacing reduce bursts, but can leave a machine idle when work is incompatible or downstream capacity is unavailable. They can also increase upstream waiting; monitor sample age and deadline slack.

**B. Combine early defect triage with prevention and controlled recollection, adapting I6.** Within two hours of receipt, identify metadata defects, unsuitable samples, and urgent corrections; distinguish repairable accession issues from samples that truly require recollection. Use agreed laboratory acceptance rules and prompt, standardized feedback to the ordering practice. Add complete-metadata checks before pickup where possible.

This protects scarce assay and validation capacity from avoidable work and addresses overall rework increasing from 7.5% to 11.8%. It assumes triage is accurate and can operate during arrival bursts. Faster rejection alone does not shorten the 60-hour peak recollection return. It can overwhelm correction specialists, physician communication, and subsequent courier intake; provide an ownership queue and priority-preserving recollection tracking. Audit rejection decisions to avoid generating unnecessary recollections.

**C. Smooth arrivals and automate complete registrations selectively, combining I7 and I8.** Shift pickup slots within transport and sample-stability limits; pre-register complete digital orders and stagger dispatch around the 10:00 and 17:00 waves. Keep incomplete metadata in an explicit exception route, with one linked order identity across tubes and recollections.

This reduces burst pressure and overtime-related error exposure without relying on longer-than-nine-hour clerk shifts. Its benefit depends on courier flexibility and digital completeness. Automation may deliver a larger burst to pre-analytical checking, correction, and assays; schedule those resources against forecast arrivals and pace deferrable preparation. Do not delay receipt or urgent processing merely to satisfy an internal flow limit.

**D. Use bounded batching and prompt release, modifying I2 and declining I9 as a default.** Batch compatible Routine samples where setup savings are demonstrated, but close a batch at its validated size limit, maximum wait, or earliest order’s latest safe start. A four-hour batch-fill wait is an initial setting to test, not a demonstrated guarantee. Use size 24 only when setup savings exceed the extra waiting and the resulting output fits downstream capacity; otherwise use smaller or partial batches. Reserve compatible urgent opportunities without allowing every urgent arrival to fragment the schedule.

Large batches risk technical-validation bursts and delayed Urgent or panel work. Smaller batches increase setup load and can make machines the bottleneck. Once an order has all required results and approvals, release it promptly. Routine orders should use the existing authorized technical-release route where eligible; Urgent and Complex-panel orders retain required medical validation. Holding Routine results for a batch report spends deadline slack at the last step. Prompt release may expose release/interface workload, so monitor that queue and automate eligible report transmission with acknowledgement and exception handling.

**E. Prepare reversible staffing and reliability responses rather than adding capacity everywhere.** Cross-train the proposed technical validators, but treat I4 as a contingent skill option. Peak technical validation is already at 101%; permanently transferring two validators to correction would trade one bottleneck for a more severe downstream shortage. Deploy them only with demonstrable technical cover and recall them when that cover disappears.

Investigate M2/night-shift repeat clustering by assay, sample type, operator, QC, and maintenance state. Association does not establish that M2 alone causes failures. Choose routing using valid results delivered per occupied machine hour, including repeats and downstream work, rather than runtime alone. Reliability improvement protects several stations, but can create a completion surge into technical validation; coordinate recovery batches and validation coverage.

Validate the package before committing to fixed allocations. Reconstruct order/tube/assay links, service intervals, equipment and staff calendars, and required approvals. Use replay or discrete-event simulation with empirical arrival bursts, parallel panel assays, failure loops, and incomplete cases. Compare the current process, each intervention, and the package under S1–S5, including a recovery period. Assess per-class deadline breaches and tails, rework, maximum backlog, oldest age, and time to drain. Prefer a package that meets agreed limits across scenarios to one with the best pooled mean. The supplied summaries cannot establish exact staffing needs or prove SLA feasibility.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

The following numerical thresholds are initial operating bands to calibrate from the reconstructed log and pilot. Use workload forecasts over the next eight hours and confirm changes every two hours; a daily dashboard alone is too slow for an 18-hour commitment.

- **Winter surge mode, S1 and S5:** activate when forecast required work exceeds 90% of staffed capacity at a critical station, or Urgent release slack enters the warning band below. Protect compatible urgent slots, apply ageing and workload limits, and activate I5 where medical work is accumulating. Schedule the temporary evening medical shift to actual assay completions and courier arrivals. It assumes eligible medical validators are available; it may overload specialist review or release, so verify those paths first. Retire the shift when medical workload is below 85% of available capacity and backlog age is falling for two shifts. End surge mode after two days below the activation bands, including a manageable oldest-case backlog.

- **Machine disruption mode, S2 and S5:** activate on outage, failed laboratory QC, or a case-mix-adjusted repeat rate above the established machine/assay control limit. Replace fixed I3 dedication with selective routing: favor reliable M1 capacity for failure-sensitive or deadline-critical assays, while allowing compatible Routine work on M1 when capacity permits. Do not fill M2 with all displaced Routine work. Route only assays for which M2 remains qualified and effective; consider partner processing or clinically acceptable deferral for the remaining work. Required repeats retain priority, but investigate their cause before another attempt when appropriate. This can overload M1, preparation for alternate methods, or partner intake. Restore ordinary routing only after QC clearance, stable valid-result yield, and a staged backlog-release plan; technical validation must be ready for recovery.

- **Validation absence mode, S3 and S5:** activate when the staffing calendar shows missing required medical/specialist coverage before projected result completion, or workload forecasts show that remaining coverage cannot clear deadline-critical work. Arrange qualified cover, use I5 for general medical work, and pull review-ready specialist cases forward before the known Friday absence where clinically permissible. Do not obtain premature approval or relax mandatory review. Continue releasable Routine work while pacing deferrable panel work, subject to sample stability. This can shift congestion into the covering validator, preparation for early panels, or release. Deactivate after qualified coverage returns and the held-case backlog is demonstrably draining.

- **Selective Complex-panel overflow, S1, S3, or S5:** activate I10 only for technically eligible panels when the predicted local release exceeds their agreed deadline and the partner’s confirmed completion estimate is better. Compare remaining local turnaround with the full transfer pathway: dispatch cutoff, the stated 12-hour transport delay, partner accession/assays/validation, result return, and local release. Confirm partner capacity before transfer and maintain complete sample and result lineage. Twelve hours consumes two-thirds of an Urgent order’s entire 18-hour budget; outsourcing is unsuitable for many already-aged Urgent orders. Prefer suitable non-Urgent panels that free scarce specialist work. Partner intake and local handoff/release become the next constraints. Stop new diversions when local capacity can meet commitments and when partner capacity or transport reliability is inadequate; retain ownership of cases already transferred.

- **Courier surge mode, S4 and S5:** activate when forecast arrivals exceed the staffed intake/checking capacity of the arrival window, a route will be materially late, or oldest received samples threaten the two-hour triage aim. Arrange feasible staggered arrivals, align intake and screening coverage, and process the least-slack clinically eligible orders first. Preserve minimum Routine service after the initial surge. A limited I4 correction deployment is allowed only if forecast technical load remains below 85% after reassignment and no technical queue is threatening deadlines. Recall staff at 90% forecast technical load or adverse slack. Faster intake can overload preparation and assays; couple this mode to downstream pacing. Return to the normal roster after two checks show intake/checking backlog clearing and later stations within their warning bands.

If S5 remains infeasible despite these measures, escalate with an explicit list of orders at risk, confirmed transfer options, and clinically permitted deferrals. Preserve required quality checks and prioritize according to clinical urgency and agreed deadlines. No scheduling rule manufactures missing capacity.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Insufficient effective capacity under compound shocks.** Peak machines and technical validation have virtually no observed headroom. Prolonged outages plus validator absence may exceed both local and contracted partner capacity.
- **Specialist dependence remains concentrated.** A general medical shift cannot substitute for a missing specialist. Panels requiring unavailable expertise remain exposed unless qualified cover or an eligible partner is secured.
- **Redesign-induced waiting and fragmentation.** Reservations, workload limits and small urgent batches can reduce utilization or increase setup time. Conversely, larger Routine batches can create validation bursts. Stable samples may wait longer upstream under pacing; ageing and stability limits must override that pacing where necessary.
- **Redesign-induced transfer and rejection risks.** Early triage may create false recollections; outsourcing adds transport, identity reconciliation, and responsibility handoffs. Faster local queues would conceal harm if these exceptions were excluded.
- **Uncertain workload and clock definitions.** Flattened orders conceal panel joins and multiple tubes, and incomplete windows may bias turnaround statistics. Repeat labels may obscure original urgency unless retained separately. Establish these fields before relying on forecasting.
- **Demand and sample quality can deteriorate together.** Courier delay, fatigue and night-shift failures may be correlated. A model that varies each independently will understate risk. Test their combinations and the recovery surge.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Use six leading-indicator groups, refreshed at least every two hours and reviewed daily during peaks. Thresholds below are provisional operating settings; laboratory QC and sample-stability limits take precedence.

| Leading indicator | Warning / escalation bands | Decision supported |
|---|---|---|
| **Required work versus available capacity**, next 8 hours, by assay family and constrained resource; include repeat work and actual staff calendars | Amber at 90–95%; red above 95% with growing backlog, or above 100% outright | Activate surge mode; constrain deferrable starts; identify the current constraint |
| **Release slack, oldest age and unfinished work by case type**, including protected-allocation exceptions | Urgent amber at ≤4 hours slack, red at ≤2 hours, overdue when negative; Routine amber at ≤12 hours and red at ≤6 hours. Use agreed panel/repeat deadlines. Flag growing backlog across two checks | Expedite feasible cases; restore aged Routine service; divert or escalate predicted breaches |
| **Courier/receipt and early-triage pressure** | Amber when forecast arrivals exceed staffed window capacity; red when oldest untriaged receipt exceeds the two-hour aim or transport threatens remaining slack/sample stability | Stagger feasible arrivals; align intake/checking coverage; flag urgent delayed samples |
| **Quality-loop pressure**, by defect cause, machine, assay and shift; track outstanding recollections and expected repeat workload | Amber above baseline statistical control limits; red for failed QC or repeated adverse signals with increasing repeat backlog. Overall rework near the observed 11.8% peak level warrants investigation | Investigate causes; change qualified routing; prevent repeated avoidable attempts |
| **Validation coverage and ready-result workload**, separately for technical, medical and specialist review | Amber when required approval will fall outside planned coverage or forecast load exceeds 90%; red when projected queue clearance exceeds a case’s latest safe validation time | Add targeted evening cover; arrange specialist cover; consider eligible panel overflow |
| **Transfer/release handoff integrity** | Amber when partner estimates leave little deadline slack or approved results remain unreleased beyond the agreed short release limit; red for an unacknowledged transfer, identity mismatch, or overdue promised partner result | Stop further diversion when necessary; resolve exceptions and ensure approved results reach the requester |

Keep machine availability and protected-service violations visible within these groups. Queue medians alone are insufficient: show workload to clear, oldest age, and eligible versus blocked work.

**Lagging outcomes**, reported separately, are Urgent releases over 18 hours, Routine releases over 72 hours, per-class p90/p95 turnaround, Complex-panel/repeat deadline breaches, final rework, cancellations, and cases experiencing a queue over 12 hours. Use registration cohorts with follow-up or explicit censoring and include outstanding overdue orders; do not assess success only among completed orders. The redesign is acceptable only if scenario-specific outcomes improve while quality remains controlled and unfinished work drains after the shock.
