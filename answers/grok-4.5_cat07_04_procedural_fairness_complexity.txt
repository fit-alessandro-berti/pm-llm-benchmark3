# 1. Fairness dimensions

**Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ sharply (A 78%, B 63%, C 69%, D 58%). Appeal and reopen/rework rates are also higher for B, C, and D. Raw parity is not expected given different case mix; it is a starting signal, not proof of unfairness.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month, residual gaps remain: approval 5.5 pp (B), 2.2 pp (C), 3.1 pp (D); cycle time +2.6 d (B), +4.1 d (C), +0.9 d (D); elevated supervisor review, document requests, and appeals especially for B and D. These residuals are smaller than raw gaps but not zero, so complexity controls do not fully explain outcome or process differences.

**Procedural burden.** Groups B and C are funneled into longer variants (V2, V4, V5) with extra steps (Language Support, Accommodation Review, Request Docs) and more handovers. Rights-preserving steps are legitimate in purpose but increase path length, handovers, and waiting even after mix adjustment. Group D is routed heavily into Fraud Review (V3), consistent with higher risk scores but still burdensome.

**Timeliness.** Median and p90 cycle times are substantially worse for B and C. Waiting-time table shows concentrated delays at Language Support  Eligibility (3.8 d), Accommodation Review  Eligibility (4.7 d), Completeness  Request Docs for B (1.6 d), and Supervisor  Decision for B/C. In-person channel and specialized queues drive delay. Group D’s cycle-time residual is smaller; much of its raw delay is risk-related routing.

**Consistency of routing.** Same nominal triggers (language need, accommodation, high risk) should produce comparable paths, but resource concentration and regional backlog create inconsistent experience: B cases often general  bilingual  general; C cases general  specialist  supervisor. Two regions show extra handovers and supervisor backlog. Routing rules appear policy-consistent at a high level but operationally uneven.

**Quality/accessibility of support.** Language support depends on only 7 bilingual caseworkers; accommodation on 5 specialists (two also doing supervisor review). Capacity scarcity can mean delayed contact, thinner interaction quality, and weaker explanation of decisions—none of which is captured in case_outcome. Policy labels these steps rights-preserving, yet SLAs do not protect their timeliness.

**Appeal/rework burden.** Higher appeal rates (B 13%, D 16%) and reopen/rework (B 22%, C 24%, D 20%) vs A (6% / 11%) impose extra process load on applicants who already face longer paths. Adjusted appeal probability remains elevated for B (+4.4 pp) and D (+5.7 pp). Appeals may reflect genuine complexity, poorer first-round explanation, or distrust; the log does not separate these.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** A is spread across 52 caseworkers; B and C are concentrated on small specialist pools; D feeds fraud queue. Concentration creates queueing externalities borne by the groups that need support. Workload is only daily-granularity, so true overload and quality dilution are under-measured.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Goal: separate (i) disparity (observed difference), (ii) legitimate routing (policy-required given attributes), (iii) residual disparity that may indicate discrimination or structural unfairness, and (iv) uncertainty from biased or incomplete covariates.

**Matching or stratification variables.**  
Stratify or match on: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month, disability_accommodation_requested, language_preference. Within strata, compare approval, cycle time, path length, number of handovers, supervisor/fraud/support step incidence, and appeal/rework. Prefer exact matching on binary needs (language, accommodation) plus coarsened exact matching or inverse-probability weighting on continuous/ordinal factors. Report stratum-specific and pooled residual effects.

**Path/variant comparison.**  
Compare variant distributions (V1–V6 and long-tail) within matched strata. Ask: conditional on language need, is V4 timing and handover count similar across regions? Conditional on accommodation request, is V5 supervisor rate and wait similar? Conditional on high risk, is V3 fraud and supervisor intensity comparable for D vs others? Use DFG/performance DFG per group within strata; test whether “extra” loops (rework, repeated doc request) differ after conditioning.

**Resource-assignment comparison.**  
Build handover-of-work and working-together networks by group and region. Measure: concentration (Gini/HHI of caseworkers per group), specialist share, mean handovers per case, ping-pong (ABA) rates. Compare assignment of similar cases (same risk, docs, channel) to overloaded vs generalist pools. Discover organizational roles and activity-based resource similarity to see whether B/C work is siloed.

**Waiting-time decomposition.**  
Insert service and waiting times; decompose total cycle time into: queue before first touch, wait at each arc (especially support and supervisor arcs), service time per activity, and rework loops. Attribute wait to capacity (specialist queue), routing (extra steps), region backlog, or channel (in-person). Compare decompositions within matched strata so that “B waits longer because of language step” is separated from “B waits longer *on the same step* than a similarly complex A case.”

**Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.**  
Treat risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as potentially post-treatment or historically biased.  
- Re-estimate residuals omitting risk_score_band; omitting documentation_completeness; and with both omitted.  
- Bound analysis: if risk scores systematically inflate for B/D, how large must bias be to eliminate residual approval/appeal gaps?  
- Use language_preference and channel as partial proxies for barriers that affect “completeness.”  
- Check whether documentation incompleteness mediates language/channel effects (suggesting measurement of barrier, not only applicant fault).

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.**  
Interact group with region and channel. Identify whether residual gaps concentrate in the two backlog regions or in in-person channel. If disparities vanish in well-resourced regions/online channel, the problem is operational capacity/routing, not only case mix. If gaps persist everywhere, deeper rule or decision bias is more plausible.

**Inferential stance.**  
- Difference explained by policy-required steps + neutral controls  legitimate routing (still monitor burden).  
- Residual difference on timeliness/handovers for rights-preserving paths  structural procedural unfairness even without intent.  
- Residual approval/appeal gaps after rich controls and sensitivity  concerning, but not automatic proof of discrimination; needs decision-level audit.  
- Wide bounds under covariate bias  indeterminate pending better measures.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Most important procedural disparities**

1. **Support-step bottlenecks (B and C).** V4 (language) is 24% of B vs 1% of A; median 19.2 d. V5 (accommodation) is 31% of C vs 2% of A; median 21.6 d. Waits: Language Support  Eligibility 3.8 d; Accommodation  Eligibility 4.7 d. Only 7 bilingual and 5 accommodation specialists; two specialists also do supervisor review. Adjusted cycle-time gaps (+2.6 d B, +4.1 d C) align with these queues.

2. **Document-request and completeness path (B, partly C).** V2 share higher for B (34%); Completeness  Request Docs wait 1.6 d for B vs 0.4 d A. Adjusted P(Request Missing Documents) +9.5 pp for B. Incomplete documentation share is high for B (46%), partly entangled with language/channel barriers.

3. **Fraud/supervisor intensity (D, partly B).** V3 is 28% of D vs 9% of A; high risk share 51% for D. Adjusted supervisor review +8 pp B, +5.5 pp C, +2 pp D; fraud routing for D is largely risk-aligned. Supervisor  Decision waits longer for B/C (1.9 / 1.6 d), tied to regional backlog and dual-role specialists.

4. **Handover patterns.** B: general  bilingual  general in two regions. C: general  accommodation specialist  supervisor. More handovers increase delay, information loss, and applicant burden without necessarily improving decision quality.

5. **Appeal-heavy tails.** V6 and adjusted appeal elevation for B and D add long cycle times (median 30.7 d for V6) on top of already slower first-round paths.

**What may be justified by complexity**  
- Higher fraud review for D given higher risk_score_band (with caveat that risk may be biased).  
- Presence (not necessarily duration) of Language Support and Accommodation Review when requested—these are rights-preserving.  
- Some extra document requests when documentation_completeness is truly lower.  
- Somewhat higher supervisor review when risk, accommodation, or fraud flags are present.

**What remains concerning**  
- Magnitude of waits on rights-preserving steps and lack of SLA protection for those steps.  
- Extreme resource concentration (7 / 5 specialists) producing group-specific queueing.  
- Extra handovers and regional inconsistency not required by policy.  
- Residual adjusted gaps in approval, cycle time, doc requests, supervisor review, and appeals after controls.  
- Higher rework/appeal burden that may stem from thin staffing and weaker first-round process quality rather than only case difficulty.  
- Treating support steps as “the applicant’s complexity” when delay is largely capacity- and design-driven.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate risk- and rights-based routing.** High risk_score_band triggers Fraud Review; language and disability flags trigger support steps; incomplete files trigger Request Missing Documents. This mechanism explains much of V3/V4/V5 incidence and part of raw cycle-time gaps.

2. **Capacity scarcity on protected paths (concerning structure).** Fixed small pools of bilingual and accommodation staff create long specialized queues. Applicants who exercise rights experience slower service *because* they exercise rights—an organizational, not individual, failure. Dual-role specialists (accommodation + supervisor) couple support delay to decision delay for C.

3. **Measurement and barrier confounding.** Language and channel barriers reduce measured documentation_completeness and increase in-person share, which then “justifies” longer paths and lower approvals. Risk scores may embed historical enforcement or demographic correlates, over-routing D (and partly B) into fraud. Controls then under-adjust or over-adjust depending on interpretation.

4. **Handover and regional backlog friction.** Split responsibility (generalist vs specialist) and backlog in two regions add waits and inconsistent supervisor intensity. Same case type gets different timeliness by region/resource draw—procedural inconsistency.

5. **Appeal and rework feedback.** Slower, more fragmented first-round process and weaker explanations increase appeals and reopens, which further load specialist and supervisor queues—amplifying disparities (especially B and D).

6. **Possible residual decision-level disparity (uncertain).** After routing and mix, small residual approval gaps could reflect unmeasured need, biased risk/docs, or differential scrutiny/discretion. Evidence is insufficient to confirm intentional discrimination but sufficient to require decision audits.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias.** risk_score_band may encode historical disparities, differential policing, or correlated demographics rather than only current fraud risk; using it as a neutral control can “explain away” unfair routing. documentation_completeness may reflect language access, channel usability, and staff help at intake—not only applicant diligence—so it is partly endogenous to the process’s accessibility.

**Missing applicant needs.** No direct measures of explanation quality, digital literacy, trust, transportation cost of in-person steps, severity of disability beyond a binary flag, or intersectional need (e.g., language *and* accommodation). Dignity of treatment and whether support was effective are unobserved.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Assignment is visible, but workload only at daily granularity. True multitasking, case difficulty per worker, time-of-day queues, and quality dilution under overload are not seen. Handover networks show structure, not whether information was lost.

**Appeal quality.** appeal_flag and V6 show incidence, not whether appeals overturn errors, whether reasons were understandable, or whether some groups under-appeal due to access barriers (which would bias appeal rates as fairness metrics).

**Historical bias and feedback.** Prior_case_count and risk scores can reflect past process outcomes. Groups with more past contact may be scored riskier and scrutinized more—locking in disparity. case_outcome is binary approval-centric; partial awards, conditions, and delayed activation effects are thin.

**Counterfactual opacity.** We lack experimental or strong quasi-experimental variation in specialist capacity and routing rules, so residual gaps remain partly indeterminate between discrimination, unmeasured complexity, and structural delay.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**1. Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.**  
Hire/train more bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; reduce dual-role conflict.  
*Side effects:* cost; ramp-up time; if standards differ across new staff, consistency risk; must avoid creating a permanent segregated track with lower approval norms.

**2. Service-level targets for support steps.**  
Explicit SLAs on Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and waits into Eligibility (e.g., median and p90 caps), reported by group and region.  
*Side effects:* gaming (shallow contacts to hit SLA); pressure to rush complex accommodation; need quality metrics alongside speed.

**3. Routing redesign to reduce handovers.**  
Case ownership models: bilingual or accommodation-capable owners who carry Eligibility/Decision where possible; warm handoff protocols; co-location or joint queues in high-B/C regions.  
*Side effects:* harder scheduling; specialists may become generalist bottlenecks; training cost; risk of under-using deep expertise if over-flattened.

**4. Decision-explanation improvements.**  
Standardized plain-language and translated decision rationales; teach-back at notification; clearer document request lists.  
*Side effects:* more staff time per decision; may increase cycle time slightly while reducing appeals/rework; requires monitoring whether explanations are substantive, not boilerplate.

**5. Monitoring adjusted disparities.**  
Ongoing dashboards: residual approval, cycle time, waits by step, handovers, appeals, rework—stratified and adjusted as in §2; region × group alerts.  
*Side effects:* metric fixation; disputes over control sets; privacy/small-n risk in thin strata; need governance so monitors drive fixes not blame.

**6. Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.**  
Fairness and calibration audit of risk model by group; reconstruct whether completeness flags correlate with language/channel after same checklist; remediate scoring features that proxy protected need; improve intake assistance before completeness fails.  
*Side effects:* if risk tool is weakened without replacement, fraud detection may drop; political resistance; temporary instability in routing volumes.

**Additional complementary options.**  
Regional backlog surge support; separate supervisor capacity from accommodation specialists; applicant-facing time estimates for support paths; prohibit counting protected-step wait against applicants in any informal “difficulty” narrative used in decisions.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **not fully procedurally fair**, but the evidence supports a **calibrated, structural finding** rather than a blanket claim of intentional discrimination or simple outcome bias.

Raw approval and timeliness gaps are partly explained by legitimate case-mix and policy-required routing (risk-based fraud review; language and accommodation steps; incomplete documentation). Adjusted comparisons, however, still show residual disadvantages—especially longer cycle times and higher procedural intensity for Groups B and C, and elevated appeal burden for B and D. The strongest fairness concern is **not equalized approval rates**; it is that **rights-preserving support steps are operationally under-resourced**, unprotected by SLAs, and embedded in handover-heavy paths, so applicants who need language or disability support systematically experience more waiting, more fragmentation, and more burden. Resource concentration (7 bilingual workers, 5 accommodation specialists) and regional supervisor backlog make these burdens group-patterned rather than case-idiosyncratic.

Group D’s heavier fraud path is more consistent with measured risk, but because risk_score_band may embed historical bias, that justification is only provisional pending audit. Residual approval differences after controls are **modest and indeterminate** as to discrimination: they could reflect unmeasured complexity, biased covariates, differential scrutiny, or explanation quality.

**Summary judgment:** procedurally **unfair in structural burden and timeliness for support-dependent groups (B and C)**; **partly justified but audit-dependent for risk-routed group (D)**; **outcome disparities partially explained but not fully neutralized**. Confidence is moderate given covariate endogeneity and missing quality/dignity measures. Priority remedies are capacity and SLA protection for support steps, handover-reducing ownership, region backlog relief, and audits of risk and completeness—not removal of support rights and not raw-rate equalization alone.
