## 3. Identification strategy
| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staggered DiD (Callaway & Sant'Anna) | Group-time indicator: 1 if branch adopted by month t | Never-treated (Delta, Epsilon) + not-yet-treated (Gamma pre-Jul) | `arrivals`, `fte`, `high_complexity_pct`, month FE | ATT_avail, ATT_median, ATT_tail | Conditional parallel trends, no anticipation, stable unit treatment value (SUTVA) | Pre-trend equality test, placebo rollout dates, coefficient stability across windows | Small number of clusters (N=5) invalidates asymptotic inference; heterogeneous timing biases TWFE |
| Synthetic Control | Weighted combination of Delta, Epsilon, and pre-treatment Gamma | Custom synthetic counterpart per treated branch | Pre-rollout trajectory matching on cycle time, arrivals, complexity | ATT_avail (branch-specific) | Donor pool spans counterfactual space, no post-treatment shocks unique to treated | Mean squared prediction error (MSPE) in pre-period, jackknife leave-one-out, residual plot | Poor fit if donor branches diverge structurally; sensitive to single donor dominance |
| Treatment-Intensity IV/Regression | Continuous `automated_fast_path_pct` as regressor | All branches (pooled) | Branch FE, month FE, `arrivals`, `fte`, `high_complexity_pct` | ATT_intensity | Monotonicity, exclusion restriction (if IV used), no unmeasured intensity confounders | First-stage strength, overidentification test (if IV), residual autocorrelation | Endogenous learning curve correlates with unobserved manager capability; intensity is a post-treatment variable |

The staggered DiD approach is preferable because it explicitly models group-time treatment variation, avoiding the negative-weight bias that plagues conventional two-way fixed effects when treatment effects are heterogeneous over time. It allows dynamic effect tracing (learning curve, tail amplification) and cleanly separates pre-treatment periods for parallel-trend validation. Given the small branch count, inference must rely on wild bootstrap or permutation tests rather than asymptotic standard errors. The design also accommodates covariate adjustment for arrivals, FTEs, and complexity, which is critical here because routing and product changes directly alter workload composition. While synthetic control offers intuitive counterfactuals, it struggles with staggered timing and multiple treated units. Treatment-intensity regression is useful for dose-response mapping but risks post-treatment bias if intensity is endogenous to unobserved branch capability.

The scale-up decision rule: expand to Epsilon and remaining branches only if (1) median cycle time falls 5% relative to the internal manual control, (2) p90 cycle time remains within ±5% of baseline, and (3) escalation rate does not exceed specialist capacity thresholds. This design converts rollout into a learning system, preserving operational continuity while generating defensible causal evidence.

## 10. Final claim ladder
- `Observed:` Automation availability coincides with a 2–3 percentage point increase in specialist escalation and a widening gap between median and p90 cycle times in high-volume branches.
- `Suggestive:` Higher automated fast-path usage correlates with modest median cycle-time reductions, but tail delays and rework show no consistent improvement, likely due to concurrent complexity surges and staffing shifts.
- `Conditionally causal:` If specialist queue capacity is held constant and case-mix is stabilized, automation plausibly reduces median cycle time by 1–2 hours when fast-path adoption exceeds 40%, based on intensity-adjusted contrasts and Gamma’s trajectory.
- `Not supported:` A uniform claim that “automation reduces cycle time by X%” or improves customer satisfaction is not defensible given endogenous rollout, unmeasured queue spillovers, survey nonresponse bias, and divergent median-versus-tail behavior.
