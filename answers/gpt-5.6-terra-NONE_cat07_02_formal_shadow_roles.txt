# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

## R07_Giorgio: commercial shadow and senior front-office hybrid

Giorgio is the clearest commercial shadow role.

- His formal position is Senior Service Agent in Front Office, with entitlement and pricing certifications.
- His routine profile overlaps with Amira and Ben: registration, validation, standard response, and closure remain substantial.
- However, he also performed 72 pricing reviews and 88 customer negotiations, far more commercial work than ordinary front-office agents.
- During Fatima’s six-day absence, he carried 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations.

This is beneficial because it provides continuity during absence, gives customers a familiar front-office contact, and prevents a single-point failure in commercial exception flow.

The risk is that substitution can conceal a capacity or competency gap. Pricing-exception duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes while Giorgio substituted. This does not mean he was unsuitable; it may reflect lower experience, the complexity of cases deferred to him, concurrent front-office obligations, or more consultation requirements. Management should treat him as a documented backup with guardrails, commercial mentoring, and a clear threshold for escalation to Fatima or Hana.

## R13_Mateo: cross-functional escalation broker

Mateo is a bridging role across technical, commercial, security, quality, and supervisory streams.

Evidence includes:

- Broad but non-dominant participation across nearly every activity.
- Strong involvement in reopened cases: 42 A13 events.
- Escalation-related work: 44 A9 events, 16 A6-related contacts, 10 A7, 28 A8.
- High-risk case mix: median complexity 0.77 and 76 critical cases.
- Sequence evidence: Reopen  Mateo coordinate  relevant specialist  Hana approval or close.
- During the security surge, he filtered low-risk security referrals, reducing false-escalation complaints.
- During Hana’s absence, he performed 21 preliminary approvals, but 13 required later recheck.

Mateo should not be classified simply as a supervisor, technical analyst, security analyst, or generalist. He appears to be an escalation broker who reduces coordination costs and determines the correct next specialist or decision path.

Benefits include reduced unnecessary referrals, faster cross-functional routing, and better continuity in reopened or exceptional cases. Risks include bottleneck creation, ambiguous accountability, informal authority expansion, and delayed urgent cases. The two critical security cases waiting more than two days show that referral filtering needs explicit urgency and override rules.

## R05_Erik: rare but critical specialist

Erik is a shadow-like role only in the sense that his organizational importance is not proportional to his event frequency. He is more accurately a rare, high-consequence specialist.

- A6 Security Review is strongly concentrated in Erik: 96 events.
- His median complexity is 0.86, the highest human value in the table.
- He handled 55 critical cases despite comparatively low overall event counts.
- His profile overlaps weakly with Mateo (0.39), which is expected: Mateo routes and coordinates, while Erik provides substantive security expertise.

Erik’s role should be protected from frequency-based utilization management. A low raw event count may be entirely appropriate if each event has high risk, requires deep expertise, and prevents serious loss. During the Enterprise Security surge, Mateo’s filtering reduced false escalations, which helped Erik focus. However, two critical cases still waited more than two days, demonstrating that reduced noise must not suppress urgent escalation.

The desired design is not “send every security concern to Erik” or “filter aggressively.” It is risk-tiered referral with explicit service-level targets, critical-case override rules, and backup security coverage.

## R08_Hana and R13_Mateo: authority bridge, not interchangeable supervisors

Hana and Mateo both touch escalations and approvals, but they have different roles:

- Hana has formal supervisory authority and is the accountable approver.
- Mateo can prepare, triage, coordinate, and perhaps make bounded preliminary decisions.
- The recheck of 13 of 21 preliminary approvals is direct evidence that Mateo’s temporary work did not carry identical authority.

This is a healthy bridge when documented. It becomes risky if the system cannot distinguish preliminary review from final approval.

## R09_Ivan and R10_Julia: quality gatekeepers and knowledge bridge

Ivan and Julia bridge operational delivery and organizational learning.

- They perform quality checks after technical implementation.
- They update the knowledge base, likely translating recurrent defects, fixes, and quality findings into reusable guidance.
- Their technical touches should be interpreted carefully: they may test, verify, or correct quality issues rather than own engineering decisions.

Their benefit is consistent quality control and feedback into process improvement. Their risk is being misclassified as spare technical capacity and reassigned away from quality work, weakening the control loop.

## R14_Nora: automation boundary role

Nora is a boundary role between customer intake and human case handling.

Automation improves speed and consistency for standard requests, but it should not erase human accountability for exceptions. The reopen evidence involving temporary-agent entitlement notes suggests the organization should distinguish:

1. automated precheck,
2. human validation or note completion where required,
3. downstream closure authority.

Nora’s overlap with front-office activity labels should therefore not be interpreted as interchangeable human role membership.

import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load data

# The source may be CSV, Parquet, database output, or an existing DataFrame.
df = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution_event_log.csv")

log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"

# Preserve/confirm useful analysis fields.
required_extra = [
    "case_complexity",
    "customer_segment",
    "product_line",
    "temporary_substitution",
    "escalation_level",
    "org:resource"
]
missing = [c for c in required_extra if c not in log_df.columns]
if missing:
    raise ValueError(f"Missing required analysis columns: {missing}")

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
activity_resource_counts = pd.crosstab(
    log_df["org:resource"],
    log_df["concept:name"]
)

resource_case_complexity = (
    log_df.groupby("org:resource")["case_complexity"]
    .agg(
        events="size",
        median_complexity="median",
        mean_complexity="mean",

    )
    .reset_index()
)

resource_escalation = (

# Convert role output to a reviewable table.
# Adjust this adapter to the installed PM4Py version's return schema.
def roles_to_dataframe(roles_result):
    rows = []

    # Illustrative handling for common list-like role outputs.
    if isinstance(roles_result, list):
        for i, role in enumerate(roles_result, start=1):
            # Depending on version, role may be dict-like or object-like.
            if isinstance(role, dict):
                members = role.get("resources", role.get("members", []))
                activities = role.get("activities", role.get("activity_set", []))
                role_name = role.get("role", role.get("name", f"role_{i}"))
            else:
                members = getattr(role, "resources", getattr(role, "members", []))
                activities = getattr(
                    role, "activities", getattr(role, "activity_set", [])
                )
                role_name = getattr(role, "name", f"role_{i}")

            for resource in members:
                rows.append({
                    "discovered_role": role_name,
                    "org:resource": resource,
                    "role_activities": list(activities)
                })

    elif isinstance(roles_result, dict):
        # Alternative generic interpretation: {role_name: role_definition}
        for role_name, role_def in roles_result.items():
            if isinstance(role_def, dict):
                members = role_def.get("resources", role_def.get("members", []))
                activities = role_def.get("activities", [])
            else:
                members = role_def
                activities = []

            for resource in members:
                rows.append({
                    "discovered_role": role_name,
                    "org:resource": resource,
                    "role_activities": list(activities)
                })

    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

A practical analysis sequence is:

1. Run role discovery and similarity to obtain behavioral candidate groups.
2. Profile each resource by activity, complexity, escalation level, reopen involvement, and substitution status.
3. Inspect handover and working-together networks to identify routing, approval, and quality-gate positions.
4. Compare normal periods with absence, surge, and temporary-substitution periods.
5. Validate proposed roles against formal title, certification, policy delegation, audit records, and outcome quality.
6. Preserve hybrid and shadow-role labels where evidence indicates legitimate cross-functional work.

# 6. Operational interpretation

Management should use the discovered structure as an operational design model, not as an automatic reorganization chart.

## Staffing

The routine staffing model should recognize at least five distinct capacity pools:

1. **Automated intake capacity**: Nora handles predictable A1/A2 work, but automation exceptions still require human accountability.
2. **Front-office capacity**: R01/R02 handle routine resolution; R11/R12 provide bounded seasonal capacity.
3. **Technical engineering capacity**: R03/R04 handle diagnosis, configuration, and implementation.
4. **Specialist control capacity**: Erik, Fatima, Hana, and quality analysts provide security, commercial, approval, and quality controls.
5. **Escalation coordination capacity**: Mateo prevents complex/reopened cases from becoming unowned or repeatedly misrouted.

Staffing should not be based on overall event volume alone. Erik may have lower volume than front-office staff but should not be treated as underutilized, because his work concentrates on high-consequence security cases. Likewise, Mateo’s broad and moderate-volume profile may be essential because it reduces coordination delay and unnecessary escalations.

## Training and succession

A tiered training model is appropriate:

- **Temps R11/R12** should receive targeted entitlement-note and validation-quality coaching before expanded closure autonomy. The reopen evidence indicates a training and control issue, not merely an individual-performance issue.
- **R01/R02** can be developed as differentiated front-office tiers. Amira may be a candidate for more complex-case support if quality and decision outcomes support it.
- **R04 Dina** can be developed toward Chen’s complex technical capability through supervised high-complexity assignments, security-adjacent exposure, and outcome monitoring.
- **R07 Giorgio** should have explicit commercial-shadow training, pricing policy refreshers, and documented authority thresholds. His substitution performance suggests real value, while the longer pricing duration suggests a need for additional support or protected backup time.
- **R13 Mateo** needs clear escalation, triage, and preliminary-approval rules so that coordination work remains effective without silently expanding unreviewed authority.
- **Security succession** is especially important. Erik’s concentration of critical security work creates a resilience risk unless there is trained backup coverage, documented playbooks, and urgency-based escalation protocols.

## Risk control

The main control principle is to separate execution, specialist review, coordination, and final approval where appropriate.

- Keep **Erik’s security review** distinct from technical execution when policy requires independent risk review.
- Keep **Hana’s final approval** distinct from Mateo’s coordination or preliminary assessment.
- Preserve **quality checks** by Ivan and Julia as an independent gate rather than treating them as expendable technical capacity.
- Implement explicit **critical-security escalation SLAs**, including a bypass or immediate-alert path when filtering could delay a critical case.
- Track **reopen rates by resource, activity path, complexity, and temporary-substitution status**, especially for closure after validation.
- Distinguish in the log between **recommendation**, **preliminary approval**, **final approval**, and **recheck**. Otherwise process mining will incorrectly infer that all A9 events have the same authority.

## Resilience and continuity

The evidence suggests several single-point or near-single-point dependencies:

- Fatima for commercial exceptions.
- Erik for security review.
- Hana for final approvals.
- Mateo for complex escalation coordination.
- Possibly Chen for high-complexity technical and security-adjacent engineering.

Recommended resilience measures include:

- Maintain documented backup rosters, not just informal substitutes.
- Use controlled shadowing and simulation exercises for pricing, security, and approval paths.
- Reserve protected time for backups to substitute; otherwise Giorgio’s front-office workload may slow pricing work.
- Build surge plans for Enterprise Security cases, including critical-case triage rules and a secondary security-review path.
- Use quality and knowledge-base outputs to convert repeated reopen causes into training, automation, or checklist improvements.

## Cautions about changing staffing based only on mined roles

1. **A mined activity cluster is not a formal authority model.**  
   If Hana and Mateo cluster together because both perform approval-related events, it does not follow that Mateo can permanently replace Hana. The recheck evidence shows that apparently similar activity labels may encode different authority levels.

2. **Temporary substitution is not proof of permanent competence or capacity.**  
   Giorgio’s commercial substitution and the temporary agents’ routine closure work demonstrate useful flexibility, but the longer pricing duration and entitlement-note-related reopens show why substitution must be interpreted with quality, complexity, supervision, and workload context.

3. **Low-frequency work may be strategically critical.**  
   Reducing specialist staffing because Erik’s event count is lower than front-office counts would be unsafe. Criticality, downstream risk, and waiting-time consequences matter.

4. **Similarity does not imply interchangeability.**  
   Chen and Dina are highly similar in activity profile, yet Chen handles substantially more high/critical work. R01/R02 and R11/R12 similarly overlap on routine activity but differ in tenure, certification, contract status, and error exposure.

The recommended outcome is a deliberately layered organization: routine work is standardized and automated where safe; technical, commercial, security, quality, and approval controls retain explicit ownership; and shadow or bridging roles are formally recognized, trained, measured, and bounded rather than left invisible.
