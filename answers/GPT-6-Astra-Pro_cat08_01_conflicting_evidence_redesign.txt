## 1. Current-process diagnosis

**The objective should be timely, correct resolution—not simply a fast first approval or first close.** The current process combines a commercially attractive replacement route with expensive downstream failures, while reviewed routes trade substantial delay for better observed quality. Neither “automate everything” nor “review everything” is supported by the evidence.

**The fast-path advantage is real, but incomplete.** The frequency DFG shows substantial traffic through `Intake claim → Auto eligibility screen` and `Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval`: 3,600 and 2,550 transitions respectively. However, fast-approval cases have a 14.1% complaint/reopen rate, compared with 3.3% for cases using the quoted-repair sequence. Their reported cost advantage falls from 31% before rework to 9% after complaints, reshipments, and service credits. Thus, the supplied accounting still favors fast approval on cost, but by much less than the initial handling-cost comparison suggests. This does not establish a complete risk-adjusted cost advantage.  

**The appropriate trade-off differs sharply by cohort.**

| Reported cohort             | Median hours: fast / manual or reviewed route | Complaint/reopen: fast / manual or reviewed route | Redesign implication                                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------------: | ------------------------------------------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Premium low-risk accessory  |                                        8 / 46 |                                           6% / 3% | Blanket manual review threatens the 24-hour service-credit exposure point. Preserve speed while targeting specific failure mechanisms. |
| Standard low-risk accessory |                                       13 / 52 |                                           8% / 4% | There is more room for assurance, provided completion and status visibility remain consistent with the stated 72-hour tolerance.       |
| Premium high-value device   |                                       20 / 96 |                                          22% / 5% | The quality gap is large, but simply moving everyone into the existing slow reviewed route is commercially unattractive.               |
| Standard high-value device  |                                      28 / 120 |                                          18% / 6% | Avoiding wrong replacements has substantial value, but the reviewed route still needs waiting-time reduction.                          |

These are observed route associations, not randomized treatment effects. Product difficulty, evidence completeness, customer behavior, and routing selection may explain part of the differences. Mid-risk devices have no separately reported outcomes, so their optimal routing remains unresolved. 

**The performance DFG identifies several different delay mechanisms—not one universal bottleneck.** The evidence-return edge has a mean of 34 hours and a p90 of 88 hours; quote acceptance has a mean of 30 hours and a p90 of 80 hours; complaint-to-manual-review takes another 40 hours on average. These intervals suggest opportunities in evidence coordination, customer decision support, and recovery handling. They are inter-event elapsed times, however, not measured labor or pure queueing times. Their medians and p90s cannot be added to obtain a valid path-level median or p90. 

The temporal-profile findings provide more specific explanations: the manual-review-to-quote transition is often too slow for Premium cases, while the long-tail dispatch-to-complaint pattern is linked to incorrect replacement selection. The resource evidence also attributes quote delay mainly to part-price confirmation, suggesting that adding technicians alone may not address that delay.  

**Compliance must be treated as a constraint, with the rules interpreted precisely.**

* **R1:** A claim **above EUR 400** needs either manual eligibility review or fraud/risk review before fast approval. “High-value device” should not replace the actual monetary test, and the rule does not require both reviews.
* **R2:** Evidence completeness must be confirmed before replacement dispatch, except for the specified **Premium low-risk accessory** exception. That exception does not waive R1.
* **R3:** Following a reopen, manual eligibility review must occur before the subsequent final close or denial. A review conducted before the reopen is insufficient. 

The replay fitness of 0.91 is not a 91% policy-compliance rate; only 68% of traces fit the supplied normative net. Alignment findings require semantic validation as well: approval before evidence completeness is not, by itself, proof of violating R2, which explicitly constrains dispatch. Likewise, absence of fraud review alone does not establish an R1 violation when a qualifying manual review occurred. A complaint after close may represent a genuine customer recovery episode that an overly rigid model fails to accommodate. The low-precision discovered model should therefore not become the redesigned policy specification. 

**Evidence fragmentation and repair quality are separate problems.** Repeated evidence requests affect 26% of cases entering that activity. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort has a 140-hour median and the highest abandonment. Repair is not uniformly successful either: repaired high-value devices have a 12.5% rework rate, versus 4.0% for low-risk devices. A redesign must address these mechanisms separately rather than treating every review or repair as beneficial.  

**Finally, the aggregated artifacts do not support a complete numerical reconstruction.** Listed terminal transitions total 3,625 rather than 5,000. Listed dispatch inflows total 2,365, while its outflows total 2,580. Thus, the supplied DFG is not a balanced, closed flow table. Missing edges, extraction boundaries, or differing scopes require reconciliation; the remaining 1,375 cases cannot simply be labeled open. Edge counts also do not establish complete-trace frequencies or unique-case complaint counts. These limitations preclude defensible exact simulation, portfolio ROI, or causal route comparisons from the aggregates alone. 

## 2. Redesign A: Policy-safe, replacement-led routing

**Redesigned process flow.**

`Intake → segment/amount/risk/defect classification → updated auto screen → required evidence and review controls → Fast approval → replacement compatibility check → Dispatch replacement → Quality/compliance close`

Clearly eligible low-risk claims retain an automated route. Claims above EUR 400 receive one of the qualifying reviews before fast approval. Unclear evidence or uncertain eligibility routes to targeted clarification or manual review rather than optimistic approval. Fraud specialists handle actual risk triggers rather than every expensive device.

Replacement assurance checks whether the selected part or device matches the claimed product and defect. Straightforward, verifiable matches can use automated checks; uncertain selections require human inspection. **The availability and effectiveness of automated compatibility data are design assumptions, not supplied capabilities.** Until demonstrated, planning must allow for the stated 6–18-hour gate delay.

Every dispatch records its evidence-completeness state or the precise R2 exception. Every reopen returns through a new manual review before final close or denial. Failed controls cannot be overridden merely to preserve speed.

**Target segments, paths, and activities.**

This design primarily targets the replacement route, especially clean low-risk accessory claims and wrong-selection failures after dispatch. Standard low-risk accessories are the best initial cohort; Premium accessories are appropriate only after the added assurance proves compatible with their service expectations. High-value replacement remains possible, but only with the applicable review and evidence controls.

The redesign directly addresses outdated, non-segment-aware screening and the identified dispatch-to-complaint failure mechanism. Updating screening rules alone is reported as deployable in two weeks; that is not an implementation estimate for the entire redesign.  

**Mechanism of improvement.**

The design preserves low handling cost where automation is defensible while adding assurance close to the irreversible shipment decision. Its economic case depends on preventing actual wrong selections, not assuming that every complaint is a replacement error.

For a dispatched claim, the relevant comparison is:

$$
\text{additional assurance cost + delay-related cost}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\Delta p_{\text{wrong replacement}}\times C_{\text{wrong replacement}}
+\text{other avoided costs}.
$$

The supplied wrong-replacement costs are EUR 95 for accessories and EUR 410 for high-value devices. Neither the achievable reduction in error probability nor the gate’s active labor requirement is supplied, so break-even cannot yet be calculated. Avoided costs must not be counted twice if those expected error-cost figures already include reshipment components. 

Reviewer capacity is a binding rollout consideration. Seven reviewers provide **252 productive hours weekly**; 82% utilization corresponds to approximately **206.6 busy hours**. Under the explicit assumption of unchanged average review effort and staffing, a 15–35% inflow increase multiplies utilization by 1.15–1.35. Remaining below 82% would require initial utilization below approximately **71% for the smaller increase or 61% for the larger increase**. Current utilization is unknown.  

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.**

Some dispatches become slower, and initial handling costs rise. A gate near the upper end of the 6–18-hour range can consume much of a Premium accessory’s 24-hour exposure window. Existing spare inspector capacity cannot be assumed to persist during repair campaigns. This design also leaves evidence coordination, part-price confirmation, and repair workmanship largely unresolved. Its promise is **safer fast replacement**, not universal end-to-end simplification.  

## 3. Redesign B: Assisted evidence completion with continuous case ownership

**Redesigned process flow.**

`Intake → named case owner and evidence-sufficiency triage → consolidated evidence request, when needed → portal or assisted submission → evidence validation → coordinated eligibility and technical decision → repair or replacement → Quality/compliance close`

The owner consolidates requirements from eligibility, technical, and risk functions into one intelligible request wherever possible. A subsequent request requires a recorded reason: genuinely new information, changed circumstances, or an avoidable omission.

Customers can use the portal or receive assisted submission through a staffed channel. Submitted material is validated before another team requests it again. Clean, complete cases bypass the extended evidence-assistance stage.

The owner coordinates decisions and customer communication but does not replace qualified reviewers, technicians, or risk specialists. Reopened cases retain continuity of ownership while still receiving the mandatory post-reopen manual review.

**Target segments, paths, and activities.**

The principal target is the unclear-defect/missing-evidence cohort and the loop:

`Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review → Request missing evidence`.

It also addresses abandonment and the slow restart after a complaint. The reported 140-hour median, repeated evidence requests, and 40-hour mean complaint-to-review interval make this a distinct improvement opportunity from replacement assurance.    

**Mechanism of improvement.**

The intervention changes how information is assembled and responsibility is maintained, rather than merely making the existing portal faster. The expected benefits are fewer avoidable evidence cycles, less repeated handling, clearer customer expectations, and reduced loss of context at reopens.

The proposed portal could reduce repeated evidence requests by 30–45%, but that estimate is **not** a 30–45% reduction in total cycle time. It also does not automatically translate into the same reduction in the percentage of cases with repeat requests; the denominators and mechanisms must be checked. Assisted access is essential because the supplied evidence explicitly warns of increased abandonment among customers with low digital literacy. 

Status communication should distinguish “waiting for customer evidence,” “evidence received but awaiting validation,” and “decision pending.” This supports the stated Standard-accessory tolerance for a longer process when visibility is good, without hiding elapsed time behind internal status changes. 

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.**

Ownership and assistance consume capacity. No available coordinator staffing or cost is supplied; these must be funded or explicitly reallocated rather than treated as free. Universal detailed intake would unnecessarily slow straightforward claims, so assistance must be triggered by need.

This design accepts higher front-end effort in exchange for potentially fewer later loops. It does not directly fix wrong replacement selection or unreliable repairs. It must also preserve legitimate requests for additional evidence: “one consolidated request” is a coordination objective, not a prohibition on discovering new facts.

Compared with Redesign A, its central intervention is **continuity and information completeness**, not stronger automated routing.

## 4. Redesign C: High-value repair cells with parallel quote preparation

**Redesigned process flow.**

`Intake and eligibility/evidence assessment → technical diagnosis with parallel, nonbinding part-price/availability preparation → repairability decision`

For viable repairs:

`Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair device → defect-specific Quality check → Quality/compliance close`

For unsuitable or unacceptable repairs:

`reviewed replacement decision → applicable approval/evidence controls → verified replacement selection → dispatch → close`

A small technical cell coordinates quote preparation, repair execution, and quality feedback. It uses explicit work-in-progress limits and reserved inspection appointments rather than releasing more repairs than downstream capacity can absorb.

Part-price lookup begins once sufficient product identification exists, alongside technical work where feasible. This is reversible preparation, not authorization to approve or dispatch before the required controls. Final quotes incorporate confirmed prices and the technical decision.

**Target segments, paths, and activities.**

The initial target is Standard high-value devices, followed selectively by Premium high-value devices. These cohorts show much lower complaints on reviewed/repair routes, while wrong high-value replacements carry an expected EUR 410 error cost. The design specifically targets manual-review-to-quote delay, price-confirmation waiting, and high-value repair rework.  

Low-risk accessories are not default repair candidates merely because their manual-route complaint rates are lower. Premium high-value cases need separate service-acceptance criteria; the 24-hour exposure threshold supplied for Premium accessories must not be silently applied to them.

**Mechanism of improvement.**

This design tries to retain reviewed-route quality while removing avoidable serial waiting. A validated price catalog, time-bounded supplier confirmation, or earlier nonbinding price requests are candidate mechanisms to test—not existing capabilities assumed to be available.

Quality inspectors help define defect-specific acceptance checks and feed recurrent failures back to technicians. High-value repairs may receive an earlier checkpoint before final assembly, but final quality verification remains. This matters because the observed high-value repair rework rate is 12.5%; repair-first without workmanship improvement could merely replace reshipment problems with repeated repairs. 

The cell should draw on the three quote technicians and two inspectors only to the extent their skills and schedules support it. Reported inspector underuse is an opportunity to investigate, not proof of sufficient campaign-time capacity or cross-functional competence. 

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.**

The design sacrifices some replacement speed and requires more technical coordination. It does not promise to match the current 20-hour Premium high-value fast-path median. Customer approval remains a potentially substantial interval, and customers must retain a clear explanation of the repair proposition rather than being pushed into repair to improve internal costs.  

Supplier responsiveness, repair execution capacity, and repairability economics remain unquantified. The daily escalation board is reserved for genuine exceptions, not made the routine decision authority: its single 45-minute daily slot is not scalable. 

Unlike A and B, this redesign changes the **fulfillment strategy and technical work organization**, not primarily routing or information collection.

The baseline observations and stated intervention ranges come from the segment, rework, and resource evidence. All net redesign effects remain to be established. 

* **“No” means no Pareto domination is demonstrated by the supplied evidence—not proof that a design lies on the true Pareto frontier.** For example, B could outperform A for evidence-deficient claims while adding unnecessary overhead to clean accessories. C could be attractive for repairable high-value devices and unattractive for inexpensive accessories.

The current process is also **inadmissible wherever mandatory rules are violated**. Inadmissibility is different from Pareto dominance: faster or cheaper noncompliance is not a legitimate alternative, but that does not prove another design is faster and cheaper on every dimension.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

### Redesign A

**Failure through reviewer overload.** More conservative rules may send enough additional work to manual review to cross the reported 82% congestion threshold. Overload could then create shortcuts and exceptions that undermine the redesign. Rollout should be limited by measured reviewer work-hours and queue tails, not by the number of automated decisions deployed.  

**Failure through an ineffective or slow quality gate.** Compatibility checks may miss defects caused by poor product data or misdiagnosis, while still adding delay. A gate that helps Standard accessories could harm Premium accessories through service-credit exposure. Measure actual prevented selection errors and time added to the critical path separately.

**Likely bottleneck migration:** from post-dispatch complaints and escalation to **pre-dispatch inspection and manual eligibility review**. The same inspectors may then delay repair checks during campaigns.

### Redesign B

**Failure through digital exclusion.** Fewer repeated requests could appear to be an improvement simply because more customers abandon. Portal use must not become a condition for access to assistance, and abandonment must remain a separate outcome rather than disappearing from completion statistics.

**Failure through overloaded or ineffective ownership.** An owner without access to reviewers becomes an extra handoff; an owner with too many cases becomes another queue. Conversely, suppressing legitimate follow-up requests to meet a “one request” target can lower evidence quality.

**Likely bottleneck migration:** from repeated customer-request loops to **evidence validation and assisted support**. More customers successfully completing evidence may also increase downstream review and repair demand. That increase can represent improved access rather than operational failure, but it requires capacity.

### Redesign C

**Failure through unresolved supplier dependence.** Earlier price requests may merely move the timestamp at which waiting starts. An outdated price catalog could cause inaccurate quotes, renewed customer approval, or margin loss. Measure supplier response and quote revisions, not just internal quote preparation.

**Failure through repair or inspection saturation.** More high-value repairs can amplify the existing rework problem. Reserving inspectors for the pilot may improve its results while worsening other customers’ waiting times. Premium-priority repair slots can similarly displace Standard work unless service floors are monitored.

**Likely bottleneck migration:** from eligibility-to-quote waiting to **repair execution, final inspection, or customer approval**. None should be treated as unlimited simply because it is downstream of the current measured delay.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

### Stage 0: Establish a common compliant baseline and reliable measurement

Immediately correct confirmed R1–R3 violations across all arms; do not randomize customers into known noncompliance. Where an automated control cannot yet be verified, use a controlled manual fallback.

Before evaluating redesign benefits, reconcile DFG scope, terminal outcomes, unique-case denominators, and reopen episodes. Instrument actual review service times, evidence confirmation, claim amount, product/defect identifiers, replacement selection, customer contact, and policy version. Separate elapsed waiting from active work.

Shadow-test the proposed routing changes against actual reviewer workload before releasing additional inflow. Shadow evaluation should estimate operational consequences without allowing a knowingly noncompliant live decision.

Define first fulfillment separately from resolution after observed reopens. Continue to report denials and abandonment as distinct outcomes; neither should make successful-resolution time look better by removing difficult cases.

### Stage 1: First operational pilot—A for complete-evidence Standard accessories

Start with clearly classified, complete-evidence Standard low-risk accessories, initially excluding ambiguous risk and unresolved high-value classification. This cohort provides more timing tolerance than Premium accessories while testing the identified wrong-selection mechanism. The comparator is the **same compliant replacement route without the additional proposed assurance intervention**, not the uncorrected historical process.

Measure mature wrong-part shipments, complaints/reopens, intake-to-first-fulfillment time, 72-hour completion attainment, fully loaded cost, and workload added to reviewers and inspectors.

**Proposed decision thresholds—not predicted effects:** seek a practically meaningful reduction in wrong-selection failures, such as a pre-registered **15–25% relative improvement**, with no deterioration beyond an agreed small margin in completion attainment or abandonment. Cost should be non-increasing unless the sponsor explicitly approves an additional quality budget.

Any confirmed mandatory-rule breach pauses the affected automated route. Reviewer utilization approaching approximately **75–80%** is a proposed warning range; crossing the supplied **82%** congestion point should block expansion pending capacity or routing changes. The warning range is a design choice, while 82% comes from the evidence.

Premium accessories enter only after actual tail performance demonstrates that assurance can coexist with their 24-hour exposure point. Their current eight-hour median is not sufficient evidence of headroom.

### Stage 2: Test B for unclear defects and missing evidence

Compare assisted, owner-led evidence completion with the existing compliant request process. Assignment should occur before the redesigned assistance is delivered, so outcomes are not assessed only among customers who successfully use the portal.

Track repeated request events per evidence entrant, the fraction of entrants experiencing repeat requests, total reviewer touches, evidence-stage median and p90, abandonment, and customer-reported clarity. Also report these outcomes per assigned case so changing entry or abandonment rates cannot manufacture improvement.

The supplied **30–45% potential reduction in repeated requests** is a hypothesis to validate, not a rollout guarantee. Go forward only if fewer requests coincide with better elapsed-time outcomes and no material accessibility harm. A proposed stop/review margin could be an **additional 1–2 percentage points of abandonment** in an assistance-needing group, interpreted with sample uncertainty rather than a single noisy estimate. 

### Stage 3: Test C in a limited Standard high-value cohort

Begin with product/defect groups whose repairability can be assessed consistently. Compare the cell with a contemporaneous compliant reviewed route. Explicitly reserve—and account for—technician and inspector time.

Monitor quote-wait median and p90, price revisions, repair work in progress, first-pass quality, repair rework, mature complaints, fallback replacements, and total customer elapsed time. Expand only when quote delays fall **without** rising repair queues, quality failures, or waiting imposed on non-pilot cases.

Premium high-value expansion requires a separate customer-service test. The observed quality advantage of the reviewed route does not establish that its waiting time is acceptable.

### Distinguish real improvement from downstream displacement

Use stratified randomization where feasible, balancing customer tier, value, defect type, initial evidence state, and relevant calendar effects. Because shared queues create interference, case randomization alone may contaminate both arms. Team/time-block or cluster designs may be preferable, provided campaigns and Monday risk peaks are accounted for and non-pilot outcomes remain visible.

Adopt an initial **30-day post-fulfillment complaint observation window**, with a **60-day sensitivity check**. These are proposed measurement choices, not claims that all failures occur within those periods. Retain unresolved claims as censored observations, compare cohorts with equal follow-up, and include all observed reshipments, repairs, credits, and recovery labor. Do not multiply inter-event waiting hours by labor rates.

A redesign passes only when improvement survives four checks: it persists through reopens, includes all assigned customers, includes downstream costs, and does not rely on worsening other queues. Avoid introducing A, B, and C simultaneously into the same cohort without a design that can distinguish their contributions.

**Recommendation:** establish the shared compliance foundation first, then pilot A in Standard accessories, B in evidence-deficient claims, and C in Standard high-value repairs. This sequence tests three different mechanisms against their strongest-fit populations. Portfolio adoption should follow demonstrated segment-level benefits—not selection of one universal path from an aggregated DFG.
