# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in a benefits process is not one quantity. At minimum eight distinguishable dimensions are visible in this evidence, and they point in different directions — which is itself the central finding.

**1.1 Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates range from 78% (A) to 58% (D); a 20-point spread. Raw parity is a *screening* signal only. Groups differ sharply in documented complexity (D has 51% high-risk band and 49% high prior-case count; B has 46% incomplete documentation and 57% needing language support), and a benefits process is *supposed* to produce different outcomes for applicants with different eligibility facts. Raw gaps establish that something needs explaining, not that something is wrong.

**1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controls, the gaps shrink but do not vanish: B −5.5 pp, C −2.2 pp, D −3.1 pp on approval probability. The controls "explain" roughly 63% of B's raw gap, 76% of C's, and 85% of D's. Two cautions. First, precision: with n = 820 (C) and n = 980 (D), a crude standard error on an approval-rate difference against A is around 1.7–1.8 pp, so C's −2.2 pp is not reliably distinguishable from zero and D's −3.1 pp is borderline, whereas B's −5.5 pp (SE ≈ 1.4 pp) is comfortably outside noise. No confidence intervals were reported; they must be. Second, interpretation: the residual is *unexplained by these covariates*, which is not the same as *caused by group membership*. It could be unmeasured eligibility differences, mismeasured covariates, or differential treatment — the data as presented cannot separate these.

**1.3 Procedural burden.** This is where the evidence is strongest and where an outcome-only analysis fails completely. Burden means how much process an applicant must absorb to get the same decision: number of steps, number of document requests, number of handovers, number of separate people to deal with, number of re-explanations. Adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp for B and +4.2 pp for C *even though documentation_completeness is a control variable*. Adjusted probability of Supervisor Review is +8.0 pp for B and +5.5 pp for C. B and C are subjected to more procedure at the same recorded complexity. Burden is a fairness outcome in its own right, independent of the final decision: two applicants who are both approved have not been treated equally if one was approved after three document requests, two handovers, and a supervisory escalation.

**1.4 Timeliness.** Median cycle time runs 8.1 d (A) to 15.9 d (C), and the 90th percentiles diverge more (21.4 d vs 38.8 d), meaning the tail — the worst-served applicants — is where inequality concentrates. Adjusted cycle-time differences are +2.6 d (B), +4.1 d (C), +0.9 d (D). Note the inversion: C has the *longest* cycle time but the *smallest* adjusted approval gap, while D has the largest raw approval gap but nearly no adjusted cycle-time gap. Timeliness and outcomes are separate fairness problems with separate mechanisms. For a benefits program, delay is not a cosmetic metric: it is a period during which an entitled household does not have money.

**1.5 Consistency of routing.** Fairness requires that like cases be routed alike, and that routing rules be applied by rule rather than by discretion correlated with group. Legitimate routing exists here (high risk → Fraud Review; accommodation request → Accommodation Review; minority language → Language Support Contact) and it explains most of D's Fraud Review exposure. What is *not* rule-explained is why B's supervisor-review and document-request probabilities remain elevated after conditioning on the recorded triggers. Consistency also has an under-service side: are there cases where a required rights-preserving step was *skipped* (accommodation requested but no Accommodation Review; minority language preference but no Language Support Contact)? That is a procedural-fairness violation that no outcome metric would reveal, and it is directly checkable in the log.

**1.6 Quality and accessibility of support.** The support steps exist, so nominal access is satisfied; effective access is not established. A Language Support Contact that happens after a 3.8-day queue, is followed by a document request the applicant did not understand the first time, and precedes 22% rework, is a step that formally preserves a right while materially degrading it. Accessibility should be judged by whether the step *resolves* the barrier (does a supported case afterwards look like an A case?), not by whether the event appears.

**1.7 Appeal and rework burden.** Appeal rates are 6% / 13% / 10% / 16%, with adjusted differences of +4.4 pp (B), +1.1 pp (C), +5.7 pp (D). Reopen/rework runs 11% / 22% / 24% / 20%. Appeal rate is *directionally ambiguous* and must not be read as a fairness indicator on its own: a higher appeal rate can mean worse first decisions, worse explanations, greater legitimate disagreement, or better advocacy access. Without appeal *outcomes* (overturn rate) the interpretation is undetermined — a high appeal rate with a high overturn rate indicts first-instance decision quality; with a low overturn rate it suggests explanation or expectation problems. Rework is less ambiguous: 22–24% versus 11% is a burden and error-cost disparity, and it is consistent with the handover pattern.

**1.8 Resource-allocation fairness.** This is the structural dimension. 3,600 A cases spread across 52 caseworkers (~69 cases/worker) versus 1,250 B cases concentrated on 7 bilingual caseworkers plus 3 overloaded regional officers, and 820 C cases on 5 accommodation specialists. Capacity per case is dramatically unequal, and the policy compounds this by declaring these steps rights-preserving while *setting no timeliness target for them*. Two of the five accommodation specialists also perform Supervisor Review, which couples two queues that C cases must traverse sequentially and simultaneously weakens the independence of the second-level review. The governing principle: when a step exists to protect an entitlement, the cost of that step should not be borne by the rights-holder. Here it is.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to partition the observed gaps into (a) legitimate routing driven by verifiable case facts, (b) disparity of burden or timing that is not explained by those facts, (c) potential differential treatment, and (d) irreducible uncertainty. The strategy must also avoid the two symmetric errors named in the constraints: calling raw gaps discrimination, and dismissing gaps because case mix differs.

**2.1 Define estimands before estimating.** Three separate targets, not one:
- *Burden estimand*: total waiting time, count of handovers, count of distinct resources, count of Request Missing Documents events, count of escalations, per case.
- *Timeliness estimand*: cycle time and per-transition waiting time, at medians and 90th percentiles (quantile regression, not means only — the tail is the equity problem).
- *Decision-quality estimand*: approval conditional on true eligibility. True eligibility is unobserved, so this can only be approached indirectly, e.g. appeal overturn rate by group (an approval that had to be won on appeal is evidence the first decision was wrong) and blind re-adjudication of a stratified case sample.

**2.2 Matching / stratification.** Use coarsened exact matching on risk_score_band × documentation_completeness × channel × household_size_band × prior_case_count band × region × quarter, then propensity-score matching within strata for residual imbalance. Two additional requirements that the preliminary model appears to lack:

- *Match on entitlement profile, not just complexity.* Compare B language-support cases with A/C/D language-support cases, and C accommodation cases with accommodation cases in other groups. Comparing a group that mostly uses a support step against a group that mostly does not is not a comparison of treatment; it is a comparison of process designs.
- *Add caseworker and office fixed effects.* Region and month are in the preliminary model but the *resource* is not, so it cannot distinguish "B cases go to overloaded workers" (allocation unfairness) from "the same worker treats B cases differently" (differential treatment). These are different findings with different remedies. The sharpest specification is within-worker, within-day (or within office-week), which holds congestion roughly constant. If assignment is by rotation or first-available within an office-day, that quasi-randomness should be tested and exploited.

**2.3 Handle the mediator problem explicitly — run two specifications, not one.** Note that the preliminary model controls for risk band, documentation, channel, household size, prior cases, region and month, but *not* for language_preference or disability_accommodation_requested. That choice matters enormously and cuts both ways:

- The *gross-burden* specification deliberately does **not** condition on the support step. It answers: "how much more process and delay does an applicant in this group experience in total?" This is the right question for a rights-based fairness assessment, because the delay caused by an understaffed accommodation queue is a burden the agency created, not a characteristic of the applicant.
- The *within-need* specification conditions on the entitlement profile. It answers: "among applicants who both need accommodation, is one group served more slowly?"

Reporting only the first invites the objection "but those cases are genuinely more complex"; reporting only the second commits over-adjustment and mathematically erases the very burden under investigation. Report both and state which question each answers. Similarly, `channel` and `documentation_completeness` are plausibly post-treatment / partly agency-determined, so their status as controls is a judgement, not a technicality.

**2.4 Path and variant comparison.** Compare variant distributions by group; then, crucially, compare cycle time and burden *within* variant across groups. Within-variant comparison is the cleanest available adjustment for process complexity: if B and A cases both follow V2 (Submit → Verify → Completeness → Request Docs → Eligibility → Decision → Notify) and B is still slower, complexity has been held fixed by construction and the residual is service or queueing. Also test **positivity/common support**: V4 is 24% of B and 1–3% of everyone else, and V5 is 31% of C and 2–3% of everyone else. For those paths there are effectively no comparators in other groups, so no statistical adjustment can compare them — any model estimate there is extrapolation. The absence of overlap is itself a finding: a large share of B and C cases travel a route that A cases essentially never travel, and its properties (19.2 d and 21.6 d medians) must be evaluated against the *standard* the program promises, not against a nonexistent A counterpart.

**2.5 Resource-assignment comparison.** Quantify: cases per resource by group; Gini or Herfindahl concentration of assignment by group; distinct resources and handover count per case; handover-of-work and working-together networks per group; role discovery to confirm the specialist/supervisor role overlap. Then test whether outcomes and delays vary systematically by *which* worker handled the case, and whether group predicts assignment to high-backlog workers. Decompose: gap = (allocation to slower resources) + (slower service by the same resource) + (more handovers).

**2.6 Waiting-time decomposition.** Split every case's cycle time into service time and waiting time per transition, and classify waiting into agency-side queueing (Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents; Language Support Contact → Eligibility; Accommodation Review → Eligibility; Supervisor Review → Decision Draft) versus applicant-side response time (Request Missing Documents → next agency event). Then run an additive decomposition:

E[cycle | group] ≈ Σ_transitions P(transition | group) × E[duration | transition, group]

The gap versus A splits into a **composition** term (B and C traverse extra transitions — legitimate routing) and a **conditional-duration** term (the same transition takes longer for them — not explained by routing). This single decomposition is what separates "legitimate complexity" from "unequal service," and it should be run at the median and 90th percentile.

**2.7 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.** `risk_score_band` and `prior_case_count` are suspect because past adverse decisions plausibly generate future re-applications and higher risk scores; conditioning on them can adjust away the footprint of historical bias. `documentation_completeness` is suspect because it may partly measure whether the applicant could read the instructions. Therefore:
- Fit the models with and without each suspect covariate and report the *range* as the honest interval. If D's adjusted approval gap widens materially when risk band is dropped, the conclusion "D's gap is explained by risk" is conditional on the risk score being fair.
- Blind re-review: have assessors re-score a stratified sample of applications for completeness, without group or language indicators, and estimate the mislabeling rate by group. This converts a speculation into a measurement.
- Bias bounds: compute how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be (Rosenbaum-style / E-value) to explain away B's −5.5 pp and +9.5 pp. If the required confounder is implausibly strong, the finding is robust.
- Negative-control transition: Decision Draft → Applicant Notification is a step where no legitimate group difference should exist. It shows 0.5 / 0.8 / 0.7 / 0.5 days — small. Use it as a placebo: because it is *not* strongly disparate, the disparities elsewhere are less likely to be an artifact of a generalized measurement or congestion effect, and more likely to be localized to specific steps.

**2.8 Subgroup checks by region and channel.** The notes flag backlog in two regions and more B handovers in two regions. Pooled models with region as a covariate absorb the average region effect but hide region × group interactions. Estimate group effects per region and per channel, test interactions, and check whether the entire B supervisor-review delay is driven by two offices — a very different (and much more tractable) problem than a system-wide one. Also examine intersections (B and C simultaneously; in-person B), pre-register the subgroup list, control the false-discovery rate across the many group × metric tests, and do a power calculation first so that null results in small cells are reported as *uninformative* rather than as evidence of fairness.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Ranked by how much they matter and how well complexity explains them.

**3.1 Excess document requests for Group B — the most concerning single number.** Adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp for B *while documentation_completeness is held constant*. If completeness were measured neutrally and applied by rule, this coefficient should be near zero. Three candidate explanations survive: the completeness measure is itself language-contaminated (documents were adequate but not legible to the reviewer, or instructions were only in the majority language); the request is issued but not effectively communicated, so it must be repeated; or reviewers exercise more caution with cases they cannot easily discuss. The first two are agency-side defects; the third is differential treatment even if well-intentioned. None of them is "B applicants have messier files," because that is the variable being controlled. Compounding it: the Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents wait is 1.6 d for B versus 0.4 d for A — a fourfold difference at a step that is pure internal administration.

**3.2 Group C's accommodation route is the slowest path in the process, and it is structurally, not substantively, slow.** V5 (Accommodation Review → Eligibility → Supervisor Review) carries 31% of C's cases at a 21.6-day median, versus 5.8 days for V1. The Accommodation Review → Eligibility wait alone is 4.7 days, the largest single queue in the table, attributed to a five-person specialist team with no service-level target. Note what the path itself shows: exercising the accommodation right routes the case through Supervisor Review as a matter of course, so the rights-preserving step also imports an extra control step. And two of the five specialists also perform supervisor review, so the same scarce people are the bottleneck at both stages a C case must clear — a queue-coupling defect, and an independence defect, since second-level review of a case is less meaningful when performed by someone from the team that handled it. C's profile — worst timeliness (15.9 d median, 38.8 d P90), worst rework (24%), but the smallest and statistically indistinguishable adjusted approval gap (−2.2 pp) — is the signature of a burden and capacity problem rather than an outcome-discrimination problem. An outcome-parity-only audit would have given Group C a clean bill of health.

**3.3 Group B's language route: legitimate step, illegitimate queue.** V4 holds 24% of B cases at a 19.2-day median, with a 3.8-day Language Support Contact → Eligibility wait explicitly attributed to "only 7 bilingual caseworkers." The step is required and correct; the wait is a staffing decision. That B's cases are concentrated on 7 bilingual staff plus 3 already-overloaded regional officers, while A's spread over 52 caseworkers, is the clearest instance of unequal resource allocation per case in the dataset. The extra handovers (general → bilingual → general) add a second cost: each handover is a context transfer, and B's 22% rework rate is consistent with information loss at those seams. Adjusted +8.0 pp Supervisor Review for B likely reflects the same dynamic — escalation as a response to communication friction rather than to case facts.

**3.4 Group D: mostly explained, but explained by the covariates that are least trustworthy.** D's fraud-review routing follows from a 51% high-risk share, its Risk Screening → Fraud Review wait (2.8 d vs 2.2 d) is only modestly elevated, its adjusted cycle-time gap is small (+0.9 d), and adjusted Supervisor Review is only +2.0 pp — the lowest of the three, precisely because the recorded risk facts absorb it. On its face this is legitimate risk-based routing. But D's raw approval gap is the largest (−20 pp) and the adjustment doing the explaining runs entirely through `risk_score_band` and `prior_case_count`, the two variables the scenario itself flags as possibly encoding historical disparity. And D retains the largest residual appeal difference (+5.7 pp) alongside −3.1 pp approval: applicants are disputing these decisions more often even after adjustment. "Explained by risk score" is therefore a conditional verdict, valid only to the extent the risk score is fair — which has not been established.

**3.5 Supervisor Review → Decision Draft backlog.** B waits 1.9 d and C 1.6 d versus A's 1.0 d, attributed to backlog in two regions. Since region is a control in the preliminary model, the residual pattern needs a region × group breakdown; a two-office backlog affecting group-concentrated caseloads produces a system-level disparity from a local capacity failure, and it is fixable locally.

**3.6 What is *not* disparate is analytically important.** Decision Draft → Applicant Notification (0.5–0.8 d) and Risk Screening → Fraud Review (2.0–2.8 d) show only small differences. The disparities are *localized* at the two support-step queues, at the document-request step, and at supervisor review — not spread uniformly across every transition. That localization argues against a generalized "everything is slower for minority applicants" story and in favour of specific, identifiable capacity and communication failures. It also makes the disparities more actionable.

**Justified versus concerning, summarized.** Justified by documented complexity: D's fraud-review routing; the *existence* of Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review; part of B's and C's extra steps and hence part of their cycle time; some portion of the approval gaps, given real eligibility differences. Concerning and not explained by complexity: the +9.5 pp document-request excess for B at constant recorded completeness; the 4.7 d and 3.8 d support-step queues with no service-level targets; per-case capacity of 7 and 5 specialists versus 52 caseworkers; the specialist/supervisor role overlap for C; +8.0 pp supervisor review for B; B's −5.5 pp adjusted approval gap and +4.4 pp appeal gap; D's +5.7 pp appeal gap; the 22–24% rework rates.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**4.1 Capacity-constrained specialist queues (structural, no differential intent).** Demand for bilingual and accommodation capacity exceeds supply, so the queue discipline itself allocates delay to whoever needs those steps. No caseworker treats anyone differently; the disparity is produced by staffing ratios and the absence of a timeliness target for those steps. Predicts: gaps concentrate in queue waiting, not service time; gaps scale with specialist utilisation; no within-worker group effect.

**4.2 Measurement bias in `documentation_completeness` (label bias).** If forms, instructions, or checklists work less well across a language barrier, cases are recorded as incomplete that are substantively complete. This mechanism simultaneously produces B's high incomplete share (46%), the +9.5 pp adjusted document-request excess (because the control variable is contaminated and cannot do its job), and part of the approval gap if incompleteness drives denial. Predicts: blind re-review finds a higher false-incomplete rate for B; document requests for B are more often satisfied by resubmitting the same document.

**4.3 Discretion under uncertainty / defensive escalation.** When a caseworker cannot easily communicate with an applicant or the file is unfamiliar, the low-effort safe action is to request more documents or escalate to a supervisor rather than resolve it. This is often well-intentioned and would be denied as bias by the people doing it, yet it produces exactly the observed +8.0 pp supervisor review and +9.5 pp document requests for B. Predicts: within-worker group effects; effects strongest for workers with least experience of these caseloads; effects strongest under high workload.

**4.4 Handover-induced information loss and rework.** Each general → specialist → general transfer loses context, causing repeated requests, re-verification and reopening. Consistent with B (22%) and C (24%) rework versus A (11%), and with the specific handover chains flagged in two regions. Predicts: rework and cycle time rise monotonically with handover count, within variant.

**4.5 Historical bias propagating through `risk_score_band` and `prior_case_count` (feedback loop).** Past denials generate re-application, which raises prior-case count, which raises the risk band, which triggers Fraud Review and adverse-inference scrutiny, which raises denial probability. If this loop operates, controlling for risk band and prior count *removes the evidence of the original unfairness* and makes D's process look procedurally clean. Predicts: D's adjusted gap widens when those covariates are dropped; risk-band audit shows unequal calibration or error rates across groups; overturn rate on appeal is elevated for fraud-reviewed denials.

**4.6 Applicant-side response time interacting with agency deadlines.** In-person and minority-language applicants may take longer to return documents (postal channel, translation, transport, work schedules). Part of the cycle time is then not agency-side. However, this is only exculpatory up to a point: if fixed response deadlines then convert slower responses into incompleteness denials, the agency's deadline design turns an access barrier into an outcome. Predicts: the gap sits in Request-Docs → next-event intervals rather than internal queues; denial-for-incompleteness is elevated for B; a deadline-extension policy change moves outcomes.

**4.7 Office-level congestion and channel selection.** In-person applicants concentrate in particular offices, and if those offices are the two with backlog, group-level disparity is really office-level congestion mediated by channel choice. Predicts: the group effect largely disappears with office fixed effects, and reappears as a between-office inequity — still a fairness problem, but one of geographic resourcing rather than differential treatment.

**4.8 Genuine differences in eligibility.** Some of the approval gap may reflect real differences in program-relevant circumstances that the coarse controls (banded household size, banded risk) capture only partially. This mechanism is entirely legitimate and cannot be ruled out — but it also cannot be verified here, because no eligibility facts (income, assets, program rules) are in the log. It should be stated as a live possibility, not used as a default explanation.

**4.9 Information asymmetry at notification, and advocacy access.** Elevated appeals for B and D could arise from decisions that were correct but poorly explained, from decisions that were wrong, or from better access to advocacy organisations. Without overturn rates these are indistinguishable, and they imply opposite conclusions about the fairness of the first-instance decision.

**4.10 Aggregation artifacts.** With group caseloads unevenly distributed across regions, channels and months, pooled comparisons can reverse within-stratum patterns (Simpson's paradox). Some portion of any observed gap may be composition rather than treatment.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in the covariates that do the explaining.** `documentation_completeness` is a caseworker judgement, generated *inside* the process being audited, plausibly correlated with language barrier, and partly post-treatment (recorded after a first review). `risk_score_band` is an opaque score whose features, training data and calibration are not in the log; it cannot be audited from the event log at all. Adjusting for these variables and then declaring the residual small is circular if the variables themselves carry the disparity. `prior_case_count` is endogenous to prior agency decisions. `channel` is partly a consequence of accessibility, not an exogenous applicant trait. Each of these is simultaneously a plausible confounder and a plausible mediator, and the log contains nothing that resolves which.

**Missing applicant needs and eligibility facts.** There is no income, asset, household-composition detail beyond a band, no benefit type, no accommodation *type* or severity, no literacy or digital-access measure, no which-language detail, no interpreter identity or quality. Consequently the single most important counterfactual — "should this application have been approved?" — is unmeasurable, so error rates by group cannot be computed and the direction of the approval gap cannot be evaluated. A lower approval rate cannot be shown to be either wrong or right.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity, with no queue lengths, no roster, no leave/absence, no shift boundaries, no concurrent caseload per worker, no arrival times into each queue. This makes it hard to separate congestion (a resourcing failure) from differential handling (a treatment failure) — the distinction that determines the remedy. Daily aggregation also hides within-day priority ordering, i.e. whose case gets picked up first from the same queue, which is precisely where preferential sequencing would appear.

**Appeal quality is invisible.** `appeal_flag` is a binary with no appeal outcome, no overturn rate, no grounds, no representation status, no timeliness of the appeal decision. Appeal rate is therefore an ambiguous metric that could indicate poor first decisions, poor explanations, or good access to help. Rework/reopen is likewise unclassified: rework triggered by agency error is a very different signal from rework triggered by new applicant information.

**Dignity, explanation and communication quality are entirely absent.** The log records that Applicant Notification occurred, not whether the notice was comprehensible, in the applicant's language, or contained adequate reasons and appeal instructions. It records that Language Support Contact occurred, not whether it worked. Procedural fairness in the legal and administrative-justice sense — being heard, being told why, being treated with respect — is largely unmeasured. Complaint records, notice templates, and applicant survey data would be needed.

**Attrition and censoring.** Applicants who abandon a burdensome process may never reach Case Closure, may be closed administratively, or may not appear as recognisable cases at all. If burden causes abandonment, the observed cases are a survivor sample and every disparity estimated here is *understated*. Withdrawal and administrative-closure handling must be checked explicitly.

**Common support and construct validity of the groups.** V4 and V5 have essentially no comparators outside B and C respectively, so for roughly a quarter to a third of those caseloads there is no like-for-like counterpart in Group A and "controlling for complexity" is extrapolation rather than comparison. The groups themselves are analyst constructs defined by overlapping proxies (language, accommodation, risk, prior cases) rather than clean protected categories; intersections (a minority-language applicant who also requests accommodation) are not separately visible, and once stratified by region, channel and month the cells become small.

**Statistical limitations.** No confidence intervals, no model specification, no functional form, no interaction terms, no resource fixed effects are reported. With n = 820 and n = 980, adjusted differences of 2–3 pp are within sampling noise, and there are roughly fifteen group × metric comparisons in the tables, so some apparent signals are expected by chance and some real ones will be missed. Absence of a detected disparity in the smaller groups is not evidence of fairness.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**6.1 Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.** Recruit bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists; supplement with on-demand telephone/video interpretation and cross-trained generalists so that the constraint is not a fixed headcount. *Side effects and trade-offs:* budget cost and long hiring lead times; a rushed expansion can dilute expertise and reduce the quality of the support (trading delay for worse support is not a win); relieving these queues may simply shift the bottleneck downstream to Eligibility Assessment or Supervisor Review, so capacity should be modelled end-to-end rather than step-by-step; interpretation-on-demand raises confidentiality and accuracy risks that need quality assurance; and there is a real risk of measuring success by throughput per specialist, which recreates the overload. Also decouple the C bottleneck by ensuring accommodation specialists are not simultaneously the supervisor-review pool — this both shortens the coupled queue and restores the independence of second-level review, though it reduces the number of qualified reviewers in the short term.

**6.2 Set service-level targets for the rights-preserving support steps.** The policy currently designates these steps as rights-preserving but sets no separate timeliness threshold, which makes the delay invisible to management. Add explicit targets for Submit → Language Support Contact, Language Support Contact → Eligibility, and Accommodation Review → Eligibility, and — critically — hold the *overall* target constant so that using a support step does not consume the applicant's clock. *Side effects:* targets are gameable — pressure to record a perfunctory two-minute "contact" to stop the clock, or to skip the step and mark the case as not needing it; therefore pair every timeliness target with a quality and completeness measure (was the step actually delivered, did the applicant's subsequent path improve) and monitor for suspicious step-omission. Beware "stop the clock" exclusions that move delay off the books. Hard targets can also cause cream-skimming (easy supported cases first, complex ones parked), so monitor the tail (P90), not just the median, and monitor the longest-waiting cases by name.

**6.3 Routing redesign to reduce handovers.** Move from general → specialist → general relay toward end-to-end ownership by a qualified bilingual or accommodation-trained caseworker, or a stable pair, with a warm handoff protocol and a structured case summary when transfer is unavoidable. *Side effects:* end-to-end ownership concentrates work on scarce staff and creates key-person risk and single points of failure (absence produces long stalls); it risks creating a separate, parallel track that could become systematically under-resourced or less scrutinised — "separate but unequal" — so the redesigned track must be held to the *same* published standards and audited jointly; fewer handovers also mean fewer independent pairs of eyes, which may reduce error detection; and specialisation benefits can be lost if generalists are cross-trained too thinly. Reducing handovers should not become a pretext for reducing the number of substantive reviews an applicant receives.

**6.4 Improve decision explanations.** Structured reason codes for every decision, plain-language narrative, notices in the applicant's preferred language, explicit statement of which document was missing and why it mattered, and clear appeal rights and deadlines. Similarly, replace generic document requests with specific, translated, itemised requests plus proactive assistance at intake so the Request-Docs loop is entered less often. *Side effects:* drafting takes longer, so cycle time may rise in the short term — an honest trade-off of speed against comprehensibility that should be decided explicitly; templates can degrade into boilerplate that satisfies the metric while explaining nothing; better explanations may *increase* the appeal rate initially because applicants finally understand the basis for the decision and can contest it, which will look like deterioration on any dashboard that treats appeal rate as a defect. Reframe the metric: track appeal *overturn* rate and time-to-resolution, and treat a rising appeal rate with a falling overturn rate as success. Note also that proactive intake help is *differential* by design; that is equitable support, not favouritism, but it must be documented as policy so it is applied consistently rather than at individual discretion.

**6.5 Monitor adjusted disparities routinely.** A standing dashboard reporting, by group and by region/channel: raw and adjusted outcome gaps with confidence intervals; burden measures (handovers, document requests, escalations, distinct resources); per-transition waiting times at median and P90; within-worker gaps; and step-omission rates for entitlement steps. *Side effects:* the choice of controls silently determines the answer, so publish both gross-burden and within-need specifications and pre-register the specification to prevent post-hoc control shopping; over-adjusted monitoring can produce false reassurance, and underpowered cells produce "no significant disparity" findings that get read as clean bills of health — always publish the power and the interval, not just the point estimate; monitoring requires retaining sensitive group attributes, which raises privacy and small-cell disclosure risks and needs access controls; and any monitored number can become a target and be managed rather than improved. Monitoring is diagnosis, not treatment; it needs a defined escalation path or it becomes an archive.

**6.6 Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`.** For the risk score: obtain features, weights and training data; test calibration and error rates by group; check for proxy variables (address, language, channel, prior-contact history) and for feedback from prior agency decisions; test whether Fraud Review outcomes justify the routing volume. For completeness: blind re-review of a stratified sample of applications with group and language indicators masked, to estimate differential mislabeling; review whether forms, checklists and instructions are available and usable in all served languages. *Side effects:* a biased-but-predictive score presents a genuine accuracy/equity trade-off that recalibration cannot make disappear, only relocate; simply removing the score may push the same judgements into unstructured discretion, which is typically less consistent and less auditable — replace, don't just delete; the audit may be unable to establish ground truth, since the labels themselves are agency-generated, so results may be bounds rather than answers; and audits are costly, slow and organisationally uncomfortable, which tends to produce narrow scoping. Fix the *upstream* cause too: translated forms and assisted completion reduce mislabeled incompleteness at source.

Explicitly rejected: removing or bypassing Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review to improve cycle-time statistics. That would improve every metric in the tables while destroying the entitlement the steps exist to protect. The target is to make exercising the right cheap for the applicant, not to make the right disappear.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**Verdict: indeterminate on discriminatory outcomes; more likely than not procedurally inequitable in burden and timeliness.** These are separate conclusions with different evidentiary strength, and collapsing them would misstate the evidence.

On **outcomes**, the honest answer is indeterminate. Controls absorb most of the raw approval gaps (63% for B, 76% for C, 85% for D), the residuals are modest (−5.5, −2.2, −3.1 pp), and for C and D they are at or near the limit of what these sample sizes can resolve. Only B's residual is clearly outside sampling noise. More fundamentally, the log contains no eligibility ground truth and no appeal outcomes, so it is impossible to say whether any group's decisions were *wrong* — only whether they differed. And the covariates carrying most of the explanatory weight, `risk_score_band` and `prior_case_count` for D and `documentation_completeness` for B, are exactly the variables the scenario flags as possibly encoding historical disparity, so "explained by complexity" is a conditional verdict that stands or falls with an audit that has not been done. Different raw approval rates are not evidence of discrimination here — and equally, the shrinkage of those rates under adjustment is not evidence of fairness.

On **procedure**, the evidence is materially stronger and more specific. Three findings are hard to explain benignly. First, Group B's adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp *with documentation completeness held constant*, and its adjusted supervisor-review probability is +8.0 pp; a rule applied consistently to equally-documented files should not produce that. Second, the two rights-preserving steps generate the two longest queues in the process (4.7 d and 3.8 d), are staffed by 5 and 7 people against 52 general caseworkers, and are explicitly exempt from any timeliness target — a design in which exercising an entitlement predictably costs the applicant time. Third, Group C carries the worst cycle time and rework in the system (15.9 d median, 38.8 d P90, 24% rework) while showing the *smallest* adjusted approval gap, which is precisely the pattern an outcome-parity audit would score as fair and a procedural audit scores as the most burdened caseload. That the disparities are localised at these specific steps — while the negative-control step, Decision Draft → Applicant Notification, differs by only 0.2–0.3 days — makes generalised measurement artifact a less likely explanation than concrete capacity and communication failures.

What **cannot** be inferred: intent, differential treatment by identifiable individuals, causal effects of group membership, whether any specific denial was wrong, whether the higher appeal rates for B (+4.4 pp) and D (+5.7 pp) reflect worse decisions or better-informed applicants, and whether the support steps are effective as opposed to merely recorded. Nothing here establishes discrimination in the legal sense. What **can** reasonably be inferred: applicants who use rights-preserving steps experience systematically more process and more delay than similarly documented applicants who do not; a substantial part of B's extra procedure is not accounted for by the recorded complexity measures; and the resource allocation supporting those steps is inadequate relative to demand by a wide margin.

The practical implication is that the most actionable defects do not depend on resolving the discrimination question at all. Adding bilingual and accommodation capacity, setting timeliness targets for the support steps without charging that time to the applicant's overall clock, decoupling the accommodation and supervisor-review pools, reducing handovers, and improving the specificity and language of document requests and decision notices would all reduce measured procedural inequity regardless of whether differential treatment exists. In parallel, three investigations are needed before any confident outcome verdict: an audit of `risk_score_band` for historical feedback and differential calibration, a blind re-review of `documentation_completeness` labelling by language group, and collection of appeal *outcomes* so that appeal rates stop being an uninterpretable metric. Until those exist, the correct posture is: procedurally concerning in identifiable and fixable respects, outcome fairness not established either way — and none of the remedies should involve weakening, bypassing, or removing the support steps in order to make the timeliness numbers look better.
