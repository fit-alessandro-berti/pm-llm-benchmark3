# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

The directions below concern naive case-level calculations using `queue_arrival`, `start`, and `complete`. A corrected analysis should report item throughput and resource occupancy separately wherever one physical action creates several case records.

**G1.** Waiting from queue arrival to 09:00 is probably a useful batch-level wait (roughly 2–6 minutes), although it is not evidence that each item waited independently for a worker. The three-minute per-item service duration is a valid fixture residence time if the four-slot interpretation is correct, but summing four such durations overstates active cell time by about fourfold. Treating RBT-7 as a one-capacity resource and summing case durations inflates utilization and can falsely identify the cell as a bottleneck; using the union of intervals gives roughly three minutes of cell occupancy. Item throughput is four items per cycle, so a single-item service model underestimates capacity by about fourfold. Conformance models also need a batch or parallel-capacity construct, otherwise the four overlapping cases look like violations of sequential execution.

**G2.** The queue-to-10:00 values are waits to the group action, not necessarily waits to each inspection. If actual item work begins after setup, per-item waiting is understated and the common start time charges setup to all cases. The logged service durations, 8–27 minutes, can consequently be inflated for items whose work began later; their sum also double-counts shared setup and any overlap. A union-of-intervals utilization estimate for T-B measures the 27-minute group span, while a sum-of-durations estimate inflates it. Throughput by completion time may be reasonably represented because completions are separate, but a model that assumes all four began at 10:00 can underestimate the station's parallel capacity or misread the staggered completions as long service. Bottleneck and conformance results are sensitive to whether the group begin is modeled as setup, a parallel start, or a logging event.

**G3.** Waiting to the displayed starts may be approximately usable for the review-start portion, but waiting to downstream availability is inflated because all four cases appear incomplete until the 11:30 folder submission. Service is biased upward: the displayed durations of 28, 20, 13, and 6 minutes include time between actual review end and common submission, and the 67-minute sum double-counts shared folder handling. Completion-based utilization similarly overstates active review and may attribute the whole 11:02–11:30 span to T-C. The event count is correct over the broad period, but throughput time series is falsely bursty at 11:30 rather than spread over the actual review completions. This can create an artificial review bottleneck and false conformance deviations in a process model that expects each case to complete at its own review end.

**G4.** Queue waiting from arrival to start is likely directionally correct: the first item waits seven minutes and later items wait progressively longer, subject to clock alignment. Four-minute service intervals are also credible station-occupation intervals. Because the intervals are adjacent and non-overlapping, summing them or taking their union gives the same 20 minutes of busy time; utilization and serial throughput are therefore much less biased than in the other clusters. The station's one-item-per-four-minute rate and its growing queue are evidence consistent with a bottleneck, although a process-level bottleneck requires comparison with other stations. Conformance should favor a serial model, with an explicit initial setup or blocked period if that is confirmed.

**G5.** Queue-to-start waits are about 15–16 minutes, but ownership of each wait is not known because `analyst_pool` is shared. Case service durations (10–25 minutes) may be useful at case level, yet they cannot safely be converted into one-resource occupancy. Summing them yields 70 minutes of apparent work across a 30-minute wall-clock span and can report utilization above 100% for one named resource; taking only the union would instead hide the work of multiple analysts. Throughput and case durations may be roughly right, while resource throughput, capacity, and individual utilization are not identifiable. A false pool bottleneck, false parallelism, and spurious conformance violations can all result. Badge identities and pool capacity are required before making a resource-performance claim.

**G6.** Literal queue waiting is inflated: the displayed waits to 17:00 range from about 35 to 175 minutes, but those values may be time spent waiting for a bulk submission rather than waiting for ticket work. Literal service is zero for every case because `start == complete`; that is near-zero database-event duration, not evidence that seven tickets required no work. This is the important G6 pattern: a delayed common timestamp makes waiting look large while the same timestamp pair makes service look zero. Interval utilization based on lifecycle duration is consequently near zero and misses preparation, decision work, and the bulk transaction's operator time. Count throughput is preserved, but the rate time series shows a fictitious burst of seven at 17:00. Queue analysis will falsely nominate an upstream wait bottleneck while service analysis dismisses the close activity; conformance will see a set of zero-duration late completions.

**G7.** Point queue waits are not reliable and some are literally negative: M02–M05 arrive after the stored 14:00 start. This is a direct reason to interval-censor or exclude the cluster from queue calculations. The 15-minute service value can be too high, too low, or merely a rounded bin width; it cannot support an exact service distribution. Stored overlap can overstate utilization if the work was serial, while a union can understate it if the five items really were parallel and the source omitted their internal timing. Throughput is artificially concentrated at 14:15, and intra-bin order is unknowable. Bottleneck and conformance conclusions should be treated as artifacts of coarse resolution until raw times and rounding semantics are recovered.

**G8.** Assuming the scanner and upstream clocks are reconciled, queue waiting is probably real and accumulates after the first delayed start: the first item waits about 38 minutes and later items wait longer. The seven-minute intervals and serial throughput are credible, so resource utilization over the scanner's active span and bottleneck detection are relatively robust. The safety event suggests a capacity-loss or blocked period before 15:38, but its separate clock means that causal attribution and the exact 15:00–15:35 duration require correction. A rate model should represent the 35-minute serial busy period plus the blocked state, not treat the whole interval as unexplained service. Conformance should be serial with an explicit safety/blocked transition.

```python
def pairwise_overlap_rate(group):
    intervals = [(a, b) for a, b in zip(group["start"], group["complete"])
                 if pd.notna(a) and pd.notna(b) and b >= a]
    possible = len(intervals) * (len(intervals) - 1) // 2
    overlaps = sum(a1 < b2 and a2 < b1
                   for (a1, b1), (a2, b2) in itertools.combinations(intervals, 2))
    return overlaps / possible if possible else 0.0


def equality_groups(group, col):
    counts = group.groupby(col, dropna=False).size()
    repeated = counts[counts > 1]
    parts = []
    for stamp, n in repeated.items():
        label = "NaT" if pd.isna(stamp) else stamp.strftime("%H:%M:%S")
        parts.append(f"{label}x{int(n)}")
    return ";".join(parts) if parts else "none"


def max_pending_queue(group):
    # Arrivals are processed before starts at an equal clock time. This is a
    # descriptive queue build-up indicator, not proof of FIFO dispatch.
    events = []
    for value in group["queue_arrival"].dropna():
        events.append((value, 0, 1))
    for value in group["start"].dropna():
        events.append((value, 1, -1))
    pending = 0
    maximum = 0
    negative = 0
    for _, _, delta in sorted(events):
        pending += delta
        maximum = max(maximum, pending)
        negative += int(pending < 0)
    return maximum, negative

def to_dataframe(value):
    if isinstance(value, pd.DataFrame):
        return value.copy()
    return pm4py.convert_to_dataframe(value)


def batch_summary(value, config):
    result = to_dataframe(value)
    batch_columns = [c for c in result.columns if "batch" in str(c).lower()]
    if not batch_columns:
        return {"config": config, "pm4py_categories": "no batch-id column returned", "n_batches": 0}
    batch_col = batch_columns[0]
    case_col = next((c for c in ["case_id", "case:concept:name"] if c in result.columns), None)
    if case_col is None:
        return {"config": config, "pm4py_categories": f"{batch_col} (no case key)", "n_batches": result[batch_col].nunique()}
    mapping = pm_input[["case_id", "cluster"]].drop_duplicates()
    result = result.merge(mapping, left_on=case_col, right_on="case_id", how="left")
    result = result[result[batch_col].notna()].copy()
    if result.empty:
        return {"config": config, "pm4py_categories": "none", "n_batches": 0}
    counts = result.groupby(["cluster", batch_col], dropna=False).size()
    categories = ";".join(f"{cl}:{int(n)}" for (cl, _), n in counts.groupby(level=0).sum().items())
    return {"config": config, "pm4py_categories": categories, "n_batches": int(counts.size)}


# Use two merge distances and two minimum sizes. A timedelta is the PM4Py
# batch-discovery unit; the displayed data also lets us inspect sensitivity to
# one-minute versus five-minute temporal merging.
pm_results = []
for merge_distance in [timedelta(seconds=1), timedelta(minutes=5)]:
    for min_batch_size in [2, 4]:
        config = f"merge={merge_distance},min_size={min_batch_size}"
        try:
            discovered = pm4py.discover_batches(
                formatted,
                merge_distance=merge_distance,
                min_batch_size=min_batch_size,
            )
            pm_results.append(batch_summary(discovered, config))
        except Exception as exc:
            pm_results.append({
                "config": config,
                "pm4py_categories": f"error:{type(exc).__name__}",
                "n_batches": 0,
            })

print("\nPM4Py batch categories (cluster counts; compare with the independent table)")
print(pd.DataFrame(pm_results).to_string(index=False))
```

The independent table is essential because PM4Py's output is a temporal grouping under a selected tolerance and minimum size. For example, PM4Py may group G1, G2, G3, G5, G6, and G7 for different parameter settings, but those groups do not have the same physical meaning. G4 and G8 should remain non-overlapping serial patterns even when batch discovery reports adjacent or same-activity structures.

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

If resource IDs are trusted as unique execution resources, the evidence supports a four-position machine batch in G1, group-start semantics in G2, a logging/submission artifact in G3, serial stations in G4 and G8, and a shared-account exception in G5. Under that reading, overlap on `analyst_pool` is not evidence that one analyst multitasked; it is evidence that the resource key is not a person key. T-B and T-C still identify a screen/form context rather than a guaranteed physical worker, so their IDs should not be used to prove or disprove concurrency.

If resource IDs are treated as potentially shared accounts, G5's four overlapping intervals become compatible with up to three real analysts and the apparent one-resource utilization is unusable. The same caution must be applied to any human-facing identifier such as T-C or T-F if operational validation finds account sharing. G4 and G8 remain serial at the scanner/station level because the event order is adjacent, even if different operators could have used the station. G1 is primarily a machine-capacity question, so account sharing is less relevant; its missing fixture-slot signal is the bigger uncertainty.

If timestamps are exact to their documented precision, one-second G4/G8 adjacency and G1's three-second recording spread are strong temporal evidence, while one-minute equalities remain exact only to the minute. If timestamps are interval-censored by their precision, the one-minute clusters cannot be treated as point events: equal starts or ends may represent times anywhere in the same capture bin. G7 must be interval-censored most aggressively; its 15-minute precision makes the 14:00–14:15 point interval unsuitable for exact waiting, service, or utilization calculations. G6 remains a bulk-submission concern even under exact timestamps because the semantic problem is that the event is a form transaction rather than the ticket's actual work.

At least four identical temporal patterns have multiple defensible physical explanations:

- Equal starts can mean a genuine four-item fixture batch (G1), a group setup button (G2), a shared-login or group-record artifact (G5), or quarter-hour rounding (G7).
- Equal completes can mean a true common machine completion (G1), serial work recorded when a folder is submitted (G3), or end-of-shift bulk entry (G6).
- Overlapping intervals for one resource can mean real parallel work, multitasking, a shared account representing several workers (G5), or a lifecycle field that spans waiting and submission rather than active work (G2/G3).
- Adjacent non-overlapping intervals can indicate a genuinely serial station (G4/G8), but can also be produced by a system that records each item only at a transaction boundary and hides pauses or setup between them.

The conclusion is consequently robust about temporal patterns but conditional about physical semantics. Smaller PM4Py tolerances test sensitivity to the stored timestamps; they do not substitute for operational identifiers or clock accuracy.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

Priority 1 is to separate execution from logging. Each case should receive immutable, timezone-aware source timestamps for queue entry, dispatch/start, actual completion, and database insertion. Group-begin, setup-complete, form-submit, and bulk-commit must be different event types; a submission timestamp must never overwrite an item completion timestamp. Record the source clock ID, UTC offset, synchronization status, clock correction, sequence number, and event ID so cross-system ordering can be reconstructed.

Priority 2 is to make capacity and identity explicit. Capture a stable badge or service-account identity per human action, session/workstation ID, machine/station ID, fixture position, and the station's available capacity. Shared logins should be retired or supplemented with badge-level attribution. For a robot or multi-position fixture, record the machine cycle and slot membership rather than relying on repeated case IDs with the same resource.

Priority 3 is to add explicit batch structure. Emit a batch/group/folder/lot/transaction ID, a group-begin and group-end event, and the case-to-group membership. Preserve whether the group is a physical execution batch, a common setup, a review folder, or merely a bulk database submission. This allows item throughput to be calculated without treating shared setup or one commit as item service.

Priority 4 is to record state changes around waiting and interruption. Capture queue enter, queue leave/dispatch, blocked, safety-check, downtime reason, pause, resume, cancel, and rework events, plus station capacity changes. A safety event should use the same synchronized clock as the repair scanner or carry a measured offset. This is needed to distinguish ordinary queue waiting from capacity loss.

Priority 5 is timestamp metadata. Document the actual capture precision and accuracy separately, rounding direction and rule, missing-value semantics, timezone, clock source, and whether a value is an interval bound or a point estimate. Retain raw high-resolution values alongside rounded reporting fields; never infer exact concurrency from a rounded display value. For legacy systems that cannot be upgraded, publish lower and upper bounds for each start and completion and mark those records as interval-censored in the data contract.

# 7. Final adjudication

G1 can be interpreted with high confidence as a four-item machine-cycle batch, although fixture-slot data is needed before converting it into a quantitative capacity model. G4 and G8 can be interpreted with high confidence as serial station patterns; G8 additionally has a credible, but cross-clock, capacity-loss explanation for its initial queue. G6 can be interpreted with high confidence as a bulk or end-of-shift logging pattern, but not as seven zero-service ticket executions.

G2 is high-confidence evidence of a common group start, not of physical simultaneity. G3 is high-confidence evidence of common completion/submission logging, not of simultaneous review completion. G5 is high-confidence evidence that the resource key is shared or otherwise non-person-level; actual parallel analyst work is plausible but cannot be measured from this extract. G7 is high-confidence evidence of coarse timestamping, while its underlying physical batch or serial behavior is indeterminate.

For queue analysis, exclude G6 from point waiting and service metrics unless the metric is explicitly “time to bulk submission”; interval-censor or exclude G7; and avoid using G3's completion for downstream waiting or service. G2's start-based wait and G5's case-level waits can be retained only with semantic caveats, while their resource utilization should be withheld. G1 should use batch-level occupancy and item-level throughput as separate measures. G4 and G8 are the strongest candidates for ordinary queue and service analysis after checking clock alignment and station-state events.

Operational validation is still required before performance conclusions for G1's fixture capacity, G2's actual inspection overlap, G3's folder workflow, G5's analyst assignment, G6's true ticket-close times, G7's rounding rule, and G8's relationship to the safety event. The defensible final position is therefore: trust the serial order in G4/G8 and the machine-cycle signal suggested by G1; treat G2/G3/G5 as semantic ambiguities; and treat G6/G7 as logging/timestamp cases that must not be interpreted as ordinary item-level lifecycle data.
