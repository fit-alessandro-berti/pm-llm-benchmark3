## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Because multiple stages share resources and treatments affect future queue states, standard Natural Direct/Indirect Effects (NDE/NIE) are generally ill-defined here due to cross-world counterfactuals and interference. Instead, I rely on **interventional analogues** (often called separable or stochastic direct/indirect effects) that manipulate specific mechanisms while holding the distribution of other mechanisms constant.

### Confounding, interference, and feedback (Explanation)
**Exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding** occurs because `arrival_load` (a collider/mediator of past treatments) affects both manual queue wait and cycle time. **Interference** is explicit: a flagged case forced into manual review increases the queue length for the *next* case (flagged or unflagged), violating the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA). **Treatment-confounder feedback** occurs because the `flag` increases manual review occurrence, which increases system congestion (`arrival_load`), which acts as a confounder for the wait times of subsequent cases.

### Pilot Design: Policy P3 (Protected Manual-Review Capacity)
**Eligibility:** All incoming cases arriving over a 2-week period. 
**Randomization/Assignment Rule:** Odd-numbered days will operate under P0 (shared queue). Even-numbered days will operate under P3, where one dedicated reviewer (e.g., from the existing team) is assigned *exclusively* to review flagged cases.
**Queue-Capacity Guardrail:** If the unflagged manual review queue length exceeds 5 cases, the dedicated reviewer must temporarily switch back to the shared queue to prevent starvation of unflagged cases.
**Primary and Secondary Outcomes:** 
1. *Primary:* Median total cycle time for flagged cases.
2. *Secondary:* Decision outcome (Approval/Rejection rate) to ensure quality does not degrade.
**Minimum Process Data Needed:** Exact entry/exit timestamps for Triage, Manual Review, and Escalation queues; reviewer identifier; final outcome.
**Stopping Conditions:** The pilot will halt immediately if the rejection rate for flagged cases drops by more than 15% relative to the control days, or if the unflagged cases' cycle times increase by more than 25%, indicating unacceptable spillover.

## 8. Reversal conditions
1. **Hidden risk:** If unflagged cases have drastically higher hidden unobserved risk (`U_underlying_risk`), then bypassing manual review (Policy P2) would lead to catastrophic decision outcomes, reversing the justification for removing the bottleneck.
2. **Queue interference:** If queue interference is negligible and the manual review queue length is strictly driven by external background load rather than the flagged cases themselves, then Policy P3 (Protected capacity) will fail to reduce wait times.
3. **Decision quality:** If decision quality (e.g., accurate approvals/rejections) scales linearly with processing time and wait time (e.g., waiting allows more information to arrive), then accelerating cycle times via P3 or P4 might severely degrade outcome accuracy.
4. **Timestamp semantics:** If the `manual_start` timestamp is actually recorded when a batch of cases is *assigned* to a reviewer rather than when work *begins*, then the wait time calculation is flawed, and the true bottleneck might be processing capacity, not queue routing.
5. **Positivity / Lack of overlap:** If virtually all high-complexity cases receive the flag regardless of risk score, it is impossible to disentangle the effect of the flag from the effect of intrinsic complexity. 
6. **Behavior change by reviewers:** If reviewers, upon learning they are operating under a protected-capacity pilot (Policy P3), deliberately slow down their service times (a "pace-making" behavior), the queue will back up just as it did previously, reversing the expected time savings.

## 9. Final synthesis

**What is directly observed:** 
The timestamp data clearly shows that flagged cases are successfully prioritized at the initial triage stage, reducing their wait time from over 2.5 hours to roughly 15 minutes compared to unflagged cases. However, once triage is completed, these flagged cases are almost universally routed into a manual review queue. They wait an average of 6.5 hours for this review, and those subsequently escalated wait an additional 6 hours. Unflagged cases often skip these stages entirely, resulting in total cycle times that are less than half those of flagged cases.

**What is a plausible mechanism:** 
The paradox of "fast triage but slow cycle time" is driven by a structural bottleneck created by the policy itself. By mandating manual review for flagged cases, the policy forces high volumes into a shared, capacity-constrained queue. This downstream congestion completely eclipses the time saved at triage. 

**What remains unidentified:** 
Because `risk_score` and `complexity` are highly correlated with the flag, we cannot definitively separate the delay caused by the *policy* (the flag forcing the case into the queue) from the delay caused by the *case* (high complexity naturally taking longer to review). We also cannot perfectly measure the interference effect—how much a single flagged case's arrival delays the unflagged case behind it.

**Which intervention produces the most useful next evidence:** 
A pilot of **Policy P3 (Protected Manual-Review Capacity)** is the most informative next step. By dedicating a reviewer specifically to flagged cases, we can isolate the causal impact of queue congestion. If cycle times drop significantly under P3, we confirm that shared-queue interference is the true culprit. If they do not, we must conclude that the cases themselves are just fundamentally more time-consuming to process, shifting the focus to process standardization rather than queue management.
