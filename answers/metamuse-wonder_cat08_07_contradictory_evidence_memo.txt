## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee  
**Subject:** Recommendation on Redesign R for fiber installation  
**Recommendation:** Do **not** scale Redesign R nationally in its current form. Adopt the hybrid option E: scale it only for simple, low-risk houses; retain coordinator ownership with capacity and backup controls; immediately suspend survey-skipping for medium-risk apartments and old buildings; and prohibit billing before successful activation except under an explicitly approved exception. Run a controlled, better-instrumented expansion study before any wider rollout.

**Why:** R produced a genuine-looking improvement for the easy majority and a serious deterioration for complex cases. Median cycle time improved from 14.2 to 10.8 days, handovers fell, and visible cost fell from EUR 184 to EUR 161. However, p90 duration rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 from 45 to 62 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, second visits rose from 11% to 18%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and estimated total cost including downstream handling rose from EUR 198 to EUR 207 — despite incomplete downstream-cost capture.

The segment pattern explains the conflict. Simple low-risk houses gained 6.0 median days, 8.0 p90 days, +3 percentage points first-time-right, and fewer complaints. Apartments with medium risk and old permit-sensitive buildings saw much worse tails, rework, and complaints. That is consistent with the survey-skip rule misclassifying risk and with permit checks being missed. Process-mining evidence reinforces this: frequent billing before activation, repeat installation without survey, missing permit checks for older apartments, and complaints after billing.

The coordinator model also has a capacity risk. Handovers fell from 34% to 12% of cases, but coordinator utilization reached 93%, second-visit team utilization rose to 92%, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent. Fewer handovers improved accountability, but created key-person fragility.

**Trade-off:** A full rollback would discard real gains for simple houses and from clearer ownership. National scaling would institutionalize tail delays, rework, billing-before-working-service complaints, and possible permit/activation control failures. The hybrid captures proven value where risk is low while stopping the mechanisms most plausibly causing harm, and buys decisive evidence for the disputed segments.

**Decision requested:** Approve E, with guardrails and evidence thresholds in Section 6, and fund measurements M6, M1, M4, and M3 first.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision:** Fitness measures how well the discovered model can replay observed pilot traces; precision measures whether the model also allows much unobserved behavior. Fitness of 0.94 says most pilot cases fit the simplified coordinator model. Precision of 0.51 says that model is too permissive: it allows many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and early billing whether or not they occurred in controlled ways. Low generalization of 0.56 further suggests the model is neither a stable nor disciplined representation. So the process became easier to replay but harder to govern.

- **Improved median but worse tail duration:** The median reflects the middle case, increasingly a simple house benefiting from merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers. The tail reflects apartments, old buildings, failed visits, permits, second visits, and activation exceptions. If easy cases get faster while a minority enters long recovery loops, median falls while p90/p95 rise sharply. A worse case mix in pilot regions could amplify, but not by itself explain, segment-internal deteriorations such as old buildings' p90 +25 days.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Handovers were reduced by concentrating ownership, context, and queue priority in one coordinator. That reduces transfer loss for routine cases. At 93% utilization, however, the coordinator becomes a bottleneck and single point of failure; absence, overload, or complex exceptions stall cases because context is not shared. The simpler handover network coexists with a more centralized working-together network. Efficiency from specialization traded against slack and substitutability.

- **Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost:** Merging tasks, skipping surveys, and starting billing earlier reduce measured upstream effort: EUR 184 to EUR 161 before complaint handling. But failure demand — second visits at 18%, manual activation at 15%, complaints at 9.8% — shifts cost to revisit teams, activation specialists, and customer service. Because cost data exclude downstream complaint handling after week 4, EUR 207 is an incomplete estimate, not a reliable total. Apparent savings may be cost displacement in time and across teams.

- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings:** Simple houses fit R's assumptions: low risk, little need for survey/permit, merged validation/scheduling removes a queue. For medium-risk apartments, the predictive skip rule was often wrong, leading to failed/repeat visits without survey and worse InstallationActivation times. For old buildings, missing permit checks and complex physical conditions turned skipped preparation into long-tail delay. The same redesign is therefore beneficial under low complexity and harmful where upfront discovery has high value.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Conditions under which reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Fastest capture of median-time, handover, and visible-cost gains; consistent operating model | Institutionalizes tail, rework, complaint, billing-before-activation, and permit risks; coordinator/second-visit capacity already strained; evidence confounded by case mix and incomplete cost | Only if controlled evidence showed total 60-day cost, p90/p95, quality, and compliance non-inferior across segments and capacity headroom existed. Current evidence does not meet this |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Restores higher precision, activation/permit discipline, survey capacity use; avoids known pilot harms | Loses strong simple-house gains and coordinator accountability; reintroduces handover fragmentation and appointment waiting; baseline itself had only 82% first-time-right and 13% SLA breach | Reasonable if audit showed billing/permit deviations were true control breaches at unacceptable scale, or if segment gains proved to be measurement artifacts |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey-skip and post-billing activation** | Retains accountability benefit while removing two most plausible harm mechanisms; simpler to govern and monitor | Coordinator bottleneck/absence risk remains; merged validate/schedule benefit not isolated; may still fail in apartments if coordination capacity is the binding constraint | Reasonable as national guardrail if coordinator staffing, backup, utilization caps, and escalation rules are added and monitored |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Best causal resolution by segment/risk; prevents premature national lock-in; can test skip rule, ownership model, and activation sequencing | Delays scaling; requires randomization discipline, instrumentation, and sample size; pilot regions may remain atypical | Reasonable where uncertainty is decision-changing — which it is for survey skipping and medium/old segments — and where experiment can run without exposing customers to known billing/permit risks |
| **E. Hybrid: R only for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale** | Captures proven segment benefit now; stops highest-risk pathways; creates evidence for expansion segment by segment; aligns controls with complexity | Requires reliable upfront risk triage; boundary errors could misroute cases; two operating models add management complexity | Recommended now. Reasonable if low-risk rule is conservative, misclassification is audited, coordinators have backup/capacity limits, and expansion is gated by thresholds |

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranking is by expected decision value — probability of changing a consequential decision × scale of consequence ÷ delay — not by implementation convenience. M7 guardrails should start immediately even though their information value ranks lower, because the risk is already observed.

| Rank | Measurement / experiment | Uncertainty resolved | How it could change the decision |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | **M1 Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum** | Causal effect of skipping, separately for medium apartments, old buildings, and other strata | If skipping is non-inferior on p90, first-time-right, complaints, and 60-day cost within a stratum, expand skipping there; if harmful, permanently retain survey |
| 2 | **M6 Validate predictive rule against field-survey outcomes** | Whether the rule can identify truly survey-free addresses; false-negative rate by segment | A highly accurate, calibrated rule could support E expansion without full randomization everywhere; poor discrimination confirms rollback of skip logic |
| 3 | **M4 Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start** | True total cost including complaints, revisits, credits, disputes, churn handling | If R remains cheaper in a segment, scaling case strengthens; if simple-house savings vanish downstream, narrow or stop expansion |
| 4 | **M3 Structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, second visit** | Which failure mechanism dominates: access, permit, survey miss, equipment, customer reschedule, activation defect | Targets the fix — permit control, survey, scheduling, activation capacity, or customer readiness — instead of generic rollback/scale |
| 5 | **M7 Conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit absence** | Real-time prevalence and whether deviations are recording gaps or actual breaches | Persistent true breaches block scaling regardless of speed; recording gaps redirect effort to logging discipline |
| 6 | **M5 Compare coordinator vs shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether resilience loss is intrinsic to single ownership or fixable with backup/coverage | If shared/backup model preserves handover gains without stalls, adopt C more broadly; if not, redesign ownership before scaling |

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Adopt E now, incorporating C's suspensions for all non-low-risk segments and D's controlled measurement before any broader rollout. Reject A at this time; do not choose full B.**

**Immediate action (next 2 weeks)**
- Route only verified simple, low-risk houses to the full R path, including merged validate/schedule and coordinator ownership.
- For all other segments, restore mandatory field-survey assessment where risk is not proven low, mandatory permit check for old/apartment buildings, and successful activation test before billing start.
- Start M7 conformance monitoring, M3 reason codes, M6 rule validation, and M4 60-day cost tracking. Audit a sample of "permit check not observed" and "billing before activation" cases to separate real breaches from logging gaps.

**What to pause**
- Pause survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old/permit-sensitive buildings, business, and rural cases pending M1/M6.
- Pause post-billing activation as a routine practice nationally; allow only documented exceptions with named owner and customer consent.
- Pause any national scaling decision and any coordinator hiring model based on 93% utilization.

**What to scale**
- Scale coordinator ownership only with utilization target at or below 85%, named deputy/shared case context, absence cover, and escalation queues.
- Scale merged validation/scheduling and R's fast path for simple low-risk houses, with weekly segment monitoring.
- Preserve survey capacity; redeploy freed survey capacity temporarily to clear second-visit and permit backlogs rather than cutting it.

**What to measure next**
- Run M1 randomized survey-skip evaluation by risk stratum, powered to detect p90, first-time-right, complaint, and 60-day cost differences.
- Complete M6 before expanding the skip rule; report false-negative rate, not just overall accuracy.
- Instrument M2 where feasible, collect M3 on every exception, track M4 for all cases, and use M5 to test backup ownership in one high-utilization region.

**Threshold for broader rollout — all must hold for the specific segment for at least 8 consecutive weeks**
- Median no worse than baseline and p90/p95 not more than 5% worse than segment baseline; for expansion beyond simple houses, p90 must be non-inferior within 2 days.
- First-time-right 82%, second visit 11%, manual activation 8%, complaint within 30 days 6.5%, SLA breach 13%.
- Estimated fully loaded 60-day cost per case  baseline segment cost, with complete downstream capture.
- Billing before successful activation <1% and only approved exceptions; permit-check conformance 99% with zero unaudited misses in old buildings.
- Coordinator utilization 85% sustained, no absence-related stall cluster, and second-visit team utilization 85%.
- Predictive skip rule false-negative rate low enough that skipped cases meet the same quality/cost gates; if any gate fails, that segment returns to survey-first processing.

This path protects customers and controls now, keeps the demonstrated simple-house gains, and converts the current conflicting process-mining signals into segment-specific evidence management can act on.
