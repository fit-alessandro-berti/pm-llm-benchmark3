# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

**Handover-of-work network.**
What it implies: Mira_Coordinator is a dominant relay point in the directed flow of work. Her out-degree centrality (0.82) and betweenness (0.77) are the highest of any human resource, and the edge table shows she both receives work in bulk from Bot_Rules (920 handovers, the largest bot-to-human edge) and distributes it to essentially every downstream human role: both specialists (Omar_Medical 210, Priya_Legal 180) and all four adjusters (350/330/290/280). She also receives return flows from the specialists (Omar 160, Priya 140) and from both temporaries (190/170). Structurally, she is the hub through which exception work is routed, dispatched, and re-collected.
What it does *not* imply: handover-of-work in PM4Py counts *directly-follows relations between resources within a case*. High handover centrality says nothing about how long she works on a case, whether she adds substantive content, whether she is a decision-maker, or whether removing her would break anything. A pure "forwarding" resource (even a mail-merge script) would look identical. It also does not distinguish routing that follows codified rules from routing that relies on discretionary judgment — that distinction is exactly what the compliance reviewer is worried about, and the network alone cannot resolve it.

**Working-together network.**
What it implies: Mira_Coordinator co-occurs on cases with each adjuster only 4–8% of the time, versus 19–27% for the intra-team lead–adjuster pairs. The stable, high-co-occurrence clusters (Alice–Carla–Elise; Bruno–Diego–Farid) confirm two genuine standing teams. Mira is not a member of either community; she sits outside both.
What it does *not* imply: peripherality here does not mean unimportance. Working-together edge strength is driven by (a) how many cases a resource touches and (b) whether it repeatedly co-occurs with the *same* partners. Mira touches only 22% of claims, and those claims are spread across *both* teams and both specialists, so her co-case rate with any single partner is mechanically diluted. Moreover, the metric counts case co-membership, not interaction intensity or timing — her short routing/reopen/clarification events (noted explicitly in the operational evidence) count as full case participation but represent thin touches. Working-together networks measure community structure, not brokerage; a broker is *by definition* someone with low embeddedness in any one community, so her low scores here are not a contradiction of her high handover centrality — they are its natural complement (see §2).

**Subcontracting network.**
What it implies: the subcontracting pattern (A → B → A within n=2 events) is the strongest evidence that Mira is not a mere forwarder. She hands work to Omar_Medical (126 occurrences) and Priya_Legal (98) and then *reappears on the same case within two events* — the classic delegate-and-resume signature. Her subcontracting betweenness (0.69) dwarfs everyone else's. This says she retains functional ownership of exception cases: she sends a question out to a specialist and takes the case back to decide what happens next (Assign Specialist / Reopen).
What it does *not* imply: subcontracting counts a temporal pattern, not an authority relationship. It cannot tell us whether she is *directing* the specialists (a scheduler/dispatcher), *consulting* them (an owner seeking input), or simply the mandated return-address for the exception queue. It also cannot distinguish healthy consult loops from rework loops caused by her own imprecise initial routing. Note the same signature appears for the team leads with their senior adjusters (86/82) — subcontracting is a normal supervisory pattern in this log, not something unique to Mira; hers is just larger and points at specialists rather than subordinates.

**Performance/time statistics.**
What they imply: her median service time per event is short (4.2 min) — consistent with routing/coordination rather than case work — but her *cycle-time contribution is "high variance"*, and the natural-experiment evidence is the most probative item in the whole dataset: during her 6-day absence, ordinary claims were unaffected while the 90th-percentile cycle time of exception-flagged cases rose from 8.4 to 13.9 days (+65%). That cleanly separates her function from routine flow and locates it precisely in the exception tail. Her exception exposure (48% of her cases flagged, vs. ~0.19–0.36 for most others; only the specialists are higher) confirms she works the hard segment, and 64% of reopens / 71% of complaints pass through her.
What they do *not* imply: the absence experiment is n=1, 6 days, uncontrolled. Queues can accumulate for other reasons (seasonality, a fraud spike, specialist leave overlapping hers). It shows exception throughput is *currently coupled to her presence*; it does not show her way of working is optimal, nor that the function could not be performed by a rules-based router or a trained backup. High variance in cycle-time contribution could reflect case difficulty rather than her performance. Also note that the largest *raw* time components in the process sit with the vendors (Vendor_Verify 2.1 h median, Vendor_Pay 1.3 h, both "high waiting-time component") — any bottleneck narrative about Mira must be sized against those, because for ordinary claims the vendor legs, not Mira, dominate elapsed time.

**Role-discovery / activity-profile similarity.**
What it implies: `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` clusters resources by the multiset of activities they execute. Mira's profile (Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, Assign Specialist) overlaps partially with the team leads (Reopen), with the junior adjusters and Temp_1 (Request Missing Information), and contains activities nobody else performs (Escalate Complaint, Assign Specialist). She would likely emerge as a singleton role or an awkward hybrid — activity-based similarity would place her *near no one*. That is itself informative: the organization has exactly one person doing this bundle, which is the formal definition of a key-person dependency at the role level.
What it does *not* imply: activity labels are conventions. "Assign Specialist" appears only in her row and in fragment descriptions, not in the canonical 14-activity list — suggesting either a logging extension used only by her tooling or an activity recorded inconsistently. Role discovery inherits every recording artifact: if her queue tool auto-logs events under her ID (batch reassignments, mass reopens), the discovered "role" is partly an artifact of instrumentation, not behavior. Similarity metrics also ignore *when* and *why* activities occur, so they cannot distinguish "gatekeeper who reopens for quality reasons" from "clerk who reopens on request."

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

**H1. Formal coordinator / exception desk (the by-design reading).**
- *Mechanism:* Bot_Rules routes cases that trip exception logic to a designated exception desk; Mira staffs it. Her job is to diagnose the exception, obtain missing information, engage the right specialist, and re-inject the case into normal flow. High handover/subcontracting centrality is the intended shape of the design.
- *Supporting evidence:* the Bot_Rules → Mira edge is annotated "exception desk routing" and is the largest bot-to-human edge (920); her exception-flag share (0.48) is far above team leads; the process owner's description ("queue cleaner"), while dismissive, is consistent with a formally assigned queue; the Bot_Rules ↔ Mira subcontracting loop (62, "Fraud Screen → manual exception → Fraud Screen update") looks like a designed human-in-the-loop checkpoint on the rules engine.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* a purely formal desk role would usually come with a documented mandate, a backup, and bounded discretion — yet the absence experiment shows no functioning backup, the compliance reviewer says her specialist-selection discretion is uncontrolled, and adjusters describe her value as tacit knowledge ("knows where hard cases should go"), which exceeds a clerk mandate.
- *Predictions:* if true, a documented routing policy exists and her assignments should be largely reproducible from case attributes (case_type, amount_band, exception_flag). A decision-tree fit of her Assign Specialist choices against case attributes should achieve high accuracy. New exception-desk staff should converge to her routing distribution within weeks.

**H2. Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency.**
- *Mechanism:* whatever the intended design, the exception pathway has degraded into a single-threaded queue: all exception diagnosis, specialist access, reopens, and complaints serialize through one person, with no redundancy.
- *Supporting evidence:* the absence experiment (+65% at P90 for exception cases; queues "accumulated"); betweenness 0.77 on handover and 0.69 on subcontracting with no comparable second node; 64% of reopens and 71% of complaints touch her; she is a singleton in activity-profile space, so no one else can absorb her work without retraining.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* her *median* service time is only 4.2 min, and ordinary claims were unaffected by her absence — so she is a bottleneck only for the exception tail, not the process; the raw waiting-time mass sits with Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay; "high variance" cycle-time contribution could mean many cases pass through her fast. A bottleneck claim also needs queue-length/waiting-time evidence on her worklist specifically, which we only have indirectly (the absence period).
- *Predictions:* if true, waiting time *before* her events should grow with her daily intake (utilization-dependent queueing); exception-case cycle time should correlate with her workload calendar; planned absences should reproduce the P90 degradation; adding a trained second coordinator should cut exception-tail cycle time even with no other change.

**H3. Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker (resolves the central-vs-peripheral "contradiction").**
- *Mechanism:* the organization consists of cohesive clusters — Team North, Team South, the specialist pair, the temps, the vendors — that do not talk to each other directly. Mira occupies the structural holes between them: she moves cases *across* community boundaries. Brokerage produces exactly the observed signature: very high betweenness on directed-flow networks (every cross-cluster path runs through her) combined with low weighted degree in the working-together network (she belongs to no cluster and repeats with no fixed partner). Add the measurement effect — her events are short and she touches only 22% of cases, spread thinly across all partners — and the "contradiction" disappears entirely: **handover betweenness measures flow brokerage; working-together degree measures community embeddedness; a broker is high on the first and low on the second by construction.**
- *Supporting evidence:* she is the *only* human bridge between the bot layer and the specialists (no Alice/Bruno → Omar/Priya edges appear in the top handover edges at all); Omar–Priya co-occur in only 4% of cases, meaning the specialists themselves are siloed and reachable mainly via her; adjusters across *both* teams receive reassignments from her at similar rates (350/330/290/280), i.e., she spans the North–South boundary that the team leads do not cross.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* boundary spanning is usually accompanied by knowledge transfer and relationship maintenance, which we cannot see in an event log; alternatively her cross-team distribution may be enforced by the queue tool (round-robin), making the "spanning" mechanical rather than social. The top-edges table is truncated — rarer direct team-lead → specialist edges may exist below the cutoff, overstating her monopoly.
- *Predictions:* if true, removing her (without replacement) should not just slow exceptions but *fragment* the network: direct team → specialist edges should appear, formal Escalate Complaint events should rise (the operations manager predicts exactly this), and specialist workload should become imbalanced. Community-detection on the working-together graph should show modularity increase when her events are removed.

**H4. Quality gatekeeper / informal control point.**
- *Mechanism:* her Reopen Claim and Escalate Complaint concentration means she functions as a post-hoc quality control: she detects defective closures/outcomes and forces rework, acting as an informal second line of defense.
- *Supporting evidence:* 64% of reopens flow through her; Temp_2 → Mira handovers are annotated "closure exceptions" (temps close claims, she catches bad closures); the Fraud Screen update loop with Bot_Rules suggests she corrects or overrides automated screening.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* reopens routed through her may simply be a workflow rule ("all reopens go to the exception desk"), not her judgment; we have no outcome data (were her reopens justified? did reopened claims end differently?); gatekeeping without a mandate is itself a control weakness, so this hypothesis and the compliance concern are two faces of the same evidence.
- *Predictions:* if true, claims she reopens should show measurably different final outcomes (payment corrections, fraud confirmations) than a matched control group; her reopen rate should track upstream quality (e.g., rise when temps handle more closures).

**H5. Data/instrumentation artifact (partial).**
- *Mechanism:* part of her centrality is manufactured by logging: a shared exception-queue account or her assignment tool writes short system events under her org:resource ID, inflating out-degree and handover counts while contributing little real work.
- *Supporting evidence:* the log's own annotation that many of her events are short "routing/assignment" records; median service 4.2 min with 960 events across 905 cases (almost never twice in the same case — thin touches); "Assign Specialist" exists only in her profile, hinting at tool-generated events; Bot_Rules → Mira mean elapsed 3.5 min is compatible with auto-assignment.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* the absence experiment is fatal to a *pure*-artifact reading — a logging artifact does not cause a 65% P90 increase when the person goes on leave; colleagues attribute real tacit knowledge to her. So the artifact can only inflate the *magnitude* of her centrality, not explain away its substance.
- *Predictions:* if partly true, intra-case time between her consecutive events should show sub-minute bursts (batch processing); events logged under her ID should continue (from the tool/shared account) during her documented absences; field-level audit of the source system would show system-generated writer IDs.

**H6. Specialist scheduler / capacity allocator.**
- *Mechanism:* Omar and Priya are scarce (330/260 events, very high service times); someone must ration them. Mira is the de-facto admission control for specialist capacity, deciding which cases merit a 37–51 minute expert review.
- *Supporting evidence:* essentially all specialist inflow in the top edges comes from her; the subcontracting loops show she sequences specialist consults and resumes the case; the operations manager's warning that removing her would cause "specialist overload" presumes she is currently filtering demand.
- *Contradicting/limiting evidence:* we cannot observe rejected requests (cases she *didn't* send to specialists) in these tables; scheduling may be trivial (all medical exceptions → Omar) rather than discretionary.
- *Predictions:* if true, specialist utilization should be smooth while she is present and spiky/overloaded in her absence; the exception-case backlog during her absence should be concentrated in specialist-bound cases specifically.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. The best-calibrated composite: she is a **formally seated exception desk (H1) whose practice has evolved into cross-boundary brokerage (H3) with de-facto gatekeeping and specialist rationing (H4/H6), creating a genuine key-person dependency (H2), with some centrality inflation from instrumentation (H5)**. The evidence weights H1+H3+H2 most strongly; H4/H6 are plausible but under-evidenced; H5 is a modifier, not an explanation.

# 3. Risk assessment

**Bottleneck risk — moderate, segment-specific, well evidenced.** The absence experiment shows exception-flagged cases (roughly a fifth to a third of volume depending on the resource lens) degrade sharply without her, while ordinary claims are untouched. This is a *tail* bottleneck, not a throughput bottleneck. Calibration: keep in mind that the largest absolute waiting-time components in the whole process are the vendor legs (Vendor_Verify 2.1 h median service with high waiting; Vendor_Pay similar); fixing Mira's queue would not fix ordinary-claim cycle time.

**Coordination risk — moderate-to-high.** All cross-cluster coordination (bots→humans on exceptions, teams↔specialists, temps→escalation) currently runs through one node. If she is unavailable, mis-calibrated, or overloaded, the failure mode is not just delay but misrouting and fragmentation — the operations manager's "more formal escalations, specialist overload" scenario. This risk is structural and visible directly in the network topology.

**Key-person risk — high, the clearest finding.** She is a singleton in activity-profile space, has no observed backup, and holds tacit routing knowledge that colleagues explicitly name. A 6-day absence produced measurable harm; a permanent departure would be worse and slower to repair. This risk exists *regardless of which role hypothesis is true*.

**Compliance/control risk — real but currently unquantified; no evidence of wrongdoing.** The compliance reviewer's concern is a segregation-of-duties design issue: the same person detects exceptions, selects the specialist, and can reopen closed claims. In a regulated claims process, detection, assignment, and reopening are three control-relevant powers concentrated without documented criteria or four-eyes review. The event data show *opportunity*, not *abuse*: nothing in the tables indicates biased routing, favoritism toward outcomes, or improper reopens. The claim should be phrased as "uncontrolled discretion exists" — a finding about the control environment — not as suspicion about the person.

**Workload fairness risk — moderate.** She handles 48% exception-flagged cases (versus 0.21–0.26 for the team leads), absorbs complaints and reopens, and shows "high variance" cycle-time contribution — a profile associated with burnout. Symmetrically, her reassignment flows to adjusters (350/330/290/280) shape *their* workloads with no visible balancing rule; junior adjusters (Elise/Farid) receive nearly the same rework volume as seniors despite lower capacity. If her routing embeds unexamined habits, unfairness compounds silently.

**Data-quality risk — moderate and analytically important.** (a) "Assign Specialist" is not in the canonical activity list — inconsistent activity vocabulary will distort any role discovery. (b) Short tool-generated events under her ID may inflate her centrality (H5). (c) The working-together metric is known (from the log's own notes) to under-represent her — any dashboard built naively on co-occurrence would mislabel her as peripheral, which is presumably how the "just a queue cleaner" narrative survives. (d) Vendor resources aggregate many external individuals under two IDs, so *their* network positions are under-resolved too; comparisons of human vs. vendor centrality are not apples-to-apples.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

**Handover-of-work network.** Compute `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` separately on the sublogs `exception_flag=True` vs. `False` (via `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values`), and per 4-week time window. Prediction tests: under H1/H3 her centrality should be concentrated almost entirely in the exception sublog; time-windowed networks covering her 6-day absence reveal which edges appeared as substitutes (did Alice/Bruno → Omar/Priya edges emerge?). Visualize with `pm4py.view_sna` / `save_vis_sna`.

**Working-together network.** Recompute `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` (i) restricted to exception-flagged cases and (ii) after filtering out sub-minute "routing/assignment" events. If her weighted degree jumps in (i) and (ii), the peripherality is confirmed as a dilution/measurement effect (H3/H5) rather than genuine non-involvement. Additionally run community detection (e.g., NetworkX Louvain on the exported graph) and measure the modularity change when her node is removed — a large increase quantifies her bridging role.

**Subcontracting network.** Run `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` with n=1, 2, 3 and stratified by case_type and exception_flag. Distinguish *consult loops* (Mira → specialist → Mira followed by forward progress) from *rework loops* (followed by Reopen/Request Missing Information again). A rising share of rework loops would support the bottleneck/quality-problem readings over the healthy-broker reading. Also test whether Bot_Rules ↔ Mira loops (Fraud Screen overrides) cluster in specific amount_bands or regions — a control-relevant pattern.

**Activity-based resource similarity and role discovery.** Use `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` on the cleaned log (after harmonizing "Assign Specialist"). Verify whether Mira is a singleton role. Then, the key follow-up: train an interpretable classifier (decision tree on case_type, priority, amount_band, region, channel, exception_flag) to predict *which specialist/adjuster she assigns*. High, stable accuracy → her routing is rule-expressible (supports H1 and makes a backup or partial automation feasible; also mitigates the compliance concern by making criteria explicit). Low accuracy → tacit discretion (supports H3, strengthens key-person and compliance risks).

**Filtering by exception_flag, activity, variants, time ranges.** (a) `pm4py.filter_variants` / variant analysis on reopened cases: compare variants of reopens that pass through Mira vs. those that do not — do her reopens correct different defects? (b) Filter to the absence window and the two weeks after: quantify backlog drain time and whether misrouting (extra handovers per case) rose. (c) Filter Escalate Complaint cases and test the operations manager's claim: is formal-escalation frequency lower in periods when her informal routing throughput is higher?

**Service/waiting-time analysis.** Use `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` to decompose exception-case cycle time into: waiting before Mira's first touch, her service time, waiting for specialists, specialist service, vendor waiting. This sizes her queue against the vendor and specialist queues — the single most important calibration for the bottleneck claim. Complement with a utilization/queueing view: daily intake to her queue vs. waiting time before her events (a convex relationship is the signature of a saturating server, H2). Sequence mining or a simple survival analysis on time-to-first-Mira-touch during vs. outside her absence completes the natural experiment.

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately (low regret, addresses the highest-confidence risks):**
1. **Create redundancy for the exception-desk function.** Cross-train at least one team lead or senior adjuster as a formal backup, and have them shadow-route a sample of live exception cases now — before the next absence. This targets key-person risk, the clearest finding, without altering the process.
2. **Document the routing and reopen criteria.** Have Mira externalize her assignment logic (workshop + the decision-tree analysis from §4). Publishing criteria simultaneously mitigates the compliance concern (discretion becomes reviewable), preserves the coordination function, and creates training material.
3. **Add a lightweight control on the detect-assign-reopen concentration:** periodic sample review (four-eyes) of her reopens and specialist assignments by a team lead or compliance. This addresses the segregation-of-duties gap without removing her from the role — an audit trail, not a workflow change.
4. **Fix the logging:** harmonize "Assign Specialist" into the activity vocabulary and tag tool-generated events, so future organizational analyses are trustworthy.

**Monitor (decide later with data):**
- Waiting time in front of Mira's queue and the P90 cycle time of exception-flagged cases, weekly (the KPI the absence experiment validated).
- Specialist (Omar/Priya) utilization and overload signals, since she is their de-facto admission control.
- Formal Escalate Complaint frequency, as the leading indicator that informal coordination is failing.
- Distribution of her reassignments across adjusters (fairness) and the consult-vs-rework ratio in her subcontracting loops (quality of routing).
- Her own workload and exception share, for burnout risk.

**Do not change yet (insufficient evidence):**
- **Do not remove or reassign Mira, and do not automate her routing.** The absence experiment shows the coordination function is load-bearing, and we do not yet know whether her routing is rule-expressible (H1) or tacit (H3). Removing her before the backup exists and the criteria are documented would predictably reproduce the absence-period degradation permanently; the operations manager's warning is consistent with the network evidence. Any future removal/automation plan must specify how brokerage between the bot layer, the teams, and the specialists is replaced.
- **Do not restructure the two adjuster teams or the specialist pool** on the basis of these networks; the co-working clusters look healthy, and the true capacity constraints (vendors, specialists) need the §4 waiting-time decomposition first.
- **Do not act on the compliance concern beyond the monitoring control above** — there is opportunity for uncontrolled discretion, but no evidence of misuse; heavier intervention should wait for the routing-criteria analysis and reopen-outcome comparison.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira looks contradictory in our analyses — extremely central in some network views, marginal in others — but the contradiction is an artifact of what each view measures. Handover and subcontracting networks measure *flow*: nearly every difficult claim (exceptions, reopens, complaints) passes through her on its way between the automated triage, the two adjuster teams, and the medical/legal specialists. The working-together network measures *membership in a stable team*: she belongs to none, because her job is to bridge them with many short touches. Both views are correct: she is a broker, not a teammate. The practical consequences: when she was away for six days, ordinary claims were unaffected but hard cases slowed dramatically — evidence of a real single-person dependency. There is also a control gap: one person both detects problems and chooses who handles them, without documented criteria. We recommend keeping the function, training a backup, writing down her routing rules, adding a light review of her reopens and assignments, and monitoring the exception-case queue — before considering any structural change.
