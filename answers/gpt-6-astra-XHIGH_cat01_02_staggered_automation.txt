## 1. What is observed

Automation became available in Alpha and Beta in April and Gamma in July; Delta and Epsilon remained without availability through August. The recorded automated-fast-path share nevertheless equals 4–6% before adoption and in untreated branches. This discrepancy requires clarification, potentially concerning legacy automation or metric definitions. After adoption, this share rises to 48%, 50%, and 32%, respectively. It measures completed-path composition, excluding automated escalations, rather than all automated triage attempts or randomized treatment receipt.

Alpha and Beta were selected for high volume and poor historical cycle times; Gamma followed a readiness pilot. The rollout therefore spans different branches, calendar conditions, and lengths of exposure. Alpha and Beta have five post-adoption months; Gamma has two.

A shared fraud desk opened in March. Adoption coincided with routing increases, while staffing changes, a May product change, and rising recorded complexity also occurred. Productive FTEs and arrivals describe available resources and incoming demand, but do not measure service capacity, backlog, or utilization directly.

The outcomes are monthly aggregates. Averages of monthly medians or p90s are not pooled case-level quantiles. Rework and escalation percentages lack event detail and verified case denominators. Customer satisfaction describes survey respondents; varying response rates and missing response counts prevent recovery of the all-customer mean or its sampling uncertainty. Case-cohort definitions, unfinished-case handling, specialist waits, and fraud-desk waits are unavailable.

## 2. Causal estimands

Define availability at the branch level and actual assignment at case registration separately. For E1 and E3–E8, specify the intervention as the **rollout package as delivered**: availability, implementation support and workflow adaptations, and the adoption-linked routing campaign. Its comparator defers that package. Unrelated product and staffing schedules belong in both counterfactuals. This is an availability-policy effect, analogous to an intention-to-treat effect but without random assignment; it is not the effect of software alone.

Let Z denote exposure to other branches' use of the shared fraud desk and any redistribution of incoming cases through routing. The branch contrasts below hold other branches' deployment schedules fixed and include the treated branch's own downstream queue consequences. Outcomes may depend on those schedules, so effects need not transport to simultaneous expansion. The data do not distinguish all implementation versions.

For distributional outcomes, the package can change who arrives through routing. Thus a total policy effect includes composition changes. A standardized effect on a fixed incoming population is another estimand and requires case-level pre-triage characteristics. Natural uptake and induced queues are mediators of availability; adjusting them away changes the question.

The primary strategy respects the two adoption cohorts and keeps already-treated branches out of the counterfactual comparison. It permits effects to differ by branch and exposure month without imposing one constant coefficient. That matters because uptake rises after launch and Gamma contributes only two treated months. An event-study plot must show those support limits rather than suggest a balanced five-month learning curve.

With five branches, transparency is more valuable than elaborate propensity models or synthetic weights estimated from three early pre-months. For cohort g and month t, calculate the adopter change since g−1 minus the same-calendar change in the donor average. Report individual branches alongside cohort averages. Baseline averaging and donor exclusion are useful sensitivity analyses, not interchangeable definitions of the counterfactual.

This approach remains assumption-dependent. Targeting weak branches creates regression-to-the-mean risk; rollout-linked routing belongs to the package, while unrelated differential operational shocks threaten its identification. Calendar differencing handles only shocks with sufficiently similar effects. Shared fraud-desk congestion can contaminate even never-treated donors. Accordingly, the supplied data support descriptive cohort-time contrasts and a conditional causal argument, not a validated causal estimate. Prospective randomization is preferable for the next decision; the historical event study organizes evidence and exposes the assumptions that evaluation must repair.

Thus, Alpha/Beta's observed tail deterioration can coexist with possible mitigation relative to a worsening counterfactual. It does not demonstrate an absolute tail improvement. Equal-weighting the three adopter-specific D values gives −4.29 median hours, −6.59 p90 hours, −2.00 rework pp, +0.89 escalation pp, and +0.089 respondent-CSAT points. This is a summary of these particular branch/window contrasts, not a common-duration or organization-wide effect.

Using the primary event-study reference month instead—March for early adopters and June for Gamma—the average post-month median/p90 contrasts are Alpha −5.30/−5.81 hours, Beta −3.04/−5.59, and Gamma −3.73/−6.80. At event month zero, the early cohort averages −2.25/−4.10 hours versus Gamma's −3.65/−6.70. The differing later patterns do not support imposing a common learning curve.

**Treatment-intensity analysis.** From April to August, Alpha and Beta each gain 23 pp in completed automated-fast-path share. Their median changes are −5.0 and −2.3 hours, but p90 changes are +3.9 and +3.6 hours. Gamma's July–August intensity rises 7 pp while its median/p90 fall 2.4/4.4 hours. Over those same two months, donors fall 2.25/4.20 hours, leaving Gamma only −0.15/−0.20 hours of relative improvement. Rising recorded intensity alone is weak evidence of a dose effect.

A descriptive equal-weight branch/month fixed-effects regression on intensity, scaled per 10 pp, yields:

| outcome | intensity association: branch and month effects | additionally condition on arrivals/FTE and recorded complexity |
|---|---:|---:|
| Median hours | −1.30 | −0.62 |
| p90 hours | −1.84 | −1.01 |
| Rework pp | −0.53 | −0.24 |
| Escalation pp | +0.25 | +0.12 |
| CSAT points | +0.026 | +0.010 |

The change under adjustment illustrates specification sensitivity; neither column estimates a causal dose response. Completed fast-path share is endogenous to case eligibility, successful completion, congestion, and learning. Load and case mix can themselves be consequences of the rollout package. These regressions also impose a common linear relationship despite staggered, potentially heterogeneous effects. No instrumental-variable ratio is warranted: availability changes routing, workflow, and queue exposure beyond fast-path use, violating a credible exclusion restriction.

**Pre-trends and regression to the mean.** From January to March, Alpha's median/p90 change is −1.0/−1.9 hours, Beta's is +1.0/+1.7, and donors' is +0.3/+0.5. Gamma changes +3.2/+5.9 before its later rollout. The gaps therefore are not perfectly stable. Three early pre-months, including the fraud-desk launch, offer little power to establish parallel untreated trends. A nonsignificant pre-trend test would not validate them.

Selection for historical poor performance can produce improvement without treatment, especially when the selection history is missing. Gamma's June-to-post median drop is 8.70 hours, but donors drop 4.98 hours over the same comparison. Relative improvement is 3.73 hours. This directly shows why choosing a peak month exaggerates the raw improvement; it does not establish that the remaining difference is causal.

For the last row, D = treatment effect + B, where B is the adopter-minus-donor untreated trend difference. The assumed drift allowances are on the scale of observed operational movements: donors' median increases 5.22 hours over the early window, while their p90 falls 7.95 hours between May–June and July–August. Those common movements do not prove differential drift of that size; they make this a relevant stress test in a panel with different staffing and risk changes. Allowing wider B widens the intervals. A favorable unobserved relative drift of 4.29 median hours or 6.59 p90 hours would eliminate the respective summary benefit.

Customer satisfaction needs an additional selection sensitivity. Suppose, solely for illustration, response rates were 30% in every comparison cell and the all-customer population were otherwise comparable. With respondent D = +0.089, an unobserved nonrespondent contrast between −0.20 and +0.20 points gives an all-customer contrast of 0.30 × 0.089 + 0.70 × [−0.20, +0.20] = **[−0.113, +0.167] points**. Actual varying response rates introduce further uncertainty. Even a known monthly rate r and respondent mean m only bound the all-customer mean by [r m + (1−r) × 1, r m + (1−r) × 5] without assumptions about nonrespondents. The observed respondent gain cannot establish a customer-wide benefit.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Start with a capped Delta pilot: its lower recorded complexity and recent productive-staffing increase make it a reasonable first operating test. Defer Epsilon's broad rollout until a safety gate, then evaluate it separately because its complexity reaches 38%. This sequencing is operational prioritization, not random branch assignment.

Before launch, identify independently staffed service units with stable case routing. Within Delta, randomize matched units to immediate availability or a 12-week delay; repeat this design in Epsilon after the gate. Allocate cases to units before triage and analyze all registrations by assigned unit. Keep training, eligibility, escalation safeguards, and planned routing changes explicit and common across comparable arms. This initially tests a specified automation protocol rather than recreating the historical routing package. If independent units are unavailable, randomize eligible cases at registration and acknowledge that the result estimates assignment effects under shared queues, not the total branch-capacity effect. Two branch clusters alone cannot support precise policy inference. Size the trial using unit-level variation and intracluster correlation; case counts cannot substitute for independent units. Shared-desk exposure also limits inference about full network saturation.

Obtain at least 12 weekly pre-period observations and 12 post-period weeks, with four ramp-up weeks reported separately and eight mature weeks. Retrieve a full prior year where available for seasonal context. Follow registration cohorts through closure plus 30 days; extend observation if tail precision, backlog clearance, or quality-event counts are insufficient.

Collect case IDs, registration risk and product, assignment, attempted automation, overrides, complete activity timestamps, specialist/fraud destinations, service and wait times, productive hours, backlog ages, reopens, complaints, and survey invitation/response counts. Report median and p90 separately, SLA breaches, validated resolution quality, and completions per productive hour. Track desk inflow, staffing, queue age, and branch-specific delays across all five branches; assess spillovers explicitly.

Prespecify operating thresholds: immediate review or suspension after serious preventable customer/fraud harm; otherwise pause allocation increases if p90 rises over 10%, rework/reopens over 2 pp, or consistently measured CSAT falls over 0.15 points against concurrent controls. Scale beyond the pilot only after mature-period evidence supports at least a 5% median improvement or 5% productivity gain, with uncertainty excluding those harm margins and no material network-queue deterioration. These are proposed decision thresholds, not estimated benefits. If evidence remains inconclusive, continue the controlled phase and add independent units.

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Availability and recorded automated-fast-path shares increased; Alpha/Beta's average monthly p90 times rose about 10%, while median and customer outcomes varied by branch.
- Suggestive: Same-calendar comparisons favor adopters on median, p90, recorded rework, and respondent satisfaction, while specialist escalation rises; routing, staffing, case mix, selection, and spillovers remain competing explanations.
- Conditionally causal: With parallel untreated trends, no anticipation, consistent measurement, and no differential donor spillovers or unrelated shocks, cohort-time contrasts would estimate the specified rollout-package effects for these adopters and observed horizons.
- Not supported: “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” as a universal software effect, a causal effect of completed fast-path use, or a guaranteed benefit from simultaneous full expansion to Delta and Epsilon.
