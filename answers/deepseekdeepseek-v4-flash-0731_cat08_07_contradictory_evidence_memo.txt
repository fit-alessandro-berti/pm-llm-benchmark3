**To:** Executive Team  
**From:** Process and Operations Strategy  
**Subject:** Redesign R — next decision for fiber-installation process  

**Recommendation:** Do not scale Redesign R nationally as a bundle. Do not roll it back to baseline either. Implement a constrained version now: keep the single-coordinator ownership model and the merged validation/scheduling step for simple, low-risk houses; suspend the predictive field-survey skip; suspend post-billing activation; restore mandatory field surveys for medium/high-risk addresses and mandatory permit checks for old/permit-sensitive buildings. Add backup and load controls for coordinators. In parallel, run targeted measurement to decide what can be safely expanded later.

The pilot is not an all-good or all-bad story. Evidence directly shows that simple, low-risk houses improved strongly: median duration fell by 6 days, p90 fell by 8 days, first-time-right increased by 3 percentage points, and complaints fell by 1 percentage point. But apartment, old-building, business, and rural segments generally got worse on rework, complaints, or tail duration. Apartment medium-risk cases had a 12-day worse p90, first-time-right down 8 percentage points, and complaints up 5 percentage points. Old permit-sensitive buildings had a 25-day worse p90 and first-time-right down 11 percentage points. The overall median improved mainly because the simple-house segment improved and because the new merged path created a faster central route. The tail worsened because complex cases were more likely to be surveyed incorrectly, miss permit checks, fail installation, and require second visits or manual activation.

The process-mining evidence points the same way. The pilot model has high fitness but low precision: it can replay observed cases but also allows too many combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeated visits, and billing-start moves. This is the signature of an underconstrained process, not a stable standardized redesign. Alignments show billing start before successful activation, installation visits repeated without a preceding field survey, missing permit checks, and complaint escalation after billing start. Those are not merely statistical anomalies; they are concrete mechanisms for the rework and complaint increases.

Cost evidence is incomplete but directionally concerning. Visible cost before complaint handling improved from EUR 184 to EUR 161, but the more complete estimate including complaint and revisit handling worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207. Because complaint-handling cost beyond week 4 is excluded, the apparent cost improvement may largely be shifted cost, not saved cost.

The initial beneficial piece of R is not the full bundle; it is the coordinator-ownership model. Handover-heavy cases fell from 34% to 12%. That matters. But the same centralization caused coordinator utilization to reach 93% in two regions and made cases stall when coordinators were absent. We should keep accountability but fix resilience.

**Immediate actions:**

1. Pause the predictive field-survey skip everywhere.
2. Pause activation checks after billing start everywhere.
3. Require successful activation test before billing start.
4. Restore field surveys for medium/high-risk addresses and mandatory permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings.
5. Keep coordinator ownership, but add a documented backup owner and a utilization cap to avoid single-point failure.
6. Start collecting structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, second visits, and complaints.
7. Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start.
8. Run conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit-check absence.

Broader rollout should be allowed only when targeted evidence shows that a component is safe for a defined segment: first-time-right and complaints no worse than baseline, tail duration not increasing, total downstream cost no worse, and no unresolved process-integrity violations. Until then, we should scale the safe core, suspend the risky rules, and learn from the pilot rather than treating it as a final answer.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

These results look contradictory only if we treat the metrics as measuring one simple “better or worse” outcome. In fact, they measure different parts of the process, different segments, and different time horizons.

### High fitness but low precision

Fitness and precision answer different questions:

- Fitness asks: “Can the model reproduce the event sequences we observed?”
- Precision asks: “Does the model avoid allowing event sequences we did not observe?”

The pilot model has high fitness because it can replay most observed pilot cases. It has low precision because it also permits many combinations that are not consistent with a disciplined process: skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits, billing start before activation, missing permits. That is not a contradiction. It is a sign that the process became more variable and less standardized. The model fits the mess because it is flexible enough to allow it.

### Improved median but worse tail duration

The median is a middle-of-the-distribution measure. It can improve while the worst cases get worse if the distribution shifts in different directions for different segments. That is exactly what happened:

- Simple houses improved substantially.
- Apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural cases often had worse p90 or p95 durations.

The new fast path made many simple cases quicker, pulling the median down. But the same redesign increased failures, second visits, and complaint loops in complex cases, pushing the tail out. The p90 and p95 are more sensitive to those long-delay cases. So the central tendency improved while the tail worsened.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Handover count is a structural metric: how many times work passes from one person or team to another. It went down because the coordinator owned more of the case. But resilience is different. Resilience depends on whether the organization can absorb absences, workload spikes, and unexpected exceptions.

The pilot shows high coordinator utilization, stalled cases when coordinators were absent, and a more centralized working-together network. Fewer handovers reduced one type of friction but created a single point of failure. A process can have fewer handovers and still be more brittle if too much context is locked inside one role.

### Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

The cost result depends on where you stop the measurement clock.

The “visible cost” of EUR 161 stops before downstream complaint handling. The redesign moved some activation checks after billing start, so issues that would previously have been caught before billing could now appear later as complaints, manual activations, or second visits. Those costs are partially outside the visible window.

The more inclusive estimate, EUR 207, is worse than baseline, and it is explicitly incomplete. On the rework side, first-time-right is down, second visits are up, and complaints are up. That is strong evidence that the cheaper visible cost is not a real saving; it is a cost-shifting artifact.

### Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

The segment-level results show heterogeneous treatment effects. Simple low-risk houses have low information complexity: a skipped survey and merged scheduling are unlikely to hide a wrong installation. Apartments and old buildings are different: installation depends on building age, permits, access, signal conditions, and coordination with building managers.

When the redesign skipped surveys or omitted permit checks in those contexts, the initial process looked faster but later failed more often. The overall average hides this because the large simple-house segment improved enough to offset the losses elsewhere. This is not a paradox; it is a warning against averaging across fundamentally different case types.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision:** Implement a constrained version of Redesign R now. This is essentially **C with E guardrails**, supported by the D measurement agenda.

### Immediate action

- Keep the single-coordinator ownership model.
- Keep merged address validation and appointment scheduling for simple, low-risk houses.
- Require successful activation test before billing start.
- Require field survey for all medium/high-risk addresses.
- Require permit checks for old or permit-sensitive buildings.
- Add a backup owner for every coordinator case to prevent stalling during absence.
- Set a sustained coordinator utilization cap, for example below 85%, to reduce bottleneck risk.

### What to pause

- Pause all national scaling of Redesign R as a bundle.
- Pause the predictive field-survey skip rule.
- Pause activation checks after billing start.
- Pause any rollout that would apply the same process to apartments, old buildings, and simple houses without segment-specific safeguards.

### What to scale

- Scale the coordinator-ownership model with shared context and backup ownership.
- Scale the simplified validation/scheduling path only for cases where the evidence supports it: simple, low-risk houses.
- Scale measurement and conformance monitoring so that further rollout is based on evidence, not averages.

### What to measure next

- Launch **M6** immediately to validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes.
- Launch **M3** immediately to collect structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, second visits, and complaints.
- Start **M4** to measure full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start.
- Add **M7** conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit-check absence.
- If M6 indicates a potentially safe stratum, design **M1** as a randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.
- Run **M5** when expanding coordinator ownership beyond the pilot, to test shared-team ownership and absence handling.

### Threshold for broader rollout

Wider rollout should be triggered only when:

- **Survey skipping** can be shown to be non-inferior on first-time-right, complaints, and p90 duration in a defined risk segment.
- **Activation before billing** has no conformance violations, and manual activation or complaint rates return to baseline levels.
- **Full 60-day downstream cost** is no worse than baseline for the same segment.
- **Coordinator utilization** remains sustainable and cases do not stall during coordinator absence.
- **SLA breach** and tail-duration metrics are not worse in the segment being expanded.

Until those thresholds are met, the right strategy is to act on what is already clear, protect the segments that were harmed, preserve the accountability gains that are valuable, and use targeted measurement to decide how much of Redesign R can eventually be scaled.
