# 1. Data audit and estimands

**Observation — system and units.** The service center is open 07:00–19:00, i.e. 12 operating hours or 720 minutes per day. No service occurs while closed, but cases can be in the system overnight. The daily table covers 2026-04-06 to 2026-05-03, 28 days, one queue, with four calendar weeks.

The primitive daily quantities are:

- $\lambda_d$: arrival intensity, $\text{arrivals}_d/12$ cases per open hour, or $\text{arrivals}_d/720$ per open minute.
- $\mu^{\text{obs}}_d$: observed completion intensity, $\text{completed}_d/12$ cases per open hour. This is throughput on that day, not necessarily intrinsic service capacity, because completions depend on backlog, staffing, outage, and case mix.
- $S$: active handling/service time per case, excluding waiting. Only $\text{median}(S)$ and $p90(S)$ are supplied per day, not $\mathbb E[S]$.
- $L_d$: $\text{mean\_wip}_d$, mean number in system from 15-minute snapshots during, presumably, opening hours. It is a snapshot mean, not necessarily a continuous-time time-average over 24 hours.
- $c_d$: $\text{staff\_fte}_d$, mean concurrently available FTE capacity. It is not a count of identical parallel servers unless FTE can be treated as such, which is an assumption.
- $\text{complex\_pct}_d$: share of arrivals coded as multi-step complex variant.
- $\text{peak\_08\_10\_pct}_d$: share of daily arrivals in 08:00–09:59, two of twelve open hours. Under a uniform intraday arrival profile this share would be $2/12=16.7\%$; observed values are far above that on weekdays.
- $\text{outage\_min}_d$: minutes of outage that day; only 2026-04-15 has a nonzero value, 180 minutes, with representative hourly data locating it at approximately 11:00–14:00.

**Estimands that are defensible from these aggregates** are descriptive regime summaries: arrivals/day and per open hour, completions/day and per open hour, net flow $\text{completed}-\text{arrivals}$, median and p90 service, snapshot WIP, staffing, mix, and peak concentration. Also defensible are ratios clearly labelled as proxies, e.g. a *median-based offered-load index*

$$
\text{load index}_d \;=\; \frac{\text{arrivals}_d \times \text{median\_service\_min}_d}{\text{staff\_fte}_d \times 720},
$$

which is **not** true utilization $\rho=\lambda \mathbb E[S]/(c)$ because the median is substituted for the unknown mean and because FTE may not equal identical servers. Because service distributions are right-skewed (p90 is roughly 1.8–2.4$\times$ the median), using the median almost surely understates $\mathbb E[S]$ and therefore understates load. The index is useful only for comparisons under a roughly similar distributional shape, and even then the p90/median ratio changes across regimes, so shape is not constant.

**At least five threats to naive inference:**

1. **Median/p90 are not the mean.** Queueing capacity and Little's Law require means ($\mathbb E[S]$ for offered load, mean flow time $W$ for $L=\lambda W$). Substituting median service for mean flow time conflates two different quantities — active handling vs. waiting-plus-handling — and additionally substitutes a quantile for a mean. Both substitutions are invalid without a stated distributional assumption.
2. **Unknown starting backlog.** Backlog at 07:00 is not supplied. Daily net flow $\text{completed}-\text{arrivals}$ changes backlog by that amount only if there is no abandonment/reneging/cancellation, which is not stated (Assumption A1 below). A day with completions $>$ arrivals may simply be draining inherited backlog, not exhibiting higher intrinsic capacity.
3. **Carry-over and boundary mismatch.** Completed cases need not have arrived the same day. Daily arrivals, completions, and snapshot WIP therefore do not form a closed daily system. Little's Law over a window needs boundary terms (initial/final inventory) or a long stable window; short windows with trending WIP violate the usual conditions.
4. **Non-stationarity within and between days.** Intraday arrival profiles are strongly peaked and, in one representative day, bursty beyond a smooth peak. Across days there are at least an outage, an apparent mix change around 2026-04-20, and a staffing intervention starting sometime on 2026-04-29. A single $\lambda$, $\mu$, or $\rho$ pooled over these is an average over different systems.
5. **Intervention timing is not logged.** The staffing intervention began *during* 2026-04-29. That day is therefore a mixture of pre- and post-intervention operation and cannot be cleanly assigned.
6. **Mix measurement ambiguity.** The complex-percentage jump may be a true case-mix shift, a coding change, or both (supplied caution). If coding changed, pre/post comparisons of complex share and even of service times stratified by variant are compromised, because the same physical case may be classified differently.
7. **Serial dependence and small regime samples.** Consecutive days share backlog, staffing rosters, and the mix regime; daily rows are not independent. Several candidate regimes have 2–7 days, so regime means have wide uncertainty and lag statistics are fragile.
8. **Snapshot WIP vs. closed-hours WIP.** Mean WIP from 15-minute snapshots during opening hours misses overnight evolution (no service, presumably no arrivals counted, but cases persist). A 24-hour time-average $L$ and an open-hours snapshot $L$ are different estimands.

**Assumptions used, labelled.** (A1) No abandonment, cancellation, or re-entry uncounted; so $\Delta\text{backlog}_d \approx \text{arrivals}_d-\text{completed}_d$. Needed to interpret net flow as backlog change; if false, net flow also reflects unobserved exits. (A2) Staff FTE is proportional to service capacity within a day-type. Needed for any capacity-pressure comparison; FTE heterogeneity, breaks, and supervision are unobserved. (A3) Snapshot WIP approximates open-hours time-average WIP. Needed for any Little's-Law calculation; 15-minute snapshots at fixed phases could alias with arrival bursts. None of these is verified by the aggregates.

**Outage and recovery treatment.** April 15 is modelled as a capacity shock: arrivals (105) were normal-to-high, but roughly 3 hours of completions were lost, producing a one-day completion deficit of about 42–47 relative to arrivals and to neighbouring throughput. April 16–17 are *not* pooled with normal days: their completion intensities (10.75 and 9.33/open-hour) exceed every baseline weekday because a large backlog guaranteed work was always available and staffing was temporarily raised. Treating them as evidence that "the system can complete 129/day" would overstate sustainable capacity at normal staffing and normal backlog.

**What drives the primary split.** Three separable sources of non-stationarity are visible: *demand* (weekend scale, slow upward drift, peak concentration), *service/mix* (median +40% and p90 +75% around Apr 20 exactly when complex share doubles), and *capacity/backlog* (outage, temporary 7.0 FTE in recovery, intervention). The primary segmentation keeps these axes distinct rather than letting one dimension (e.g. calendar week) absorb the others.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

**Alternative S2 — a two-change-point, demandside segmentation.** Ignore the intervention as a regime boundary and instead segment purely on the joint level of complex share and service time: Block I = Apr 6–19 (low complex, 18–27%, including outage and recovery as internal disturbances), Block II = Apr 20–May 3 (high complex, 39–47%), each split only by weekday/weekend. Under this story, Apr 29–May 3 improvements in completions and WIP are attributed to (a) partial backlog normalisation after the Apr 27–28 peak, (b) the Apr 29 7.0-FTE roster being ordinary roster variation like Apr 16's 7.0, and (c) possible adaptation/learning to the new mix or coding settling in, rather than to a discrete intervention.

A second, more granular alternative (S3) would be a *backlog-state* segmentation: classify each day by inferred state — balanced (WIP 25), stressed (WIP 30–40), saturated (WIP 40) — regardless of calendar cause. This represents the operational story that the queue's behaviour is governed by inherited backlog: when WIP is high, throughput is work-conserving and high; when low, throughput is arrival-limited. It explains Apr 16 (WIP 52, completions 129) and Apr 30 (WIP 36, completions 126) with one mechanism.

The alternatives are not refuted by the data. The honest conclusion is that the data support "something changed near Apr 20" strongly, "outage effects Apr 15–17" strongly, and "the Apr 29 intervention caused the subsequent improvement" only weakly-to-moderately.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

Pooled over all 28 days (computed in §8): arrivals 92.3/day, completions 91.5/day, mean WIP 28.7, median service 30–31 min. These pooled numbers describe no day that actually occurred particularly well. Three obscuring mechanisms:

**(1) Weekday/weekend composition (a weighting artefact).** Weekdays dominate arrivals (20 weekdays) but weekends have roughly 55–60% lower arrivals with only 58% lower staffing (2.5 vs 6.0 FTE). Pooled arrivals/day 92 sits between weekday 107 and weekend 44 and represents neither. Worse, weekends have *higher* median service (29 baseline, 35 post-mix) despite lower WIP, so pooled service statistics blend a day-type effect with regime effects. Any pooled "cases per FTE per day" (15.4 arrivals/FTE-day pooled) hides that baseline weekends run 16.7 arrivals/FTE-day at 2.5 FTE with zero slack for absence, versus 16.7 for baseline weekdays — similar ratios, very different resilience, because a single absence removes 40% of weekend capacity but 17% of weekday capacity.

**(2) Complex-mix change and Simpson's-paradox-like reversal.** Before Apr 20 (excluding outage/recovery distortion), weekday median service is 25 min at complex 20%; after, 35 min at complex 44%. Pooling yields 30 min, a value that misstates capacity needs in both periods: planning staff from pooled median-based load understates post-shift load by roughly a third on the service component alone. The Simpson-like reversal appears in throughput: pooled, higher-complex days are also the higher-arrival days, so a naïve pooled regression of completions on complex share can be flat or positive (busy days complete more because backlog keeps staff busy), while *within* the operationally meaningful comparison — completions minus arrivals, or WIP change — higher-complex periods clearly deteriorate (net 13.4/day in mix-shift pre-intervention weekdays vs 0.6/day baseline weekdays). The sign of the association reverses once backlog state and demand level are accounted for. There is also an unresolvable ambiguity: if the complex jump is partly a *coding* change, then "complex cases take longer" may partly be tautological reclassification, and the true capacity shock is smaller than the raw medians suggest; the aggregates cannot separate these.

**(3) Outage, recovery, and intervention pooled into "average capacity".** The pooled completion mean counts Apr 15's 58 and Apr 16's 129 as ordinary variation around 91.5. In reality the first is lost capacity and the second is backlog-fuelled drain at temporarily raised staffing. A pooled capacity estimate therefore simultaneously (a) understates normal throughput (dragged down by the outage) and (b) overstates sustainable throughput at 6.0 FTE (inflated by recovery days and inherited backlog). Similarly, the intervention's pooled signature is diluted: average staff is 5.3 FTE pooled, hiding the stepwise 6.07.0 weekday change; and because the intervention coincides with the high-mix regime, a pooled pre/post-Apr-29 comparison confounds staffing with two weeks of accumulated backlog dynamics. Segmented, the fair comparison is mix-shift pre (WIP 44, net 13.4/day) vs mix-shift post (WIP 34 falling, net +6.5/day over Apr 30–May 1) — suggestive of improvement, but based on 2 clean days and entangled with falling median service (3532.5) at constant complex share.

A further pooled distortion: pooled peak share 33% averages baseline (29%), outage-era, and post-mix (46%) weekdays. Staffing to the pooled peak understaffs the current morning peak by a wide margin.

Key distinction: a Fano factor >1 across *deterministic time bins* does not by itself prove a bursty stochastic process; a fixed daily peak inflates bin variance. Apr 7 illustrates pure profile non-stationarity (Fano 3–4 with a smooth single peak), whereas Apr 21 illustrates profile *plus* burstiness (isolated 34- and 24-case hours inconsistent with its own neighbouring bins 8, 8, 5). Even Apr 21 could be one day's realisation of scheduled group arrivals (e.g. batch referrals), so process-level overdispersion (e.g. variance of daily counts given regime) cannot be separated from intraday profile shape with one vector per archetype. Daily-count overdispersion within the cleanest block (baseline weekdays, arrivals 92–107, SD 5, variance/mean 0.25) is actually *under*dispersed relative to Poisson — consistent with scheduled/capped demand or a short stable window — which warns against fitting a single Poisson or negative-binomial story globally.

**Daily sequence, lag dependence, change points.** The daily series is dominated by blocks, so lag-1 autocorrelation of raw arrivals/completions/WIP largely measures block membership, not day-to-day dynamics — a classic spurious-persistence trap. WIP is the most plausibly truly autocorrelated series mechanically (today's ending backlog is tomorrow's starting backlog, A1), and its trajectory shows regime memory: 475235 after the outage; 374146444323204950 across the mix-shift build-up. A transparent heuristic (§8) — comparing rolling means/CUSUM of complex share, median service, and WIP, plus a simple one-change-point scan minimising within-segment squared error on complex share — places the dominant break at Apr 19/20 (complex 24%41%, median 2934 into the sustained 40%+ plateau) and secondary level shifts at Apr 15–16 for WIP/completions and Apr 29 for staff. These are *descriptive* breaks aligned with known events; with 28 serially dependent points they are not definitive statistical change-point detections, and multiple testing over candidate dates is uncorrected. Four representative hourly days cannot establish a stochastic model: they are labelled archetypes ("ordinary candidate", "weekend candidate"), possibly selected *because* they look typical/extreme, with no sampling scheme; they can falsify a homogeneous-Poisson intraday model but cannot identify an arrival process (e.g. MMPP vs. batch arrivals vs. time-varying Poisson).

**Stationarity verdict.** No 28-day stationary model is defensible. Within-block stationarity is plausible only for baseline weekdays/weekends (7 and 4 days) and is untestable at that size; the post-intervention blocks (2+2 days) are best treated as an unfolding transient (WIP falling 433632), i.e. explicitly non-stationary.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law, $L=\lambda W$, relates long-run averages: $L$ = mean number in system, $\lambda$ = throughput (in steady state equal to arrival rate), $W$ = mean flow (sojourn) time. Here $W$ must be mean *waiting + service*, in the same time units as the rate, and $L$ must be the time-average over the same window. Using median service anywhere in this formula is invalid. Snapshots are open-hours only, so any implied $W$ below is an *open-hours-equivalent* flow time: it treats the 12-hour day as the time base, $\hat W = L/(\text{cases per open hour})$, expressed in open hours. Because cases also age overnight in calendar time (closed 12h, no service), calendar flow time would be longer; the two must not be conflated. $\hat W$ values printed precisely in §8; rounded values here.

# 7. Operational implications

**Staffing.** Plan capacity by regime and day-type, not by pooled averages. Under the post-Apr-20 mix, weekday demand is 114 arrivals/day with median service 35 min and p90 82–88 min; even the (biased-low) median-based load index rises from 0.60 (baseline weekday) to 0.90+ pre-intervention, and because the mean service exceeds the median under right skew, true utilisation at 6.0 FTE was plausibly at or above sustainable levels — consistent with the observed monotonic WIP accumulation. The move to 7.0 weekday / 3.0 weekend FTE is directionally justified; whether it is *sufficient* cannot be confirmed from 4 post days with falling WIP — the correct criterion is whether WIP stabilises at an acceptable level over the next 2–3 weeks at constant mix, not whether 2 days of completions exceeded arrivals.

**Backlog recovery.** Distinguish three backlogs with different causes: outage backlog (+47 on Apr 15, largely drained Apr 16–17 at temporary 7.0/6.5 FTE), mix-shift structural backlog (built steadily Apr 20–28 because arrivals structurally exceeded completions — this recurs unless capacity or mix changes, which is what the intervention addresses), and ordinary overnight carry-over. Recovery staffing (as on Apr 16) works for the first type but is not a fix for the second; budgeting recovery overtime as if Apr 16's 129 completions were normal productivity would institutionalise overwork and misforecast.

**Peak smoothing.** Current weekdays put 40–50% of arrivals in 08:00–10:00, plus Apr 21 shows additional 13:00 and 16:00 surges. Options: stagger scheduled/appointment demand away from 08:00–10:00, pre-07:00 backlog triage so morning capacity meets new peaks rather than inherited work, flex staffing concentrated in 08:00–14:00 rather than flat FTE averages, and investigate whether Apr 21-type surge hours are batch referrals that can be scheduled. Because mean daily FTE hides intra-day mismatch, rostering to the daily average will systematically fail the morning peak.

**A policy that looks beneficial pooled but is risky segmented.** *Cutting weekday staffing back toward 6.0 FTE (or weekend to 2.5) because pooled completions  arrivals (91.5 vs 92.3) and pooled "productivity" looks balanced.* Pooled, the books appear balanced. Segmented, the balance is an artefact: it averages an accumulating pre-intervention regime (net 13.4/day, WIP50) with a draining recovery, an outage deficit, and low-volume weekends. Under the current mix at 6.0 FTE the best estimate is continued backlog growth, rising flow times, and eventual service failure concentrated in the 08:00–10:00 peak. The same warning applies in reverse to *declaring the intervention a success and locking it in permanently* on 2 clean days: the segmented view supports continuing it provisionally with explicit monitoring, not an irreversible conclusion.

**Data collection (highest value first).** (1) Log intervention and roster-change timestamps. (2) Record event-level data — case ID, arrival timestamp, service start/end per step, variant, completion — enabling true mean service and flow times, waiting-time distribution, and per-variant queues; this converts the present aggregate archaeology into an event-log analysis. (3) Record 07:00 opening backlog and end-of-day backlog daily to close the flow balance and test A1 (abandonments). (4) Record intraday staffing by hour, not daily mean FTE. (5) Audit the complex-variant coding around Apr 20 (dual-code a sample) to separate real mix shift from reclassification. (6) Keep hourly arrival/completion vectors for *all* days, not four archetypes, so burstiness and profile can be estimated with sampling uncertainty.

def best_single_break(series):

    best = []
