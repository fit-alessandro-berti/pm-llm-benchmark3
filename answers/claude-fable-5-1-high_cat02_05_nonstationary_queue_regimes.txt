# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Quantities, units and conventions

| Symbol | Definition used here | Unit | Source column / derivation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening window | 07:00–19:00, 12 opening hours per day, 7 days a week; no service outside | h/day | scenario text |
| λ_a (arrival intensity) | arrivals / 12 | cases per opening hour | `arrivals` |
| λ_c (completion intensity) | completed / 12 | cases per opening hour | `completed` |
| Net accumulation | arrivals − completed | cases/day | derived; positive = backlog grows |
| L (WIP) | `mean_wip`, time average of 48 quarter-hour snapshots during opening hours | cases | `mean_wip` |
| S (service time) | active handling time, excluding waiting; only median and p90 supplied | min | `median_service_min`, `p90_service_min` |
| c (capacity) | `staff_fte`, mean concurrently available FTE; available minutes = c × 720 − outage | FTE, min | `staff_fte`, `outage_min` |
| ρ (capacity pressure) | offered: arrivals × E[S] / available minutes; realised: completed × E[S] / available minutes | dimensionless | derived, requires an estimate of E[S] (Assumption A1) |
| W (flow time) | mean time a case spends in the system, arrival to completion, in opening-hour time | opening hours (÷12 → opening days; clock time adds 12 h per overnight) | not observed; only implied via L/λ where Section 6 allows |
| Complex share | share of daily arrivals following the multi-step variant | % | `complex_pct` |
| Peak share | share of daily arrivals in 08:00–09:59 as labelled | % | `peak_08_10_pct` |

Labelled assumptions:

- A1 (service-time mean). Only median and p90 of S are given. To get any offered-load number I assume a lognormal shape: σ = ln(p90/median)/1.2816, E[S] = median·exp(σ²/2). This yields E[S] ≈ 28 min in the baseline (median 25, p90 47), ≈ 44 min in the complex-mix weeks (35, 83), ≈ 40 min after the intervention (33, 74). It is needed because ρ requires a mean, not a median; it is a shape assumption and the resulting ρ values are ±15 % at best. I never use it as a flow time.
- A2 (WIP is a time average over the same window as the flow counts). Needed for any L = λW statement; 15-minute snapshots over 12 h make this reasonable but the population counted in WIP is not documented (see threat T1).
- A3 (staff_fte is fully assignable to this queue). Needed for ρ; if FTE is shared with other queues, ρ is underestimated.
- A4 (the seven-day week is fully open). Needed to treat Saturday/Sunday rows as ordinary opening days with reduced staffing.

## Limitations of the aggregates and threats to naive inference

- T1 Flow conservation fails. Σ(arrivals − completed) over the 28 days is +110 cases, yet `mean_wip` moves only from 18 (first Monday) to 19 (last Sunday). Within 20–28 April alone the net inflow is +100 while mean WIP rises only by ~13. Every Friday→Saturday transition shows WIP falling by 10–20 cases while the daily net flow is positive (17→18 Apr: 35→16 with net +2; 24→25 Apr: 43→23 with net +8; 1→2 May: 32→22 with net +2). So either (a) cases exit through uncounted channels (abandonment, cancellation, transfer, auto-closure after 19:00), (b) `mean_wip` counts a narrower population than arrivals/completions (e.g. only actively queued cases, excluding parked or pending-customer cases), or (c) `completed` misses closures logged outside opening hours. Consequence: L and λ may not refer to the same population, which undermines any Little's-Law computation; every implied flow time below inherits this caveat.
- T2 Medians are not means. Median service time (24–36 min) is a lower-tail statistic of handling time, not the mean flow time; the p90/median ratio of 1.8–2.4 indicates a right-skewed service distribution whose mean is 15–30 % above the median. Substituting the median for W would understate flow time by a factor of 5–10 in every regime.
- T3 Unknown 07:00 backlog and overnight carry-over. Cases carry over overnight and completions need not have arrived the same day; without the daily opening backlog, daily arrival-vs-completion comparisons are only conservation differences, not per-case measures.
- T4 Peak-share column is not reconcilable with the hourly vectors. With bins 07–08, 08–09, …, the vectors give 08:00–09:59 shares of 16.8 %, 18.1 %, 9.6 % and 11.8 % for the four representative days; the table says 31, 32, 47, 22. Shifting one bin (09:00–10:59) reproduces three of four (31.7, 33.3, 21.6 %) but not 21 April (36.5 vs 47 %). Either the hourly bin labels or the peak column are offset by one hour (clock/timezone or labelling inconsistency), and the 21 April vector is not the exact daily profile (no adjacent pair of bins sums to 47 % × 115 ≈ 54). The outage zeros sit in bins 11–14, matching the note, so the vectors are at least internally consistent with their own labelling.
- T5 The intervention start is not timestamped. 29 April has 7.0 FTE as a daily mean but WIP still at 43; it is a transition day and must not be assigned to either side.
- T6 Case-mix vs coding change. `complex_pct` jumps from 25–27 % to 41 % between Friday 17 and Monday 20 April with no intermediate values. A recoding would jump the same way; the fact that median and p90 service also jump (29→34 min; 60→78 min) and that WIP and net accumulation rise means the workload change is real, but the size of the "real" mix change cannot be separated from a recoding.
- T7 Daily rows are not independent. Regime level shifts, weekly seasonality, and backlog carry-over create serial dependence, so n = 28 is not 28 independent observations (Section 5).
- T8 Utilisation from FTE is crude. `staff_fte` is a daily mean; intraday staffing, breaks, and multi-tasking are unknown, so ρ is an order-of-magnitude indicator.
- T9 Four representative hourly vectors are hand-picked. They illustrate shapes; they cannot support an estimate of the hourly arrival-count distribution or autocorrelation structure.

Arrival and completion estimates are means over the regime's days with the observed range; λ in cases per opening hour. ρ values rely on A1.

Outage and recovery treatment: R3 and R4 are removed from every "normal" estimate. R4's 129 completions is the single highest completion count in the table and would, if pooled with weekdays, inflate the apparent capacity by ~20 %; it was achieved with 7 FTE and a backlog of already-arrived cases, i.e. under conditions that do not exist on ordinary days. Equally, R3's 58 completions is not a low-demand day; arrivals were normal (105) and the hourly arrival shape is ordinary.

Reading across regimes, the non-stationarity decomposes into four separately identifiable sources:

- Demand level: weekday ≈ 100 → 114 arrivals/day (+14 %) from 20 April; weekend 42 → 47–49.
- Service mix: complex share 20 % → 44 %, median service +10 min, p90 +35 min; E[S] up ≈ 55 % under A1. This, not the +14 % in arrivals, is what pushes offered load from 0.65 to 1.17.
- Capacity: 6.0 → 7.0 FTE on 16–17 Apr (recovery) and from 29 Apr (intervention), weekend 2.5 → 3.0; the 180-min outage removed 25 % of a day's capacity.
- Backlog state: WIP doubles on 15 April and again on 20 April, and only partly returns; the direction of net flow (accumulating vs draining) changes on 15, 16, 20 and 29 April.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## Alternative A: backlog-state segmentation (balanced / accumulating / draining), weekday vs weekend

Ignore the causes and classify each day by its net flow: balanced (|net| < 5), accumulating (net ≥ +8), draining (net ≤ −5). The operational story is "what is the queue doing to the customer today", the question a service manager watching a dashboard asks.

| State | Days | Arrivals/day | Completions/day | Net total | Mean WIP | Median / p90 service | Mean FTE | Offered ρ (A1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balanced weekday | 7 (= R1) | 100 | 99.6 | +4 | 20.3 | 25 / 47 | 5.9 | 0.66 |
| Balanced weekend | 7 (R2, R8, plus 26 Apr whose net +4 falls under the threshold) | 44 | 43.3 | +5 | 16.6 | 30 / 58 | 2.6 | 0.86 |
| Accumulating weekday | 8 (R3 + R5) | 113 | 95 | +141 | 44.6 | 35 / 82.5 | 5.9 | 1.18 |
| Accumulating weekend | 1 (25 Apr) | 51 | 43 | +8 | 23 | 36 / 85 | 2.5 | 1.28 |
| Draining weekday | 5 (R4 + R7t + R7) | 111 | 121 | −48 | 39.6 | 33 / 72 | 6.8 | 0.88 |

Strengths: it is directly observable without knowing causes; it groups all days on which λ_c is a capacity-bound quantity (accumulating) apart from days on which λ_c includes backlog work (draining), which is exactly the distinction Little's Law needs; it naturally treats 29 April as draining without needing the intervention timestamp.

Weaknesses: it merges the outage day (a supply shock with normal demand) with the complex-mix days (a workload shift with normal supply), which have opposite remedies; it merges recovery (6.75 FTE, baseline mix) with intervention (6.75–7 FTE, complex mix), whose sustainable completion rates differ (120 vs ~116/day is not distinguishable with two days each); the thresholds (±5, +8) are arbitrary and the 26 April net +4 lands in "balanced" although the weekend is clearly above baseline WIP; and it is not forecastable, since the state is an outcome, not a driver.

## Alternative B: two-epoch case-mix segmentation with staffing as a covariate

Split only at the complex-mix change point (before/from 20 April), keep weekday/weekend, and treat FTE, outage and intervention as covariates within each epoch. Story: "a permanent product/process change happened on 20 April; everything else is operational noise around it." The change-point screen (Section 5) puts its strongest split for `complex_pct`, `median_service_min` and `peak_08_10_pct` exactly at 20 April. Strength: parsimonious, matches the one structural change that is unlikely to be reversed. Weakness: within the post-20-April epoch, completions per day range 94–126 and WIP 32–50 solely because of staffing, so a single "post-change" completion intensity (≈ 9.0/h) or WIP (≈ 43) describes no actual day; it also lets the 15–17 April shock contaminate the "before" epoch unless removed by hand.

## Alternative C: intraday segmentation (peak vs off-peak) nested inside the daily regimes

Because the peak share rose from ~30 % to ~45 % and the 21 April vector shows hourly arrivals of 34 and 24 against a completion ceiling of about 13/h, the queue on complex-mix weekdays is dominated by within-day dynamics: on 21 April the intraday net inflow peaks at +27 cases by 14:00 before the afternoon drains it to +14. A regime defined as "10:00–15:00 on complex-mix weekdays" has effective ρ well above 1 while "16:00–19:00" has ρ below 1. This is the right segmentation for shift scheduling, but only one bursty day is available, so it is a hypothesis about a regime, not an estimate of one.

Comparison: the primary segmentation is best for attributing cause and designing interventions; Alternative A is best for deciding when Little's Law is usable and for daily monitoring; B is best for long-range planning; C is what staffing by hour needs but is under-supported by data.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

All 28 days pooled: arrivals 90.0/day (range 36–121), completions 86.0, mean WIP 29.9 (12–52), median service 32.5, p90 72, mean FTE 5.1, complex share 33 %, offered ρ ≈ 0.92, completions/arrivals 95.6 %. Each of these describes no observed regime.

1. Weekday/weekend composition. Pooled arrival rate 7.5/h is a 20:8 mixture of 9.0/h (all weekdays) and 3.7/h (all weekends); the pooled arrival variance is 902 (Fano 10.0), while within weekdays it is 71 (Fano 0.66) and within R1 it is 26.5 (Fano 0.26). More than 90 % of the pooled "overdispersion" is the weekday/weekend mixture plus the 20 April level shift, not stochastic demand. Pooled WIP 29.9 sits between the weekday mean 34.9 and weekend mean 17.4 and is the WIP of no actual day type. The pooled ρ ≈ 0.9 looks like a busy but feasible system; it hides that weekend days have ρ ≈ 0.75–0.97 with 2.5–3 servers, where the same ρ generates much longer queueing than with 6–7 servers.

2. Complex-case mix. Pooled complex share 33 % and pooled median service 32.5 min are averages of two epochs (21 % / 25 min before 20 April; 44 % / 35 min after) with no day in between. Pooled weekday completions/arrivals is 95.6 %, which looks like a 4 % shortfall; segmented, it is 99.4 % in R1 and R2, 88.2 % in R5, 87.2 % in R6, 55 % on the outage day and 106–114 % in R4/R7. A planner using 95.6 % would size a small recovery effort; the R5 figure means 13 cases/day are being added to a backlog on every ordinary weekday at 6 FTE.

3. Staffing intervention and outage. Pooled correlation of `staff_fte` with `mean_wip` across all 28 days is +0.65 (more staff, more WIP), +0.29 within weekdays, and −0.43 within the complex-mix weekdays (R5 + R7). The pooled sign is a Simpson-type reversal: staff was raised on the days when WIP was already high (reverse causation across regimes), while within the complex-mix regime the extra FTE lowers WIP day by day (43→36→32). Pooling "after 29 April" with weekends gives completions/arrivals of 102 % and could be read as "problem solved with a small nudge"; the weekday-only view shows completions of 121/day at 6.75–7 FTE against arrivals of 114.5, a drain of 6–7 cases/day against a backlog of roughly 100 accumulated cases (by net flow) or ~15–20 excess WIP (by snapshots; T1) — i.e. two to fifteen more weekdays of 7 FTE depending on which backlog measure is right. Similarly, pooling 15–17 April gives 316 arrivals vs 299 completions (95 %), which hides a day at 55 % followed by two at 114 %.

4. Service-time reversal by day type. Before 20 April weekends had longer median service than weekdays (29 vs 25.5 min); from 20 April both are ≈ 35 min. Pooled over the month, "weekend service is 32 vs weekday 32.5" suggests no day-type effect, whereas the segmented view shows a baseline weekend penalty (fewer specialised staff or higher complex share, 21–24 % vs 18–24 %) that the mix change then swamps.

5. Implied flow-time ratios (Section 6). Pooled L/λ_c = 29.9/7.17 = 4.2 opening hours; the regimes give 2.4 h (R1), 4.0 h (R2), 5.3 h (R5, and not a valid flow time), 3.4 h (R7). The pooled number is longer than the healthy weekday, shorter than the overloaded weekday and coincidentally equal to the healthy weekend; it therefore misdescribes every regime it averages.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## Hourly vectors

| Day | Arrivals: Fano (var/mean) | CV | lag-1 autocorr | Max arrivals/h | Completions: Fano, CV, max/h | Shape χ² vs 7 Apr profile | Max intraday net inflow |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 Apr ordinary | 3.2 | 0.62 | +0.62 | 20 | 1.7, 0.45, 15 | 0 (reference) | +6 |
| 15 Apr outage | 4.1 | 0.69 | +0.65 | 22 | 2.3, 0.68, 10 | 2.3 | +55 |
| 21 Apr bursty | 10.1 | 1.03 | −0.13 | 34 | 1.0, 0.34, 13 | 58.9 | +27 |
| 25 Apr weekend | 1.3 | 0.55 | +0.70 | 8 | 0.8, 0.47, 6 | 4.8 | +8 |

Interpretation:

- On the ordinary weekday the Fano factor of 3.2 is mostly a deterministic intraday profile (a smooth hump peaking 10–12) with positive lag-1 autocorrelation (0.62): adjacent hours are similar because the mean moves slowly. This is non-stationarity of the mean, not stochastic burstiness; a Poisson process with this time-varying rate would show roughly this Fano factor. Hourly arrivals on such a day are compatible with a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP) but four vectors cannot test that.
- On 21 April the Fano factor of 10 with negative lag-1 autocorrelation and a χ² of 59 against the ordinary profile is genuine burstiness: 34 cases in 10–11, 24 in 13–14 and 14 in 16–17, each followed by a trough. This pattern (spikes at roughly three-hour spacing, immediate drop afterwards) is what batch releases or upstream systems flushing queues produce, not a smooth demand hump. The completion vector for the same day has Fano ≈ 1 and CV 0.34 with a maximum of 13/h: completions are capacity-smoothed and cannot follow arrivals, so the intraday backlog reaches +27 by 14:00 and is still +14 at closing. On such days arrivals are not the throughput; completions are, and they measure capacity.
- The outage day has an ordinary arrival profile; its dispersion is on the completion side (three zero hours) and the intraday net inflow reaches +55 (the daily +47 after some afternoon catch-up). Recovery within the day was minimal (4–7/h after 14:00), so the backlog carried to 16 April.
- The weekend vector is near-Poisson-like (Fano 1.3) with a low, flat hump and completions that track arrivals with a one-hour lag; small counts (1–8/h) mean any estimate has wide sampling error.

## Daily sequence

- Dispersion of daily arrivals: pooled Fano 10.0 (CV 0.33); weekdays only 0.66 (CV 0.08); R1 alone 0.26; R5 alone 0.29; weekends 0.73. Within a regime, daily counts are underdispersed relative to Poisson (Fano < 1), which points to a partly scheduled or capped intake (appointments, quotas, or upstream batching that is stable day to day). Pooled overdispersion is therefore a mixture artefact of day type and regime.
- Serial dependence: lag-1 autocorrelation of arrivals is 0.39 over all days (weekly seasonality) and 0.51 over weekdays (driven by the 20 April level shift); mean WIP over weekdays has lag-1 of 0.75, the signature of backlog carry-over. After removing regime means, weekday arrivals have lag-1 ≈ −0.20 and daily net accumulation ≈ −0.03, i.e. no evidence of residual serial dependence at n = 20. The dependence that matters is structural (regimes and backlog), not an AR process in demand.
- Change-point evidence: a greedy least-squares binary segmentation on weekday series (minimum segment 3 days) puts splits for `complex_pct`, `median_service_min` and `peak_08_10_pct` at 20 April at both a 15 % and a 30 % SSE-gain threshold, for `mean_wip` at 15 April, and for `staff_fte` at 29 April. At the lax threshold it also proposes 9 and 14 April, which correspond to 1–2-point wobbles in a very flat baseline and are an example of why the heuristic must not be read as ground truth. Net accumulation produces no split because its within-regime variance is large relative to its mean shifts; it is better read as sign changes (positive from 20 April, negative from 29 April).
- Stationarity verdict: at most R1 (7 days) and R2 (4 days) look weakly stationary in level and net flow. R5 is stationary in arrivals but not in state (WIP trending 37→50, backlog growing). R4 and R7 are transients by construction. Nothing after 15 April satisfies the "long window, start state ≈ end state" requirement of a steady-state model.

## Why four representative days cannot establish a stochastic model

They are hand-selected extremes (one ordinary, one outage, one bursty, one weekend), so they are informative about shapes but not about frequencies: the data do not say how many of the seven R5 weekdays were bursty like 21 April, or whether burst timing is fixed (a batch job) or random. Twelve hourly bins per day give a single realisation of an intraday process; an NHPP versus a batch (compound) process cannot be distinguished from one day, and the peak-share column's inconsistency with the vectors (T4) means even the intraday clock is uncertain. Any hourly staffing model built from these vectors should be treated as a scenario, not a fit.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Conventions: L = mean of `mean_wip` over the window (cases, time-averaged over opening hours, A2); λ_c = total completed / (12 × days), λ_a likewise for arrivals, in cases per opening hour; W = L/λ in opening hours (multiply by 60 for minutes; a case spanning a night adds 12 clock hours per night). Completions are the preferred throughput proxy when the window is stationary (arrivals ≈ completions, either will do) or draining (completions describe what left, which is what W measures); arrivals are preferred only when completions are capacity-truncated and one wants the flow time cases would experience if the system were stable — which it is not, so that number is a lower bound on the eventual flow time of the arriving cohort. The T1 population mismatch applies to every row.

Summary of where the law can be used: only R1 yields a flow time with reasonable support (~2.4 opening hours, i.e. about 2.5 h of clock time for cases that do not span a night), and even that number is conditional on `mean_wip` counting the same population as arrivals/completions. All other windows either violate the balanced-boundary condition or have too few days, and their L/λ values should be reported as descriptive ratios with brackets.

# 7. Operational implications

Staffing

- The intervention level (7.0 FTE weekdays, 3.0 weekends) is not surplus; it is approximately the break-even capacity for the current mix. Under A1, 114 arrivals × ~40 min ≈ 4,600 handling minutes/day against 7 × 720 = 5,040 available (ρ ≈ 0.91). Reverting to 6 FTE returns to R5 (ρ ≈ 1.1–1.2) and to accumulation of 10–16 cases/day. The staffing intervention should be treated as the new baseline unless the complex share reverts.
- Backlog drain is slow: −6 to −7/day at 7 FTE. If the net-flow backlog (~100 cases above early April) is real, clearing it takes ~15 weekdays; if the snapshot WIP (~15 excess) is right, ~3 days. Resolving T1 is therefore a staffing decision, not a data nicety: temporary 7.5–8 FTE for one to two weeks is justified only under the net-flow reading.
- Weekends: ρ ≈ 0.95 with 3 servers gives longer queueing per case than weekdays at the same ρ. Either 3.5 FTE or a triage rule routing complex weekend arrivals to Monday (with explicit customer expectation) would reduce weekend flow time (currently ~5 h of a 12-h opening day).

Backlog recovery

- Separate the two backlog mechanisms: outage backlog (a one-off pulse, recovered in two days with +1 FTE and a still-simple mix) and mix-driven backlog (a persistent imbalance that no short recovery clears). Recovery days should be flagged in reporting so that their 112–129 completions never enter a capacity baseline.
- Publish a daily 07:00 backlog count and the day's net flow rather than "completed/arrived %"; the latter reads 95.6 % pooled while the queue grows.

Peak smoothing

- The 21 April profile (34 arrivals 10–11, 24 at 13–14, 14 at 16–17 against a 13/h completion ceiling) indicates batch injection. Identify the upstream source (batch upload, scheduled transfer, appointment release) and either spread it across the morning or align a short staffing peak (e.g. two extra FTE 10:00–15:00 on complex-mix weekdays). Because the peak-share column and the vectors disagree by an hour (T4), fix the clock before scheduling by hour.
- If burst timing is deterministic, a 30-minute earlier start for two agents flattens the 10–11 spike more cheaply than an all-day FTE.

Data collection

- Timestamp interventions (29 April start time), outages (start/end), and staffing changes; record the 07:00 backlog daily; log case exits by type (completed, abandoned, cancelled, transferred, auto-closed) so that conservation can be checked; record the recoding date if the complex flag definition changed; keep a case-level event log so mean flow time can be measured directly (Section 8 comments) instead of implied through L/λ; reconcile the hourly binning clock with the peak-share column.

A policy that looks good pooled but is risky segmented

- "Pooled utilisation is ~0.9 and completions are 96 % of arrivals, so reduce weekday staff to 6 FTE after the backlog is gone and shift half an FTE from weekdays to weekends." Under the pooled averages this looks like a near-neutral rebalancing. Segmented: at 6 FTE the complex-mix weekday runs at ρ ≈ 1.15, the queue grows 13 cases/day and the daily peak (34/h vs 13/h) has no slack at all; and the "spare" weekday FTE moved to the weekend would leave weekdays even shorter. The pooled view never shows this because it averages seven saturated weekdays with eight slack-looking weekends and two 129-completion recovery days.
- A second example: "Use completions per FTE-day as productivity target" — the pooled figure (~17/FTE-day) is beaten on recovery days (18.4) and matched on saturated R5 days (17.0), so productivity looks stable while the backlog grows; the segmented view shows completions/FTE is capacity, not performance, in R4, R5 and R7.

# 6. Transparent change-point heuristic: binary segmentation on weekday series
#    (least-squares mean shifts, min segment 3, stop when SSE gain < threshold).
#    This is a screening device, not ground truth.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
def binseg(y, min_seg=3, min_gain=0.15, offset=0, out=None):
    out = [] if out is None else out
    y = np.asarray(y, float); n = len(y)
    if n < 2 * min_seg:
        return out
    sse0 = ((y - y.mean()) ** 2).sum()
    best, best_k = None, None
    for k in range(min_seg, n - min_seg + 1):
        sse = ((y[:k] - y[:k].mean()) ** 2).sum() + ((y[k:] - y[k:].mean()) ** 2).sum()
        if best is None or sse < best:
            best, best_k = sse, k
    if sse0 > 0 and (sse0 - best) / sse0 >= min_gain:
        out.append(offset + best_k)
        binseg(y[:best_k], min_seg, min_gain, offset, out)
        binseg(y[best_k:], min_seg, min_gain, offset + best_k, out)
    return sorted(out)
