```python
def bisect_seg(x, dates, min_len=4, depth=0, max_depth=3, out=None):
    x = np.asarray(x, float); n=len(x)
    if out is None: out=[]
    if n < 2*min_len or depth>=max_depth: return out
    cs = np.cumsum(x - x.mean())
    ks = np.arange(min_len, n-min_len)
    k = ks[np.argmax(np.abs(cs[ks]))]
    # crude significance screen: relative CUSUM amplitude
    stat = abs(cs[k]) / (x.std(ddof=1)*np.sqrt(n))
    out.append((str(dates.iloc[k]), round(float(cs[k]),1), round(float(stat),2)))
    bisect_seg(x[:k+1], dates.iloc[:k+1], min_len, depth+1, max_depth, out)
    bisect_seg(x[k+1:], dates.iloc[k+1:], min_len, depth+1, max_depth, out)
    return out
```

## Alternative 2 (sketch): queueing-*state* segmentation

Classify days by stock dynamics — **accumulators** (C<A: 4/15, 20–24, 27–28), **clearers** (C>A: 4/16–17, 4/25–26 partly, 4/29–5/3), **balanced** (rest). This is the natural state space for a backlog-control policy and predicts delay differences on equal-demand days. Its weaknesses are endogeneity (today's class is caused by yesterday's) and circularity if used for explanation rather than control.

**Comparison with the primary (event-anchored) segmentation:** the primary segmentation wins for attribution and action (who to roster, when, responding to what); Alternative 1 wins for estimating the causal mix effect and for anyone fitting state-independent service distributions; Alternative 2 wins for control. They agree on the central fact — a mix-driven capacity shortfall emerged on ~4/20 and was only partly closed on ~4/29 — but disagree on how much of the 4/29–5/1 "improvement" is durable capability versus backlog consumption.

# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

**(D1) Day-type composition.** Pooled daily arrivals average 90.0/day — a value describing *no actual day*: weekdays run 108.0/day, weekends 44.9 (range 36–53). Consequences: (i) pooled daily arrival dispersion is enormous (Fano  9) while within-stratum dispersion is  0.16–0.7 — a ~30–60× Simpson-style inflation; (ii) pooled lag1 autocorrelation of arrivals is *negative* (weekly alternation artifact), fabricating "anti-persistence"; (iii) sizing from pooled per-FTE productivity (~16.7 completions/FTE/day) yields a uniform  5.4 FTE every day — starved weekdays in regime E, wasted weekends (weekends are demand-limited).

**(D2) Complex mix and utilization reversal.** Pooled complex% = 32.9 "on average," hiding two clusters: 20.7% (EraI weekdays) vs 44.6% (EraII weekdays); weekends mirror this (22.8  40.5), which is why the pooled number looks deceptively stable. Median service pools to  31 min — matching neither cluster center (25 vs 35). Cross-checks: eraE imputed E[S]  44 min agrees within ~2% with the saturation-implied mean (720 min × 6.0 FTE ÷ 100.6 completions  43 min), and _E  1.16 predicts  15–16 cases/day of accumulation versus the observed 13 to 16/day — the accounting closes.

**(D3) Event contamination (outage and intervention).** Averages over "Wednesdays" or "the month" bury both structural episodes. Mechanically, losing 180 of 720 staffed minutes should cost ~25% of ~100 completions  25 cases; the observed shortfall on 4/15 was  45 — the extra  20 indicates restart/ramp costs or selective difficulty, invisible in any pooled mean. Symmetrically, the intervention looks like "+15.5 completions/day" if 4/29–5/1 (mean 121.0) is compared with 4/27–28 (105.5); era-controlled, the improvement is a swing in net balance from 14/day to +6/day at   0.93 — the intervention *closed the eraE deficit*, it did not create a +15/day capability, and the durable margin is thin. Meanwhile pooled per-FTE completions (16.5–17.9 on every unblocked day) tempt the wrong conclusion "productivity is staffing-invariant," which is true only because backlog made the system work-conserving.

Ranges rather than means are used wherever cells are small (Sunday: 4 observations split across eras; 4/29 partially censored).

**Representative hourly vectors** (counts per 1-h bin; see code for exact values):

With n = 12 bins per vector, sampling error on CV² is of order ±0.3 relative ((2/(n1))), so none of these values supports fitting a specific count process; they only rule models out (stationary Poisson is dead on arrival for all four).

**Daily sequence.** Pooled daily arrivals: Fano  9 (regime mixture) against within-regime 0.16 (A) to 0.73 (B) — notably **underdispersed relative to Poisson within the baseline regime**, which hints that intake is managed (appointments, channel caps, batch-and-smooth scheduling). This coheres with two other regularities: completions/FTE  16.5–17.9 on every unblocked day, and eraA flows balancing almost exactly. Working hypothesis H3 (flagged as hypothesis): *arrivals are capacity-planned off eraA service norms; regime E broke the plan because per-case workload nearly doubled while the intake target did not.* Pooled lag1 autocorrelation of arrivals is strongly negative (weekly cycle artifact); lag7 autocorrelation is strongly positive; WIP is highly persistent (lag1 well above +0.7) and leads completions (stock feeds next days' departures, e.g., 4/16's 129 completions consuming 4/15's +47 build). Rows are therefore visibly **not independent**; any confidence interval that treats 28 rows as iid is too narrow.

**Change-point evidence (heuristic only).** Recursive CUSUM binary segmentation (standardized split statistic, implemented in §8) flags: complex_pct split between 4/19 and 4/20 (statistic  2.5 — the dominant structure in that column), corroborated independently by median service; net-flow outliers at 4/15 and the 4/284/29 sign flip. With n = 28, no formal test has decent power, boundaries could move ±1 day, the 4/16–4/19 band is genuinely transitional, and multiple testing across the three probed series is uncorrected. These are **candidate boundaries**, not ground truth.

**Why four representative days cannot yield a stochastic model:** they were selected (by whom, under what criterion, is unknown), they provide no replication within (day-type × hour) cells, the deterministic profile dominates the residual noise, arrival timestamps do not exist (the one supplied peak metric is internally inconsistent, T3), and service start/end times — needed to separate waiting from service — are entirely absent.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Applicability rules used: LL (L = _effective · W) asserts a *population* entering and leaving the system, averaged over a stationary window. Here the system **closes nightly** (12 h/day dark, cases persist), the 07:00 census is unknown, W is unobservable, and either arrivals or completions must stand in for . Implied flow times below are computed as **W_implied = L / X** in **opening-hours units** — a coherent quantity ("mean open-hours exposure per completing case"), a **lower bound on clock flow time** (night time is invisible to the census), and *not* an estimate of end-to-end delay. Completions are used as the throughput proxy because every candidate window with meaningful WIP is capacity-limited (work-conserving); using arrivals would bias W downward during drains and upward during accumulations. Substituting median service (25–35 min) for W is categorically wrong: it would invert to absurd throughputs (e.g., L=20, W=0.42 h  X48/hr vs the observed 8.3/hr).

# 7. Operational implications

**Staffing (regime-conditional, not calendar-averaged).**
- Base weekday roster: 6.0 FTE suffices only under era-A norms (  0.65). Under era-II mix, eraE load  83 personnel-hours/day   > 1 at 6.0 FTE. Arithmetic says **one added FTE ( 12 h ÷ 44 min  +16 cases/day of capacity) almost exactly closes the observed 13 to 16/day deficit**; targeting   0.85 needs  8.2 FTE. Trigger the uplift on an observed mix indicator (complex% > ~35 or p90_service drifting > ~75), not on the date.
- Weekends are demand-limited: erasing the pooled illusion (D1) prevents both over-rostering weekends and the fatal "5.4 FTE uniform day" policy.

**Backlog recovery.**
- By 4/28 flow accounting showed  +128 cases of cumulative net inflow ( +110 by 5/3, assuming zero abandonment; see T6 caveat). At the observed +6/day drain pace, clearance needs ~3 working weeks; with one targeted surge FTE ( +16/day when backlog guarantees work availability), roughly one week. This generalizes the organically successful 4/16–17 pattern (surge staffing + 7.0 FTE  +30 over two days) into a standing **post-outage protocol**: same-evening/next-morning surge rather than diffuse make-up, plus a documented restart checklist (the  20-case "excess loss" beyond the mechanical 25-case capacity loss suggests restart friction).
- Measurement discipline: judge intervention by **net balance and **, never by gross completions, which surge during drains (D-recovery logic) and regress once backlog clears.

**Peak smoothing.** Pending reconciliation of `peak_08_10_pct` (T3), the safest moves are upstream: if the 4/21 vector reflects batch releases at ~10:00 and ~13:00, negotiate staggered upstream dispatch; if the morning-concentration trend (28–33%  40–50%) survives re-measurement, introduce appointment/hold-back slots across 08:00–10:00. Do not hard-code smoothing capacity against a metric known to be internally inconsistent.

**A policy that looks good pooled but is risky segmented (featured).** "Maintain the average day" — staffing every day at pooled demand ÷ pooled productivity ( 90 ÷ 16.7  5.4 FTE) — is self-consistent under the pooled lens (P95 utilization ~0.9, completions/FTE flat). Under segmentation it simultaneously (i) starves era-E weekdays (persistent  14 cases/day accumulation, runaway backlog), and (ii) wastes weekend capacity that demand cannot absorb. A sibling trap: throughput-linked incentives, which reward cherry-picking simple backlog during drains and misread recovery periods as capability gains.

**Data collection (highest-leverage fixes).**
1. Log the exact intervention and any outage start/end timestamps (removes T8; enables clean censoring).
2. Record **arrival timestamps at queue entry** separately from registrations, and reconcile/redefine `peak_08_10_pct` (fixes T3).
3. Capture **07:00 and end-of-day WIP censuses** and abandonment/redirection counts (resolves T1 and T6).
4. Store per-case identifiers with per-step start/stop times: separates waiting from service, identifies multi-step revisit double-counting, and — critically — makes mean flow time computable instead of guessed.
5. Retain full service-time distributions (not just median/p90) and tag the coding scheme version of `complex_pct`; run a controlled annotation audit to adjudicate mix-shift vs coding-change (T9).
6. Extend the horizon through the next few regime changes so change-point dates can be validated rather than inferred from 28 points.

```python
# 5) CHANGE-POINT HEURISTIC (recursive CUSUM binary segmentation).    #
#    Transparent search, NOT ground truth; small n, no formal test.   #
# ------------------------------------------------------------------ #
def bisect_seg(vals, dates, min_len=4, depth=0, max_depth=3, out=None):
    x = np.asarray(vals, float); n = len(x)
    if out is None: out = []
    if n < 2*min_len or depth >= max_depth: return out
    cs  = np.cumsum(x - x.mean()); ks = np.arange(min_len, n-min_len)
    k   = ks[np.argmax(np.abs(cs[ks]))]
    stat = abs(cs[k])/(x.std(ddof=1)*np.sqrt(n))
    out.append({"split_after": str(dates.iloc[k].date()), "cusum_at_split": round(float(cs[k]),1),
                "standardized_stat": round(stat,2)})
    bisect_seg(x[:k+1], dates.iloc[:k+1], min_len, depth+1, max_depth, out)
    bisect_seg(x[k+1:], dates.iloc[k+1:], min_len, depth+1, max_depth, out)
    return out
```
