## 1. Stress scenarios

The redesign should be based on the **peak mix**, not the historical average. Weekly volume rises from about 2,300 cases/week in normal periods to 3,300/week in peaks, but the important change is mix: Urgent roughly doubles from 414/week to 957/week, and Complex-panel from 299/week to 660/week, while Routine volume is essentially flat. That means Urgent and Complex-panel cases, not generic volume, create the structural stress.

**S1. Winter wave with Urgent/Complex-panel mix shift**  
- Urgent: 30–35% of orders; Complex-panel: 20–25%; Repeat-after-failure: 9–12%.  
- No major equipment outage, but all resource pools run near peak utilization.  
- Contractual Urgent risk: peak Urgent median is already 17h and p90 is 33h, above the 18h penalty threshold.

**S2. Assay-machine degradation or partial outage during high critical-case mix**  
- M2 becomes less reliable or M1 is temporarily unavailable.  
- Repeat-after-failure clusters after night shifts and M2 use become worse.  
- Peak assay utilization is already 98%, median queue 18h, and repeat-assay loop time rises from 10h to 26h.

**S3. Medical validator or specialist-review absence during Complex-panel surge**  
- Complex-panel share above 20%, Urgent share around 30%, and the specialist is unavailable on the second Friday.  
- Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need medical validation and 2.4× more likely to enter specialist review.  
- Peak medical-validation queue is 22h; specialist-review queue is 36h.

**S4. Courier arrival compression or delay with Urgent samples**  
- Couriers arrive in sharper 10:00 and 17:00 spikes or a delayed wave arrives late in the day.  
- Courier intake utilization is 88% with 7.5h median queue in peaks.  
- Late or poor handling increases pre-analytical defects and recollections; the recollection loop has a 60h median in peaks.

**S5. Front-end defect/accession storm**  
- More incomplete orders, poor samples, or metadata errors, possibly from temporary clerks or physician-order changes.  
- Accession correction specialists are already at 91% utilization with 14h median queue in peaks.  
- Recollection rate rises from about 4% normal to 8% peak; recollections also consume intake capacity because clerks explain errors to physicians.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

**S1: Urgent/Complex-panel mix shift**

Likely sequence:

`Registration/courier  Pre-analytical check  Preparation/Assay  Technical validation  Medical validation/Specialist review  Release`

The peak logs show that the process does not merely get uniformly slower. The mix shift changes which paths dominate:

- Pre-analytical check reaches 96% utilization with 9.5h median queue.
- Preparation reaches 89% with 11h median queue; batch wait appears more often.
- Assay machines reach 98% with 18h median queue.
- Technical validators exceed capacity at 101%.
- Medical validators reach 93% with 22h median queue.
- Specialist review reaches 87% with 36h median queue.

The DFG also shows the migration toward medical work: peak technical-validation-to-release falls to 4,050 cases, while technical-validation-to-medical-validation is 3,420 and hold-for-specialist-review rises to 610, versus only 290 holds in normal periods. With fewer total cases than the 8-week normal period, peak medical/specialist demand is disproportionately high.

Relief traps:

- **Strict Urgent-first dispatch, I1**, clears Urgent cases locally but pushes Routine and Complex-panel cases into longer queues. That increases WIP and can move the bottleneck to medical validation because Complex-panel cases arrive late to the same validators.
- **Larger Routine batches, I2**, improve setup efficiency but can bury Urgent cases if exceptions are weak. Conversely, more Urgent bypassing fragments machine schedules, reducing assay efficiency.
- **Registration automation, I8**, may reduce the 2.8h registration queue but exposes pre-analytical check and assay, which are already the true constraints.
- **Evening medical-validation shift, I5**, helps only if assay and technical validation can feed it. If upstream queues remain, the evening validators idle or create another handoff delay.

**S2: Assay-machine degradation or outage**

Likely sequence:

`M2/M1 failure  More repeat assay loops  Preparation/Batch wait growth  Assay queue growth  Technical-validation surge after recovery  Medical/specialist backlog for critical cases`

Evidence:

- Peak repeat assay count is 1,260, higher than the normal 1,140 despite lower total volume.
- Repeat-assay edge time rises from 10h to 26h.
- Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and cluster after night shifts and M2 usage.
- Batch-wait-to-assay time rises from 9h to 22h.

Relief traps:

- Adding preparation capacity alone only creates a larger queue in front of assay.
- Increasing batch size before a degraded assay runs creates large WIP waves and longer waits for Urgent cases.
- **I3, dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex and sending Routine to M2**, can protect critical cases initially, but if M2 is the degraded machine, all Routine volume may generate more repeats. If all critical volume moves to M1, M1 becomes a single point of failure.
- Speeding up assay after recovery shifts the bottleneck to technical validators, already at 101% utilization, and then to medical validators for Urgent/Complex cases.

**S3: Medical validator or specialist absence**

Likely sequence:

`Technical validation completes  Medical-validation queue grows  Hold-for-specialist queue grows  Urgent SLA breaches and Complex-panel backlog`

Evidence:

- Medical validators: 93% peak utilization, 22h median queue.
- Specialist review: 87% peak utilization, 36h median queue.
- Hold-for-specialist-review edge rises from 6h normal to 12h peak, and hold-to-medical-validation rises from 18h to 48h.
- Complex-panel cases are much more likely to need these steps.

Relief traps:

- Urgent-first rules push more cases into medical validation without creating more validators.
- Adding an evening medical shift helps if validators are available and upstream flow is stable; if the specialist is absent, cases still stall in hold.
- Bypassing specialist review reduces queue time but creates clinical and traceability risk.
- **I10 outsourcing** can relieve specialist review, but the 12h transport delay makes it unsuitable for most Urgent cases and can obscure failure feedback and traceability.

**S4: Courier compression or delay**

Likely sequence:

`Courier spike/delay  Registration queue  Pre-analytical queue  Defects/recollections  Preparation/Assay instability  Downstream validation waves`

Evidence:

- Courier intake: 88% peak utilization, 7.5h median queue.
- Registration: 85% peak utilization, 2.8h median queue.
- Pre-analytical check: 96% peak utilization, 9.5h median queue.
- Recollection loop: 60h median in peaks; recollection-to-cancel rises from 290 normal to 370 peak.

Relief traps:

- **I7 courier smoothing** helps the front end, but if smoothed arrivals are not coordinated with assay and validator schedules, the process can simply create a new synchronized wave at assay.
- Early rejection of poor samples, **I6**, is valuable only if clinicians respond quickly; otherwise it converts a pre-analytical defect into a long recollection loop.
- Automating registration can hide metadata defects until pre-analytical check, shifting rather than removing work.

**S5: Front-end defect/accession storm**

Likely sequence:

`Manual accession correction queue  Pre-analytical blockage  Preparation starvation  Recollection loop  Intake/clerk overload  Late downstream surge`

Evidence:

- Accession correction: only two specialists, 91% peak utilization, 14h median queue.
- Manual correction-to-test-preparation time rises from 5h to 16h.
- Pre-analytical-to-recollection rises from 760 to 780 cases even with lower peak total volume; the recollection rate roughly doubles.
- Recollections reduce intake capacity because clerks spend time explaining errors.

Relief traps:

- **I4 cross-training technical validators into accession correction** can unblock many orders, but technical validators are already at 101% utilization. Moving them can create a technical-validation bottleneck.
- More automated registration helps only cases with complete digital metadata; incomplete or falsely “complete” records become concentrated in the small correction pool.
- More aggressive pre-analytical rejection can improve assay purity but overload physicians and recollection intake.

## 3. Robust redesign package

**B1. Replace blanket Urgent-first with aging-capped, SLA-based priority lanes**  
Use a controlled version of I1, not strict Urgent-first.

- Urgent cases get priority when projected release is approaching the 18h contractual threshold.
- Complex-panel cases get protected access to medical validation and specialist review, because they are disproportionately likely to need those resources.
- Routine cases remain batchable but receive an aging override after a defined wait, for example after 8–12h in preparation or assay queue.
- Repeat-after-failure cases receive priority only when they are likely to be resolved before the original Urgent SLA is lost; otherwise they should be scheduled into a controlled repeat window.

Why robust: this protects the Urgent SLA without allowing all Urgent cases to starve everything else. It also prevents Complex-panel cases from silently accumulating behind Urgent work.

Assumptions: accurate case-type labels, reliable projected completion times, and manager discipline not to override aging caps.

Downstream overload risk: if medical validation and specialist review are not protected, priority merely pushes cases faster into those queues. The priority rule must therefore be linked to validator/specialist triggers in Section 4.

Failure mode: if Urgent labeling is abused or if a sudden Urgent surge exceeds reserved capacity, Routine and Complex-panel cases will still age too much.

**B2. Front-end quality and metadata control: I6 + selective I8 + baseline I7**  
Create an early pre-analytical defect triage that can reject or correct poor samples within 2h. Automate registration only for orders with complete, trustworthy digital metadata. Smooth courier slots to reduce 10:00 and 17:00 spikes.

Why robust: the largest avoidable rework loop is front-end. Recollection has a 60h median in peaks and consumes intake capacity; manual correction has a 14h queue in peaks. Preventing defects before preparation and assay protects every downstream resource.

Assumptions: defects are detectable within 2h, clinicians respond to recollection requests quickly, and digital metadata quality is auditable.

Downstream overload risk: aggressive triage can increase recollection volume and physician explaining work. Registration automation can expose pre-analytical and assay bottlenecks by feeding them faster.

Failure mode: if triage adds a step without reducing defects, or if “complete metadata” is falsely complete, the redesign increases hidden rework.

**B3. Cross-train with floors, not unrestricted reassignment: modified I4**  
Cross-train two technical validators to support accession correction only when correction queue exceeds a threshold, while preserving a minimum technical-validation staffing floor.

Why robust: accession correction unblocks many cases and has only two specialists. A small flexible pool can prevent preparation starvation during defect storms.

Assumptions: cross-trained staff can maintain accuracy; accession tasks are simple enough to learn quickly; technical validation has enough slack at the moment of reassignment.

Downstream overload risk: technical validators are at 101% utilization in peaks. Moving them during an assay recovery wave can create a technical-validation queue and delay both Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

Failure mode: simultaneous accession and technical-validation surges; the cross-training policy then has no safe place to move staff.

**B4. Use controlled Routine batching; avoid blanket I2 and avoid I9 as a default**  
Keep batch sizes flexible. Increase from 12 to 24 only for Routine cases when preparation is the bottleneck and assay has available capacity. Exclude Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure from large batches. Cap batch wait so Routine cases do not drift toward the 72h target.

Why robust: Routine cases tolerate batching; critical cases do not. Controlled batching reduces preparation setup load without destroying Urgent response.

Assumptions: batch composition can be controlled and urgent exceptions are enforced.

Downstream overload risk: larger batches create assay bursts, which can overload assay machines and then technical validators. Holding Routine releases for batch reports, I9, adds waiting and should not be used when Routine p90 is already 120h in peaks.

Failure mode: if Urgent share becomes very high, Urgent exceptions fragment batches so much that the efficiency gain disappears.

**B5. Dynamic assay routing and repeat control, not fixed M1 dedication**  
Use a conditional form of I3. Reserve some M1 capacity for Urgent and Complex-panel cases when M1 is healthy, but do not send all Routine work to M2. Route repeat-prone cases away from the machine or shift associated with failure. Add confirmation rules after night shifts and after M2 maintenance events.

Why robust: protects critical cases while limiting repeat loops, the main capacity Multiplying factor.

Assumptions: machine-level failure data are available by shift and case type; M1 and M2 capacities can be dynamically scheduled.

Downstream overload risk: protecting M1 can overload it; overusing M2 can increase repeats; improving assay flow pushes work into technical validation, already above 100% utilization in peaks.

Failure mode: M1 outage while Urgent/Complex share is high; the reserve policy must be suspended immediately.

**B6. Medical-validation surge capacity with selective overflow**  
Add a temporary evening medical-validation shift, I5, when queues cross trigger levels. Use specialist-review checklists and pre-case packet completeness rules to reduce avoidable holds. Keep I10 as a pre-qualified contingency for stable Complex-panel overflow only, not as the default Urgent solution.

Why robust: directly addresses the peak bottleneck created by Urgent and Complex-panel cases without permanently expanding capacity everywhere.

Assumptions: evening validators are available, specialist review can be standardized, and the partner lab’s 12h transport delay still beats internal queue time for selected cases.

Downstream overload risk: medical validators can be starved by upstream assay/technical queues or overwhelmed by specialist packets; outsourcing creates traceability and feedback delays.

Failure mode: if Complex-panel cases are actually unstable or Urgent-like, the 12h transport delay makes outsourcing unacceptable.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Cross-training can create the next bottleneck.** If accession queues and technical-validation queues spike together, moving validators to accession correction protects preparation but starves technical validation, which is already at 101% utilization in peaks.

- **Priority rules can hide starvation.** Urgent-first behavior may reappear informally even with aging caps, causing Routine and Complex-panel cases to accumulate. This is especially risky because Complex-panel cases need medical validation and specialist review later.

- **Triage can become a extra queue instead of a quality gate.** If defects cannot be identified within 2h or clinicians do not respond, I6 adds handling time without preventing recollection loops.

- **Automation can expose hidden bottlenecks.** Selective registration automation may reduce clerical time but push incomplete or falsely complete metadata into pre-analytical check, where failure creates recollections.

- **Assay protection depends on machine reliability.** Reserving M1 for Urgent and Complex-panel cases is fragile if M1 degrades or if M2 becomes overloaded with Routine volume and repeat generates extra cycles.

- **Outsourcing creates traceability risk.** I10 can relieve specialist review, but the 12h transport delay, lower visibility, and slower failure feedback may delay corrective action and complicate Urgent contractual reporting.

- **Courier smoothing assumes compliance.** If urgent or high-value samples still arrive in unannounced spikes, the front-end capacity plan can be invalidated despite average arrival improvements.

- **Dashboard gaming.** Case managers may label borderline cases as Urgent or Complex-panel to obtain priority, which would invalidate the priority policy and worsen WIP.
