# 1. Objectives and constraints

## 1.1 What we are actually optimizing

This is a multi-objective batch decision under uncertainty (more cases will arrive during the shift), so there is no single optimum — only a defensible point on a trade-off surface. The objectives, stated so they can be measured later:

| # | Objective | Operational proxy |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | Meet commitments | Expected SLA-breach cost, weighted by priority tier (urgent breaches cost far more than low-priority ones) |
| O2 | Get work right the first time | Expected rework/appeal probability of the assignment |
| O3 | Keep flow moving today | Expected completions per available hour, including waiting time, not just hands-on time |
| O4 | Grow capability | Supervised executions that move someone from level 1→2 or 2→3 on a documented priority |
| O5 | Sustainable, fair load | Even distribution of *sustainable* load (hours and case count), plus even distribution of desirable work, not just volume |
| O6 | Preserve case ownership | Fewer, warmer handovers; especially on complex and already-reassigned cases |
| O7 | Stay resilient | Reserve capacity for intraday arrivals and escalations; reduce single-person dependence on scarce skills |

These conflict pairwise in ways that matter here: O4 costs O1 and O3 (trainees are slower); O2 pushes work to specialists, which harms O5 and O7 (queues form behind two people); O6 conflicts with O2 whenever the current owner is not the best-skilled person; O5 conflicts with O6 whenever the overloaded person is the owner. The policy's job is not to dissolve these conflicts but to make the chosen balance visible and revisable.

## 1.2 Hard constraints — safety, compliance and authorization

These are never traded off by the scoring function. A violation is a defect, not a low score.

- **H1. Authorization floor.** Skill 0 means *not allowed*. It is not a "trainee with supervision" case; supervision cannot manufacture authorization. (This bites immediately — see §1.4.)
- **H2. Security Review** (rule 6): executed by skill 3, or by skill 1–2 only with a *named* supervisor from {R7_Gita, R8_Hugo} who has the capacity to actually review.
- **H3. Final Approval** (rule 7): finalized only by skill 2–3. Skill 1 may prepare the file but the finalize event must carry a skill-2+ resource.
- **H4. No unsupervised trainee on high/urgent work** (rule 2): priority in {high, urgent} requires skill ≥ 2, or skill 1 with an explicit skill-3 supervisor recorded on the case.
- **H5. Batch eligibility** (rule 8): Quality Check may be batched only for normal/low priority with >24h SLA remaining.
- **H6. Assignment cap** (rule 1): at most 4 new assignments per resource per decision batch, where "new" means the resource was not already the case owner. Supervision counts as 0.5.
- **H7. Capacity truth.** Committed hours must not exceed ~85% of hours remaining, or the work must be explicitly scheduled to the next shift with SLA slack to cover it. Over-committing is a silent SLA breach.
- **H8. Segregation of duties (proposed, to be confirmed with compliance).** The Quality Check on a case should not be performed *or reviewed* by the person who executed the work being checked; Final Approval should not be performed by the person who did the Rework Correction on the same case. This is not in the stated rules but is standard in service processes; I flag it rather than assume it, and one assignment below carries a caveat because of it.

## 1.3 Soft constraints — preferences with documented override

These are scored, and may be overridden, but the override must be written into `assignment_reason` with a reason code so it is auditable.

- **S1. Training share** (rule 3): ≥20% of normal/low-priority assignments support a documented training priority, *if* SLA risk remains acceptable. The conditional clause is part of the rule, not an excuse — when it is invoked, the skipped training becomes logged "training debt" rather than quietly disappearing.
- **S2. Overtime protection** (rule 4): avoid new work for high-overtime-risk resources unless SLA is urgent and no safer alternative exists. I read this as being about *today's hours*: a low-effort case with 48h of slack, scheduled to the next shift, is a different act from adding to today's pile.
- **S3. Continuity** (rule 5): for high-complexity, high-handover-cost cases, prefer the current owner unless they are skill 0–1 with no feasible supervision.
- **S4. Reserve capacity**: keep at least one skill-3 resource per critical activity below ~70% utilization for escalations and arrivals.
- **S5. Cross-team pairing**: prefer pairings that thicken the working-together network across team boundaries, as insurance against absence.

## 1.4 Two data problems found before any assignment was made

A policy that silently routes around bad reference data is not auditable. Two inconsistencies fall out of cross-checking the skill matrix against the stated training priorities:

1. **Four of nine documented training priorities are unexecutable.** R4_David and R8_Hugo have training priority *approval* but hold Approval = 0; R5_Eva and R10_Jonas have training priority *security* but hold Security = 0. Under H1 they cannot perform that activity at all, supervised or not — so no case assignment can develop them. Note in particular that C015's note ("training candidate for Eva/Jonas with supervision") is not executable as written. Either the matrix is stale, or the development plans were written without reference to authorization levels. Until it is resolved, these four can only *shadow* (observe, no case actions), which I count separately from training and never toward the 20% target.
2. **Two of these priorities look misaligned with where the person actually is.** David is Security = 2 — one step from the scarcest skill in the whole matrix — while being pointed at Approval = 0. Hugo is Quality/Rework/Security = 3 and pointed at Approval = 0. Redirecting David toward Security 2→3 would relieve the single worst structural bottleneck in this org; a development plan is also a capacity plan.

Both are escalated to capability management in §3.4, not patched over.

# 2. Assignment policy

## 2.1 Shape of the algorithm

Four phases: hard filtering, scored matching in two waves (SLA-critical first, then a batch optimization for the rest), global repair against the batch-level constraints, and explanation emission. Weights are urgency-dependent and are a *published governance choice*, not a tuned secret.

```
INPUT: queue Q, resources R, skill matrix S, rules, calibrated timing model
OUTPUT: assignments with reasons, fallbacks, and monitoring flags

PHASE 0 — FEATURES
  for each case c:
      est_hours(c, r)   <- timing model (§2.2), depends on activity, complexity, skill(r)
      breach_risk(c, r) <- (est_hours + expected_queue_wait) / sla_remaining
      needed_level(c)   <- 3 if complexity >= 0.75 else 2 if complexity >= 0.45 else 1
  flag data defects: training priorities where skill == 0 (cannot be satisfied by assignment)

PHASE 1 — HARD ELIGIBILITY FILTER  (produces the allowed set A(c))
  r is eligible for c iff ALL of:
      S[r][activity(c)] >= 1                                        # H1, never overridable
      activity == Security Review  -> S == 3, or (S in {1,2} and named supervisor
                                       in {Gita, Hugo} with >= 0.5h review capacity)   # H2
      activity == Final Approval   -> S >= 2 for the finalize event                    # H3
      priority in {high, urgent}   -> S >= 2, or S == 1 with a named skill-3 supervisor # H4
      committed(r) + est_hours(c,r) <= 0.85 * hours_remaining(r)
          OR case can be scheduled next shift (sla_slack > est_hours + one shift)       # H7
      new_assignments(r) < 4                                                            # H6
      SoD: r did not execute the work now being checked/approved                        # H8
  if A(c) is empty -> EXCEPTION LADDER (§2.9)

PHASE 2 — SCORE  (higher is better)
  score(c, r) =  w_sla   * Urgency(c, r)
               + w_skill * SkillFit(c, r)
               + w_cont  * Continuity(c, r)
               - w_load  * Load(r)
               - w_ot    * Overtime(r, when)
               + w_train * Training(c, r)
               + w_res   * Resilience(c, r)

PHASE 3 — MATCH IN TWO WAVES
  Wave A (urgent, then high): earliest-deadline-first; assign argmax score;
          commit hours; record a NAMED FALLBACK with a TRIGGER TIME.
  Wave B (normal, low): solve a min-cost assignment (Hungarian) over the remaining
          cases and residual capacity, so that load-levelling and training slots are
          chosen jointly rather than greedily. Allow "schedule next shift" as a column.

PHASE 4 — GLOBAL REPAIR
  check S1 (training share >= 20% of normal/low assignments)
  check H6 caps, S4 reserve capacity, and per-resource supervision load (<= 2 learners)
  if violated: swap the lowest-regret pair(s) until satisfied, or log an explicit
               override with a reason code and a training-debt entry

PHASE 5 — EXPLANATION
  for each case emit: top-3 contributing score terms, the runner-up resource, and
  why the runner-up lost. Write to assignment_reason so it is minable later.
```

## 2.2 Timing model (calibrated, not invented)

`est_hours(c, r) = base_hours(activity) × (0.7 + 0.6 × complexity) × skill_factor(S[r][a])`, with `skill_factor = {3: 0.85, 2: 1.0, 1: 1.5}` and a supervisor overhead of `0.25h + 0.15 × est_hours`.

`base_hours` and `skill_factor` are **estimated from the event log**, not assumed: `pm4py.get_service_time` per activity, and median service time per (resource, activity) pair versus the specialist median, gives an empirical competence estimate (§7). This matters because it lets us detect when the declared skill matrix disagrees with observed performance.

## 2.3 SLA urgency scoring

```
breach_risk = (est_hours + expected_queue_wait(r)) / sla_remaining
Urgency = min(1, breach_risk) * tier_multiplier[priority]
tier_multiplier = {urgent: 1.0, high: 0.7, normal: 0.35, low: 0.15}
```

Two deliberate choices. First, urgency uses *time-to-breach relative to the work still required*, not the raw deadline: a 6h deadline on 2h of work is calmer than a 9h deadline on 8h of work. Second, `expected_queue_wait` is per-resource — a specialist with three cases in front of this one is not "fast" for this case, which is exactly the failure mode of specialist-only routing.

## 2.4 Skill-match scoring

```
SkillFit = (S[r][a] - 1) / 2  -  0.5 * max(0, needed_level(c) - S[r][a])
           +  0.15 if r is a specialist (S == 3) and complexity >= 0.75
```
The penalty term is asymmetric on purpose: being under the needed level for the complexity hurts much more than being over it helps. A level-3 resource on a 0.2-complexity eligibility check is a mild waste; a level-1 on a 0.85-complexity security case is a defect generator.

## 2.5 Continuity and handover-cost scoring

```
Continuity = +1                       if r == current_owner(c)
           = -handover_penalty(c, r)  otherwise

handover_penalty = band_cost[cost_band] * (1 + complexity) * (1 + 0.25 * reassignment_count)
                   + 0.5 if reassignment_count >= 2        # crossing the observed risk threshold
                   - 0.4 if handover is WARM (see below)
band_cost = {none: 0, low: 0.15, medium: 0.35, high: 0.60}
```

**Warm versus cold handovers.** The historical evidence says an owner change on a high-complexity case adds a median 2.7 hours of waiting. That number is measured over handovers as they are normally done: the case is dropped into someone's queue and the context is rebuilt from the file. Most of those 2.7 hours are queueing and context reconstruction, not irreducible physics. A *warm* handover — a live 10–15 minute briefing from the outgoing owner, an immediate start by the incoming one, and the outgoing owner retained as customer contact — is a materially different mechanism. The policy distinguishes the two, prices them differently, and §6 measures them separately so the discount is validated rather than assumed.

**A caution about the reassignment evidence.** "Cases with >2 reassignments have 1.8x higher rework" is observational and confounded: cases get reassigned *because* they are hard, and hard cases rework more regardless of who touches them. Treating reassignment count as a purely causal cost would over-freeze difficult cases with the wrong owner. The policy therefore treats it as a **risk marker that raises the burden of justification**, not as a fixed penalty, and §6 estimates the handover effect stratified by complexity band.

## 2.6 Workload and overtime fairness scoring

```
Load = 0.5 * norm(open_cases[r]) + 0.5 * (1 - norm(hours_remaining[r]))
Overtime = ot_weight[risk] * timing_factor
ot_weight = {low: 0, medium: 0.3, high: 1.0}
timing_factor = 1.0 if scheduled today, 0.3 if scheduled next shift
```
Load deliberately combines case count and remaining hours, because they disagree here: R4_David has 14 open cases *and* 3 hours left (severely constrained), while R7_Gita has 10 open and 4.5 hours (moderately constrained).

**Anti-dumping guard.** Pure load-levelling has a well-known failure mode: the least-loaded person becomes the batch's dumping ground and is least-loaded no longer, every single batch. The policy caps the share of a batch's new assignments that may go to any one resource (H6) and §6 tracks a dumping index.

## 2.7 Training and development scoring

```
Training = 1.0  if a in training_priority(r) and S[r][a] in {1,2}
                and priority in {normal, low}
                and sla_slack >= 3 * est_hours
                and a qualified supervisor has review capacity      # supervised execution
         = 0.4  if the same but the learner only shadows            # observation, no case actions
         = 0    otherwise
```
Only *supervised execution* counts toward the 20% target. Shadowing is logged and valued but is not the same thing, and conflating them would let us report training that never happened. Training slots are scheduled against cases with generous slack so that the extra service time is absorbed by the deadline rather than by the customer.

## 2.8 Resilience and bottleneck avoidance

```
Resilience = - scarcity(a) * utilization_pressure(r)          # protect scarce specialists
             + 0.2 if pairing crosses team boundaries          # thicken the network
             + 0.15 if r would become a second capable person for a critical activity
scarcity(a) = 1 / (number of resources authorized at level 3 for a)
```
Concretely: Security Review has exactly two authorized executors (Gita, Hugo) out of ten resources, and the rules forbid everyone else except supervised level 1–2 — of whom only R4_David (S=2) and R3_Celine (S=1) exist. Every security assignment therefore consumes a scarce, non-substitutable resource, and the policy refuses to run both security specialists above ~80% utilization in a batch. Final Approval is nearly as thin: one level-3 (Farid) and two level-2s (Bruno, Eva).

## 2.9 Tie-breaking and exception handling

**Tie-breaking**, in order: (1) prefer the current owner; (2) prefer the lower-loaded resource; (3) prefer the lower overtime risk; (4) prefer the assignment that supports a documented training priority; (5) prefer the one that preserves specialist reserve; (6) prefer the resource who has had fewer high-visibility cases recently (equity of desirable work); (7) rotate deterministically by resource ID so ties do not always break the same way.

**Exception ladder**, when the eligible set is empty or every option breaches:
1. Split the activity into prepare + finalize (legitimate for Final Approval under H3) so a constrained specialist only spends minutes.
2. Recruit supervised capacity: a level-1/2 executor with a named skill-3 supervisor, if H4/H2 allow.
3. Warm-handover to the best available specialist with an immediate start.
4. Schedule to the next shift, if SLA slack permits, and say so on the case.
5. Request an SLA extension and notify the customer *proactively and in parallel*, not after the breach.
6. Escalate to the team lead as an unassignable case with the blocking constraint named. An unassignable case must be visible, never quietly parked.

**Named fallback with a trigger time.** Every urgent assignment carries a second resource and a clock ("if work has not started by T+45min, it moves to X"). This converts a silent risk into a scheduled decision.

**Human override.** Assignments are strong defaults, not commands. A worker or lead can decline or swap with one click; the system records who, what, and why. Overrides are reviewed weekly and are the primary signal for re-tuning weights — a policy that is overridden 30% of the time is wrong about something, and the workers know what.

## 3.3 The three calls that could reasonably go the other way

The queue does not have one right answer. Three decisions are close, and the team should know which way to flip them if conditions change.

**C001 — hand over, or keep Celine under supervision?** The rules as written point at keeping her: S3 says prefer continuity unless the owner is skill 0–1 *and no supervision is feasible*, and supervision is feasible (Gita and Hugo are both available). But the arithmetic is unforgiving. Celine at Security = 1 on a 0.88-complexity case is roughly 2.4 hours of work plus supervisory review, against 3 hours of SLA, with the historical evidence that keeping a weak-skilled owner adds a median 3.3 hours of service time. Hugo cold would be worse still — a 2.7h median handover delay plus 1.4h of work overshoots the deadline outright. The warm handover is the only path with a plausible finish inside 3 hours, and the enterprise customer's specialist expectation is closer to contractual than preferential. **Flip it if** the SLA extension is granted, in which case Celine-under-Hugo becomes the better long-run choice because it builds the third security capability this organization badly needs.

**C003 — move it, or keep David?** Moving takes reassignment_count from 2 to 3, into the band with 1.8x observed rework. Keeping it respects continuity but parks the case behind David's 14 open cases and 3 remaining hours, and the "repeated diagnosis loop" note suggests the current approach is not working. I weighted the stuck loop over the reassignment statistic partly because that statistic is confounded by complexity (§2.5). **Flip it if** the loop turns out to be caused by missing information rather than missing expertise — in which case a new owner learns nothing new and you should chase the information instead.

**C012 — train Bruno, or protect him?** Rule S2 says avoid new work for high-overtime-risk resources. Bruno is high risk with 12 open cases. Strictly, giving him anything is an override. I made it anyway because the alternative has a nasty dynamic: chronic overload becomes permanent exclusion from development, which keeps the person slow, which keeps them overloaded. So the slot is made as cheap as possible — the lowest-complexity, lowest-priority case in the queue, 48 hours of slack, executed next shift, supervised, roughly 1 hour — and it is **funded** by moving C010 off him rather than stacked on top. Net, his workload goes down. **Flip it if** Bruno prefers to keep C010 and skip the training this cycle; that is his call to make, and the deferral gets logged as training debt so it resurfaces next batch.

## 3.4 Escalations that no assignment can fix

- **Authorization blocks development for four people.** David and Hugo (approval priority, skill 0) and Eva and Jonas (security priority, skill 0) cannot be developed through case assignment at all. Requested: a formal level-0→1 onboarding path (N shadow sessions plus a competency sign-off) so shadowing converts into authorization, or a correction if the matrix is simply out of date.
- **Realign two development plans with the capacity gap.** David is Security = 2, one step from the organization's scarcest skill, but is pointed at Approval = 0. Redirecting him to Security 2→3 would create a third security executor and directly relieve the Gita/Hugo bottleneck. Hugo's approval plan should either get a real onboarding path or be replaced.
- **Two structural single points of failure.** Security Review: two authorized executors for the entire org. Final Approval: one level-3 and two level-2s, with Farid simultaneously the only level-3 negotiator. Any absence by Farid, Gita or Hugo turns a normal day into a breach day. This is a staffing and training decision, not a routing decision — no assignment policy can route around a skill that nobody has.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

## 4.1 What the baselines actually do on this queue

Tracing them concretely is more informative than describing them in the abstract.

**First-available** (lowest open cases with enough hours, ignoring specialization except skill 0). Resources sorted: Jonas 4, Farid 5, Celine 6, Hugo 7, Eva 8, Amal 9, Gita 10, Iris 11, Bruno 12, David 14. It breaks the safety rules on its first three cases:
- C001 (urgent Security, cx 0.88): Jonas and Farid are Security = 0, so it lands on **Celine at level 1** — a violation of rule 6 unless supervision is bolted on, on the hardest security case in the queue with 3 hours left.
- C002 (urgent Final Approval): lands on **Jonas at Approval = 1**, who under rule 7 may prepare but not finalize. Violation.
- C011 (urgent VIP Negotiation): lands on **Jonas at Negotiation = 1** — a violation of rule 2, unsupervised trainee on urgent work, with a VIP.

It would also dump four-plus cases on Jonas (hitting the cap immediately), move C007 to a fourth owner despite its high handover cost, and pull C016 off Eva — a 0.79-complexity, high-handover case with 7 hours left — for no reason other than someone else's queue being shorter.

**Specialist-only** (highest skill, ties broken by open cases). Diagnosis level 3 is Celine (6 open) and David (14), so Celine takes **C003, C009 and C016**; rework level 3 includes Celine at the lowest open count, so she also draws **C007 and C014**. That is five cases and far beyond both her 6 hours and the 4-case cap — the policy needs a fallback it does not have. Security goes to Hugo on tie-break (**C001, C008, C015**, all three), leaving Gita with nothing and no escalation reserve. Approval level 3 is Farid alone (**C002, C010**), negotiation level 3 tie-breaks to Farid (**C005, C011**) — four cases on Farid. Meanwhile David, Iris, Bruno and Gita receive nothing at all. Training is structurally impossible: a policy that always picks the highest skill never lets anyone practice.

## 4.2 Dimension-by-dimension

These are model-based estimates from the timing and risk assumptions in §2, not measurements. They are directionally useful and should be replaced by measured values after a few weeks of running (§6).

| Dimension | First-available | Specialist-only | Proposed policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Expected throughput** | Highest *start* rate, lowest finish rate: work begins fast but level-1 resources on complex cases run ~1.5x longer and generate mid-case handoffs. Good on trivial work (C004, C013), bad everywhere else | Fastest per-case execution but severe head-of-line blocking — five cases queued behind Celine and three behind Hugo, while four people idle. Total hands-on hours lowest; wall-clock completions mediocre | Middle on hands-on hours (two trainee executions cost ~1 extra hour) but best on completed-by-deadline, because capacity and deadlines are matched case by case. Deliberately leaves ~2 resources in reserve, which costs throughput today and buys it tomorrow |
| **SLA risk** | Worst. The three urgent cases all land on unqualified or level-1 resources; expect 3–5 breaches or forced emergency reassignments | Better per case, worse in aggregate: C002/C010/C005/C011 all serialize behind Farid and C001/C008/C015 behind Hugo. Expect 1–3 breaches from queueing, exactly the "queues in Security and Approval" the evidence predicts | Lowest, ~0–1 breach. C001 is the genuine risk (see §3.3) and is hedged with a warm handover plus a parallel extension request. Every urgent case carries a named fallback and a trigger time |
| **Error / rework risk** | Highest. Skill mismatch on complex work plus the most mid-case reassignments; C007 to a fourth owner is a rework generator by the log's own evidence | Lowest per executed case — this is the baseline's real strength — but partially given back through rushed work in overloaded queues and cases sitting past their comfortable window | Slightly above specialist-only. Two supervised trainee executions add a little risk, ring-fenced to the two lowest-risk cases in the queue and covered by mandatory review. Complex work (C001, C007, C016, C003) all goes to level 2–3 |
| **Fairness of workload** | Superficially fair (it targets the lowest count) but produces the dumping pathology: Jonas absorbs the batch and is no longer least-loaded, while nothing protects Iris, Bruno or David from tomorrow's version of the same | Worst by a distance. Five cases to Celine, four to Farid, three to Hugo; David, Iris, Bruno and Gita get nothing. Skill becomes a punishment for the capable and a permanent exclusion for everyone else | Best, and fair on two axes rather than one. Volume is levelled (max 2 new per person), overtime exposure is actively reduced for Iris, Bruno and David, and the distribution of *interesting* work is tracked, not just the count. Note honestly that Jonas's slack is limited by his skills, not by scheduling — some of this unfairness is only fixable by training |
| **Skill development** | Accidental and dangerous: it does put level-1 people on work, but on urgent cases without supervision, which is how you produce a bad experience and a rework loop rather than a competent colleague | Zero, by construction, and self-reinforcing — the skill matrix on which it depends can never improve | 22% of normal/low assignments are supervised executions on documented priorities, plus shadow slots. Honest about limits: four of nine documented priorities are blocked by authorization and no routing policy can unblock them |
| **Continuity of ownership** | Poor. Ownership is not an input at all, so it churns cases the log says are the most expensive to churn (C007 at 3 reassignments, C016 high handover cost) | Poor-to-medium and arbitrary: it preserves ownership only when the owner happens to be top-skilled. It would take C016 off Eva and pay the 2.7h high-complexity handover penalty against a 7h deadline | Best. Eleven of sixteen cases keep their owner. Three moves (C001, C003, C010) each have a written justification, and one of them (C001) is executed as a warm handover with the outgoing owner retained as customer contact |
| **Resilience to absence / bottlenecks** | Accidentally spreads work across people, which helps a little, but leaves no coherent reserve and no cross-training, so an absence produces chaos rather than a plan | Worst. It maximizes concentration on exactly the people who are already scarce; losing Farid or Hugo on the day would take out the whole approval or security lane | Best available given the skill matrix. Gita is held as security reserve, Jonas as intake reserve, cross-team pairings (Celine–Farid, Hugo–Jonas) thicken the network, and every urgent case has a named fallback. It cannot fix having only two security specialists — that is escalated as a staffing issue |

## 4.3 When each baseline is the right answer

The proposed policy is not universally better, and pretending otherwise would be the wrong lesson. **First-available wins** when work is genuinely homogeneous and low-stakes, when queue length is the dominant customer complaint, or during a surge where any competent hands beat idle time — its simplicity is a real virtue and it needs no skill matrix to maintain. **Specialist-only wins** when error cost dominates everything (safety-critical, regulatory, irreversible decisions), when volumes are low enough that queueing does not bite, or during an incident when you want the best person on it and nothing else matters. The proposed policy carries real costs of its own: it needs a maintained and *accurate* skill matrix (which we have just shown is partly stale), it needs supervisor time, it is harder to explain than "next free person", and its weights are value judgements that someone must own and defend.

# 5. Explanation to workers

**How today's assignments were put together**

Every morning we look at the open queue and try to match cases to people in a way that is good for the customer, sustainable for you, and useful for your development. Here is what we consider, in plain terms, and you can see the specific reasons on each case in your list.

**We start from who already has the case.** If you are already working something, our default is that you keep it. You have the context, the customer knows you, and moving a case costs real time — our own data says a change of hands on a complicated case typically costs about two and a half hours of waiting. Today eleven of the sixteen cases stayed exactly where they were. When we do move something, we will tell you why, and we do it as a proper conversation between the two of you rather than by dropping it in a queue.

**We look at the deadline against the work still needed**, not just the clock. A case with six hours left and two hours of work on it is calmer than one with nine hours left and eight hours of work. That is why some cases with later deadlines are being started first.

**We look at what the case needs and what the rules allow.** Some work is restricted for good reasons: security reviews need a security specialist or a specialist signing off, and final approvals need someone qualified to finalize. These are not judgements about anyone — they are the boundaries we all work inside.

**We look at your hours and your open cases, to protect you rather than to score you.** If you are carrying a lot right now, we will not add more, and we may move something off your plate. Iris, Bruno and David are getting no new work today for exactly that reason: not because of anything about their work, but because they are carrying the most and we would rather they finish what they have than start something new at six in the evening. Nobody is measured on how many cases they hold, and none of these numbers appear in anyone's performance review.

**We protect a small amount of time for learning.** Today two cases are set up as learning assignments — Amal on a diagnosis case with Celine reviewing, Bruno on a quality check with Jonas reviewing. Both were chosen because they are low-pressure cases with plenty of time, so nobody is learning under a deadline and no customer waits longer because of it. We also fund them: Bruno's learning slot is paid for by moving a different case off him, so his day is lighter, not heavier. If you would rather not take a learning slot on a particular day, just say so — it will be offered again.

**Some development plans are stuck and it is not your fault.** Four people have a development goal in an area they are not currently authorized to work in at all. That is something we need to fix at our end, with a proper onboarding route, and we are raising it this week. In the meantime you can sit in and observe — Jonas will be sitting in on a security review with Hugo today — and we are counting that as progress toward getting you signed off, not as a substitute for it.

**A note on specialists.** Where we route something to a specialist, it is about the shape of that particular case, not a ranking of people. It also means specialists carry a load that is easy to overlook — being the only person who can do something is not a privilege, it is a constraint on your day, which is why we hold some of their time free rather than filling it.

**You can change any of this.** These are suggestions with reasons attached, not instructions. If you know something we do not — the customer only speaks to you, the case is not what the file says, you are not in the state to take a hard case today — swap it or hand it back. We record the change and the reason, and we read them every week. When people override us in the same direction repeatedly, that means our reasoning is wrong and we fix the policy. That has already happened once, which is why we now brief handovers live instead of dropping cases into a queue.

**What we watch, and what we do not.** We track things at the team level: how often cases change hands, how evenly work is spread, how often the deadline is met, whether learning slots actually happen. We do not use these numbers to rank individuals, and we do not use them to compare speed between people doing different work. If they ever start being used that way, tell us — a measure that makes it risky to take a hard case will quietly ruin the whole system.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Each indicator specifies its measurement, its purpose, and a starting threshold. Thresholds are initial guesses to be replaced by measured baselines after four to six weeks — publishing a target before you have a baseline is how you end up gaming the target.

## 6.1 Handover and continuity

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Handover frequency** | Handovers per 100 cases and per case-variant, from the handover-of-work network. Segment by cross-team versus intra-team, and by activity transition | Rising trend with flat volume means the policy is churning. Cross-team handovers are not inherently bad — they indicate healthy cross-coverage |
| **Handover concentration** | Share of all handover edges carried by the top 5 resource pairs; network in-degree and out-degree per resource | A few dominant edges indicate an informal escalation path that should be made explicit |
| **Warm vs cold handover delta** | Median waiting time between the handover event and the next work event, split by whether a briefing/co-work event was recorded within 30 minutes | This validates or refutes the 0.4 warm discount in §2.5. If warm handovers are not measurably faster, the discount comes out of the score |
| **Reassignment count** | Distribution of `reassignment_count` at case close: mean, p90, share above 2 — **stratified by complexity band** | Stratification is essential: the raw >2 rework correlation is confounded by case difficulty. Estimate the handover effect within complexity bands, or with propensity weighting on complexity, priority and activity |
| **Continuity retention** | Share of cases whose `current_owner` is unchanged at close; separately for high-complexity/high-handover cases, where soft rule S3 applies | Target: overall ≥70%; ≥85% in the S3 subgroup |

## 6.2 Skill, compliance and rework

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Skill mismatch rate** | Share of executed events where the executor's skill is below `needed_level(complexity)`, without a recorded supervisor | Soft target ≤10%; every instance on a high/urgent case is reviewed individually |
| **Hard-rule violations** | Count of events breaching H1–H5: skill-0 execution, unsupervised security by level 1–2, finalize-approval by level 1, unsupervised trainee on high/urgent, out-of-window batching | **Must be zero.** A non-zero count is an incident, not a KPI |
| **Rework after assignment** | Probability that a Rework Correction follows within N days of a given assignment, attributed to that assignment. Compare supervised-trainee executions against specialist executions on comparable complexity | If supervised trainee work reworks at more than ~1.2x the specialist rate, the supervision model is not working — usually because reviews are being skipped under time pressure |
| **Appeal rate** | Appeals per closed case, by executing resource band and by reassignment count, stratified by complexity | Same confounding caveat as above |
| **Declared vs observed competence** | Per (resource, activity): median service time and rework-follow rate versus the level-3 median. Compare with the declared skill matrix | This is the automated detector for the stale-matrix problem found in §1.4. Any pair where declared and observed disagree by more than a level goes to capability management |

## 6.3 Workload, overtime and fairness

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Workload distribution** | Gini coefficient and coefficient of variation of assignments per resource per day; repeated on *service-time-weighted* load, because case counts hide difficulty | Weighted Gini is the one that matters; unweighted fairness with weighted unfairness is a common illusion |
| **Dumping index** | Share of each batch's new assignments going to the single least-loaded resource, and the run length of consecutive batches in which the same person is the top recipient | Watch: >40% to one person, or the same person topping three batches in a row |
| **Overtime risk exposure** | New assignments to high-overtime-risk resources per week; committed hours beyond `hours_remaining`; count and reason codes of documented S2 overrides | Every override needs a reason code. A rising override count means the constraint is being normalized away |
| **Equity of desirable work** | Distribution across resources of high-complexity, high-visibility and customer-facing cases, and of training slots | Guards against the quiet pattern where the same people always get routine work and never develop |

## 6.4 SLA and flow

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **SLA miss rate** | Breach rate by priority tier, plus the distribution of remaining slack at closure (p10 slack matters more than the mean) | Urgent tier is the binding one; report tiers separately, never blended |
| **Near-miss rate** | Share of cases closing with under 10% of their SLA window remaining | A leading indicator. Near-misses rise before breaches do |
| **Waiting vs service decomposition** | Per activity and per resource, from inserted case service/waiting times: how much of the elapsed time is queueing versus hands-on work | Tells you whether the problem is capacity or matching. High waiting with low service time means a routing/queueing problem, not a skill problem |
| **Deadline-driven behaviour** | Fraction of work started only in the final quartile of its SLA window | Chronic last-minute starts mean the batch is over-committed regardless of what utilization says |

## 6.5 Development

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Training assignment share** | Supervised executions on a documented training priority, divided by eligible normal/low-priority assignments | Rule 3 floor: 20%. Shadowing is reported separately and never counted here |
| **Training debt** | Planned training slots that were deferred or dropped under SLA pressure, with age | Debt older than two weeks escalates. This is the honest counterpart to the 20% metric |
| **Supervision integrity** | Share of supervised executions with a recorded supervisor review event before closure, and the review's median duration | A review of two minutes is not a review. This is the most likely way the model degrades silently |
| **Progression evidence** | Time for a learner's median service time on an activity to converge toward the specialist median; count of level upgrades per quarter | The only measure that shows training produced capability rather than activity |
| **Blocked development** | Count of documented training priorities where the person's skill is 0 (unexecutable), by resource | Currently 4 of 9. Should trend to zero through authorization paths, not by rewriting the plans |

## 6.6 Resilience and bottlenecks

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Specialist bottleneck** | Queue length and median waiting time immediately before Security Review and Final Approval; utilization of each level-3 resource | Waiting time before an activity rising while its service time stays flat is the signature of a capacity bottleneck |
| **Single-point-of-failure index** | Herfindahl index of activity volume by executor, per activity; count of activities where the top resource carries >50% | Security and Final Approval will both fail this today. That is the point of measuring it |
| **Coverage depth** | Number of resources authorized *and active* (≥1 execution in the last 30 days) per activity | Authorized-but-never-active is a paper capability that will not be there in an emergency |
| **Reserve adherence** | Share of batches where at least one level-3 per critical activity ended below 70% utilization | Reserve is what absorbs the arrivals the batch decision could not see |
| **Network robustness** | Working-together network density and articulation points; simulated removal of each resource | Identifies whose absence disconnects the process |
| **Batching health** | Discovered batches versus policy-authorized batches; batch sizes and the waiting time batching induces | Batching that is discovered but not authorized is usually a symptom of overload, and it silently trades waiting time for efficiency |

## 6.7 Policy conformance and the meta-layer

| Indicator | Measurement | Threshold / watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Deviation from policy** | Share of executed assignments differing from the recommendation, with override reason codes, by resource and by team | Under 5% may mean the policy is being followed unthinkingly; over 25% means it is wrong. Both are findings |
| **Override outcome comparison** | SLA and rework outcomes of overridden assignments versus recommended ones, matched on complexity and priority | If overrides outperform recommendations, the humans have knowledge the model lacks — mine the reason codes and change the weights |
| **Explanation completeness** | Share of assignments carrying a populated, parseable `assignment_reason` with the top contributing factors | Target ~100%. An assignment without a reason is unauditable |
| **Worker acceptance** | Periodic short survey on perceived fairness and explanation clarity, alongside the override rate | The socio-technical counterweight. A policy that is statistically optimal and universally resented will be worked around, and then none of the numbers above mean anything |

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

```python
"""
Learn and audit the dynamic resource-assignment policy from the event log.

Three layers:
  A. LEARN   - estimate the parameters the policy needs (competence, handover cost,
               service/waiting times, roles) from history rather than assumption
  B. AUDIT   - compute the §6 indicators on the deployed policy
  C. REPLAY  - counterfactually score the policy and the baselines on historical data
"""

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

ACTIVITY_TO_SKILL = {
    "Eligibility Review": "Eligibility", "Technical Diagnosis": "Diagnosis",
    "Customer Negotiation": "Negotiation", "Security Review": "Security",
    "Final Approval": "Approval", "Quality Check": "Quality",
    "Rework Correction": "Rework",
}

# --------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. LOAD
# --------------------------------------------------------------------------

def load_log(path: str) -> pd.DataFrame:
    df = pd.read_csv(path)
    df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
    log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    # org:resource, required_skill, case_priority, sla_deadline, case_complexity,
    # current_owner, team, assignment_reason, reassignment_count, workload_snapshot,
    # handover_cost_band are carried through as ordinary columns.
    return log


# ==========================================================================
# A. LEARN THE POLICY PARAMETERS
# ==========================================================================

def empirical_competence(log: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Observed competence per (resource, activity), to validate the declared matrix.

    This is the automated detector for the stale-skill-matrix problem: a resource
    declared at level 0 who nevertheless appears in the log, or a declared level-3
    whose service times sit far above the specialist median, is a data defect.
    """
    log = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
        log,
        case_id_key="case:concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
        start_timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    svc_col = next(c for c in log.columns if "service" in c.lower())

    per_pair = (
        log.groupby(["org:resource", "concept:name"])
           .agg(n=("case:concept:name", "nunique"),
                median_service=(svc_col, "median"),
                median_complexity=("case_complexity", "median"))
           .reset_index()
    )
    best = per_pair.groupby("concept:name")["median_service"].min().rename("best_service")
    per_pair = per_pair.join(best, on="concept:name")
    # >1 means slower than the fastest performer of that activity
    per_pair["speed_ratio"] = per_pair["median_service"] / per_pair["best_service"]
    per_pair["observed_level"] = np.select(
        [per_pair.speed_ratio <= 1.15, per_pair.speed_ratio <= 1.45],
        [3, 2], default=1,
    )
    return per_pair


def rework_rate_by_executor(log: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Probability that Rework Correction follows a given resource's work on a case."""
    reworked = (
        log[log["concept:name"] == "Rework Correction"]["case:concept:name"].unique()
    )
    touched = log.groupby(["org:resource", "concept:name"])["case:concept:name"].unique()
    rows = []
    for (res, act), cases in touched.items():
        cases = pd.Index(cases)
        rows.append({
            "org:resource": res,
            "concept:name": act,
            "n_cases": len(cases),
            "rework_rate": cases.isin(reworked).mean(),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def handover_cost_model(log: pd.DataFrame, warm_window_min: int = 30) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Estimate the delay cost of an owner change, WARM vs COLD, stratified by complexity.

    Stratification matters: the raw ">2 reassignments -> 1.8x rework" figure is
    confounded, because difficult cases both get reassigned more and rework more.
    Comparing only within complexity bands removes most of that confounding; a
    propensity model on (complexity, priority, activity, tenure) would remove more.
    """
    log = log.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
    g = log.groupby("case:concept:name")
    log["prev_resource"] = g["org:resource"].shift(1)
    log["prev_ts"] = g["time:timestamp"].shift(1)
    log["gap_hours"] = (log["time:timestamp"] - log["prev_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    log["is_handover"] = (
        log["prev_resource"].notna() & (log["prev_resource"] != log["org:resource"])
    )
    # A warm handover shows a briefing/co-work event, or a very short gap: the
    # incoming resource picked the case up immediately rather than queueing it.
    log["is_warm"] = log["is_handover"] & (log["gap_hours"] * 60 <= warm_window_min)
    log["complexity_band"] = pd.cut(
        log["case_complexity"], [0, 0.35, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0],
        labels=["low", "medium", "high", "very_high"],
    )
    return (
        log.groupby(["complexity_band", "is_handover", "is_warm"], observed=True)["gap_hours"]
           .agg(["count", "median", "mean"])
           .reset_index()
    )


def discover_structure(log: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    """Resource networks, roles and similarity - the socio-technical picture."""
    hw   = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log, beta=0)
    wt   = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log)
    sim  = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log)
    roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log)

    pm4py.save_vis_sna(hw, "audit/handover_network.png")
    pm4py.save_vis_sna(wt, "audit/working_together.png")
    # pm4py.view_sna(hw)  # interactive alternative

    # Roles discovered from behaviour vs the declared team column: a resource whose
    # discovered role sits outside their team is doing informal cross-team work,
    # which is both a resilience asset and a governance question.
    # Resource similarity identifies who is genuinely substitutable for whom -
    # the input to the coverage-depth and bus-factor indicators.
    return {"handover": hw, "working_together": wt, "similarity": sim, "roles": roles}


def bottleneck_profile(log: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    """Where the queues form. Performance DFG separates waiting from service."""
    perf_dfg, start, end = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
    service = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")
    batches = pm4py.discover_batches(log)  # unauthorized batching shows up here
    return {"perf_dfg": perf_dfg, "start": start, "end": end,
            "service_time": service, "batches": batches}


# ==========================================================================
# B. AUDIT THE DEPLOYED POLICY  (the §6 indicators)
# ==========================================================================

def audit(log: pd.DataFrame, skill_matrix: dict, period: str = "W") -> dict:
    ind = {}

    # --- handover frequency and concentration -----------------------------
    hw = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log)
    edges = pd.Series(hw.connections).sort_values(ascending=False)
    n_cases = log["case:concept:name"].nunique()
    ind["handover_per_100_cases"] = 100 * edges.sum() / n_cases
    ind["top5_edge_share"] = edges.head(5).sum() / max(edges.sum(), 1e-9)

    # --- reassignment, stratified by complexity ---------------------------
    per_case = log.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        reassignments=("reassignment_count", "max"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "max"),
        priority=("case_priority", "last"),
    )
    per_case["band"] = pd.cut(per_case.complexity, [0, 0.35, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0])
    ind["reassignment_p90"] = per_case.reassignments.quantile(0.90)
    ind["share_over_2_by_band"] = (
        per_case.assign(over2=per_case.reassignments > 2)
                .groupby("band", observed=True)["over2"].mean()
    )

    # --- skill mismatch and hard-rule violations --------------------------
    def level(row):
        return skill_matrix.get(row["org:resource"], {}).get(
            ACTIVITY_TO_SKILL.get(row["concept:name"], ""), np.nan)

    log = log.copy()
    log["skill_level"] = log.apply(level, axis=1)
    log["needed"] = np.select(
        [log.case_complexity >= 0.75, log.case_complexity >= 0.45], [3, 2], default=1)
    supervised = log["assignment_reason"].fillna("").str.contains("supervis", case=False)

    ind["skill_mismatch_rate"] = (log.skill_level < log.needed).mean()
    ind["violation_skill_zero"] = int((log.skill_level == 0).sum())
    ind["violation_security"] = int((
        (log["concept:name"] == "Security Review") & (log.skill_level < 3) & ~supervised
    ).sum())
    ind["violation_approval"] = int((
        (log["concept:name"] == "Final Approval") & (log.skill_level < 2)
    ).sum())
    ind["violation_unsupervised_trainee"] = int((
        log.case_priority.isin(["high", "urgent"]) & (log.skill_level < 2) & ~supervised
    ).sum())
    # all four must be zero

    # --- workload distribution and dumping --------------------------------
    per_res = log.groupby(["org:resource", pd.Grouper(key="time:timestamp", freq=period)]) \
                 .size().unstack(fill_value=0)
    def gini(x):
        x = np.sort(np.asarray(x, dtype=float)); n = len(x)
        return 0.0 if x.sum() == 0 else (2*np.arange(1, n+1) - n - 1).dot(x) / (n * x.sum())
    ind["workload_gini"] = per_res.sum(axis=1).pipe(gini)
    ind["dumping_index"] = (per_res.max(axis=0) / per_res.sum(axis=0).replace(0, np.nan)).mean()

    # --- overtime exposure -------------------------------------------------
    ind["overrides_overtime"] = int(
        log["assignment_reason"].fillna("").str.contains("OVERRIDE_OT", case=False).sum())

    # --- SLA misses and near misses ---------------------------------------
    closed = log.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        end=("time:timestamp", "max"),
        deadline=("sla_deadline", "last"),
        priority=("case_priority", "last"),
    )
    closed["deadline"] = pd.to_datetime(closed["deadline"], utc=True)
    closed["slack_h"] = (closed.deadline - closed.end).dt.total_seconds() / 3600.0
    ind["sla_miss_by_priority"] = closed.assign(miss=closed.slack_h < 0) \
                                        .groupby("priority")["miss"].mean()
    ind["near_miss_rate"] = ((closed.slack_h >= 0) & (closed.slack_h < 1.0)).mean()

    # --- rework / appeal after assignment ---------------------------------
    ind["rework_by_executor"] = rework_rate_by_executor(log)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    ind["looping_variant_share"] = sum(
        c for v, c in variants.items() if list(v).count("Technical Diagnosis") > 1
    ) / max(sum(variants.values()), 1)

    # --- training share and supervision integrity -------------------------
    reason = log["assignment_reason"].fillna("")
    normal_low = log.case_priority.isin(["normal", "low"])
    trained = reason.str.contains("TRAINING_SUPERVISED", case=False)
    shadow  = reason.str.contains("TRAINING_SHADOW", case=False)
    ind["training_share"] = trained[normal_low].mean()      # rule 3 floor: 0.20
    ind["shadow_share"] = shadow[normal_low].mean()          # reported separately
    ind["training_debt_open"] = int(reason.str.contains("TRAINING_DEFERRED").sum())

    # --- specialist bottleneck --------------------------------------------
    perf = bottleneck_profile(log)
    ind["waiting_before_activity"] = perf["perf_dfg"]        # (a,b) -> median transition time
    exec_share = log.groupby(["concept:name", "org:resource"]).size() \
                    .groupby(level=0).apply(lambda s: (s / s.sum()) ** 2).groupby(level=0).sum()
    ind["hhi_by_activity"] = exec_share                      # single-point-of-failure index
    ind["active_coverage"] = log.groupby("concept:name")["org:resource"].nunique()
    ind["discovered_batches"] = perf["batches"]

    # --- deviation from policy --------------------------------------------
    ind["override_rate"] = reason.str.startswith("OVERRIDE").mean()
    ind["missing_reason_rate"] = (reason == "").mean()
    return ind

def focused_views(log: pd.DataFrame):
    """Targeted slices for the recurring questions."""
    security = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, "concept:name", ["Security Review"], level="event", retain=True)

    urgent = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log, "case_priority", ["urgent"], level="case", retain=True)

    # The long tail: cases taking more than ~3 days end to end. Compare their
    # handover networks against the fast cohort to see which handover patterns
    # actually cost time.
    slow = pm4py.filter_case_performance(log, 3 * 24 * 3600, 60 * 24 * 3600)
    fast = pm4py.filter_case_performance(log, 0, 3 * 24 * 3600)

    return {
        "security_bottleneck": pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(security),
        "urgent_sla": pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(urgent),
        "slow_handovers": pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(slow),
        "fast_handovers": pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(fast),
    }


def counterfactual_replay(historical_log, policy_fn, baselines: dict) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Replay decision points from history under each policy and compare indicators.

    Caveat that must be stated whenever these numbers are shown: replay assumes
    the timing model transfers to counterfactual assignments, which it only
    approximately does. Replay is for ranking policies, never for forecasting
    absolute SLA rates. A shadow-mode pilot - the policy recommends, humans
    decide, and both are logged - gives a far more honest comparison, and its
    disagreements are the best training signal available.
    """
    results = []
    for name, fn in {"proposed": policy_fn, **baselines}.items():
        simulated = fn(historical_log)      # returns a log with reassigned org:resource
        ind = audit(simulated, skill_matrix=SKILL_MATRIX)
        results.append({
            "policy": name,
            "sla_miss_urgent": ind["sla_miss_by_priority"].get("urgent", np.nan),
            "skill_mismatch": ind["skill_mismatch_rate"],
            "workload_gini": ind["workload_gini"],
            "training_share": ind["training_share"],
            "handover_per_100": ind["handover_per_100_cases"],
            "max_hhi": ind["hhi_by_activity"].max(),
        })
    return pd.DataFrame(results)
```

**How the pieces feed the policy.** `empirical_competence` and `rework_rate_by_executor` calibrate `skill_factor` and `SkillFit`, and flag where the declared matrix disagrees with observed behaviour — the check that would have caught the Eva/Jonas security-0 problem before it reached a case note. `handover_cost_model` calibrates `band_cost` and tests the warm-handover discount, stratified so we are not fooled by complexity confounding. `discover_structure` supplies substitutability (resource similarity), informal role structure (role discovery), and bus-factor analysis (working-together network). `bottleneck_profile` and the performance DFG supply the `expected_queue_wait` term and the scarcity weights. `filter_case_performance` and `filter_event_attribute_values` support the slow-versus-fast cohort comparison that reveals which handover patterns genuinely cost time.

**Deployment sequence.** Run in shadow mode first, log every recommendation alongside the human decision, and after four to six weeks compare outcomes on matched cases. Then publish the weights, review them monthly with the team present, and treat a sustained override rate above 25% in any direction as evidence that the model is missing something the workers can see.
