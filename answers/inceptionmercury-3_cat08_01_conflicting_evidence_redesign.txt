# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The core contradiction is not “fast is bad” or “slow is good.” It is that the current process measures the wrong lifecycle boundary. The most frequent path—`Intake claim -> Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement -> Quality/compliance close`—is locally fast and cheap, but process-mining shows it shifts cost and risk into downstream complaints, rework, compliance exceptions, and service credits.

Key artifacts:

- **DFG dominance:** 3,600 of 5,000 cases enter the auto screen, 2,550 go to fast approval, and 2,300 go directly to dispatch replacement. The fast path is the dominant behavior by volume.
- **Local speed is attractive:** `Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval` has median 0.4 hours, and `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement` has median 1.8 hours. Auto screen cost is only EUR 0.20 per case.
- **Downstream performance says otherwise:** `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` has mean 96 hours, median 70 hours, and p90 180 hours. Complaints often arrive well after the case has been closed in the system.
- **Rework statistics show the hidden cost:** Fast approval cases have 14.1% complaint/reopen, versus 3.3% for `Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device`. Fast approval cases are 31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and service credits.
- **Conformance is weak:** Log fitness is 0.91, but only 68% of traces fit the normative Petri net. Alignment diagnostics frequently show three deviations: fast approval before evidence completeness, dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices, and customer complaint/reopen after close.
- **Low precision:** Many low-frequency paths mix auto approval, risk review, and repair in ways not allowed by policy. The discovered model is looser than the normative process.
- **Segment-specific outcomes confirm the risk:** Premium high-value devices on the fast path have 22% complaints/reopens versus 5% on the reviewed/repair path. Standard high-value devices have 18% versus 6%. For low-risk accessories, the fast path is more acceptable, though still not clean.
- **Evidence handling is a major failure source:** Among cases entering evidence request, 26% receive at least two requests. `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` has median 26 hours, and the same edge is a repeated loop.
- **Resource constraints explain the difficulty:** Manual eligibility review has 7 reviewers and queues grow sharply above 82% utilization. Fraud/risk review has only 2 specialists and bottlenecks on Mondays and after marketing campaigns. Escalation board capacity is one 45-minute slot per day. Quality check inspectors are underused except after repair campaigns.

The conclusion: the fast path is the right process for some segments, especially Premium low-risk accessories with complete evidence and strong entitlement checks. The reviewed/repair path is right for high-value devices and unclear claims. The problem is that the process does not systematically enforce segment-aware, evidence-aware, risk-aware routing.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-Tiered Eligibility Gating with a Protected Premium Fast Lane

**Redesigned process flow:**

- `Intake claim` captures segment, defect type, claim value, and customer tier.
- `Auto eligibility screen` is redesigned as a classifier, not an approver.
- Premium low-risk accessory claims with complete evidence and no fraud flags go directly to `Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement`, using the legitimate R2 exception. This preserves the 24-hour premium expectation.
- Standard low-risk and mid-risk claims with complete evidence and no fraud flags can still use the fast path, but only when the claim is not a high-value device.
- High-value devices over EUR 400 are blocked from fast approval by default. They must pass `Manual eligibility review` and, when fraud signals exist, `Fraud/risk review`, before any approval.
- Unclear defect or missing-evidence claims are sent to a structured evidence-completion stage before the technical decision. They do not bounce repeatedly between `Request missing evidence` and `Manual eligibility review`.
- Premium high-value claims receive a prioritized manual-review slot so they do not wait behind Standard cases.

**Segments/paths targeted:**

- High-value devices: Premium and Standard.
- Premium low-risk accessories: protected fast lane.
- Unclear/missing-evidence claims: early evidence routing.
- Current paths that violate R1 or R2.

**Mechanism of improvement:**

- Policy is enforced at the decision point, not after the fact.
- The fast path is narrowed to cases where speed is safe.
- Manual eligibility reviewers and fraud specialists are focused on high-value and complex cases instead of routinely reviewing low-risk claims.
- This directly reduces the 22% and 18% fast-path complaint rates for high-value devices and improves conformance to R1 and R2.

**What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**

- It will increase manual-review inflow, possibly by 15–35%, and will slow high-value cases that previously slipped through fast approval.
- It does not solve wrong replacement selection. A high-value case can still pass manual review and then prompt the wrong part.
- It does not fix the evidence-loop problem on its own.
- If manual capacity is not protected, Premium high-value cases risk service-credit exposure when reviewed-path cycle times approach 96 hours.

# 3. Redesign B: Evidence-First Fulfillment with a Pre-Dispatch Quality Gate

**Redesigned process flow:**

- No technical decision is made until an evidence package is complete.
- The repeated `Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review` loop is replaced by a single structured `Evidence completion` stage, supported by a new evidence portal and an assisted channel for customers with low digital literacy.
- If evidence is still incomplete after one structured request, the case goes to manual review rather than automatically re-entering the evidence loop.
- At the technical-decision point, any replacement order—especially high-value—passes through a lightweight `Pre-dispatch quality gate` before `Dispatch replacement`.
- The pre-dispatch gate verifies part compatibility, eligibility, evidence completeness, and risk-review results.
- Quality check inspectors, who are currently underused except after repair campaigns, are repurposed to run or audit this gate.
- Reopened claims still pass manual eligibility review to respect R3, but the review is pre-populated with the original claim data and complaint history, avoiding a full replay of the front end.

**Segments/paths targeted:**

- Unclear defect / missing-evidence segment, currently 22% of cases.
- High-value replacement paths, where wrong replacement costs are EUR 410 per error.
- Post-dispatch complaint paths: `Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen` and `Fast approval -> Customer complaint/reopen`.
- The long-tail pattern linked to incorrect replacement part selection.

**Mechanism of improvement:**

- Front-loading evidence removes repeated 30+ hour evidence loops and reduces the 26% repeated-request rate.
- The evidence portal is expected to reduce repeated evidence requests by 30–45%.
- The pre-dispatch quality gate catches replacement errors before shipment, reducing reshipment costs, complaints, and service-credit exposure.
- It converts quality checks from reactive rework into preventive control.

**What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**

- It adds 6–18 hours to dispatch, depending on resource design. This conflicts with Premium 24-hour expectations unless the gate is selectively applied or heavily automated for low-risk accessories.
- A digital evidence portal may increase abandonment among customers with low digital literacy.
- It does not directly address manual eligibility or fraud/risk bottlenecks, and it does not solve Monday/campaign fraud surges.

# 4. Redesign C: Dynamic Orchestration with Closed-Loop Service Recovery

**Redesigned process flow:**

- A process-orchestration layer monitors real-time queue loads, risk scores, and historical downstream outcomes for every claim.
- Routing is dynamic, not purely rule-based:
  - Low-risk and Premium low-risk claims use the fast lane when queues are healthy.
  - High-value and unclear claims are routed to manual eligibility and evidence completion even if the fast lane is fast.
  - Standard claims with tolerant 72-hour expectations can be deliberately scheduled later when manual review or fraud review queues are congested.
  - On Mondays and after marketing campaigns, when fraud/risk review is the bottleneck, low-severity cases are held or filtered by a pre-screen so specialist capacity is reserved for genuine risk.
- Customer complaint/reopen is treated as a specific service-recovery lane, not as a full restart.
  - `Customer complaint/reopen -> Manual eligibility review` is retained for R3 compliance, but the manual review is accelerated and pre-populated.
  - `Escalation board` is reserved for systemic failures and high-stakes cases, not routine reopens.
- Downstream outcomes are fed back into the auto-screen rules. If a product/segment combination generates high post-dispatch complaints, that combination is automatically routed to manual review or the pre-dispatch quality gate until the cause is corrected.

**Segments/paths targeted:**

- All segments, but especially high-value devices and peak-demand periods.
- Bottlenecks: manual eligibility queue, fraud/risk review, escalation board, and the post-close complaint long tail.
- The process-mining signal that many low-frequency paths combine approval, risk review, and repair in nonstandard ways.

**Mechanism of improvement:**

- It balances speed and cost under capacity constraints instead of assuming a single path is always best.
- It reuses idle or underused capacity and prevents any single queue from becoming a systemic bottleneck.
- The closed-loop feedback turns complaint/reopen data into a preventive control, reducing the likelihood of repeating the same fast-path mistakes.

**What it deliberately sacrifices or leaves unresolved:**

- It is the most complex and least deterministic redesign.
- It requires strong data infrastructure, governance, and auditability.
- It does not fix underlying evidence gaps or wrong part selection by itself; it routes around them unless combined with Redesign B.
- Conformance may initially look worse because the process becomes more variable, even if outcomes improve.

# 6. Failure Modes and Displaced Bottlenecks

## Redesign A

**Failure modes:**

- Manual eligibility inflow rises 15–35% as predicted, but the 7 reviewers cannot absorb it. Utilization stays above 82%, queue times explode, and Premium high-value cases miss service expectations, converting operational cost into service credits.
- The updated auto-screen rules misclassify high-value devices or unclear claims, so fast approvals still happen for the highest-risk cases. Compliance improves less than expected, and the same 22%/18% complaint pattern persists.

**Bottleneck migration:**

- The bottleneck moves from the auto screen to Manual eligibility review and Fraud/risk review, especially on Mondays and after marketing campaigns.

## Redesign B

**Failure modes:**

- Low-digital-literacy customers abandon the new evidence portal. Abandonment rises above the current 320 abandoned claims, and assisted-channel call volume increases, offsetting the portal’s efficiency gain.
- The pre-dispatch quality gate is applied to all replacement orders, adding 6–18 hours to every shipment. Premium 24-hour expectations are missed, service-credit exposure rises, and customers complain during the waiting period even though later complaints drop.

**Bottleneck migration:**

- The bottleneck moves to quality-check inspectors and evidence-support staff. If 100% of replacements are gated, the currently underused inspector capacity becomes a new queue.

## Redesign C

**Failure modes:**

- Dynamic routing produces many nonstandard journeys. Customers cannot predict what happens next, and auditors cannot map cases to the normative process. Conformance and precision drop further, and escalation board volume rises for process confusion rather than technical failure.
- The feedback loop overreacts to a short-term complaint spike, sending too many cases to manual review and fraud review. This recreates the original queue bottleneck while increasing cycle time.

**Bottleneck migration:**

- The bottleneck moves from manual queues to the orchestration/analytics team, then to the escalation board if the service-recovery lane is not scaled.

# 7. Recommended Experiment Sequence

## First pilot

Run **Redesign A** on high-value device claims first.

Why:

- It is the fastest to deploy: auto-screen rule changes can be deployed in about two weeks.
- High-value devices carry the biggest compliance, complaint, and wrong-replacement risk.
- The comparison is clean: high-value claims randomly assigned to the new gating logic versus the current process.

Metrics to monitor:

- Fitting-trace percentage, starting from 68%.
- R1/R2 violation rates.
- High-value complaint/reopen rate, starting from 22% Premium and 18% Standard.
- Manual eligibility queue utilization.
- Premium high-value cycle time and service-credit exposure.
- Total lifecycle cost proxy: processing cost + reshipments + service credits + rework.

Stop/go thresholds:

- Go if high-value complaint/reopen falls by at least 4–5 percentage points and manual utilization stays mostly below 82%.
- Stop or redesign if manual utilization exceeds 82% for more than two weeks, or if premium high-value cycle times rise so much that service-credit costs exceed the complaint-reduction savings.

## Second pilot

Run **Redesign B** after A is stable, on the unclear-defect/missing-evidence segment and on high-value replacement dispatch.

Why:

- A changes routing rules, but B changes evidence structure and quality gates. Running them simultaneously on the same population would confound the results.
- B is the natural next test because repeated evidence requests and wrong replacement selection are independent of A’s gating logic.

Metrics to monitor:

- Repeated evidence request rate, currently 26% of cases entering evidence.
- Abandonment rate, currently 320 claims.
- Post-dispatch complaint/reopen rate.
- Wrong-replacement cost incidents.
- Customer satisfaction with status visibility.

Stop/go thresholds:

- Go if repeated evidence requests drop by 30–45% and abandonment does not rise by more than 5 percentage points.
- Go if the pre-dispatch gate catches at least some replacement errors before shipment.
- Stop or narrow the gate if the added 6–18 hours causes premium SLA violations that outweigh the quality improvement.

## Third pilot

Run **Redesign C** last, initially in shadow mode.

Why:

- C is an orchestration layer that depends on stable rules and stable quality data.
- It should be tested after A and B have established predictable segment-aware process behavior.
- Shadow mode lets the team compare the dynamic routing recommendations against actual outcomes without disrupting live operations.

Metrics to monitor:

- Variance in cycle time, not just average cycle time.
- Queue utilization across manual review, fraud review, and quality check.
- Complaint/reopen rates by segment and by route.
- Auditability and conformance after dynamic routing is enabled.
- Escalation board volume.

Stop/go thresholds:

- Go if dynamic routing reduces queue saturation and complaint rates without degrading audit trails.
- Stop or constrain the model if conformance degrades significantly or if the escalation board becomes the new bottleneck.

## Sequencing logic

1. **A first** because it addresses the highest-risk compliance and customer-experience issue with the smallest change.
2. **B second** because it changes process structure and requires new customer-facing capabilities.
3. **C last** because it is a control layer that should sit on top of stable rules.

## How to know improvements are real rather than shifted downstream

Track every pilot case for 90 days after its final event. Compare the full lifecycle cost and full complaint curve, not just the cycle time to `Quality/compliance close`. Watch for displacement:

- If A reduces post-approval complaints but increases waiting complaints before approval, customer experience may not improve.
- If B reduces post-dispatch complaints but increases pre-dispatch impatience, service-credit exposure may simply move earlier.
- If C improves average cycle time by pushing difficult cases into abandonment or escalation, that is not a real improvement.

Use randomized control groups within each pilot segment. Avoid crossing A, B, and C experiments on the same population at the same time. The goal is not to prove that one path is universally best; it is to learn which segments benefit from speed, which require review, and which need different evidence and quality controls.
