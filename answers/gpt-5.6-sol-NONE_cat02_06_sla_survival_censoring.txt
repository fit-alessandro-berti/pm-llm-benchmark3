# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** the recorded `Submit` event.  
**Analysis time:** elapsed hours since `Submit`.  
**SLA horizon:** \(\tau=72\) hours.  
**Administrative cutoff:** the common extract freeze; open cases are right-censored at their supplied age. Because calendar start and cutoff dates are absent, staggered-entry and calendar-time effects cannot be assessed.

The term “breach” should be tied to the operational question.

### Estimand A: probability of not being resolved by 72 hours

\[
R_{\text{NR}}(72)=P(T_{\text{Resolve}}>72),
\]

where cases that withdraw, reject, or transfer before 72 remain “not resolved internally by 72.” This is an **internal-service success/failure estimand**, not a conventional single-event survival estimand. It matches SLA governance when the promise is specifically that the internal process will reach `Resolve`.

For cases still observed at 72, status at 72 is known. Cases censored before 72—S10, S15, S29, and S31—have unknown status at 72 and require survival estimation or bounds rather than automatic classification.

### Estimand B: cumulative incidence of late internal resolution

\[
F_{\text{late-res}}(t)=P(72<T_{\text{Resolve}}\le t),
\]

with `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and usually `Transfer` treated as competing events. Its eventual limit is the probability of an observed internal resolution occurring late, not the probability that a case was unresolved at 72.

This is useful for **forecasting how much late-resolution workload will eventually return to the resolving team**. It excludes cases removed by other dispositions.

A related scalar is:

\[
P(T_{\text{Resolve}}>72\mid \text{eventual internal Resolve}),
\]

but that conditions on a future outcome and is unsuitable as the primary operational measure.

### Estimand C: probability of no terminal disposition by 72 hours

Let terminal disposition mean `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`:

\[
R_{\text{active}}(72)=P(T_{\text{any terminal}}>72).
\]

This measures the proportion still in the observable internal pipeline at 72. It supports **queue capacity, aging inventory, and intervention planning**. It differs sharply from Estimand A: a rejection at 9 hours fails internal resolution but does not remain active at 72.

If transfer is not regarded as a meaningful terminal disposition because external work continues, a sensitivity version treats transfer as loss to follow-up or transition into an external state.

### Estimand D: cause-specific disposition risks

For cause \(k\in\{\text{Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}\}\),

\[
F_k(t)=P(T\le t,J=k),
\]

estimated as cumulative incidence in a competing-risks framework. In particular, \(F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\) measures timely internal resolution while respecting that competing outcomes remove cases from the possibility of later internal resolution in the observed system.

### Contributions of the requested examples

| Case | Estimand A: not resolved by 72 | Estimand B: late internal resolution | Estimand C: no terminal disposition by 72 | Competing-risk interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S03: resolved at 76 | Known breach | Late-resolution event at 76 | Still active at 72 | Resolve cause at 76 |
| S09: open at 92 | Known breach because observed unresolved through 72 | At risk through 92, then right-censored; may later resolve late | Known still active at 72 | Censored at 92 without terminal cause |
| S06: withdrawn at 27 | Not resolved by 72 under internal-success definition | Withdrawal competes at 27; cannot later have observed internal resolution | Not active at 72 | Withdrawal cause at 27 |
| S08: transferred at 68 | Not internally resolved by 72 under internal-system definition; external success unknown | Usually transfer competes at 68; alternatively censor or count as unresolved failure | Terminal by 72 if transfer is a terminal disposition; unknown otherwise | Transfer cause, loss to follow-up, or composite failure depending on assumption |

An **already-observed breach** includes a resolution after 72 and an open case observed beyond 72. An open case aged less than 72 is merely right-censored before its SLA outcome becomes known.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| Observed status | Resolve event | Any-terminal event | Competing-risk code | Primary censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | 1 at duration | 1 at duration | 1 = Resolve | Never censored before event | Split into timely (\(\le72\)) and late (\(>72\)) resolution |
| `withdrawn` | 0 | 1 | 2 = Withdraw | Competing event, not ordinary censoring | Composite non-resolution failure; censor only for a cause-specific hypothetical analysis, with strong assumptions |
| `rejected` | 0 | 1 | 3 = Reject | Competing event | Combine with withdrawal; composite non-resolution failure; hypothetical censoring |
| `transferred` | 0 internally | 1 if transfer ends internal process | 4 = Transfer | **Primary:** distinct competing event | **Scenario T1:** composite failure/not resolved; **T2:** censor at transfer as loss to follow-up; **T3:** external absorbing state with unknown later outcome; bounds assign all transferred cases eventual timely success versus no timely success |
| `open` | 0 so far | 0 so far | 0 = censored | Right-censor at age at cutoff | IPCW/joint sensitivity if censoring depends on cohort, priority, stage, or inactivity |

Three defensible transfer assumptions answer different questions:

1. **Internal accountability:** transfer is a competing terminal outcome. It prevents a later internal `Resolve` in this extract.
2. **SLA failure:** transfer counts in a composite “not internally resolved by 72” outcome. This is appropriate if transfer does not discharge the SLA.
3. **Loss to follow-up:** censor at transfer. This targets eventual resolution regardless of organization, but requires the implausible or at least unverified assumption that transfer is conditionally independent of external resolution time.
4. **External-state model/bounds:** transfer enters an unobserved external state. Until linkage is obtained, report best/worst-case external outcome bounds rather than a point estimate.

Withdrawal and rejection should not casually be censored: they are observed dispositions related to the process and likely to case characteristics. Censoring them estimates a hypothetical world in which those competing outcomes are removed, not the actual operational risk.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A calculation restricted to completed resolved cases would use only 13 cases and discard information from open and competing-outcome cases.

- **Survivor/selection bias:** S03 and S13 are observed late resolutions at 76 and 110 hours, but S09 is already open at 92 hours and S26 at 104 hours. Dropping the latter two systematically removes unresolved long-duration cases and can make performance appear better.
- **Right-censoring is not completion:** S10 is open at 47 hours and S15 at 35 hours. Their 72-hour result is unknown, so neither should be labeled timely nor breached. By contrast, S09 at 92, S11 at 81, S25 at 79, and S26 at 104 are known to have remained unresolved through 72 and are already observed SLA breaches under Estimand A.
- **Competing risks:** S06 withdrew at 27 and S05 was rejected at 9. They did not resolve, but they also did not remain in the active queue through 72. Counting them as ordinary censored observations can inflate estimated resolution probability; counting them as active breaches would inflate backlog risk.
- **Transfers are plausibly informative:** S08 transferred at 68, S21 at 26, and S32 at 96; all contain escalation. Transfer may be selected for complexity or ownership, so censoring at transfer likely removes high-risk cases selectively. S08 also illustrates ambiguity: it is terminal internally before 72 but externally unresolved as far as the data show.
- **Long-gap censoring may be informative:** S11 is open at 81 with a 55-hour last-event gap, S25 at 79 with a 48-hour gap, and S26 at 104 with a 72-hour gap. These may represent stalled work rather than independent administrative censoring. In contrast, S24 is age 67 with a 4-hour gap, suggesting recent activity. The extract cannot prove stalling, but independent censoring is questionable.
- **Cohort imbalance and policy confounding:** cohort B follows a policy change, but assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete. B has more cases whose prefixes contain `AutoCheck`, yet some B cases—S20, S23, S26, and S30—do not. A-versus-B differences can reflect priority mix, case complexity, calendar conditions, or compliance rather than policy effect.
- **Below versus above 72:** S31 open at 23 contributes exposure only to 23 and has unknown SLA status. S26 open at 104 is known to breach at 72 and remains at risk for later disposition through 104. Treating both simply as “open” discards the central SLA distinction.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## Descriptive checks and nonparametric estimates

First report cohort- and priority-stratified counts at 72:

- known timely internal resolution;
- known unresolved at 72;
- terminal non-resolution before 72;
- censored before 72 and therefore unknown;
- active without terminal disposition at 72.

This avoids hiding ambiguity behind one estimate.

**Kaplan–Meier (KM)** is appropriate for time to *any terminal disposition*, where open cases are administratively censored. Then \(S_{\text{any}}(72)\) estimates Estimand C, conditional on defensible independent censoring. KM is not the right primary estimator for actual resolution probability when withdrawal, rejection, and transfer compete: censoring competing events would treat them as if they remained capable of internal resolution and overstate real-world resolution incidence.

**Aalen–Johansen (AJ)** should be the **primary estimator** for observed disposition. Estimate cumulative incidence curves for Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer, stratified descriptively by cohort and priority. From the resolution curve, report timely resolution incidence \(F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\); from all causes, report the probability of remaining without terminal disposition at 72. Because only 32 cases and few cause-specific events exist, emphasize estimates, denominators, and wide uncertainty rather than complex inference.

For direct internal-SLA governance, supplement AJ with a transparent 72-hour status table and bounds for cases censored before 72. A simple observed-status lower/upper interval is useful:

- lower bound for non-resolution at 72: cases known not resolved by 72;
- upper bound: lower bound plus cases censored before 72 whose eventual status at 72 is unknown.

## Regression and adjusted comparisons

**Cause-specific hazards** model the instantaneous rate of each disposition among cases still event-free. They are useful for staffing and process diagnostics: for example, whether escalation-associated states have a lower instantaneous resolution rate. Competing events are censored mathematically, and interpretation is conditional on still being at risk—not a direct probability comparison.

**Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards** relate covariates to cumulative incidence while retaining competing events in a modified risk set. They can support prognosis of actual resolution incidence, but the hazard ratio is less intuitive and proportionality is doubtful with process states that change over time. With this sample, Fine–Gray should be exploratory and heavily regularized, not primary.

**Restricted mean time (RMT):**

- restricted mean time without terminal disposition through a prespecified \(\tau^\*\), such as 120 hours if follow-up supports it;
- restricted mean time unresolved through 72.

RMT gives an interpretable “expected unresolved hours” quantity and avoids relying on a median that may not be reached. The maximum horizon must be chosen from supported follow-up, not extrapolated beyond observed data.

## Time-aware process methods

**Landmarking** is the recommended secondary prognostic approach. At operational landmarks such as 24, 48, and 60 hours, include only cases still active and use information known by that landmark: current stage, number of reviews so far, escalation already observed, rework so far, and inactivity since the latest event. Estimate conditional risk of no resolution or no terminal disposition by 72. Exact timestamps are required to implement this correctly; prefixes alone do not reveal when intermediate activities occurred.

A **multi-state model** is the preferred process representation once exact event times are available. It estimates transition hazards among stages rather than reducing the trajectory to a single baseline label. AJ naturally generalizes to nonparametric state-occupation probabilities, while transition-specific Cox models can incorporate baseline and time-varying predictors.

## Recommendation

1. **Primary:** AJ competing-risk disposition curves plus a 72-hour status/bounds table, stratified descriptively by cohort and priority. Treat transfer as a distinct competing event.
2. **Secondary:** KM for time to any terminal disposition and landmark models for conditional 72-hour risk.
3. **Exploratory:** transition-specific cause-specific hazards; Fine–Gray only if event counts become substantially larger.
4. Avoid adjusted causal claims about cohort B. Any regression is prognostic/associational unless assignment and rollout mechanisms are adequately controlled.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Because only ordered prefixes—not intermediate timestamps—are supplied, several useful features can be extracted, but their availability time must be respected.

| Feature | Example derivation | Role and warning |
|---|---|---|
| Priority | P1/P2/P3 | Baseline predictor |
| Cohort | A/B | Baseline/calendar-policy proxy; confounded, not randomized treatment |
| First activity after Submit | `AutoCheck` vs `Screen` | Early path feature; post-baseline unless known immediately |
| AutoCheck observed so far | activity membership | Time-varying process exposure; final-prefix use at Submit leaks future information |
| Current stage | last activity in prefix for open cases | Valid at cutoff or a properly timed landmark |
| Review count | number of `Review` occurrences | Time-varying; repeated review can indicate looping |
| Rework so far | count of `Rework` | Time-varying and post-treatment; may mark complexity |
| Escalation observed so far | whether `Escalate` has occurred | Time-varying, not a safe baseline covariate |
| Escalation timing | elapsed time to first escalation | Requires exact event timestamps; unavailable here |
| Path pattern/variant | full ordered prefix | Descriptive for completed traces; leakage if used for start-time prediction |
| Last-event inactivity | `last_event_gap_h` | Dynamic feature at cutoff/terminal time; terminal rows all have zero by construction, so using it in a baseline model would leak outcome/status |
| Time since current-state entry | current time minus last-event timestamp | Valuable dynamic predictor but unavailable without timestamps |
| Policy compliance | actual policy-eligible action sequence | Must be defined from rollout rules; cohort alone is insufficient |

`escalated` and `rework_count` summarize the final observed prefix. They can be used descriptively, or as time-varying covariates after the corresponding event occurs. Treating “ever escalated” as fixed at Submit creates **immortal-time bias**: to be classified as escalated, a case must remain in process long enough to reach escalation. It also uses future information unavailable at prediction time.

A proposed multi-state structure is:

```text
Submit
  > Screen > AutoCheck > Review
                                > Rework > Review
                                > Escalate > Review
                                > Resolve
                                > Withdraw
                                > Reject
         > Review
         > Escalate
         > Reject/Withdraw/Resolve
  > AutoCheck > Screen or Escalate

Escalate > Resolve | Review | Transfer
Terminal internal states: Resolve, Withdraw, Reject
External/unobserved state: Transfer
Observation cutoff: right-censoring from any transient state
```

The actual observed graph should be learned from event-log directly-follows relations rather than forced to match this simplified diagram. Stage-specific hazards would estimate, for example, the rate of `ReviewResolve`, `ReviewRework`, and `EscalateTransfer`, conditional on occupation of the source state.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least the following analyses should be reported side by side.

### 1. Transfer treatment

- **Primary:** transfer is a separate competing event.
- **Failure scenario:** transfer counts as non-resolution under the internal SLA.
- **Censoring scenario:** censor at transfer for an eventual-resolution estimand.
- **Bounds/external-state scenario:** assume transferred cases all achieve external resolution within SLA versus none do.

A conclusion that escalation is associated with poor resolution could weaken or reverse if transferred escalated cases resolve quickly externally. Conversely, censoring transfers could make escalated paths look artificially successful if difficult cases are selectively transferred.

### 2. Withdrawal and rejection

- Treat them as distinct competing outcomes.
- Combine them into a “closed without resolution” cause.
- For a hypothetical latent-resolution analysis, censor them—with an explicit warning that independent censoring is unlikely.
- For governance, count them as failure of the internal-resolution SLA.

Cohort rankings can change if one cohort closes more cases quickly through rejection/withdrawal: it may have low active backlog but poor resolution success.

### 3. Informative administrative censoring

- Standard independent-censoring KM/AJ.
- Stratified or inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting using baseline priority, cohort, and time-updated state/gap once timestamps exist.
- Pattern-mixture stress tests in which open cases with long inactivity gaps have higher or lower future resolution hazards than otherwise similar cases.
- Worst/best-case 72-hour bounds for cases censored before 72.

If long gaps indicate stalled cases, standard estimates may understate tail risk. If gaps instead represent unrecorded external progress, the direction could reverse.

### 4. Cohort-policy interpretation

- Unadjusted A-versus-B curves.
- Stratification/standardization by priority and baseline eligibility variables.
- Per-protocol-like analysis based on actual compliant path, clearly labeled selection-prone.
- Intention-to-policy-period comparison based on cohort, clearly labeled confounded by calendar time and assignment.
- Interaction checks between cohort and priority, reported descriptively given the sample size.

Any apparent cohort B advantage could reverse after standardization or under alternative transfer handling. Actual use of `AutoCheck` must not be interpreted causally without controlling its assignment mechanism and time of occurrence.

### 5. SLA boundary and outcome definition

Use the contractual convention explicitly: breach at \(T>72\) versus \(T\ge72\), and define whether transfer, withdrawal, and rejection discharge the SLA. No case lies exactly at 72 here, but the rule matters prospectively.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival output should be converted into decisions at clearly defined prediction times.

- **At intake:** use only baseline attributes such as priority and valid pre-Submit information. Report predicted probability of internal resolution by 72 and uncertainty.
- **At landmarks:** at 24, 48, and 60 hours, recompute conditional tail risk using only events observed by that time. A 60-hour alert might trigger senior review, reassignment, or an escalation check.
- **Workload planning:** use \(P(\text{no terminal disposition by }72)\) and restricted mean active time to forecast aging inventory and staffing needs.
- **Disposition governance:** report timely-resolution incidence separately from withdrawal, rejection, and transfer incidence. A lower backlog caused by more transfers is not necessarily better service.
- **Stalled-case actions:** combine conditional SLA risk with time since last activity; route long-gap cases for data-quality or ownership checks rather than assuming inactivity equals complexity.
- **Capacity-aware alerts:** choose thresholds based on available intervention capacity and expected benefit/cost, not only statistical discrimination.

Risk scores are **prognostic associations**, not causal effects. “Escalated cases have higher predicted risk” does not imply escalation causes delay; escalation may be a response to complexity. Similarly, cohort effects are not policy effects under nonrandom assignment and incomplete compliance.

Validation should include:

- calibration-in-the-large and calibration curves for resolution and active-status risk at 72;
- time-dependent Brier score and discrimination, with bootstrap uncertainty;
- separate calibration by P1/P2/P3 and cohorts A/B;
- alert rate, false-negative rate, and positive predictive value by priority and cohort;
- assessment of whether thresholding systematically deprioritizes groups with lower historical resolution caused by process access rather than need;
- temporal validation across later cutoffs, because policy and workload drift can invalidate calibration.

Priority-specific thresholds may be operationally justified because the cost of missing a P1 breach differs, but they should be documented as policy choices and monitored for unequal service consequences.

# No intermediate timestamps are fabricated: only ordered event positions are stored.
# For full survival curves one could use lifelines; the fallback below creates
# KM/Aalen-Johansen-ready records and exact empirical 72-hour status/bounds summaries.

import pandas as pd
from io import StringIO

raw = """case|cohort|priority|duration_or_age_h|observed_status|prefix|escalated|rework_count|last_event_gap_h

"""
df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(raw), sep="|")
t = df["duration_or_age_h"]
s = df["observed_status"]

# Estimand 1: internal non-resolution at 72.
# 1 = known not resolved at 72; 0 = known resolved by 72; NA = censored open before 72.
df["not_resolved_72"] = pd.Series(pd.NA, index=df.index, dtype="Int64")
df.loc[(s == "resolved") & (t <= 72), "not_resolved_72"] = 0
df.loc[(s == "resolved") & (t > 72), "not_resolved_72"] = 1
df.loc[s.isin(["withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"]), "not_resolved_72"] = 1
df.loc[(s == "open") & (t >= 72), "not_resolved_72"] = 1

# Estimand 2: no terminal internal disposition at 72.
# Here transfer is terminal; open <72 is unknown, open >=72 is known active at 72.
df["active_72_transfer_terminal"] = pd.Series(pd.NA, index=df.index, dtype="Int64")
df.loc[s.isin(["resolved", "withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"]), 
       "active_72_transfer_terminal"] = (t[s.isin(
           ["resolved", "withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"])] > 72).astype(int).values
df.loc[(s == "open") & (t >= 72), "active_72_transfer_terminal"] = 1

# Competing-risk code: 0 censor/open, 1 resolve, 2 withdraw, 3 reject, 4 transfer.
cause = {"open": 0, "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2, "rejected": 3, "transferred": 4}
df["cr_code"] = s.map(cause)
df["cr_event"] = (df["cr_code"] != 0).astype(int)

# Three transfer-treatment scenarios, all preserving open cases.
# T1 distinct competing event; T2 composite SLA failure; T3 censor/loss to follow-up.
df["T1_competing_code"] = df["cr_code"]
df["T2_composite_failure"] = (
    s.isin(["withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"]) |
    ((s == "resolved") & (t > 72)) |
    ((s == "open") & (t >= 72))
).astype(int)
df["T2_known_at_72"] = ~((s == "open") & (t < 72))
df["T3_resolve_event"] = (s == "resolved").astype(int)
df["T3_censored"] = s.isin(["open", "transferred"]).astype(int)

# KM-ready time-to-any-terminal data and cause-specific/AJ-ready data.
km_ready = df[["case", "cohort", "priority", "duration_or_age_h"]].copy()
km_ready["any_terminal_event"] = (s != "open").astype(int)
aj_ready = df[["case", "cohort", "priority", "duration_or_age_h",
               "cr_code", "cr_event"]].copy()

# Cohort/priority summaries, including unknown pre-72 censoring.
def status72(g):
    x = g["not_resolved_72"]
    known = x.notna().sum()
    return pd.Series({
        "n": len(g),
        "known_timely_resolve": int((x == 0).sum()),
        "known_not_resolved_72": int((x == 1).sum()),
        "unknown_at_72": int(x.isna().sum()),
        "known_risk": (x == 1).sum() / known if known else float("nan"),
        "lower_bound_population_risk": (x == 1).sum() / len(g),
        "upper_bound_population_risk": ((x == 1).sum() + x.isna().sum()) / len(g)
    })

strat = df.groupby(["cohort", "priority"], observed=True).apply(
    status72, include_groups=False
).reset_index()

# Sensitivity: transfer terminal versus transfer regarded as externally active/unknown.
df["active_72_transfer_external"] = df["active_72_transfer_terminal"].copy()
# Transfers observed at or before 72 have unknown external disposition at 72.
df.loc[(s == "transferred") & (t <= 72), "active_72_transfer_external"] = pd.NA

def bounds(col):
    x = df[col]
    return {
        "known_active": int((x == 1).sum()),
        "unknown": int(x.isna().sum()),
        "lower_rate": round((x == 1).sum()/len(x), 3),
        "upper_rate": round(((x == 1).sum()+x.isna().sum())/len(x), 3)
    }

# Prefix-derived control-flow features, without timestamps.
df["activities"] = df["prefix"].str.split(">")
df["review_count_from_prefix"] = df["activities"].apply(lambda z: z.count("Review"))
df["has_review_loop"] = (df["review_count_from_prefix"] > 1).astype(int)
df["variant"] = df["activities"].apply(tuple)
df["current_observed_stage"] = df["activities"].str[-1]

# PM4Py-compatible construction and a relevant control-flow method.
# event_index records order only and is deliberately NOT represented as a timestamp.
events = []
for _, row in df.iterrows():
    for pos, activity in enumerate(row["activities"]):
        events.append({
            "case:concept:name": row["case"],
            "concept:name": activity,
            "event_index": pos
        })
event_df = pd.DataFrame(events)

try:
    import pm4py
    from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event
    log = EventLog()
    for case_id, grp in event_df.groupby("case:concept:name", sort=False):
        trace = Trace(attributes={"concept:name": case_id})
        for rec in grp.sort_values("event_index").to_dict("records"):
            trace.append(Event({"concept:name": rec["concept:name"],
                                "event_index": rec["event_index"]}))
        log.append(trace)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    pm4py_variant_counts = {str(k): len(v) for k, v in variants.items()}
    print("PM4Py distinct variants:", len(pm4py_variant_counts))
except ImportError:
    # Exact fallback equivalent for variant counting.
    pm4py_variant_counts = df.groupby("prefix").size().to_dict()
    print("PM4Py unavailable; pandas variant-count fallback used.")
    print("For KM use lifelines.KaplanMeierFitter on km_ready; "
          "for AJ use lifelines.AalenJohansenFitter on aj_ready if installed.")

print("\nKM-ready rows (open cases retained):", len(km_ready),
      "; open/censored:", int((km_ready["any_terminal_event"] == 0).sum()))
print("AJ cause counts (0=open/censored):")
print(aj_ready["cr_code"].value_counts().sort_index().to_string())
print("\nCohort/priority 72-hour non-resolution summaries:")
print(strat.to_string(index=False))
print("\nTransfer sensitivity for active-at-72 estimand:")
print("Transfer terminal:", bounds("active_72_transfer_terminal"))
print("Transfer external/unknown:", bounds("active_72_transfer_external"))
print("\nPrefix features:")
print(df[["case", "current_observed_stage", "review_count_from_prefix",
          "has_review_loop"]].head(10).to_string(index=False))
print("\nMost common variants:")

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The strongest priorities for additional data are:

1. **Exact timestamps for every event.** These are essential for transition hazards, escalation timing, state-entry duration, landmark features, queueing delays, and avoiding fabricated timing assumptions. The current order-only prefixes cannot support a proper time-varying model.
2. **External outcomes after transfer.** Obtain destination, transfer time, external resolution/disposition time, and whether the SLA continues across organizations. Without linkage, transfer effects are only identifiable under assumptions or bounds.
3. **Submit dates, cutoff date, and extraction logic.** A common stated cutoff is not enough to assess calendar drift, staggered follow-up, delayed recording, or whether open-case censoring is independent.
4. **Policy assignment and rollout compliance.** Record eligibility rules, intended assignment, actual automated-screening exposure, implementation dates by team, exceptions, and reasons for noncompliance. These are required to distinguish policy-period effects from treatment effects.
5. **Baseline case mix and complexity.** Case type, channel, jurisdiction, documentation completeness, requester characteristics, and severity would improve adjustment and censoring models.
6. **Time-varying workload and resources.** Queue size, staffing, shift/calendar, holidays, reviewer identity, handoffs, and concurrent P1 demand may explain both transitions and cohort differences.
7. **Reasons for withdrawal, rejection, escalation, and transfer.** These distinguish customer choice, eligibility failure, capacity routing, and case complexity.
8. **Recording quality and latent activity.** Long gaps may mean inactivity, work outside the system, or delayed event logging. Audit logs and ownership status are needed to interpret them.
9. **SLA contract details.** Clarify whether withdrawal, rejection, or transfer discharges the SLA; whether pauses are allowed; and whether the 72-hour boundary is strict.
10. **Larger samples and later cutoffs.** With only 32 cases and sparse cause-specific events, cohort-by-priority estimates and regression coefficients will be unstable. Repeated frozen extracts would also reveal eventual outcomes for currently open cases and permit temporal validation.
