# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency for high-complexity cases**  
   Average cycle time may remain stable if simple cases speed up. The central queue introduces disproportionate delays for high-complexity cases because they require multiple specialist interactions. The 90th or 95th percentile of cycle time per complexity bucket can inflate while the mean stays flat, masking severe degradation for the most vulnerable cases.

2. **High-complexity cases bounce and rework loops**  
   Even if mean rework rate stays around 8%, complex cases may experience 2–3 rework loops through the central specialist, each adding queue time. Process models discovered from event logs (e.g., performance DFG) may show longer cycle times on the “Rework  Specialist Review” edge without affecting overall average if lowcomplexity cases flow smoothly.

3. **Local knowledge erosion and informal triage loss**  
   The event log will show a gradual disappearance of `local_expertise_flag=True` activities. This slow erosion does not immediately increase average cycle time but reduces firstpass quality and increases rework months later. By the time metrics degrade, institutional knowledge may be hard to rebuild.

4. **Fairness across customer segments**  
   Average cycle time improvement in East may be offset by worsening for North/South regional customers. Segmentblind averages hide that certain groups (e.g., nonEnglish speakers, rural customers) experience disproportionately long waits and lower satisfaction, as they lose dedicated local support and receive standardised advice that requires corrections.

5. **Role clarity and accountability diffusion**  
   Reassignment_count may rise while median cycle time seems stable because cases are passed among central resources without clear ownership. Logs will show longer waiting time between handovers and more “ambiguous” handover_reason codes, but these can be hidden if overall throughput is maintained. Lack of clear ownership slows exception handling and escalations.

6. **Learning and skill atrophy**  
   The variety of activities performed by local staff will shrink (observable via resourceactivity matrix and role mining). Over time, the organisation’s ability to handle unusual cases without central support declines. This increases vulnerability to central queue shocks, but the effect only becomes visible in a crisis (e.g., pandemic, specialist departure) when cycle time spikes catastrophically, not gradually.

7. **Resilience to central queue failure**  
   Average cycle time may improve under normal load, but a single central bottleneck can synchronise waiting times across all locations. The log will show simultaneous degradation in service waiting time for all teams if the central pool is understaffed or sick. This fragility is invisible when measuring only central tendency during steady state.

# 5. Pilot design

### Pilot scope
- Convert **East team only** into a consumer of the central Technical Shared Service, while North and South continue with their embedded specialists.
- Keep Specialist Review (legal/risk) local for all teams initially; do not change Supervisory Approval.
- This creates a natural comparison: East undergoes the core restructuring (central tech specialist), while North/South preserve the current model.

### Comparison group
- North and South teams serve as concurrent control groups.
- Use historical East data as an additional baseline (prepilot 12 weeks).

### Observation window
- Prepilot observation: 12 weeks of stable operations before any change.
- Pilot period: 12 weeks after a 2week transition cutover.
- Postpilot: optionally a rollout decision point after 12 weeks.

### Segmentation dimensions
Analyse all logderived metrics separately by:
- **Team**: East (treatment) vs North/South (control)
- **case_complexity**: low, medium, high
- **required_specialty**: technical vs non-technical (for East, technical cases now go to central)
- **customer_segment**: identify segments with regional accommodations (language, accessibility)
- **priority**: high vs normal, to check if priority queue management works in central pool

### Guardrail metrics (triggers for rollback)
- Central queue waiting time p50 must remain below 5h and p90 below 15h for East technical cases.
- East rework rate after central specialist must not exceed 12% (current East baseline ~11.5%, tolerance +0.5pp).
- East SLA miss rate must not increase by more than 2 percentage points overall; no customer_segment subgroup may exceed 12%.
- North/South performance must not deteriorate beyond normal variation (2sigma control limits) as a spillover effect.
- Staff feedback score on “I feel I am maintaining/developing my technical skills” (survey) must stay above 3.5/5 for East local staff.

### Staff feedback collection
- Weekly 5question pulse survey to East team members: clarity of handovers, perceived expertise loss, satisfaction with central specialist response, fairness of workload, ownership feelings.
- Monthly structured interviews with a sample of case handlers and supervisors in all teams to capture qualitative dynamics not in logs.

### Rollback / adjustment triggers
- If any guardrail metric is breached for two consecutive weeks, pause the pilot and revert East to its original local specialist model.
- If guardrails hold but staff feedback declines significantly, initiate a “sharedlocal hybrid” model where East retains one local tech specialist for complex regional cases and uses central pool for overflow.
- After 12 weeks, perform a full comparison. If East shows clear improvement without harming North/South, gradual rollout can proceed. If results are mixed, extend the pilot with modifications before any fullscale implementation.

# 6. Recommendation

**Proceed only with a phased pilot as described; do not implement full restructuring immediately.**

The evidence from processmining patterns suggests that consolidation can benefit overloaded locations (East), but the risk of central bottlenecks and local knowledge loss is substantial and unevenly distributed. Management should condition any expansion on the following:

- **Do not infer success from early average cycle time stability**: Average cycle time may temporarily improve for East while masking rising tail latency and fairness gaps. The decision to scale must be based on segmented p90/p95, waitingtime distributions per queue, and rework rates by complexity and customer segment.
- **Monitor leading indicators continuously**: If central queue waiting time or reworkafterspecialist edges upward within the first 4 weeks of the pilot, halt and diagnose before it becomes systemic.
- **Preserve minimal local specialist capability**: Even if the central pool is implemented, retain one “hybrid” specialist per region who handles languagesensitive and extremely complex cases and acts as a local knowledge steward. This preserves informal triage and mitigates complete deskilling.
- **Use organisational role mining to track atrophy**: Regularly run ```pm4py.discover_organizational_roles``` and check that local staff still execute a diverse set of activities. If their profiles narrow too much, introduce rotation or joint handling of complex cases.
- **Embed fairness monitoring**: Segment all performance measures by customer_segment and team, and set equity thresholds (e.g., no segment’s p90 cycle time may exceed the overall p90 by more than 30%). If fairness degrades, the sharedservice rules must be adjusted.
- **Plan for resilience testing**: Simulate a peakload week or a central specialist absence during the pilot to observe the subcontracting network and service waiting times. If the system cannot absorb a 20% load spike without p90 waiting times doubling, the design is too fragile.

A full rollout should only follow a successful pilot that demonstrates sustained improvement on all guardrails and a clear understanding of the necessary mitigations.
