## 1. Analytical assumptions

A case notion specifies which object identifies a trace and which events enter it. My baseline creates one case per object of the selected type, includes only its direct E2O events, and orders those events by timestamp. O2O relationships provide interpretation, not implicit event membership. For example, M2 partially_settles V2, but a Payment case contains e22 alone, not V2's invoice event e19.

Convergence means one event participates in multiple cases of the selected type: e16 enters three Order cases. Divergence means one case combines several subordinate lifecycles, potentially producing repeated activities or interleaved branches: O1 combines P1 and P2. I distinguish observed trace divergence from latent heterogeneity, such as P3's two items represented by one packing event. Neither phenomenon automatically indicates bad data or process failure.

Copies are both valid participation evidence and duplicate observations of a single event. Counting e16 three times answers “how many orders participated?”; counting it once answers “how many dispatch events occurred?” Neither count establishes workload, cost, or item quantity beyond the recorded relationships.

I permit explicitly named, bounded, qualifier-aware O2O propagation variants, with source event IDs and inferred-relation provenance retained. They must remain separate from the direct baseline. I reject unrestricted transitive closure as a default: D2 connects O1 to O2/O3 through packages, and P4 connects O3 to O4, so all orders lie in one connected component. Reachability would contaminate every order with the entire process.

DFG edges express observed adjacency within the selected projection, not causality. Durations describe this snapshot's observation windows; no SLA, amounts, resource effort, or general population distribution is supplied.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order—split shipments resemble repetition.** O1 contains Pack→Pack (e11→e12), Dispatch→Dispatch (e15→e16), and Confirm→Confirm (e23→e24). These are P1/P2 branches, not repeated attempts on one package. The five-minute dispatch gap is not P1 processing time. Informative for split-order experience; harmful when interpreted as rework or a mandatory sequential route.

2. **Order—cross-order P3 inflates operations.** Packing event e13 appears in O2 and O3. Across all orders, four physical packing events produce six occurrences because e14 is also shared. In O2, e13 directly precedes e16; in O3, e14 intervenes. One P3 packing event therefore contributes different successor contexts. Participation is informative; operation totals and package routing inferred from these edges are misleading.

3. **Order—cross-order P4 induces false dependencies.** O3's e13→e14 gives Pack→Pack across P3 and P4. Its next packing-to-dispatch edge, e14→e16, spans 45 minutes but links P4 packing to D2, which does not carry P4. P4's actual dispatch is e17, 55 minutes after packing. This is harmful for package waiting-time diagnosis, although the chronology is accurate for O3.

4. **Package—cross-order composition disappears.** P1 and P3 both show Pack→Dispatch→Confirm, despite P3 joining I21/O2 and I31/O3. P4 similarly joins O3/O4. Treating these identical variants as equivalent order executions loses shared service dependencies: P3's e24 completes O2's physical delivery, whereas O3 still awaits P4/e27. The simplification is useful for package movement, harmful for order completion inference.

5. **Package/Item—consolidated D2 biases averages.** D2's e16→e24 lasts 4h55m. It appears once in Delivery, twice in Package, and three times in Item. D1 and D3 last 3h and 22h45m. Mean transit is consequently 10h13m20s per delivery, 8h53m45s per package, and 10h32m30s per item. These are legitimate different weighting schemes; calling them the same transport-performance KPI creates a contradiction.

6. **Order—consolidated invoicing inflates transaction counts.** V2's e19 appears in O2 and O3, so three invoice events become four order-level occurrences. Issue Invoice→Record Payment occurs four times across Order traces but three times across Invoice traces. O2/O3 share one invoice-to-first-payment interval of 35 minutes, not two independent invoicing observations. Useful for exposure per order; harmful for transaction volume and independent-sample statistics.

7. **Invoice—partial payment resembles a loop or completion.** V2 shows e22→e25, Record Payment→Record Payment, with 20h20m between records. O2O identifies M2 as partial and M3 as completing settlement. Using first payment yields 35 minutes from e19; completion at e25 yields 20h55m. The loop is informative for instalments, harmful if labelled retry or if e22 is treated as full settlement. No monetary balance can be reconstructed.

8. **Order—interleaving hides the instalment edge.** In both O2 and O3, e24 intervenes between e22 and e25. Their DFG contains Payment→Confirm→Payment, while Invoice exposes Payment→Payment. O3 additionally has e25→e27, Payment→Confirm. Inferring that payment causes confirmation, or that every order has one fixed payment/delivery ordering, would be harmful; the differing views accurately expose different adjacency scopes.

9. **Payment—singleton performance is vacuous.** M1–M4 each contain one Record Payment event. A zero-duration case and a skeleton requiring one activity reveal recording coverage only. They provide neither payment initiation-to-completion times nor evidence of instant settlement. This is a harmful efficiency interpretation of a useful transaction inventory.

10. **Item/Delivery—short lifecycle boundaries change the question.** I11 runs from e05 to e23, 4h45m; D1 runs from e15 to e23, 3h. O1 runs from e01 to e28, 26h, and its final physical confirmation is e24 rather than e23. All three durations are valid under their definitions. Substituting either shorter interval for order fulfilment conceals O1's remaining shipment and administrative closure delay.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

Create the six direct projections as linked analytical products, retaining source event IDs. Use Order for creation-to-closure, last-confirmation-to-closure, and observed customer chronology; Item for addition-to-confirmation; Package for pack-to-dispatch and package transit; Delivery for delivery-weighted transit; Invoice for issue-to-first-payment and qualifier-supported final settlement. Treat Payment as an event inventory, not a performance process. “Final confirmation” requires checking all the order's supplied item/package links, especially O1 and O3.

Use an OC-DFG to compare typed adjacency: Invoice preserves e22→e25 while Order inserts e24. Its multiple perspectives retain participation information, but typed adjacency still does not prove causation or explain settlement qualifiers.

Inspect the OTG and object interaction summary alongside the qualified O2O links for P3's O2/O3 connection, P4's O3/O4 connection, and D2's consolidation. Validate interaction edges against packs, carries, and bills: co-participation alone does not establish those business relationships. Use the ET–OT graph to expose observation coverage: Payment participates only in Record Payment, Delivery only in Dispatch/Confirm, and Item lacks finance. This explains apparent process simplicity.

Order-rooted equivalent-execution clustering is a secondary structural diagnostic under the decision below. It can test whether apparently similar activity sequences conceal different shared-object structures. Audit the extraction boundary and member IDs first: unrestricted connectivity merges all four orders, while order-centred contexts can overlap through P3, P4, D2, and V2. Clusters are not independent observations or certified business equivalence.

Reconcile discrepancies by matching source event IDs, boundaries, and denominators. Trace the four-versus-three invoicing count to e19; trace transit-mean differences to D2/P4 multiplicities. Retain multiple labelled KPIs when they answer distinct questions. Investigate any remaining mismatch as a projection or data-quality issue rather than choosing the visually cleaner model.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.** Select Order as the primary reporting anchor, with Package, Delivery, and Invoice as required secondary lifecycles; use Item and Payment for detailed attribution.

- **Decision criteria:** Customer commitment, explicit lifecycle boundaries, direct event coverage, and auditability of shared participation matter. Operational and financial measures must use their own appropriate denominators. Trace length alone is not a criterion.
- **Strongest supporting evidence:** Each order has explicit Create/Close events, and every source event directly relates to at least one order. O1 retains both shipments; O3 retains P3/P4 and both V2 instalments. Order thus connects observed customer outcomes without inferred E2O links.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Shared P3/P4, D2, and V2 mean Order cases are neither isolated executions nor independent workload units. Their 42 occurrences can misrepresent 31 events, and e14→e16 can misattribute package waiting time.
- **Residual risk:** Users may privilege the primary dashboard and mistake order adjacency for operational routing. Required linked secondary metrics, explicit denominators, and event-level drill-down reduce but cannot eliminate this risk. Four orders cannot establish stable variants or reliable population performance.
- **Conditions for change:** Choose C if the objective becomes cross-order resource coordination, if order associations become incomplete, or if a primary dashboard cannot preserve secondary attribution. For a narrowly bounded transport study, choose Delivery as that study's lead; it would not become an end-to-end substitute. Additional timestamps or relationships could change the coverage assessment.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

The outline keeps the supplied E2O baseline, stores source-level duplication evidence, and separates structural discovery from business interpretation. Skeletons describe this sample's regularities, not approved rules. Clustering is exploratory because Order is the reporting anchor; inspect its extracted contexts before interpreting results.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

print(ocel)
event_id = ocel.event_id_column
object_id = ocel.object_id_column
object_type = ocel.object_type_column
activity = ocel.event_activity

source_events = ocel.events.drop_duplicates(event_id)
source_objects = ocel.objects.drop_duplicates(object_id)
activity_frequencies = source_events[activity].value_counts()
object_type_frequencies = source_objects[object_type].value_counts()
print(activity_frequencies)
print(object_type_frequencies)

# One membership per source event/object pair; no O2O propagation.
direct_memberships = (
    ocel.relations[[event_id, object_id]].drop_duplicates()
    .merge(source_objects[[object_id, object_type]],
           on=object_id, validate="many_to_one")
)
event_type_multiplicity = (
    direct_memberships.groupby([object_type, event_id])[object_id]
    .nunique().rename("case_copies").reset_index()
)
membership_weights = direct_memberships.merge(
    event_type_multiplicity,
    on=[object_type, event_id], validate="many_to_one"
)
membership_weights["event_weight"] = 1.0 / membership_weights["case_copies"]
direct_membership_activity_frequencies = (
    direct_memberships.merge(source_events[[event_id, activity]], on=event_id)
    .groupby([object_type, activity]).size()
)

types = ["Order", "Item", "Package", "Delivery", "Invoice", "Payment"]
flattened_logs = {}
traditional_dfgs = {}
empirical_log_skeletons = {}
projection_audit_rows = []

for typ in types:
    flat_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, typ)
    flat_log = flat_log.sort_values(
        ["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"], kind="stable"
    )
    flattened_logs[typ] = flat_log
    edge_counts, start_counts, end_counts = pm4py.discover_dfg(flat_log)
    traditional_dfgs[typ] = {
        "edges": edge_counts, "starts": start_counts, "ends": end_counts
    }
    empirical_log_skeletons[typ] = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(flat_log)
    memberships = direct_memberships[direct_memberships[object_type] == typ]
    unique_events = memberships[event_id].nunique()
    projection_audit_rows.append({
        "object_type": typ,
        "cases": flat_log["case:concept:name"].nunique(),
        "rows": len(flat_log),
        "unique_source_events": unique_events,
        "excess_copies": len(flat_log) - unique_events,
    })

projection_audit = pd.DataFrame(projection_audit_rows).set_index("object_type")
expected_rows = dict(Order=42, Item=24, Package=12, Delivery=6, Invoice=7, Payment=4)
assert projection_audit["rows"].to_dict() == expected_rows
print(projection_audit)

object_centric_dfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
event_type_object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
object_interaction_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

# Preserve supports as well as counts: edge event metrics count event pairs.
ocdfg_edge_supports = object_centric_dfg["edges"]
ocdfg_edge_frequency_counts = {
    metric: {
        typ: {edge: len(support) for edge, support in typed_edges.items()}
        for typ, typed_edges in ocdfg_edge_supports[metric].items()
    }
    for metric in ("event_couples", "unique_objects", "total_objects")
}

order_rooted_execution_clusters = pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel(ocel, "Order")
# Audit cluster membership/overlap and extraction scope before comparing groups.
# Keep O2O qualifiers available: activity labels cannot distinguish M2 from M3.
# membership_weights supports event-frequency sensitivity, not automatic DFG
# weighting. Edge weighting requires source event-pair/object provenance.
# No model or visualization alone establishes causality, settlement allocation,
# independent executions, or a universally valid case notion.
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Copies and denominators.** Compare ordinary participation counts with inverse-multiplicity event weights: e16 gets weight 1/3 in each Order case, totaling one dispatch. Separately test removing shared events or assigning one owner; either damages coverage and creates artificial adjacency across removed events. For example, removing e16 changes P2's observed dispatch history. Report removals explicitly. Deduplicate DFG evidence by source event pairs; event weights alone do not specify edge weights.

2. **Direct versus propagated relations.** Test one-hop Invoice→billed Order propagation for finance; e19/e22/e25 should gain no order links because they already identify O2/O3. Separately propagate invoice events one hop to payments linked by settlement qualifiers. M2/M3 then acquire e19 and each other's records, extending M2 beyond its own payment and M3 before its recording. Label these invoice-context traces. Unrestricted closure would give each order all 31 events—124 occurrences—and erase meaningful boundaries.

3. **Change the lead and endpoints.** Recompute the D1/D2/D3 transit means under Delivery, Package, and Item weighting, and compare Order closure with last item confirmation. O3 completes physical delivery at e27, while O2 completes at e24 despite their shared V2. Check whether conclusions persist after endpoint and denominator alignment; differing legitimate KPIs should remain separate.

4. **OC-DFG frequency semantics.** Compare distinct events at nodes, distinct event pairs at edges, unique participating objects, and total object occurrences. For Item Dispatch→Confirm, the e16/e24 pair contributes one event pair, three unique items, and three object occurrences. For the entire Order Record Payment→Confirm edge, four distinct event pairs involve four unique orders but five occurrences: O2/O3 share e22/e24, and O3 contributes again at e25/e27. These measures answer different questions.

5. **Semantic and structural validation.** Reconcile the 31 source events against all six retention counts and 24 inventory objects. Verify P3/P4 membership and D2's two packages before attributing order completion. Validate V2's final-payment endpoint with completes_settlement, not last-event heuristics alone. Review any skeleton rule or execution cluster against these named counterexamples; overlapping contexts and four orders preclude treating discovered patterns as independent, general business laws.
