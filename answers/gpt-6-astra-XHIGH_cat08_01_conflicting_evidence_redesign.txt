# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The frequent fast path is an attractive starting point for selective automation, but a poor universal template. The redesign objective should be **time and total cost to a durable, compliant resolution**, alongside the time until the customer has a working product. R1–R3 are constraints; speed and cost savings cannot compensate for violating them.

- **Frequency DFG: volume concentrates in rapid replacement, while failures feed back into eligibility.** There are 3,600 Intake → Auto screen transitions, 2,550 Auto screen → Fast approval transitions, and 2,300 Fast approval → Dispatch transitions. However, complaints generate 310 returns to Manual review and 120 escalations. Those transitions suggest an opportunity to prevent incorrect decisions before fulfillment rather than accelerate the same decisions. The 350 Fast approval → Close transitions also warrant checking for legitimate resolutions without fulfillment or missing events.
- **Performance DFG: waiting and downstream discovery dominate the problematic paths.** Request evidence → Manual review has a 26-hour median and 88-hour p90; Manual review → Repair quote has a 15-hour median and 60-hour p90. Part-price confirmation, rather than necessarily technical work, explains much of quote delay. Dispatch → Complaint has a 70-hour median and 180-hour p90, followed by Complaint → Manual review at 31/100 hours and Complaint → Escalation at 42/130 hours. A claim can appear successfully closed well before its expensive failure becomes visible.
- **Rework and cost evidence contradict the apparent fast-path advantage.** Fast approval claims have 14.1% complaint/reopen incidence versus 3.3% on the specified repair sequence. Their cost advantage shrinks from 31% before rework to 9% after complaints, reshipments, and credits. Repeated evidence requests affect 26% of evidence-request entrants. Repair is not failure-free: rework affects 12.5% of repaired high-value devices versus 4.0% of repaired low-risk devices.
- **Conformance evidence shows material control failures despite reasonably high replay fitness.** Fitness of 0.91 does not mean 91% compliance: only 68% of traces fit the normative net. Alignments identify approval before evidence completion, complaints after close, and high-value dispatch without risk review. Low discovered-model precision indicates that it permits excessive behavior; discovering a path does not make it permissible. Model permissiveness and actual rule violations require separate investigation.
- **Segment outcomes reverse the preferred trade-off.** Premium accessories take 8 hours on the fast path versus 46 manually, with complaints of 6% versus 3%; the manual median already exceeds their 24-hour service-credit boundary. Standard accessories can tolerate 72 hours with good visibility, making additional verification more plausible. Premium high-value claims trade 20 versus 96 hours against complaints of 22% versus 5%; Standard high-value claims trade 28 versus 120 hours against 18% versus 6%. Wrong replacements are also much more expensive for high-value devices: EUR 410 versus EUR 95 for accessories. The unclear-defect/evidence group, 22% of cases with a 140-hour median, needs a different intervention from either straightforward replacement or repair.
- **Resource evidence rules out simply reviewing everything.** Seven reviewers provide 252 productive hours weekly; 82% utilization corresponds to roughly 207 hours of workload, beyond which queues grow sharply. Updating automation could add 15–35% to manual-review inflow. Handling times and current utilization are unknown, so that increase cannot be translated directly into required headcount. Fraud specialists have Monday/campaign peaks, inspectors have spare capacity outside repair campaigns, and the daily 45-minute escalation board cannot absorb routine recovery work.

Two interpretation safeguards matter. First, the DFG is an aggregate of transitions, not a count of unique complete routes. Its listed terminal transitions total 3,625 against 5,000 starts, and several internal counts do not balance. Reconcile extraction windows, repeated activities, omitted events, and unfinished cases before estimating route probabilities. Do not infer that the remaining 1,375 cases were abandoned, or add edge medians/p90s to estimate end-to-end times. Second, the path comparisons are observational: value, defect, evidence completeness, and customer selection may explain part of the differences. They justify targeted experiments, not a causal claim that repair alone reduces complaints.

All three designs use explicit control events: evidence completeness and its basis; claim amount; review outcome; replacement compatibility; and reopen-specific review. R1 permits **Manual review OR Fraud/risk review** before Fast approval above EUR 400; “high-value” is not a substitute for the actual amount. R2's dispatch exception applies only to Premium low-risk accessories. R3 requires Manual review after each reopen and before subsequent final close or denial. Investigate whether the alignment's high-value risk-review requirement reflects an additional policy or a misconfigured net; do not silently replace R1's either/or rule with mandatory specialist review. Model legitimate reopening explicitly while retaining R3.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-tiered replacement with controlled dispatch

**Redesigned flow.** Intake captures segment, amount, defect, product/part identifiers, and available evidence → updated segment-aware auto screen → one of three lanes:

1. **Clear accessory claim:** confirm eligibility and evidence, then Fast approval → compatibility validation → replacement dispatch → recorded fulfillment and compliant close. Only Premium low-risk accessories can use the documented R2 evidence exception; R1 still applies if their amount exceeds EUR 400.
2. **High-value or uncertain eligibility:** obtain evidence and qualifying Manual review; send actual fraud indicators to specialists → technical replacement decision → Fast approval where appropriate → pre-dispatch quality gate → dispatch → close.
3. **Unclear defect, unsuitable replacement, or unresolved evidence:** exit automation into a named reviewer's existing compliant diagnostic/repair route. Mid-risk devices initially use this reviewed route because separate outcome evidence is missing.

Every reopened claim receives a fresh Manual review before final disposition. A named owner handles routine recovery; only exceptional cases reach the escalation board.

**Targets.** Primarily clear accessories, with controlled expansion to high-value claims suitable for replacement. It directly changes Auto screen → Fast approval and Fast approval → Dispatch, addressing outdated rules and incorrect replacement selection.

**Mechanism of improvement.** Preserve the low-cost EUR 0.20 screening step while making routing sensitive to risk and service expectations. Validate SKU, compatibility, and defect-to-replacement mapping before dispatch. Use inspectors for ambiguous selections and high-value replacements, with reserved dispatch windows; straightforward accessory checks should be embedded in picking where feasible. This uses otherwise underused inspection capacity without assuming it remains available after campaigns.

The gate's value depends on the reduction in wrong-replacement probability multiplied by EUR 95 or EUR 410, relative to its labor, delay, and service-credit costs. Complaint rates are not wrong-part probabilities, so these data do not establish a break-even result. Test both catches and errors escaping the gate.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.** Some claims lose their present speed advantage, and manual-review demand may rise 15–35%. A gate adding 6–18 hours can jeopardize the Premium accessory promise; an 8-hour current median does not establish adequate tail headroom. Avoid a blanket human gate for that lane until its benefit and capacity are demonstrated. Replacement-first handling still incurs inventory and product costs, and this design only contains—not fundamentally solves—the repeated-evidence and repair-pricing loops. Low-risk residual complaints may remain higher than on a fully reviewed path.

# 3. Redesign B: Evidence and diagnosis-led resolution pods

**Redesigned flow.** Intake → named case owner and defect-specific evidence checklist → one consolidated evidence request when needed, with assisted phone/store upload available → evidence completion and eligibility assessment → technical diagnosis → repair-or-replace decision.

While evidence is being collected, the owner arranges technician availability and retrieves part prices; these preparatory tasks do not authorize fulfillment. For repairable cases: issue a quote from confirmed prices or an approved pricing band → customer approval → repair → quality check → compliant close. For unsuitable repairs: reviewed replacement decision → compatibility check → dispatch after evidence completion → close. Repair rework returns to the same pod for root-cause review; repeated failure triggers a deliberate replace/escalate decision rather than indefinite repair cycling. Reopening returns to the owner and includes the required new Manual review.

**Targets.** The unclear-defect/missing-evidence group and Standard high-value devices are the principal cohorts. Premium high-value devices receive urgent diagnosis and quote slots where repair is credible. Clear accessories retain a compliant rapid route; repairing or manually investigating every accessory would waste their speed advantage.

**Mechanism of improvement.** Change the unit of work from repeated departmental referrals to a case owned through diagnosis and fulfillment. The owner consolidates requests, carries evidence forward, and explains exactly what is missing. An evidence portal supports that interaction, with an assisted alternative; it does not become a mandatory digital barrier. Its forecast 30–45% reduction concerns repeated requests, not automatically total cycle time or abandonment.

A shared part-price source, time-bounded supplier confirmation, and technician/reviewer coordination attack the 60-hour p90 from Manual review to Quote. Begin feasible administrative tasks in parallel to remove serial waiting. Repair when diagnostic confidence, parts availability, and expected total cost support it, reducing expensive incorrect high-value replacements. Use existing inspectors for release checks and feed high-value repair failures back into diagnosis and repair instructions.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.** More human attention and repair work increase upfront labor, even if less replacement and recovery eventually offset it. The 3.3% repair-path complaint rate is an observed comparator, not a promised result for newly routed cases. The 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate remains a substantial challenge. Some customers will still wait for physical repair, and Premium expectations may exceed what faster quoting can deliver. Ownership cannot eliminate supplier or parts shortages, and a portal alone cannot resolve genuinely ambiguous defects.

# 4. Redesign C: Temporary device restoration alongside final repair

**Redesigned flow.** Intake → evidence completion, eligibility review, and technical triage → approved temporary-support plan and customer consent → two coordinated workstreams:

- **Restore use:** select a compatible loaner from a tested pool → claim-specific pre-dispatch verification → delivery and confirmation that the customer has a working device.
- **Resolve the claim:** collect the original device → detailed diagnosis and confirmed quote → customer approval → repair and quality check, or an approved permanent replacement → deliver the final device → retrieve, reconcile, and retest the loaner → compliant final close.

The temporary dispatch does not waive evidence or review controls: high-value cases have complete evidence and qualifying review before dispatch, including R1 before any Fast approval. Give the customer a clear account of loaner responsibilities and the final repair decision. A recovery owner coordinates both streams, with Manual review after any claim reopening. Record “working device restored” separately from claim closure.

**Targets.** Initially Premium high-value devices, where the reviewed/repair path has substantially fewer complaints but a 96-hour median. Standard high-value customers could opt in later if the pool economics and their need for continuity justify it. Accessories generally do not justify two-way loaner logistics.

**Mechanism of improvement.** Decouple the customer's loss of device use from the duration of diagnosis and repair. A reusable loaner lets the organization retain a reviewed technical decision while relieving disruption earlier. Batch testing returned loaners and reserving compatible stock moves some quality work ahead of individual claims; final compatibility verification still occurs before dispatch. This is a different operating model from A's permanent replacement decision and B's faster sequential resolution: it introduces a circulating asset pool and two fulfillment streams.

Mine linked claim, original-device, loaner, shipment, and return records so one fast shipment cannot conceal an unresolved claim or missing asset. Customer communications show both restoration and final-resolution commitments.

**Deliberate sacrifices and unresolved issues.** This design prioritizes customer continuity over minimum transaction cost. It adds inventory funding, reverse logistics, loss/damage exposure, inspection work, and possibly another customer setup or data-transfer burden. It may shorten time without a working product while leaving final cycle time unchanged or longer. Evidence delays still block dispatch, and a compatible loaner may be unavailable. None of these costs or benefits is quantified in the prompt; the pilot must establish demand and economics before buying a large fleet.

# 5. Pareto-style comparison table

Effects are hypotheses relative to the current process for comparable segments, including recovery costs. “No” means no domination has been demonstrated, not proof that a design lies on the true Pareto frontier. Current noncompliant behavior is a descriptive baseline, not an acceptable experimental treatment.

| Design | Cycle-time effect | Cost effect | Quality/rework effect | Compliance risk | Customer-experience effect | Resource pressure | Implementation complexity | Best-fit segment | Dominated? yes/no/conditional |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current process | Fast initial completion; long evidence and recovery tails | Fast path 31% cheaper before rework, only 9% after | Fast approval complaints 14.1%; repair-route 3.3%; repeat evidence common | Material observed deviations | Strong initial speed, uneven durability; Premium delays and abandonment | Review queues, specialist peaks, quote waits, limited board | Existing operation; remediation still required | Clear accessories benefit most from current speed | Conditional: compliant subsets may remain competitive; current violations require correction |
| A: Controlled replacement | Similar for clear claims; targeted gates add 6–18 hours; recovery tail may fall | Upfront increase for selected checks; total flat/down if avoided errors cover it | Fewer wrong replacements expected; diagnostic and evidence rework persist | Lower with enforceable routing and evidence/reopen gates | Preserves accessory speed; greater reliability; possible Premium gate delays | More reviewer inflow; dispatch inspection and campaign peaks | Low–medium: two-week rule update plus gate staffing and integration | Clear accessories; replacement-suitable reviewed devices | No: plausible speed/cost advantage, subject to quality and capacity results |
| B: Resolution pods | Evidence/quote waiting should fall; repair remains slower than direct replacement | Higher initial labor; fewer replacements and recovery costs may offset it | Better evidence and diagnosis expected; high-value repair rework remains | Lower with owned decisions and explicit closure controls | Fewer requests, clearer status, durable resolution; physical repair wait remains | Case owners, technicians, part-price confirmation, repair inspectors | Medium–high: ownership, assisted portal, pricing and repair coordination | Unclear defects/evidence; Standard high-value devices | No: plausible quality and total-cost advantage for suitable repairs |
| C: Temporary restoration | Working device sooner if stock exists; final closure flat/slower | Likely higher logistics and capital cost; credit/recovery offsets uncertain | Reviewed final repair retained; loaner and return failures added | Lower if controls cover both streams; dual records add execution risk | Less device downtime; added exchanges/setup may burden customers | Loaner pool, reverse logistics, inventory reconciliation, inspection | High: new service, asset tracking, stock and return operations | Premium high-value devices | Conditional: dominated if restoration gains are small or customers prefer a one-step resolution |

No design wins across all dimensions. A's shorter path can remain preferable for accessories despite somewhat higher residual rework; B's review and repair effort is easier to justify for costly devices and uncertain defects. C is defensible only when reduced device downtime is valuable enough to pay for its additional operations. Segment mix, selection effects, missing activity times, and unmeasured logistics costs prevent exact ROI or dominance claims.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**A — controlled replacement**

- Incorrect risk classifications or stale product mappings can create compliant-looking but wrong replacements. Audit the inputs and the decision outcome, not just the existence of a check event.
- The 15–35% review-inflow increase can overwhelm the seven reviewers, while the quality gate pushes Premium accessories beyond 24 hours. Routing everything uncertain to fraud would worsen the two-specialist peak bottleneck.
- **Likely migration:** complaints/escalations move upstream into manual review and dispatch inspection. Reserve capacity, route ordinary eligibility questions to reviewers, and limit rollout when projected workloads approach the observed utilization threshold.

**B — resolution pods**

- A mandatory portal may reduce requests among customers who finish while increasing abandonment among those needing assistance. Provide a staffed alternative and assess all originally eligible customers.
- Consolidated requests can become an excessive checklist, and presumed pricing shortcuts can produce inaccurate quotes. Limit evidence to the defect and risk, and authorize clear pricing bounds.
- Repair-first routing can overload technicians and reproduce the 12.5% high-value repair-rework problem; pod ownership can also fail when its owner is absent. Use pooled backup and explicit replace/escalate criteria.
- **Likely migration:** removed evidence and handoff queues expose parts availability, technician capacity, and post-repair inspection as constraints. Measure these queues separately and pace intake to available repair capacity.

**C — temporary restoration**

- Loaner stockouts or incompatible devices eliminate the restoration benefit and generate another complaint path.
- Late returns, loss, damage, and customer setup burdens can exceed any service-credit benefit. Test customer acceptance and return performance before scaling inventory.
- Teams may mark restoration as closure, concealing long-running repairs or bypassing reopened-claim review. Separate milestones, accountable owners, and linked asset records are essential.
- **Likely migration:** waiting shifts from the customer to the loaner pool, reverse logistics, repair backlog, and reconciliation. Track fleet availability and overdue final resolutions together; good restoration times alone cannot justify expansion.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0 — establish a usable baseline and enforce the rules.** Reconcile the 5,000 starts with the terminal-event extract, link complaints and reshipments to original claims, and distinguish queue time from labor time. Capture actual amounts and joint customer/defect/evidence attributes rather than treating reporting labels as risk rules. Validate the normative net against R1–R3 and any additional high-value policy. Introduce explicit control checks in every pilot and comparator; do not randomize customers into known violations.

Use a common measurement set throughout:

- **Time:** intake-to-final-resolution median and p90, reopen-adjusted resolution, open-case aging, first working-device restoration, and segment-specific service attainment. Measure Premium accessory completion within 24 hours and Standard accessory completion within 72 hours. Do not invent a 24-hour promise for Premium high-value devices.
- **Cost:** actual handling time at stated labor rates, parts/devices, inspection, reshipments, repair rework, credits, escalations, and—under C—loaner capital, shipping, loss, and refurbishment. Report cost per initiated claim alongside successful-resolution and abandonment rates so inexpensive abandonment cannot masquerade as savings.
- **Quality/compliance:** wrong-part escapes, complaints and repeated reopens, repair rework by value/defect, repeated evidence requests, and audited R1–R3 violations. Track fitting traces and alignment deviations as supporting diagnostics, not substitutes for rule-specific checks.
- **Experience/resources:** abandonment and complaints by segment and assistance need, status visibility, reviewer utilization, specialist peak queues, quote waiting, inspection backlog, and board referrals.

**Stage 1 — first pilot: A in Standard low-risk accessories.** This 25% segment offers a 72-hour tolerance and an 8% fast-path complaint baseline, allowing a targeted dispatch check to be tested before exposing Premium customers to its delay. Compare against a contemporaneous policy-compliant accessory route, stratified by defect and initial evidence status. A first 100–200 cases can test workflow and capacity; it cannot establish rare-event safety or a modest complaint-rate improvement. Size the outcome phase around a prespecified meaningful effect and uncertainty interval.

Proposed decision thresholds—not forecasts—are:

- Any verified R1–R3 breach triggers immediate containment of the affected lane and correction before further exposure.
- Expand only with projected reviewer utilization below roughly 75–80%, leaving headroom; pause expansion near or above 82%, or when specialist/inspection queues show sustained deterioration.
- Seek complaint incidence around 4–6% versus the 8% comparable fast-path baseline, 90–95% completion within 72 hours, and mature total cost no more than about 0–5% above the comparator. A cost increase needs an explicit quality justification.
- Extend to Premium accessories only after demonstrating the agreed 24-hour attainment target, provisionally at least 95%, with no service-credit increase. Expand into reviewed high-value replacements only after checking both reviewer headroom and error prevention.

**Stage 2 — test B in evidence-incomplete claims and Standard high-value devices.** Compare pods with the best compliant existing reviewed route within each cohort. Assess the entire package first; if it succeeds, separately test whether the portal or coordination changes account for the gain. Keep assisted evidence submission available in both treatment design and measurement.

Seek a 30–45% relative reduction in repeated requests, consistent with the portal hypothesis, and roughly 20–30% lower p90 evidence/quote waiting versus comparable controls. If the population and denominator remain comparable, the former would move a 26% repeat rate to approximately 14–18%; also measure requests per initiated claim to detect denominator changes. Stop or modify the portal if abandonment rises by more than about 2 percentage points, including within the assistance-needing cohort. Require no material deterioration in high-value repair rework or mature complaints; a faster quote is insufficient if final resolution worsens. Permit a modest, pre-agreed cost increase only where durable quality or service gains warrant it.

**Stage 3 — test C in a limited Premium high-value cohort.** After controls and reviewed-resolution performance are understood, use a small compatible loaner pool and compare the offer with the best compliant high-value route established earlier. Preserve offer-based analysis: customers declining a loaner remain in the offered cohort, with uptake reported separately.

An illustrative go criterion is a 30–50% reduction in p90 time without a working device, no more than roughly 10% deterioration in final-resolution p90, no material increase in mature complaints, and incremental cost within a sponsor-set willingness to pay per day of downtime avoided. Stop expansion if stockouts, overdue returns, or losses breach the pilot's explicitly costed limits. Do not approve C on restoration speed alone.

**Establish that improvement persists downstream.** Randomize within eligible segment/defect/evidence strata where feasible; otherwise use a staggered rollout with contemporaneous matched controls and state the remaining selection uncertainty. Include Monday and campaign periods. Shared reviewer or inspector capacity can worsen controls when treatment expands, so monitor total-system queues and use cluster/time-block assignment if interference is substantial.

Follow each resolved claim for at least 30 days, with a 60-day sensitivity check and separate tracking of still-open cases; these are proposed observation windows, not proof that later failures disappear. The dispatch-to-complaint p90 of 180 hours makes same-week closure comparisons inadequate. Analyze abandonment as a separate adverse outcome and unfinished cases with censoring-aware measures rather than dropping them. Compare complaint, rework, credit, and asset-return outcomes by intake cohort after equal follow-up. Re-mine the event log to verify that reduced eligibility/evidence loops have not become dispatch errors, repair rework, overdue loaners, or late reopens.

Use the experiments to select routing by segment: A is the initial candidate for clear accessories, B for ambiguous claims and repair-suitable Standard high-value devices, and C for Premium high-value customers whose continuity benefit justifies its cost. Retain a redesign only where its measured trade-off is acceptable; do not select one universal path from the aggregate average.
