# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed change is best interpreted as a combination of **composition, within-worker change, and operating context**:

\[
\text{Observed change}
=
\text{case/activity mix}
+
\text{within-stratum performance}
+
\text{selection and measurement effects}.
\]

Those components need not move in the same direction. A falling mean service time can coexist with rising errors.

**Early sequence positions, 1–5.** These events are slow because they combine shift-start backlog, high complexity, high-priority work, and possibly initial task warm-up. Early-shift evidence is especially important: the Early shift begins with a queue of 61 and 48% high-complexity cases in its first hour, and seniors often take the complex carryover. Consequently, early service time and error differences are not clean measures of worker learning or fatigue. Some genuine warm-up is plausible, but the first-hour case mix already works against it.

**Positions 6–30.** Service time and errors both improve. This is compatible with task familiarity and stabilization: workers know the queue, routines, and likely exceptions. However, complexity, priority, and queue length are also falling. Activity mix may be changing as well—for example, later events may more often be routine document or closure activities. The slight error reduction from 2.8% to 2.5% is consistent with learning, but it is also consistent with easier assignment.

**Positions 31–50.** This is the strongest apparently “good” period: low complexity, relatively low queue, lower errors, and lower rework. Residual learning may still be present, but a large part of the performance is probably selection: the easiest and most favorable mix of work is reaching these positions.

**Positions 51 and later.** Service time continues to fall, but queue length rises sharply and errors and rework increase. This is where queue pressure and workarounds become more plausible than learning. Under low WIP, later error rates for all resource groups remain close to their early rates. Under high WIP, they rise to 4.9% for seniors, 6.8% for mid-level staff, 12.4% for juniors, and 8.2% for Night staff. That interaction is the clearest evidence that time-in-shift alone is not driving errors.

The WIP/complexity table reinforces this. Even after coarse complexity adjustment, high WIP has faster service and substantially higher errors. Thus the late speed-up is not merely a complexity-composition artifact. Possible explanations include:

- workers clear easy queued items first while leaving difficult items active;
- under visible backlog pressure, workers shorten active handling or defer review;
- sequential batching creates artificially short event durations;
- the mix of completed events changes, because slow or defective cases remain in WIP;
- bot classifications are accepted rapidly and corrected later by humans;
- end-of-shift quality batches make short correction or closure events more common.

Therefore, faster event-level service in this period does not necessarily mean the overall claim system is faster. A better queue may have many easy completed cases and a growing residual backlog of difficult or failed cases.

Fatigue may amplify the late error increase, but it is not necessary to explain it and cannot be the primary explanation by itself. If fatigue were dominant, one would expect later service time to rise or become much more variable, and errors to worsen even under low WIP. The data show the opposite: service falls, and low-WIP quality is comparatively stable. Cumulative fatigue can nevertheless coexist with rushing, so it should be tested using actual exposure and break data rather than inferred from sequence position.

Circadian and shift effects also modify the pattern. Day shift combines a late demand peak with a queue increase from 38 to 75 and a 7.5% late error rate. Late shift inherits much of that backlog. Night shift is slower, has a high first-hour complexity share of 56%, and handles fewer but more urgent exceptions. These are different demand and selection environments, not equivalent experiments in worker fatigue.

The resource comparisons also show that worker group is not a sufficient explanation:

- Seniors are stable under low WIP, suggesting useful experience or careful work.
- Juniors have elevated early errors, consistent with both learning needs and early assignment of more difficult work.
- Juniors’ later low-WIP error rate is lower than their early rate, which argues against a simple “juniors get worse over time” narrative.
- The very high junior error rate under high WIP is an exposure and management-condition effect unless assignment and process controls show otherwise.

Thus the best integrated interpretation is:

1. **Early service time is partly warm-up and partly difficult carryover work.**
2. **Middle-period improvement combines learning with favorable selection and activity mix.**
3. **Late service speed is sustained by simpler completed work, prioritization, batching, and possible work compression under pressure.**
4. **Late errors arise primarily from pressure-sensitive interactions with complexity, automation review, fatigue/circadian exposure, and end-of-shift batching—not from elapsed time alone.**

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Define outcomes and data quality first.**

- Separate active service time, `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`, from waiting time and total case cycle time. Validate whether batched events have reliable start/end timestamps.
- Standardize timestamps and timezone, including Night shifts crossing midnight. Remove or quarantine negative, zero, duplicate, and implausibly long human durations.
- Construct a stable `shift_date` from `shift_start_time`, rather than calendar date alone.
- Use cases—not only events—as the denominator for rework and repeated-event analyses. A case contributing several events should not be treated as independent evidence.
- Restrict seven-day rework analyses to cases with a complete follow-up window, or use survival analysis for right censoring.
- Record whether an error was detected during active work, during later quality review, or only after rework. Busy periods may generate more review opportunities and therefore more observed errors.
- Include activity and activity-family fixed effects. A late position with more routine closure or quality events is not directly comparable with an early triage event.

**Within-resource comparisons.**

- Compare the first 20 events with later events for the same resource on the same shift-date, ideally within the same activity and complexity band.
- Use both `sequence_position_within_shift` and cumulative prior work by that resource. Add prior number of similar cases to distinguish task learning from simple elapsed-time effects.
- Use matched cases, exact activity matching, or propensity weighting based on complexity, priority, activity, case type, queue, and shift.
- Fit resource-specific learning or fatigue slopes with partial pooling. Report estimates and uncertainty by resource rather than rankings based on raw rates.
- Test whether improvement persists across shifts and prior similar-case volume. Learning should normally carry over or improve across days; fatigue should worsen with cumulative exposure and reset after a shift or break.

**Within-shift position effects.**

- Fit separate models for early versus late events within the same shift-date and resource.
- Compare sequence position with minutes since shift start, absolute clock hour, and time since the last break. Fit position-only, clock-only, and joint specifications to determine which representation is more informative and to identify collinearity.
- Include `shift_id × position` interactions. A position effect present only in the late Day shift is different from one shared across all shifts.
- Use flexible splines for position rather than assuming a linear learning curve. Identify change points around the transition to high WIP.
- Include activity and priority interactions: learning may be activity-specific, and queue pressure may affect triage differently from quality checks.

**WIP and queue stratification.**

- Analyze `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` separately. WIP measures active ownership; queue length may measure waiting demand.
- Use low/medium/high bands for interpretability, but also fit continuous nonlinear models because the apparent effect may begin before 70.
- Test lagged queue and WIP values at 15, 30, and 60 minutes to reduce simultaneity and reverse-causality concerns.
- Estimate `WIP × complexity`, `WIP × sequence`, `WIP × activity`, and `WIP × resource experience` interactions.
- Include arrivals, completions, staffing ratio, and oldest-case age. If WIP is high because prior cases failed, contemporaneous WIP is partly an outcome of earlier errors.
- Seek quasi-exogenous variation from unexpected demand changes, approved staffing changes, or shift handovers. An alert rollout alone will not make WIP exogenous.

**Complexity adjustment and selection analysis.**

- Model complexity continuously with splines rather than only as “low” and “high.” The supplied coarse bands can hide important severity gradients.
- Adjust for priority, activity, activity family, case type, customer/channel, data completeness, and bot involvement.
- Estimate the probability that a case is assigned to each resource or sequence band. Use inverse-probability weighting, matching, or a doubly robust estimator to compare comparable assignments.
- Produce standardized predictions: for example, predict each resource’s error rate under the same WIP, complexity, activity, and priority distribution.
- Treat adjustment as associational unless assignment and relevant confounders are sufficiently understood. Complexity scores can themselves be incomplete or measured after assignment.

**Shift and time-of-day controls.**

- Include shift, date, weekday/weekend, holiday, hour of day, month, and handover backlog.
- Represent clock hour cyclically with sine/cosine terms or a cyclic spline. Night work spans dates, so use both calendar timestamp and elapsed time since shift start.
- Include staffing ratio and shift overlap. Early and Day shifts overlap from 09:00 to 14:00, making clock and sequence non-equivalent.
- Model Night separately if sample sizes are small; a pooled Night estimate can conceal large uncertainty.
- Use a causal DAG to identify which variables are confounders, mediators, or outcomes. For example, queue may mediate the effect of a staffing decision; controlling for it changes the estimand.

**Batching detection.**

- Apply `pm4py.discover_batches` to Document Review, especially Junior_H and Junior_I in the Day shift, and to end-of-shift Quality Check activity.
- Use several prespecified batch-size thresholds as a sensitivity analysis rather than selecting the threshold that produces the preferred result.
- Validate batches against event gaps, the same queue identifier, and workarounds recorded by staff.
- Estimate within-batch service, batch size, and downstream correction/rework effects at multiple lags.
- Test whether batch membership explains part of the apparent late-period service-time reduction and error increase.

**Resource-experience interactions.**

- Treat `resource_experience_months` as continuous where possible; three broad groups may conceal substantial differences.
- Estimate experience interactions with sequence, WIP, complexity, shift, and clock hour.
- Include a prior-case learning measure; nominal tenure is not equivalent to experience with a specific claim type.
- Use hierarchical models so small Night and resource-specific estimates are shrunk appropriately.
- Compare assignment balance and workload, not raw error rankings.

**Rework and downstream error analysis.**

- Use `get_rework_cases_per_activity` and path analysis to determine which upstream activities predict later rework.
- Model time from source activity to rework, not just seven-day rework incidence. Account for cases closed without rework as a competing outcome.
- Link bot classifications to subsequent human corrections and downstream rework.
- Examine whether end-of-shift Quality Check batches merely relocate error discovery. Compare service time with total defect age and customer-impacting rework.
- Analyze error-to-rework pathways by complexity, WIP, reviewer, shift, and batch membership.

A mixed-effects model could then be specified as follows:

```python
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from statsmodels.genmod.cov_struct import Exchangeable
from statsmodels.genmod.families import Binomial

d = log.copy()
d = d[d["org:resource"] != "Bot_Risk"].copy()

# Independent audit of active service time.
d["service_min"] = (
    d["time:timestamp"] - d["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

d["hour"] = (
    d["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
    + d["time:timestamp"].dt.minute / 60.0
)
d["weekday"] = d["time:timestamp"].dt.day_name()
d["high_wip"] = (d["wip_at_start"] > 70).astype(int)
d["high_complexity"] = (
    d["case_complexity_score"] >= COMPLEXITY_CUTOFF
).astype(int)
d["late_position"] = (
    d["sequence_position_within_shift"] > 30
).astype(int)
d["error"] = d["error_flag"].astype(int)
d["resource_shift_date"] = (
    d["org:resource"].astype(str)
    + "_"
    + d["shift_start_time"].astype(str)
)

# Fit position-only, clock-only, and joint models separately.
service_formula = """
np.log(service_min) ~
    bs(sequence_position_within_shift, df=4)
  + bs(wip_at_start, df=3) * high_complexity
  + bs(queue_length_at_start, df=3)
  + bs(minutes_since_shift_start, df=4)
  + np.sin(2*np.pi*hour/24) + np.cos(2*np.pi*hour/24)
  + C(Q("concept:name")) + C(activity_family)
  + C(priority) + C(shift_id) + C(weekday)
  + resource_experience_months
"""

service_model = smf.mixedlm(
    service_formula,
    d,
    groups=d["org:resource"]
).fit()

error_formula = """
error ~
    bs(sequence_position_within_shift, df=4)
  * high_wip * high_complexity
  + resource_experience_months * high_wip
  + bs(minutes_since_shift_start, df=4)
  + np.sin(2*np.pi*hour/24) + np.cos(2*np.pi*hour/24)
  + C(Q("concept:name")) + C(activity_family)
  + C(priority) + C(shift_id) + C(weekday)
  + wip_at_start + queue_length_at_start
"""

# A production analysis should use a multilevel logistic or Bayesian model
# with resource, case, and shift-date effects; GEE is shown schematically.
error_model = smf.gee(
    error_formula,
    groups=d["resource_shift_date"],
    family=Binomial(),
    cov_struct=Exchangeable()
).fit()
```

Report marginal predictions at common WIP and complexity levels rather than raw coefficients. For example, compare each resource’s predicted error rate under low, medium, and high WIP with the same observed case mix. If evaluating a new alert, break, or staffing policy, use a stepped-wedge rollout, cluster-randomized crossover, or difference-in-differences with shift-date fixed effects and pre-trend checks.

Threshold gaming, alert fatigue, suppression of WIP recording, and diversion of useful cases are possible. Validate the threshold and use a sustained breach rather than a single observation. | Number of alerts and alert-to-action time; duration above 70; arrivals versus completions; queue age; WIP before and after alerts; service and error by WIP band; bot corrections; staffing interventions; false alerts and alert overrides. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias** | Randomization can reduce systematic differences in assigned complexity and make performance comparisons more credible. It may also improve perceived fairness. | Fully random assignment can mismatch skill and task, especially for Specialist Referral or complex Risk Classification. Use stratified randomization by qualification, activity, priority, and complexity instead of unrestricted randomization. | Assignment balance by complexity, activity, priority, and case type; assignment propensity; adjusted service/error; waiting by priority; learning curves; rework; workload dispersion; cases requiring reassignment; training completion. |
| **5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours** | If fatigue is contributing, breaks may reduce omissions, variability, and late errors. Clustered or staggered breaks can preserve coverage. | Breaks can temporarily increase WIP, interrupt batches, create handoffs, or move work into the next shift. Mandatory breaks without coverage planning may worsen queues. | Break start/end and adherence; pre/post-break errors; service-time variability and P90; queue and WIP during breaks; missed handovers; batch fragmentation; downstream rework; voluntary fatigue and workload measures. |
| **6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift** | A controlled deferral could smooth the end-of-shift burst, reduce rushed review, and make workload more stable. | It can lengthen total cycle time and delay discovery of defects. Quality checks must not be removed or bypassed. Any deferral needs a documented due time, accountable next-shift owner, risk classification, and end-to-end tracking. | QC backlog and oldest-QC age; completion within SLA; seven- and 30-day rework; escaped defects; customer impact; end-shift correction bursts; cross-shift ownership; cases lacking an assigned reviewer. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks** | Likely to help because seniors maintain low error rates under low WIP and may support complex cases, bot corrections, and quality review. | Cost, senior overload, and a new queue bottleneck are possible. Removing seniors from direct work may also reduce junior learning opportunities and leave complex decisions concentrated elsewhere. | Senior utilization and queue; complex-case waiting; error and rework by complexity and activity; bot correction quality; junior workload and learning; total cycle time; escalation volume; reviewer agreement. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | A nominal deterrent or signal is sometimes claimed, but it is not a reliable evidence-based performance intervention. | Raw late-shift rates conflate case mix, WIP, urgency, review intensity, and management-created queues. Punishment can suppress reporting, reduce help-seeking, worsen morale, increase turnover, and shift work toward safer-looking employees. It should not be implemented on raw error rates. | If leadership nevertheless evaluates such a policy, monitor reporting completeness, voluntary disclosures, help requests, morale, absenteeism, turnover, assignment avoidance, and adjusted—not raw—outcomes. These are warning indicators of harm, not proof of benefit. |

The most defensible sequence is to combine WIP/queue visibility with an actionable response, targeted senior support at known peaks, and competency-aware assignment. Break and shift-length policies should be piloted with genuine exposure and recovery data. Penalties should be rejected.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection bias is substantial.** Seniors handle more complex carryover early, juniors may receive difficult work and less favorable queues, and Night staff handle urgent exceptions. Comparing their raw rates attributes a management-created assignment environment to personal ability.
- **Early work is not a fair benchmark.** Early-shift cases have the highest complexity and high-priority share. “First 20 events” is therefore not a neutral control period.
- **Queue pressure is partly management-created.** Demand allocation, staffing, prioritization, and work release affect WIP. Treating high WIP as an employee performance variable can hide the source of the risk.
- **WIP is endogenous.** Errors and rework can leave cases active, causing high WIP. High WIP may therefore be both a cause and consequence of poor performance.
- **Measurement error matters.** Batched events, coarse timestamps, system cut-offs, and the bot’s instant processing can make active service time incomparable. Waiting, service, and batch size must be separated.
- **Error detection is not uniform.** A busy period may contain more quality reviews, making errors more likely to be observed even if underlying error incidence is unchanged. Conversely, rushed work may conceal errors until later.
- **Rework has censoring and attribution issues.** Recent events may not have a full seven-day window, and rework discovered at end of shift may belong to an earlier upstream event.
- **Automation quality is contextual.** Bot_Risk is instantaneous, but that is not comparable productivity. Human correction may reflect changed input data, a difficult queue, reviewer disagreement, or a bot defect. The system should not attribute all correction to the bot—or all correction difficulty to the human reviewer.
- **Raw worker comparisons are unfair and unstable.** Rates based on few cases have high variance. Resource, activity, complexity, and exposure must be modeled jointly, with uncertainty intervals.
- **Punitive use creates Goodhart effects.** Workers may avoid difficult cases, under-report errors, omit batch information, or seek lower-queue assignments. Error rates become unsuitable as a reward or penalty metric.
- **Fair assignment may require more than randomization.** Full randomization can be unsafe or inefficient for specialized work. Use auditable assignment policies, record assignment reasons, and measure whether protected or tenure groups receive systematically harder or more overloaded work.
- **Individual conclusions require stronger evidence than the aggregate tables provide.** The correct unit for worker evaluation should normally be a case- and opportunity-adjusted, multi-period estimate with workload and assignment transparency—not an end-of-shift error percentage.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

| Indicator | Operational definition | Required views and safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Active service time** | Median and P90 of `time:timestamp - start_timestamp`, in minutes. Report mean only as a secondary statistic. | By activity, shift, resource cohort, complexity band, WIP, and position. Show humans and Bot_Risk separately. Exclude or quarantine invalid timestamps. |
| **2. Waiting time and queue age** | Time from predecessor completion or release to work start, plus age of the oldest waiting case. | P50, P90, and P95 by priority and shift; show queue-owner changes. Distinguish waiting from active service and show total case cycle time. |
| **3. WIP and flow balance** | Number of active cases by resource, queue, shift, and complexity; arrivals minus completions; duration above the chosen pressure threshold. | Time series with WIP > 70 highlighted. Include staffing ratio, oldest-case age, and batch completion bursts. |
| **4. Error rate** | Confirmed errors per 100 eligible events and per 100 eligible cases, with opportunity counts. | Show raw and standardized rates adjusted for WIP, complexity, priority, activity, shift, and review exposure. Include confidence intervals; no raw worker league table. |
| **5. Rework rate and time-to-rework** | Percentage of eligible cases with confirmed rework within seven days, plus days from source activity to rework. | By source activity, complexity, WIP, batch, shift, and reviewer. Enforce complete follow-up or display censoring. Include customer-impacting rework separately. |
| **6. Complexity and priority mix** | Weighted mean complexity score, percentage above the prespecified high-complexity threshold, activity-family share, and high-priority share. | By position, resource cohort, shift, queue, and assignment source. This is essential for interpreting service and error changes. |
| **7. Batching indicators** | Percentage of events in detected batches, median and maximum batch size, within-batch service time, and post-batch correction rate. | Detect with `discover_batches`; report by activity, resource, shift, and queue. Flag unusually large or late-shift batches. |
| **8. Handover and queue-ownership changes** | Number of ownership changes per case, percentage of cases crossing resource or shift boundaries, time waiting after handover, unowned WIP, and queue-owner changes. | Show by case type, priority, shift boundary, and complexity. If ownership is not explicit, derive it from resource/team changes and add a `queue_owner`/handover-reason field. |
| **9. Worker-load fairness** | Per worker: active minutes, events, cases, concurrent WIP, complexity-weighted work, priority-weighted work, high-WIP exposure, breaks, and handovers. | Compare load distributions across experience groups using medians, percentiles, and Gini or max/min ratios. Do not collapse this into an error-based individual score. |
| **10. Throughput and arrivals** | Arrival, start, completion, and finish rates; cases completed per staffed hour; backlog inflow and outflow. | By shift and hour. Pair with waiting and WIP so apparently higher throughput is not mistaken for a healthier queue. |
| **11. Quality-check backlog and end-shift burst** | Number and age of pending Quality Checks, reviews completed in the final 45 minutes, and corrections arising from those reviews. | By shift, reviewer, activity, complexity, and deferral status. A deferral is not complete until the next-shift review and downstream rework are visible. |
| **12. Bot correction and review rate** | Corrections per 100 bot classifications, human review coverage, reviewer agreement, and correction time. | By bot version, complexity, data completeness, WIP, and queue. Show review coverage so higher correction counts are not mistaken for higher intrinsic bot failure. |
| **13. Case cycle time and incomplete work** | Start-to-close time, oldest active case age, and percentage of cases open beyond SLA. | By priority, complexity, shift, WIP, and handover. This catches cases that look fast at the event level but remain unresolved. |
| **14. Data-quality indicators** | Missing timestamps, invalid durations, duplicate events, missing complexity, unreviewed bot decisions, and unassigned rework. | Display beside operational metrics so apparent improvements caused by missing data or denominator changes are visible. |

The dashboard should have filters for date, shift, activity, resource cohort, sequence position, WIP/queue band, complexity, priority, and case type. It should display both **raw** and **standardized** outcomes side by side, with minimum-denominator rules and uncertainty intervals. Alerts should trigger process responses—such as redistribution, senior support, or workload inspection—not automatic individual sanctions.
