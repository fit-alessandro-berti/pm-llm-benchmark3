# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Fiber Operations Management  
**Subject:** Next decision on Redesign R  
**Recommendation:** Adopt Alternative E: retain and expand the redesign only for **simple, low-risk houses**, immediately suspend two high-risk design elements—**survey skipping for medium-risk/older buildings** and **billing start before successful activation**—and run a controlled, instrumented experiment before any broader rollout.

Redesign R has demonstrated a real operational benefit, but not a universally safe one. For simple low-risk houses, it reduced median duration by six days, reduced p90 duration by eight days, improved first-time-right performance, and lowered complaints. The coordinator model and merged validation/scheduling path therefore appear valuable for a clearly defined segment.

However, the aggregate pilot result is not suitable for national scaling. While median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%, the tail worsened materially: p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days and p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days. Rework and customer harm indicators also worsened: first-time-right fell six percentage points, second visits rose from 11% to 18%, complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%, and billing-before-successful-activation became a recurring conformance deviation.

The process-mining evidence indicates that the redesign has simplified the common path but made exception handling less controlled. Pilot fitness is high (0.94), meaning the discovered model can replay the observed cases well, but precision is low (0.51), indicating a permissive and variable process with many possible combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeated visits, and billing events. This is consistent with the observed tail and complaint patterns rather than evidence of a stable, scalable process.

The practical trade-off is therefore **speed and lower visible operating cost for easy cases versus higher rework, tail delay, customer complaints, and possible billing/compliance exposure for complex cases**. The current pilot also cannot cleanly isolate the redesign effect from case mix: pilot regions have more apartments and older buildings, while risk and customer-segment data are incomplete in early pilot weeks. Cost estimates also omit some downstream complaint handling.

Management should not roll back the coordinator concept wholesale. Fewer handovers and strong results for simple houses indicate that ownership and merged early-stage work can improve flow. But coordinators reached 93% utilization in two regions, and case context was insufficiently shared during absences. Therefore, the scaled version must include workload caps, a shared coverage model, and explicit escalation ownership after failed visits and after billing.

For apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural installations, revert temporarily to guarded controls: require field-survey or validated risk review where appropriate; require permit-check conformance for permit-sensitive buildings; and prohibit billing start until activation has successfully passed or an explicitly governed exception is approved. These controls should remain until targeted evidence shows that the predictive skip rule and activation workflow are safe by segment.

Over the next 8–12 weeks, run a stratified regional experiment, instrument critical timestamps and reason codes, measure full 60-day downstream cost, and monitor conformance exceptions daily. Broader rollout should be contingent on improvement in both central flow and exception outcomes—not merely median cycle time or handovers.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

These metrics answer different questions.

- **High fitness (0.94)** means the discovered pilot model can replay most observed event traces with relatively few deviations.
- **Low precision (0.51)** means the model permits many behaviors that were not actually observed, often because the observed process contains many optional paths, loops, exceptions, and weakly enforced controls.

The pilot can therefore be “well explained” by a flexible model while still being operationally unstable. The combinations of skipped survey, repeated installation, manual activation, billing, complaints, and missing permit checks produce a process that is easy for a permissive model to fit but difficult to govern predictably.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

The redesign improved the standard path, especially for simple houses: merged validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership removed waiting and handover delay. This shifts the **median** downward.

At the same time, complex cases appear to enter more costly exception loops: failed visit, delayed second visit, manual activation, billing disputes, missing permits, and complaint escalation. These less frequent but much longer cases dominate the **p90 and p95**. A faster common path and a worse exception path can occur simultaneously.

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Fewer handovers can reduce queues, duplication, and loss of accountability. That likely explains the large reduction in cases with three or more handovers.

But the working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. When a coordinator is absent or overloaded, context is not sufficiently shared and cases stall. Thus, fewer routine handovers does not automatically mean a more resilient operating system; resilience requires backup ownership, accessible case context, and clear exception escalation.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

The visible cost measure improved because the redesign likely reduced routine coordination, field surveys, and early process time. However, it excludes or incompletely captures later effects such as second visits, manual activation, customer-service calls, billing corrections, complaint handling, credits, and possible churn.

The increase in failed visits, manual activations, second visits, and complaints makes it plausible that savings were shifted downstream rather than eliminated. The estimated inclusive cost already points in that direction, but is incomplete.

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings

Simple, low-risk houses are more likely to fit the assumptions behind the merged workflow and survey-skip logic. Their outcomes support that mechanism.

Apartments and old buildings have more coordination dependencies, access constraints, building-specific infrastructure uncertainty, and permit sensitivity. Skipping surveys or missing permit checks can therefore create failures that only emerge during installation or after billing. The redesign is not necessarily poor in principle; its eligibility and controls appear insufficiently differentiated by risk.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Reasonable when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures speed gains, lower handovers, and visible cost savings quickly; standardizes a simplified model. | Would expand worsened tails, rework, complaints, billing-before-activation deviations, permit-control gaps, and coordinator overload. Pilot mix/data limitations make national impact uncertain. | Only after segmented evidence shows controlled tail, first-time-right, activation, and full-cost outcomes across complex segments. Current evidence does not meet this condition. |
| **B. Roll back to baseline** | Immediately restores established survey, activation, and handover controls; may limit customer and billing risk. | Discards strong gains for simple houses, including faster completion and better quality outcomes; reinstates avoidable handovers and delays. | Reasonable if billing-before-activation cannot be quickly controlled, permit compliance risk is confirmed, or harm persists even after targeted safeguards. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation** | Retains the most promising structural change while removing two likely sources of harm. Easier to operationalize than full R. | Coordinator centralization may still create overload and absence-related stalls; may not preserve all speed gains. It does not by itself establish the optimal survey policy. | Reasonable as an immediate safety-oriented operating policy, provided shared coverage, workload caps, and exception ownership are added. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Best causal evidence; can separate redesign effect from region/building mix and test components. Addresses incomplete segmentation and cost data. | Delays benefits; requires operational discipline and may be difficult if regions resist different procedures. | Reasonable whenever national rollout is not urgent and uncertainty about survey skipping, activation timing, and ownership resilience is decision-critical. |
| **E. Hybrid: R for simple houses; controls for complex cases; measure before wider scale** | Preserves demonstrated benefit for the beneficiary segment while limiting exposure in harmed segments. Supports learning without an all-or-nothing decision. | Requires reliable eligibility criteria; risk score is currently unvalidated and early pilot attributes are incomplete. Can create operational complexity. | Best current option if low-risk-house identification can be made conservative and monitored, while complex cases retain survey, permit, and activation controls. |

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E, implemented with the immediate safeguards of Alternative C and supported by a controlled experiment.**

## Immediate action

1. **Continue and cautiously expand the coordinator-owned, merged validation/scheduling workflow only for conservatively defined simple, low-risk houses.**
2. Set coordinator workload limits and introduce named backup coverage, shared case notes, and escalation ownership for failed visits, manual activations, and post-install issues.
3. Establish daily conformance monitoring for:
   - billing start before successful activation,
   - missing permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings,
   - repeated installation visits without required risk review,
   - complaint escalation after billing.

## What to pause

Pause the following across all segments except explicitly approved test cells:

- Survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old buildings, permit-sensitive addresses, business customers, and rural/complex installations.
- Billing start before successful activation, except under a documented and governed exception process.
- Broad national rollout of the current end-to-end Redesign R.

## What to scale

Scale only the components supported by current evidence:

- Coordinator ownership for low-risk simple houses.
- Combined validation/scheduling for low-risk simple houses.
- Reduced routine handovers, **with** backup ownership and cross-trained exception support.

## What to measure next

Launch an 8–12 week stratified regional study using:

- **M1** randomized survey-skipping tests by validated risk stratum;
- **M6** predictive-rule validation against actual survey outcomes;
- **M7** continuous conformance monitoring for activation and permits;
- **M3** structured exception reason codes;
- **M4** complete 60-day downstream cost measurement;
- **M5** coordinator-versus-shared-team resilience testing;
- **M2** timestamp instrumentation for installation and activation queues;
- **M8** separate customer measures for appointment speed and service-working quality.

## Threshold for broader rollout

Broader rollout should require evidence, by segment and against a comparable control, that the redesigned process:

1. Maintains a meaningful median-time improvement;
2. Does **not** worsen p90 or p95 duration beyond an agreed tolerance;
3. Restores first-time-right to at least baseline performance or demonstrates compensating full-cost and customer benefits;
4. Reduces second visits, manual activation, and 30-day complaints toward baseline;
5. Eliminates or tightly governs billing-before-successful-activation and missing-permit deviations;
6. Shows full 60-day cost no worse than baseline;
7. Keeps coordinator and second-visit utilization below levels that create persistent queues or absence-related stalls.

Until those conditions are met, management should treat R as a **segment-specific process improvement**, not a nationally proven operating model.
