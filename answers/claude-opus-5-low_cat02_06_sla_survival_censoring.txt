# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin (t = 0).** The `Submit` event of each case. Every case in the extract begins with `Submit`, so the origin is uniform and no left truncation is needed for the internal process (a case that had been submitted before the extract window opened and is not in the table would be a left-truncation problem; I assume — **Assumption A1** — that the extract is a complete cohort of cases submitted inside the window, because otherwise no unbiased risk set can be reconstructed from the supplied fields).

**Time scale.** Elapsed calendar hours (`duration_or_age_h`), not working hours. This matters: the SLA is stated as "72-hour elapsed-time", so calendar time is the contractually correct scale, but hazards will be non-stationary (nights/weekends) in a way we cannot model without absolute timestamps.

**Horizon.** τ = 72 h, fixed and administrative, identical for all cases.

**Primary event of interest.** `Resolve` (operational success).

**Competing events.** `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer` — these are terminal *for the internal observation process* and preclude a later internal `Resolve`. They are **not** censoring in the classical sense: after `Withdraw` the case cannot resolve, so the counterfactual "resolution time had it not withdrawn" is ill-defined or at least not the operational quantity of interest.

**Censoring.** Only `open` rows are censored, at t = `duration_or_age_h` (age at the frozen observation cutoff). This is administrative right censoring *in principle*; §3 and §6 argue it is plausibly **informative** here.

**Crucial asymmetry: censoring above vs below the horizon.** An `open` case whose age already exceeds 72 h is **not** censored with respect to the estimand "resolved by 72 h" — its status at τ is fully observed (it was not resolved by 72 h; it was still active). Censoring only destroys information about the τ-horizon estimand when the censoring time is **< 72 h**.

- Open with age ≥ 72 h: **S09 (92), S11 (81), S25 (79), S26 (104)** → observed non-resolution at τ, and observed "still active at τ".
- Open with age < 72 h: **S10 (47), S15 (35), S24 (67), S29 (51), S31 (23)** → genuinely censored for the τ question. Only these 5 of 32 cases require extrapolation.

## Estimands

**E1 — All-cause SLA breach: P(not resolved by 72 h).**
E1 = 1 − CIF_Resolve(72), where CIF_Resolve is the cause-specific cumulative incidence of `Resolve` under competing risks (Aalen–Johansen). This is the *contractual* estimand: a case that withdrew, was rejected, or was transferred and never came back with a `Resolve` stamp did not deliver the SLA outcome. Pooled estimate from the data (calculation in §8, transfers = competing event): **CIF_Resolve(72) ≈ 0.304 ⇒ E1 ≈ 0.70**.

**E2 — Cumulative incidence of *late* resolution: CIF_Resolve(∞) − CIF_Resolve(72).**
The mass of cases that do resolve, but after the horizon. This isolates "slow but successful" from "never successful", and is the estimand for capacity/queue interventions rather than for contract exposure.

**E3 — Probability of no terminal disposition at 72 h: S_any(72).**
Overall event-free survival treating *any* terminal outcome (Resolve/Withdraw/Reject/Transfer) as the event. Pooled: **S_any(72) ≈ 0.38**. This is the *work-in-progress* estimand — it counts cases that will still be sitting in someone's queue when the clock expires.

**E4 — Cause-specific risks.** CIF_Withdraw(72), CIF_Reject(72), CIF_Transfer(72), each estimated under the competing-risk structure. Pooled non-resolution mass at 72 h decomposes roughly as ≈ 0.38 still active + ≈ 0.32 absorbed by Withdraw/Reject/Transfer.

**E5 (secondary, continuous) — Restricted mean time to resolution / restricted mean time lost**, RMT over [0, 72] or [0, 120], which summarises the whole curve rather than a single point and is stable under the small-n instability of a point estimate at exactly τ.

## Which estimand serves which decision

| Decision | Estimand | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Contractual/regulatory SLA reporting, penalty exposure | **E1** | Counts every case that failed to reach `Resolve` in time, regardless of *why*. |
| Staffing / queue sizing / "how much work spills past the deadline" | **E3** then **E2** | E3 is the expected open inventory at τ; E2 is the tail of eventual completions to plan for. |
| Process redesign, root-cause work ("are we losing cases to withdrawal or to slowness?") | **E4** | Separates demand-side attrition from throughput failure. |
| Early-warning alerts on live cases | conditional E1 given the observed prefix and elapsed time (landmark; §4, §7) | Alerting needs P(breach \| survived to landmark, current state). |
| Comparing cohort A vs B, or priorities | **E5 / RMT** and cause-specific hazards | Less sensitive to the exact τ crossing with n = 32. |

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Primary (reference) coding: **transfers, withdrawals and rejections are competing events**; only `open` is censoring.

| observed_status | Cases | Event indicator δ_Resolve | Competing-risk code (cause) | Time used | Censoring rule (reference) | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | S01–S04, S07, S13, S16–S20, S27, S28 | 1 | cause = 1 (Resolve) | duration to Resolve | never censored | none needed; optionally split "resolved ≤ 72" vs "> 72" for descriptive reporting |
| `withdrawn` | S06, S12, S22, S30 | 0 | cause = 2 (Withdraw) | duration to Withdraw | not censored (absorbing) | (a) treat as independent censoring at that time (KM-style; assumes a withdrawn case would have resolved like a still-active peer); (b) treat as **breach** (customer-visible failure); (c) treat as **out of SLA scope** — exclude from denominator entirely, which changes the population, not just the estimator |
| `rejected` | S05, S14, S23 | 0 | cause = 3 (Reject) | duration to Reject | not censored (absorbing) | (a) treat as a *legitimate terminal disposition* and therefore SLA-compliant if within 72 h (a defensible business reading: the case was "handled"); (b) censoring; (c) breach |
| `transferred` | S08 (68), S21 (26), S32 (96) | 0 | cause = 4 (Transfer) | duration to Transfer | not censored (absorbing) | see three explicit assumptions below |
| `open` | S09, S10, S11, S15, S24, S25, S26, S29, S31 | 0 | cause = 0 | age at cutoff | **right-censored** at age | (a) assume independent administrative censoring; (b) informative-censoring stress test tied to `last_event_gap_h` (§6); (c) for τ-horizon estimands, cases with age ≥ 72 need no assumption at all |

## Transfers under three explicit assumptions

Transfer is the hardest object in this dataset: the internal system stops observing, so the case is neither known-failed nor known-succeeded, and — critically — the *reason* for transfer is almost certainly correlated with difficulty (all three transferred cases are P1 and escalated: S08, S21, S32).

- **T1 — Transfer as a competing event (reference).** The case can no longer produce an internal `Resolve`; CIF_Resolve conditions on the internal process only. Interpretation: "probability the *internal organisation* delivers resolution within 72 h." Honest about what is observed; conservative about credit. This is my primary coding.
- **T2 — Transfer as independent right censoring.** Assumes a transferred case's residual resolution hazard equals that of a comparable still-active internal case. Given that all transfers are P1+escalated — the slowest, hardest stratum — this assumption is almost certainly **optimistically biased**: it imputes the resolution behaviour of easier cases onto harder ones. Use only as a bound, and preferably stratified/adjusted so the imputation borrows from P1-escalated peers rather than from the pooled cohort.
- **T3 — Transfer as an SLA breach (worst case).** Transfer = the internal SLA outcome was not delivered; count as non-resolution at the transfer time (an immediate failure). This is the correct coding if the SLA is owned by the internal organisation regardless of downstream handoff. Note S32 transfers at 96 h — under T3 it is a breach anyway (it had already passed 72 h unresolved), so only S08 and S21 differ materially between T1 and T3 at τ. In fact **T1 and T3 give identical E1 at τ = 72 only if the estimator's redistribution is nil**; they differ because T1 removes S21/S08 from the risk set (which slightly reduces the resolution CIF's denominators later) whereas T3 keeps them as permanent non-resolutions.
- **T4 (bonus, partial-information) — probabilistic imputation.** If the external organisation's historical resolution-within-remaining-time rate π were known, credit transfers with weight π. With π unknown, sweep π ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} as a tipping-point analysis (§6).

Because n = 32 and only 3 cases are transferred, the transfer coding moves E1 by a few percentage points, not by an order of magnitude — but it moves the **P1 stratum** substantially, since 3 of the 6 P1 cases (S08, S21, S32; the others are S04, S11, S18, S25, S29 — 8 P1 cases in total) are transfers or long-open. That is where the assumption must be stated loudly.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**The naive analysis.** Take only the 13 `resolved` cases and compute the fraction with duration ≤ 72 h: A resolves S01 (18), S02 (41), S04 (29), S07 (54) within, S03 (76) and S13 (110) late → 4/6; B resolves S16 (16), S17 (32), S18 (12), S19 (58), S27 (39) within, S20 (74) and S28 (88) late → 5/7. Pooled naive breach rate = **4/13 ≈ 31 %**. The competing-risk estimate is **E1 ≈ 70 %**. The naive number understates breach risk by more than a factor of two.

**(a) Survivor / completion bias.** Restricting to `resolved` conditions on the outcome. Every slow-but-still-running case is deleted precisely because it is slow: **S26 (open at 104 h), S09 (92), S11 (81), S25 (79)** are all guaranteed breaches and all invisible to the naive calculation. Conversely, fast cases are over-represented because they have had time to finish. This is length-biased sampling induced by the cutoff.

**(b) Competing risks silently dropped.** **S06 (withdrawn 27 h), S12 (15), S22 (44), S30 (70), S05 (rejected 9 h), S14 (63), S23 (11)** all vanish from the naive denominator. Under E1 they are non-resolutions; deleting them removes 7 non-resolutions from a 32-case denominator. Worse, a KM analysis that "censors" them instead assumes a case withdrawn at 15 h (S12) would otherwise have resolved on the same schedule as still-active peers — which is exactly the assumption withdrawal violates, since withdrawal is usually driven by the same friction that causes delay.

**(c) Informative censoring.** The `open` cases are not censored by a mechanism independent of the outcome. **S26** has `last_event_gap_h = 72` (no activity for 72 h) and **S11** has 55, **S25** 48 — these are stalled, not merely young. Meanwhile **S31 (gap 3, age 23)**, **S15 (gap 2, age 35)**, **S24 (gap 4)** and **S29 (gap 6)** are actively progressing. Treating all nine open cases as exchangeable "administrative censoring" ignores that the censoring *time* and the residual hazard are related through the same latent variable (case difficulty / abandonment). Formally, independent censoring requires C ⟂ T; here `last_event_gap_h` is a measured proxy for the dependence, which is fortunate — it lets us condition on it or stress-test it (§6) rather than merely worry.

**(d) Transfer-induced informative loss.** All three transfers (**S08, S21, S32**) are P1 + escalated. If transfer is treated as neutral censoring (T2), the estimator replaces those hard cases with the average residual behaviour of the risk set, systematically overstating internal resolution probability, most severely in the P1 stratum.

**(e) Cohort imbalance and differential follow-up.** Cohort B (17 cases) started later than A (15), so B's cases are on average less mature at the cutoff — yet B's *open* ages are not uniformly smaller (S26 = 104 h). Because assignment was non-random and rollout compliance incomplete, cohort B also contains a mixture of policy-exposed and policy-unexposed cases: **S20, S23, S26, S30** show prefixes with no `AutoCheck` at all, i.e. apparent non-compliance with the new screening policy, while the rest of B begins `Submit>AutoCheck>...`. A naive A-vs-B comparison is therefore an intention-to-treat-like contrast diluted by non-compliance, not a per-protocol effect, and it is additionally confounded by follow-up maturity. Any per-protocol split on `AutoCheck` presence is itself post-baseline conditioning (§5).

**(f) Open below 72 h vs open above 72 h — the key operational distinction.**
- **Above τ (S09 92, S11 81, S25 79, S26 104): already-observed breaches.** No statistical machinery is required to know these failed the SLA; the only unknown is *when* (or whether) they will eventually finish, which matters for E2, not E1. Excluding them (naive) or "censoring them away" (careless KM) discards hard, negative information.
- **Below τ (S10 47, S15 35, S24 67, S29 51, S31 23): genuinely censored.** Their τ-status is unknown and must be estimated from the hazard of comparable cases. These five are the only true extrapolation in E1 — which is why E1 is fairly robust here (27 of 32 cases have a directly observed τ-status) and why quoting a wide interval is honest but the point estimate is not fragile.

The correct framing: naive completed-case analysis answers "among cases that finished, how fast were they?", which is a *conditional-on-the-future* question. The SLA question is "among cases that start, what fraction reach `Resolve` within 72 h?", which requires the full at-risk cohort.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Kaplan–Meier (cause-specific, censoring competing events).** Estimates the "net" resolution probability in a hypothetical world without withdrawal/rejection/transfer. With 7 competing terminal events plus 3 transfers out of 32 cases (~31 % of the cohort), 1 − KM materially **overstates** resolution incidence — the classic upward bias. Useful only as (i) an all-cause curve for E3 (where every terminal outcome is the event and only `open` is censored — there KM is exactly right), and (ii) a bound in sensitivity analysis. **Not** the primary estimator for E1.

**Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence function.** Estimates CIF_Resolve(t) and CIF_Withdraw/Reject/Transfer(t) consistently in the presence of competing risks, using the correct risk set (open cases stay at risk until their cutoff age). This directly delivers E1, E2 and E4 and is the natural primary estimator here. It requires only independent *censoring* (of `open` rows), not independence between competing causes.

**Cause-specific hazards (Cox, one model per cause).** Right tool for *aetiology*: "does escalation raise the rate of resolution among those still at risk?" With 13 resolutions, the rule-of-thumb 10 events per covariate allows at most **one** covariate; realistically this is a descriptive, one-covariate-at-a-time exercise (cohort, or priority, or stage), never a multivariable model.

**Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray).** Right tool for *prediction/risk communication*, because its coefficients map monotonically onto the CIF a manager is quoted. But the subdistribution risk set keeps competing-event cases in the risk set with decaying weights, which is conceptually awkward for `transferred` cases (are they really "still at risk of internal resolution"?). With 13 events and 32 cases, Fine–Gray here is illustrative only.

**Restricted mean time (RMTL / RMST).** RMST for resolution truncated at 72 h, or restricted mean time *lost* to each competing cause, gives a single interpretable number ("expected hours of the 72 delivered as resolution") without relying on the tail, and is far more stable at n = 32 than a point estimate at τ. Strong candidate for the cohort comparison.

**Landmarking.** Essential for anything involving `Escalate`, `Rework`, or repeated `Review`, all of which occur *after* t = 0. Pick landmarks (e.g. 24 h and 48 h), restrict to cases still event-free and uncensored at the landmark, define covariates from the prefix **observed up to the landmark only**, and model residual time to 72 h. This is the cleanest defence against immortal-time bias (§5) and maps directly onto the operational alert use case ("at 24 h into the clock, what is this case's breach risk?").

**Multi-state model.** The richest representation: states Submitted → Screened/AutoChecked → Under Review → Escalated → {Resolved, Withdrawn, Rejected, Transferred}, with `Rework` and repeated `Review` as back-transitions. Transition-specific hazards would tell us *where* time is lost. The blocker is data: we have no per-event timestamps, only total duration and `last_event_gap_h`, so transition times are unidentified. A multi-state model is the **target design**, achievable only after the timestamp gap in §9 is closed.

## Recommendation

- **Primary: Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence for E1/E2/E4, stratified by cohort and by priority, with `transferred` coded as a competing event (T1), plus the all-cause KM curve for E3.** Justification specific to these data: 31 % of cases end in a non-resolution terminal state, so competing risks are the dominant structural feature; only 5 of 32 cases are censored below the horizon, so the estimator leans on very little extrapolation; and stratification (rather than regression) is the only defensible adjustment at n = 32.
- **Secondary: landmark analysis at 24 h and 48 h with prefix-derived, landmark-frozen covariates, summarised by RMST over [0, 72] and reported alongside the T1/T2/T3 transfer sweep.** Justification: it is the only design that lets `Escalate`/`Rework`/`Review`-repetition enter without immortal-time bias, and RMST is more stable than a τ-point estimate here.
- Explicitly **not recommended as primary**: multivariable Cox or Fine–Gray (event-poor), any completed-case comparison, and 1 − KM as a breach probability.

**Illustrative results (computed in §8, T1 coding).**

| Stratum | n | ĈIF_Resolve(72) | Ê1 = P(not resolved by 72) | Ŝ_any(72) = E3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pooled | 32 | 0.304 | 0.696 | 0.380 |
| Cohort A | 15 | 0.292 | 0.708 | 0.339 |
| Cohort B | 17 | 0.316 | 0.684 | 0.416 |

The A-vs-B difference (~2.4 percentage points in resolution incidence) is far inside sampling noise for n = 15 vs 17 with 6 and 7 resolutions; a bootstrap CI would comfortably span zero and both signs. **No policy effect is demonstrable in these data.** Note also that B has a *higher* estimated E3 (more still-active cases at τ), which is consistent with B's shorter follow-up maturity rather than with a genuine slowdown.

## Immortal time and leakage — concretely

Comparing "escalated" (S03, S04, S08, S10, S11, S13, S18, S21, S25, S28, S29, S32) with "not escalated" as if `escalated` were a baseline attribute creates **immortal time**: to be classified as escalated, a case must have survived, un-terminated, long enough for `Escalate` to occur. S13 (110 h, escalated at step 6) contributed a long stretch of *guaranteed-non-escalated* person-time that the naive coding attributes to the escalated group. The result is a spurious survival advantage for escalation, or — because escalation is also assigned to hard cases — an uninterpretable mixture of confounding by indication and immortal time pulling in opposite directions.

Correct handling, in order of preference:
1. **Time-varying covariate**: `escalated(t) = 1{t ≥ T_escalate}` in a cause-specific Cox model. Requires the escalation timestamp — **not available**, so this is a design recommendation, not something executable on this extract.
2. **Landmarking**: at landmark L, classify cases by whether escalation occurred before L; analyse only residual time after L. Executable in spirit here, but again needs event times to know whether escalation preceded L. With only prefix order, we can implement a *degenerate* landmark using `duration_or_age_h > L` as the eligibility rule and prefix order as an ordinal proxy — and must label it as such.
3. **Multi-state model** with `Escalated` as an intermediate state — the principled answer.

The same argument applies to `Rework` and to repeated `Review`. `prefix_length` and `last_event_gap_h` are the strongest leakage risks in a naive predictive model because both are mechanically driven by elapsed time, and elapsed time is the outcome.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

All comparisons keep the same time origin, horizon, and risk-set construction; only the coding of non-resolution outcomes and of censoring changes.

**S-A — Transfer coding sweep (T1 vs T2 vs T3).**
- T1 (competing event, reference): CIF_Resolve(72) ≈ 0.304.
- T2 (independent censoring at transfer time): removes S21 (26 h) and S08 (68 h) from the risk set early, so the resolution CIF is redistributed upward — the estimate rises modestly (a few points pooled), and rises much more in the **P1 stratum**, where 3 of 8 cases are transfers. Direction is unambiguous: T2 ≥ T1.
- T3 (transfer = breach): S08 and S21 remain permanent non-resolutions; T3 ≤ T1 at the horizon. S32 (96 h) is unaffected — it had already breached.
- **Reversal risk:** any claim that P1 meets the SLA at a rate comparable to P2/P3 is entirely an artefact of T2. Under T1/T3 the P1 stratum is dominated by transfers and long-open cases (S11 81 h open, S25 79 h open). **This is the single most fragile qualitative conclusion in the analysis.**
- Tipping-point variant (T4): credit transfers with probability π. Report the π at which the P1-vs-P2 ordering flips; with only 3 transfers, that π is likely extreme, which is itself a useful robustness statement.

**S-B — Withdrawal/rejection coding.**
- Reference: competing events (non-resolutions).
- Alt-1: independent censoring → 1 − KM. This deletes 7 cases from the "failure" pool and redistributes their mass onto resolvers; the estimated resolution incidence rises substantially, and E1 could fall from ~0.70 toward roughly ~0.5 or below. This is the most *numerically* consequential switch in the whole analysis, and it is also the least defensible: S12 withdrew at 15 h and S05 was rejected at 9 h — neither was ever going to resolve.
- Alt-2: rejections count as *legitimate dispositions within SLA* (a genuine business reading — "the case was adjudicated"). Then S05 (9), S23 (11) and S14 (63) become SLA successes, and E1 drops by roughly 3/32 ≈ 9 points. **Reversal risk:** whether the process "meets" a 30 %-breach target depends more on this definitional choice than on any estimator.
- **Recommendation:** report E1 under the strict definition as primary, and a "disposition-based" variant (Resolve or Reject within 72 h counts as handled) as a clearly labelled secondary KPI. Never present Alt-1 as the headline.

**S-C — Informative censoring stress test on `open` cases.**
Reference assumes independent censoring for all 9 open rows. Alternative: assume stalled cases (`last_event_gap_h` large) have a *depressed* resolution hazard. Concretely:
- Worst case: treat S26 (gap 72), S11 (55), S25 (48) as never resolving (δ-adjustment / "censoring = failure" for stalled cases). These three are all already past 72 h, so **E1 at τ is unchanged** — an important and reassuring finding.
- The censoring assumption only bites for the five open cases below 72 h (S10, S15, S24, S29, S31), and their gaps are 19, 2, 4, 6, 3 — i.e. four of five are actively progressing, and only S10 (gap 19, age 47, escalated) looks at risk of stalling. Assigning S10 a zero resolution hazard changes E1 by well under 2 points.
- **Conclusion:** informative censoring is a serious threat to **E2** (late-resolution incidence and the tail of the curve) but only a minor threat to **E1** at τ = 72. That asymmetry should be stated explicitly rather than issuing a blanket caveat.
- Formal version if timestamps arrive: inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting with `last_event_gap_h`, priority and current stage in the censoring model, or a sensitivity parameter on the hazard ratio between censored and uncensored cases.

**S-D — Cohort/policy effect specification.**
- ITT-style: A vs B as recorded → CIF_Resolve(72) 0.292 vs 0.316, difference ≈ +0.024 in B's favour, not distinguishable from noise.
- Per-protocol / compliance-adjusted: within B, split on presence of `AutoCheck` (compliant: S16, S18, S19, S21, S22, S24, S25, S27, S28, S29, S31, S32; apparently non-compliant: S20, S23, S26, S30). The non-compliant subgroup contains S20 (74 h, late resolve), S26 (104 h open), S30 (70 h withdrawn) — i.e. the visibly worst B outcomes — which would make the compliant subgroup look markedly better. **This comparison is post-baseline conditioning on a variable plausibly caused by case difficulty; the apparent per-protocol benefit is not a causal effect.**
- Maturity-adjusted: restrict both cohorts to cases with follow-up ≥ 72 h, or compare RMST over a common truncation, to remove differential follow-up.
- **Reversal risk:** "the new screening policy improved SLA performance" is supportable only under the per-protocol split, and is *not* supportable under ITT. Given non-random assignment and incomplete rollout, the honest conclusion is **no evidence of an effect at this sample size**, with the direction of the point estimate (slightly favouring B) noted as suggestive at best.

**Cross-cutting fragility ranking.** (1) Withdrawal/rejection coding — largest numerical swing; (2) transfer coding — largest swing *within the P1 stratum*; (3) cohort specification — determines the headline policy claim; (4) informative censoring — matters for the tail, not for E1.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**From curves to alerts.** Deploy the landmark model, not the marginal curve. At each landmark (e.g. 12, 24, 48 h of case age), compute for each live case the conditional breach probability P(no Resolve by 72 h | still active at L, state at L, priority, entry pattern, inactivity gap). Convert to three bands with thresholds set from the decision economics (cost of an unnecessary expedite vs cost of a breach), not from arbitrary cut-points: **watch / expedite / escalate-to-management**. Cases like S31 (age 23, gap 3, still at `Screen`) should be scored on *residual* risk; cases like S25 (age 79) need no model — they are already breached and belong in a recovery queue, not an alert queue.

**Workload actions.** E3 = S_any(72) ≈ 0.38 is a direct capacity statement: roughly 38 % of a submission cohort is still open when the clock expires, so the recovery/overflow queue must be sized to about 0.38 × arrival rate, and E2 tells us how much of that will eventually clear internally versus persist. The `Escalate → Review → …` loop and the `Rework` loop are where the tail lives (S13 = 110 h with rework + escalation + double review), so the operational lever is loop reduction, not simply "work faster".

**SLA governance.** Report the estimand explicitly with every number: "E1 (strict, transfers as competing events): 70 % of submissions did not reach `Resolve` within 72 h; of that, ~38 pp were still open and ~32 pp had been withdrawn/rejected/transferred." Publish the transfer and withdrawal coding alongside the figure and keep it fixed across reporting periods — most apparent quarter-on-quarter "improvements" in SLA metrics of this kind are coding changes. Governance should also track a *disposition-based* secondary KPI so that operations are not penalised for legitimate rejections, provided both are published.

**Do not present risk scores as causal effects.** Escalation is associated with long durations (S03 76, S13 110, S11 81 open, S25 79 open) — but escalation is *assigned to hard cases*, and using it as a baseline covariate also carries immortal-time bias. A dashboard tile saying "escalation increases breach risk by X%" would be wrong twice over. Correct phrasing: "cases that have been escalated and are still open at 48 h have historically had a higher residual breach rate" — a prognostic, landmark-conditional, non-causal statement. Any claim that the cohort-B policy *caused* an improvement requires the assignment mechanism and rollout-compliance data listed in §9; from this extract, the honest statement is "no detectable difference".

**Calibration checks.** (i) Time-dependent calibration curves at each landmark and horizon: bin predicted breach probability into deciles and compare with the Aalen–Johansen-estimated observed incidence within bin (competing-risk-aware, so open cases are retained). (ii) Brier score / integrated Brier score with inverse-probability-of-censoring weights. (iii) Time-dependent AUC for discrimination, reported separately from calibration — a well-discriminating but miscalibrated score is dangerous for threshold-based alerting. (iv) Recalibrate on a rolling window, since the cohort-B policy change means the hazard is non-stationary in calendar time.

**Fairness / subgroup checks.** Report calibration and error rates **separately by priority (P1/P2/P3) and cohort (A/B)**, and by entry pattern. Specific concerns for these data: P1 cases are disproportionately transferred (S08, S21, S32) and long-open (S11, S25), so under T1 the model will learn "P1 → low internal resolution incidence" and may then *deprioritise* P1 cases in a triage tool — a feedback loop that would invert the intended priority semantics. Guard with (a) a monotonicity constraint or explicit business rule that P1 never receives less attention than P2/P3 regardless of predicted risk; (b) subgroup calibration slopes reported alongside the headline; (c) monitoring for the degenerate outcome where the alert system optimises the metric by encouraging early rejection or transfer of hard cases — i.e. gaming the competing-risk structure. That last risk is a direct consequence of choosing E1 (which credits only `Resolve`) and must be watched precisely because the estimand is strict.

def encode(df, transfer_rule):
    """transfer_rule in {'competing' (T1), 'censor' (T2), 'breach' (T3)}."""
    d = df.copy()
    d["time"]  = d["duration_or_age_h"]
    d["cause"] = d["observed_status"].map(BASE_CAUSE)
    if transfer_rule == "censor":                       # T2: transfer -> independent censoring
        d.loc[d.observed_status == "transferred", "cause"] = 0
    elif transfer_rule == "breach":                     # T3: transfer -> permanent non-resolution
        # keep as an absorbing non-resolution cause (code 4); identical risk-set handling to T1,
        # but it is additionally counted as a definite breach in the direct-tally estimand below.
        pass
    elif transfer_rule != "competing":
        raise ValueError(transfer_rule)
    return d

# ------------------------------------------- 3. risk sets: KM + Aalen-Johansen, no case dropped
def km_and_aj(d, causes_of_interest=(1,)):
    """
    Returns (S_any, CIF) where
      S_any(t) = P(no terminal disposition by t)        [ESTIMAND E3; any cause = event, open = censored]
      CIF[c](t) = Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence of cause c   [feeds E1 and E2]
    Open cases stay in the risk set until their cutoff age -- never dropped.
    """
    d = d.sort_values("time")
    times = sorted(d.loc[d.cause > 0, "time"].unique())
    n_at_risk = lambda t: int((d.time >= t).sum())
    S, curve, cif = 1.0, [(0.0, 1.0)], {c: [(0.0, 0.0)] for c in causes_of_interest}
    running = {c: 0.0 for c in causes_of_interest}
    for t in times:
        n = n_at_risk(t)
        if n == 0:
            continue
        d_all = int(((d.time == t) & (d.cause > 0)).sum())
        for c in causes_of_interest:                     # increment uses S(t-) -> AJ estimator
            d_c = int(((d.time == t) & (d.cause == c)).sum())
            running[c] += S * d_c / n
            cif[c].append((t, running[c]))
        S *= (1 - d_all / n)                             # all-cause KM (E3)
        curve.append((t, S))
    return curve, cif

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Structural limitations of this extract.**
- **n = 32 with 13 resolutions.** Every stratified figure (especially P1, n = 8, of which 3 are transfers) is descriptive. No regression adjustment is defensible; confidence intervals from a bootstrap would be wide enough to contain both directions of the A–B contrast. All numbers above should be read as point estimates with wide, unstated uncertainty.
- **No event timestamps** — the single most binding constraint. It blocks the multi-state model, time-varying covariates, correct landmarking, and any decomposition of where in the path time is lost.
- **No absolute calendar dates or cutoff date** — prevents adjusting for differential follow-up maturity between cohorts, for calendar-time seasonality, and for working-hours vs elapsed-hours effects.
- **Transfer outcomes unobserved** — makes the primary estimand assumption-dependent in exactly the stratum (P1) where it matters most.
- **`escalated` supplied as a case-level flag** invites the immortal-time error; it should not exist in this form in an analytic table.

**Prioritised data requests.**

1. **Per-event timestamps for every activity in every prefix.** Unlocks: multi-state / transition-hazard models, correct time-varying `Escalate`/`Rework` handling, valid landmarking, waiting-vs-processing decomposition. Highest value by a wide margin; everything else in §4 that is currently "design recommendation" becomes executable.
2. **External outcome and outcome time after `Transfer`.** Collapses T1/T2/T3 into a single estimand and removes the largest interpretive fragility in the P1 stratum. Even a coarse aggregate ("the external org resolves X % within the residual window") would allow a principled T4 weight π instead of a sweep.
3. **Observation cutoff timestamp and per-case submission dates.** Enables correct maturity adjustment, left-truncation checks (is the extract really a complete submission cohort? — Assumption A1), and calendar-time hazard modelling. Without this the A-vs-B comparison cannot be de-confounded from follow-up length.
4. **Policy rollout compliance indicator (intended cohort assignment vs actual exposure).** Currently inferred, unreliably, from the presence of `AutoCheck` in the prefix. A recorded compliance flag turns the per-protocol analysis from post-baseline conditioning into a proper ITT/per-protocol pair, and enables instrumental-variable or CACE estimation of the policy effect.
5. **Assignment mechanism / triage rules** (how priority is set, what triggers escalation, what triggers transfer). Needed to reason about confounding by indication; without it, every association between a process feature and delay is uninterpretable causally.
6. **Time-varying workload and staffing** (queue length, analyst availability, backlog by stage). The most plausible unmeasured confounder of both the cohort contrast and the escalation association, and the lever most operational interventions actually pull.
7. **Case-mix and complexity attributes measured at submission** (case type, claim size, channel, requester segment). These are genuine baseline covariates and would allow adjustment without leakage — currently the only clean baseline variables are `priority` and `cohort`.
8. **SLA clock rules** (business-hours pauses, permitted stop-the-clock events, whether transfer stops the clock contractually). This changes the *definition* of the estimand, not just its estimate, and should be settled before any of the above analysis is published.
9. **A second, larger extract or a longer follow-up window.** Reduces the censoring below τ (currently 5 cases) toward zero and makes E2 — the late-resolution tail — estimable rather than speculative.
