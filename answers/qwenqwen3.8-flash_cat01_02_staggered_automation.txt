## 1. What is observed

Automation availability was introduced at different branch-months: Alpha and Beta from April, Gamma from July, Delta and Epsilon remained unadopted. Availability is not treatment intensity: `automated_fast_path_pct` rose from about 4% pre-adoption to 25-50% among adopters, showing gradual actual use. Branches were not randomly selected; Alpha and Beta were chosen because they had high volume and historically weak cycle times, and Gamma followed a readiness pilot. Contemporaneous changes complicate the panel: a centralized fraud desk began in March, routing sent more cases to Alpha/Beta from April and Gamma from July, FTE counts changed, and a May product change increased high-complexity shares. Outcomes are branch-month aggregates. Median and p90 summarize different parts of the cycle-time distribution. Rework and escalation are percentages of cases, not queue durations. `csat_1to5` is a survey mean with variable response rates and no response counts, so it may reflect who responded as well as who was satisfied. `high_complexity_pct` is recorded at registration and may be affected by routing or risk-class changes. Because the data are aggregated, we cannot see case paths, fraud-desk waits, or manual fallback behavior. These facts describe the setting without attributing any observed change to automation.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: staggered event-study with not-yet-treated controls and intensity dose | `automation=1` by branch cohort; relative time to first available month; `automated_fast_path_pct` as dose | Delta and Epsilon never treated; Gamma before July; Alpha/Beta before April | `high_complexity_pct`, `log(arrivals)`, `fte`, month fixed effects, branch fixed effects, routing campaign indicators if available | dynamic ITT-like effect of availability; descriptive dose-response | conditional parallel trends, no anticipation, no spillovers, stable treatment version, SUTVA | pre-trend plots, placebo pre-period tests, balance on FTE/arrivals/complexity, intensity trends, branch-clustered uncertainty | selection into adoption based on weak cycle times and concurrent operational shocks |
| Alternative 1: matched or synthetic control for Alpha/Beta | binary availability starting April | weighted combination of Delta, Epsilon, and Gamma pre-April | pre-outcomes, arrivals, FTE, high-complexity share | ATT for Alpha/Beta on median and p90 | good pre-fit, no unobserved post-period shocks, controls unaffected by rollout | pre-fit residuals, placebo synthetic controls, leave-one-out donors | only five branches; controls are also affected by fraud desk, product change, and routing |
| Alternative 2: interrupted time series with treatment intensity | `automated_fast_path_pct` continuous; branch-specific trend before and after adoption | own branch pre-period plus Delta/Epsilon calendar controls | case mix, staffing, arrivals, month effects, learning trend | per-protocol or dose-response effect on process outcomes | correct functional form, no unobserved time-varying confounding, intensity not driven by outcome expectations | autocorrelation checks, intensity-vs-outcome scatter, branch-specific slopes | actual use is endogenous; managers may increase intensity when queues are already manageable |

The primary strategy is preferable because the only credible source of variation is staggered timing: Alpha/Beta adopt in April, Gamma in July, and Delta/Epsilon remain untreated. A relative-time event-study can separate pre-trends from post-adoption dynamics and can use not-yet-treated branches as controls, whereas a simple pre/post or two-way fixed-effects regression would impose a common effect and ignore that early adopters differ from late adopters. The design also accommodates the central problem that availability is not use: by reporting intensity alongside the binary availability estimand, we can see whether effects move with `automated_fast_path_pct` rather than merely with the rollout calendar. It is still weak with only five branches, so uncertainty must be clustered at branch level and interpreted as descriptive. The strategy is preferable because it makes the most of the available timing variation while explicitly exposing the assumptions that are likely violated: selection into adoption, fraud-desk spillovers, and simultaneous routing, staffing, and case-mix changes.

Recommendation: do not issue a single X% cycle-time claim or expand indiscriminately. Next rollout should target Delta first, then Epsilon only after Delta’s first two post-implementation months meet guardrails. ## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Automation availability, actual fast-path intensity, arrivals, FTE, high-complexity share, escalation, and cycle-time summaries changed together across staggered branch-months.
- Suggestive: Alpha and Beta showed less deterioration in median and p90 than Delta and Epsilon over the same calendar window, and Gamma showed a sharp post-adoption drop after an elevated immediate pre-period.
- Conditionally causal: If conditional parallel trends, no fraud-desk spillovers, no anticipation, and stable treatment intensity hold, availability may have attenuated median deterioration by roughly 3-6 hours and tail deterioration by roughly 6-7 hours, but these are not precise causal estimates.
- Not supported: A single leadership statement that “automation reduces cycle time by X%,” or any claim that automation improved customer satisfaction, is not supported by the supplied branch-month data.
