# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in a benefits process is multi-dimensional. Equal approval rates are neither necessary nor sufficient for it. The following dimensions should be assessed jointly; each can be measured from the event log, and each can move in a different direction.

**1.1 Outcome parity (raw).** Approval rates range from 78% (A) to 58% (D), a 20-point spread. This is a *descriptive* disparity and, on its own, evidence of nothing causal: the case-mix table shows the groups differ substantially on documented complexity (Group D has 51% high risk band and 49% high prior-case count; Group B has 46% incomplete documentation). Raw parity is still worth reporting, because it is what applicants and oversight bodies experience, but it is the starting point of the analysis, not its conclusion.

**1.2 Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior-case count, region and month, the approval gaps shrink but do not vanish: B −5.5 pp, D −3.1 pp, C −2.2 pp. The residual is the quantity of interest, with two strong caveats. First, the controls may themselves be contaminated (see §5): conditioning on documentation_completeness when language barriers cause incompleteness partly *conditions away* the disparity of interest, so −5.5 pp for Group B may be an under-estimate of the total effect of being a minority-language applicant. Second, residual ≠ discrimination; unmeasured legitimate complexity can also produce it. The honest reading is: a residual gap of a few points persists, direction consistent across all three groups, magnitude uncertain and sign of the bias in the estimator itself arguable.

**1.3 Procedural burden.** This is the dimension where the evidence is strongest, and it is largely independent of outcomes. Adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +9.5 pp for B and +4.2 pp for C; adjusted probability of Supervisor Review is +8.0 pp for B and +5.5 pp for C. These are *conditional on* documented documentation completeness and risk. Being asked for more documents and being escalated to a supervisor more often, at equal recorded complexity, is a real cost borne by the applicant: more effort, more opportunities to drop out, more asymmetry of power. Burden should be measured in applicant-facing actions (document requests, contacts required, number of distinct staff the applicant deals with), not only in days.

**1.4 Timeliness.** Median cycle time: A 8.1d, B 13.7d, C 15.9d, D 12.1d; the 90th percentiles spread further (21.4d vs 38.8d for C), meaning the tail — the applicants waiting longest, typically those most in need — is where inequality concentrates. Adjusted cycle-time differences remain +2.6d (B), +4.1d (C), +0.9d (D). Timeliness in a benefits context is not a mere service-quality metric; delayed subsistence support is a partial denial of the benefit. Notably the group with the *smallest* adjusted delay (D) is the group whose routing is most obviously complexity-driven, while the groups routed through *rights-preserving* steps carry the largest delays — the opposite of what a well-designed system should produce.

**1.5 Consistency of routing.** Fairness requires that like cases be handled alike: the same decision logic, the same triggers for escalation, the same evidentiary standard. Measurable proxies are variant entropy within complexity strata, the rate at which the declarative constraints (e.g. "Fraud Review only after Risk Screening with high band", "Decision Draft preceded by Eligibility Assessment") are satisfied per group, and the variability of routing across regions and resources. Inconsistency that correlates with group membership is a fairness problem even where the average outcome is equal, because it means discretion is being exercised differently.

**1.6 Quality and accessibility of support.** Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review exist to preserve rights. Their *availability* is visible in the log; their *quality* is not. Two signals suggest quality problems: the 3.8d and 4.7d waits after these steps, and the fact that policy explicitly excludes them from service-level targets — a support step with no timeliness commitment is a right that is nominally granted and practically rationed. Accessibility also has an uptake dimension: 57% of Group B needs language support but only 24% of Group B cases traverse V4, raising the question of whether the remaining minority-language applicants were served in their language at all, or simply proceeded without support.

**1.7 Appeal and rework burden.** Appeal rates are 6/13/10/16% and rework 11/22/24/20%. Adjusted appeal probability is +4.4 pp (B) and +5.7 pp (D). Appeals are ambiguous evidence and must not be read one way only: elevated appeals may indicate erroneous or poorly explained first decisions (a fairness failure), or better access to advocacy (a fairness success), or a decision quality that is fine but communicated badly. Rework rates of 22–24% are more clearly a burden signal — repeated loops mean the process failed to get it right the first time for those applicants. V6 (appeal path) has a 30.7d median, so appealing roughly quadruples the time to resolution relative to V1.

**1.8 Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A is served by 52 caseworkers; Group B effectively by 7 bilingual staff plus 3 overloaded regional officers; Group C by 5 specialists, 2 of whom also perform supervisor review. This is a structural inequity in queueing capacity, and it is the most mechanically plausible cause of the timeliness gaps: a small server pool produces long, high-variance waits at any given utilization, which explains the fat 90th percentiles. The overlap of accommodation specialists with supervisor review is separately concerning: it creates a self-review-adjacent dynamic and couples two queues so that congestion in one propagates to the other. Resource fairness also has an independence dimension — whether the same small group of staff both supports and adjudicates the same applicants.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to partition the observed disparity into four buckets: (a) **legitimate routing** — differences fully explained by policy-sanctioned triggers applied consistently; (b) **disparity** — differences that persist at equal documented complexity, whose cause is not yet identified; (c) **plausible discrimination or structural inequity** — disparity localized to discretionary decision points or to capacity allocation that the organization controls; (d) **uncertainty** — quantities the data cannot identify. Every reported number should be tagged with which bucket it can support.

**2.1 Matching and stratification.** Prefer exact or coarsened exact matching (CEM) on the policy-relevant complexity variables over a single regression, because it makes the common-support problem visible instead of extrapolating over it. Strata: risk_score_band × documentation_completeness × channel × household_size_band × prior_case_count band × region × application month (or quarter). Estimate group contrasts within strata and aggregate with stratum weights. Report the share of each minority group's cases that has *no* Group A match: if, say, Group D high-risk/high-prior cases have thin overlap with Group A, then the "adjusted" D contrast is largely model extrapolation and should be reported as such rather than as an estimate. Additionally, run a version that matches on *pre-decision process state only* (steps up to Completeness Check) to avoid conditioning on variables that are themselves downstream of the treatment.

**2.2 Path and variant comparison.** Within each stratum, compare variant distributions across groups (e.g. Jensen–Shannon divergence between group-conditional variant distributions, plus per-variant share differences with confidence intervals). Key questions: conditional on identical complexity attributes, are B and C more likely to enter V2 (Request Docs) and to receive Supervisor Review? Is the *sequence* different, or only the durations? Separate "extra activity" disparities (an added step) from "extra loop" disparities (a repeated step) — the latter indicates process failure, the former may be policy. Also compute the counterfactual variant: for a Group B case, what does the path look like if the Language Support Contact step is removed — is the remainder of the path identical to a matched Group A case, or does it also carry extra document requests and escalations? That decomposition separates the *cost of the support step itself* from *additional differential treatment*.

**2.3 Resource-assignment comparison.** Build the case→resource assignment matrix and measure: number of distinct resources per group (concentration via Herfindahl or effective number of servers), handovers per case, and the caseload/utilization of the resources each group depends on. Then, critically, test whether resource identity mediates the outcome: within the *same* caseworker, is there still a group difference in approval, document requests, or escalation? A disparity that disappears within-resource points at allocation (which staff serve which applicants) rather than at differential judgement; a disparity that persists within-resource points at how individual decision-makers treat cases. Both are actionable, but the remedies differ. Handover-of-work and working-together networks make the B (general → bilingual → general) and C (general → specialist → supervisor) patterns explicit and quantifiable.

**2.4 Waiting-time decomposition.** Decompose total cycle time per case into: (i) service time of each activity, (ii) waiting time before each activity, (iii) time attributable to *extra* activities (support steps, document requests, escalations), and (iv) rework/appeal loops. Then attribute the group-level gap additively: how much of Group C's +4.1 adjusted days is the 4.7d Accommodation Review → Eligibility wait alone? How much of Group B's +2.6d is the 3.8d language-support queue plus the 1.6d wait for bilingual staff at document request? This is the single most informative analysis available, because it converts an abstract "disparity" into a small number of named queues that management can fix. Separate *queueing* delay (waiting for a scarce server) from *applicant-side* delay (waiting for the applicant to return documents) — only the former is the organization's responsibility, and mixing them will overstate institutional fault while hiding the fact that document requests impose applicant-side burden too.

**2.5 Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates.** risk_score_band and documentation_completeness are the two suspect controls. Run the primary analysis in three specifications: (S1) controlling for both; (S2) omitting risk_score_band; (S3) omitting documentation_completeness. Report the range. If the Group B approval gap is −5.5 pp with S1 and materially larger without documentation_completeness, then documentation completeness is mediating rather than confounding, and the total disparity exceeds the adjusted figure. Complement with a formal bounding exercise (e.g. E-value / Rosenbaum-style sensitivity): how strongly would an unmeasured legitimate complexity factor have to be associated with both group and outcome to explain away a −5.5 pp gap? If the required strength is implausibly large relative to the observed covariates, the disparity is robust. Also audit the risk model directly: calibration by group (does a "high" band mean the same realized fraud rate for D as for A?) and, if the score's inputs are available, whether it uses prior contact with the agency as a proxy.

**2.6 Subgroup checks by region and channel.** The notes say backlog occurs in two regions and handover patterns differ there. Estimate the group contrasts region by region and channel by channel. Three findings are distinguishable: (i) disparity is uniform across regions → likely systemic (policy, tooling, or the score); (ii) disparity is concentrated in the two backlogged regions → likely a capacity/queueing phenomenon, fixable by staffing; (iii) disparity is concentrated in the in-person channel → the intake channel itself is the burden mechanism. Also check interaction: Group C in-person versus Group C online, since accommodation needs and channel are plausibly correlated. Guard against multiplicity — pre-register the subgroup list and report intervals, not just point estimates, since region-level cell counts (820 Group C cases spread over regions and strata) will be small.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

**Most important disparities, in rough order of concern:**

**(a) The rights-preserving steps are the slowest queues in the process, and they are unmonitored.** Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment: 3.8d for Group B. Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment: 4.7d for Group C. These are the two largest single waits in the table, roughly 4–6× the Decision Draft → Notification wait, and they are precisely the steps excluded from service-level targets. The *existence* of the step is legitimate and required; the *duration* is a capacity decision, not a legal requirement. V5 (the accommodation path, 31% of Group C) has a 21.6d median, nearly 4× V1's 5.8d. This is the clearest concerning finding: applicants exercising a right pay for it in time, and the organization has no target that would surface the cost. It substantially explains Group C's +4.1 adjusted days.

**(b) Server-pool concentration.** 7 bilingual caseworkers for a group with 57% language-support need, and 5 accommodation specialists (2 shared with supervisor review) for 820 cases, versus 52 caseworkers for Group A. Standard queueing behaviour predicts exactly the observed signature: modestly higher medians but much fatter tails (B 90th pct 34.2d, C 38.8d, vs A 21.4d). This is not justified by case complexity — it is an allocation choice. It is the most tractable cause and probably the dominant one behind the timeliness gaps.

**(c) Adjusted excess of Request Missing Documents and Supervisor Review for Group B (+9.5 pp, +8.0 pp).** These are conditional on recorded documentation_completeness, so they are *not* explained by the raw 46% incompleteness figure. Two readings compete and the data cannot separate them: either documentation_completeness is a coarse band that under-measures the real incompleteness of Group B files (so the control is inadequate and the routing is legitimate), or comparable files from Group B attract more scrutiny and more escalation (differential treatment). The within-resource comparison in §2.3 and finer-grained document data would discriminate between these. Until then this is a genuine, unresolved disparity, and it is the one most suggestive of a fairness problem in discretion rather than in capacity.

**(d) Handover chains.** Group B: general → bilingual → general; Group C: general → accommodation specialist → supervisor. Each handover adds queueing delay, risks information loss, and increases the number of staff an applicant must deal with. Some handover is inherent to specialization and therefore legitimate; the *round-trip* pattern for Group B (out to a bilingual worker and back) suggests the language capability is bolted on rather than embedded, which is a design defect rather than a necessity. The C pattern's terminus in supervisor review, performed by people who are themselves accommodation specialists, raises an independence question.

**(e) Appeal and rework loads.** B 22% and C 24% rework, D 16% appeals with +5.7 pp adjusted. V6's 30.7d median means the appeal route is very costly to applicants. Elevated appeals for D are consistent with the fraud-review path (V3, 28% of D) producing more contested denials; whether those denials are wrong, or merely correctly adverse and poorly explained, cannot be determined from this log.

**Plausibly justified by case complexity:** Group D's routing into Risk Screening → Fraud Review (V3) given 51% high risk band, *conditional on the risk score being valid* — and its adjusted cycle-time penalty is small (+0.9d), which is what one expects when routing is complexity-driven and adequately resourced. The *existence* of V4 and V5 for B and C. The longer Submit → Identity Verification wait for in-person applications, which is a channel effect, though the availability of only slower channels to some groups is itself worth examining.

**Remaining concerning:** the magnitude of the support-step waits; the exclusion of those steps from SLAs; the adjusted excess of document requests and supervisor review for B; the concentration of staff; the specialist/supervisor role overlap; the tail (90th percentile) inequality; and the possibility that risk_score_band launders historical disparity into an apparently legitimate routing trigger for Group D.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**M1 — Capacity rationing of support steps (structural, concerning but not intentional).** Language and accommodation support are provided by very small teams with no SLA. Utilization is high, queues form, and the delay falls entirely on the groups that need those steps. No individual acts unfairly; the aggregate effect is that exercising a right costs time. Predicts: waits concentrated at exactly the support→next-step transitions; strong day-of-week/backlog seasonality; delays worst in the two backlogged regions. Largely consistent with the data.

**M2 — Communication friction generating documentation churn (mixed).** Minority-language applicants receive instructions and document requests that are harder to act on correctly, so submissions are more often judged incomplete, triggering Request Missing Documents and repeat loops. Predicts: high rework for B, elevated Request Docs even at equal recorded completeness (because the recorded band does not capture *why* the file is incomplete), and improvement if forms and notices are translated. Consistent with the +9.5 pp figure. Legitimate in the sense that the step is warranted given the file; concerning in that the agency's own communications created the file's state.

**M3 — Differential scrutiny / discretion (concerning).** Caseworkers, facing an unfamiliar language, an accommodation request, or a high prior-case count, escalate to supervisor review more readily at equal evidence. Predicts: +8.0 pp supervisor review for B persisting *within* resource and within complexity stratum, variation across individual caseworkers, and no corresponding increase in the rate at which supervisor review overturns anything. The last test is decisive and cheap: if escalations for B rarely change the decision, the escalation is friction, not diligence.

**M4 — Historical bias encoded in risk_score_band (concerning, partly invisible).** If the score is trained on or built from prior agency contact, prior investigations, or neighbourhood features, then Group D's 51% high-risk share partly reflects past disparate treatment rather than present risk. The routing to Fraud Review then looks legitimate on the face of the log while reproducing the earlier disparity, and — worse — controlling for risk_score_band in the adjusted model *hides* the effect. Predicts: miscalibration by group (high-band D cases confirming fraud at a lower rate than high-band A cases). Testable only if fraud-review outcomes are recorded.

**M5 — Genuine complexity differences (legitimate).** Some of the gap simply reflects that the cases differ: larger households, more incomplete files, more prior interactions, genuinely more risk. This mechanism is real and is why the adjusted gaps are much smaller than the raw ones. Group D's near-zero adjusted cycle-time penalty is the best evidence that at least one group's raw disparity is mostly compositional.

**M6 — Channel effects (mixed).** In-person applicants (38% of B) face fixed office hours, travel, and manual data entry, adding delay at intake and possibly transcription errors downstream. Legitimate in that in-person handling really is slower; concerning if the in-person channel is the only realistic option for some applicants and is under-resourced relative to the online one.

**M7 — Queue coupling through shared staff (structural).** Two of five accommodation specialists also do supervisor review, so a supervisor-review backlog delays accommodation cases and vice versa. Predicts correlated delays across the two steps for Group C specifically, and the observed C pattern of both a 4.7d accommodation wait and a 1.6d supervisor wait.

**M8 — Feedback loop via appeals and reopens (concerning).** Poorly explained or rushed decisions produce appeals and reopens, which consume the same scarce staff, which lengthens queues, which increases rushing. This self-reinforcing dynamic would explain why the same groups appear at the top of both the delay and the rework columns.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias in the controls.** documentation_completeness is recorded by the same staff whose behaviour is under examination, and is plausibly caused by the language barrier the analysis wants to measure. It is therefore simultaneously a confounder (real files really are incomplete) and a mediator/collider (assessed incompleteness partly reflects communication failure and possibly assessor judgement). Conditioning on it biases the Group B estimate toward zero; omitting it biases the other way. No specification is correct; the honest output is a range, not a point.

**Historical bias in risk_score_band.** The band is treated as an exogenous complexity measure by the adjusted model, but its provenance is unknown. If it is built from prior agency contact — and Group D's 49% high prior-case-count share is suggestive — it partly re-expresses the group. Controlling for it then removes exactly the disparity of interest and makes routing to Fraud Review appear justified by construction. Without the score's inputs, its training data, and validation outcomes by group, calibration cannot be checked and the neutrality of the routing trigger cannot be established.

**Missing applicant needs and context.** The log has no measure of the applicant's actual circumstances: literacy, digital access, work schedule, caring responsibilities, health, transport, or urgency of need. Two applicants with identical recorded attributes may face very different burdens from the same document request. Nor is unmet need visible: applicants who needed language support but did not receive it (the gap between B's 57% need and 24% V4 traversal), or who abandoned the process before Decision, are invisible or under-counted. Selection out of the process is itself a fairness outcome and it is missing.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity, so intra-day queueing, concurrent caseloads, absences, part-time fractions, and true utilization cannot be reconstructed. Distinguishing "this delay is unavoidable given staffing" from "this delay reflects deprioritization of these cases" therefore rests on assumption rather than measurement. Nor is staff skill, tenure, or training visible, so within-resource comparisons cannot separate individual bias from individual inexperience.

**Appeal quality and decision quality.** appeal_flag is binary. Appeal *outcomes* are absent, so the most diagnostic question — are appeals from Group B and D being upheld, meaning the original decisions were wrong? — cannot be answered. Nor is the reason for appeal, the presence of representation, or whether the applicant understood the decision. An appeal rate is thus an ambiguous signal that can be read as evidence of error, of access to advocacy, or of poor communication, and this log cannot arbitrate between them.

**Dignity, explanation, and interaction quality.** The scenario notes this explicitly and it deserves emphasis: procedural fairness in its classic sense includes voice, respectful treatment, comprehensible reasons, and a genuine opportunity to be heard. None of this is in an event log. A process could be perfectly equal on every timestamp-derived metric while being experienced as demeaning by one group. Any conclusion drawn here covers only the mechanical, observable layer of procedural fairness.

**Statistical limitations.** Group C has 820 cases; once stratified by risk band × completeness × channel × region × month, many cells will be empty or near-empty, so region- and channel-level subgroup estimates will be imprecise and vulnerable to multiplicity. Common support between Group D's high-risk/high-prior cases and Group A may be thin. And the whole design is observational: no quantity here is a causal effect without untestable assumptions.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**6.1 Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.** Recruit or train more bilingual staff and accommodation specialists; consider on-demand professional interpretation to decouple language support from a fixed 7-person pool. *Expected effect:* directly attacks the 3.8d and 4.7d waits and the fat tails, which is where most of the C and B timeliness gap sits. *Side effects:* recruitment lead time is months; interpretation services may reduce continuity and rapport relative to a bilingual caseworker who owns the case; hiring into a specialist silo can entrench the silo rather than mainstreaming the capability; budget is finite, so capacity moved here comes from somewhere else — the trade-off should be made explicitly rather than by default. Avoid the failure mode of adding capacity while leaving routing unchanged, which just moves the queue.

**6.2 Service-level targets for support steps.** Extend SLAs to cover Language Support Contact → next activity and Accommodation Review → next activity, and — importantly — report cycle time *inclusive* of support steps rather than excluding those cases from headline figures. *Side effects:* targets create gaming pressure. Staff may perform the support step perfunctorily to stop the clock, converting a timeliness problem into a quality problem that the log cannot detect. Mitigate by pairing every timeliness target with a quality measure (interpreter-assisted contact actually completed, applicant confirmation, accommodation actually granted) and by monitoring the tail (90th percentile) rather than only the median, since median-focused targets encourage abandoning the hardest cases.

**6.3 Routing redesign to reduce handovers.** For Group B, embed language capability in the primary caseworker assignment so the case does not travel general → bilingual → general; assign a bilingual owner at intake where language preference is known. For Group C, separate accommodation specialists from supervisor review to break queue coupling and preserve reviewer independence. *Side effects:* early routing on language_preference or accommodation_requested means the process explicitly branches on a protected-adjacent characteristic — defensible as reasonable accommodation, but it must be documented as such and monitored so the branch does not become a slower, lower-status track. Specialist assignment can also reduce the specialists' exposure to standard cases and narrow their calibration. Reducing handovers may concentrate a whole case on one person, removing the second pair of eyes that sometimes catches errors.

**6.4 Decision-explanation improvements.** Standardized, plain-language, translated decision notices stating the specific evidence relied on, the rule applied, and the appeal route; the same for every Request Missing Documents notice, listing exactly which document and why. *Side effects:* better explanations may *raise* the appeal rate initially by making appeal rights comprehensible — which is a fairness improvement that will look like a regression on a dashboard, so commit to that interpretation before measuring. Templates risk boilerplate that is formally compliant and substantively uninformative. Drafting time per decision increases, adding to the same queues that mitigation 6.1 is trying to relieve.

**6.5 Monitoring adjusted disparities.** Stand up a recurring report of adjusted group differences in approval, cycle time, document requests, supervisor review, appeals and rework, with confidence intervals, stratified by region and channel, and with the S1/S2/S3 sensitivity variants shown side by side. *Side effects:* monitoring can create false reassurance — an adjusted gap that is "not significant" in a 820-case group may simply be underpowered, so report intervals and power, not verdicts. There is also a real risk that the adjusted number becomes the official truth and the raw disparity (which is what applicants experience) drops out of view; report both, always. Guard against optimizing to the metric at the expense of unmeasured dimensions.

**6.6 Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness.** For the risk band: obtain its inputs, test calibration by group against realized fraud-review findings, and remove or reweight features that proxy for group membership or prior agency contact. For documentation completeness: audit a sample of files rated "incomplete" across groups to check whether the same objective file state is rated the same way, and test whether incompleteness falls when materials are translated. *Side effects:* the audit may find the score is biased, which invalidates the routing rule currently in production and forces an uncomfortable interim state; recalibration typically means more fraud reviews for some other group, with its own fairness implications. Auditors need access to data the agency may be reluctant to expose. And a well-calibrated score can still be unfair if the underlying label (past detected fraud) is itself the product of biased detection — calibration is a necessary, not sufficient, check.

**6.7 (Cross-cutting) Do not remove or shorten support steps to improve cycle time.** The obvious "fix" for Group C's 21.6d V5 median is to skip Accommodation Review; this would improve every dashboard and destroy the right the step exists to protect. Explicitly rule it out, and structure targets so the step's cost is charged to the organization's capacity budget rather than to the applicant's waiting time.

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

```python
import pandas as pd, pm4py

df = pd.read_csv("benefits_log.csv")
log = pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id="case:concept:name",
                             activity_key="concept:name",
                             timestamp_key="time:timestamp")

# ---------- 0. Case-level table: attributes + outcomes ----------
case_attrs = ["applicant_group","channel","language_preference","risk_score_band",
              "documentation_completeness","household_size_band",
              "disability_accommodation_requested","prior_case_count","region",
              "case_outcome","appeal_flag"]
cases = log.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
    {**{a: "first" for a in case_attrs},
     "time:timestamp": ["min","max"]})
cases.columns = case_attrs + ["t_start","t_end"]
cases["cycle_days"] = (cases.t_end - cases.t_start).dt.total_seconds()/86400
cases["stratum"] = (cases.risk_score_band.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.documentation_completeness.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.channel.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.household_size_band.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.prior_case_count.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.region.astype(str) + "|" +
                    cases.t_start.dt.to_period("M").astype(str))

# ---------- 1. Variant analysis, overall and within stratum ----------
variants_all = pm4py.get_variants(log)

group_logs = {g: pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
                     log, "applicant_group", [g], level="case", retain=True)
              for g in ["Group A","Group B","Group C","Group D"]}
group_variants = {g: pm4py.get_variants(l) for g, l in group_logs.items()}
# -> per-group variant share table; compute Jensen-Shannon divergence A vs B/C/D.

# Stratified variant comparison: only compare within cells that contain
# BOTH Group A and the comparison group (common support check).
def variant_shares(sub_log):
    v = pm4py.get_variants(sub_log)
    n = sum(len(t) if hasattr(t, "__len__") else t for t in v.values())
    return {k: (len(t) if hasattr(t,"__len__") else t)/n for k, t in v.items()}

for s, cell in cases.groupby("stratum"):
    present = set(cell.applicant_group.unique())
    if "Group A" not in present or len(cell) < 30:
        continue                      # report these as "no common support"
    ids = set(cell.index)
    cell_log = log[log["case:concept:name"].isin(ids)]
    for g in present:
        gl = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(cell_log, "applicant_group",
                                                 [g], level="case", retain=True)
        variant_shares(gl)            # collect -> stratum-weighted contrasts

# Targeted variant filters for the paths named in the scenario
v_docs   = pm4py.filter_variants(log, [("Submit Application","Identity Verification",
            "Completeness Check","Request Missing Documents","Eligibility Assessment",
            "Decision Draft","Applicant Notification")])
v_appeal = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, "concept:name",
            ["Appeal Intake"], level="case", retain=True)

# Activity-incidence disparities (burden), conditional on stratum:
for act in ["Request Missing Documents","Supervisor Review","Fraud Review",
            "Language Support Contact","Accommodation Review","Appeal Review"]:
    touched = set(log.loc[log["concept:name"] == act, "case:concept:name"])
    cases[f"has_{act}"] = cases.index.isin(touched)
# -> logistic regression / stratified difference of has_* by applicant_group.

# ---------- 2. DFG and performance DFG per group ----------
for g, l in group_logs.items():
    dfg, s, e = pm4py.discover_dfg(l)                       # routing structure
    pdfg, s2, e2 = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(l)        # edge waiting times
    # Compare edge sets (routing consistency) and edge medians (timeliness)
    # for the transitions of interest:
    #   Language Support Contact -> Eligibility Assessment
    #   Accommodation Review     -> Eligibility Assessment
    #   Supervisor Review        -> Decision Draft
    #   Completeness Check       -> Request Missing Documents

# Which cases are driving a slow edge?
slow_support = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    log, ("Accommodation Review","Eligibility Assessment"),
    min_performance=3*86400, max_performance=10**9)

slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(log, 20*86400, 10**9)   # tail analysis

# ---------- 3. Service and waiting time decomposition ----------
log_swt = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
    log, service_time_column="@@service_time",
    sojourn_time_column="@@sojourn_time",
    waiting_time_column="@@waiting_time")

svc = pm4py.get_service_time(log, aggregation_measure="median")     # per activity
durations = {g: pm4py.get_all_case_durations(l) for g, l in group_logs.items()}
```

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

**The process is not demonstrably fair, and on the procedural-burden and timeliness dimensions it shows disparities that the available complexity controls do not explain. On the outcome dimension the evidence is indeterminate. I would characterize the overall picture as "likely procedurally unfair in identifiable, fixable respects, with the discrimination question unresolved."**

What the evidence supports with reasonable confidence: the groups that use rights-preserving support steps (B and C) wait substantially longer, those waits are concentrated at the support steps themselves, the steps are served by very small staff pools, and they carry no service-level target. That combination is a structural inequity the organization controls, and it does not depend on resolving any causal question — the 3.8d and 4.7d waits and the 5-and-7-person teams are facts on the record. Group C's +4.1 adjusted days are largely traceable to this. I would call this finding robust.

What the evidence suggests but does not establish: that Group B receives more document requests (+9.5 pp) and more supervisor escalation (+8.0 pp) than comparable Group A cases. This is a real residual, but documentation_completeness is a contaminated control, so the estimate could be understating a treatment effect or reflecting complexity the band fails to capture. The within-resource and escalation-overturn-rate tests in §2 and §7 would substantially narrow this, and should be run before any conclusion is drawn.

What the evidence cannot determine: whether the adjusted approval gaps (−5.5/−2.2/−3.1 pp) reflect discrimination. They are small, they rest on controls of uncertain validity — particularly risk_score_band, whose neutrality is untested and which may be laundering historical disparity into Group D's routing — and they are estimated on group sizes that will not support confident stratified inference. Equally undeterminable from this log: decision quality, explanation quality, whether appeals are being upheld, whether applicants who needed language support received it, and the dignity dimension of procedural fairness, which is where much of the applicant experience actually lives.

The two things I would not conclude: that the raw 20-point approval spread is evidence of discrimination (most of it is compositional), and that the disparities are excused because case mix differs (case mix does not explain the support-step queues, the staffing concentration, or the adjusted escalation gaps). The right next step is not a verdict but a targeted set of tests — audit the risk score's calibration by group, compare within-caseworker, check whether B/C escalations change any decisions, and measure unmet support need — combined with immediately actionable remedies (support-step capacity and SLAs) that are justified on their own terms regardless of how the causal questions resolve.
