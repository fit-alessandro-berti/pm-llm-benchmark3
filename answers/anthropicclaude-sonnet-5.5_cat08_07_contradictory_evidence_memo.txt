# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee  **Re:** Redesign R for fiber installation

**Recommendation: adopt Alternative E now. Keep R for simple, low-risk houses, pause the risky components elsewhere, and build randomized measurement into the staged rollout. Do not scale R nationally and do not roll it back wholesale.**

**What the evidence shows.** R delivers a real gain for a well-defined segment. Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot cases) saw a median of 6 days, p90 of 8 days, first-time-right (FTR) of +3pp, complaints of 1pp, and fewer SLA breaches. Overall, however, R worsened p90 (31.038.5 days), p95 (4562 days), second visits (11%18%), manual activation (7%15%), 30-day complaints (6.1%9.8%) and SLA breaches (13%16%). The damage concentrates in apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business and rural customers.

The process-mining artifacts point to specific mechanisms:
- Billing start before a successful activation test.
- Repeat visits without a preceding survey.
- Missing permit checks in older buildings.
- Complaints escalating after billing.
- Precision falling from 0.79 to 0.51, meaning the new process permits many loosely controlled combinations.

**The trade-off.** R buys speed and accountability for the typical case. It pays with tail risk, rework and billing-before-working-service exposure for harder cases. The visible €23 saving per case is probably not real. Once complaints and revisits are included, cost is an estimated €207 against €198, and that estimate is incomplete.

**Why not the alternatives.**
- **Scaling R (A):** this would extend skip-rule errors, missing permit checks and billing-before-activation to a national base. Those issues are compliance-like and customer-harming, and they have no offsetting benefit outside simple houses.
- **Rollback (B):** this discards a proven gain and the fewer-handover benefit. The harms trace to separable components (survey skipping, post-billing activation, permit omission, coordinator overload), so they can be targeted.
- **Coordinator-only rollout (C):** this is plausible but premature. Coordinator load (93%) and stalls during absences are unresolved, and we cannot yet separate ownership effects from skip-rule effects.

**Immediate actions (next 2 weeks)**
1. Scale R's merged validate/schedule and coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses, with a caseload cap (target 80–85% utilization) and a named backup coordinator.
2. Pause survey skipping for apartments, old buildings, business and rural cases except inside a randomized test.
3. Pause post-billing activation everywhere. Billing starts only after a successful activation test, with a documented, reason-coded exception.
4. Reinstate mandatory permit checks for older buildings.
5. Launch conformance monitoring as a live guardrail (M7).

**Measure next (in value order):** validate the risk rule retrospectively (M6), run a randomized survey-skip test by risk stratum (M1), track 60-day downstream cost (M4), add reason codes (M3) and start timestamps (M2). Coordinator-absence comparison (M5) and separate customer-experience measures (M8) are lower priority.

**Gate for broader rollout.** Proceed segment by segment, and only if a segment meets all of these for four consecutive weeks on adequate volume (proposed thresholds, to be ratified):
- p90 and p95 no worse than baseline.
- FTR within 2pp of baseline.
- Second visits and manual activation within 1pp of baseline.
- Complaints no more than 0.5pp above baseline.
- Billing-before-activation below 1%, all as approved exceptions.
- Full 60-day cost per case at or below baseline.

**Cost of this approach.** We forgo some of R's headline savings for about one quarter. In exchange we avoid a national scale-up whose downside is poorly measured and hard to reverse once customers have been billed before service works.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.**
- Fitness asks whether the model can replay the observed cases. Precision asks whether the model allows much behavior that was never observed.
- The pilot model was discovered from pilot data, which mix the simplified coordinator path with skipped surveys, manual activation, repeat visits and early billing. It therefore replays nearly everything (fitness 0.94). To do so it must permit many combinations (precision 0.51).
- Low precision here signals a process with weak control and many legitimate-looking paths. It does not say the process is good or bad on its own.
- The alignment findings are the operational reading of this. The model tolerates Billing before Activation, repeat visits without a survey, and absent permit checks.
- Caveat: baseline and pilot scores come from different logs and periods, so the comparison is directional.

**Improved median but worse tail.**
- The median is driven by the majority of cases, dominated by simple houses, which benefit from merged tasks and single ownership.
- The tail is driven by a minority that hit the new failure loops: failed visit, repeat visit, missing permit, manual activation, complaint, and stalls while a coordinator is saturated or absent.
- The segment table shows exactly this pattern. Apartments show 2 days at the median but +12 at p90. Old buildings show +1 day and +25 days.
- Possible measurement artifact: if the endpoint is billing start, moving activation after billing shortens the measured median without completing the work.
- The pilot window is short, so the slowest cases may not be fully observed, which would understate the tail.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.**
- The handover network measures transfers of work. It is simpler because the coordinator now owns each case.
- The working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists, so load and context now sit in fewer people. Coordinator utilization reached 93%, and cases stalled during absences because context was not shared.
- Fewer handovers reduce transfer delays and ambiguity but remove redundancy. Accountability improves in the normal case and fragility increases in the exceptional case.
- Activation ownership after billing also became unclear, so some handover reduction was really ownership ambiguity.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework and downstream cost.**
- Visible cost captures surveys, handovers and scheduling, which R reduces. Survey-team utilization fell from 78% to 52%.
- The costs R increases are later and partly unobserved: second visits (team utilization 66%92%), manual activation, complaints, billing disputes, and complaint handling after week 4, which the data exclude.
- The estimate including revisit and complaint handling reverses the sign, at €207 vs €198, and is still incomplete.
- Cost was shifted in time and across teams, not removed. Until the 60-day downstream cost is measured, the saving should be treated as unproven.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings.**
- The components of R work differently by risk. Merging validation and scheduling and single ownership suit simple houses, where uncertainty is low.
- The skip rule and relaxed checks suit them less well in apartments and old buildings, where hidden conditions matter: access, permits, wiring, building-owner approvals. The skip rule is often wrong for medium-risk apartments, and permit checks went missing for old buildings.
- Business customers suffer from manual activation creating billing disputes. Rural customers find rescheduling harder despite fewer handovers.
- The aggregate is a mix. Simple houses are 34% of cases, while apartments and old buildings together are 47%. Because the pilot regions over-represent those harder segments, the aggregate understates R's likely performance in a simpler national mix. It also shows why scaling the whole design would be a mistake.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E, with randomized elements from D and the coordinator-ownership tests from C as the planned second stage. Do not scale R nationally.**

**Immediate action (first 2 weeks)**
- Classify incoming cases by building type, risk and customer segment at intake. Make these attributes mandatory fields, since they were incomplete before week 3.
- Route simple, low-risk houses through R: merged validate/schedule and coordinator ownership.
- Apply baseline controls to apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business and rural cases, except for randomized test arms.
- Set coordinator caseload caps and backup coverage, with shared case notes.
- Switch on conformance monitoring (M7) with weekly reporting and alerts.

**What to pause**
- Survey skipping for all non-simple segments, except inside the M1 randomized test.
- Post-billing activation checks everywhere. Billing starts only after a successful activation test. Any exception must carry a reason code and a named owner.
- Permit-check omission for older buildings.
- Any national expansion of R.

**What to scale**
- R for simple low-risk houses, nationally where they can be reliably identified. This is the segment with clear, consistent gains.
- Coordinator ownership with capacity controls, as a second-stage expansion to other segments once evidence supports it (C).

**What to measure next:** M6 and M7 first, then M3, M4 (start now), M1 (randomized by stratum), M2, then M5 and M8. Also redefine cycle time to end at "service confirmed working" and report p90/p95 by segment, not just the median.

**Evidence required for broader rollout.** These are proposed gates, to be ratified by management. Each segment must meet them for four consecutive weeks on adequate volume (for example, at least 1,500 cases per segment so the tail is estimable):
- p90 and p95 cycle time no worse than baseline (with "service confirmed working" as the endpoint).
- FTR within 2pp of baseline; second visits and manual activation within 1pp of baseline.
- 30-day complaints no more than 0.5pp above baseline; SLA breach not above baseline.
- Billing-before-activation below 1% and permit-check compliance at or above 98% for older buildings.
- 60-day full cost per case at or below baseline.
- Coordinator utilization at or below 85%, with no stalled cases from absence.
- For skipping in any stratum: M1 shows no material increase in second visits or complaints, and M6 shows an acceptable false-negative rate of the rule.

**Stop conditions.** Halt R in any segment, reverting it to baseline (B for that segment), if:
- Complaints exceed baseline by more than 2pp, or p95 worsens by more than 10% for two consecutive weeks.
- Billing-before-activation reappears beyond the allowed exception rate.

**Residual uncertainty.** The real national mix, the true downstream cost and the causal split between ownership and skipping remain unresolved. This recommendation is chosen to be robust to all three. It captures the demonstrated gain, contains the plausible downside, and buys the information that would justify a larger rollout or a rollback.
