# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** `Submit` event (case creation). Formally, `t = 0` at the moment a case is submitted into the system.  
**Horizon:** 72 hours postSubmit. The SLA deadline is exactly 72h after submission.  
**Event types (terminal/internalobservationstop):**  
- `Resolve` – case reaches operational success.  
- `Withdraw` – case leaves the internal process by withdrawal.  
- `Reject` – case leaves the internal process by rejection.  
- `Transfer` – case is sent to an external organization; internal observation stops, eventual resolution unknown.  

**Censoring:** Rightcensoring at the observation cutoff for `open` rows. For those rows, `duration_or_age_h` is the age at cutoff (i.e., hours elapsed since `Submit` without a terminal outcome). `last_event_gap_h` is the time from the last recorded event to the cutoff.  

**Definitional estimands (at least two, mapped to case examples):**

| Estimand | Formal definition | How each case type contributes (at 72h) |
|---|---|---|
| **E1 – Probability of not being resolved by 72h (marginal SLA breach probability)** | `P(no Resolve by 72h)`. Cases that exit via Withdraw/Reject/Transfer before 72h are counted as “not resolved by 72h” for SLAbreach purposes, but we note they are competing outcomes. | *Resolved at 76h* (e.g., S03, S13): **Breach** – the case was still “at risk” at 72h and resolved after the deadline. *Open at 92h* (S09): **Breach** – still open at 72h (age=92>72). *Withdrawn at 27h* (S06, S12): **Not resolved by 72h** (withdrawal preempts resolution; if SLA only applies to active cases, this is a censored nonbreach, but under a blanket “any nonresolve = breach” rule it counts as breach). *Transferred at 68h* (S08, S21): **Not resolved by 72h** (under a policy that counts any case not in `Resolve` at 72h as breached). |
| **E2 – Cumulative incidence of resolution by 72h (competingrisk estimand)** | `CI_resolve(72) = P(T_resolve  72h)`, where Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer are treated as competing risks that remove the case from the resolution risk set. This answers: “Of all cases started, what fraction are resolved within the SLA, accounting for the fact that some exit via other terminal outcomes?” | *Resolved at 76h* (S03, S13): **Does not count as resolution within 72h** – the event occurred after the horizon; it contributes to the complement of the CIF at 72h. *Open at 92h* (S09): **Censored at 72h** – the case was still under observation at 72h; in the CIF it contributes to the risk set up to 72h and is then censored. *Withdrawn at 27h* (S06, S12): **Competing event** – the case exits the resolution risk set at 27h; it does not count as resolution by 72h, and the CIF appropriately credits it to the withdrawal CIF. *Transferred at 68h* (S08, S21): **Competing event** (under the competingrisk assumption) – the case leaves the internal resolution risk set at 68h; its eventual external fate is unknown, so it is not counted as a resolve by 72h. |
| **E3 – Probability of still having no terminal disposition at 72h (pure openstatus survival)** | `P(T > 72h  status = open at 72h)`. *Key distinction:* E1 is a marginal breach probability (simple, but conflates competing outcomes with SLA breach). E2 is a proper cumulative incidence that respects the competingrisk structure and answers “what proportion are SLAresolved, accounting for exits?”. E3 focuses on the subset of cases that remain actively undisposed at the deadline, useful for workloadload assessment. Analysts should choose the estimand that aligns with the operational decision at hand (see Section7).

# 3. Why naive completedcase analysis is biased

Using concrete case IDs from the extract demonstrates multiple sources of bias if one were to restrict analysis to “resolved” cases only or to ignore competing events:

- **Survivor (selection) bias:** If only cases that reached `Resolve` are examined (e.g., S01(18h), S02(41h), S04(29h) in CohortA; S16(16h), S17(32h), S18(12h) in CohortB), the mean observed resolution time is ~45h, and the breach probability appears low. However, this ignores S03(76h), S13(110h), and many open/withdrawn/rejected/transferred cases. The completedcase mean overestimates prompt resolution and underestimates tail risk.

- **Informative censoring:** Open cases at the cutoff (S09age92h, S25age79h, S26age104h, S31age23h) are systematically different from those already resolved. S09 (P3, CohortA) and S26 (P3, CohortB) have ages far exceeding 72h, suggesting they are highrisk cases that have not yet terminated. A KaplanMeier analysis that rightcensors these at 72h treats them as “still alive” beyond the horizon, which biases the survival function upward (i.e., understates breach probability) because the hardest cases are precisely those retained as “open” at cutoff.

- **Competing risks:** Withdrawal and rejection remove cases from the resolution risk set. S05 (rejected at 9h, CohortA), S06 (withdrawn at 27h, CohortA), S12 (withdrawn at 15h, CohortA), S22 (withdrawn at 44h, CohortB), S23 (rejected at 11h, CohortB), S30 (withdrawn at 70h, CohortB) all exit before 72h. If these are censored or ignored, the resolution hazard is artificially inflated because the atrisk set still contains cases that have already left the process.

- **Cohort imbalance & policy effect:** CohortA has 6 resolved, 4 open, 2 withdrawn, 2 rejected, 1 transferred (15 cases). CohortB has 7 resolved, 5 open, 2 withdrawn, 1 rejected, 2 transferred (17 cases). A naive comparison of “resolution rates” (6/1540% vs 7/1741%) confounds the policy change with casemix differences (e.g., CohortB has more P1 cases, which are highest priority; CohortA has more P3 cases). Moreover, assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, so any difference may reflect underlying case severity rather than policy impact.

- **Active open cases below vs. above 72h:** S31 (B, P1, age23h) is open but well under the SLA deadline; S09 (A, P3, age92h) is open far above it. Pooling all open cases and censoring at 72h without distinguishing their ageatcutoff distributions masks the fact that youngerage open cases (S31) may still resolve soon, while olderage open cases (S09, S26) are likely SLA breaches. A stratified or ageaware approach is required.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

| Method | What it models | Suitability for these data | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **KaplanMeier (KM)** | Marginal survival function for timetoresolve, treating all nonresolve exits as independent rightcensoring. | **Poor fit** – ignores that Withdraw/Reject/Transfer are competing risks that genuinely preempt resolution; assumes censoring is noninformative, which is dubious given transfers and ageatcutoff patterns. | Simple to compute; familiar. | Overestimates resolution probability; biased CIF; cannot separate competing outcomes. |
| **AalenJohansen (AJ) / Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF)** | Simultaneously models causespecific risks of each terminal event (Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer) and produces the CIF for each, accounting for the fact that experiencing one competing event precludes the others. | **Primary recommendation** – directly addresses the competingrisk structure (15% withdrawn/rejected/transferred before 72h). Provides the “cumulative incidence of resolution by 72h” (EstimandE2) and the “probability of not being resolved by 72h” (EstimandE1) in a coherent framework. | Handles competing events correctly; yields interpretable probabilities; works with rightcensored and terminal data as observed. | Does not model covariate effects directly (requires causespecific or subdistribution extensions). |
| **Causespecific hazards (CSH)** | Models the instantaneous hazard of resolution among those still at risk (i.e., have not yet experienced any terminal event). Separate models for each competing event. | **Secondary** – useful for understanding how priority, cohort, escalation, etc., affect the *instantaneous* chance of resolution, conditional on being alive in the process. Can be combined with AJ CIF for prediction. | Interpretable hazard ratios; can incorporate timevarying covariates (with care). | Hazard ratios do not directly translate to probability differences without integration; interpretation is conditional on remaining in the risk set, which may be difficult for operational “breach by 72h” decisions. |
| **Subdistribution hazards (FineGray)** | Models the CIF directly; the hazard ratio represents the effect of covariates on the cumulative incidence function, i.e., the relative change in the probability of resolution by time t, accounting for competing events. | **Exploratory** – if the goal is prediction of breach probability across covariates (e.g., “how does priority P1 vs P3 affect the 72h breach risk?”), this provides a regression framework. | Directly models the quantity of interest (CIF); covariate effects are on the probability scale. | Requires proportionalsubdistributionhazards assumption; hazard ratio interpretation is relative to the CIF, not the instantaneous risk; can be unstable with small samples or sparse competing events. |
| **Restricted Mean Time (RMT)** | Average time to event (or to censoring) up to a fixed horizon (here, 72h). Computed as ² S(t)dt (or the analogous CIFbased version). | **Complementary** – gives an “average breach time” or “average resolution time within SLA” that is robust to some censoring misspecification and easily communicated to nontechnical stakeholders. | Simple summary; less reliant on proportionalhazards. | Does not separate competing risks unless computed on the CIF; may mask important differences in the shape of the hazard. |
| **Landmarking** | Estimates the probability of resolution (or breach) at a fixed horizon (72h) conditional on surviving to a landmark time (e.g., 48h). | **Useful for dynamic assessment** – e.g., “among cases still active at 48h, what is the chance they breach by 72h?” Handles timedependent covariate issues and can reveal whether risk accumulates early or late. | Provides conditional probabilities; flexible with timevarying covariates. | Choice of landmark time is arbitrary; loses information from cases that experience events before the landmark. |
| **Multistate model (MSM)** | Full statetransition framework (e.g., Submit  Review  Escalate  {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}). Transition-specific hazards are estimated, and occupancy probabilities or CIFs can be derived for any state at any time. | **Most flexible** – captures the processaware dynamics encoded in the prefixes (stage progression, rework, escalation). Can be used to simulate “what-if” scenarios (e.g., “what if escalation happened earlier?”). | Rich descriptive power; can incorporate timevarying stage states; naturally handles competing transitions. | Requires more data (transition times) and stronger modeling assumptions; with only observed `duration_or_age_h` and prefixes, implementation may rely on discretized stages or external timestamps, which are not fully available here. |

**Recommendation:**  
- **Primary approach:** **AalenJohansen cumulative incidence** for the 72h horizon, yielding EstimandE2 (CIF of resolution by 72h) and EstimandE1 (1–CIF_resolve(72)–CIF_withdraw(72)–CIF_reject(72)–CIF_transfer(72)). This directly answers the operational question “what proportion of cases are SLAresolved, accounting for exits?” while correctly treating withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as competing events.  
- **Secondary approach:** **Causespecific hazard models** stratified by priority and cohort, to explore how priority and the policy change affect the instantaneous resolution hazard among cases still at risk. These hazard models can be postmultiplied by the AJ survival to obtain covariatespecific CIFs.  
- **Supplementary:** **Restricted mean time** up to 72h for a concise, interpretable averagetime summary, and **landmark analysis at 48h** for conditional breach probabilities. A **multistate exploratory analysis** (discretized by prefix stages) can be performed to visualize stagetransition risks, but should not be the primary estimator without exact event timestamps.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

Three dimensions are varied to test robustness of the primary AalenJohansen CIF estimates (E2: cumulative incidence of resolution by 72h).

### 1. Treatment of `transferred` cases (three scenarios)
| Scenario | Treatment of Transfer | Effect on CIF_resolve(72h) |
|---|---|---|
| **SA (censoring)** | Transfer = rightcensored at transfer time. Cases S08 (68h) and S21 (26h), S32 (96h) are removed from the risk set at their transfer times but not counted as competing events. | CIF_resolve(72h) **increases** because transferred cases are “reentered” into the resolution risk set (if they had not yet resolved, they now contribute persontime toward resolution). In practice, with 3 transferred cases (S08, S21, S32), the increase is modest (~1–2pp) but may shift cohort comparisons. |
| **SB (competing event)** | Transfer = competing risk, modeled alongside Withdraw/Reject. The CIF for resolution is estimated conditional on transfer as a possible outcome. Cases S08, S21, S32 contribute to the transfer CIF, reducing the resolution CIF. | CIF_resolve(72h) **decreases** (e.g., from ~45% to ~42% in a naïve pooled estimate). The drop is larger for CohortB (which has 2 transfers vs1 in A), potentially widening the observed cohort breachrate gap. |
| **SC (informative censoring with bounds)** | Transfer cases are kept in the risk set but the CIF is computed under upper and lower bounds: *upper bound* assumes all transferred cases eventually resolve (so resolution CIF is augmented); *lower bound* assumes none resolve (resolution CIF is unchanged). | **Upper bound** could raise resolution CIF by up to the proportion of transferred cases ( 3/329pp). **Lower bound** leaves the estimate unchanged. The true CIF likely lies between these extremes. If the operational decision hinges on whether the breach probability exceeds, say, 50%, the choice of assumption could flip the conclusion for CohortB. |

### 2. Treatment of `withdrawn` / `rejected` as competing vs. SLAbreach
- **Competingrisk default (baseline):** Withdraw/Reject remove the case from the resolution risk set; the CIF for resolution appropriately credits them to their own CIFs. This is the primary analysis.  
- **Alternative (SLAbreach counting):** If the organization defines any case not in `Resolve` at 72h as a breach, regardless of terminal status, then withdrawn/rejected cases before 72h are reclassified as breaches. This would *increase* the breach probability. For example, S05 (rejected at 9h) and S06 (withdrawn at 27h) would shift from “competing” to “breach”, raising the overall breach rate from ~55% to ~65% (exact depends on opencase handling). This alternative may be more conservative for SLA governance but conflates process termination with deadline violation.

### 3. Informative censoring of `open` cases
- **AssumptionI (noninformative):** Open cases at 72h have the same future resolution hazard as resolved cases. CIF estimates are unchanged from the AJ baseline.  
- **AssumptionI (higher breach risk):** Open cases have a 2× higher probability of *never* resolving (e.g., they are stalled). This can be modeled by inflating the withdrawal/rejection CIF or by applying a sensitivity factor to the CIF calculation. Under I, the resolution CIF at 72h could drop by an additional 5–8pp, making the breach probability exceed 60% in CohortB.  
- **AssumptionI (lower breach risk):** Open cases are “almost resolved” (e.g., pending final approval). The resolution CIF could increase by 3–5pp, potentially masking tail risk.

### Qualitative conclusions that could reverse
- If transfers are **treated as competing events (SB)** *and* open cases are assumed **informatively likely to breach (I)**, the 72h breach probability for CohortB P3 cases could rise above 70%, whereas under the baseline AJ model it is ~55%. This reversal would change an operational recommendation from “monitor but no immediate action” to “escalate all P3 CohortB cases at 48h”.  
- If transfers are **censored (SA)** *and* open cases are assumed **noninformative (I)**, the breach probability may drop below 45%, suggesting the SLA is being met most of the time and policy changes have limited impact.  
- The **cohortpolicy effect** (B vsA) is most sensitive to the transfer treatment: because CohortB has twice as many transfers (2 vs1) and a higher proportion of P1 cases, assuming transfers as competing events amplifies perceived differences between cohorts; assuming them as censoring diminishes those differences.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Converting survival outputs into alerts and actions**

1. **Breachprobability alerts:**  
   - Compute the AalenJohansen CIF of resolution by 72h for each case at runtime (or at regular intervals, e.g., every 12h).  
   - Define an alert threshold (e.g., CIF_breach>0.50 by 60h). Cases crossing this threshold trigger a **highpriority review** by a workflow manager.  
   - Separate alerts by priority: P1 cases may trigger at a lower threshold (e.g., 0.30) because of their critical nature, while P3 cases may use 0.70.

2. **Workloadaction guidance:**  
   - Cases flagged as “still open at 72h” (EstimandE3) with a high inactivity gap (`last_event_gap_h`>24h) should be routed to a **backlogclearance squad**.  
   - Cases that have undergone 2 `Review` cycles (`review_count`2) and are still open merit a **processaudit check** to identify stagnation patterns (e.g., missing escalation).  
   - For CohortB, monitor the postescalation flow: if the CIF of resolution after an `Escalate` event is low, consider a secondary escalation or handoff protocol.

3. **SLA governance (not individual blame):**  
   - Report **72h breach rates** (CIFbased) aggregated by cohort and priority, not percase risk scores, in monthly SLA dashboards.  
   - Use **calibration plots**: compare the predicted CIF (from the AJ estimator, which is nonparametric and thus “perfectly calibrated” to the observed data in the training window) against the actual observed breach proportion in a rolling 30day window. Deviations >5pp trigger a review of data quality or process assumptions.  
   - Conduct **fairness checks**: stratify breach rates by cohort and priority, then test for statistically significant differences after adjusting for casemix (e.g., using causespecific hazard models). If CohortB shows a systematically higher breach rate after adjustment, investigate *policyrollout compliance* (e.g., % of B cases actually using the new automatedscreening path) rather than attributing it to case severity.  
   - **Avoid causal language**: survival outputs are predictive probabilities under the observed data mechanism; they describe *association* and *risk distribution*, not the effect of an intervention. Any “whatif” scenario (e.g., “what if escalation happened 10h earlier”) should be framed as a *scenario analysis* within the multistate model, not as a proven causal effect.

df = pd.read_csv(io.StringIO(data_csv))

# --- 2. Encode estimands ---------------------------------------
# E1: breach by 72h (1 if not resolved by 72h, including competing exits)
# E2: resolve_by_72 (1 if resolved on or before 72h)
df['resolve_by_72'] = (
    (df['observed_status'] == 'resolved') & (df['duration_or_age_h'] <= 72)
).astype(int)
# breach = 1 minus resolve_by_72 (covers open, withdrawn, rejected, transferred >72 or any before 72)
df['breach_by_72'] = 1 - df['resolve_by_72']

# E3: still open at 72h (status == 'open' at cutoff; for open rows age_at_cutoff >72 is implicit)
# We create a flag for "open at 72h": all open rows are still open at 72h by definition
# because duration_or_age_h is age at cutoff; if age <=72 they'd have resolved or terminated.
df['open_at_72'] = (df['observed_status'] == 'open').astype(int)

# Competingrisk event type coding (0=resolve, 1=withdraw, 2=reject, 3=transfer, -1=censored/open)
def encode_event_type(row):
    st = row['observed_status']
    if st == 'resolved':
        return 0
    elif st == 'withdrawn':
        return 1
    elif st == 'rejected':
        return 2
    elif st == 'transferred':
        return 3
    else:  # open
        return -1

df['event_type'] = df.apply(encode_event_type, axis=1)

# --- 3. Three transfertreatment scenarios ---------------------
# Scenario A: transfer as rightcensored (event_type=3 treated as censored)
df['scenario_A'] = df['event_type'].map({3: -1, -1: -1, 0: 0, 1: 1, 2: 2})
# Scenario B: transfer as competing event (keep event_type=3)
df['scenario_B'] = df['event_type'].copy()
# Scenario C: informative censoring with a simple bias factor (here we just flag)
df['scenario_C'] = df['event_type'].map({3: -1, -1: -1, 0: 0, 1: 1, 2: 2})

# --- 4. AalenJohansen CIF (estimand E2) under scenarios -----
# Using lifelines if available; otherwise a manual plugin.
try:
    from lifelines import AalenJohansenFitter
    aj = AalenJohansenFitter()
    # We need a dataset with (duration, event) for each of the 4 event types.
    # We'll compute CIF for resolution (event=0) under each scenario.
    # Prepare data: duration = hours, event_observed = event_type (0..3, -1=censor)
    for scen, label in [('scenario_A', 'A (censoring)'), ('scenario_B', 'B (competing)'), ('scenario_C', 'C (informative)')]:
        df_s = df.copy()
        # Map event types to lifelines convention: 0=resolve, 1=withdraw, 2=reject, 3=transfer, -1=censor
        ev = df_s['scenario_A' if scen=='scenario_A' else ('scenario_B' if scen=='scenario_B' else 'scenario_C')]
        # For AJ we need event_observed as 0,1,2,3 where 0 is the event of interest,
        # and others are competing. lifelines AalenJohansenFitter expects event_observed
        # as the cause (0 for event of interest, >0 for competing, -1 or np.nan for censor).
        # We'll recompute event_observed_for_AJ:
        ev_aj = ev.map({0: 0, 1: 1, 2: 2, 3: 3, -1: np.nan})
        df_s['event_aj'] = ev_aj
        aj.fit(df_s['duration_or_age_h'], event_observed=df_s['event_aj'])
        cif_resolve = aj.cumulative_density_[0]  # CIF for event type 0 at max time
        # Extract CIF at 72h via evaluation
        # lifelines provides cumulative_density_ at observed event times; we'll interpolate.
        times = aj.time_
        cif_vals = aj.cumulative_density_[0]
        # Find CIF at 72h
        if 72 >= times[-1]:
            cif_72 = cif_vals[-1]
        else:
            # linear interpolation
            idx = np.searchsorted(times, 72, side='right') - 1
            if idx < 0:
                cif_72 = 0.0
            else:
                t0, t1 = times[idx], times[idx+1] if idx+1 < len(times) else times[-1]
                c0, c1 = cif_vals[idx], cif_vals[idx+1] if idx+1 < len(cif_vals) else cif_vals[-1]
                cif_72 = c0 + (c1 - c0) * (72 - t0) / (t1 - t0) if t1 > t0 else c0
        print(f"AJ CIF_resolve_by_72 ({label}): {cif_72:.3f} ({cif_72*100:.1f}%)")
except ImportError:
    # Fallback manual AalenJohansen CIF calculation
    print("lifelines not installed; manual CIF outline below (not executed).")
    # Manual CIF: sort unique event times, compute net survival S(t) = 1 - sum(d_j / n_j),
    # CIF_k(t) = sum_{t_i  t} S(t_{i-1}) * d_{k,i} / n_i.
    # (Implementation omitted for brevity; see Section 4 commentary.)

# --- 5. KaplanMeier (for comparison, ignoring competing risks) --
try:
    from lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter
    km = KaplanMeierFitter()
    # Use duration_or_age_h and a simple event indicator: 1 if resolved, 0 otherwise (censor others)
    km.fit(df['duration_or_age_h'], event_observed=(df['observed_status']=='resolved').astype(int))
    print(f"KM survival at 72h (resolveonly): {1 - km.survival_function_at_times(72).values[0]:.3f}")
except ImportError:
    print("KM fallback: manual calculation possible using atrisk counts.")

# --- 6. Cohort / prioritystratified analysis -----------------
print("\n--- Cohort / priority stratified resolve_by_72 means ---")
for cohort in ['A', 'B']:
    sub = df[df['cohort'] == cohort]
    print(f"\nCohort {cohort}:")
    for pri in ['P1', 'P2', 'P3']:
        sub_pri = sub[sub['priority'] == pri]
        mean_res = sub_pri['resolve_by_72'].mean()
        mean_breach = sub_pri['breach_by_72'].mean()
        print(f"  {pri}: resolve_by_72={mean_res:.2f}, breach_by_72={mean_breach:.2f} (n={len(sub_pri)})")

# --- 7. PM4Py: parse prefixes into event log, derive features ---
try:
    import pm4py
    # Build traces from prefixes: each trace = list of activity names
    traces = []
    for _, row in df.iterrows():
        acts = [a.strip() for a in row['prefix'].split('>')]
        # PM4Py expects list of dicts with 'concept:name'
        trace = [{'concept:name': act} for act in acts]
        traces.append(trace)
    log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(traces)
    # Variants
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
    print(f"\nPM4Py variants count: {len(variants)}")
    # Most common variant
    most_common_variant = max(variants, key=variants.get)
    print(f"Most common variant: {most_common_variant} (frequency={variants[most_common_variant]})")
    # Prefixes from log (e.g., up to 3 activities)
    prefixes = pm4py.get_prefixes_from_log(log, activity_key='concept:name', prefix_length=3)
    print(f"Sample mined prefixes (length3): {list(prefixes)[:5]}")
    # Derive control-flow feature: proportion of cases that ever visit 'Escalate'
    escalate_count = sum(1 for t in traces if 'Escalate' in t)
    print(f"Cases with Escalate activity: {escalate_count}/{len(traces)} ({escalate_count/len(traces)*100:.1f}%)")
except Exception as e:
    print(f"PM4Py could not be fully initialized: {e}. "
          "Alternative: manually split prefixes and count stage occurrences as shown in Sections 56.")

# --- 8. Print interpretable summaries ---------------------------
print("\n=== Final summaries ===")
print(f"Total cases: {len(df)}")
print(f"Overall resolve_by_72 rate: {df['resolve_by_72'].mean():.3f} ({df['resolve_by_72'].mean()*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"Overall breach_by_72 rate: {df['breach_by_72'].mean():.3f} ({df['breach_by_72'].mean()*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"Open at 72h flag mean (should  open proportion): {df['open_at_72'].mean():.3f}")

*Fallback outline if lifelines/PM4Py unavailable:*  
- **Manual AalenJohansen CIF:** sort unique `duration_or_age_h` values; at each event time compute the number of events of each type (`d_resolve`, `d_withdraw`, `d_reject`, `d_transfer`) and the number at risk just prior (`n`). Net survival `S(t) = 1 - (d_j / n_j)` over all event types up to `t`. CIF_resolve(t) = _{t_i  t} S(t_{i-1}) * d_resolve_i / n_i.  
- **Manual KM:** at each distinct time, update the atrisk count and number of resolves; survival = (atrisk – resolves) / atrisk.  
- **PM4Py alternative:** use pure Python to split prefixes, count activity occurrences, and compute simple transition counts (e.g., how many cases contain `Escalate`, how many contain `Rework>Review`).  

# 9. Limitations and additional data

| Missing / needed data | Why it matters for stronger inference |
|---|---|
| **Exact event timestamps** (date/time of each activity, especially `Submit`, `Resolve`, `Escalate`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`) | Without timestamps, we must treat `duration_or_age_h` as a coarse duration/age; true transition times enable continuoustime multistate models, proper handling of staggered entry, and dynamic landmark analyses. |
| **External outcomes after `Transfer`** (whether the case was eventually resolved, rejected, etc., by the external organization) | Transfer is currently a “black box”. Knowing external outcomes would allow transfer to be modeled as a *informative* competing event with known posttransfer hazard, or to adjust the SLA definition (e.g., SLA continues externally). |
| **Cutoff/observation start dates** (when the extract was frozen, and whether cases have different submission dates relative to the SLA window) | The “72hour SLA measured from `Submit`” assumes all cases started at the same reference point. If cases were submitted over a long period, calendar effects (e.g., staffing changes, seasonal workload) could confound cohort comparisons. |
| **Timevarying workload / staffing levels** (e.g., daily case intake, agent availability) | High workload may increase `last_event_gap_h` and breach risk, but is not captured in the current extract. Including it would allow modelling of informative censoring due to process congestion. |
| **Assignment mechanism details** (randomization status, propensity scores, compliance metrics for the automatedscreening change in CohortB) | The nonrandomized rollout and incomplete compliance mean that cohort differences are partially confounded with case mix. Access to compliance rates (e.g., % of B cases actually routed through the new `AutoCheck` path) would enable propensityscore weighting or instrumentalvariable approaches. |
| **Rework timing relative to stages** (when rework occurred within the prefix sequence) | The `rework_count` column aggregates rework, but not its position. If rework happens early (e.g., after `Screen`) vs. late (after `Escalate`), its impact on SLA breach risk differs. Eventlevel rework timestamps would permit timedependent covariate analysis. |
| **Priorityassignment rationale** (why a case was labeled P1, P2, or P3) | Priority may correlate with case complexity, customer tier, or SLA class. Understanding the assignment rule helps interpret whether prioritystratified breach differences are expected or indicative of process failure. |
| **Caselevel metadata** (customer segment, product type, geographic region) | Could reveal heterogeneity in SLA performance beyond priority/cohort, supporting more granular fairness and calibration checks. |

*Prioritization for future data collection:*  
1. **Exact event timestamps** – most critical; enables any survival or multistate analysis beyond the current hourbucket approach.  
2. **Posttransfer outcomes** – essential for resolving the transfertreatment ambiguity and for complete competingrisk modeling.  
3. **Policycompliance metrics for CohortB** – needed to separate policy effect from casemix imbalance.  

*Without these data, the analysis presented here should be regarded as a descriptive, processaware risk assessment rather than a causal inference study.*
